A unified deep learning application framework for forensic shoeprint analysis under data-limited conditions
Authors: Moonsoo Jang, Changhee Hwang, Soyoung Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents a data-efficient framework for applying deep learning to forensic shoeprint matching under realistic casework constraints. In forensic practice, deployment is often limited by scarce labeled data, distributional differences between reference collections and casework evidence, and a lack of practical guidance on model selection, augmentation, and adaptation. Rather than proposing a new architecture, this study focuses on how existing deep learning components can be systematically adapted to improve reliability under such conditions. We examine three key aspects commonly encountered in practice: the selection of pretrained representations, the design of augmentation strategies, and adaptation to new domains using limited target-domain data. Experiments on two complementary shoeprint datasets demonstrate that generic pretrained models without adaptation exhibit unstable behavior, whereas domain-aligned backbones provide more consistent performance. In terms of data augmentation, standard geometric transformations can lead to non-monotonic and sometimes degraded performance, while morphology-preserving generative augmentation based on a convolutional autoencoder yielded more stable improvements. Under cross-domain dataset shift, few-shot fine-tuning with only 1-3% of labeled target-domain data recovers a substantial portion of the performance achieved with full-data training. In addition, combining generative and standard augmentation strategies produces competitive performance, suggesting that different augmentation mechanisms may introduce complementary variability, although systematic exploration of such combinations remains an open problem. Overall, this study provides practical, evidence-based guidance for applying deep learning in forensic shoeprint analysis under data-limited and domain-shifted conditions, with implications for other domains where similar constraints arise.Keywords: Shoeprint, Shoeprint Augmentation, Deep Learning, Few-shot Learning, Domain ShiftSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Shoeprints constitute an important form of forensic evidence; however, deploying deep learning methods in operational casework remains challenging. In practice, forensic laboratories often face multiple constraints simultaneously, including (1) limited availability of labeled outsole impressions that can be legally shared, (2) uncertainty regarding which modeling strategies preserve probative tread characteristics, and (3) domain shift between publicly available datasets and locally acquired casework imagery. These constraints are not unique to forensic science. Similar challenges arise in other high-stakes domains, such as medical imaging, where legal, ethical, or privacy considerations restrict data sharing and annotation, and datasets are often fragmented and sensitive1. Traditionally, footwear examination relies on visual comparison of outsole design, wear patterns, and randomly acquired characteristics, with conclusions expressed using established forensic terminology2. While this approach is well established in practice, it can be inherently subjective and does not scale efficiently to large reference collections. Recent reports have therefore emphasized the importance of quantitative validation, transparent performance assessment, and error-rate characterization in feature-comparison disciplines, including footwear and tire impression analysis3–5. Recent advances in deep learning have demonstrated strong performance across a range of forensic pattern-recognition tasks, including face recognition6, fingerprint analysis7,8, and writer identification9. In the context of footwear evidence, convolutional neural network (CNN)-based descriptor learning and retrieval approaches have also been explored10, with more recent work emphasizing robustness to degradation, partial impressions, and cross-condition variability11–14. Despite these advances, many existing methods are developed and evaluated under controlled experimental conditions and do not directly translate to operational forensic settings, where data are limited and acquisition conditions vary. In practice, applying deep learning to forensic shoeprint data typically follows an incremental process. Practitioners often begin with an existing pretrained model, introduce data augmentation to compensate for limited training samples, and subsequently consider adapting the model to locally available casework data if additional labels become available. Such modeling components are widely adopted in data-limited deep learning settings15, but are often considered in isolation, despite their strong interdependence in determining overall performance and reliability13,14. This gap motivates the need for a structured perspective that organizes these decisions under realistic forensic constraints. Each of these components introduces its own challenges under limited data conditions. Pretrained models from visually unrelated domains may produce less stable or less discriminative representations when adaptation is constrained. Data augmentation, while widely used to improve generalization, must be carefully designed in forensic contexts to avoid distorting fine-scale tread features that carry evidential value. Beyond conventional geometric transformations, alternative augmentation paradigms may be required to better preserve source-consistent structural characteristics. Furthermore, when a domain shift is present, applying pretrained models without adaptation (i.e., in a zero-shot setting) may lead to reduced performance, highlighting the importance of efficient adaptation strategies. To address these challenges, this study adopts a unified, data-efficient transfer-learning framework for forensic shoeprint matching. Rather than introducing a new model architecture, we organize commonly used modeling components into a structured workflow consisting of three practical considerations: (1) selection of a domain-aligned pretrained backbone, (2) selection of augmentation strategies that preserve tread morphology, and (3) adaptation to the target domain using limited labeled data. By evaluating these components within a consistent experimental design using two complementary datasets, we provide practical insight into how different modeling choices influence performance and stability under realistic forensic conditions. Importantly, the goal of this study is not to replace expert judgment but to support forensic decision-making by providing a structured and reproducible approach to applying deep learning when data are limited. The framework is intended to assist practitioners in systematically exploring and selecting appropriate modeling strategies, thereby enabling more reliable and scalable analyses of signature evidence in operational settings.

Unified deep learning framework

The proposed framework presents a practical workflow for deploying deep learning in data-limited forensic settings. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the framework consists of three stages that correspond to a natural progression in applied settings.

Overview of the proposed unified deep learning framework for forensic shoeprint analysis.

Stage 1: Domain-aligned pretrained backbone selection In many forensic scenarios, the available training data are insufficient to support training a deep neural network from scratch. Consequently, practitioners typically begin with a pretrained model and assess whether its learned representation is suitable for the target task. When data are limited, models pretrained on datasets that share morphological characteristics with short-term impressions tend to provide more stable and informative representations than generic pretrained models. Conversely, pretrained models from visually unrelated domains may yield less stable performance when only limited adaptation is possible.

Stage 2: Source-consistent augmentation selection

Once a pretrained backbone is selected, the next challenge is to improve generalization with limited training data. Data augmentation is a common strategy; however, in forensic shoeprint analysis, augmentation should be carefully constrained to avoid altering probative thread features. Beyond conventional geometric transformations, augmentation strategies should aim to preserve source-consistent structural characteristics while introducing meaningful variability. This stage focuses on identifying augmentation approaches that expand training diversity without compromising the evidentiary integrity of shoeprint patterns.

Stage 3: Few-shot adaptation to the target dataset

Finally, when a limited amount of labeled target-domain data is available, few-shot fine-tuning provides an effective means of adapting pretrained representations to domain-specific characteristics. Rather than retraining from scratch, this approach enables efficient recalibration of the model to the target distributions. As demonstrated in this study, even a small proportion of target-domain samples can substantially improve performance under domain shift, making few-shot adaptation a practical strategy for real-world forensic deployment.

Previous work

Recent shoeprint-specific studies have increasingly focused on robust retrieval and matching under degraded crime-scene conditions, often combining CNN-based feature representations with correlation- or ranking-based similarity measures and evaluating performance under realistic noise and partial-print scenarios11–13. While these approaches have demonstrated promising results, they are typically developed and evaluated under controlled experimental settings, and their behavior under data-limited and domain-shifted conditions remains less systematically understood.

Broader surveys and systematic reviews emphasize that many remaining challenges in forensic footwear analysis are not solely architectural, but arise from practical constraints such as limited publicly shareable datasets, severe impression degradation, and domain mismatch between controlled reference points and casework evidence16. These observations highlight the need for evaluation strategies that more closely reflect realistic deployment scenarios, rather than focusing exclusively on architectural improvements.

In parallel, recent studies in applied machine learning have explored structured evaluation of modeling choices, such as pretrained model selection, data augmentation, and adaptation strategies- particularly in settings where labeled data are limited. Rather than treating these components in isolation, this line of work considers how different design decisions interact to affect performance and stability under constrained conditions13,14.

Motivated by these observations, this study adopts a framework-level perspective to systematically examine how commonly used modeling choices behave under data-limited and domain-shifting forensic settings. Rather than proposing a new architecture or assuming a single optimal configuration, we focus on empirically characterizing how base model selection, augmentation strategy, and limited-data adaptation influence performance and reliability in practice.

Data augmentation for forensic shoeprints

In forensic shoeprint analysis, the availability of labeled training data is inherently limited due to legal, logistical, and casework constraints. As a result, data augmentation is commonly used to expand training diversity without requiring additional data collection17. However, unlike general computer vision tasks, augmentation in this context must be treated as a constrained design choice, since shoeprint comparison relies on fine-scale tread morphology and subtle, source-specific characteristics. Augmentation strategies that alter or obscure these structures risk degrading evidentiary integrity rather than improving generalization.

Widely used image augmentation techniques, such as rotation, translation, scaling, and shearing, are commonly employed to improve robustness to variability in natural image analysis17. In shoeprint imagery, however, such transformations do not necessarily correspond to realistic within-source variation. While they may simulate changes in orientation or position, they can also distort local tread features critical to forensic comparison. Figure 2 illustrates the conceptual difference between transformation-based augmentation and structure-preserving generative augmentation considered in this study.

Comparison of two augmentation paradigms considered in this study.

To address this limitation, an alternative approach is to consider augmentation methods that preserve the structural characteristics of the original impression while introducing limited variability. Generative models, including autoencoders and generative adversarial networks (GANs), have been widely studied for synthesizing realistic image variations18,19. In this study, we adopt a convolutional autoencoder (CAE)20, which reconstructs input impressions through a learned latent representation while preserving spatial structure via convolutional encoding and decoding. Such architectures have been shown to retain local spatial relationships and improve reconstruction fidelity in image-based tasks21.

It is important to note that the goal of this study is not to identify the most advanced generative model for shoeprint synthesis, nor to exhaustively compare all augmentation techniques. Instead, we focus on comparing two representative paradigms–transformation-based augmentation and structure-preserving generative augmentation–under a controlled experimental setting. This allows us to examine how different augmentation strategies influence model behavior and performance stability when training data are limited.

The choice of augmentation strategy has implications beyond within-dataset performance. Augmentation that preserves morphological fidelity provides a more stable foundation for subsequent adaptation under domain shift. Similar challenges have been reported in other domains, such as medical imaging, where models trained on one data distribution often fail to generalize to unseen acquisition conditions, and data-efficient adaptation strategies have been shown to improve reliability under distributional mismatch22. These parallels highlight that the data scarcity and domain-shift issues encountered in forensic shoeprints are part of a broader class of real-world deployment challenges that require carefully constrained, data-efficient augmentation and adaptation strategies.

Transfer learning and deep learning approaches

In forensic shoeprint analysis, the limited availability of labeled data makes training deep neural networks from scratch impractical. Transfer learning provides a natural solution by leveraging models pretrained on large-scale datasets and adapting them to the target task. This approach reduces the need for extensive training data while enabling the reuse of general visual representations learned from diverse image sources.

Convolutional neural networks(CNNs) pretrained on datasets such as ImageNet have been widely adopted as feature extractors in various image analysis tasks6,23. In forensic applications, Siamese network architectures have been particularly effective for pairwise comparison tasks, as they learn representations that directly encode similarity between two input images24. These approaches have also been applied to shoeprint comparison and cross-domain matching problems25.

More recently, domain-specific models have been introduced to better capture the characteristics of footwear impressions. The Shoe-MS algorithm26 integrates pretrained convolutional features with task-specific fine-tuning for shoeprint comparison, reflecting the importance of aligning pretrained representations with the morphological properties of the target data. Such domain-aligned models have been shown to provide more stable and discriminative features than generic pretrained models when training data are limited.

Rather than focusing on developing new architectures, this study builds on these existing approaches and examines how different choices of pretrained backbones influence performance under limited data and domain shift. This perspective aligns with the practical setting of forensic deployment, where selecting and adapting existing models is often more feasible than training new models from scratch.

Current examiner workflow and reporting in shoeprint analysis

In forensic footwear examination, practitioners traditionally rely on visual comparison of outsole design, size, wear patterns, and randomly acquired characteristics to assess the relationship between questioned and reference impressions. Established guidelines, such as those provided by SWGTREAD2, define terminology and reporting practices to support consistent interpretation and communication of findings. These procedures form a structured workflow that emphasizes careful observation and expert judgment in evaluating shoeprint evidence.

Recent discussions in forensic science have highlighted the importance of complementing such qualitative assessments with quantitative validation and transparent performance evaluation3–5. In particular, there is increasing recognition that computational methods can support forensic analysis by providing reproducible similarity measures and systematic comparison across large datasets.

In this study, we consider deep learning-based approaches to be assistive tools that operate within the existing forensic workflow. Rather than replacing examiner judgment, the goal is to support decision-making by improving consistency, scalability, and the ability to evaluate large numbers of candidate impressions under data-limited conditions.

Methods

Data description

This study utilizes two shoeprint datasets with complementary characteristics to simulate a domain-shift deployment scenario. The first dataset (DB1), provided by CSAFE27, contains 2D impressions from a wide variety of shoes with different outsole designs and is used as a reference dataset for model development. The second dataset (DB2), collected from a smaller set of shoes with repeated acquisitions over time28, is used as the target dataset for evaluation and adaptation.

For DB1, the dataset comprises impressions from 150 distinct shoes, each scanned five times for both left and right outsoles, resulting in a total of 1,500 images. The data are partitioned at the shoe level into training, validation, and test sets in a 100:25:25 ratio, yielding approximately 1,000, 250, and 250 images, respectively. For DB2, the dataset includes impressions from 158 shoes, partitioned into 86 training, 12 validation, and 60 test shoes, with multiple scans per shoe. All dataset splits are performed at the shoe level prior to pair construction to prevent information leakage. Matching and non-matching pairs are then generated exclusively within each split. Ensuring that impressions from the same physical shoe do not appear across different partitions. The resulting datasets reflect realistic comparison scenarios in which non-matching pairs are substantially more frequent than matching pairs.

Figure 3 presents representative examples and data splits for both datasets. While DB1 contains impressions from a broader set of outsole designs, DB2 emphasizes repeated observations from a more limited set of shoes, resulting in differences in dataset composition and task structure.

This experimental setup enables a controlled analysis of domain shift by training models on DB1 and evaluating them on DB2, with optional fine-tuning using limited subsets of DB2 training data. The domain shift considered in this study arises from differences in dataset composition and acquisition characteristics rather than from a contrast between laboratory and real casework data.

Problem formulation

The shoeprint comparison task is formulated as a pairwise binary classification problem. Each input consists of a pair of shoeprint images, denoted as , and the objective is to determine whether the two impressions originate from the same physical shoe.

Given an input pair, the model outputs a similarity score , where higher values indicate a greater likelihood that the two images belong to the same source. The ground-truth label is defined as y=1 for matching pairs (same source) and y=0 for non-matching pairs (different source).

Model training is performed using binary cross-entropy loss, and predictions are obtained by applying a threshold to the similarity score. In this study, we focus on evaluating the relative behavior of different model configurations and augmentation strategies under this formulation, rather than optimizing a specific decision threshold. This formulation is consistent across all experiments, allowing controlled comparison of different design choices under identical evaluation conditions.

Model configuration

To examine the effect of model design under limited data conditions, we consider three representative configurations that differ in their use of pretrained representations and fine-tuning strategies.

The first configuration (M1) is based on the SHoe-MS model26, which employs a domain-aligned pretrained convolutional backbone with additional task-specific layers for shoeprint comparison. The pretrained backbone is partially fine-tuned to adapt to the target task.

The second configuration (M2) adopts a Siamese architecture with a pretrained VGG16 backbone23. In this setting, the convolutional layers of the backbone are kept frozen, and only the similarity learning component is trained. This configuration uses generic pretrained features without task-specific adaptation.

The third configuration (M3) consists of a Siamese convolutional neural network trained from scratch without any pretrained initialization. This model serves as a baseline to evaluate the effect of learning representations directly from limited data.

All configurations take a pair of shoeprint images as input and produce a similarity score through a sigmoid activation function. Training is performed using binary cross-entropy loss under identical optimization settings to ensure a controlled comparison across models.

Augmentation strategies

To examine the effect of data augmentation under limited data conditions, we consider two representative strategies: standard geometric augmentation and generative augmentation using a convolutional autoencoder (CAE).

Standard augmentation includes commonly used geometric transformations such as rotation, translation, scaling, and shearing. In contrast, the generative approach produces additional training samples by reconstructing images through a learned representation, preserving the overall structure of the original impression.

Augmentation is applied only to the training data. For each original pair , augmented samples are generated by keeping unchanged while replacing with its augmented version. This preserves the pairwise structure of the dataset while increasing the number of training samples.

To control the effect of augmentation, additional samples are generated at four levels: 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20% of the original training set size. These settings allow us to systematically analyze how different augmentation strategies and data expansion levels influence model behavior.

Experimental design

To systematically analyze the effects of different design choices under limited data and domain shift, we construct a controlled experimental design that evaluates combinations of model configurations, augmentation strategies, and data expansion levels. Rather than selecting a single optimal configuration sequentially, we consider a range of combinations to examine how each factor influences model behavior.

Within the reference dataset (DB1), models are trained on the DB1 training split and evaluated on the DB1 test split to assess within-domain performance. For each model configuration, augmentation strategies are applied at four levels (5%, 10%, 15%, and 20% of the original training data), enabling a controlled comparison of how data expansion and augmentation type affect performance.

To evaluate domain shift, models trained on DB1 are directly applied to DB2 without additional adaptation (zero-shot setting). This setup reflects a practical deployment scenario in which a model developed using one reference collection is applied to a different dataset with distinct characteristics.

In addition, we consider a data-efficient adaptation setting in which models pretrained on DB1 are fine-tuned using limited subsets of DB2 training data. Specifically, small fractions of the DB2 training set are used to simulate few-shot conditions, and performance is evaluated on the fixed DB2 test split. This design allows us to examine how model performance evolves as target-domain data becomes available.

All experiments are conducted under consistent training and evaluation settings, including identical optimization procedures and data splits, to ensure that observed performance differences are primarily attributable to the design choices under investigation rather than confounding factors.

Evaluation metrics

Model performance is evaluated using standard metrics for binary classification. The primary evaluation metric is the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), which measures the model’s ability to distinguish between matching and non-matching pairs across all decision thresholds.

In addition, classification accuracy is reported using a fixed threshold of 0.5 on the predicted similarity score. To provide a more comprehensive assessment, we also report the equal error rate (EER), which corresponds to the point where the false acceptance rate and false rejection rate are equal.

These metrics are computed consistently across all experiments, enabling direct comparison of different model configurations and augmentation strategies under identical evaluation conditions.

As a result, without further adaptation, fixed pretrained representations may not provide sufficiently discriminative features for this task.

Overall, these results indicate that the model performance is influenced not only by the use of pretrained features but also by how well those features align with the target domain and whether they can be adapted during training. Configurations that enable task-specific representation learning – either through fine-tuning (M1) or training from scratch (M3) – tend to exhibit more stable and balanced performance than models that rely on fixed pretrained features.

Domain shift and adaptation

We further investigate model behavior under domain shift by evaluating performance on DB2 while varying the amount of target-domain data used for fine-tuning. In this analysis, data augmentation is applied only to the source-domain training data (DB1-train). Specifically, an additional 20% of DB1-train samples are generated using each augmentation strategy, and the model is trained on this augmented dataset. The trained model is then fine-tuned with varying proportions of the DB2-train samples and evaluated on the DB2 test set.

Figure 6 illustrates the performance of generative and standard augmentation strategies across different data expansion levels, reporting both AUC (solid lines) and accuracy (dashed lines).

Performance under domain shift for (a) standard augmentation and (b) generative augmentation as the proportion of DB2-train samples used for fine-tuning increases. Solid lines represent AUC, and dashed lines represent accuracy.

In the zero-shot setting (p=0), both augmentation strategies exhibit reduced performance relative to within-domain evaluation, reflecting the distributional mismatch between DB1 and DB2. As the number of target-domain samples increases, performance improves for both approaches, indicating effective adaptation through limited fine-tuning.

However, distinct differences emerge in how each augmentation strategy responds to increasing data. Generative augmentation exhibits a relatively smooth and consistent improvement in both AUC and accuracy as p increases, with performance stabilizing at higher data levels. In contrast, standard augmentation shows more variable behavior, particularly at lower data regimes, where accuracy fluctuations are more pronounced, and improvements are less consistent.

The relationship between AUC and accuracy further highlights differences in prediction behavior. While both methods achieve comparable AUC values at higher data levels, standard augmentation tends to exhibit greater variability in accuracy, suggesting it is more sensitive to decision thresholds. Generative augmentation, on the other hand, maintains a more consistent trend between AUC and accuracy, indicating more stable decision boundaries as additional data are introduced.

These observations suggest that under domain shift, the effectiveness of augmentation is influenced not only by the amount of target-domain data but also by the stability of the representations learned from the source domain. In particular, augmentation strategies that yield consistent behavior across both threshold-independent (AUC) and threshold-dependent (accuracy) metrics may enable more reliable adaptation when only limited target-domain data are available. Additional experiments with different augmentation ratios (e.g., 5%, 10%, and 15%) are provided in the Appendix for completeness.

To further explore the interaction between augmentation strategies, we also consider a mixed setting in which generative and standard augmentations are applied jointly in equal proportions. As in the previous experiments, this combined augmentation is applied to DB1-train to generate an additional 20% of training data, after which the model is fine-tuned using carving proportions of DB2-train samples. The mixed augmentation setting achieves competitive performance across all data levels, with AUC values comparable to those obtained using individual augmentation strategies and strong performance in higher-data regimes. This observation suggests that different augmentation methods may introduce complementary forms of variation, potentially enriching the learned representations.

However, these results should be interpreted with caution. The current analysis considers only a limited set of combinations, and a systematic investigation of optimal mixtures and their effects on performance is beyond the scope of this study. Further exploration of such combinations may provide additional insight into how different augmentation mechanisms interact under domain shift.

Discussion

This study examined a data-efficient framework for applying deep learning to forensic shoeprint matching under realistic casework constraints. Rather than introducing a new model or emphasizing absolute performance, we focused on how commonly used modeling components behave under data-limited and domain-shifted conditions, and how these choices can be organized into a structured, practitioner-oriented workflow.

The results indicate that model performance in forensic shoeprint analysis is influenced not only by architectural choices but also by how representations are constructed and adapted under constrained data conditions. In particular, the findings suggest that starting from a domain-aligned pretrained backbone selection provides a more stable foundation for downstream adaptation than relying on generic pretrained features or training from scratch. While the Shoe-MS model serves as a representative example, the implication is broader: when training data are limited, alignment between the pretrained representation and the task-specific structure is an important factor in achieving reliable performance.

A similar consideration applies to data augmentation. The experiments show that augmentation should not be treated as an unconstrained mechanism for increasing data quantity. Standard geometric transformations, although widely used, can introduce variations that do not necessarily correspond to realistic within-source changes and may lead to variability in performance. In contrast, generative augmentation tends to produce more consistent behavior across different augmentation levels, suggesting that preserving structural characteristics of shoeprints can contribute to more stable learning. These observations indicate that the effectiveness of augmentation depends not only on the amount of data generated but also on whether the generated samples retain task-relevant structure.

The analysis under domain shift further reinforces this observation. While both augmentation strategies benefit from increasing amounts of target-domain data, generative augmentation exhibits more stable trends across both AUC and accuracy, particularly in low-data regimes. Standard augmentation, on the other hand, shows greater variability in threshold-dependent metrics, indicating sensitivity to decision boundaries under distributional mismatch. These findings suggest that the stability of representation – rather than absolute performance at a single operating point – is a key factor for reliable deployment in forensic settings.

The qualitative examples of changes in similarity scores provide additional insight into this behavior. For matching pairs, the similarity score increases progressively as more target-domain data are introduced, whereas for non-matching pairs, the score decreases toward lower values, indicating improved separation between classes (Fig. 8). This trend illustrates how few-shot adaptation refines decision boundaries in a manner consistent with forensic interpretation, where both increased similarity for true matches and decreased similarity for non-matches are essential for reliable comparison.

Examples of similarity scores changes under few-shot fine-tuning. The top row shows a matching pair of impressions, while the bottom row shows a non-matching pair. The blue dashed line denotes a naive decision threshold of 0.5.

To address domain shift between datasets, we employed a few-shot fine-tuning strategy using limited labeled data from the target domain. The results demonstrate that meaningful performance improvements can be achieved with a relatively small fraction of target-domain samples, indicating that effective adaptation need not rely on large-scale data collection. Importantly, all datasets were partitioned at the shoe level prior to pair construction, ensuring that improvements reflect genuine adaptation rather than memorization of specific shoe instances.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The datasets used in this study, while representative, do not fully capture the variability of real-world casework, including diverse substrates, partial impressions, and uncontrolled acquisition conditions. In addition, the framework focuses on discrimination performance and does not explicitly address interpretability or uncertainty quantification, both of which are critical for forensic reporting. Furthermore, the augmentation analysis is limited to specific configurations and does not exhaustively explore the full space of possible transformations or generative models. Additional experiments with different augmentation levels and configurations are provided in the Appendix, but a comprehensive investigation remains beyond the scope of this study.

More broadly, the principles demonstrated here – task-aligned transfer learning, morphology-preserving augmentation, and efficient few-shot adaptation – extend beyond forensic footwear analysis. Similar constraints arise in domains such as medical imaging, remote sensing, and industrial inspection, where labeled data are scarce, and acquisition conditions vary. In such settings, the central challenge is not only improving model performance but ensuring stable and reliable behavior under distributional shift. The structured perspective adopted in this study may therefore provide useful guidance for deployment in other data-limited environments.

Conclusion

This study examined how commonly used deep learning components can be organized and applied for forensic shoeprint matching under data-limited and domain-shifted conditions. Rather than introducing a new model, the focus was on understanding how different design choices influence performance and reliability in practical settings.

The findings suggest that reliable performance depends on a combination of factors, including the choice of pretrained representation, the design of augmentation strategies, and the availability of target-domain data. In particular, selecting representations aligned with task-specific structures, applying augmentations that preserve relevant characteristics, and incorporating even small amounts of target-domain data for adaptation all contribute to improved, more stable performance.

From a practical perspective, these results suggest that under data-limited forensic conditions, it is generally preferable to start with a domain-aligned pretrained model, apply structure-preserving augmentations rather than relying solely on geometric transformations, and incorporate even a small amount of target-domain data through fine-tuning whenever available.

These observations highlight that effective deployment in forensic shoeprint analysis is not determined by a single modeling choice, but by how multiple components are selected and combined under practical constraints. The perspective presented in this study provides guidance on applying deep learning methods in a structured, data-efficient manner, supporting more consistent and reproducible use within forensic workflows.

Future work may further investigate how different augmentation strategies can be combined and extended, as well as how interpretability, uncertainty quantification, and more diverse real-world datasets can be incorporated to better support operational forensic applications.

Consistent with the observations in the main text, generative augmentation demonstrates relatively stable performance across different augmentation levels, while standard augmentation exhibits greater variability, particularly at intermediate levels. These results further support the conclusion that augmentation strategies that preserve structural characteristics lead to more consistent performance under data constraints (Table 3).
