Evaluating high‐sensitivity troponin levels in people with sickle cell disease in the emergency department
Authors: Abdelrhman Mohammed, Najibah A Galadanci, Gerhard Hellemann, Julie Kanter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study shows that sickle cell disease patients have elevated high‐sensitivity cardiac troponin I (hs‐cTnI) that is not considered clinically significant but are outside of the normal range. Elevated hs‐cTnI was associated with low haemoglobin and a history of heart failure.Keywords: acute pain crises, high‐sensitivity troponin I, myocardial infarction type II, non‐ST elevation myocardial infarction, sickle cell disease

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is the most common inherited clinically significant blood disorder, affecting at least 100 000 Americans and millions around the world. Red blood cells (RBCs) with sickle haemoglobin (HbS) are prone to haemolysis and microvascular obstruction, resulting in anaemia and the potential for tissue ischaemia as well as multiorgan complications. 1 The most frequent acute complication of SCD is pain, classically termed ‘acute pain crisis’ (APC). Pain related to SCD is thought to be caused by vaso‐occlusion resulting from RBC aggregation and inflammation, which leads to tissue ischaemia, subsequent reperfusion and pain. 2 However, there are other potential causes of acute pain in people with SCD including neuropathic pain, exacerbations of chronic pain and pain caused by other aetiologies such as musculoskeletal injury or infection. Thus, each pain episode must be individually assessed for the potential of vaso‐occlusion, since it can lead to organ dysfunction, as well as for other non‐SCD causes of pain. 3

When people with SCD present to the emergency department (ED) with an APC, they often undergo a simultaneous evaluation for both SCD and non‐SCD aetiologies of the APC. In people with SCD presenting with chest pain, it is especially critical that the evaluation includes cardiac aetiologies of pain as well as SCD‐mediated vaso‐occlusive crisis (VOC) (e.g. acute chest syndrome, rib infarction, pulmonary embolism). As part of institutional protocol, all patients presenting with chest pain undergo evaluation for acute coronary syndrome, including electrocardiogram (ECG) and serial high‐sensitivity cardiac troponin (hs‐cTnI and hs‐cTnT) measurements. 4 , 5 hs‐cTnI and hs‐cTnT are regulatory proteins essential for cardiac muscle contraction that are released into the circulation following cardiac myocyte injury or death. These biomarkers have been validated as highly specific diagnostic markers for acute myocardial infarction (MI). Per definition, a cTnI concentration >99th percentile indicates myocardial injury. 6 , 7 As a result of the risk for MI, even young adults with SCD may undergo cardiac assessment including hs‐cTnI when presenting to the ED with chest pain (in addition to standard SCD evaluation). However, the use of hs‐cTnI to diagnose MI in people with SCD is challenging as it is unclear if there may be baseline abnormalities. 8

In this study, we investigated the clinical significance of hs‐cTnI in people with SCD presenting to the ED with APC, aiming to understand its role in assessing for cardiac injury in this population. We hypothesized that people with SCD would have elevated levels of hs‐cTnI without evidence of cardiac injury.

This is a single‐centre, Internal Review Board (IRB)‐approved retrospective cohort study of confirmed persons with SCD (all genotypes) who presented to the ED at our centre from 1 June 2021 to 31 December 2024. We only included adults with at least one hs‐cTnI measurement obtained during the ED visit. For patients with multiple ED visits during the study window, each visit was treated as an independent observation.

Data collection included demographic, clinical, laboratory, imaging and outcomes extracted from electronic health records. For this study, MI and type II non‐ST elevation MI (NSTEMI) were defined following standard criteria (2018). 9 Patients were categorized into three groups based on hs‐cTnI values: Group 1 (non‐detectable, hs‐cTnI), Group 2 (>1 ng/L but below the 99th percentile identified as non‐clinically significant) and Group 3 (>99th percentile by sex). 10 For individuals with serial hs‐cTnI assessments, only the first measurement was included.

Additional laboratory variables collected included haemoglobin (Hb), reticulocyte count and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). We also recorded SCD genotype, body mass index (BMI), history of heart failure (HF) and imaging results of chest x‐ray or Computed Tomography (CT) scan pulmonary angiography findings if available.

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median interquartile range (IQR) and compared using one‐way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) or Kruskal–Wallis tests, as appropriate. Categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages (n, %) and compared using chi‐square (χ 2) or Fisher's exact tests. A two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software, Version 9.4 of SAS for Windows.

The median age of the overall cohort was 34.7 years (IQR 16–84) and the cohort had a mean BMI of 26.6 ± 7.5 kg/m2. SCD genotype was available for 1011 persons and HbSS was the most common (58.5%). Observations were classified into three groups based on the initial hs‐cTnI levels. Group 1 (hs‐cTnI = 0, n = 276, 25.7%), Group 2 (hs‐cTnI >1 but <99th% n = 714, 66.6%) and Group 3 (hs‐cTnI for males >20 ng/L, females >15 ng/L; n = 85, 7.9%).

ECG were performed in all individuals per standard of care. There were non‐specific ST changes found in 44% of all visits with only 8 persons found to have ST elevation, all of whom had hs‐cTnI levels in Group 2. None of these individuals were diagnosed with MI (n = 8, 2%; p = 0.03). Classic MI was uncommon and was only diagnosed in one person in Group 3 (1%, p = 0.08).

CT pulmonary angiography was performed in 234 encounters due to concern for pulmonary embolus (PE). Segmental PE was identified in 22 encounters (9.4%), and there were no significant differences between the groups in the rate of CT pulmonary angiography or PE.

Within this cohort, 10% of persons (103 encounters) had a history of HF and these individuals were more likely to be in Group 3 (p < 0.0001). People with a history of HF were 3x more likely to have hs‐cTnI levels in Group 3 compared to Groups 2 and 1 (29.4% vs. 7.8% and 8.0%, respectively) as shown in Table 2.

Similarly, hypertension on arrival to the ED, defined as BP > 140/80 did influence hs‐cTnl. More people with hypertension were likely to have hs‐cTnl level in Group 3 compared to Groups 2 and 1 respectively (58.5% vs. 35.1% vs. 39.9%). The correlation seen between hypertension and troponin elevation is independent of the SCD genotype. Tachycardia (Heart Rate (HR) > 120) was not statistically significant.

Laboratory markers of Hb and haemolysis (LDH, reticulocyte count) were obtained per clinical care and evaluated. Lower Hb was significantly associated with higher hs‐cTnI (10.1 ± 1.8 g/dL in Group 1, 9.3 ± 1.8 g/dL in Group 2, and 9.2 ± 2.6 g/dL in Group 3, p < 0.01). Similarly, haemolysis markers (LDH, reticulocyte count) were also higher in patients in Group 2 and Group 3 as per Table S1. Of note, people with HbSS disease were more likely to have higher levels of hs‐cTnI, as shown in Table 1 consistent with the findings of lower Hb and higher haemolysis.

Our study found that people with SCD presenting with APC often had elevated, non‐clinically significant hs‐cTnI. Only one person had type I MI. Thus, most hs‐cTnI elevations appear to reflect chronic myocardial injury or supply–demand mismatch caused by the APC rather than obstructive coronary thrombosis. Notably, hs‐cTnI also correlated with worsened haemolytic anaemia (lower Hb, increased LDH) similar to other previous studies which also supports a hypothesis of chronic supply–demand mismatch. 11

According to the Definition of MI (2018), type 2 MI is caused by a mismatch between myocardial oxygen supply and demand. 9 Applied to SCD, the troponin increase seen here may occur without significant coronary vessel occlusion due to microscopic tissue hypoxia. 12 The results of this study suggest that hs‐cTnl is a measure of microscopic tissue injury unique to the pathophysiology of SCD, rather than primary atherothrombotic events as seen in the non‐SCD population. Similarly, other studies also demonstrated that troponin release in SCD may occur independently of classic electrocardiographic changes, possibly reflecting apoptotic rather than necrotic myocyte death. 13 This hypothesis is supported by the inverse relationship between Hb and troponin elevation as the increase may be due to global (vs. focal) myocardial hypoxia. People with SCD with HF were more likely to be in Groups 2 and 3 which also suggests that elevated hs‐cTnl may serve as an early biomarker of myocardial abnormalities; detectable before a change in ECG or echocardiogram is evident. A previous study showed that echocardiographic systolic function remained normal in SCD children with elevated troponin during VOC, this support biomarker elevation may precede detectable functional decline. 14 Also, our results align with other studies of persons without SCD with HF where elevated, non‐clinically significant hs‐cTnI levels are also observed. 15

This study has several limitations. Primarily, because the study was retrospective and single‐centre, the findings regarding causation are limited. Further, we treated each ED visit as an independent observation and that may overrepresent patients with frequent acute pain. However, the findings deserve a prospective study as hs‐cTnI elevation may serve as an important biomarker of acute organ injury during APC.

A prospective study of hs‐cTnI in people with SCD both at baseline and during APC is in development. Prospective longitudinal studies are needed to understand the use of troponin both to identify early cardiac injury and as a potential biomarker of APC severity.
