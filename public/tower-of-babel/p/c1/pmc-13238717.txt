Long‐Term Clinical and Radiologic Evaluation of Micronized Acellular Dermal Matrix‐Assisted Reconstruction in Diabetes‐Related and Traumatic Foot Wounds With Bone Defects
Authors: Ha Jong Nam, Jino Bae, Se Young Kim, Hwan Jun Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTo investigate whether micronized acellular dermal matrix (mADM) can be used to treat diabetes‐related and traumatic foot wounds with bone defects after bone or joint resection. We retrospectively reviewed 52 patients who underwent bone or joint resection, followed by mADM‐assisted reconstruction of diabetes‐related or traumatic foot wounds between 2021 and 2024. mADM was applied using sheet‐type, paste‐type or combined formulations in one‐ or multi‐stage procedures. Wound healing, contour preservation and radiological alignment were assessed. Among the 52 patients (43 with diabetes and 9 with trauma), complete epithelialization was achieved in 48 (mean follow‐up: 14.8 months; mean time to wound closure: 5.6 weeks). Four cases showed recurrence or delayed healing and five developed mild early local infection; all were controlled with additional wound care or antibiotics, with no progressive osteomyelitis or graft loss. In 40 radiologically evaluable cases, the toe length and alignment were generally preserved (mean toe length ratio: 91.6% and no angular deformity > 10°). mADM‐assisted reconstruction may be useful for treating diabetes‐related and traumatic foot wounds with bone defects after bone or joint resection. Its role appears to lie in dead‐space management, contour preservation and durable wound coverage, rather than bone replacement.Keywords: acellular dermal matrix, bone defects, diabetes‐related foot, tissue regeneration

Introduction

Bone and soft‐tissue defects in diabetes‐related or traumatic foot wounds remain major reconstructive challenges, particularly when infection control requires bone or joint resection. Although surgical debridement is often necessary to eradicate infected or non‐viable tissue, these procedures frequently leave residual dead space and compromise the surrounding soft tissue envelope [1, 2, 3, 4]. Inadequate management of post‐resection dead space may contribute to delayed wound healing, contour deformities and recurrent wound breakdown. Despite their different etiologies, diabetes‐related and traumatic foot wounds with bone defects share a common reconstructive problem after bone or joint resection: persistent dead space and loss of soft tissue support [3, 4].

Conventional reconstructive options, including flap coverage and local dead‐space fillers, may be useful in selected cases but are not always feasible in high‐risk patients [5, 6, 7]. In patients with diabetes, poor vascularity, neuropathy, multiple comorbidities and a high risk of infection often limit the use of extensive reconstructive procedures [6, 8]. In addition, secondary healing after bone excision or partial forefoot amputation may lead to altered residual foot contours and recurrent ulceration, predisposing the wound to repeated breakdown [8]. Therefore, a reconstructive strategy that can fill the residual dead space, support wound stability and preserve digital contours with minimal additional surgical burden remains clinically relevant [5, 7].

Micronized acellular dermal matrix (mADM) is a human dermis‐derived collagen matrix that primarily functions as a biological scaffold and soft‐tissue filler in complex wounds [9, 10]. Because mADM can be used to fill residual soft tissue defects, including tunnelling or irregular wound cavities, it may be useful after phalangeal or metatarsal debridement or resection, where dead space and soft tissue volume loss are common [11, 12]. Rather than serving as a bone substitute, mADM appears to support wound coverage and soft tissue stability during healing [9, 11, 12, 13].

Previous studies have reported favourable short‐term outcomes of acellular dermal matrices in patients with chronic and diabetes‐related wounds [12, 14]. However, data on the long‐term clinical, radiological and morphological performance of foot wounds with bone defects are limited [11]. In the present study, we aimed to evaluate the long‐term clinical and radiologic outcomes of mADM‐assisted reconstruction in diabetes‐related and traumatic foot wounds with bone defects. Because the clinical goal of this reconstruction was not bone regeneration but durable wound coverage and maintenance of the post‐resection soft‐tissue envelope, radiological analysis was incorporated to assess post‐resection structural stability, toe length preservation, alignment and soft‐tissue collapse over time.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patients

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a single institution and was approved for exemption by its institutional review board. We reviewed consecutive patients treated between January 2021 and December 2024 for diabetes‐related or traumatic foot wounds that required bone or joint resection followed by mADM‐assisted reconstruction. Eligible patients had [1] a post‐debridement or post‐excisional bone defect, [2] reconstruction performed using mADM (CGDerm matrix, CGPaste or CGReallo‐Paste; CGBio, Seoul, Korea) and [3] a minimum clinical follow‐up of 6 months. Patients were excluded if they had an active uncontrolled infection or residual uncontrolled osteomyelitis at the time of definitive reconstruction. However, a history of osteomyelitis itself was not an exclusion criterion if infected bone had been adequately resected, the wound bed was clinically controlled and further reconstruction was judged feasible. Patients were also excluded if they had critical ischemia that was not amenable to revascularization (ankle‐brachial index < 0.4 or TcPO2 < 20 mmHg), or insufficient clinical records for outcome assessment. Data on demographic characteristics, comorbidities, wound aetiology, defect location, reconstruction method and follow‐up duration were collected retrospectively. Cases lacking adequate paired radiographs or standardized serial photographs were retained in the clinical cohort but were excluded from the corresponding morphometric sub‐analyses. The detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are summarized in Table 1. Individual patient consent was waived owing to the retrospective design and IRB‐granted exemption; written informed consent for the use of clinical photographs was obtained from all patients whose images are presented.

Surgical Technique

All procedures were performed by a single reconstructive surgeon under general anaesthesia or regional anaesthesia, including spinal anaesthesia or peripheral nerve block, depending on the patient's condition, operative extent and anaesthesiologist's decision. After serial or definitive debridement and, when indicated, bone or joint excision, the wound bed was irrigated and prepared until all grossly non‐viable tissues had been removed. Initial wound management and deep bone defect reconstruction were primarily performed in the operating room, whereas limited dressing changes and follow‐up care were conducted in the ward or clinic setting. mADM was applied as sheet‐type (CGDerm matrix), paste‐type (CGPaste or CGReallo‐Paste) or combined sheet‐and‐paste reconstruction. Specifically, sheet‐type mADM was used when the exposed cortical bone was wide or surface padding was required. Paste‐type mADM was indicated for filling narrow cavities, tunnels, undermined spaces or irregular dead spaces. The combined method was employed when both an exposed bone surface and an irregular residual cavity were present. In these combined cases, a dual‐layer technique was used, either by filling the cavity with paste‐type mADM and covering the surface with sheet‐type mADM or by placing the sheet‐type mADM directly over the exposed cortical bone and reinforcing the surrounding dead space with paste‐type mADM.

The material was placed directly over the exposed bone or packed into the post‐resection cavity, and additional layers were added as needed to obliterate the residual dead space and restore the soft tissue contour (Figure 1). In cases suitable for definitive reconstruction after the initial debridement, mADM was applied in a single‐stage procedure. When residual contamination, questionable perfusion or insufficient granulation precluded immediate definitive reconstruction, a multistage approach was used with initial space maintenance, followed by interval reapplication after wound bed optimization. When adequate infection control and perfusion were achieved, surface coverage was performed based on the following criteria: primary closure was selected when tension‐free approximation was possible and infection and perfusion were stable; skin grafting was performed when the granulation bed was sufficient and surface coverage was required after the exposed bone or tendon was stabilized by the matrix; local flap coverage was used when adjacent soft tissue was adequate and durable coverage was needed and secondary intention healing was chosen when the defect was small, closure tension was anticipated or reducing the surgical burden of additional flap or graft procedures was advantageous due to high‐risk comorbidities. Negative pressure wound therapy was selectively administered at the surgeon's discretion during the early postoperative period to assist with drainage control and matrix adherence.

Adjunctive Evaluation and Diagnostic Modalities

Preoperative perfusion status was assessed using the ankle‐brachial index and transcutaneous oxygen pressure (TcPO2; TCM400, Radiometer, Denmark). Toe pressure was not routinely available in this retrospective cohort; therefore, perfusion was primarily assessed using ABI and TcPO2, which were consistently available in our institution. When additional assessment of local perfusion or wound‐bed viability was needed, adjunctive imaging modalities including near‐infrared fluorescence imaging (Fluobeam, Fluoptics, France) and thermal imaging with a forward‐looking infrared camera (FLIR, Teledyne Technologies, USA) were selectively used (Figure 2). Microbiological cultures were obtained during the initial debridement and when clinically indicated to confirm infection control before definitive mADM implantation. These assessments were used to support perioperative decision‐making, including the timing of reconstruction and wound‐bed optimization.

Radiologic and Morphometric Analysis

Standardized dorsoplantar and lateral radiographs were reviewed immediately after surgery, at early follow‐up (2–6 weeks) and at the final follow‐up. When available beyond 12 months, these were considered as long‐term radiographic assessments. All images were calibrated using a picture archiving and communication system software to allow pixel‐to‐millimetre conversion. Quantitative radiological analyses were performed only in patients with adequately paired radiographs for comparison with the contralateral side. The measured parameters included defect length, residual bone length, toe length ratio, alignment angle and soft‐tissue envelope thickness. The toe length ratio was defined as the ratio of the reconstructed toe length to the contralateral normal side.

The soft tissue envelope thickness was measured on lateral radiographs as the shortest skin‐to‐cortex distance in the reconstructed segment. Soft‐tissue atrophy was categorized using a study‐defined four‐grade scale according to the envelope thickness ratio between the affected and contralateral sides: Grade 0, ≥ 90%; Grade 1, 75%–89%; Grade 2, 50%–74% and Grade 3, < 50%. This grading system is not a validated external scale but a semi‐quantitative measurement defined for this study to assess post‐resection collapse based on contralateral ratios. Radiological alignment was assessed by measuring the angular deviation between the proximal and distal phalangeal axes. Bone continuity was evaluated on serial radiographs by preserving the cortical continuity and the absence of progressive bone resorption. All measurements were performed independently by two plastic surgeons who were blinded to the clinical outcomes, and discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Representative measurement methods and radiographic landmarks are shown in Figure 3. In addition to radiological evaluation, gross photographic morphometry was performed only in patients with standardized serial clinical photographs to estimate changes in soft tissue contour and volume. The length, width and height were measured using a surgical ruler, and the estimated volume was calculated using the formula V = L × W × H × k, where k = 0.52 represents an ellipsoidal correction factor (Figure 4). This supplementary analysis was used to support radiologic atrophy grading by providing a gross semi‐quantitative assessment of contour and volume changes over time.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcomes were complete epithelialization with durable wound coverage, preservation of toe length and alignment and the radiological soft tissue atrophy grade at the final follow‐up. Secondary outcomes included the time to wound closure, recurrence or delayed healing, early local infection, readmission or additional surgical intervention, graft loss, pain, gait disturbance and functional limitations. Time to wound closure was defined as the interval from definitive mADM‐assisted reconstruction to complete epithelialization. It did not include the preceding period of drainage, debridement, infection control, vascular optimization or wound‐bed preparation. Quantitative radiologic parameters, including toe length ratio, alignment angle and soft‐tissue envelope thickness, were evaluated in patients with adequately paired radiographs. In patients with standardized serial clinical photographs, gross photographic morphometry was performed to supplement the radiologic assessment of contour changes and estimated soft tissue volume loss.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation unless otherwise indicated, and categorical variables are expressed as numbers (percentages). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons between the one‐stage and multistage reconstruction groups, continuous variables were analysed using the independent t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate and categorical variables were analysed using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test. For paired quantitative comparisons, including affected versus contralateral radiographic measurements and immediate postoperative versus follow‐up photographic measurements, the paired t‐test or Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was used, as appropriate. Correlations between the radiologic atrophy grade and photographic parameters, including the estimated volume reduction and surgeon's visual grade, were assessed using Spearman's rank correlation analysis. Statistical significance was defined as a two‐sided p‐value < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Patient Demographics and Clinical Characteristics

A total of 52 patients, comprising 43 patients with diabetes‐related foot ulcers and 9 patients with traumatic foot wounds, were included in this study. The mean age was 62.4 ± 10.7 years, and 37 patients (71.2%) were male. The mean follow‐up duration was 14.8 ± 5.1 months. The reconstruction sites included the distal interphalangeal joint in 15 cases (28.8%), proximal interphalangeal joint in 18 (34.6%), metatarsophalangeal joint in 12 (23.1%) and metatarsal region in 7 (13.5%). This cohort represented a medically complex population. Hypertension was present in 31 patients (59.6%), peripheral arterial disease in 22 (42.3%) and renal disease in 14 (26.9%). Chronic kidney disease without end‐stage renal disease was present in 6 patients (11.5%), and end‐stage renal disease requiring haemodialysis was present in 8 patients (15.4%). Among patients with diabetes‐related foot ulcers, the mean HbA1c was 8.3% ± 0.7% and the median HbA1c was 8.3% (IQR, 7.9–8.7). Thirteen patients (25.0%) had a current or former smoking history. Pre‐reconstruction vascular optimization, including angioplasty or bypass, when indicated, was performed in 15 patients (28.8%), predominantly in patients with diabetes‐related foot ulcers. The mean ankle‐brachial index was 0.83 ± 0.13, and the mean transcutaneous oxygen pressure was 31.1 ± 8.6 mmHg. The mADM categories were mutually exclusive. Sheet‐type only mADM was used in 27 cases (51.9%), paste‐type only mADM in 18 cases (34.6%) and combined sheet‐and‐paste reconstruction in 7 cases (13.5%). All wounds were free of uncontrolled active infection at the time of definitive reconstruction, and no patient required additional vascular intervention during follow‐up. Surgical and Reconstructive Characteristics

Reconstruction was performed in a one‐stage setting in 38 patients (73.1%) and in a multistage setting in 14 patients (26.9%), according to the wound bed condition, residual contamination and local perfusion status. Bone resection was the most common bony procedure, performed in 36 cases (69.2%), followed by minor amputation in 10 cases (19.2%) and joint disarticulation in 6 cases (11.5%). Additional revision debridement before definitive reconstruction was required in 7 cases (13.5%). Primary closure was used for definitive surface coverage in 19 cases (36.5%), local flap coverage in 14 (26.9%), split‐thickness skin grafts in 9 (17.3%), secondary intention healing in 8 (15.4%) and full‐thickness skin grafts in 2 (3.8%). Negative‐pressure wound therapy was selectively administered to 17 patients (32.7%). Detailed surgical and reconstructive characteristics are summarized in Table 3.

Clinical Outcomes

Complete epithelialization was achieved in 48 of the 52 cases (92.3%), with a mean time to wound closure of 5.6 ± 1.9 weeks. Four patients (7.7%) showed recurrence or delayed healing; two required short‐term readmission for additional wound management, whereas two were managed with outpatient wound care alone. Mild early local infection occurred in 5 cases (9.6%) and resolved with short‐term antibiotic therapy and local wound care. No matrix‐related local complications, including allergic reaction, matrix‐related pain, seroma, hematoma, peri‐wound erythema, maceration or matrix extrusion or loss, were observed. No patient required matrix removal due to matrix‐related complications. No progressive osteomyelitis or graft loss was observed after the reconstruction. At the final follow‐up, no patients reported pain, gait disturbances or ambulation limitations. No significant differences were observed between the one‐stage and multistage groups in terms of complete epithelialization, time to closure, recurrence or delayed healing or early local infection. Radiologic Outcomes

Radiological evaluation was available for 40 of the 52 patients (76.9%) with adequate paired radiographs for comparison with the contralateral side. Twelve patients were excluded from the radiologic analysis because of amputation, loss of the corresponding toe or the absence of a comparable reference view. At the final follow‐up, toe length and alignment were preserved in the radiologically evaluable cohort. The mean toe length ratio relative to the contralateral side was 91.6% ± 5.8%. The mean alignment angle was 6.1° ± 2.3° on the affected side and 5.8° ± 1.9° on the contralateral side, with no significant difference between sides (p = 0.32). No angular deformity of > 10° was observed. Soft‐tissue envelope thickness was reduced on the affected side compared with the contralateral side (3.2 ± 0.6 mm vs. 3.7 ± 0.5 mm, p = 0.04). According to the radiologic atrophy grading system (Grade 0: ≥ 90% soft‐tissue envelope thickness preservation, Grade 1: 75%–89%, Grade 2: 50%–74%, Grade 3: < 50%), Grade 0 was noted in 10 cases (25.0%), Grade 1 in 14 (35.0%), Grade 2 in 12 (30.0%) and Grade 3 in 4 (10.0%), indicating that most cases showed mild‐to‐moderate atrophy at the final follow‐up. Serial radiographs demonstrated preserved cortical continuity without progressive bone resorption in all the evaluable cases. Gross Photographic Volume Analysis

Standardized serial clinical photographs were available for 35 of the 52 patients (67.3%) and were used for gross photographic morphometric analysis of soft tissue contours and estimated volume change. The length, width and height were measured from serial images using a surgical ruler, and the estimated volume (cm3) was calculated as V = L × W × H × k, where k = 0.52 was used as the ellipsoidal correction factor.

The length, width, height and estimated volume were significantly reduced in the immediate postoperative and final follow‐up photographs. The mean estimated soft‐tissue volume decreased from 6.7 ± 2.1 cm3 immediately after reconstruction to 4.2 ± 1.8 cm3 at final follow‐up, corresponding to a mean relative volume reduction of 37.3% ± 9.4%. Based on the surgeon's visual grading on a 0–5 scale, the median atrophy grade was 2 (IQR, 1–3).

The radiologic atrophy grade was significantly and positively correlated with both the photographic volume reduction ratio (ρ = 0.68, p < 0.001) and the surgeon's visual grade (ρ = 0.72, p < 0.001). Detailed gross photographic morphometric data are summarized in Table 6.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort, complete epithelialization was achieved in 48 of the 52 wounds (92.3%), with a mean time to wound closure of 5.6 weeks. Recurrence or delayed healing occurred in 4 cases (7.7%), and mild early local infection occurred in 5 cases (9.6%); all were managed with additional wound care or short‐term antibiotics, and no progressive osteomyelitis or graft loss was observed. These findings suggest that mADM‐assisted reconstruction may provide durable wound coverage in select diabetes‐related and traumatic foot wounds with bone defects after bone or joint resection [11, 12, 14, 15].

From a clinical perspective, mADM is a human dermis‐derived collagen matrix that primarily functions as a biological spacer, dead‐space filler and soft‐tissue scaffold [9, 10, 13]. Previous studies have reported the usefulness of acellular or micronized dermal matrices in chronic wounds and diabetes‐related foot wounds, including defects with deep exposure, tunnelling or cavity formation [11, 12, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. Our findings are also consistent with recent clinical experience using injectable micronized putty‐type human‐derived acellular dermal matrix for diabetes‐related foot wounds [21]. The availability of both sheet and paste‐type formulations allows adaptation to different defect configurations. Sheet‐type mADM was used in our cohort when broader surface coverage or padding over exposed bone was required, whereas previous reports support the use of paste‐type or flowable matrices for narrow, tunnelled or cavity wounds [16, 17, 18, 19]. In selected cases, the combined or staged use of these configurations appeared to be a feasible strategy to manage both residual dead space and surface continuity. Rather than serving as a bone substitute, mADM may provide temporary volume support while acting as a scaffold‐like matrix for soft‐tissue stabilization [9, 10, 13, 18, 20].

Quantitative radiological evaluation showed that toe length and alignment were generally preserved after mADM‐assisted reconstruction. Importantly, radiologic analysis was not performed to prove bone regeneration but rather to evaluate post‐resection structural stability, including toe length preservation, alignment and soft‐tissue envelope thickness. Because clinical photographs alone cannot objectively assess internal collapse or bone alignment, serial radiographs were used as an adjunctive morphometric tool. Although partial soft tissue atrophy was observed over time, most cases showed mild‐to‐moderate changes and overall wound stability and contours were maintained.

Furthermore, although minor digit preservation, such as reconstruction of the second to fourth toes, may not aim for major gait improvement, reconstruction in these areas may help stabilize the local soft‐tissue envelope, obliterate residual dead space and reduce the risk of recurrent ulceration or contour collapse. Partial foot defects and recurrent ulceration after forefoot procedures remain clinically relevant because local contour, pressure distribution and shoe‐wear tolerance can influence long‐term wound stability [22, 23]. In this context, preserving adequate local padding may be meaningful in selected high‐risk patients with multiple comorbidities.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective, single‐center study without a control group. Second, mADM was evaluated as part of an overall reconstructive strategy rather than as an isolated intervention. Third, toe pressure was not routinely available in this cohort, which may limit the assessment of perfusion in patients with calcified vessels [24]. Fourth, the radiologic atrophy grading was a study‐defined system lacking external validation. Finally, functional outcomes were based on clinical follow‐up rather than standardized patient‐reported outcome measures or gait‐related biomechanical assessments. Future studies incorporating standardized imaging modalities, such as ultrasound‐based assessment of plantar soft tissue or fat‐pad thickness, may help refine the evaluation of soft‐tissue atrophy after reconstruction [25, 26].

Conclusion

mADM‐based reconstruction may be a useful adjunctive option for treating diabetes‐related and traumatic foot wounds with bone defects after bone or joint resection. In this study, mADM appeared to function primarily as a biological spacer and soft tissue scaffold, supporting dead space management, contour preservation and durable wound coverage. Although partial atrophy was observed during follow‐up, the overall wound stability was maintained in most cases. Further prospective comparative studies are required to clarify its role in reconstructive strategies for high‐risk foot wounds.
