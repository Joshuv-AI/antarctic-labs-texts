Immunological Biomarkers in Glioblastoma: Targeting T and NK Cells for Enhanced Diagnosis and Prognosis
Authors: Amir Mohammad Akbari, Shahriyar Bourbour, Maryam Sadat Seyedmehdi, Sara Shiari, Auob Rustamzadeh, Farhad Seif, Shirin Farivar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlioblastoma (GBM) is an aggressive brain tumor with a poor prognosis, even with many therapeutic interventions, including surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. Recent immunotherapies have broaden horizons to the utilization of the immune system to eradicate GBM. However, the clinical efficacy of such approaches is limited, owing to the tumor’s multifaceted immunosuppressive microenvironment and the blood-brain barrier (BBB) structure. The dysfunction of immune cells, especially T cells and natural killer (NK) cells, is significantly influenced by the immunosuppressive mechanisms in GBM. Novel therapeutic strategies are needed to overcome these mechanisms. In the current review, we explore the main functions of T cells, especially cytotoxic CD4+ and helper CD8+ T cells, alongside NK cells, in response to GBM. We further discuss the mechanisms contributing to their dysfunction, focusing on T cell exhaustion, altered receptor expression, and metabolic reprogramming within the GBM microenvironment to establish diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers. Moreover, we highlight recent advances in immunotherapeutic approaches, including immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI), miRNA therapy, oncolytic viruses, and Chimeric Antigen Receptor (CAR) T/NK cell therapy to provide new opportunities for enhancing GBM treatment and biomarker-guided precision medicine.Keywords: glioblastoma, tumor microenvironment, immunotherapy, oncolytic viruses, immune checkpoint inhibitors, miRNA Therapy

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most malignant and lethal solid brain tumor, characterized by uncontrolled growth, high heterogeneity, rapid angiogenesis, and invasiveness, generating an immunosuppressive microenvironment.1 Despite conventional treatments, patients’ survival is low (approximately 14–18 months), reflecting the need for novel therapies.2 The immunosuppressive mechanisms involved in GBM origins from genetic and epigenetic modifications, immune system interactions, especially T cells and natural killer (NK) cells, and diverse microenvironmental components. This review discusses immune functions and dysfunctions, tumor evasion mechanisms, and recent immunotherapies, focusing on T and NK cells in GBM.

Lymphocytes

Lymphocytes, key players of adaptive immune system, include B and T cells. B cells mature in bone marrow, express CD19/CD20, and produce antibodies. They also present antigens to helper T cells and generate either memory B cells or plasma cells. On the other hand, T cells mature in the thymus, express CD3 with CD4 or CD8, and include helper (Th), cytotoxic (TCTL), and regulatory (Treg) cells, which coordinate immune responses and maintain tolerance.3 Some T cells become memory cells. In GBM, adaptive immunity is dysregulated because tumor cells implement immunosuppressive pathways to evade. Here, we first provide a brief overview of the role of each T cells and NK cells in defending against GBM cells; then we evaluate the cellular and molecular aspects of their dysfunction.

CD4+ and CD8+ T Cells

CD4+ Th cells recognize antigens presented by major histocompatibility complex II (MHC-II) molecules and proliferate into effector and memory T cells.4 Th1 cells are primarily involved in promoting cell-mediated immunity, particularly against intracellular pathogens, by producing cytokines such as interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and interleukin-12 (IL-12). Th2 cells stimulate antibody production and are essential for combating extracellular parasites, secreting cytokines such as IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13. Th17 cells are involved in neutrophil-riched anti-fungal responses and are characterized by their production of IL-17, IL-21, and IL-22, which promote intestinal integrity. Treg cells are vital for maintaining immune tolerance and preventing autoimmune responses by suppressing excessive immune activity. Tumors use the immunosuppressive role of CD4+CD25+FOXP3+ Treg cells to evade immune responses.5 However, the exact mechanisms have not been fully understood, with current studies only establishing correlations between Treg levels and tumor activity.6 In GBM, immune homeostasis is disrupted. Within the GBM microenvironment, the dysfunction of Th cells and the imbalance in Th1:Th2:Treg are significant contributors to GBM progression. The presence of immunosuppressive molecules is a crucial factor causing Th cell dysfunction in the GBM microenvironment.7 CD8+ cytotoxic T cells (TCTL) eliminate intracellular antigens like tumor cells and virus-infected cells via MHC-I.8 Activated CD8+ T cells then clonally expand, migrate to the GBM site, recognize tumor cells, secrete cytokines, and initiate cytotoxicity.9 However, GBM evades CD8+ T cells through strategies, including MHC-I downregulation and inhibitory cytokines and ligands.

Natural Killer Cells

Natural killer (NK) cells, constituting over 10–15% of lymphocytes, belong to innate lymphoid cells (ILCs). They rapidly respond to tumor cells and virus-infected cells in which MHC-I is downregulated or missed.10,11 Their function is regulated by counterbalance of activatory and inhibitory receptors. Inhibitory receptors bind MHC-I molecules, whereas activating receptors recognize ligands on stressed cells. Optimal NK activity requires stimulation by type I IFNs or other cytokines such as IL-15, IL-12, and IL-18.11 In one study, by testing various cytokine combinations, researchers identified IL-12, IL-18, and IL-15 as the ideal combination to generate NK cells with high purity and cytotoxicity.12 GBM employs evasion strategies to suppress NK cells. The tumor secretes immunosuppressive cytokines such as TGF-β and IL-10, which dampen NK cell activity.13,14 Despite this suppression, the presence of NK cells in the tumor site correlates with a better prognosis, highlighting their role in immune surveillance and potential for immunotherapy.15 One study in 2022 focused on NK cell hypoactivity. This study suggested that overexpression of inhibitory Killer-cell Immunoglobulin-like Receptors (KIRs) might be one of the reasons for low NK cell cytotoxicity. IL-2 treatment of NK cells after the blockade of these receptors significantly augmented NK cell activity and GBM cell killing.16 Some tumor cells can cause changes in NK cell function through various regulatory mechanisms that prevent them from performing their normal immunosurveillance function, resulting in tumor cells’ immune escape.17 A recent work by Li et al demonstrated that CD276 in gliomas is involved in a poor prognosis.18 In this regard, Lee et al identified that CD276 expression is positively associated with poor patient survival, and it is recognized as a risk gene in cancers. Higher CD276 levels showed significant correlations with NK cell infiltration and attenuated NK cell cytotoxicity toward cancer cells.19 Another study found that CHMP2A, a protein involved in vesicle trafficking, reduces tumor cell sensitivity to NK cell-mediated killing when overexpressed. Lowering CHMP2A increased susceptibility to NK cell cytotoxicity, revealing a link between intracellular trafficking and immune evasion.20

Novel Therapies Targeting T and NK Cells

Immune Checkpoints

T cell exhaustion, a state of diminished effector function due to chronic antigen exposure, is a key contributor to immunosuppression in GBM.21 Exhausted T cells express immune checkpoint receptors such as T lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4), programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), and T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3 (TIM-3), and lose proliferative capacity (Figure 1).22 In GBM, tumor cells and others express PD-L1, which binds PD-1 to impair T cells and enable immune evasion. This PD-L1 expression is regulated by hypoxia and oncogenic mutations.23,24 CTLA-4, expressed on exhausted T cells, competes with CD28 for B7 ligands (CD80/CD86), diminishing T cell activation during priming in lymphoid organs.25 TIGIT expression on CD8+ T cells competitively binds CD155, displacing the stimulatory DNAX accessory molecule-1 (DNAM-1, CD226) and promoting exhaustion, which also reduces NK cell cytotoxicity.26 LAG3 binds MHC-II with high affinity and is expressed on activated/exhausted T and Treg cells. It inhibits TCR signaling by disrupting the association of CD4/CD8 with the kinase Lck.27 TIM-3 is highly expressed on terminally exhausted T cells, often alongside PD-1. Dual blockade of PD-1 and TIM-3 shows synergistic therapeutic potential.28 Consequently, TIM-3 seems to have a more multifaceted influence on T cell regulation, functioning in both inhibitory and stimulatory roles, contingent upon the context. Recent studies highlight CD39-expressing T cells, which are scarce in the GBM tumor microenvironment (TME). This paucity, along with reduced inflammatory signals, limits T cell infiltration.29 Another study found T lymphocytes induce GBM cells to secrete galectin-9, which is elevated 2 to 3-fold in tumors and cooperates with checkpoint proteins to reinforce immunosuppression.30 Altogether, a comprehensive understanding of T cell exhaustion mechanisms specific to GBM is essential to develop effective immunotherapies for GBM.

Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors

Immune checkpoint therapies have become a promising approach for treating GBM (Figure 2). These blockers target immune checkpoint proteins, which tumors use to evade immune detection. Blocking these checkpoints aims to reactivate the immune system’s capacity to identify and destroy cancer cells. Despite their limited efficacy and ongoing investigation into resistance mechanisms, recent research has focused on using immune checkpoint blockers to improve the survival rates of GBM patients.31,32 In this regard, de Dios et al found that tumor-intrinsic NKG2C expression correlated with improved responses to PD-1 blockade and may serve as a predictive biomarker.33 Moreover, Fukuta et al developed neutrophil “backpack” micro-patches combined with PD-1 blockade, which cured orthotopic GBM in mice by amplifying systemic and local immunity.34 Also, Sprooten et al reported that DC vaccination alone expanded PD-L1+ macrophages in GBM, limiting efficacy. Adding PD-L1 blockade depleted these cells and revived T-cell responses, leading to tumor regression.35 In the same year, Kim et al created PD-L1-blocking ferritin nanocages that crossed the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and suppressed GBM growth in mice. This delivery system restored T cell activity and addresses key hurdles in checkpoint therapy.36 Another study used a redox-sensitive micelle co-encapsulating immune checkpoint inhibitor αPD-L1 and PTX. This micelle penetrated the BBB, remained in the reductive TME, suppressed the primary and recurrent GBM, and promoted the accumulation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes with long-lasting immunological memory.37 Finally, in 2025, Senent et al combined epigenetic inhibitors with a BCLXL inhibitor and PD-1 blockade, which significantly reduced tumor growth and enhanced CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration in GBM models.38 Moreover, Nguyen et al demonstrated that AURKA inhibition downregulated PD-L1 and sensitized GBM cells to NK- and T-cell activity, prolonging survival when combined with PD-1 therapy.39

Innovative Combination Therapies with ICIs

Combinational therapy has been investigated as a promising method in recent years. In 2022, Bausart et al prepared a plasmid DNA vaccine, pTOP, encoding VSV-G and tumor epitopes. After the insertion of one CD8 epitope, the vaccine induces an antigen-specific IFN-γ response against the GBM antigen TRP2. The combination of pTOP with anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 in vivo increased effector T cells, but survival rates were not different in this study.40 Another study used CXCL10 and Nrf2-overexpressing MSCs in combination with ICIs. This potentiated the recruitment of T cells, tolerance to oxidative stress, and iron accumulation, significantly inhibiting tumor growth and prolonging survival.41 Moreover, a combination of anti–PD–1 with engineered extended half-life IL-2, termed ECB, impressively improved survival and robust immunologic responses in glioma models.42 In 2024, Liu et al activated CXCR7 to reverse CXCL12-driven macrophage polarization and PD-L1 expression, restoring CD8⁺ T cell function and improving survival with PD-L1 blockade.43 These findings contribute to the continuing search and development toward the employment of ICIs in the treatment of GBM. While single-agent efficacy remains limited, combining ICIs with therapies that reprogram the TME, stimulate innate immunity, or enhance antigen presentation offers a multi-pronged strategy to overcome resistance and improve clinical outcomes in GBM.

Some studies have the potential to be combined with ICIs. For instance, the neoantigen-reactive T cells, including AAco-AlbiVax vaccine-induced neoantigen-specific CD8+ T cell responses, improved therapeutic efficacy.44 Moreover, Jones et al demonstrated that temozolomide (TMZ) -targets fast-dividing cells- plus the PARP inhibitor, niraparib, increased NKG2D ligand expression and enhanced γδ T cell cytotoxicity.45 Another approach involved developing an in situ hydrogel system co-encapsulating anti-CD47 antibody with TMZ. The anti-CD47 antibody augments immune recognition of cancer cells and activating immune cells such as NK and T cells against them. The localized delivery of these two agents with hydrogel significantly reduced recurrence and increased survival rates compared to classic treatments, underscoring the importance of this system in improving survival rates.46 Moreover, Hou et al developed a B cell-based vaccine (BVax) using activated 4–1BBL+ B cells stimulated with CD40 agonist, IFN-γ, and BAFF. BVax demonstrated superior antigen cross-presentation, outperformed dendritic cells in stimulating CD8+ T cells, and activated stem-like memory T cells with high anti-tumor potential.47 In 2024, Ridolfi et al integrated dendritic cell vaccination into standard chemoradiotherapy for resected GBM in a Phase II trial. The vaccine was well tolerated, immune-activating, and met early efficacy endpoints.48 In another study, an attempt was made to take advantage of one of the peculiarities of GBM—abnormal vascularization—utilizing an agonistic anti-Tie2 antibody. Targeting Tie2, a receptor implicated in angiogenesis, resulted in decreased density and morphological abnormalities of the blood vessels within the tumor and enhanced infiltration of NK cells, T cells, and macrophages, representing a way to interfere with vascular support of GBM and improve immune-mediated elimination of tumors.49 Additionally, Li et al developed a magnesium–phenolic nanoeditor encapsulating the BACE1 inhibitor, MK-8931, which reprogrammed M2 macrophages and enhanced TCTL responses, leading to improved survival in preclinical models.50

Epigenetics

Epigenetic changes in GBM, including DNA methylation and histone modifications, influence immune responsiveness and exacerbate immune escape.13 Qiu et al demonstrated that the YY1-CDK9 transcription complex increases m6A modification, leading to downregulation of MHC and interferon-related genes and promoting Treg infiltration, suggesting it as a novel therapeutic target.51 Moreover, targeting the YY1-CDK9 complex may be a novel approach to modulating immune responses by altering the balance between effector and Treg cells in GBM. Wu et al found the lncRNA MIR155HG is highly expressed in GBM due to promoter hypomethylation and transcription factor SP1 binding. Its expression correlates with immune cell infiltration and checkpoint expression, positioning it as a potential prognostic biomarker and immunotherapeutic target.52 This highlights the importance of epigenetic regulators like MIR155HG in shaping the TME and suggests that modulating its expression could enhance GBM immunotherapy outcomes.

miRNAs

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are short non-coding RNAs that post-transcriptionally regulate gene expression, and their dysregulation is linked to tumorigenesis and immune escape.53,54 It has been demonstrated that the dysregulation in the expression of miRNAs is associated with tumorigenesis, including aspects such as invasiveness, DNA repair, and acquired resistance, among others.55 Lee et al showed that miR-29c-3p directly targets CD276. A Myc-inhibited miR-29c-3p signaling loop regulates CD276, which suppresses NK cell cytotoxicity, revealing a critical mechanism for immune evasion.19 The processes by which miRNAs are released from GBM cells and how they cross the BBB to enter the systemic circulation still need to be fully comprehended. Studies indicate that more than 90% of miRNAs are found extracellularly, coupled with the Ago2 protein, protecting them from degradation.56,57 Interestingly, these circulating miRNAs could serve as non-invasive biomarkers for GBM diagnosis and prognosis, offering a potential avenue for early detection and monitoring treatment efficacy. miRNAs can also be transported via extracellular vesicles (EVs), which protect their contents and can influence distant immune cells, contributing to systemic immunosuppressio.58 Understanding the role of EV-associated miRNAs in shaping the immune microenvironment will be crucial for developing novel therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring immune function in GBM.

miRNA-Based Therapies

Nowadays, miRNA therapy is a new strategy for treating GBM due to its ability to modulate gene expression and oncogenic pathways through two main approaches: the downregulation of oncogenic miRNAs and the restoration of tumor-suppressive miRNAs59–61 (Figure 2). miRNA-4315, enriched in exosomes through anti-PD1 treatment, reduced expression of the pro-apoptotic protein Bim, leading to chemotherapy resistance. Targeting of miRNA-4315 re-sensitized GBM cells to temozolomide-induced apoptosis.62 Similarly, hypoxic GBM cells release miR-25/93-loaded extracellular vesicles that inhibit cGAS-STING activity in macrophages, thereby reducing the release of type I IFN and T-cell activation. Hence, targeting miR-25/93 may represent a new approach to reactivating T cells in the GBM microenvironment.63 Further studies have continued to unravel a range of miRNA functions relating to GBM evasion. The circRNA-derived molecule EZH2-92aa suppresses NKG2D ligands on GBM stem cells, thereby impairing NK cell-mediated killing. At the same time, the downregulation of EZH2-92aa enhanced NK cell activity and improved responses to anti-PD1 therapy.64 Whole transcriptome and miRNA expression profiling in GBM patient tissues identified that miR-138 was drastically downregulated and inversely correlated with CD44 expression. miR-138 negatively modulates CD44 by binding to its 3’UTR. Expression of miR-138 inhibits GBM cell proliferation through cell cycle arrest, and ectopic expression of miR-138 increased survival rates in mice with intracranial xenograft tumors.65 EV-delivered miR-124 exhibited potent antitumor activities that retarded tumor growth and promoted NK cell infiltration.66 Finally, studies showed that miR-1983, secreted by glioma cells, serves as an endogenous ligand for TLR7 via the triggering of TLR7/MyD88 signaling to enhance NK cell-mediated activity against glioma cells, hence presenting a new avenue to recruit NK cells.67

More recently, Meister et al reported an unprecedented antitumor response after the administration of miRNA-based chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells targeting the EGFRvIII mutation, enhanced with IL-12 and IFNα2, in preclinical models with high specificity and potential for reduced side effects.68 Berneman et al conducted a phase I/II trial of WT1 mRNA–loaded dendritic cell vaccines, showing feasibility, strong immunogenicity, and improved survival in GBM patients; stable disease correlated with longer outcomes.69 Trivedi et al developed a modular mRNA vaccine platform encoding patient-specific neoantigens that elicited robust T cell responses in preclinical GBM models.70 The growing body of evidence illustrates the potential of miRNA-based therapies to modulate T and NK cell activity in GBM, whether by restoring tumor-suppressive miRNAs, silencing oncogenic miRNAs, or integrating miRNA strategies. These approaches provide a promising complement to existing immunotherapies and may help overcome the immunosuppressive barriers characteristic of GBM.

Oncolytic Viruses

Recent progress in oncolytic virotherapy of GBM has pointed out the potential of this modality to act as an adjuvant to conventional treatments. Oncolytic viruses (OVs) are genetically engineered to selectively target and lyse tumor cells while inducing a simultaneous immune response against the cancer (Figure 2). Significant development has occurred in this field, with most efforts aimed at improving targeting mechanisms and combining OVs with other therapeutic methodologies to overcome the immunosuppressive environment posed by GBM.71–73 In the following, some OV-based platforms are discussed.

HSV-Based Platforms

Herpes Simplex Virus (oHSV) platforms are among the most clinically advanced strategies. In 2020, a study using oHSV showed that mouse CT2A GBM cells engineered to express the human equivalent HSV1 receptor, Nectin-1, enhanced both viral replication and tumor antigen-specific CD8+ T cell infiltration, leading to a profound reduction in tumor volume in mouse models.74 Another study engineered oHSV to express a full-length anti-human CD47 antibody, disrupting the “do not eat me” signal. Two virus variants were tested: OV-αCD47-G1, which enabled both antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis and antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity mechanisms, and OV-αCD47-G4, which lacked these functionalities. Both constructs blocked CD47 and allowed macrophage phagocytosis, but OV-αCD47-G1 demonstrated greater efficacy through mentioned mechanisms, significantly enhancing survival in GBM mouse models.75

In 2021, another study used anti-CD8 PET imaging to monitor CD8+ T cell infiltration following oHSV therapy in glioma models. The study revealed that higher T cell activity, particularly that of CD8+ T cells, is a key indicator of effective tumor targeting and a robust immune response in oHSV therapy.76 Indeed, in 2022, a phase Ib study evaluated G207, a genetically engineered oHSV. Treatment accelerated the deepening of T cell infiltration and induced adaptive immune responses associated with more prolonged survival in recurrent patients with GBM. The virus was well-tolerated, with modest survival benefit in the trial.77 In 2023, Hong et al developed an oncolytic virus (oHSV-shPKR) that inactivates the PKR pathway, enhancing viral replication and immune activation. This approach significantly improved survival by boosting anti-tumor immunity in preclinical models, demonstrating the potential for PKR modulation in virotherapy.78 Newer HSV-based viruses have incorporated additional immunomodulatory functions. For instance, in 2023, a Phase I trial with CAN-3110, a genetically engineered oHSV, showed that the virus reshaped the TME and augmented immune responses through increased T-cell activation and expansion of specific T-cell clonotypes.79 Also, Noh et al engineered an IGF2-decoy oHSV to overcome virus resistance, increasing T cell infiltration and survival compared with conventional oHSV.80

Adenovirus-Based Platforms

Oncolytic adenoviruses have also advanced in recent years. For instance, Delta24-RGD was evaluated in a Phase I trial using convection-enhanced delivery (CED), bypassing the BBB and directly reaching tumor tissue. This trial showed that Delta24-RGD was well-tolerated, with mild adverse effects, and promoted increased infiltration of CD8+ T cells and macrophages into the TME. Evidence of viral replication confirmed the virus’s selectivity in infecting and destroying tumor cells, supporting its potential as a treatment for recurrent GBM.81 Similarly, Delta-24-ACT, engineered to express the 4–1BB ligand, significantly improved survival and immune memory when combined with PD-L1 blockade in glioma models.82 ICOVIR17, another adenovirus, was modified to express hyaluronidase, breaking down hyaluronan in the TME. This promoted CD8+ T cell infiltration and macrophage activation, and when combined with anti-PD-1 therapy, led to long-term remission and prolonged survival in animal models.83 Also, Koch et al observed that high-dose dexamethasone impaired the efficacy of the non-replicating adenovirus CAN-2409 by reducing CD8+ T cell infiltration and immune responses, leading to poorer survival in GBM models.84 Finally, Kumar et al created an IL24S/IL15 fusion superkine delivered via an adenovirus and with ultrasound-assisted BBB opening, which enhanced NK and T cell infiltration and significantly prolonged survival in GBM models without systemic toxicity.85

Other OV-Based Strategies

Zika Virus (ZIKV) has also emerged as a promising candidate for GBM virotherapy. Preclinical tests combining ZIKV with irradiated tumor cell vaccines showed enhanced survival in rodent models, accompanied by strong CD4+ memory T-cell responses providing long-term tumor immunity.86 A separate study tested ZIKV with an immune checkpoint inhibitor, showing enhanced CD8+ T-cell infiltration and improved anti–PD-1 efficacy, prolonging survival and providing durable protection against tumor recurrence.87 In another study, Reovirus infection was observed to downregulate NKG2D ligands, potentially impacting NK cell activity and emphasizing the need to understand OV-immune interactions for optimal therapy.88 Measles virus platforms have also advanced: a phase I trial of intratumoral MV-CEA in recurrent GBM patients showed that the virus was safe and induced pro-inflammatory remodeling with increased CD8+ T cell infiltration.89 Finally, combining MV expressing H. pylori neutrophil-activating protein with anti-PD1 therapy cured up to 80% of GBM mouse models, with greatly enhanced CD8+ T cell responses.90 In this regard, several novel combination therapies in recent years have emerged. For instance, some studies used genetically engineered OVs to express immune-stimulatory molecules. One example involved a cetuximab-CCL5 fusion protein, which targeted EGFR-expressing GBM cells while enhancing NK cell, dendritic cell, and T cell recruitment and activation. This dual-targeting approach induced robust immune responses, with high CD8+ and CD4+ T cell infiltration and significant anti-tumor activity.91 In 2024, Long et al identified CMV-driven immunosuppressive subsets in GBM and showed that CMV-specific TCR-T cells restored immunity and reduced tumor growth.92 These advances highlight the evolving landscape of oncolytic virotherapy in the treatment of GBM. The combination of engineered oncolytic viruses, immune modulators, and innovative delivery methods offers new perspectives for more effective and durable treatments for this highly challenging cancer.

CAR-T/NK Cell Therapy

Chimeric Antigen Receptor (CAR) therapies, including CAR T cells and CAR NK cells, have been used for cancer immunotherapy (Figure 2). CAR T cell therapy, in which T cells are genetically modified to target tumor-specific antigens, has successfully treated hematological cancers. Nevertheless, its application on solid tumors like GBM is not so easy due to problems like high costs, complicated manufacturing processes, severe side effects, and the need to be more effective. These problems have made people look for different methods, especially CAR NK-cell therapy. CAR NK-cell therapy could be an “off-the-shelf” solution with fewer side effects because of the natural cytotoxicity of NK cells and a low risk of graft-versus-host disease (GVHD). Besides, CAR NK cell therapies are still in the research phase, as the studies are concentrating on enhancing their persistence, expansion, and clinical efficacy.93–95 CAR-T/NK studies can be categorized by target antigens to better appreciate comparative efficacy and safety. In the following, some of these targets are discussed.

EGFR/EGFRvIII-Targeted CAR-T/NK Cell Therapies

EGFRvIII, a tumor-specific mutation of EGFR, has been one of the most extensively explored CAR targets in GBM. In the study conducted in 2020, researchers used CRISPR/Cas9 to knock out the expression of PD-1 in CAR-Ts that recognized EGFRvIII. Such cells exhibited enhanced antitumor capacity in vitro against GBM. In this case, CRISPR-mediated knockout of PD-1 augmented the functionality of CAR-T cells by overcoming tumor-mediated immune suppression.96 Moreover, in the next year, in 2021, one study looked at the application of an oncolytic virus that expressed IL-15/IL-15Rα and EGFR-targeted CAR NK cells, showing substantial tumor regression and improved survival in animal models with minimal toxicity.97

Recently, Martins et al boosted anti-EGFRvIII CAR-T therapy by engineering cells to secrete a CD47 blocker, enhancing macrophage phagocytosis and tumor clearance in GBM,98 while Gutova et al showed that Wnt/β-catenin inhibition with ICG001 promoted tumor differentiation and CAR-T infiltration, improving tumor control and decompressed tumor vasculature.99 In the other study, Ohta et al created “PRIME” CAR-T cells expressing IL-7 and CCL19, which target EGFRvIII-positive GBM. This approach improved T-cell survival and recruitment, leading to complete rejection of GBM in mouse models.100 Moreover, in a first-in-human study, Choi et al tested bispecific CARv3-TEAM T cells targeting EGFR/EGFRvIII and secreting a bispecific T-cell engaging antibody, which led to the induction of rapid tumor regressions in early-phase GBM patients.101 In a separate study in 2024, investigators examined the use of EGFR BATs, a bispecific antibody-armed T-cell therapy, in combination with radiation and temozolomide in treating newly diagnosed grade 4 astrocytoma. EGFR BATs target the epidermal growth factor receptor expressed in tumor cells. The combination proved well-tolerated and had manageable side effects, besides having some promising results, such as reducing tumor burden and an increase in survival.102 Finally, Courot et al enhanced umbilical cord–derived NK cells with an anti-EGFR antibody, improving antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity and resilience in immunosuppressive conditions.103

IL-13Rα2-Targeted CAR-T/NK Cell Therapies

IL-13Rα2 is another well-studied target in GBM, as it is highly expressed in tumors but minimally in normal tissue. In 2021, one study used engineered CAR T cells, which were made to express modified IL-13 to target IL-13Rα2. These cells demonstrated selective cytotoxicity against IL13Rα2-expressing GBM cells, and their infusion suppressed tumor growth in preclinical models.104 Another research used a Bi-Specific Killer Cell Engager (BiKE) that targets IL-13Rα2-positive gliomas, which increased the NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity and, therefore, the survival of the preclinical models.105 Finally, in a phase I clinical trial, Brown et al reported that locoregional IL-13Rα2 CAR-T therapy was safe, feasible, and controlled disease in 50% of evaluable recurrent GBM patients, with a favorable toxicity profile.106 These findings emphasize that IL13Rα2 therapies are well tolerated and clinically promising, though strategies to enhance CAR persistence and prevent antigen loss are needed.

HER2-, B7H3-, and CD44v6-Targeted CAR-T/NK Cell Therapies

Beyond EGFR and IL13Rα2, multiple alternative antigens have been pursued. Recently, investigators have shown very potent antitumor HER2 CAR-T/NK cells against GBM. Injections around the tumor had higher in-vitro and in-vivo effects than intravenous injections, denoting high tumor suppression and better animal survival rates.107 In another study, new strategies emerged, comprising AAV-mediated delivery of a checkpoint inhibitor gene and HER2-targeted CAR NK cells, which are intended to strengthen immune reactions to GBM.108 Moreover, Strassheimer et al found that HER2-specific CAR-NK therapy combined with PD-1 blockade reprogrammed GBM’s “cold” microenvironment and achieved long-term survival in preclinical models.109 Finally, in 2023, another clinical trial evaluated the safety and efficacy of intracranially injected HER2-targeted CAR NK cells; the treatment was well-tolerated, but the number of objective responses was limited.110 In another study, Tachi et al demonstrated that B7H3-targeted CAR-T and CAR NK cells potently eradicated patient-derived GBM in xenografts.111 Additionally, Boulifa et al engineered CD44v6-targeted CAR-NK92 cells that selectively killed GBM cells in vitro and significantly reduced tumor burden in vivo.112 These alternative targets, often combined with strategies such as checkpoint blockade or dual-antigen targeting, may overcome limitations of single-target CAR therapies.

Other CAR T/NK Cell Approaches

CAR NK cell therapy is being investigated as an “off-the-shelf” approach with fewer side effects.

In 2021, a research team presented an NFκB-driven alpha retroviral vector to increase CAR NK cell therapy via cytokine production at the tumor site, thereby improving NK cells’ cytotoxicity against solid tumors.113 Another approach used NK cells with dual-specific CARs targeting GD2 ligands and NKG2D. This method effectively targeted tumor cells and adenosine production in the TME, thus improving the immune response.114 Later in 2022, Gargett et al investigated GD2-targeted CAR T cells expressing IL-15. These cells have an improvement in infiltration and tumor growth control in mouse GBM models.115 Another research was devoted to examining NK cells with either genetically modified or cytokine-stimulated super-charging modifications for targeting GBM stem-like tumorospheres. These NK cells exhibited very high cytotoxicity, especially in the case of a combination of temozolomide, which resulted in better tumorosphere reduction than chemotherapy treatment alone.116 Moreover, one study tested the addition of NK cells to bevacizumab and irinotecan in a GBM mouse model. Treatment with this combination significantly reduced tumor size compared to bevacizumab and irinotecan treatment alone, along with improved survival rates, thus showing synergy that can favorably impact treatment outcomes.117

In 2023, another step forward was made in fine-tuning the CAR T and NK-cell therapies to treat GBM. One study found that the usage of IL-15-modified CAR T cells not only targeted GBM cells but also depleted immunosuppressive myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and recruited the overall immune response in the TME.118 Also, a new method of creating NK cells from pluripotent stem cells has been worked out, enabling the production of highly functional NK cells that can kill many tumor cells.119 Moreover, CXCL11-armed oncolytic adenoviruses combined with CAR T cell therapy were evaluated. This method enhanced recruitment of NK and CAR T cells to the tumor site, improving anti-tumor responses and the remodeling of the TME toward less immunosuppression.120 In 2024, Qin et al genetically deleted the NKG2A checkpoint in pluripotent stem cell–derived NK cells, enhancing cytotoxicity and prolonging survival121 In the next year, Sætersmoen et al further engineered NK and T cells with an NKG2A/C switch receptor to reverse HLA-E–mediated inhibition, improving anti-tumor activity with a favorable safety window.122 Finally, Meehan et al demonstrated that intracranial NK92MI therapy immediately after temozolomide eradicated mesenchymal GSC-driven tumors in mice, stressing the importance of precise timing.123

Next-Generation CAR T/NK Strategies: Synergizing TME Remodeling and Safety Enhancement

In one study, researchers demonstrated that Intratumoral IL-12 delivery significantly enhanced CAR-T therapy against GBM in a preclinical model. IL-12 enhanced CAR-T cells’ cytotoxicity and reshaped the TME to one less immunosuppressive, driving durable antitumor responses with minimal systemic toxicity.124 Also, Alizadeh et al studied the indirect effects of CAR T-cell therapy in the context of GBM tumor models. They concluded that CAR T-cells selectively attacked antigens on tumors, intratumorally activated myeloid cells, and elicited endogenous T-cell responses, the latter driven through IFNγ. This TME remodeling supported antitumor immunity and augmented CAR T-cell efficacy and persistence, thus developing superior therapeutic outcomes.125 In 2024, Nakazawa et al enhanced NK cell function in hypoxic GBM by knocking out HIF-1α using CRISPR. These NK cells overcame TME suppression and improved tumor killing.126 In the same year, Lei et al enhanced CAR-T cell therapy by fusing it with an intracellular protein degradation system (eMIATAC), reducing immunosuppressive proteins and boosting antitumor effects.127 Moreover, Akhavan et al showed that stereotactic radiation prior to CAR-T infusion reshaped the TME and significantly improved CAR-T efficacy in GBM.128 Finally, in the next year, Liu et al found that sodium valproate increased NKG2D ligand expression, making GBM cells more susceptible to CAR-T cytotoxicity in preclinical models.129 Clinical trials have also evaluated the safety and efficacy of some of these therapies. For instance, a clinical trial in 2021 analyzed autologous adoptive immune-cell therapy for recurrent GBM, yielding safe and durable responses in some of the patients, which may result in better survival.130 Across all targets, CAR-T/NK therapies have shown manageable safety profiles, with severe cytokine release syndrome and neurotoxicity remaining rare. EGFRvIII and IL13Rα2 targeting has advanced furthest in clinical development, but antigen heterogeneity and limited CAR persistence continue to challenge durable responses. Newer strategies targeting HER2, B7H3, and CD44v6, or incorporating immune-modulating features (eg, CD47 blockade, IL-7/CCL19 secretion), have enhanced antitumor activity in preclinical models and may translate into improved outcomes in future trials.

Conclusion

Despite prominent immunotherapeutic advances, the prognosis for GBM patients remains poor, reflecting the tumor’s complex immunosuppressive microenvironment, heterogeneity, and the physical barrier caused by the BBB. A central insight from this review is that immune cell dysfunction—exhaustion of cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cells, impaired activity of CD4⁺ helper T cells, NK cell hypo-responsiveness, and the immunosuppressive roles of macrophages, DCs, and MDSCs—remains a major barrier to effective therapy. These abnormalities are aggravated by epigenetic alterations that sustain immune evasion. Future research must focus on immunotherapy concepts that simultaneously reverse these immunosuppressive mechanisms and enhance effector responses. Combination therapies demonstrate particular promise; preclinical data support pairing CAR-T/NK cell therapies with checkpoint blockade, miRNA therapy or oncolytic virotherapy to improve persistence and tumor infiltration. Innovative delivery methods—including convection-enhanced delivery, nanoparticle-based carriers, and intratumoral hydrogel depots—are showing early clinical potential and should be further optimized. Finally, combining immunotherapies with standard modalities such as radiotherapy or tumor-treating fields may synergize to remodel the TME and promote durable antitumor immunity.
