Assessment of respirable silica in ambient air of Dhaka and evaluation of human health risks
Authors: Md Nazmul Islam Nahin, Md Safiqul Islam, Shahid Uz Zaman, Shatabdi Roy, Abdus Salam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRespirable Silica (RS) is a substantial pollutant in urban air, and it is associated with severe health hazards, including lung inflammation, kidney diseases, poor lung function, and silicosis. RS levels in close proximity to occupational and emission sources are widely reported; however, ambient exposure from non-occupational sources is still not properly studied. This study examines the concentration of RS in ambient air during different seasons of the year and the diverse sources that contribute to RS concentrations in Dhaka City, Bangladesh. Ambient fine particulate matter (PM) was collected using a low-volume air sampler on Teflon filters at an average flow rate of 15.4 ± 1.36 L min⁻¹, with each sample collected continuously over 24 h to assure a representative daily exposure at five different locations. RS concentrations were quantified spectrophotometrically, with a limit of detection (LOD) of 0.05 µg m⁻³. The highest recorded concentration was 14.2 µg m⁻³, while the mean RS concentration was 3.74 ± 3.12 µg m⁻³. Seasonal patterns indicated increased RS concentrations in winter (6.28 µg m⁻³) relative to the pre-monsoon (1.97 µg m⁻³) and post-monsoon (3.74 µg m⁻³) phases. Among the five study sites, the Teacher Student Center (TSC) exhibited the highest RS concentration (7.33 µg m⁻³) due to proximity to metro-rail construction, whereas the academic building site recorded the lowest (0.95 µg m⁻³), indicating relatively cleaner air. Health risk assessments indicated an average hazard quotient (HQ) of 1.25, suggesting significant non-cancer risks, and a cancer risk of 14.2 cases per 10,000, emphasizing the urgent need to address RS exposure. These findings emphasize the substantial public health impact associated with ambient RS pollution in urban environments, urging quick regulatory and mitigation steps in rapidly rising cities such as Dhaka.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21348-0.Keywords: Particulate air pollution, Urban construction emissions, Spectrophotometric analysis, Carcinogenic risk, Seasonal air quality variationSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Chemistry

Introduction

Respirable Silica (RS) is a hazardous fine dust containing very small particles of respirable silica (particles of less than 10 microns in size), a common mineral found in the Earth’s crust1,2. RS predominantly originates from anthropogenic activities such as construction, mining, and industrial processes. Its small particle size allows it to become airborne and enter the respiratory system, leading to severe health effects. Prolonged exposure to RS can result in silicosis—an irreversible lung disease—as well as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), lung cancer, and kidney disease3,4. These adverse health impacts emphasize the necessity of quantifying RS concentrations in atmospheric particulate matter (PM) to assess associated risks.

Many research investigations worldwide have reported RS concentrations in various environments, emphasizing both occupational and non-occupational environments. For example, Bhagia5 reported significantly higher amounts in the vicinity of slate pencil industries in India, whereas Puledda et al.6 discovered lower concentrations in urban areas. Although many approaches, including X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), have been utilized for RS measurement, these methods sometimes need costly equipment and specialized knowledge, making them less accessible for resource-limited areas7. The study utilizes UV-Visible Spectroscopy as a method for measuring RS due to its cost-effectiveness. UV-Vis spectroscopy provides a cost-effective alternative to traditional methods like XRD and FTIR, offering adequate sensitivity and accessibility for resource-limited areas. The research examines RS in Dhaka, Bangladesh, one of the world’s most polluted cities. Despite the increasing amount of research on RS exposure, there is a lack of data on its concentrations in densely populated and rapidly urbanizing cities such as Dhaka, where construction and industrial activities substantially contribute to air pollution. In addition to construction and vehicular emissions, regional waste treatment activities and secondary aerosol formation may also contribute to elevated particulate matter concentrations, as evidenced through multi-source identification studies using PMF and PCA in Southern Thailand and Bangkok7,8. The majority of current research concentrates on work-related exposure in industrial environments, while there are fewer studies that examine urban ambient air quality and its broader public health implications.

The objective of this investigation is to address these limitations by: (i) Quantifying the concentrations of RS in the ambient air during various seasons in Dhaka City, a region that is affected by significant urbanization-induced air pollution, (ii) Identifying and analyzing major RS sources at selected urban and construction sites to acquire a more comprehensive understanding of their impact on the overall quality of the air, and (iii) Conducting a comprehensive assessment of the public health consequences of RS exposure, including both carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic health hazards. This research advances the development of accessible air quality monitoring techniques by utilizing UV-visible spectroscopy as a cost-effective substitute for traditional RS measurement methods. The results will be helpful in the development of mitigation strategies for air pollution in rapidly expanding cities such as Dhaka, providing policymakers, public health officials, and environmental researchers with valuable insights.

Methodology

Sampling location

To evaluate ambient RS levels, air sampling was carried out across five strategically selected urban locations in Dhaka City, each characterized by distinct anthropogenic activities and environmental circumstances (Fig. S1). The selected sites are as follows: (i) Mukarram Hossain Khundker Biggan Bhaban (MHK): Located within an academic environment with minimal industrial or construction activity, this site was used as a control to establish background RS levels. (ii) Green Model Town (GMT): A rapidly developing residential area undergoing continuous construction. The presence of unpaved roads and active building projects makes it a representative high-dust environment. (iii) Teacher Student Center (TSC): Situated adjacent to an ongoing metro-rail construction site, this location was selected to represent exposure conditions near major infrastructure development. (iv) Dhaka Medical College (DMC): An area influenced by waste incineration and hospital-related activities, contributing to particulate emissions potentially containing RS. (v) Meradia Kacha Bazar (MKB): A densely populated marketplace with intense pedestrian and vehicular activity, known for high dust and particulate matter levels.

Sample collection

Ambient fine particulate matter (PM) was collected using a low-volume air sampler over 24-hour sampling periods. Pre-weighed 47 mm Teflon filters were used to selectively capture airborne PM smaller than 5 micrometers in diameter. The air sampler was positioned approximately 5 m above ground level to ensure representative sample collection, following established methodologies10,11. The sampling system operated at an average flow rate of 15.4 ± 1.36 L min⁻¹. The total air volume for each sample was calculated from the difference between initial and final gas meter readings. A total of 45 samples were collected: 17 samples each from MHK and GMT, and 3 samples each from TSC, DMC, and MKB. Additionally, one blank sample from each location; total 5 blank samples were collected as controls. Sampling frequency varied among the sites based on study objectives: MHK and GMT were sampled repeatedly to observe seasonal variations, whereas TSC, DMC, and MKB were sampled once to represent regular exposure levels.

Determination of PM concentrations

The mass of PM collected on each filter was determined using a precision microbalance (Radwag, Model: AS220.R2, accuracy: 0.0001 g). Filters were weighed before and after sampling, and the difference in weight represented the 24-hour mass of PM collected. The PM concentration was calculated by dividing this mass by the corresponding sampled air volume. To preserve the integrity of the samples for subsequent chemical analysis, the filters were stored in petri dishes at temperatures below 0 °C in a refrigerator. This precaution minimized potential contamination and ensured sample stability12.

Extraction of PM

Half of each filter paper was cut and placed into a polystyrene beaker. To extract the PM, 10 mL of 5% sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution was added to the beaker. The mixture was shaken using a mechanical shaker set to 25 °C for 90 min to dislodge particulates. After shaking, the beaker was placed in an ultrasonicator and sonicated at 35 °C for 30 min to further aid the extraction process. Next, 12 mL of 5% nitric acid (HNO₃) solution was added to the same beaker, and the sonication process was repeated under identical conditions (35 °C for 30 min) to ensure complete extraction of the particulates. The extracted solution was then passed through Whatman filter paper (Cat No. 1001 − 125) to remove any remaining solid residues. From the filtered solution, a 5 mL aliquot was transferred into a polystyrene conical centrifuge tube for silica analysis using UV-visible spectroscopy.

Preparation of standard solution

Silica calibration standards were prepared using sodium metasilicate nonahydrate (Na2SiO3.9H2O). A stock solution equivalent to 100 ppm of silica (SiO2) was prepared by dissolving an appropriate amount of sodium metasilicate nonahydrate (Na2SiO3.9H2O) in water at room temperature, following the procedure outlined by Manno et al.13. The stock solution was then diluted stepwise to produce working standards with silica concentrations ranging from 0.05 to 3.00 µg m⁻³ for calibration (Fig. S2). To avoid contamination during preparation and analysis, borosilicate glassware was not used. Instead, all standard solutions and extracted samples were handled using polystyrene equipment. This ensured the precision and reliability of the analytical measurements performed using the UV-visible spectrophotometer. The method was validated by comparing the results with a reference technique, X-ray Diffraction (XRD), in a separate analysis, showing comparable results for silica concentrations. The methodology13,14 is adapted to achieve high sensitivity and precision, with a limit of detection as low as 0.05 µg m⁻³.

Analysis of silica content

Construction of calibration curve

The calibration curve was constructed using a series of working silicate standard solutions. For each, 5.0 mL of the solution was measured into individual tubes. At the start of the process, 125 µL of Molybdate 3 Reagent was added, followed by vortex mixing. The solutions were then allowed to stand undisturbed for 4 min. Afterward, 125 µL of citric acid (Hach) was added to each tube, and the reaction was allowed to proceed for 1 min. Subsequently, 250 µL of Amino Acid F Suspension (Hach) was introduced, and the mixtures were vortexed immediately. Absorbance measurements were performed at 816.5 nm using a double-beam UV-Vis Spectrophotometer (Model: UV-1800, SHIMADZU, Japan), with water serving as the blank reference13,14.

This method provides an accurate determination of RS concentrations in PM samples. In the presence of both silica and phosphates, molybdate ions react with these components in an acidic environment to form yellow silicomolybdic acid complexes. Citric acid selectively destroys phosphate complexes while preserving the silicomolybdic acid complexes. This ensures specificity for silica measurement. Following the Beer-Lambert law, the intensity of the blue color produced is directly proportional to the silica concentration. The calibration curve exhibited excellent linearity, with a coefficient of determination (R²) of 0.998 (Fig. S2), emphasizing the reliability of the method for silica quantification in PM samples12.

Quality control

Throughout the sampling and analytical processes, strict quality control measures were consistently followed to guarantee the highest level of reliability for the collected data. At each phase of this research, a deliberate effort was made to avoid the use of borosilicate apparatus, minimizing any possibility of introducing contamination into the samples. To guarantee precision, the air sampler underwent a thorough leak-test and calibration before commencing each sampling session. Various parameters, including the weight of the filter paper, pump readings, as well as the date and time of sampling, were meticulously recorded both prior to and after the sampling process. To maintain precision, blank samples were systematically gathered during the experiment and subsequently analyzed. To safeguard the filter papers’ integrity, they were kept in clean, dry, and sealed plastic petri-dishes. After sampling, these petri-dishes were swiftly stored in a refrigerator to prevent any potential contamination before chemical analysis. Right before the extraction process, the loaded filters were taken out of the refrigerator and left in a dessicator for 30 min, ensuring the absence of moisture. For the chemical analysis, the apparatus utilized was meticulously cleaned using acetone and appropriately rinsed with deionized water. The chemical analysis itself was carried out utilizing analytical grade reagents. To account for instrument readings, reagent blanks were also subjected to analysis via UV-visible spectrometry. Furthermore, prior to operation, the spectrometer underwent a thorough calibration process to ensure accurate and reliable measurements.

Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to assess the relationships between respirable silica concentrations and meteorological parameters (temperature, relative humidity, wind speed, solar radiation, visibility, and UV index). The distances between the weather station and the sampling sites were approximately 8 km for TSC, MHK, and DMC, 14 km for GMT, and 10 km for MKB.

Results

Variation of PM and RS concentration

PM sampling was carried out over 24 h at five locations in Dhaka city: MHK, GMT, DMC, TSC, and MKB, using a flow rate of 15.4 ± 1.36 L/min. The data revealed a mean PM concentration of 354.5 ± 151.5 µg m⁻³. The PM concentrations, as presented in Table 1, showed a wide range, from 129.2 µg m⁻³ to 740.3 µg m⁻³, indicating a substantial variation in airborne PM pollution level across different urban environments in Dhaka. The UV-visible spectrophotometer used for determining RS concentrations was rigorously calibrated and validated, ensuring accurate and precise measurements. The average RS concentration in Dhaka was found to be 3.74 ± 3.12 µg m⁻³. The highest concentration among the 45 samples reached 14.2 µg m⁻³, recorded at the GMT site during the winter season.

Discussion

Over the course of six months, from November to April 2023, variations in silica concentrations between two locations—MHK and GMT—were analyzed in detail (Fig. 1). The meteorology of Bangladesh encompassed three distinct seasons during this six-month period: post-monsoon, winter, and pre-monsoon24. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the seasonal variations in silica concentrations between two locations, MHK and GMT, have been assessed. During the winter season, both MHK and GMT exhibited the highest silica content, which crossed the limit value 3 µgm−3; MHK recorded a silica concentration of 3.49 µgm−3, while GMT measured 6.28 µgm−3. The increased silica concentrations observed during winter are likely the result of both meteorological and anthropogenic influences.

During this season, prevailing continental northwesterly winds transport air masses from the Indo-Gangetic Plain (IGP), a region known for high pollution levels, in contrast to the cleaner oceanic air masses typical of the pre-monsoon period25–27. These polluted air masses, combined with cold temperatures, low wind speeds, and stable atmospheric conditions, suppress vertical mixing and reduce pollutant dispersion, allowing silica particles to accumulate and persist near the surface28. Furthermore, intensified industrial activity and emissions from local sources, such as the Matuail Waste Treatment Plant, contribute significantly to ambient RS levels. The dry winter climate, characterized by minimal rainfall and exposed landfill surfaces, further enhances dust emissions, exacerbating silica concentrations in the ambient air.

A Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to explore the relationship between RS concentrations and meteorological parameters (temperature, relative humidity, wind speed, solar radiation, solar energy, and visibility) during the winter season (Fig. 3). RS exhibited strong negative correlations with temperature (r = − 0.68), indicating that colder conditions favor pollutant accumulation29. Wind speed also showed a moderate negative correlation (r = − 0.25), highlighting the role of low atmospheric dispersion in the persistence of RS28. Moderate inverse correlations were observed with visibility (r = − 0.50), solar radiation (r = − 0.59), and UV index (−0.60) all of which are typically suppressed under wintertime atmospheric stability. The weak positive correlation between RS and relative humidity (r = + 0.47) likely reflects reduced dry deposition and potentially enhanced particle suspension under higher moisture levels, consistent with observations in other urban winter environments30. The moderate positive correlation between RS and relative humidity (RH) (r = + 0.47) reflects reduced dry deposition and prolonged particle suspension under high RH in stagnant conditions (e.g., urban winter). This occurs because moisture increases particle hygroscopic growth, slowing their settling, while low winds prevent dispersion31,32.

Spearman Correlation coefficients between respirable silica (RS) concentrations and meteorological parameters during the winter season (p < 0.05).

In the pre-monsoon season, the silica concentration was observed to be the lowest. MHK measured 0.57 µgm−3, while GMT recorded a slightly higher concentration of 1.97 µgm−3. This reduction is largely attributed to favorable meteorological conditions, including higher humidity, increased rainfall, and enhanced atmospheric mixing, which collectively promote the dispersion and wet deposition of airborne particles. Furthermore, this season is dominated by southwesterly winds, which transport relatively cleaner air masses from the Bay of Bengal, thereby diluting local pollution levels and reducing the accumulation of respirable silica in the ambient air29.

During the post-monsoon season, intermediate silica levels were observed. MHK measured a concentration of 2.40 µgm−3, while GMT recorded a higher concentration of 5.29 µgm−3. The silica concentrations during this season may be influenced by a combination of factors, including weather patterns, industrial activities, and the ongoing construction in GMT. The transitional weather conditions during the post-monsoon season may have influenced the dispersion and accumulation of silica particles in the air. The data further highlights that throughout the seasons, the concentration of RS was consistently higher in GMT compared to MHK. The Matuail Waste Treatment Plant, particularly during the dry season, contributes significantly to particle emissions due to increased surface activity and dust discharge. Hossain et al.33 reported that pollutant emissions from municipal waste sites in Dhaka increase significantly during dry periods due to enhanced surface activity and particulate release.

The seasonal variation observed in RS levels aligns with known atmospheric processes. During the post-monsoon season, lower wind speeds and a shallower boundary layer inhibit the vertical dispersion of pollutants, leading to their accumulation near the surface25,34. These atmospheric conditions, combined with ongoing emissions from anthropogenic activities, contribute to elevated levels of RS. In contrast, during the pre-monsoon season, stronger winds and higher humidity promote pollutant dispersion and wet deposition, resulting in lower concentrations35.

Figure 4 illustrates a comparative analysis of silica concentrations across five distinct regions. TSC exhibited the highest concentration of silica, measuring 7.33 µgm−3. This concentration accounts for 47.2% of the highest concentration among the five locations. The high silica concentration in TSC can be due to the ongoing Metro-rail construction activities. Construction activities generate silica-rich airborne dust, particularly from materials such as cement, concrete, and bricks, which contributes to elevated ambient RS levels. The high RS concentrations during winter may also reflect increased emissions from nearby industrial sources, such as brick kilns and small-scale manufacturing units, which are more active in the dry season. Previous studies have shown that such activities contribute significantly to ambient silica and particulate levels in South Asian cities26,27,36–38. This supports the likely association between industrial emissions and RS concentrations in the study area. MHK, an educational institute, had the lowest concentration of silica among the five locations, measuring 0.95 µgm−3. This concentration represents 6.1% of the highest concentration observed. The lower silica concentration at MHK suggests cleaner air in the vicinity compared to the other locations. MKB had the second-highest concentration of silica, measuring 3.45 µg m−3, which represents 22.2% of the comparison. The high silica concentration in MKB could be due to the presence of a nearby market. The gathering of a large number of people in the market, especially on Wednesdays, leads to increased dust emission and subsequently higher levels of silica in the ambient air. GMT exhibited a silica concentration of 2.14 µg m−3, accounting for 13.8% of the highest concentration observed. The silica concentration in GMT may be influenced by various factors, including the presence of the Matuail Waste Treatment Plant and ongoing construction activities in the growing town. Construction sites are known sources of silica particles that can become airborne and contribute to increased silica levels in the air. DMC exhibited a silica concentration of 1.66 µgm−3, representing 10.7% of the comparison. The silica levels in DMC are influenced by the primary waste dumping of Dhaka Medical College. Recent studies have identified the dumping of medical waste near DMC as a major source of particulate pollution in the area27. In summary, the silica concentrations varied significantly across the five different locations, with TSC exhibiting the highest concentration and MHK exhibiting the lowest. The variability in silica content among the analyzed samples emphasizes the influence of factors such as construction activities, market gatherings, and nearby industrial sources on silica concentrations in ambient air.

Comparison of RS concentration of Dhaka City to global cities

Various international studies have evaluated RS concentrations in different environments as displayed in Fig. 5. Although studies on RS concentrations are available from many countries, we specifically selected studies from those countries whose sampling environments, pollution sources, and monitoring objectives closely resemble the context of our research in Dhaka. In Hotan City, China, Liu et al.39 reported variation of crystalline silica concentration (α-SiO2) in atmospheric fine particulate matter (PM2.5), ranging from 8.02 µgm−3 during dust periods to over 1.77 µgm−3 during non-dust periods, influenced by regional factors such as wind speed and desert-originated air masses. Kim et al.40 examined RS exposure levels among South Korean sanitation workers, finding a geometric mean value of 2.6 µgm−3, with coal briquette ash collectors registering the highest concentration at 24.0 µgm−3, highlighting variations linked to job tasks, seasons, employment types and city scales. Stacey et al.41 found that RS concentrations from construction and quarry activities in the United Kingdom were generally low, with urban and rural background levels below 0.30 µgm−3 and 0.02 µgm−3, respectively. Andraos et al.17 observed higher RS concentrations in certain long-term samples from mine tailings depots in South Africa, ranging from 15.0 to 37.0 µgm−3. Additionally, when monitoring the mine tailings depots, PM10 samplers recorded higher respirable silica levels ranging from 4.0 to 19.0 µgm−3, indicating the presence of 70–90% respirable silica in the sampled mine tailings depots. Richards et al.4242 measured PM4 respirable silica emissions and found downwind concentrations ranging from 0.3 to 2.8 µgm−3 near sand and gravel plants in California, USA. De Beradis et al.43 assessed silica concentrations in PM10 in an urban area of Rome, Italy and found levels ranging from 0.25 to 2.87 µgm−3. Bhagia et al.5 observed higher respirable silica concentrations near the agate industry in Khambhat, India, with average 24-hour levels of 15.3 µgm−3 near industry sites and 3.03 µgm−3 in control areas. In Dhaka, Bangladesh, this study found an average silica concentration of 3.74 ± 3.12 µgm−3, with the lowest at 0.05 µgm−3 (MHK) and the highest at 14.2 µgm−3 (GMT). The study compared the concentration of RS in Dhaka with that of other urban areas worldwide. High concentrations of RS in Dhaka city are due to a number of factors, such as industrial emissions, high construction activity, and inadequate regulatory supervision. In other cities, mitigation strategies, including improved dust control at construction sites and industrial regulations, have been effective in reducing RS exposure.

Evaluation of health risk assessment of RS

Table 1 presents a comprehensive summary of both non-cancer and cancer risks associated with exposure to RS bound PM at MHK and GMT. The non-cancer risk assessment was conducted using a hazard quotient (HQ) approach. The calculated average HQ values were 0.81 for MHK and 1.61 for GMT. Notably, an HQ value exceeding 1 at GMT indicated a potential for non-cancer health effects linked to RS exposure. At the MHK site, eight samples showed HQ > 1, six samples had HQ < 1, and three samples had non-detectable RS concentrations, which precluded the calculation of HQ values. Similarly, at the GMT site, ten samples presented an HQ > 1, while seven samples indicated an HQ < 1. Conversely, the average cancer risk estimation yielded values of 0.93 × 10−5 for MHK and 1.84 × 10−5 for GMT. According to the guidelines established by US EPA, (2000), cancer risk estimates falling between 1 in 1,000,000 individuals and 1 in 10,000 individuals are subject to regulatory concern due to the potential for cancer development over a lifetime. Applying this criterion, the highest recorded cancer risk was found to be 26.7 individuals per 10,000 individuals at the MHK location, while the GMT site exhibited a higher risk at 54.1 individuals per 10,000 individuals, as stipulated by the US EPA’s 2000 guidelines. In Table 2, the results reveal variations in the hazard quotient (HQ) values across the five different locations. Among these, TSC and MKB stand out with HQ values exceeding 1, which raises significant concerns regarding potential non-cancer health risks. Conversely, the remaining three locations- MHK, GMT and DMC- display HQ values below 1, indicating a comparatively lower level of non-cancer health risk. The evaluation of cancer risk also yields noteworthy insights. The TSC location demonstrates the highest recorded cancer risk, amounting to 27.8 individuals per 10,000 individuals. This finding underscores a potential heightened risk of cancer development within this population, according to the criteria outlined in the (US EPA 2000) guidelines. In contrast, the MHK site boasts the lowest calculated cancer risk, with a value of 3.62 individuals per 10,000 individuals. This outcome suggests a relatively lower likelihood of cancer incidence among individuals exposed to silica at the MHK location.

Conclusion

The current study presents a novel and comprehensive evaluation of ambient respirable silica concentrations in Dhaka City, with an emphasis on seasonal variation, spatial distribution across urban microenvironments, and associated health risks. The research develops air quality monitoring techniques that are appropriate for resource-limited locations by utilizing UV-visible spectroscopy as a cost-effective and accessible analytical approach. The findings demonstrate that RS concentrations frequently exceed the recommended safety threshold of 3 µg/m³, particularly during the winter and in regions that are impacted by construction activities, emphasizing an urgent requirement for regulatory attention. The study provides significant information on non-occupational RS exposure in densely populated urban regions, a relatively unexplored topic in environmental health research. Nevertheless, the study remains limited by a lack of precise source apportionment data and its dependency on generalized exposure assumptions for health risk assessment. Future attempts to incorporate source-specific emission data and localized exposure scenarios will be crucial in improving risk estimations and guiding targeted mitigation efforts.
