Impact of preconceptional vertical sleeve gastrectomy on metabolic, cardiovascular and hepatic functions of Sprague Dawley female rats and perinatal consequences
Authors: Cyrielle Payen, Abigaëlle Guillot, Julien Chaigneau, Lucile Bichot, Eugénie Testa, Jennifer Bourreau, Géraldine Gascoin, Emilie Vessières, Coralyne Proux, Valérie Moal, Laurent Loufrani, Françoise Schmitt, Céline Fassot
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractVertical sleeve gastrectomy (VSG) is the most performed metabolic bariatric surgery, and is known to improve fertility, leading to a rise in procedures among women of childbearing age. With an experimental model in Sprague–Dawley rats, we investigated the impact of pre-conceptional VSG on physiological functions of mothers, and its perinatal consequences during pregnancy. Female control rats (Control) received a standard diet, while others were fed a high-fat, high-sugar diet (HFHS). HFHS rats were divided into Obese and Sleeve groups. One month after surgery, all groups underwent gestation and subsequent lactation. Metabolic, cardiovascular and hepatic functions were evaluated 4 months after VSG. From one-month post-surgery, Sleeve mothers showed decreased leptin and insulin levels compared to Obese mothers, normal fertility with similar litter size to Control, without any effect on birth weight of pups Three months later, although body weight and white adipose tissue were also normalized, Sleeve mothers exhibited a high level of hepatic fibrosis and a hypertrophic remodelling of the thoracic aorta. In addition, we detected an endothelial dysfunction of mesenteric arteries, with a stronger involvement of COX/PGI2 pathway in the vasoreactivity. While VSG improves metabolic function at short and middle term after surgery, our results highlight an alteration of hepatic and vascular functions which could lead to cardiovascular diseases.Keywords: Bariatric surgery, Fetal programming, Metabolic and vascular diseases, ObesitySubject terms: Physiology, Cardiovascular biology, Metabolism

Introduction

Prevalence of obesity is gaining ground all over the world where up to 50% of women of childbearing age (20–39 years old) present at least an obesity I1. Maternal obesity is associated with reduced fertility and obstetric complications (preeclampsia, gestational diabetes mellitus), leading to detrimental short-term outcomes for both mother and neonate2. Furthermore, it impacts fetal development, increasing the risk for the child to develop metabolic and cardiovascular disorders during adulthood3.

In women with severe obesity, physical activity and / or dietetic interventions are not enough to significantly and durably reduce body weight and then improve postnatal health in children4. For the past 20 years, metabolic bariatric surgery (MBS) has emerged and is currently considered as the most effective treatment for severe obesity in order to improve women fertility and maternal/fetal health5. Thus, the incidence of MBS has increased among women with obesity of childbearing age before pregnancy6. Different surgical procedures have been developed to reduce body weight. The most widespread procedures are a restrictive and malabsorptive surgery with the Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB)7 and a more recent procedure, the vertical sleeve gastrectomy (VSG) which has currently become the most practiced8. Even if RYGB is more efficient than the other procedures in the midterm9, the technical simplicity of VSG associated to its lower incidence on surgical complications ensures its attractiveness10.

Current evidence indicates that pre-conceptional MBS reduces obesity-related pregnancy complications (perinatal outcomes, gestational diabetes mellitus, preeclampsia, macrosomia)11, but at the cost of a strict nutritional monitoring of the mother12. However, it appears that children born to operated mothers are at higher risk of prematurity or intrauterine growth retardation11.

In this study, we investigated the impact of VSG on maternal and offspring perinatal outcomes in an experimental rat model of moderate obesity. We evaluated metabolic parameters (i.e. glucose tolerance, lipid levels) and vascular reactivity of thoracic and mesenteric arteries at mid-term time following surgery. For the offspring, we assessed birth weight and survival rate.

Results

Impact of VSG 1 month after surgery

Body weight evolution

Before surgery, Obese and Sleeve weighted 372.5 ± 8.8 g and 361.4 ± 5.8 g, compared to Control (326.8 ± 6.5 g, p = 0.0047 vs Sleeve and p = 0.0003 vs Obese; F(2) = 156.9, p < 0.0001). VSG induced a 10%-drop of body weight during the 5 days following surgery. Afterwards, Sleeve maintained a similar body weight as Control, although Obese continued to gain weight (Fig. 1A). Interestingly, 24 h-calories intake measurement showed an increase in Obese and Sleeve groups 1 month after surgery (Fig. 1B).

Hepatic profile

Although there is no difference in liver weight between groups (Fig. 3A), we detected fibrosis without steatosis in Sleeve group (Fig. 3B), and a significant elevation of AST in plasma compared to Control group (83.1 ± 7.3U/L vs 59.7 ± 2.2U/L, p = 0.019 and 79.6 ± 6.9U/L for Obese group; H(2) = 8.466, p = 0.0094). In addition, while ALT was not detectable in plasma of Control group, we measured a higher level of ALT in Obese et Sleeve groups (14.2 ± 3.1U/L and 12.6 ± 0.9U/L).

Vascular profile

We studied vascular function (i.e. vascular reactivity) and structure of large (thoracic aorta) and small (mesenteric) arteries 4 months after surgery.

Vascular reactivity of thoracic aorta of Control, Obese and Sleeve were not different. In fact, the smooth muscle cell-dependent vasocontraction induced by KCl or PE and the endothelium-dependent (Ach) or -independent (SNP) vasorelaxation were similar between the 3 groups (Fig. 4, left panel). Despite this result, we measured a significant increase of MCSA and observed a high Media-to-Lumen ratio of thoracic aorta in Sleeve group compared to the other groups (Table 3).

In mesenteric artery segments, although KCl- or PE-induced vasocontractions were similar between the 3 groups (Fig. 4A,B, right panel), Sleeve females showed a lower maximal response to Ach-induced vasorelaxation than Control group (Emax: 64.9 ± 7.9% vs 93.4 ± 2.7%, p = 0.022; F(2) = 23.81, p < 0.0001; Fig. 4C, right panel) without alteration in endothelium-independent (SNP) vasorelaxation (Fig. 4D, right panel). In addition, blocking nitric oxide (NO) production by L-NAME induced a decrease of maximal response to ACh-induced vasorelaxation around 40% in Control, Obese and Sleeve showing an equivalent implication of NO in mesenteric artery’s vasodilation for these 3 groups (Fig. 4E). The contribution of the prostaglandins (PGI2) pathway was then evaluated with INDO; although the vasodilator response to ACh in presence of INDO was similar between Control and Obese females, we observed an altered ACh-induced vasorelaxation with a reduced maximal response between Sleeve and Obese groups (-49%, p = 0.0021, Fig. 4F). This observation suggests a stronger involvement of the COX/PGI2 pathway in mesenteric artery of Sleeve. The implication of the EDHFs pathway in endothelium-dependent vasorelaxation (explored with KCl 25 mM) was not different between groups (Fig. 4G). Finally, in presence of Tempol + catalase, Ach-induced vasorelaxation curves were equivalent to those without Tempol + catalase (Fig. 4H) showing that ROS did not impact vascular reactivity of mesenteric arteries. In parallel, histomorphometric analysis of mesenteric arteries did not show significant modification even if all the parameters, except internal diameter, seemed to be increased for Sleeve in favour of an outward hypertrophic remodelling (Table 3).

Delivery and post-natal evolution

Even if the decrease of pups/litters was not significant, we observed a negative impact of maternal obesity on litter size, which was reversed by VSG (Fig. 5A). This effect did not impact the sex ratio distribution or the birth weight of pups (Fig. 5B). But the body weight follow-up showed that OMO gained more weight until weaning than CMO and SMO males and females (Fig. 5C).

Discussion

Using an experimental surgical model on HFHS-induced obesity, we demonstrate here that females that have undergone VSG show endothelial dysfunction and hypertrophic remodelling of vessels 4 months after surgery. In addition, even if these animals do not present liver steatosis, they develop a higher level of fibrosis.

Our model of HFHS-induced obesity prior to surgery results in a 12% increase in body weight, accompanied by a 20% increase in caloric intake. This is associated with a threefold rise in circulating leptin level, an impaired glucose tolerance, the development of fatty liver and increased fat mass. It thus highlights the development of obesity and its associated comorbidities in our animal model, even in a short time, which is not the case for most published rodent models of MBS13,14. In adults, guidelines currently recommend MBS beyond obesity I15,16 which approximately corresponds to a 40% body weight gain. This is more than the 12% increase of body weight in our model, but there is growing interest to propose this treatment even in patients with less severe obesity with comorbid conditions such as diabetes mellitus17.

Nevertheless, our model mimics the clinical effects of VSG, showing a rapid decrease of adipose tissue, the restoration and maintenance of normal body weight throughout gestation and lactation, despite continued high food intake. Body composition analysis shows that this weight loss is due to a decrease in fat mass, more pronounced in subcutaneous than in visceral fat, with no change in muscle mass, as assessed by tibial muscle weight. Histological analysis of WAT shows the disappearance of hypertrophic adipocytes in both visceral and subcutaneous fat samples in the Sleeve group compared to the Obese one. We also observe a decrease of circulating leptin and insulin levels one month after surgery, with a sustained effect over time, which may be partially explained by the endocrine function of adipose tissue18. As expected, postoperative weight loss was associated with improved glucose tolerance tests and normalization of fasting insulinemia 4 months after surgery.

In our study, we demonstrate that, 4 months after surgery, Sleeve mothers exhibited impaired endothelial function of resistance arteries although thoracic aorta reactivity was preserved. The presence of an endothelial dysfunction after MBS is controversial; based on protein analysis of endothelin-1 pathway in rat’s lung tissue, Ruze et al.recently concluded that endothelial function is improved after VSG19. Conversely, other studies have reported a decrease in plasma NO bioavailability at 12 and 24 weeks post-VSG20. In our model, the blunted vasodilatory response to Ach does not appear to involve the NO or oxidative stress pathways, as no significant differences were observed with L-NAME or Tempol + catalase. Instead, the endothelial dysfunction of mesenteric arteries seems to be associated with an enhanced improved COX/PGI2 pathway, potentially involving prostacyclin. Since VSG involves the resection of a substantial portion of the stomach, alterations of endothelial function of mesenteric arteries could impact oxygen delivery and nutrient absorption in the intestinal tissues, potentially affecting their function and contributing to gastrointestinal complications.

Interestingly we observed a vascular hypertrophic remodelling of the thoracic aorta in Sleeve mothers. This hypertrophic response, characterized by an increase in vascular smooth muscle cells size and extracellular matrix deposition, is the hallmark of chronic hypertension21. The presence of such structural modification was unexpected, as clinical data indicate that over 50% of patients experience remission of hypertension one year after MBS22. Although we did not directly measure blood pressure, heart and left ventricular weights were not different between groups (data unshown), suggesting the absence of elevated blood pressure as described in rodent studies23. Moreover leptin has been described as a potential mediator of obesity-vascular remodelling associated with obesity24, independently of blood pressure25. In our study, although plasma leptin levels were normalized 4 months after VSG, we detected hypertrophic remodelling. This finding could suggest a chronic increase of blood flow, similar to what has been reported in Zucker rats with metabolic syndrome26. Such remodelling may serve to support increased oxygen turnover in the context of altered metabolic demands.

Liver histology and metabolic impact: sleeve gastrectomy was effective to normalize hepatic steatosis in our model. However, we observed a 2.5-fold increase in liver fibrosis compared to the Obese group, although fibrosis level remained within the normal range. While MBS is known to improve MAFLD and reduce fibrosis scores27,28, few studies have explored this effect in the specific context of pregnancy and lactation. Pregnancy can trigger a range of acute liver disorders that usually resolve after delivery. In case of pre-existing MAFLD or obesity, a recent study by Hussain et al. reported increased liver stiffness after delivery, particularly in association with type 2 diabetes mellitus, preeclampsia and ALT ≥ 25 as risk factors29. In human, breastfeeding seems to be protective for liver health: longer breastfeeding duration has been linked to a reduced risk of MAFLD30, and cumulative lifetime breastfeeding has been linked to a lower risk of advanced liver fibrosis31. However, Zhang et al. recently showed that dairy cows undergoing the transition period, exhibited liver fibrosis proportional to the severity of pre-existing hepatic steatosis32. In addition, Bertasso et al. reported a worsening of MAFLD after Roux-en-Y gastric bypass in female rats following pregnancy and lactation33. Our results suggest a similar hepatic vulnerability following sleeve gastrectomy, warranting further investigations.

Obstetrical outcomes and offspring growth: pre-conceptional MBS has demonstrated efficacity in reducing obesity-related adverse obstetric outcomes. However, it has also been associated with increased risks of intrauterine growth restriction, small-for-gestational age infants and preterm deliveries34. Although these outcomes have been more frequently reported following gastric bypass, similar trends have also been observed with sleeve gastrectomy35. In our study, Obese mothers had slightly fewer pups per litter, possibly due to increased in utero fetal deaths and/or reduced fertility. Contrary to previous findings in rodent models36,37, we observed no difference in birth weights between pups born to Sleeve, Obese or Control mothers. However, postnatal growth trajectories before weaning revealed patterns in both male and female offspring from Sleeve mothers, suggesting impaired nutritional intake, at least during the lactation period, whereas pups from Obese mothers exhibited accelerated growth. It may be explained by reduced milk production or differences in nutritional milk composition, as previously stated by Deer et al.38.

Limitations

Vascular reactivity using functional inhibitors only give informative results on endothelial dysfunction. Thus, pathways that appear disrupted need to be further studied (e.g. protein quantification of Cox/PGI2). Finally, since we did not measure arterial blood pressure in our model, we should not simply exclude the possibility that female rats undergoing SVG do not develop hypertension, regarding thoracic aorta remodelling and endothelial dysfunction. It will be important to monitor blood pressure in future studies.

Conclusion

The present study highlights that VSG performed in HFHS induced-obesity rats may lead to subclinical liver dysfunction and impaired mesenteric vascular reactivity.

Methods

Animal model

All experimental protocols were approved by the French Ministry of National Education, Research and Innovation and received the agreement APAFIS#10697-2017091422557044 v1. All methods were performed in accordance with the institutional guidelines (3R principles) and the recommendations for the care and use of laboratory animals of the French Ministry of National Education, Research and Innovation. All methods are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

35 female Sprague–Dawley rats, ~ 6 weeks of age, were purchased from Janvier Labs. The animals were housed in groups of 3per cage (floor area: 1820cm2) within a controlled temperature room (21–23 °C) and maintained under a 12-h light/dark cycle with free access to water and food. 11 females were randomly assigned to the normal-weight control group (Control) and were fed ad libitum a standard show diet (8.4% fat, 19.3% protein, 72.4% carbohydrate [w/w], 13.97 kJ/g, diet code: 3430, Serlab) ; the other females were fed a high-fat diet (22.6% fat, 23.0% protein, 35% carbohydrate [w/w], 16.73 kJ/g, diet code: 824,053, SDS, Dietex International Ltd.) associated with sweetened condensed milk (55% simple sugar, 8% fat, 8% protein, Nestlé®) for 8 weeks (HFHS group). HFHS females were then randomly assigned for surgery in one of the 2 following groups: obese mothers (Obese, n = 12) or obese mothers with VSG (Sleeve, n = 12). After surgery, each female was fed with its assigned food until the end of the follow-up.

Surgical procedures

Surgical interventions were performed after 4 h fasting, under isoflurane anaesthesia (3.5% v/v in 20% O2 and 80% air). Immediate pre-operative care included subcutaneous injection of 1.5 ml serum saline, 0.1 mg/Kg buprenorphine, 0.5 mg/Kg metoclopramide, 30 mg/Kg amoxicillin/clavulanic acid and 0.175 mg/Kg Fercobsang®.

All animals from the three groups underwent a midline laparotomy and a liver biopsy of the median lobe, to collect tissue for histological analysis while maintaining maximal procedural standardization across groups. After gentle mobilization of the stomach, without any surgical resection or modification, to mimic the tissue handling and stress associated with sleeve gastrectomy, the abdominal wall of Control and Obese animals was then closed in two layers using a continuous absorbable suture (V-Loc™ 3/0, Covidien/Medtronic).

For the group undergoing VSG: after the liver biopsy, the right gastric artery was ligated at the bottom of the great curvature of the stomach, and a resection of about 70% of the gastric pouch including the rumen in totality was performed. The remaining part of the stomach was then tabularised and closed using two subsequent continuous sutures of 6/0 Prolene (Peters Surgical). Finally, the abdominal cavity was closed as described previously for the Control and Obese groups.

During the three first postoperative days, they received subcutaneous hydration (1.5 ml serum saline), analgesia (buprenorphine 0.1 mg/Kg), antibioprophylaxis (30 mg/Kg amoxicillin/clavulanic acid), iron supplementation once a day and were fed with GelDiet Energy (Safe). Then all animals were given water supplemented with multivitamins (Vita Rongeur) until the end of the protocol.

Time point analysis

One month after recovery, the efficiency of MBS was assessed by body weight evolution, calories intake, glucose tolerance and plasma analysis (glycemia, insulin, leptin, triglycerides, cholesterol). Then, each female was housed together for 15 days to ensure mating with a non-obese Sprague–Dawley male to analyse the impact of obesity + /- VSG on litters (sex-ratio, birth weight and weight gain before weaning, measured each day on the first week and every 7 day thereafter) of offspring from control mothers (CMO), obese mothers (OMO) and sleeve mothers (SMO).

Four months after surgery, insulin sensitivity and glucose tolerance were evaluated, as 24 h-calories intake. Mothers were then euthanized by carbon dioxide inhalation. For each animal, thoracic aorta and second-order mesenteric arteries free of fat and adhering connective tissues were gently dissected and placed in a physiological salt solution for vascular reactivity study and histomorphometric analysis. Tibia and tibial muscle were also taken. Blood samples for plasma analysis (glycemia, insulin, leptin, triglycerides, cholesterol, AST and ALT), white adipose tissue (WAT) and liver were also collected.

24 h-calories intake

Females were placed for 24 h in individual metabolic cages (Techniplast) with controlled food and water. At the end of this period, the food consumption was measured, the 24 h-calories intake was then calculated.

Insulin and glucose tolerance tests

Insulin sensitivity was evaluated with an insulin tolerance test (ipITT). A single intraperitoneal (ip) injection (1 IU/Kg of body weight) of human recombinant insulin (Umuline®, 100UI/Ml, Lilly) was delivered after a 4 h-fast. Glucose tolerance was estimated with a glucose tolerance test (ipGTT) using a single ip injection of a D-glucose solution (100 mg/100 g of body weight) after an overnight fast. Blood glucose was measured from the tail vein just before and 15, 30, 60 and 120 min after insulin or glucose injection, using test strips and reader (ACCU-CHEK Go®, Roche). The area under curves (AUC) were calculated using the trapezoidal rule of Prism 10 software (GraphPad Prism® Software).

Plasma analysis

Total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), triglycerides (TG), alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) were measured using a C16000 chemistry analyzer (Abott®) at the Biochemistry Laboratory of the University Hospital of Angers. Insulin and leptin were measured using ELISA kits (80-INSRT-E01, Eurobio; #A05176.96, Bertin) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Vascular reactivity of thoracic aorta and mesenteric arteries

Vascular reactivity was assessed by studying the reactivity of 4-months old rats isolated thoracic aorta and second-order mesenteric arteries. Four segments (2 mm long) of thoracic aorta and mesenteric arteries from 1 animal were dissected and mounted on 2 wire-myographs (DMT). All arterial segments were bathed in a 5 ml organ bath containing a physiological salt solution (130.0 mM NaCl, 3.7 mM KCl, 1.2 mM MgSO4, 14.9 mM NAHCO3, 1.6 mM CaCl2, 5.0 mM HEPES, 1.2 mM KH2P04 and 11 mM glucose) maintained at a pH7.4, a pO2 of 160 mmHg and a pCO2 of 37 mmHg. After wall tension normalization at 90 mmHg to approximate the in vivo transmural pressure, arterial segments were allowed to stabilize for 30 min. The ability of all vascular segments to contract was first tested with KCl (80 mM) and secondly with cumulative concentration–response curve to phenylephrine (PE 1 nM to 10 µM). Then the integrity of the endothelium was tested using acetylcholine (ACh, 1 µM) after precontraction with PE (1 µM). After washout, the arteries were precontracted with PE to a level approximately equivalent to 80% of the maximal response and vasorelaxation was assessed with a cumulative dose–response curve of ACh (1 nM–10 µM). Then, ACh-induced vasorelaxation was tested in the presence of NG-nitro-l-arginine methyl ester (l-NAME, 100 μM, nitric oxide synthase inhibitor). Furthermore, vasodilatory mechanisms more specific to resistance arteries were explored in mesenteric arteries, including the EDHF pathway with KCl25 (25 mM, EDHF pathway inhibitor), the cyclooxygenase pathway with indomethacin (INDO, 10 µM, nonselective inhibitor of cyclooxygenase 1 and 2), and finally the oxidative stress-related pathway in presence of Tempol + Catalase (ROS inhibitor). At the end of the experiment, endothelium-independent vasorelaxation was studied in thoracic aorta and mesenteric arteries with a cumulative dose–response curve of sodium nitroprusside (SNP, 1 nM-10 µM). All reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Data were expressed as a % relaxation of PE-induced precontraction.

Histological analysis

White adipose tissue (WAT)

Visceral (perigonadal and perirenal) and subcutaneous (inguinal) fat pad were weighted and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde before embedded in paraffin. Adipocytes size was measured on 5 µm-tick sections stained with hematoxylin–eosin (H&E). We used the Aperio CS2 digital slide scanner (Leica Biosystem) to generate a virtual slide with high quality images (resolution: 0.5 µm/pixel). For each animal, one slide was scanned and a region of interest (ROI) of 3500 × 3500 pixels was manually selected on the virtual slide. We took care to choose an artifact-free ROI to facilitate the automatic adipocytes detection. Then, adipocyte sizes were automatically measured in the selected ROI by our software (added as a Plugin in ImageJ).

The first step of the algorithm was to detect the adipocytes represented in high intensity (near white colour) in H&E staining. Thus, we obtained a binary image (white and black) via an automatic thresholding technique and then calculated the mean diameter of each adipocyte developed in HIFIH laboratory.

Liver

A sample of the median lobe of the liver was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde before embedded in paraffin. Consecutive tissue 5 µm-tick sections were realized and stained with 0.1% picrosirius red. Image acquisition was done with Aperio CS2 digital slide scanner. Liver steatosis and fibrosis area were obtained by a semi-automatic detection using an algorithm developed by HIFIH laboratory (Angers)39. Liver steatosis corresponds to the ratio of steatosis vacuoles/area of the whole biopsy sample.

Thoracic aorta and mesenteric artery

One segment of thoracic aorta and 1 s-order mesenteric artery were embedded in Tissue-Tek (Sakura Finetek) and frozen in isopentane. Sections of 7 µm thick were stained with orcein to measure histomorphometric parameters: external and internal media diameters, medial cross-sectional area (MCSA) and intima-media thickness (IMT), after image acquisition (Aperio CS2 digital slide scanner, Leica Biosystem) and analyse using ImageJ software (NIH). Media-to-lumen ratio was calculated.

Statistical analysis

Normal distributions were tested using Shapiro–Wilk test. Tissue and morphological data are presented as median and interquartile range whereas plasma measurements and histomorphometric analysis of vessels although not normally distributed, are reported as mean ± SEM in accordance with common practices in experimental research to facilitate comparison with existing literature; these data are compared using a Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test. Moreover, body weight follow-up and vascular reactivity curves were analysed using a repeated measures two-way ANOVA followed by a Bonferroni’s multiple comparisons test. Sex repartition was analysed using a ordinary two-way ANOVA followed by a Tukey’s test. Values of p < 0.05 were significant. All statistical analyses were performed using Prism 10 software (GraphPad Prism® Software), including comparison of variances.
