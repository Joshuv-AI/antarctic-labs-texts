Prospective, multicentre trial on preoperative biliary drainage by endoscopic ultrasound-guided hepaticogastrostomy for resectable/borderline resectable pancreatic cancer with biliary obstruction: the PROLOGUE study – a study protocol
Authors: Shin Yagi, Susumu Hijioka, Yoshikuni Nagashio, Shota Harai, Daiki Agarie, Daiki Yamashige, Kohei Okamoto, Souma Fukuda, Masaru Kuwada, Yasuhiro Komori, Yusuke Kurita, Sho Hasegawa, Kensuke Kubota, Yusuke Ishida, Jun Ushio, Kotaro Takeshita, Kohei Yoshino, Hirotoshi Ishiwatari, Takuji Okusaka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPreoperative biliary drainage (PBD) is often required for patients with pancreatic cancer accompanied by biliary obstruction to ensure the safe administration of neoadjuvant chemotherapy or to manage cholangitis and jaundice. Although endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is the standard approach for PBD, it carries a significant risk of post-ERCP pancreatitis. Endoscopic ultrasound-guided biliary drainage (EUS-BD), particularly via hepaticogastrostomy (EUS-HGS), offers a promising alternative that avoids papillary manipulation. However, the clinical utility of EUS-BD as primary drainage for PBD remains unclear due to a lack of prospective studies. This multicentre prospective trial aims to evaluate the safety and efficacy of EUS-HGS as primary drainage for PBD in patients with resectable or borderline resectable pancreatic cancer.Methods and analysisThis multicentre prospective study involves seven institutions in Japan. Eligible patients will undergo EUS-HGS using a 7Fr plastic stent. The primary endpoint is clinical success, defined by improvements in bilirubin or liver enzyme levels within 14 days postprocedure. Secondary endpoints include technical success rate, adverse event incidence, stent patency and surgical outcomes. A total of 30 patients will be enrolled, considering an expected clinical success rate of 90% and a 10% dropout allowance.Ethics and disseminationThis study has been approved by the National Cancer Center Institutional Review Board (Research No. 2024-084). The results of this study will be reported at an international conference and published in an international peer-reviewed journal.Trial registration numberUMIN ID: 000055173.

Introduction

Surgical resection remains the only curative treatment for pancreatic cancer; however, pancreatic cancer often causes biliary strictures leading to obstructive jaundice. While the need for preoperative biliary drainage (PBD) in cases with mild jaundice and the possibility of early surgery remains controversial, biliary drainage (BD) is often performed when early surgery is not feasible due to cholangitis or severe jaundice.1 In addition, PBD has been suggested to reduce postoperative complications.2 Recently, the efficacy of neoadjuvant chemotherapy for pancreatic cancer has been increasingly reported,35 and effective jaundice relief via PBD is essential to ensure stable administration of preoperative chemotherapy.

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) with transpapillary BD is currently considered the first-line treatment. However, this procedure carries a risk of post-ERCP pancreatitis, which can sometimes lead to serious adverse events.6 Alternatively, endoscopic ultrasound-guided BD (EUS-BD), initially developed as a rescue technique for cases in which ERCP was unsuccessful, is now being evaluated for its safety and efficacy as a primary drainage method.715 EUS-BD allows BD without manipulation of the papilla and is therefore associated with an extremely low risk of procedure-related pancreatitis. However, reports evaluating EUS-BD as PBD are still limited. All previous studies were retrospective and most included patients with failed ERCP1622; only one case series has evaluated EUS-BD when it was intentionally performed as the initial method of PBD.22 To date, there have been no prospective studies evaluating EUS-BD as PBD for primary drainage, and its efficacy and safety remain to be established. The aim of this study is to prospectively evaluate the efficacy and safety of EUS-BD as primary drainage for PBD in patients with pancreatic cancer with biliary obstruction.

Trial examination procedures

All procedures will be performed with the patient in the prone position under sedation using midazolam or propofol, according to institutional protocols. An EUS (EG-740UT, Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan; GF-UCT260, Olympus Medical Systems, Tokyo, Japan; or equivalent) will be inserted into the stomach to visualise the intrahepatic bile ducts in the lateral segment of the left hepatic lobe (B2 or B3), and the bile duct diameter will be measured. After confirming the absence of intervening vessels with Doppler imaging, the bile duct will be punctured using a 19G or 22G EUS-FNA needle (EZ Shot 3 Plus, Olympus Medical Systems, Tokyo, Japan; or equivalent).

A 0.025-inch or 0.018-inch guidewire (Fielder, Asahi Intecc, Aichi, Japan; VisiGlide 2, Olympus Medical Systems, Tokyo, Japan; or equivalent) will then be advanced into the punctured duct under fluoroscopic guidance, and contrast cholangiography will be performed to confirm successful access. A catheter will be inserted, and bile will be aspirated. If necessary, the endoscopist may dilate the tract using dilation devices, including a mechanical drill dilator (Tornus ES, Asahi Intecc, Aichi, Japan), a bougie dilator (ES Dilator, Zeon Medical, Tokyo, Japan) or a balloon dilator (REN, Kaneka Medix, Osaka, Japan).

Finally, a 7Fr plastic stent (HarmoRay, Hanaco Medical, Saitama, Japan; Through Pass Type IT, Gadelius Medical, Tokyo, Japan; or equivalent) will be placed from the intrahepatic bile duct across the created tract into the stomach, regardless of whether the puncture site is B2 or B3 or the type of plastic stent used. In cases where EUS-HGS fails, BD will be performed by ERCP as a rescue strategy.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint is clinical success rate. Clinical success is defined as follows:

For patients with T-Bil >1.5 mg/dL at registration, a reduction in T-Bil to <50% of baseline or ≤1.5 mg/dL within 14 days after the procedure.

For patients with T-Bil >1.5 mg/dL at registration, a reduction in T-Bil to <50% of baseline or ≤1.5 mg/dL within 14 days after the procedure.

For patients with T-Bil <1.5 mg/dL and AST>100 U/L or ALT>100 U/L at registration, a reduction of AST or ALT to ≤100 U/L within 14 days after the procedure.

For patients with T-Bil <1.5 mg/dL and AST>100 U/L or ALT>100 U/L at registration, a reduction of AST or ALT to ≤100 U/L within 14 days after the procedure.

The primary endpoint, clinical success, is defined as a composite endpoint. Clinical success is achieved if either criterion (bilirubin reduction or liver enzyme normalisation) is met. Therefore, this is not considered a coprimary endpoint design, and no alpha adjustment is required.

Secondary endpoints include technical success rate (defined as the successful placement of the stent at the intended location before the procedure), adverse events rate, stent patency duration, stent patency rate at 30 days, stent patency rate at the time of surgery, rate of surgery performed, surgical procedure time, intraoperative blood loss during surgery, bile juice culture results during surgery, postsurgery complication rate, postsurgery length of hospital stay. Technical success is defined as the successful placement of the stent at the intended location before the procedure. Adverse events related to the endoscopic procedure are defined as events occurring within 30 days after the procedure. These events are assessed using the lexicon established by the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy,23 which is widely applied for evaluating adverse events in endoscopic procedures, as well as the Adverse events gastrointestinal endoscopy (AGREE) classification, an endoscopy-adapted version of the Clavien-Dindo classification originally developed for surgical adverse event assessment.24 Adverse events related to preoperative chemotherapy are evaluated according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0. Stent patency duration is defined according to the TOKYO Criteria 2024.25 In cases of stent occlusion, the duration is measured from stent placement to reintervention. In cases of stent migration, the duration is from placement to reintervention for biliary obstruction due to migration. Postsurgery complications are defined as events occurring within 30 days post-surgery due to direct surgical intervention and are classified using the Clavien-Dindo classification version 2.0.

Definitions

Resectability of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma is classified according to the Japan Pancreas Society criteria as resectable, borderline resectable or unresectable.

Resectable tumours: No contact or <180° contact without occlusion of the superior mesenteric vein/portal vein and no involvement of major arteries.

Resectable tumours: No contact or <180° contact without occlusion of the superior mesenteric vein/portal vein and no involvement of major arteries.

Borderline resectable tumours: Further classified into those with venous involvement (≥180° contact or occlusion without extension beyond the lower border of the duodenum) and arterial involvement (<180° contact with the superior mesenteric artery or coeliac axis without stenosis/deformity; contact with the common hepatic artery without extension to the proper hepatic artery or coeliac axis).

Borderline resectable tumours: Further classified into those with venous involvement (≥180° contact or occlusion without extension beyond the lower border of the duodenum) and arterial involvement (<180° contact with the superior mesenteric artery or coeliac axis without stenosis/deformity; contact with the common hepatic artery without extension to the proper hepatic artery or coeliac axis).

Timing of surgery after PBD

The interval between PBD and surgical resection is not specified in this study because both resectable and borderline resectable pancreatic cancer patients are eligible and their perioperative treatment pathways differ, particularly in borderline resectable cases where timing is individualised according to the planned neoadjuvant therapy and clinical condition. In patients with resectable pancreatic cancer undergoing neoadjuvant chemotherapy, gemcitabine plus S-1 therapy will be administered for two courses, and surgical resection will be performed approximately 2 months after the initiation of chemotherapy.

Sample size determination and statistical analysis

Based on previous reports, the rate of jaundice improvement after PBD was 94.5%–100% with EUS-BD and 70.2%–93.8% with ERCP.162226 Therefore, the threshold was set at 70% and the expected rate at 90%, with a one-sided significance level of 5% and a power of 80%, yielding a minimum required sample size of 26. Considering the discreteness of the binomial distribution and allowing for approximately 10% dropout, we set the target enrolment at 33 patients to ensure ≥80% power in the evaluable set.

The primary endpoint (clinical success) will be analysed as a proportion with its 90% CI. The study will be considered successful if the lower limit of the 90% CI exceeds the predefined threshold of 70%. Secondary endpoints, including technical success rate, adverse event rate and stent patency rates, will be summarised descriptively with proportions, medians and ranges as appropriate.

Data sharing statement

Deidentified individual participant data that support the findings of this study (including baseline characteristics, procedural details and outcome measures) will be available from the corresponding author on reasonable request. The study protocol and informed consent form will also be shared as supplementary materials. Data will be available beginning at the time of publication with no end date. Requestors will need to provide a methodologically sound proposal and obtain approval from the institutional review board.

Discussion

Previous studies evaluating EUS-BD as PBD are summarised in table 2.1622 All of these reports were retrospective studies, most of which evaluated EUS-BD as a salvage procedure in cases where ERCP was unsuccessful. In previous reports, the technical success rate of EUS-BD as PBD ranged from 95% to 100%, and the clinical success rate ranged from 94.5% to 100%, indicating favourable outcomes. The study by Tyberg et al compared EUS-BD and ERCP as PBD, and the need for endoscopic reintervention prior to surgical resection was significantly lower in the EUS-BD group, suggesting that EUS-BD may be superior as PBD method (38% vs 9%, p=0.0001).21 In the report by Okuno et al evaluating EUS-HGS as PBD, no adverse events occurred with EUS-HGS and no disadvantages were observed in the postoperative course, leading the authors to suggest the potential for expanding the indications for EUS-HGS.22 In addition, none of the studies reported cases in which EUS-BD led to surgical difficulty or was associated with perioperative complications.

In addition to EUS-HGS, other approaches such as EUS-guided choledochoduodenostomy (EUS-CDS) and EUS-guided antegrade stenting (EUS-AGS) have been reported for EUS-BD performed with the intention of PBD. Although previous studies suggest that EUS-BD may be an optimal treatment option for PBD, there is still debate as to which EUS-BD technique is most appropriate. In the present study, EUS-HGS was chosen as the EUS-BD method. This decision was based on several considerations. Pancreatic cancer is often associated with duodenal obstruction, which makes EUS-CDS difficult to perform in such cases. In addition, as previously reported, the use of large-calibre metal stents (MS) raises concerns about retrograde cholangitis caused by intestinal bacteria or food debris.19 With regard to EUS-AGS, the risk of postprocedural pancreatitis due to pancreatic duct obstruction at the papilla is similar to that seen with transpapillary stenting via ERCP.29 In addition, stents placed via EUS-HGS do not appear in the operative field, which is another advantage over other EUS-BD techniques.22 Therefore, EUS-HGS is considered the most appropriate EUS-BD approach for PBD as it can be performed regardless of duodenal obstruction, carries a lower risk of retrograde cholangitis and pancreatitis, and has minimal impact on subsequent surgical procedures.

In this study, we chose PS for EUS-HGS. To date, direct comparisons between plastic stents and MS in EUS-HGS are limited, and the optimal stent choice for this procedure remains controversial.3032 A recent study comparing PS and MS in EUS-HGS reported fewer adverse events with PS compared with MS, while demonstrating comparable outcomes in terms of recurrent biliary obstruction within 100 days.33 When EUS-HGS is performed as PBD, minimising procedure-related adverse events is critical to avoid delays in the initiation of preoperative chemotherapy or surgery. In this context, PS may be the most appropriate option, as it is associated with a lower risk of complications and demonstrates equivalent patency to MS in the short time frame prior to surgery. Therefore, PS may be the most appropriate choice for PBD in patients undergoing EUS-HGS.

This study has several methodological limitations. First, as a single-arm study without a randomised control group, the results should be interpreted with caution when compared with PBD performed by ERCP. Second, the study is conducted exclusively in Japanese high-volume centres, and thus external validity to other clinical settings may be limited.

This is the first prospective study to evaluate PBD using EUS-HGS as primary drainage. Although there is increasing recognition of the efficacy and safety of EUS-BD as primary drainage for unresectable malignant biliary obstruction, the evidence for EUS-BD as primary drainage for PBD is limited and remains controversial. The prospective evaluation of PBD using EUS-HGS as primary drainage in this study could contribute significantly to the establishment of the most appropriate PBD method for pancreatic cancer patients. It is also hoped that the results of this study will be used in future randomised controlled trials of EUS-HGS versus ERCP as PBD.

The study was approved by the National Cancer Center Institutional Review Board (Research No. 2024-084). Protocol version 1.1 was finally approved on 18 September 2024. A summary of the study, its progress and main results will be made available in the clinical registry system of the University Hospital Medical Information Network (UMIN ID: 000055173).
