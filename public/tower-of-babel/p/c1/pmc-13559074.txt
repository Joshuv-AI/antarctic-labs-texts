A multicenter prospective evaluation of a decision chart for toilet training initiation
Authors: Chisato Oyake, Yoshitaka Watanabe, Noriko Ueda, Akiko Tange, Sachiko Nozawa, Hirokazu Ikeda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere are currently no widely accepted standardized guidelines for determining the optimal timing to initiate toilet training in children. This study aimed to develop and evaluate the effectiveness of a newly designed tool—the Toilet Training Initiation Decision Chart (TTIDC).MethodsThis multicenter, prospective, open-label intervention study included children aged ≥18 months attending 70 nursery schools. Participants were divided into an intervention group, which utilized the TTIDC, and a control group, which followed conventional toilet training practices employed by each school. Weekly observation records were collected from July 1, 2022 to June 30, 2023. The primary endpoints were the duration of toilet training and the age at which toilet training was completed. The TTIDC was developed based on a prior pilot study.ResultsOf the 354 children enrolled, 43 completed toilet training during the study period. There was no significant correlation between the age of toilet training initiation and the duration required to complete training. However, the median age at completion was significantly younger in the intervention group (37.5 months) compared with the control group (40.8 months; P=0.002).ConclusionsThis chart may serve as an effective tool for initiating toilet training at an appropriate developmental stage. This chart may be associated with completion of toilet training at a younger age, without prematurely accelerating initiation or unnecessarily prolonging the process.Keywords: Developmental milestones, nursery school, pediatric intervention, toilet training, training initiation chart

Introduction

Background

Achieving independence in toileting is a critical developmental milestone in early childhood; however, the timing and methods of initiating toilet training vary widely among parents and caregivers.

Rationale and knowledge gap

According to the American Academy of Pediatrics, toilet training should begin at a developmentally appropriate stage, typically from 18 months of age onward. The decision to initiate toilet training should consider a comprehensive range of developmental factors, including physical, emotional, and language maturity; the ability to ambulate and manage clothing; bladder control; responsiveness to instructions; motivation; emotional regulation; frustration tolerance; and motor coordination. Most children are reported to complete daytime toilet training by approximately 36 months of age (1).

Similarly, the Canadian Paediatric Society advocates for child-led toilet training, emphasizing that most children acquire both bladder and bowel control between 24 and 48 months of age. Key developmental prerequisites for successful training include the ability to walk to the toilet and maintain a stable seated position (2). However, previous observational studies suggest that individual variability in readiness is considerable. For instance, Schum et al. (3) observed in a cohort of 267 children that chronological age alone is not a reliable indicator of toilet training readiness.

According to their research, the skills acquired in the early stages include:

“Understands potty words”, “shows an interest”, “tells during or after having a bowel movement”, “stays dry for 2 hours”, and “indicates a physical need to go”. They showed that skills acquired in the later stages of toilet training include “uses a regular toilet”, “stays bowel movement free during the day”, “tells before having to urinate”, “stays dry during the day”, “enters bathroom to urinate or have a bowel movement”, and “stays dry overnight”.

Concerns have also been raised about initiating training prematurely. A survey by Bakker et al. (4) involving 812 respondents indicated that forcing toilet training before developmental readiness may lead to urinary and fecal retention issues later in life due to underdeveloped bladder function. Moreover, prolonged or poorly timed toilet training can increase caregiver burden, including the need for frequent guidance and additional laundry responsibilities. Previous studies suggest that the financial burden of diaper use and a decline in the quality of care at childcare facilities are associated with prolonged toilet training (4,5).

Objective

Given these challenges, a more individualized approach—tailored to a child’s developmental progress—may be more effective. Notably, Bakker et al. (4) also reported that the average age at which toilet training is initiated has increased over time, potentially due to rising parental workforce participation and the convenience of disposable diapers. However, there is currently no established practical, evidence-based method for determining the optimal time to begin toilet training.

To bridge this gap, we developed the Toilet Training Initiation Decision Chart (TTIDC), a simple tool designed to assess physical, emotional, and cognitive markers of readiness for toilet training. The chart was created to be user-friendly and easily applicable by caregivers in real-life settings. In Japan, more than half of children aged 1 year and older attend childcare facilities, and many of them undergo toilet training there. We decided to conduct our research at childcare facilities because they provide a more consistent environment for observing, interacting with and responding to children. In collaboration with childcare facilities, we conducted this study to evaluate the efficacy of the TTIDC in facilitating timely and developmentally appropriate toilet training. We investigated the age at which toilet training was initiated, the duration of training, and the age at which training was completed in preschool children who had not previously undergone toilet training and who received a specific training program using TTIDC, as well as in a comparison group that underwent conventional toilet training without TTIDC. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2026-0398/rc).

Methods

This multicenter, prospective, open-label interventional study was conducted among children aged 18 months or older attending 70 nursery schools operated by three organizations. Children were excluded if they had already completed toilet training before meeting the initiation criteria, initiated training outside the observation period, did not begin training during the observation period, or if their legal guardians did not provide informed consent.

Each school participating in the study was assigned to either the intervention group or the control group, and each implementing agency allocated schools to the two groups so that the number of children in each group would be equal. Weekly observational data were collected from July 1, 2022 to June 30, 2023. The primary endpoints were the duration of toilet training and the age at which it was completed. Completion was defined as urinary incontinence occurring less than once per week.

In the intervention group, the TTIDC and a structured training method were implemented. The TTIDC was developed based on findings from a pilot study involving 17 children (median age: 29 months). It incorporated three key criteria: (I) voiding interval: >1 h between urinations, sustained over at least two consecutive days, and remaining dry after naps; (II) mental development: assessed as stable, indicating that the child could participate in regular childcare activities without difficulty; and (III) cognitive awareness of urination: demonstrated by the child’s ability to express the need to urinate and respond appropriately to related questions. For each of the three items, we provided options corresponding to different stages of development. Caregivers observed the children in light of the assessment checklist and compiled their observations. They assessed the children in order of priority, starting with priority 1, and determined that a child had reached the appropriate age to begin toilet training if they met or exceeded a dagger (†) designation for each item (Table 1). For children deemed ready according to the readiness assessment chart, toilet training was conducted using the following methods: (I) determining each child’s urination interval; (II) encouraging the child to use the toilet based on that interval; and (III) encouraging urination when the child indicated a need to use the toilet. In contrast, the control group continued to use conventional toilet training practices, with each nursery school independently deciding when and how to initiate training. Consequently, the frequency and timing of verbal prompts, as well as the tools used, varied across facilities.

To support implementation, informational sessions were conducted for caregivers in both groups. These sessions included videos and verbal explanations outlining the study protocol and implementation procedures. Recordings of the sessions were also distributed on a limited basis, allowing staff to review the content during the training period.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Shiba Palace Clinic Ethics Committee (approval No. 150098_rn-33062; approval date: June 2, 2022) and informed consent was obtained from the participants’ parents or legal guardians. All participating centers were informed of and agreed to the study.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, which were compared using Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Continuous variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and analyzed using either the two-sample t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, depending on data distribution. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was conducted among children who initiated toilet training during the study period, using the duration from toilet-training initiation to completion as the time-to-event variable. Intergroup differences were evaluated using the log-rank test. Children who did not initiate toilet training were excluded because the duration of toilet training could not be defined, whereas children who initiated but did not complete toilet training during follow-up, including those who left the nursery school, were treated as censored observations. All statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro 17 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), with a significance threshold set at P<0.05.

Results

The participant enrollment process is illustrated in Figure 1. A total of 354 children (191 boys and 163 girls), all aged 18 months or older, were enrolled in the study. Of these, 187 children [101 boys and 86 girls; median age: 28 months (IQR, 23–33 months)] from 37 nursery schools were allocated to the intervention group, and 167 children [90 boys and 77 girls; median age: 28 months (IQR, 23–34 months)] from 33 nursery schools were assigned to the control group. No significant differences in sex distribution or age were observed between the two groups.

Flowchart depicting participant enrollment, exclusions, and analysis sets for intervention and control groups.

The following children met the exclusion criteria and were excluded from analysis: seven children (one from the intervention group and six from the control group) had already completed toilet training before meeting the initiation criteria, 18 children (one from the intervention group and 17 from the control group) initiated toilet training outside the observation period, four children (all from the intervention group) were excluded due to lack of consent from legal guardians, and a total of 225 children (125 in the intervention group and 100 in the control group) did not initiate toilet training during the observation period. Among them, 97 children (57 in the intervention group and 40 in the control group) left the nursery school before completing training.

Among the remaining participants, 56 children in the intervention group and 44 in the control group initiated toilet training during the observation period. Of these, 19 participants in the intervention group and 15 in the control group withdrew from the study, most in March, either before completing toilet training or before the end of the observation period. Ultimately, 23 children in the intervention group and 20 in the control group completed toilet training within the study period.

Among the 43 children who completed toilet training (23 in the intervention group and 20 in the control group), the median age at initiation was 35.5 months (IQR, 31.1–38.5 months) in the intervention group and 37.8 months (IQR, 33.3–41.1 months) in the control group; this difference was not statistically significant (P=0.15). However, the median age at completion was significantly younger in the intervention group [37.5 months (IQR, 35.1–39.9 months)] than in the control group [40.8 months (IQR, 39.1–42.98 months); P=0.002].

The median duration required to complete toilet training was 130 days (IQR, 38–158 days) in the intervention group and 178.5 days (IQR, 86–266.5 days) in the control group; the difference was not statistically significant (P=0.08; Table 2).

Discussion

Key findings

Of the 354 children enrolled in this study, 100 initiated toilet training during the observation period, and 43 completed it. Thirty-four children withdrew during the observation period, primarily because they transferred to another nursery school or left the nursery school at the end of the academic year. These withdrawals were administrative in nature and were not related to training difficulty or developmental readiness. While the age at which toilet training was initiated and its duration did not significantly differ between the intervention and control groups, the age at which training was completed was significantly lower in the intervention group.

Strengths and limitations

The strength of the TTIDC is that it enables caregivers to objectively determine the appropriate time to begin toilet training.

This study has several limitations. First, the observations were limited to behavior within nursery schools, and toilet training activities at home were not assessed. It was not possible to assess whether consistency was maintained between the daycare center and the home, and this may have influenced the determination of completion. In addition, to improve compliance, we made it possible for caregivers to review the procedures repeatedly during the toilet training period; however, we were unable to verify their actual level of compliance. Second, the control group followed conventional toilet-training practices at each nursery school, and the training methods were not standardized. Third, the study included only children who initiated and completed training within the 12-month observation period, which may have led to an underestimation of training duration in both groups. Fourth, the preliminary briefing and observation protocol may have increased caregiver awareness of toilet training practices, potentially influencing the behavior of the control group and introducing a mild intervention effect. Fifth, it targets children aged 18 months or older attending nursery school and does not exclude those with developmental disorders. Children with developmental disorders may also experience delays in toileting; however, at 18 months of age, a formal diagnosis of a developmental disorder is often not made. In addition, many children left the participating nursery schools upon entering kindergarten, resulting in a small final sample size. Sixth, because the final analysis primarily included children who both initiated and completed toilet training during the study period, the possibility of selection bias cannot be excluded.

Comparison with similar research

Several studies have proposed criteria for assessing toilet training readiness. In a longitudinal study of 267 children, Schum et al. (3) identified key developmental markers, including motor milestones (e.g., sitting and walking), cognitive and language skills, emotional readiness, and awareness of bladder and bowel functions. Kaerts et al. (6) through a literature review, proposed 21 signs of readiness encompassing physical, cognitive, and emotional aspects of development. In a cross-sectional study of 409 children, Barutçu et al. (7) developed a 10-item readiness scale and reported that higher scores were associated with shorter toilet training durations.

Previous studies support the association between earlier initiation and earlier completion of toilet training. Blum et al. (8) in a prospective study of 406 children, reported that an earlier start of toilet training was correlated with earlier completion, though it was also associated with a longer training duration. Van Aggelpoel et al. (9) in an observational study involving 832 children, found that earlier initiation was associated with earlier completion. The study also revealed that starting toilet training with a developmental assessment shortens the duration of toilet training. These findings suggest that the timing of initiation influences both the age of completion and training duration. In a prospective study by Van Aggelpoel et al. (10) involving 55 participants, intensive training sessions—conducted for 2 hours per day over 2 days at a daycare center—resulted in a shorter toilet training period. Children were eligible for participation if they exhibited at least two of the following three signs: the child expresses a need to evacuate and shows awareness of the need to void or to have a bowel movement; the child insists on completing tasks without help and is proud of new skills; or the child can pull clothes up and down in a toilet training related context.

In a prospective study involving 130 parent-child pairs, Khorasani et al. (11) assessed readiness for toilet training using a checklist covering four domains—physical, cognitive, psychological, and parental readiness—and reported that the use of educational tools, specifically educational videos employing a video modeling approach, resulted in a shorter toilet training period.

Despite these efforts, there remains no consensus regarding the exact number or combination of readiness signs required for initiating toilet training.

Explanations of findings

In our study, the use of the TTIDC in the intervention group did not lead to an earlier start or extended duration of toilet training compared with the control group. However, children in the intervention group achieved toilet training completion at a significantly younger age. This suggests that the decision chart may be effective in facilitating timely and successful toilet training without unnecessarily hastening initiation or prolonging the training process. The intervention group completed toilet training approximately 3.3 months earlier than the control group. Although the clinical importance of this difference cannot be definitively established because no validated threshold for a clinically meaningful difference in toilet-training completion age has been established, earlier completion may reduce the duration of diaper use and the burden on caregivers and childcare providers.

Largo et al. (12,13) in longitudinal studies involving 413 and 309 healthy children, emphasized that urinary continence is primarily determined by physiological maturation, rather than by the early or intensive initiation of toilet training. The decision chart used in our study was designed to assess a child’s readiness based on bladder capacity, mental development, and cognitive awareness of urination—factors closely related to maturity. Therefore, the chart likely contributed to optimizing the timing of training, enabling earlier completion without increasing its duration. In addition to the developmental mechanisms discussed above, alternative explanations should also be considered. The intervention group received not only the TTIDC but also a structured toilet-training approach, which may have increased caregiver attentiveness and promoted more systematic prompting. These factors may have contributed to the observed earlier completion. Furthermore, intervention fidelity and the reliability of outcome assessments were not formally evaluated. Therefore, the relative contribution of the decision chart itself cannot be distinguished from that of the alternative factors.

Our findings are also supported by Jansson et al. (14) who demonstrated in a longitudinal study of 59 children that awareness of voiding begins around 1.5 years of age and that bladder capacity increases substantially between 2 and 3 years. The median age of toilet training initiation in our intervention group was 35.5 months, aligning with the expected period of increased bladder capacity.

Implications and actions needed

In our study, motor skills such as sitting and walking were assumed to have already been achieved by participants aged ≥18 months and were not included in the TTIDC. Instead, the chart focused on three critical factors: bladder function (e.g., voiding interval), mental development (e.g., ability to participate in group activities), and cognitive awareness (e.g., ability to express the need to urinate), which are closely aligned with developmental readiness.

Future studies should include a larger number of cases and incorporate observations of children’s behavior in the home environment.

Conclusions

The TTIDC appears to be a useful tool for assessing a child’s readiness for toilet training by evaluating bladder capacity, mental maturity, and cognitive awareness. Use of the chart may be associated with completion of toilet training at a younger age, without prematurely accelerating initiation or unnecessarily prolonging the process.

Future studies with extended observation periods are warranted to further validate the efficacy of the decision chart and explore its broader application.
