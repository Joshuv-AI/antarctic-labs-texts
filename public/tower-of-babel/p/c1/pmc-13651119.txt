Acute type A aortic dissection with Neri type C avulsion of the right coronary artery in late pregnancy: a case report
Authors: Fei Sun, Sen Mao, Kangjun Fan, Yuntao Cheng, Lijun Gan, Fanhua Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute Stanford type A aortic dissection in late pregnancy is rare but life-threatening, and Neri type C avulsion of the right coronary artery further increases perioperative risk. This case highlights the importance of early recognition and coordinated sequential management.Case presentationA 24-year-old woman at 38 weeks and 3 days of gestation presented with sudden chest and back pain. Echocardiography and aortic magnetic resonance imaging confirmed acute Stanford type A aortic dissection with suspected branch-vessel involvement. After multidisciplinary assessment, she underwent lower-segment cesarean delivery with obstetric hemostatic procedures, followed by Sun procedure, ascending aortic replacement, and coronary artery bypass grafting for Neri type C avulsion of the right coronary artery. The mother and infant recovered well, and no recurrent symptoms were observed during follow-up.ConclusionsIn late pregnancy, sudden chest and back pain should prompt evaluation for acute aortic dissection, especially when vascular or echocardiographic warning signs are present. In this high-risk case, timely multidisciplinary sequencing of cesarean delivery, obstetric hemostasis, and aortic repair was critical to achieving favorable maternal and neonatal outcomes.

Background

Acute aortic dissection (AAD) is a cardiovascular emergency with sudden onset and high mortality, with a global annual incidence of approximately 3 to 5 per 100,000 persons [1]. Acute Stanford type A aortic dissection is a rare but fatal pregnancy-related complication, with a reported incidence during pregnancy of 0.0004% [2]. Pregnancy is an established risk factor for AAD, and the increased risk is mediated by several mechanisms [3]. Gestational hypertension may act as a precipitating factor; the hyperdynamic circulatory state increases aortic shear stress; and hormone-related fragmentation of elastic fibers and degradation of the extracellular matrix in the aortic media further weaken the structural integrity and tensile strength of the aortic wall [3, 4]. This condition threatens both maternal and fetal survival. Without timely intervention, maternal mortality increases by 1% to 3% per hour [5, 6]. We report a severe case of acute Stanford type A aortic dissection in late pregnancy complicated by Neri type C avulsion of the right coronary artery, successfully managed with sequential multidisciplinary surgery.

Case presentation

A 24-year-old woman at 38 weeks and 3 days of gestation was admitted to the emergency department of the Affiliated Hospital of Jining Medical University on December 16, 2022, because of sudden persistent chest and back pain for 4 h. The pain radiated to the back and was accompanied by nausea and two episodes of vomiting. The admission electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm at approximately 75 beats/min, without significant ST-segment elevation or depression in the recorded leads suggestive of acute myocardial ischemia (Fig. 1A). According to the patient and her family, she had previously received a clinical diagnosis of Marfan syndrome at another institution. However, the original diagnostic 1records, including details of phenotypic assessment and any genetic testing, were unavailable for review. She reported no known family history of sudden death or aortic dissection. On admission, she was alert and hemodynamically stable. Her temperature was 36.8 °C, respiratory rate was 16 breaths/min, pulse was 75 beats/min, and blood pressure was 121/56 mm Hg. Breath sounds were coarse bilaterally, without dry or moist rales. Heart rhythm was regular, and no pathologic murmur was heard over the precordium. The abdomen was soft and nontender, without rebound tenderness. Pulsations of the dorsalis pedis arteries were diminished bilaterally. Obstetric examination showed a singleton fetus in breech presentation, fetal heart rate of 132 beats/min, intact membranes, and no uterine contractions.

Plain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the thoracic and abdominal aorta showed findings consistent with Stanford type A aortic dissection, with possible involvement of the right brachiocephalic trunk (Fig. 2A and B). Echocardiography confirmed Stanford type A aortic dissection with dilatation of the ascending aorta and aortic sinus, mild to moderate aortic regurgitation, and left ventricular enlargement (Fig. 2F and G).

After consultation among cardiovascular surgery, obstetrics, and anesthesiology teams, acute type A aortic dissection at 38 + 3 weeks’ gestation was confirmed. The patient was placed on strict bed rest, the condition was explained to the family, and contraindications to surgery were excluded. On December 16, 2022, multidisciplinary sequential surgery was performed under combined intravenous and inhalational general anesthesia. A lower-segment cesarean delivery was performed first. A live male infant was delivered in breech presentation, with no gross developmental abnormality. Apgar scores were 7 at 1 min and 9 at both 5 and 10 min. The neonate was diagnosed with mild asphyxia, received oxygen by mask, and was transferred to the neonatal intensive care unit.

Because of poor uterine contraction, continuous uterine massage was performed. A modified uterine sling suture was placed, and bilateral ligation of the ascending branches of the uterine arteries was performed to prevent postpartum hemorrhage. After adequate uterine hemostasis had been confirmed, the uterus was preserved. Systemic heparinization for cardiopulmonary bypass was then initiated, and cardiac surgery proceeded. Cardiopulmonary bypass was established via right axillary arterial cannulation and right atrial venous cannulation. The patient was cooled to 28 °C. After the three supra-aortic branches were clamped, bilateral selective antegrade cerebral perfusion was provided through the right axillary artery and left common carotid artery. The cardiopulmonary bypass, aortic cross-clamp, and circulatory arrest times were 299, 133, and 24 min, respectively.

Intraoperatively, the ascending aorta and aortic root were enlarged, and the dissection involved the ascending aorta, aortic arch, and descending aorta. After the ascending aorta was opened, a transverse intimal tear of approximately 3 cm was identified on the anterior wall at the sinotubular junction. The dissection extended toward the aortic root. Although the preoperative electrocardiogram did not show definite ischemic changes, direct intraoperative inspection revealed complete avulsion of the right coronary artery, consistent with Neri type C involvement. According to the Neri classification, type C involvement is characterized by circumferential detachment or complete avulsion of the coronary artery from the aortic wall [7]. The proximal right coronary artery was ligated. A long segment of great saphenous vein was harvested from the right lower extremity and anastomosed to the right coronary artery using continuous 7-0 Prolene sutures. Given the complete avulsion and the need for proximal ligation, coronary artery bypass grafting to the right coronary artery was performed to provide reliable perfusion of the right coronary territory. Postoperatively, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit and received anticoagulation under close monitoring for uterine and surgical-site bleeding, together with antimicrobial therapy, heart-rate control, fluid management, inotropic support, myocardial protection, correction of anemia, and other supportive treatment. No clinically significant postpartum uterine bleeding or surgical bleeding requiring re-exploration occurred during hospitalization.At discharge, aspirin enteric-coated tablets (100 mg once daily) were prescribed. The ICU length of stay was 9 days, and the total hospital length of stay was 21 days. Pathologic examination of the aortic wall showed cystic medial necrosis, dissecting aneurysm, and mural thrombus (Fig. 2C). Postoperative aortic computed tomographic angiography showed a well-positioned stent in the descending portion of the aortic arch, with no contrast extravasation (Figs. 1B and C and 2D and E). During regular postoperative follow-up, the patient had no recurrence of chest or back pain and recovered well. Her son is now 3 years old and has normal growth and development. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report.

Discussion and conclusions

Pregnancy-related AAD reflects the interaction between increased hemodynamic load and pregnancy-related changes in the aortic media, which make late gestation and the peripartum period particularly vulnerable [3, 5, 8, 9]. In this patient, sudden chest and back pain, diminished pedal pulses, aortic regurgitation, and ascending aortic dilatation prompted urgent evaluation. Echocardiography identified the proximal lesion, and MRI further defined the extent of dissection without ionizing radiation. In late pregnancy, these findings should not be attributed solely to obstetric discomfort; prompt multidisciplinary assessment is essential [10–13].

The key therapeutic issue was the sequence of surgery. Because the fetus was mature at 38 weeks and 3 days of gestation, direct aortic surgery under cardiopulmonary bypass and systemic heparinization would have exposed the fetus to substantial risk. Cesarean delivery before aortic repair was therefore appropriate and consistent with management principles for late-pregnancy Stanford type AAAD [12, 13]. The additional obstetric hemostatic procedures were not merely adjunctive; they reduced the risk of catastrophic postpartum bleeding during the subsequent cardiac operation, when systemic heparinization was necessary.

Coronary involvement in acute type A aortic dissection should be managed according to the extent of anatomical injury rather than by a uniform reconstructive strategy. Local coronary ostial repair or coronary reimplantation may be appropriate for Neri type A lesions and selected type B lesions when the coronary intima and peri-ostial tissue remain suitable for repair. By contrast, coronary artery bypass grafting is generally favored when there is complete avulsion, extensive intimal disruption, marked tissue fragility, or uncertainty that local repair can provide durable antegrade coronary perfusion [7, 14–17].

Technical decision-making is necessarily guided by direct intraoperative inspection. In the present case, the absence of definite ischemic changes on the admission electrocardiogram did not exclude severe right coronary injury, which was recognized only during surgical exploration. This finding supports careful assessment of the coronary ostia after the aorta is opened, particularly when aortic root involvement is present. A major technical pitfall is to rely on local repair when the ostial tissue is detached or fragile, because an anatomically unstable repair may compromise coronary perfusion after reperfusion. Conversely, a stable ostial repair or reimplantation may avoid unnecessary bypass in selected less complex lesions [14–17].

In this patient, complete avulsion of the right coronary artery at its aortic origin and the need for proximal ligation made coronary artery bypass grafting the appropriate revascularization strategy. A long segment of great saphenous vein was anastomosed to the right coronary artery to establish an alternative source of coronary blood supply while ascending aortic replacement and total arch reconstruction were completed. Thus, CABG was selected because of the anatomy of this Neri type C lesion rather than as an additional routine procedure. The lesion and revascularization strategy are summarized in Fig. 3.

Although pregnancy-related Stanford type A AAD carries a high overall mortality risk, adverse outcomes are not inevitable. The favorable maternal and neonatal outcomes in this case were not attributable to one factor alone. They depended on early recognition, rapid anatomic classification, multidisciplinary coordination between obstetric and cardiac surgical teams, appropriate sequencing of delivery and aortic repair, and active control of hemorrhagic risk [11–13]. At 24 years of age, the pathological finding of cystic medial necrosis and the reported prior clinical diagnosis of Marfan syndrome raise concern for an underlying heritable thoracic aortic disease. However, because the information regarding the prior diagnosis was provided only by the patient and her family, and the original records from the outside institution were unavailable, we could not independently verify the diagnostic criteria, detailed phenotypic assessment, or any genetic testing results. Accordingly, molecular confirmation of Marfan syndrome and formal assessment for other heritable thoracic aortic diseases, including Loeys-Dietz syndrome, could not be reported. This is a limitation of the present case. Long-term follow-up should include blood-pressure control, surveillance of the repaired and residual aorta, and consideration of genetic counseling and comprehensive evaluation for heritable thoracic aortic disease.

This report is limited by its single-case design. Although the coronary revascularization technique is established, this case provides a focused clinical illustration of lesion-directed management of complete right coronary artery avulsion and multidisciplinary sequential care in late pregnancy. In late pregnancy, sudden chest and back pain with vascular or echocardiographic warning signs should prompt immediate evaluation for aortic dissection. In selected late-pregnancy cases, timely coordination of cesarean delivery, obstetric hemostasis, and aortic repair may contribute to favorable maternal and neonatal outcomes.
