Associations Between Serum Adipokine, Antipsychotic Exposure, and Clinical Characteristics in Chronic Schizophrenia: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Yasuhiro Ohno, Kiyomi Saito, Keita Hanabusa, Ayaka Nakai, Tomoko Nagai, Noriko Hida, Kenji Sanada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPrevious meta‐analyses have reported lower serum adiponectin levels in patients with schizophrenia treated with second‐generation antipsychotics. However, the relationships among serum adipokines, psychiatric symptoms, and antipsychotic use remain unclear. This study aimed to clarify associations between serum adipokines, psychiatric symptoms, and antipsychotic treatment in patients with chronic schizophrenia compared with healthy controls.MethodsThe study included 37 inpatients with schizophrenia and 39 age‐ and gender‐matched healthy controls. Fasting blood samples were collected, and serum adipokine levels were measured using enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assays. Psychiatric symptoms were assessed with the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS), and antipsychotic doses were converted to chlorpromazine equivalents. Correlations were examined using regression and multivariate analyses, including adjustment for body mass index (BMI).ResultsNo significant case–control differences were observed in total or high‐molecular weight adiponectin levels. Female patients had a significantly higher leptin/adiponectin ratio than female controls (p = 0.041). In the schizophrenia group, adiponectin levels were positively correlated with chlorpromazine equivalents (r = 0.684, p < 0.001). This association remained statistically significant after adjustment for BMI, gender and hospitalization period. Adiponectin levels were not significantly associated with psychiatric symptom severity.ConclusionIn this cross‐sectional sample, serum adiponectin levels are associated with antipsychotic dosage but not with symptom severity. These findings suggest that adipokine regulation in patients with schizophrenia may be influenced by treatment‐related and demographic factors. Longitudinal studies incorporating comprehensive metabolic assessments are needed to explore the clinical significance of these relationships.

Introduction

Schizophrenia is a severe psychiatric disorder that affects approximately 1% of the global population. This disorder is characterized by positive symptoms, such as delusions, hallucinations, and disorganized behavior, as well as negative symptoms, including reduced motivation, emotional flatness, and social withdrawal [1]. Patients with schizophrenia often experience metabolic disturbances such as obesity, dyslipidemia, and hypertension, which can be exacerbated by second‐generation antipsychotic drugs [2, 3]. These metabolic issues can negatively impact physical health and are correlated with the severity of psychiatric symptoms [4, 5], highlighting the need for comprehensive treatment and care to manage metabolic abnormalities and improve psychiatric symptoms, enabling a better quality of life for patients with schizophrenia.

Adipokines, including adiponectin, leptin, and resistin, are hormones that are primarily secreted by adipose tissue and that play a key role in regulating metabolism, appetite regulation, and inflammatory responses [6, 7]. Adiponectin has anti‐inflammatory properties that may inhibit neuroinflammation and provide neuroprotection [8, 9]. Leptin, which is essential for appetite and energy balance, is associated with obesity and insulin resistance. Dysregulated secretion of adipokines contributes to metabolic disorders such as diabetes and hypertension and plays a role in the pathophysiology of metabolic syndrome [10]. Recent studies have suggested a link between adipokines and psychiatric disorders, including schizophrenia [6, 11]. Abnormal adipokine levels have been closely linked to both psychiatric symptoms and metabolic disturbances in schizophrenia; high adiponectin levels may be correlated with reduced psychiatric symptoms [12], and low leptin levels may contribute to impaired appetite regulation and worsened metabolic profiles, particularly in patients with prominent negative symptoms [13, 14]. Additionally, second‐generation antipsychotic drugs such as olanzapine and clozapine tend to reduce adiponectin and increase leptin, which may exacerbate obesity, insulin resistance, and cardiovascular risk [15]. In contrast, it has been reported that antipsychotics such as aripiprazole and ziprasidone do not appear to have a strong effect on the adipokine profile [16]. Taken together, these findings indicate that further research is needed to examine the relationship among psychiatric symptoms, medication use, and adipokine levels [12, 17, 18].

In the present study, we investigated the levels of serum adipokines in patients with chronic schizophrenia compared with healthy controls and examined the relationship among adipokine profiles, severity of psychiatric symptoms, and antipsychotic drug use. Based on the hypothesis that greater severity of illness is associated with a decline in daily functioning, damage to physical health, progressive adipokine dysregulation, and the need for higher doses of antipsychotics in patients with schizophrenia, we aimed to contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the metabolic risks involved in schizophrenia.

Methods

Participants

This study was registered in the UMIN Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN000051974), and the study protocol was approved by the Ethical Committee of Showa Medical University Karasuyama Hospital (B‐2019‐039). This study was conducted prospectively between March 26, 2020 and March 31, 2024. All participants were provided with a comprehensive explanation of the study objectives and procedures, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant.

A total of 40 inpatients diagnosed with schizophrenia were recruited at Showa Medical University Karasuyama Hospital in Tokyo. The individuals' current symptom severity was evaluated using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) [19]. The total dosage of antipsychotic medications was converted to chlorpromazine equivalent doses according to previously published guidelines [20]. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients aged between 20 and 60 years at the time of obtaining consent and (2) patients who met the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM‐5) diagnosis of schizophrenia. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with any cerebral organic mental disorders, drug or alcohol abuse/dependence, cancer, inflammatory bowel diseases, heart diseases, liver diseases, chronic kidney diseases, or chronic obstructive pulmonary diseases; (2) patients who currently smoke or who quit smoking within the previous year; (3) patients with symptoms of xerostomia; (4) patients undergoing treatment for allergic diseases (e.g., asthma or atopic dermatitis); or (5) patients with psychiatric symptoms that might worsen as a result of participating in this trial.

For the healthy control group, 41 participants were recruited through a volunteer bank to ensure age and gender matching with the patient group. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) individuals aged between 20 and 60 years at the time of obtaining consent; (2) individuals who provided written informed consent to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) individuals with a history of medical conditions that might affect the assessment of the study or the safety of the participant, such as drug or alcohol abuse/dependence, cancer, inflammatory bowel diseases, heart diseases, liver diseases, chronic kidney diseases, or chronic obstructive pulmonary diseases; (2) individuals diagnosed with any psychiatric disorder based on a structured clinical interview conducted by a psychiatrist; (3) individuals with symptoms of xerostomia; (4) individuals who currently smoke or who quit smoking within the previous year; (5) individuals who currently have symptoms of allergic diseases (e.g., asthma or atopic dermatitis) and are taking medication to treat them; (6) individuals deemed ineligible by the study physician for any other reason.

Blood Collection

To synchronize blood collection times, blood samples were collected between 8:00 and 10:00 am after overnight fasting in both groups. Samples were drawn from the forearm vein using a vacuum blood collection tube. Within 30 min post collection, the samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min to extract serum. The serum was divided into 1 mL portions, immediately frozen at −80°C, and analyzed within 6 months. In addition to standard clinical blood draws, an extra 5 mL of blood was collected for research purposes.

Measurement of Metabolic and Stress Biomarkers

The levels of triglycerides, total cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, and HbA1c were measured using the analyzer (BioMajesty JCA‐BM6010/C). Serum levels of total adiponectin, high molecular weight–adiponectin, leptin, resistin, TNF‐α, IL‐6, and IL‐1β were measured using enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay kits (R&D Systems Inc., Minneapolis, MN) according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were presented as medians and interquartile ranges. The significance of differences in demographics and serum biomarkers between the groups was assessed using the non‐parametric Mann–Whitney U test for independent samples. Linear regression analyses were conducted to examine the relationship between serum adiponectin levels and psychiatric symptoms in patients with schizophrenia. In these analyses, the serum adiponectin level was set as the dependent variable, and the PANSS total, positive, negative, and general psychopathology scores were set as independent variables, and the serum adiponectin level was set as the dependent variable. Similarly, linear regression analyses were performed to examine the relationship between serum adiponectin levels and antipsychotic dose expressed as chlorpromazine equivalents, including stratified analyses by gender and use of multi‐acting receptor‐targeted antipsychotics (MARTA).

Subsequently, multivariable linear analyses were conducted to determine factors associated with serum levels of adiponectin and high molecular weight (HMW)‐adiponectin. In these models, serum adiponectin and HMW‐adiponectin measures were treated as dependent variables, and gender, chlorpromazine equivalence, hospitalization period, and body mass index (BMI) were used as independent variables.

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS 25.0. A p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Overall Study Characteristics

The study involved 40 inpatients with schizophrenia who were admitted to Showa Medical University Karasuyama Hospital in Tokyo, as well as 41 healthy controls from the same metropolitan area. Three patients with schizophrenia and two healthy controls were excluded from the study because they did not meet the inclusion criteria, because they withdrew from the study, or because of clinical considerations. After applying all exclusion criteria, the final analysis included 37 patients with schizophrenia and 39 healthy controls. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1. Female patients with schizophrenia had a higher body mass index (BMI) than healthy female controls (p = 0.018), and male patients with schizophrenia were significantly younger than healthy male controls (p = 0.027). No other significant demographic differences were observed between groups. BMI values in the schizophrenia group were relatively modest, with median values of approximately 24 kg/m2 in both sexes. Association Between Serum Adiponectin Levels and Psychiatric Symptoms

There were no significant correlations between serum adiponectin levels (total or HMW) and PANSS total scores, positive scores, negative scores, and general psychopathology scores in the patient group. In sex‐stratified analyses, a non‐significant trend toward a positive correlation between total adiponectin levels and PANSS total scores was observed in male patients (r = 0.384, p = 0.078). No other significant associations were detected.

Association Between Serum Adiponectin Levels and Antipsychotic Dosage

Figure 1 illustrates a positive association between serum adiponectin levels and chlorpromazine equivalence among patients (r = 0.353, p = 0.032 for total adiponectin; r = 0.364, p = 0.023 for HMW adiponectin). This positive correlation was stronger in male patients (total adiponectin: r = 0.642, p = 0.001), whereas no significant correlations were detected in female patients. To evaluate whether this association was independent of adiposity, multivariable linear regression analyses were conducted including BMI as a covariate, together with gender and hospitalization period. After adjustment, chlorpromazine equivalence remained significantly associated with adiponectin levels (total adiponectin: β = 0.002, p = 0.018; HMW adiponectin: β = 0.002, p = 0.013). BMI showed a negative but non‐significant association with adiponectin (total: p = 0.104; HMW: p = 0.078). These findings suggest that the observed association between antipsychotic dose and adiponectin was not fully explained by BMI in this study.

Correlation Between Serum Adiponectin Levels and Use of Multi‐Acting Receptor‐Targeted Antipsychotics (MARTA)

As shown in Figure 2, there was a positive association between serum adiponectin levels and chlorpromazine equivalence (r = 0.684, p < 0.001) in patients treated with MARTA (n = 22). Similarly, a positive relationship was observed among the 18 male patients in this group (r = 0.554, p = 0.017). However, no significant correlation was detected in female patients. Given the limited sample size and pharmacological heterogeneity within the MARTA category, these subgroup findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Multivariable Analyses of Factors Associated With Adiponectin Levels

Table 3 shows the results of a multivariate analysis of serum levels of adiponectin and high molecular weight (HMW)‐adiponectin. After adjustment for covariates including BMI, gender, chlorpromazine equivalence, and hospitalization period, serum levels of both total adiponectin and HMW‐adiponectin were significantly associated with gender (p = < 0.001 for both), chlorpromazine equivalence (total: p = 0.018; HMW: p = 0.013), and hospitalization period (total: p = 0.010; HMW: p = 0.007). BMI showed a negative but non‐significant association with adiponectin levels (total: p = 0.104; HMW: p = 0.078). No evidence of problematic multicollinearity was observed.

Discussion

In this exploratory study, we examined the relationships between serum adipokines, psychiatric symptoms, and antipsychotics in patients with chronic schizophrenia compared with healthy controls. No significant case–control differences were observed in total or HMW adiponectin levels; however, we found that serum leptin levels and leptin/adiponectin ratios were higher in female patients than in female controls. Within the schizophrenia group, serum adiponectin levels showed a positive correlation with antipsychotic dosage, including in patients using multi‐acting receptor‐targeted antipsychotics (MARTA). Importantly, this association remained statistically significant after adjustment for BMI, gender, and hospitalization period. No significant associations were detected between serum adiponectin levels and psychiatric symptoms. In multivariable analyses, serum adiponectin levels were associated with gender, antipsychotic dosage, and hospitalization period.

First, we found that female schizophrenia patients showed higher serum leptin levels and leptin/adiponectin ratios compared with female healthy controls. These results are consistent with a previous study that reported elevated leptin levels in patients with schizophrenia, particularly in female patients, compared with healthy controls, even after adjusting for confounding variables such as body mass index, age, and type of antipsychotic medication [21]. Leptin is a hormone produced by adipocytes that plays an important role in energy homeostasis and neuroendocrine regulation [22, 23]. The leptin/adiponectin ratio has been proposed as a composite index reflecting metabolic balance [24, 25]. Although our cross‐sectional design does not allow conclusions regarding metabolic risk, the elevated leptin/adiponectin ratio observed in female patients may suggest altered metabolic or adipokine regulation in this subgroup. We also found that female schizophrenia patients showed higher serum TNF‐α levels compared with female healthy controls. This finding is consistent with previous studies that reported elevated TNF‐α levels in patients with schizophrenia [26, 27, 28]. TNF‐α is a pro‐inflammatory adipokine closely associated with metabolic abnormalities, insulin resistance, and chronic low‐grade inflammation [29]. The sex‐specific elevation observed in our sample may reflect interactions among chronic illness, antipsychotic treatment, and sex‐related biological factors, including hormonal influences [30]. However, the mechanisms underlying these differences remain uncertain and warrant further studies.

Second, we found a positive correlation between adiponectin levels and antipsychotic dosage, while serum adiponectin levels were not significantly associated with the severity of psychiatric symptoms. This finding appears to contradict the established relationship that metabolic abnormalities generally cause a decrease in adiponectin levels and an increase in leptin levels [25, 31]. Therefore, the observed relationship should be interpreted cautiously. Importantly, in multivariable analyses including BMI, the association between antipsychotic dose and adiponectin remained statistically significant, while BMI showed a negative but non‐significant association. This suggests that the observed relationship was not fully explained by adiposity in this relatively lean inpatient study. However, given the cross‐sectional design and the absence of detailed metabolic measures (e.g., insulin resistance indices or body fat distribution), residual confounding cannot be excluded. This positive relationship may not indicate an improvement in metabolic status but rather reflect heterogeneity or stage‐dependent responses of adipokines to antipsychotics. Although second‐generation antipsychotic drugs, particularly MARTA, are well known to be associated with metabolic dysregulation through both central and peripheral mechanism [32], the specific relationship between antipsychotic exposure and circulating adiponectin levels in humans remains poorly understood. The subgroup findings in MARTA‐treated patients should be interpreted cautiously due to limited sample size and pharmacological heterogeneity. Overall, these findings highlight the complexity of adipokine regulation in patients treated with antipsychotic medication and emphasize the need for longitudinal studies incorporating comprehensive metabolic assessments to explore the temporal and mechanistic associations.

Finally, in multivariable analyses, we found that serum adiponectin levels were associated with gender, antipsychotic dosage, and length of hospitalization. This suggests that both patient‐specific factors (e.g., gender) and treatment‐related factors (e.g., dose and duration of medication) may impact on total adiponectin and HMW‐adiponectin levels [25, 33]. However, hospitalization duration likely reflects multiple overlapping factors, including chronicity, cumulative medication exposure, reduced physical activity, and environmental influences, rather than direct biological effect. Although low levels of adiponectin are generally found in individuals with high levels of visceral fat and insulin resistance [34], the present cross‐sectional result do not allow us to determine whether the long‐term use of antipsychotic drugs directly alters adiponectin regulation through these mechanisms. The length of hospitalization may therefore reflect broader clinical and environmental influences rather than a discrete metabolic mechanism. Taken together, the current results highlight the importance of ongoing metabolic monitoring in patients with schizophrenia receiving antipsychotic treatment. However, the role of adiponectin as a clinical monitoring biomarker requires further validation in longitudinal studies incorporating direct measures of insulin resistance and body fat distribution.

The present study involved several limitations that should be noted. First, the relatively limited sample size and cross‐sectional design impede the capacity to draw definitive inferences regarding causality. Although we conducted multivariable analyses including BMI, residual confounding cannot be excluded, particularly because detailed metabolic measures such as insulin resistance indices, visceral fat distribution, and longitudinal metabolic changes were not assessed. Second, the effect size of the primary case–control comparison for total adiponectin was extremely small (Cohen's d < 0.1), suggesting that adiponectin did not act as a robust discriminator between patients and controls in this study. Third, patients were recruited exclusively from inpatients at a single hospital, whereas healthy controls were recruited from a volunteer bank. This difference in recruitment sources may have introduced selection bias and limits the generalizability of the findings, particularly to community‐based or metabolically high‐risk populations. Fourth, subgroup analyses (including sex‐stratified and MARTA‐treated groups) were underpowered and should be considered exploratory. In addition, the MARTA category included pharmacologically heterogeneous drugs, making it impossible to draw drug‐specific conclusions. Finally, we also did not consider lifestyle factors (e.g., diet, physical activity, and stress), partially hormonal influences (e.g., menopausal status), and antipsychotic medications or treatment history, which could impact biomarkers, including adipokine levels [6, 35, 36].

Conclusions

In this exploratory cross‐sectional study, we examined the relationship between serum adipokine levels, psychiatric symptoms, and antipsychotic medication in patients with chronic schizophrenia. No significant case–control differences were observed in adiponectin levels; however, female patients showed higher leptin levels and leptin/adiponectin ratios compared with female healthy controls. Within the schizophrenia group, serum adiponectin levels were positively associated with antipsychotic doses, and this association remained significant after adjustment for BMI and other covariates.

These results suggest that adipokine regulation in patients receiving antipsychotic treatment may be influenced by gender and treatment‐related factors. However, given the limited sample size, cross‐sectional design, and lack of detailed metabolic measures, the clinical implications of these associations remain uncertain.

Therefore, future longitudinal studies incorporating comprehensive metabolic assessments and diverse patient populations are required to explore the long‐term associations between antipsychotic medications and adipokine dynamics.
