Unconventional Natural Gas Charging and Enrichment Characteristics in Coal-Measure Tight Reservoirs of Southeast Ordos Basin, North China
Authors: Shuangbiao Han, Jie Huang, Xiaoyan Mu, Mengxia Huo, Jinchuan Zhang, Yongping Wan, Zhenchuan Wang, Liang Qiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Coal-measure unconventional natural gas resources are widespread globally. However, due to the diverse types of reservoirs, thin interbedding, and complex pore-fracture system structures, their exploration and production face challenges. This study aims at the core scientific problem of unclear enrichment mechanism of unconventional natural gas in coal-measure tight reservoirs in southeastern Ordos Basin. The dynamic gas–water distribution characteristics and controlling factors of coal, mudstone, sandstone, and limestone reservoirs were systematically analyzed by gas charging simulation experiment based on nuclear magnetic resonance combined with single- and multifractal theory. The results show that the gas saturation of coal and mudstone is significantly limited (12.10–34.52%) due to small pores and strong heterogeneity. Due to the advantages of large pores and connectivity, sandstone and limestone have significantly higher gas saturation (sandstone reaches 42.68–51.19%). Quantitative analysis of mineral composition revealed a strong positive correlation between quartz content and gas saturation (R 2 = 0.84), while clay minerals inhibited gas occurrence (R 2 = 0.64). Organic geochemical analysis further confirms that the gas saturation of coal is controlled by organic matter maturity and total organic carbon, while the gas saturation of sandstone depends on the large pore volume of >0.1 μm and pore throat connectivity. Nearby coal or mudstone with high organic matter content, reservoirs with effective pore volume of >0.1 μm, and low clay mineral content may be natural gas enrichment desert areas. This study reveals the charging and enrichment law of unconventional natural gas in multitype tight reservoirs of coal measures and provides theoretical support for the prediction of gas–water distribution in complex heterogeneous reservoirs and the exploration of unconventional natural gas in coal-measure tight reservoirs.

Introduction

Coal-measure gas resources represent a transitional energy source and strategic resource critical to energy security, environmental protection, and regional economy. − This resource refers to natural gas from coalbed methane in coal seams and its adjacent tight rock reservoirs, encompassing coalbed methane, tight sandstone gas, and coal-measure shale gas. , The gas from these reservoirs has a high calorific value, and its combustion produces minimal emissions, representing a premium clean energy source. − These gas resources occur in deep coal basins, such as Canada’s Laramide Basin, the United States’s Piceance Basin, and China’s Ordos Basin. − Commercial production was achieved in 2003 when 60% of Piceance Basin’s gas output originated from coal seams, followed by the successful exploitation of the Alberta Basin. − More than 30% of China’s unconventional natural gas reserves reside in coal measures, extensive coal-measure strata spanning multiple geological periods and basins. , Thus, advancing tight reservoir research has a global significance for energy exploration.

Coal-measure gas occupies different states. Most of the methane is adsorbed in coalbed methane. , Tight sandstone gas primarily occurs as a free phase, and coal-measure shale gas is characterized by hybrid states. This variability is compounded by heterogeneous reservoir lithologies, where frequent interbedding of rocks with contrasting petrophysical properties creates dynamic source-reservoir-caprock assemblages. Individual strata may serve as sources, reservoirs, or seals, resulting in “triple gas systems” (coalbed methane, tight gas, and shale gas) within a single stratum. , However, reservoir complexities including intricate pore architectures and ultralow permeability create challenging fluid dynamics, particularly in gas–water interactions that impede effective hydrocarbon accumulation. , Field observations have revealed widespread water production issuesexemplified by 594 tight gas wells in Colorado’s Piceance Basin, where the gas rates are inversely correlated with water yields. , Exploration, development, and production demand have encouraged studies of the gas–water relationship, influencing factors, and gas–water charging mechanism of coal-measure tight reservoirs in major basins. , The hydrocarbon generation capacity, transport system, structural amplitude, and reservoir heterogeneity affect the gas–water distribution of reservoirs. − Pore-scale heterogeneity manifested through diverse pore geometries, pore size distributions, and connectivity patterns critically governs gas migration pathways, diffusion processes, and accumulation dynamics in coal-measure systems. −

Many scholars have conducted in-depth research on the enrichment mechanism of unconventional natural gas. Most of them studied by combining logging data, geological structure conditions, source rocks, and basic characteristics of reservoirs, revealing the charging and enrichment law of unconventional natural gas at the macro level, and clarifying the control effect of fault structure and lithologic trap on natural gas enrichment. ,, Some studies also used low field nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) technology in natural gas charging simulations to analyze the characteristics of water in rock pores. , The pore size distribution and gas and water saturation provide information on the characteristics of gas reservoirs. − Zhou et al. used NMR technology and mathematical model research to show that the sweet spot area of tight gas is developed in the rock reservoir near the source rock, but the influence of water and pore heterogeneity on natural gas charging and enrichment is not clearly revealed. Xie et al. mainly used NMR to reveal the effective pore size of natural gas charging and enrichment. At present, the research on the mechanism of natural gas charging and enrichment is mainly aimed at a certain type of reservoir such as tight sandstone and shale, and there is no comparative study on the difference of natural gas charging and enrichment in various types of rock reservoirs (sandstone, shale, and limestone) in coal measures. Many scholars have researched the pore fractal dimension and the influencing factors of fractal dimension for various coal-measure tight reservoirs using the single-fractal and multifractal theories. − However, few researchers have combined nuclear magnetic resonance with single-fractal and multifractal theory to analyze the differences in pore structure between different types of tight reservoirs in coal measures and have not applied it to the study of natural gas charging and enrichment in tight reservoirs in coal measures.

This study utilizes NMR and performs a gas–water charging experiment to investigate the gas saturation and gas–water relationship of multitype coal-measure tight reservoirs in the southeastern Ordos Basin. The single- and multifractal theories were applied to quantify the reservoirs’ pore structure and clarify the gas charging mechanism. These findings provide information for future studies of the gas–water relationship and unconventional gas exploration in coal measures in the region.

Geological Background

The Ordos Basin is situated in the central-western part of the North China Craton; this energy-rich basin contains substantial hydrocarbon reserves. , As a large cratonic depression basin, it has experienced five principal phases of tectonic development that resulted in multiple gas-bearing strata. Featuring an asymmetrical rectangular shape with a narrower western flank and broader eastern side (Figure ), the basin displays relatively uncomplicated structural characteristics. The strata dip to the west in a monocline with a dip angle of less than 1°. , According to the tectonic evolution and current tectonic morphology, the Ordos Basin can be divided into six tectonic units. Regional tectonic evolution has resulted in a ternary sedimentary system (Lower Paleozoic carbonate rock, Upper Paleozoic marine-continental transitional facies coal measures clastic rock and Mesozoic and Cenozoic inland clastic rock) in the basin.

Geological map of the Ordos Basin and location of the research region (a) (modified from ref ). Lithology of the Carboniferous-Permian Shanxi Formation, Taiyuan Formation, and Benxi Formation in the research region (b) (modified from ref ).

The study area is located in the southeast of Yishan slope in Ordos Basin. Affected by the Caledonian movement, the Upper Paleozoic strata lack Devonian and Lower Carboniferous. The Carboniferous Benxi Formation, Permian Shanxi Formation, and Lower Shihezi Formation developed in the Lower Paleozoic are the main coal-bearing strata and unconventional natural gas production layers in the study area. In contrast to northern Ordos’ principal gas fields, the coal-measure tight reservoirs in the research region have high heterogeneity and low porosity and permeability. − The Shanxi-Xiashihezi formations display sedimentary facies transitions from marine to continental deposits, indicating the southeastern basin margin occupied a paralic depositional environment. , The coal-measure tight reservoirs including mudstone, sandstone, limestone, and coal are developed in the region.

Samples and Experimental Methods

Samples

The Upper Paleozoic coal, tight sandstone, limestone, and mudstone reservoirs in the Ordos Basin are frequently interbedded and are coal-measure multitype tight reservoirs. This study selected 37 rock samples from different reservoir types (Table S1 in the Supporting Information A.1) to study the gas charging mechanism. The samples included coal, mudstone, sandstone, conglomerate, and limestone from the Upper Paleozoic Shanxi Formation, Benxi Formation, Taiyuan Formation, and Shihezi Formation.

Methodology

As shown in Figure , this investigation is mainly based on a simulation experiment of unconventional gas charging and fractal theory. Combined with the mineral composition, organic geochemical characteristics (including coal and mudstone samples), and pore structure characteristics of the experimental samples, the dynamic changes of gas–water in unconventional gas charging in coal-measure tight reservoir samples are discussed, and the mechanism of unconventional gas charging and enrichment in coal-measure tight reservoirs is deeply studied.

Methodology of this study.

NMR-Based Gas Charging Simulation Experiment

This investigation used NMR to perform the gas charging simulation experiment using a MesoMR12-150H-I wide-bore NMR analyzer. The 12 typical rock samples had a 2.5 cm diameter and were 4–5 cm long (Figure ). Coal and mudstone are easily broken under a large saturated water and gas charging pressure, which affects the scientific nature of the experimental results. Therefore, the gas charging simulation experimental conditions in this study consider the burial depth and physical properties of different types of rock samples. The experimental parameters were maintained at 25 °C ambient temperature with a 20 MPa saturated water pressure and 15 MPa maximum gas charging pressure. The experimental steps were as follows:(1)The rock sample was transferred into a drying chamber and maintained at 105–110 °C until achieving consistent mass measurements. This thermal stabilization process continued until successive weight measurements showed no detectable variation, confirming complete moisture removal under controlled heating conditions.(2)The rock sample was placed into the measurement chamber, and the T 2 spectrum signal was acquired. The data represented the base signal of the dry sample, and continuous measurements were obtained until the signal was consistent.(3)The rock sample was placed in a vacuum pressure saturation device and immersed in distilled water under vacuum conditions. According to the type of rock sample and the density, the saturated distilled water was pressurized (the maximum pressure was 20 MPa) for 24 h, and the sample mass and volume were measured.(4)The saturated sample was loaded into the measuring chamber with the core holder, and the gas paths at both ends of the core holder were connected. The confining pressure of the holder was 20 MPa. The T 2 spectrum signal of the saturated samples was measured.(5)The samples from the previous step were subjected to gradient stepwise pressure displacement according to the test objectives. Repeated measurements were obtained at the points, and the final result was when the T 2 spectrum stopped changing or the deviation was less than 5%. The T 2 spectra of the samples with different methane displacement pressures were tested, and the final values were recorded.

Typical rock samples.

The T 2 spectrum is a parameter to characterize the distribution of transverse relaxation time. The weight distribution of T 2 relaxation time corresponding to different pore structures in rock reservoirs can be obtained, which can be used to quantitatively analyze the pore fluid distribution and pore throat radius characteristics of rocks. , Studies demonstrate the correlation between T 2 values and pore dimensions through the following equation: , 1 1T2=ρ(SV)=F(ρr) Here, ρ represents the surface relaxation rate (μm/ms) of the surface pores, S/V denotes the specific surface area per pore (μm–1), F corresponds to the geometric shape coefficient determined by the pore morphology, and r indicates the pore radius (μm).

Initially, the water saturation is 100% when the sample was saturated with water. With increasing gas charging pressure, the gas progressively displaced the pore spaces. This process resulted in a gradual decline in the level of water saturation accompanied by a corresponding rise in the level of gas saturation. The water and gas saturation are calculated as follows: , 2 Sw=MiMw×100% 3 Sg=1−Sw In this equation, S w represents the water saturation (%), Mi denotes the water signal, M w is the total semaphore of water, and S g is the gas saturation (%).

Fractal Theory

Single-fractal analysis is a powerful tool for quantifying the pore structure complexity in unconventional hydrocarbon reservoirs. It is used to characterize the pore geometry and spatial distribution. The fractal characteristics of porous systems provide critical insights into their geometric morphology and topological complexity, enabling the prediction and management of fluid flow dynamics, diffusion, and storage capacity. − Natural pore networks, including those in tight sandstones, coal matrices, and shale systems, exhibit fractal characteristics. NMR single-fractal analysis can be used to characterize pore heterogeneity in tight reservoirs. , The cumulative pore volume percentage is calculated as follows: 4 lg(Vp)=(3−DNMR)lg(T2)+(DNMR−3)lg⁡T2⁡max⁡ Here, V p represents the percentage of cumulative pore volume associated with the T 2 spectrum in a fully saturated core sample, T 2max denotes the peak transverse relaxation time measured in milliseconds, and D NMR indicates the comprehensive fractal dimension value describing the saturated pore system’s structural complexity.

Multifractal analysis is an extension of single-fractal analysis. It provides more detailed information about tight reservoir pores. Multifractal analysis transforms the complex pore structure into a quantifiable multiscale index using parameters such as D q , f­(α), α­(q), and Δα, outperforming traditional statistical methods. D q represents the generalized dimension related to q, f­(α) represents the multifractal spectrum, α­(q) refers to the singular intensity, and Δα is the width of the multifractal spectrum. , The expression of the generalized fractal dimension D q is − 5 Dq=limε→0⁡1q−1lg[∑i=1N(ε)Piq(ε)]lgε Here, q represents the order of the statistical moment, P i q (ε) denotes the probability mass distribution, N­(ε) indicates the amount of gas adsorbed in a box, and ε is the box size.

Other Experiments

Unified specifications and experimental test conditions were used to prepare the rock samples from different types of coal-measure tight reservoirs.

For analytical characterization, 5 g aliquots of homogenized coal and mudstone powders sieved through 100-mesh screens underwent total organic carbon (TOC) quantification and pyrolysis evaluation. TOC was determined using a Leco SC-632 instrument according to China GB/T 19145–2003. Rock pyrolysis test was conducted with a Rock-Eval 6 instrument according to China’s GB/T 18602–2012. The maximum vitrinite reflectance (Ro) of the coal and mudstone samples was tested using a microscope photometer.

This study used 5 g samples of powder passed through a 200-mesh sieve and a Bruker AXS D8 Discover to analyze the composition of the whole rock and clay minerals according to China’s SY/T 5163–2010.

Sample material (5 g) was passed through 60–80 mesh sieves to determine the particle size. Structures of micropores and mesopores were investigated through low-temperature gas adsorption experiments employing a Quantachrome NOVA analyzer according to ISO 15901–2:2006 and ISO 15901–3:2007. The pore structure of 0–1.5 nm pores was characterized mainly based on the density functional theory (DFT) model. The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) model and the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) model were used to characterize the pore structure of 1.6∼150 nm pores.

The macropore structure characteristics were evaluated via high-pressure mercury intrusion tests conducted on cubic centimeter-sized samples using an AutoPore IV 9505 porosimeter according to ISO 15901–1:2005.

Results and Discussion

Characteristics of Different Tight Reservoirs in Coal Measures

Organic Geochemical Characteristics

The TOC of coal is high (84.42%), with an average of 71.84%, exceeding 55%. The average Ro and Tmax are 2.95% and 591.91 °C, respectively. The dominant organic matter types are Types II and III humic. The organic matter maturity samples are mature or high-overmature, indicating good gas source rock. − Mudstone samples demonstrate moderate organic richness with mean TOC content of 2.68%, while hydrocarbon generation potential quantified by S1+S2 values averages 0.52 mg/g. The type index (S2/S3) has a large range, with an average of 1.38, indicating Type III humic. The average Ro and Tmax are 2.76% and 511.71 °C, respectively, indicating that the organic matter maturity of the mudstone is high-overmature.

Mineralogical Characteristics

As shown in Figure a, clay minerals are dominant in coal, with an average content of 61.09%, significantly higher than that of brittle minerals. The mudstones consist primarily of quartz 40.36% and clay minerals (38.19%). The dominant clay minerals in coal and mudstone are kaolinite and Illite (Figure b). The average kaolinite and Illite contents are 47.63 and 38.73% in coal and 38.43 and 34.57% in mudstone, respectively. Quartz and clay minerals are dominant in the sandstone. The average quartz content is 69.80%, much higher than the clay mineral content (25.34%). Figure a shows that sandstone and mudstone have a higher quartz content than coal. Quartz is a high-maturity mineral with strong compaction resistance during sediment burial, preserving primary pores in the reservoir. Additionally, rocks with high quartz contents are prone to microfractures, facilitating the migration of formation fluids and the dissolution of rocks to create secondary pores. Therefore, areas with a high quartz content generally have better reservoir properties. The limestone mineral composition is dominated by calcite, with an average of 97.87%. Calcite has a dual effect on the physical properties of the reservoir. Generally, carbonate cementation can reduce reservoir permeability, and a higher calcite content degrades reservoir properties. However, an appropriate amount of calcite can have positive effects on the reservoir.

Mineral composition (a) and clay components (b) of the rock samples.

Pore Structure

As shown in Figure , the coal contains Organic pores and microcracks. The mudstone has more intergranular pores than the coal. Sandstone and limestone contain high contents of brittle minerals; thus, their intergranular pores and microcracks are well developed. The low-temperature N2 adsorption analysis identifies that the rock samples have fractured pores and parallel plate pores. The trends of the adsorption curves are similar for mudstone and sandstone. The mercury injection and withdrawal curves for samples from different lithologies have different shapes. The breakthrough pressure in the mercury intrusion experiment refers to the pressure point when mercury begins to enter the rock pores in large quantities, which can be used to characterize the sorting of the pores and roars. The breakthrough pressure of the rock samples is low. The mercury withdrawal curve of the coal samples is relatively flat, and the pore throat deviation is coarse and crooked. The average mercury withdrawal efficiency is 68.83%, and the displacement pressure is less than 0.5 MPa. This shows that the pores and roars of coal rock samples are well sorted.. The average mercury removal efficiency of the mudstone samples is 80.704%, and the average displacement pressure is 13.78 MPa. Although the mudstone samples have high pore connectivity, their pore throats are insufficiently developed. The mercury injection curve of the sandstone sample has an upward trend, and the displacement pressure is 0.4–0.7 MPa, indicating that the sandstone sample has the optimum particle size distribution and good seepage ability among the rock samples. However, its average mercury removal efficiency is 53.03% and the pore connectivity is relatively low. The average mercury removal efficiency of the limestone sample is 71.80%, and the displacement pressure is 5.498–48.237 MPa. Its pore structure differs from that of the other samples. The breakthrough pressure of coal and sandstone is obviously lower than that of mudstone and limestone, which indicates that the better the sorting of the pore throat, the more favorable the gas seepage.

The storage space provided by pores of 100–1,00,000 nm is larger in coal than in mudstone due to more microfractures and fissures in coal. The main pores of sandstone and limestone are mesopores and macropores. The pore size of sandstone is larger than that of limestone, and its pore space is much larger than that of limestone.

Gas–Water Displacement Results

Gas–Water Occurrence in Pores

The material composition and pore structure characteristics of the rock samples from different types of coal-measure tight reservoirs differ substantially. The ρ of different types of rock samples was calculated using data from the high-pressure mercury injection experiment and the T 2 spectrum of the water-saturated core in the natural gas charging simulation. The transformation of T 2 relaxation times into equivalent pore diameters revealed the water occurrence characteristics in the rock pores. As shown in Figure , the occurrence of water in the pores of different types of coal-measure tight reservoir rock samples differs. Most samples exhibit a bimodal curve, with a higher left peak, indicating that the water content is significantly higher in smaller than in larger pores. For the coal samples, with over 95% of pore spaces measuring below 0.1 μm. Notably, the Y2 sample with a pore size less than 0.001 μm is 88.19%. The pore size range of the mudstone samples is 0.0001–0.1 μm. Mudstone lithologies display pore size distributions spanning 0.0001–0.1 μm, with all samples except D1 containing >99% sub-0.1 μm pores. The variation characteristics of water saturation of coarse sandstone represented by L1 with gas charging pressure are more obvious in sandstone, which can be compared with other lithology samples more clearly (Figure c). The pore size range of the sandstone samples is 0.01–100 μm. As the particle size increases, the proportion of pores with a size greater than 1 μm increases. The pore size range of the water-saturated limestone samples is 0.0001–10 μm. Significant changes in water saturation occur as the injection pressure increases.

Contribution of Different Pore Sizes to Gas Saturation

The gas charging efficiency and its influencing factors for reservoirs with different lithologies were analyzed by simulating the gas–water replacement. It was assumed that the reservoir’s pore space was filled with formation water, and the gas generated by the source rock displaced the movable water during migration. The proportion of the pore space occupied by the displaced movable water represented the gas saturation of the pores. The gas saturation in the gas charging simulation experiment is 12.10–51.19% (Table ), with coal and mudstone samples exhibiting lower gas saturation. The maximum gas saturation is 19.84% for the coal samples and 34.52% for the mudstone samples. The sandstone (42.68 to 51.19%) and limestone (38.07 to 44.53%) samples have higher gas saturation than the coal and mudstone samples. This is because the pore morphology types of mudstone and coal are mainly parallel plate pores and the content of clay minerals is high. After saturated wetting, clay minerals will absorb water and expand, resulting in a more complex pore structure, which is not conducive to the seepage and replacement of gas–water two-phase fluid.

Gas Saturation and Pore Size of Gas-Bearing Pores of Typical Rock Samples

The cumulative gas saturation of the coal samples is 12.10–19.84%. The surface relaxation rate ρ was calculated, and the pore size represented by different relaxation times of the T 2 spectrum was never obtained. Combined with the signal intensity of the T 2 spectrum, the contribution of pores of different sizes to gas saturation is calculated. As shown in Figure , coal pores with diameters <0.001 μm and from 0.001 to 0.01 μm contribute the most (43%) to total gas saturation. These are followed by pores with a diameter of 0.1 to 1 μm (21.47%) and larger than 1 μm (3.0%). The cumulative gas saturation of the mudstone samples is 23.64–28.11%. Pores with diameters less than 0.001 μm contribute the most to total gas saturation. The contributions of F1 and M5 are 60.77 and 53.51%, respectively. These are followed by pores with diameters of 0.01–0.1 μm, with contributions of 32.5 and 25.07%, respectively. The contribution of pores with diameters of 0.01–0.1 μm in the D1 sample is 67.19%. These results indicate that small pores contribute the most to total gas saturation for the three mudstone samples. The gas saturation of the three limestone samples is 38.07–44.53%. Pores with diameters of 0.001–1 μm contribute the most to gas saturation. The gas saturation of the three limestone samples does not differ significantly but is higher than that of coal and mudstone. The gas saturation of the sandstone is 42.68–51.19%. It has the highest gas saturation, and significant differences are observed for different particle sizes. Coarse sandstone has the highest gas saturation, followed by medium and fine sandstone. The primary diameters of gas-bearing pores of fine sandstone are 0.01–0.1 and 0.001–0.01 μm, with contribution rates of 46.64 and 38.73%, respectively. Those of medium and coarse sandstone are 0.1–1 μm, with contribution rates of 52.89 and 70.72%, respectively. This finding indicates that the pore diameters of sandstone are critical to gas saturation. A comprehensive analysis of rock samples from different strata and lithologies reveals that the pore diameter is correlated with the contribution to gas saturation. The larger the pore diameter, the higher the total gas saturation.

Dynamic Changes in Gas–Water Distribution

The reservoir space of rock reservoirs is filled with formation water, which is displaced as the charging pressure of methane gas increases. The water saturation changes, and the movable water content decreases. Bound water occupies the core at the maximum methane filling pressure (15 MPa). As shown in Figure , as the displacement pressure increases, water is displaced preferentially in larger pores. It is affected by the pore morphology and pore size of different types of rock samples. For example, the water in the larger pores of limestone and sandstone is almost completely displaced, whereas that in the smaller pores is slowly displaced as the methane filling pressure increases. The changes in water saturation are more pronounced in sandstone and limestone than those in mudstone and coal. The rate of change is the smallest for coal, which has a lower quartz content and a higher clay content than mudstone and sandstone, resulting in higher water saturation. The rate of change in water saturation is the largest for the L1 sandstone, followed by the medium and fine sandstone, which exhibit similar rates. This result can be attributed to the similar mineral compositions and pore structures of medium and fine sandstone. Therefore, they have similar water saturation values.

As shown in Figure , the water saturation trend differs for different samples and methane filling pressures. The coal and rock samples demonstrate stabilized water saturation levels upon reaching approximately 9 MPa methane pressure, suggesting complete cessation of water displacement. As shown in Figures and , most of the water exists in small pores (<0.001 and 0.001–0.01 μm). However, the dominant pore size where water saturation change occurs is 0.1–1 μm, and the residual water saturation is higher in the coal sample than in the other samples. The water saturation of the mudstone samples remains stable at lower methane charging pressures (≈7 MPa). The water in the mudstone also mainly occupies small pores (<0.001 and 0.001–0.01 μm), but the stable displacement pressure is lower than that of coal, indicating that the pore structure of mudstone is more complex than that of coal.

Water saturation of typical rock samples.

The water saturation of the limestone and sandstone samples remains stable at a pressure of about 12 MPa. Most of the water occupies limestone pores with a size of 0.001–1 μm. The water in the fine sandstone occupies primarily pores with a size of 0.01–0.1 μm. The dominant sizes of pores containing water in the medium and coarse sandstone is 0.1–1 μm. After the end of the gas–water charging simulation experiment, the water saturation values of fine sandstone and medium sandstone are not much different and both are smaller than those of coarse sandstone. The higher the water saturation in the pores at the maximum pressure (15 MPa), the higher the bound water content in the sample, the lower the gas saturation in the reservoir, and the lower the gas content in the rock reservoir after natural gas migration and charging. The gas saturation differs for different types of tight reservoirs, with sandstone having the highest gas saturation (average 45.60%), followed by limestone (average 41.40%), mudstone (average 25.67%), and coal (average 15.98%).

Fractal Characteristics of Pores Based on Gas Charging Experiments

Single-Fractal Characteristics of Water- and Gas-Bearing Pores

The fractal dimensions of single pores of 12 samples of six lithologies are shown in Figure . The curve has an inflection point, which corresponds to the end value of the T 2 spectrum and divides the fractal curve into two parts. Previous studies have shown that T 2cutoff can be used to divide irreducible water saturation and movable water saturation. , Based on previous research results, this study uses T 2cutoff to divide gas-bearing pores and water-bearing pores, and then uses T 2cutoff value to divide and calculate the heterogeneity of gas-bearing pores and the heterogeneity of water-bearing pores. The curve below the inflection point represents the fractal dimension of water-bearing pores (D w), while the upper portion characterizes the fractal dimension of gas-bearing pores (D g). These fractal dimensional values exhibit a measurable range between 2 and 3, where the numerical proximity to 3, the more complex the pore structure and the stronger the pore heterogeneity.

Single-fractal pore characteristics of typical rock types: (a) coal; (b) mudstone; (c) sandstone; (d) limestone.

The fractal dimension of water-bearing pores is less than 2 and cannot be used to characterize pore heterogeneity. , As shown in Table , the fractal dimension of gas-bearing pores is close to 3, with a range of 2.9345 to 2.9918, and the R 2 ranges from 0.3769 to 0.904. Multifractal Characteristics

The D (q) parameter spectrum quantifies pore size distribution complexity, and the range of D (q) reflects pore heterogeneity. The larger the range, the higher the pore heterogeneity. As shown in Figure a, the D (q) spectrum exhibits a monotonically decreasing trend with an inverted S-shape as q increases, indicating all rock samples have multifractal characteristics. The multifractal singular spectrum f­(α) corresponds to the fractal dimension of the dominant pores. The higher the value, the higher the geometric complexity of the pores. A convex relationship indicates that the pores have multifractal characteristics. Figure b shows that the pores of all of the rock samples have multifractal characteristics. The higher the symmetry of the multifractal singular spectrum f (α), the stronger the multifractal properties of the pores. The wider the peak width, the stronger the local-scale pore heterogeneity. The symmetry of the f (α) spectral curve of different types of rock samples in Figure b is high. The dashed lines in the curves of the N5 and N4 samples represent the highest points of their respective spectra. It can be seen that the curves of N5 samples shift to the left as a whole, while the curves of N4 samples shift to the right as a whole, reflecting completely different pore heterogeneity characteristics. The α­(q) spectrum indicates the degree of pore heterogeneity (Figure c), where expanded spectrum width (△α) signifies greater pore heterogeneity and more intricate pore distribution.

Factors Influencing Unconventional Natural Gas Charging

Influence of Minerals and Organic Geochemistry

Figure a shows a positive relationship between the quartz content and the gas saturation. Only one limestone sample contains quartz with a content of less than 1%, so the limestone samples were not considered. The coefficient of determination R 2 is 0.84. Figure b indicates that clay minerals of different types of tight reservoirs have adverse effects on gas saturation. Quartz is a brittle mineral, which plays a supporting role in the pore structure of the reservoir rock. The higher the content of quartz, the higher the gas storage and migration space in the rock, and the higher the gas saturation. Clay minerals are layered and have a high adsorption capacity for methane gas and water. Increased clay content amplifies rock hydrophilicity, where adsorbed water molecules at mineral interfaces competitively reduce the methane adsorption potential. The particle size of clay minerals is fine. With the progress of diagenesis, they will be filled in the pores formed by quartz and other minerals, reducing the porosity and the pore space of gas occurrence, resulting in a decrease in gas saturation..

Relationships between rock gas saturation (displacement efficiency), mineral composition, and organic characteristics. (a) Correlation of quartz content with displacement efficiency; (b) correlation of displacement efficiency with clay content; (c) correlation of gas saturation with TOC; (d) correlation of gas saturation with Ro.

The coal and mudstone samples have smaller pore sizes than the other samples, affecting gas saturation. As the methane gas charging pressure increases, the rate of change in water saturation and gas displacement efficiency decrease. This may be due to the fact that the gas saturation of coal and mudstone samples is controlled not only by mineral composition and pore structure but also by organic geochemical characteristics compared with limestone and sandstone samples. Figure c,d shows a positive correlation between the TOC content and gas saturation in the coal and mudstone samples. The higher the TOC content, the higher the number of small pores, the oxygen/carbon atom ratio, and the number of polar functional groups, affecting the gas molecule adsorption. A large amount of aromatic carbon configurations facilitate molecular gas entrapment, thereby augmenting reservoir gas storage potential. While coal exhibits progressive gas saturation enhancement with the Ro, but the correlation between the two parameters is not high. The relationship between gas saturation, TOC, and Ro of coal and mudstone indicates that the organic characteristics have a larger influence on coal than on mudstone.

Influence of Pore Structure

Figure indicates that natural gas charging in coal is related to the pore volume, porosity, mercury removal efficiency, and permeability of micropores, mesopores, and macropores. This study conducted a comparative analysis between pore heterogeneity and gas saturation, and found that there is no obvious correlation, mainly due to the large changes in pore heterogeneity of different types of rocks, and the difference in pore heterogeneity between the same lithology is relatively large. As the pore volume increases, the space for gas and migration and the gas saturation in coal increase, promoting gas accumulation. The mercury removal efficiency and permeability are inversely related to the gas saturation of coal. The R 2 between the micropore volume and gas saturation is 0.71, indicating that the micropore volume has a large influence on gas saturation in coal. In contrast, this correlation is lower for mudstone. The mercury withdrawal efficiency is highly negatively correlated with gas saturation in mudstone with an R 2 of 0.82, indicating that the pore structure has a larger influence on gas saturation in mudstone than in coal. The main mineral component of limestone is calcite, accounting for more than 95%; thus, the mineral composition differs from that of other rocks. The dominant pore size of limestone is larger than that of coal and mudstone, and the gas saturation of limestone depends on the large pore volume. As the pore volume increases, the gas saturation of limestone increases. The mercury removal efficiency and porosity are positively correlated with gas saturation. The gas saturation of sandstone is positively correlated with the pore volume, mercury removal efficiency, porosity, and permeability. It is negatively correlated with the mesopore volume. In contrast to other rock types, the gas saturation of sandstone is controlled primarily by the pore volume. The R 2 values for this relationship are 0.93 and 0.71. As the mesopore volume of sandstone increases, the macropore volume decreases. The gas saturation of sandstone decreases with an increase in the mesopore volume and increases with increasing macropore volume, indicating that larger pores provide more gas storage space, better connectivity, and a more suitable environment for gas migration.

Mechanism of Unconventional Natural Gas Accumulation

Tight reservoirs in coal measures have many strata and a complex gas charging process. Gas saturation variations across reservoirs demonstrate distinct mineralogical dependencies, with quartz-rich formations (excluding limestone) showing enhanced gas retention capabilities, while clay-dominated strata display low gas-bearing potential. This phenomenon suggests that the mineral composition significantly affects the rock’s gas content. This mineralogical influence stems from pore architecture variations; quartz’s macroporous network facilitates gas storage and mobility, whereas clay’s microporous matrix promotes pore-filling effects that impede gas accumulation. The Ro, micropore volume, and storage and migration capacity of natural gas in coal seams increase with the TOC content. Therefore, the gas content of coal is closely related to its organic composition, and a high content of organic matter and a good pore structure are critical for gas enrichment in coal. The influence of the pore volume on the gas storage capacity is lower in mudstone than in coal. The pore structure has a larger effect on the occurrence and migration of natural gas. The gas content of limestone depends on the volume of large pores, rock porosity, and the mercury withdrawal efficiency. A larger pore volume is related to a higher porosity and mercury withdrawal efficiency. The pores and channels provide space for gas accumulation; thus, gas saturation is high in limestone. The volume and porosity of the mesopores largely influence the natural gas content of the sandstone. The pore structure heterogeneity of mudstone and coal in this study is stronger, and the gas saturation value is relatively lower, indicating that the pore heterogeneity has an adverse effect on gas saturation. A low volume of mesopores, a large volume of macropores, high porosity, and weak pore heterogeneity are conducive to the migration and enrichment of natural gas in sandstone. In summary, the differences and similarities of the factors influencing gas occurrence and migration of different types of coal-measure tight reservoir rocks reflect the complexity of rock genesis, pore structure, and mineral composition.

Geological Implications

Figure shows the well connection and gas–water distribution in the research region. The tight reservoirs of different types of coal measures exhibit sand-mudstone interbedding. The sandstone is predominantly light gray, light purple, and brown and is composed of fine sand and silt. The mudstone is mostly brownish-red and purplish-red. This finding indicates that transitional facies are dominant in the sedimentary environment, and the provenance is complex and diverse. , An appropriate amount of brittle minerals is conducive to obtaining high-quality reservoirs. The content of quartz minerals in sandstone and mudstone samples in this study is greater than that of clay minerals, so as to effectively retain the primary pores of the reservoir. Rocks with high quartz contents are prone to microfractures. The kaolinite content is high in clay minerals. Kaolinite fills the intergranular spaces of the reservoirs, resulting in low porosity and permeability. The electron microscopy results show microfractures in the sandstone, mudstone, and coal samples. Calcite has two effects on reservoir properties. , It acts as the rock’s framework, preventing compaction, preserving primary pores, and providing material for acidic dissolution. Calcite dissolution generates secondary pores and microfractures, increasing the reservoir space and permeability.

Well connection and gas–water distribution in the research region.

Although the gas charging simulation experiment shows that the gas saturation of sandstone is higher than that of limestone, the gas saturation of mudstone and coal rock is the lowest. However, under actual geological conditions, mudstone and coal can be used as both source rocks and reservoirs to host high-content natural gas. The well connection and gas–water distribution in the research region are illustrated in Figure , revealing extensive gas-bearing strata across various tight reservoir lithologies. The coal in the Shanxi and Benxi formations is a crucial source rock and continuously generates large amounts of natural gas that enrich the adjacent mudstone, sandstone, and limestone. Some mudstones with high TOC contents also act as source rocks. Since coal-measure tight rock reservoirs have different temperature and pressure conditions, material compositions, and complex pore structures, the main rock types of gas occurrence differ in different wells. Tight reservoirs with larger storage spaces closer to coal and high TOC mudstone contain larger amounts of natural gas. However, reservoirs displaying poor pore interconnectivity and pronounced pore structure heterogeneity create migration barriers that impede gas percolation, resulting in dry beds that are suitable as cap beds.

Conclusions

This study analyzed the gas–water relationship and factors influencing typical coal-measure tight reservoirs in different regions of the southeastern Ordos Basin. We used NMR gas charging simulation experiments and single- and multifractal theories. The main findings are as follows:(1)The saturation behaviors of water and gas exhibited significant variation across diverse coal-measure tight reservoirs differed. Sandstone and limestone samples displayed complex and variable gas–water interactions, where pore size distributions exerted a significant influence on gas storage properties. The dominant pore size contributing to gas saturation was <0.001 μm for coal and mudstone, 0.01 to 0.1 μm for sandstone, and 0.001 to 1 μm for limestone.(2)The heterogeneity of the rock samples was high but differed for different types of coal-measure tight reservoirs. The pore heterogeneity of coal and mudstone samples is generally stronger than that of limestone and sandstone, and the pore heterogeneity of fine sandstone is stronger than that of medium and coarse sandstone. The main pore size distribution of all rock was concentrated, and large pores had a strong influence on pore heterogeneity.(3)The composition of minerals and pore structure exerted the strongest influence on the gas saturation of coal-measure compact reservoirs. Elevated quartz contents demonstrated a direct proportional relationship with gas saturation enhancement (R 2 = 0.84). The kaolinite in clay minerals filled the reservoir pores, reducing gas saturation. Coal with a large content of high-maturity organic matter and well-developed micropores had higher gas saturation.
