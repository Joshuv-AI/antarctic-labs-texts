School-associated measles outbreak driven by vaccination gaps, sociocultural barriers, and delayed detection in Gomba District, Uganda 2025

Background

Measles is a highly transmissible human viral infection. One infected person can transmit the virus to 12–18 susceptible individuals [1]. The virus spreads through droplets released when an infected person coughs or sneezes and can remain in the air for up to two hours [1, 2]. Symptoms usually appear 7–21 days after exposure, allowing the virus to spread unnoticed, especially in crowded or poorly ventilated settings [3]. Despite being preventable through vaccination, measles remains a major cause of illness and death among children worldwide. In 2023, an estimated 9.8 million cases and 107,500 deaths occurred, predominantly in low-income countries with weak health systems and suboptimal immunization coverage [4, 5]. Up to one in three children with measles develop complications such as pneumonia, diarrhea, ear infections, or vision loss [6, 7]. Severe outcomes are most common among children under five years, those who are malnourished, and those with vitamin A deficiency [2, 8–10].

Effective prevention requires achieving and sustaining ≥ 95% coverage with two doses of measles-containing vaccine to establish herd immunity and interrupt transmission [1, 9]. However, global coverage remains below this threshold; in 2024, the first and second doses of the measles–rubella(MR) vaccine reached only 84% and 76%, respectively [4, 6]. In Uganda, the MR vaccine is administered at 9 and 18 months of age, yet persistent coverage gaps, driven by dependence on outreach services, sociocultural barriers, and inadequate health infrastructure, continue to leave substantial proportions of children susceptible [3, 11].

On 27 May 2025, the Uganda Virus Research Institute Epi Lab confirmed a measles outbreak in Kabulasoke Subcounty, Gomba District, after five suspected cases tested positive for measles IgM. An alert had been raised in mid-April by a local leader following reports of clusters of children presenting with fever and rash.

By 16 June 2025, five cases had been laboratory confirmed, 13 additional suspected cases identified, and one suspected measles-related death reported. We investigated the outbreak to determine its magnitude, identify risk factors for transmission, estimate measles–rubella vaccination coverage and vaccine effectiveness, and inform control measures to prevent future outbreaks.

Methods

Outbreak area

We conducted the investigation in Kabulasoke Subcounty, one of the nine sub-counties in Gomba District, a rural area in Central Uganda with an estimated population of 43,694 residents across nine parishes [12]. Health services are provided by one Health Centre III (subcounty level) and two Health Centre II (parish level) facilities that offer routine and outreach immunization services under Uganda’s Expanded Programme on Immunization. Access to health services is primarily through public facilities, with limited private-sector provision.

Case definition and finding

We defined a suspected measles case as onset of fever and maculopapular rash plus at least one of the following: cough, coryza, or conjunctivitis, in a resident of Kabulasoke Subcounty from March 1 to August 31 2025. A confirmed case was a suspected case with laboratory-confirmed measles-specific IgM. We identified cases through review of outpatient and inpatient registers from all public health facilities and selected private clinics, supplemented by community active case search conducted by trained Village Health Teams. Case information was compiled into a line list capturing demographic characteristics, clinical presentation, residence, and vaccination status.

Descriptive epidemiology

We described the outbreak by person, place, and time. Age was categorized as 0–8 months, 9–17 months, 18–59 months, 5–14 years, and ≥ 15 years. Attack rates (AR) were calculated using population denominators from the 2024 national census [12]. Temporal patterns were assessed using an epidemic curve, and spatial distribution of cases was visualised using geospatial mapping in Quantum Geographic Information System (QGIS) software.

Exposure assessment and hypothesis generation

To identify potential exposures and inform analytic investigation, we conducted hypothesis-generating interviews with 50 early-identified case-patients using a standardized measles case investigation form. Information was collected on vaccination status, school attendance, social interactions, contact with symptomatic individuals, travel history, and healthcare-seeking behavior during the 7–21 days preceding symptom onset.

Case control study

We conducted a matched, non-blinded case–control study to evaluate risk factors for measles infection. Cases were randomly selected from the line list, restricting enrollment to the earliest symptomatic individual in households with multiple cases. Controls were selected from neighboring households in the same parishes and were matched to cases by age and sex. Controls had no history of measles-like illness during the outbreak period. Assuming a protective odds ratio of 0.35 for receipt of at least one measles–rubella (MR) vaccine dose [13], 95% confidence, 80% power, and a 1:2 case-to-control ratio, we enrolled 80 cases and 160 controls (total n = 240). Conditional logistic regression was used to estimate crude and adjusted odds ratios (cOR, aOR) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). Variables with p ≤ 0.20 in bivariate analysis, or with established biological plausibility for measles transmission regardless of statistical significance, were considered for inclusion in the multivariable model. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

We estimated MR vaccination coverage using vaccination status among controls, who were considered representative of the subcounty population. Vaccination status for both cases and controls was ascertained using identical procedures. Vaccination cards were reviewed whenever available, and caregiver recall was used when documentary evidence was unavailable. Interviewers used a standardised questionnaire and probing techniques to minimize recall bias. Doses administered within 14 days prior to symptom onset were excluded.

Behavioral and sociocultural factors

To explore sociocultural determinants of measles transmission and vaccination behavior, we conducted 5 key informant interviews (KIIs) and 10 in-depth interviews (IDIs).

KIIs were conducted with health workers and Village Health Team (VHT) members to explore service delivery experiences, outreach challenges, and health system barriers to immunization. IDIs were conducted with caregivers of children under five years, community elders, and teachers to explore demand-side factors, including illness perceptions, vaccine beliefs, gender dynamics in household decision-making, and cultural illness management practices. Participants were purposively selected until thematic saturation was achieved. Thematic saturation was assessed iteratively during data collection and analysis and was considered achieved when no new concepts or themes emerged from successive interviews. Interview guides explored perceptions of measles, vaccine beliefs, gender dynamics in household decision-making, cultural illness management practices, and barriers to immunisation. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analysed using inductive thematic analysis. Codes were generated iteratively and grouped into themes reflecting sociocultural influences on vaccination uptake and outbreak dynamics. Transcripts were independently reviewed and coded by two investigators. Coding discrepancies were discussed and resolved through consensus, and emerging themes were reviewed by the broader investigation team through peer debriefing.

Environmental assessment

We conducted a structured observational assessment in the three most affected parishes, including visits to 20 affected households in each parish, Infant School X, four additional schools, and all three public health facilities serving Kabulasoke Subcounty. We used a standardised observation checklist to assess access to immunization services, healthcare-seeking practices, infection prevention measures, school environments, and community practices that could facilitate measles transmission. Immunization registers from all three facilities were reviewed as part of the environmental assessment to obtain MR1 and MR2 coverage data, for 2023, 2024, and January–June 2025. Observations were supplemented by brief caregiver interviews to document vaccination experiences, care-seeking pathways, and management of children with fever and rash. Findings were recorded using standardised field notes and reviewed daily by the investigation team for consistency. To better understand reasons for zero-dose and under-immunised children, 67 caregivers were interviewed regarding barriers to vaccination.

Results

Descriptive epidemiology

Distribution of measles cases by person characteristics

A total of 106 measles cases were identified, including five laboratory-confirmed and 101 suspected cases, and one death. All case-patients (100%) presented with fever and maculopapular rash (Fig. 1). Additional symptoms included cough (93%), conjunctivitis (87%), and coryza (85%). Reported complications included diarrhea (23%), corneal ulcers (8%), and otitis media (4%).

Clinical presentation of case-patients during a measles outbreak in Kabulasoke Subcounty, Gomba District, March-August 2025 (n = 106)

The median age of case-patients was 24 months (interquartile range [IQR]: 8–60 months). Children aged 9–17 months had the highest attack rate (AR: 13/1,000 population), followed by those aged 0–8 months (11/1,000) and 18–59 months (8/1,000) (Table 1). Males accounted for 60% (64/106) of cases, with an attack rate of 3/1,000, compared with 1.9/1,000 among females.

Distribution of measles cases by the date of onset of symptoms

The epidemic curve demonstrated a propagated outbreak lasting approximately six months (Fig. 3a). The earliest case was a school-going male at infant school X, who developed fever and rash on March 1 2025 after returning on February 25 2025 from a cultural ceremony in Rakai District, where a measles outbreak was ongoing at the time. The index case was a five-year-old female from Lugaaga Parish who developed symptoms on March 11 2025. The primary and index cases attended infant school X and resided in the same neighborhood. Secondary cases were subsequently identified among household and school contacts by late March (Fig. 3b). The outbreak was detected on April 26 2025 after a notification to the District health team by the local council leadership, with laboratory confirmation on May 27 2025. Case counts increased through April and peaked in the second week of May 2025. Following implementation of response activities, including a reactive MR vaccination campaign conducted from July 27–30 2025, case numbers declined. Twelve additional cases were reported between August 1–12 2025, after which no new cases were detected through August 31 2025.

Environmental assessment findings

Administrative immunisation records indicated persistently low measles–rubella (MR) vaccination coverage in Kabulasoke Subcounty during January–June 2025. MR1 coverage was 33.8%, while MR2 coverage was 4.4%. A review of immunisation registers from all three health facilities showed consistently low coverage over the two years preceding and during the outbreak period. MR1 coverage was 37% (2023) and 30% (2024), compared to MR2 coverage of 5% (2023) and 33% (2024), followed by 33.8% and 4.4% for MR1 and MR2, respectively, in January–June 2025.During household visits, most caregivers reported initially seeking care for children with fever and rash from private clinics or drug shops. Among 106 cases, 78 (74%) first sought care from private providers, 21 (20%) attended public health facilities within the subcounty, and 7 (7%) sought care outside the subcounty. Vaccination status among cases showed that 32 (30%) were verified using vaccination cards, while 74 (70%) were based on caregiver recall. Among controls, 109 (68%) were card-verified and 51 (32%) were based on recall. None of the participants had received an MR vaccine within this exclusion window. MR vaccine vial monitors (VVMs) indicated acceptable vaccine integrity; however, retrospective verification of VVM status for vaccines used in Kabulasoke Subcounty prior to the outbreak was not possible. Isolation of children with fever and rash was not practiced in households or schools visited during the assessment. Use of herbal remedies for measles management was also reported among some affected households.

Reasons for non-vaccination among caregivers of unvaccinated or under-vaccinated children

Out of the 67 respondents, 43 (64%) cited fear of vaccine side effects as the most common reason for non-vaccination, followed by negative beliefs or lack of trust in vaccines (48%) and irregular outreach services (38%). One-third of caregivers (33%) were unaware of the required number of MR doses. Fewer caregivers cited long waiting times (9%), inconvenient session timing (5%), or lack of knowledge about vaccination schedules (5%) (Fig. 4).

Hypothesis generation findings

Of the 50 case-patients interviewed, thirty-seven (74%) were unvaccinated, 30 (60%) reported school attendance, 40 (80%) had contact with a symptomatic individual in their neighborhood, and 38 (76%) had participated in social activities outside the home. We hypothesised that children aged 6 months–14 years who were unvaccinated, attended school, participated in community social activities, and had contact with symptomatic individuals were more likely to develop measles than their unexposed counterparts.

Case control findings

In bivariate analysis (Table 2), receipt of at least one dose of MR vaccine was protective against measles infection (cOR = 0.27; 95% CI: 0.23–0.49). Factors associated with increased odds of measles included school attendance (cOR = 1.87; 95% CI: 1.11–3.40), attendance at Infant School X (cOR = 5.22; 95% CI: 2.49–11.1), contact with a symptomatic individual (cOR = 2.91; 95% CI: 1.60–5.31), and play interactions occurring away from home (cOR = 1.92; 95% CI: 1.14–3.42).

Behavioural and social cultural findings

Multiple behavioral and sociocultural factors influenced the dynamics of measles outbreaks. Cultural beliefs and local interpretations of the disease affected perceptions of susceptibility and severity, while restrictive gender norms constrained maternal decision-making around child vaccination. In addition, persistent concerns about vaccine safety were reported as barriers to vaccination within affected communities.

Schools as amplifiers of measles transmission

Caregivers consistently linked illness onset to school exposure, particularly at Infant School X in Lugaaga Parish, where multiple concurrent cases were reported among lower primary pupils. Both caregivers and school staff described intense in-school transmission, with continued spread during school holidays likely sustained through community mixing. These experiences are reflected in caregivers’ accounts:

“Most children were affected, but the situation at infant school X was particularly severe” (Caregiver A, Bweyaza village).

“Nearly all the children were ill… mine couldn’t open his eyes in bright light” (Caregiver B, Bweyaza village).

School staff emphasised the difficulty in pinpointing the first case:

“Measles hit us hard… but it’s difficult to pinpoint the first child who showed symptoms” (School Administrator, Infant school X).

Male-dominated decision-making restricting vaccination uptake

Patriarchal authority limited maternal autonomy in vaccinating children. Mothers reported threats of marital separation if they sought immunization without spousal approval, a barrier corroborated by health workers.

“If I take the children for vaccination, he said they would no longer be his” (Mother 1, Namirembe village).

“Women are willing to vaccinate, but men are the problem. They threaten to send their wives away if they do” (Healthcare worker 1, Namirembe village).

Historical vaccine safety concerns fueling mistrust

A previous mass vaccination event, during which several children reportedly fell ill and one died, fueled long-standing safety concerns. Caregivers, community elders, and informal providers described the event as a turning point discouraging routine immunization.

“Since that incident, even my husband refuses to discuss vaccination” (Mother 3, Bweyaza Village).

“Parents in this parish avoid vaccinating their children. They still talk about that incident” (VHT, Bweyaza village).

Cultural interpretations of measles lowered risk perception

Elders framed measles (“mulangira”) as a culturally manageable illness, historically treated with practices such as temporary bed separation. Many questioned the necessity of vaccination, noting prior generations survived without it.

“Since time immemorial, we never received vaccines for this disease. Even my children were never vaccinated and they grew up well. Now my grandchildren, who are vaccinated…are the ones struggling” (Community elder 1, Bweyaza village).

“If this disease is truly vaccine-preventable, how did we survive in our days when no one vaccinated their children? People must respect our cultural norms” (Community elder 2, Kisamula village).

Increased vaccine demand triggered by a measles-related death

Perceptions of disease severity shifted dramatically following a suspected measles-related death, driving sudden demand for vaccination, including among previously unvaccinated older children.

“That child’s death woke up the village. I even saw a 10-year-old brought for their first vaccination” (Mother 5, Kisamula village).

“Even the mother of the deceased brought her six-month-old for their first vaccination” (Mother 6, Bweyaza village).

Discussion

This investigation identified a prolonged, propagated measles outbreak in Kabulasoke Subcounty, Uganda, driven by the convergence of low population immunity, sociocultural barriers to vaccination, school-based transmission, and delays in outbreak detection and response. Although routine immunisation services were available, vaccination coverage remained below the level required to interrupt measles transmission, leaving substantial population susceptibility. Transmission occurred predominantly among unvaccinated children and was amplified within a school setting, demonstrating how persistent immunisation gaps can sustain measles transmission even in areas with established vaccination programmes.

The overall attack rate of 2.4 per 1,000 population was comparable to rates reported in previous rural Ugandan measles outbreaks, including Semuto in 2021 (1.8/1,000), Mayuge in 2016 (2.1/1,000), and Moroto in 2024 (3.2/1,000) [3, 11, 14]. However, outbreak detection was delayed for 33 days, exceeding delays reported in Semuto (14 days) and Moroto (21 days), allowing transmission to continue before coordinated control measures were initiated. Vaccination coverage in the subcounty was 72% for receipt of at least one MR dose, below the 95% coverage threshold required to interrupt measles transmission [4, 10], suggesting a level of population susceptibility that may have facilitated sustained spread.

Children aged 9–17 months experienced the highest attack rates, consistent with vulnerability during the period before completion of the recommended two-dose MR schedule [4, 10, 15].

Infection among children aged 0–8 months also suggests possible susceptibility among infants, potentially reflecting insufficient maternal antibody protection in a setting with ongoing measles transmission and suboptimal population immunity, as described in similar outbreak settings [10, 11, 14]. These findings reinforce the importance of timely MR1 vaccination and maintaining high community-level coverage to protect children who are too young to have completed vaccination.

The difference between investigation-derived vaccination coverage (72%) and facility-reported MR1 administrative coverage (33.8%) highlights the importance of interpreting immunisation indicators within their measurement context. The investigation estimates reflected vaccination status among assessed individuals at a single point in time based on vaccination card verification and caregiver recall, whereas administrative coverage represented reported doses delivered within a defined reporting period and may be affected by denominator inaccuracies, documentation gaps, and incomplete capture of doses provided through outreach activities. Conversely, investigation-based estimates may be influenced by recall error and potential over-reporting of vaccination status when documentation is unavailable. The magnitude of the discrepancy suggests that both administrative underestimation and recall-based overestimation of vaccination status may have contributed. Therefore, these estimates should not be considered directly equivalent but rather complementary indicators of immunisation programme performance.

The observed immunity gap likely reflected a combination of service delivery challenges and sociocultural determinants influencing vaccine uptake. Community reports indicated persistent concerns following a perceived cluster of adverse events after a previous mass vaccination campaign, during which several children reportedly became ill and one death was locally attributed to vaccination. However, no formal AEFI investigation documentation was available, and the cause of the reported events could not be established. The absence of documented investigation and timely communication may have allowed uncertainty and misinformation to persist, potentially influencing subsequent vaccine acceptance [16–18]. Similar effects of vaccine safety concerns on immunisation uptake have been documented elsewhere, including the Dengvaxia controversy in the Philippines, where declining confidence in vaccination contributed to reduced vaccine uptake and subsequent measles resurgence [19, 20].

Social and household decision-making dynamics further shaped vaccination behaviours. Male-dominated decision-making reportedly limited maternal autonomy to seek vaccination for children, with health workers describing situations where women who pursued immunisation without spousal approval experienced household conflict. Similar gender-related barriers affecting childhood immunisation uptake have been documented across sub-Saharan Africa [18, 21–23]. Together, these factors may have contributed to the accumulation of susceptible children and created conditions favourable for outbreak emergence.

Although receipt of at least one MR dose was associated with reduced risk of measles infection, the estimated vaccine effectiveness (VE) of 60% was lower than the 88–95% effectiveness commonly reported following a single measles-containing vaccine dose [3, 13]. The estimated VE of 60% should be interpreted cautiously. Vaccination ascertainment differed substantially between cases and controls, with vaccination status among 70% of cases relying on caregiver recall compared with 32% of controls, creating the potential for differential misclassification that may have biased the VE estimate downward. Additional factors may also have contributed to the lower-than-expected estimate, including recall error, differences in the timing of vaccination relative to exposure, and the intense exposure pressure associated with prolonged community transmission. Consequently, the observed VE may underestimate the true protective effect of a single dose of measles-rubella vaccine [3, 13, 14]. No evidence of vaccine handling failure was identified during the investigation; however, the finding highlights the importance of evaluating immunisation practices, vaccine storage conditions, and documentation systems during outbreak investigations.

Following implementation of response activities, including a reactive MR vaccination campaign from 27 to 30 July 2025, reported cases declined. However, this temporal association cannot establish causality, as the decline may also have reflected depletion of susceptible individuals, behavioural changes, or other concurrent response measures.

Transmission was amplified within educational settings, particularly Infant School X in Lugaaga Parish. More than half of cases occurred among school-going children, and attendance at this school was significantly associated with measles infection. The earlier and higher epidemic peak observed in Lugaaga Parish compared with other affected parishes suggests that Infant School X likely functioned as an amplification setting within a broader context of low population immunity, facilitating transmission among susceptible children before spread into the wider community. Schools provide environments characterised by frequent close contact and dense mixing, making them important settings for measles transmission when vaccination coverage is insufficient [3, 11, 24]. However, they also represent important platforms for prevention through school-based vaccination activities, verification of immunisation status, and targeted health education for caregivers and teachers [11, 24, 25]. Continued transmission during school holidays further suggests that household and community interactions contributed to sustaining transmission beyond the school environment.

Community perceptions of measles also influenced care-seeking and surveillance. Measles was locally described as “mulangira,” a condition perceived by some caregivers as manageable through traditional approaches rather than requiring medical attention [22, 26, 27]. This perception was associated with delayed healthcare seeking and use of herbal remedies, potentially delaying recognition and reporting of cases [28–31]. The resulting 33-day delay between symptom onset in the index case and outbreak detection exceeded the WHO 7–1–7 targets, allowing transmission to continue before response activities were implemented. Although notification occurred rapidly once the outbreak was detected, response activities extended beyond the recommended seven-day timeframe, highlighting opportunities to strengthen early detection and rapid response systems.

Health system and access barriers further contributed to delayed identification and control. Lugaaga Parish, the outbreak epicenter, lacked a public health facility providing routine immunisation and relied on periodic outreach services, creating potential missed opportunities for vaccination. In addition, many caregivers initially sought care from private clinics and drug shops, which are often less integrated into routine surveillance systems, potentially delaying reporting and outbreak recognition [23, 32].

An important observation during the outbreak was the rapid change in community vaccine demand following a suspected measles-related death. Caregivers who had previously declined vaccination reportedly sought immunisation for their children, including older children who had missed routine vaccination opportunities. This shift demonstrates how perceived disease severity can influence vaccine acceptance and suggests that risk communication strategies grounded in locally relevant experiences may be more effective in addressing vaccine hesitancy than generic messaging alone [17, 33].

Overall, this outbreak illustrates how measles transmission can persist when immunisation gaps intersect with sociocultural barriers, delayed detection, and opportunities for high-contact transmission. Addressing future outbreaks will require strengthening routine and outreach immunisation services, improving integration of private providers into surveillance systems, engaging schools as partners in immunisation and early detection, and implementing context-specific risk communication strategies that address vaccine confidence, household decision-making dynamics, and community perceptions of disease.

Study limitations

Vaccination status was partially based on caregiver recall because vaccination cards were frequently unavailable, which may have introduced recall or misclassification bias. To minimize this, caregivers were probed on vaccination timing, site, and circumstances to improve recall accuracy. Using controls drawn primarily from 2 parishes Lugaaga and Mawuuki Parishes, may limit generalizability to the entire subcounty; however, these parishes represented the outbreak epicenter and adjacent high-risk areas with similar sociodemographic characteristics and health-service access. Triangulation of epidemiological and qualitative data strengthened internal validity and provided a comprehensive understanding of outbreak drivers despite these limitations.

Conclusion

This propagated outbreak was sustained through school-based and play-related contact in the context of suboptimal vaccination coverage and delayed detection. Vaccine mistrust linked to historical adverse-event concerns, male-dominated household decision-making, and cultural interpretations of measles as a traditionally manageable illness contributed substantially to vaccination gaps. Although receipt of at least one MR dose was protective, coverage remained insufficient to interrupt transmission. Strengthening routine and outreach immunisation, implementing school-based preventive measures, enhancing surveillance timeliness, and implementing community engagement strategies that directly address vaccine mistrust linked to prior adverse events and male-dominated household decision-making barriers are essential to mitigate similar future outbreaks and build resilient local health systems.
