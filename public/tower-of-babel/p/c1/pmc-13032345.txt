Mesenchymal stromal cell therapy for post-COVID-19 syndrome: associated impact and mechanism
Authors: Yuefei Pan, Mengqi Yuan, Le Song, Weiqi Yao, Junli He, Qiong Mo, Ning Zheng, Ziying Zhang, Tengyun Dong, Kaidi Zhu, Zeyi Zhang, Jiaqi Xiong, Jinghui Dong, Jianzeng Zhang, Mengmeng Zhang, Chao Zhang, Yuanyuan Li, Limin Liu, Jing Li, Yunbo Xie, Ming Shi, Lei Huang, Zhe Xu, Yu Zhang, Bo Zhang, Lei Shi, Fu-Sheng Wang

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPost-COVID-19 syndrome (PCS) affects around 10% of individuals who experience SARS-CoV-2 infection worldwide. This four-year follow-up study investigated the long-term impact of mesenchymal stromal cell (MSC) therapy on both the occurrence of PCS and its underlying mechanisms.MethodsWe enrolled 148 survivors of severe COVID-19 (92 who received MSC therapy and 56 who served as controls). The clinical evaluations included safety (new-onset comorbidities, oncogenic risk) and efficacy outcomes (computed tomography [CT] imaging, functional capacity, pulmonary function, 36-Item Short-Form Survey [SF-36], PCS prevalence). Serum proteomic profiling was performed to identify the differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) across the PCS subtypes, including chronic fatigue-like syndrome (CFs), respiratory syndrome (REs), chronic pain syndrome (CPs), and neurosensorial syndrome (NSs).ResultsMSC therapy had a favorable long-term safety profile. The overall prevalence of PCS was 73.6% (109/148), with CFs being the most common subtype (50.7%, 75/148). Moreover, the incidence of CFs was significantly lower in the MSC group than in the control group (41.3% vs. 66.1%; odds ratio = 0.361, p = 0.003). MSC therapy was also associated with a significant improvement in the SF-36 Role-Emotional domain. No significant differences were observed in the participants’ CT outcomes, functional capacity, or pulmonary function. A predictive model integrating proteomic and clinical data for CFs achieved an area under the curve of 0.773. Unsupervised clustering identified three molecular endotypes, with one being both highly enriched for respiratory syndrome and characterized by dysregulated immune pathways.ConclusionThis 4-year follow-up shows a sustained association between MSC therapy and a lower likelihood of CFs in severe COVID-19 survivors, with a favorable safety profile. Integrated proteomic and clinical analysis reveals distinct biological pathways underpinning PCS heterogeneity, suggesting potential biological bases underlying the subtype-specific associations observed with MSC therapy and highlighting potential targets for future precision medicine approaches.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-07902-y.Keywords: Mesenchymal stromal cell therapy, Post-COVID-19 syndrome, Chronic fatigue-like syndrome, Proteomics, Molecular endotypes

Introduction

As of 2025, the World Health Organization (WHO) has recorded over 760 million confirmed cases of COVID-19 worldwide [1]. In the post-pandemic era, nations are facing the challenge of the long-term sequelae of COVID-19, which affect around 10% of individuals who experience SARS-CoV-2 infection [2]. These new or significantly worsened symptoms, such as fatigue, arthralgia, cognitive dysfunction, shortness of breath which begin approximately three months after the initial COVID-19 infection and persist for at least two months, constitute a clinical condition recognized by the WHO as long-COVID, post-COVID-19 condition, or post-COVID-19 syndrome (PCS) [3–6]. More specifically, PCS encompasses chronic fatigue-like syndrome (CFs), respiratory syndrome (REs), chronic pain syndrome (CPs), and neurosensorial syndrome (NSs) [6, 7]. The proposed pathogenic mechanisms of PCS include the persistence of SARS-CoV-2 in tissues [8–10], immune dysregulation with or without reactivation of the underlying pathogens [11–13], autoimmunity triggered by molecular mimicry [9], microvascular blood clotting accompanied by endothelial dysfunction [14, 15], and impaired neural signaling in the brainstem [9, 16]. Whatever the mechanism, more than four years after the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic, millions continue to suffer from these long-term sequelae, which impose a substantial burden on individuals, healthcare systems, and economies worldwide [7, 17, 18]. While various vaccines and antiviral therapies have been deployed, treating patients with severe or critical COVID-19 remains difficult [19, 20], and the high incidence of PCS among survivors cannot be overlooked. As a consequence, there remains an acute unmet need to better understand and mitigate the chronic impacts of SARS-CoV-2 infection, particularly in survivors of severe COVID-19.

Mesenchymal stromal cell (MSC) therapy has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for severe COVID-19 due to reducing acute lung injury and hyper-inflammation in randomized controlled trials [21]. Our previous studies, consistent with findings from other groups, have demonstrated the favorable safety profile and short-term efficacy of MSCs in patients with COVID-19, with the follow-up data extending to three years suggesting possible long-term benefits [21–29]. However, the long-term (over four years) safety, especially the tumorigenicity risk, and incidence of PCS among patients who undergo MSC therapy remain unclear. Moreover, a deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying the different PCS subtypes is essential to accurately interpret the potential long-term benefits of MSC therapy.

To address these issues, we conducted a comprehensive four-year follow-up study among a longitudinal cohort of 148 survivors of severe COVID-19, combining participants from two prospective trials to enhance the statistical power and generalizability. Our investigation was designed to achieve two primary goals—namely, (1) to evaluate both the long-term safety and potential subtype-specific efficacy of MSC therapy and (2) to characterize the molecular heterogeneity of PCS.

Methods

Study design

This long-term follow-up study was designed to evaluate the four-year safety and efficacy of MSC therapy in survivors of severe COVID-19, with a particular focus on MSC therapy’s impact on PCS and its subtypes. To enhance the statistical robustness and account for the expected attrition, we pooled the participants from two prospective cohorts established in Wuhan, China, in early 2020. Both cohorts applied identical inclusion criteria and utilized the same umbilical cord-derived MSC (UC-MSC) treatment protocol. The primary distinction between the two lay in their original trial designs—that is, Cohort 1 (NCT04288102) was a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase 2 trial, whereas Cohort 2 (ChiCTR2000031430) was a prospective, open-label, multicenter trial conducted in accordance with the Good Clinical Practice principles [30]. All participants received conventional therapy and standard post-discharge care in accordance with the Chinese National Health Commission’s COVID-19 treatment guidelines (Trial Version 7) [31]. Notably, structured rehabilitation protocols were consistent across both the MSC and control cohorts. A comprehensive follow-up assessment of each participant was conducted between March 16 and May 9, 2024, including clinical evaluation, laboratory testing, and serum proteomic profiling (Fig. 1).

Study flow chart

Participants

A total of 210 patients were initially treated across the two cohorts: 100 in Cohort 1 (65 who received UC-MSC and 35 who received placebo) and 110 in Cohort 2 (52 who received UC-MSC plus conventional therapy and 58 who received conventional therapy alone). All the surviving patients were invited to participate in the four-year follow-up study. Ultimately, 148 survivors of severe COVID-19 (92 in the MSC group and 56 in the control group) completed the follow-up assessment. The control group comprised both the placebo recipients from Cohort 1 and the conventional therapy-only participants from Cohort 2. All the participants provided written informed consent prior to completing the screening and study procedures. In accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision), this study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fifth Medical Center of the Chinese PLA General Hospital (approval no. 2020–023-D).

Outcomes

The outcomes at the four-year follow-up point were categorized into safety and efficacy indicators. The safety outcomes included (1) new-onset comorbidities from baseline to the end of the four-year follow up; and (2) tumor markers. Moreover, the efficacy outcomes included (1) lung imaging outcomes according to the number of normal computed tomography (CT) images and total severity score (TSS) [32–34]; (2) functional exercise capacity evaluated based on the results of the six-minute walk test (6-MWD) and the 6-MWD below the lower limit of normal (LLN) [35–37]; (3) pulmonary function tests, specifically measuring the diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide (DLCO), forced vital capacity (FVC), and modified Medical Research Council scale (mMRC) [38, 39]; (4) quality of life according to the 36-Item Short-Form Survey (SF-36) [40]; and (5) PCS-related symptoms, including fatigue, cough, sore throat, chest tightness, dizziness, taste disorder, myalgia, dyspnea, headache, arthralgia, olfactory dysfunction, rhinitis, skin rash, alopecia, and reduced activity.

Procedures

The patients in the MSC groups in both cohorts received three intravenous infusions of UC-MSC in addition to the standard treatment. The placebo group in Cohort 1 and the control group in Cohort 2 received the standard treatment but did not receive the MSC infusions. The preparation, characterization, and administration of the UC-MSCs (supplied by VCANBIO Cell & Gene Engineering Corp.) followed the standardized protocols detailed in our previous work [22].

During the four-year follow-up assessment, each participant underwent a comprehensive physical examination conducted by senior physicians and was asked to complete questionnaires regarding their health-related quality of life (SF-36), and the presence of any symptoms of PCS. Notably, PCS-related symptoms, including CFs, were based on patient-reported persistent fatigue lasting ≥3 months, consistent with the WHO clinical case definition. It is important to note that these symptoms were not assessed using validated quantitative instruments, such as the Chalder Fatigue Scale [41] or Multidimensional Fatigue Inventory [42], which is a limitation of this study. Reflecting the clinical heterogeneity of PCS, participants could meet the criteria for multiple, non-exclusive subtypes concurrently. Consequently, prevalence calculations and group comparisons for each subtype (CFs, REs, CPs, and NSs) are based on this overlapping framework.

The evaluation protocol also included a series of investigations—namely, high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) of the chest, a standardized 6-MWD test, and pulmonary function testing. The laboratory analyses comprised routine blood tests, biochemical profiles, and tumor marker assays. Furthermore, any new comorbidities that had emerged in the four years following the participants’ initial enrollment were systematically recorded.

The lung CT images were all independently assessed by three radiologists who were blinded to the treatment assignments during the analysis [27]. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus. The TSS was based on the severity of the involvement of each anatomical lobe, while the morphological features were assessed based on the distribution, density, morphology, internal lesion structure, and mediastinum. The 6-MWD was performed according to the guidelines published by the American Thoracic Society (ATS) [35], and the calculation method used for the relevant results is shown in Table S2. The pulmonary function parameters were tested as previously reported [26].

Proteomic profiling and data processing

Proteomic analysis was performed on 147 samples (as one patient declined specimen collection due to personal reasons) as previously described [43]. In brief, the proteins were enriched using superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs), followed by digestion, and the resulting peptides were then analyzed by means of liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) on a timsTOF Pro instrument coupled to an UltiMate 3000 RSLCnano System using data-independent acquisition (diaPASEF). The raw data were processed using the Data-Independent Acquisition Neural Network (DIA-NN) (v1.9.2) against the UniProtKB Homo sapiens database, with a 1% false discovery rate (FDR) filter at the precursor level and MaxLFQ normalization applied. The subsequent bioinformatic processing was conducted in R (version 4.3.3). Any missing values were imputed via a stochastic method (Gaussian distribution centered at 1.8 standard deviations below the mean), and only those proteins with valid values in ≥ 50% of the samples in any group were retained for the downstream analysis. Given the exploratory, hypothesis-generating nature of this proteomic analysis and the moderate cohort size, we identified differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) using thresholds of an absolute fold change of >1.2 and a nominal p-value of <0.05 (two-sided Student’s t-test). This inclusive threshold strategy was adopted to maximize sensitivity for detecting potential biological signals relevant to PCS heterogeneity, while acknowledging an increased risk of false-positive discoveries. To develop a concise biomarker panel, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was applied to the DEPs, and the selected proteins were used to construct a logistic regression model. The cohort was partitioned via stratified random sampling into a training set for model construction, with five-fold cross-validation and an independent validation set for the final performance evaluation, with metrics including the sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC). Unsupervised consensus clustering was performed using the ConsensusClusterPlus package to identify the molecular subtypes, with the optimal number of clusters (k = 3) determined based on the maximum average silhouette width. Functional enrichment analysis of the quantified proteins and DEPs was conducted using Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways, and MSigDB Hallmark gene sets, as assessed via Fisher’s exact test (significance: p < 0.05 and ≥3 DEPs). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed, with significant sets defined by a normalized enrichment score (NES) > 1, a nominal p-value < 0.05, and an FDR q-value < 0.25. Unless otherwise specified, a two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for the inferential tests, and no adjustments for multiple comparisons were applied in this study.

Clinical statistical analysis

The safety and efficacy outcomes were analyzed using descriptive statistics, including the confidence intervals (CIs) and p-values, which were employed solely for descriptive purposes and not for inferential testing, given that no predefined hypotheses were established in this study. The continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The statistical differences between the two groups were tested using the two-sample t-test because the relevant data satisfied the following conditions: independence, normality, and homogeneity of variance. The categorical variables are summarized as the n/N (%), with the 95% CIs calculated via the Clopper–Pearson method. Group comparisons for the categorical data were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Pairwise comparisons among the PCS subtypes (K = 3 and K = 4) were performed using Fisher’s exact test, with the Bonferroni correction applied for multiple comparisons, which required significance levels of α = 0.0167 for K = 3 and α = 0.0083 for K = 4. The odds ratios (ORs) were derived from the logistic regression models. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on age (<65 years vs. ≥ 65 years) and body mass index (BMI) (≤24 kg/m2 vs. > 24 kg/m2). Aside from the SF-36 items, no other missing safety or efficacy data were imputed or processed. All the statistical analyses were carried out using SAS software (version 9.4; Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Characteristics of the study population at the four-year follow-up visit

A total of 148 survivors of severe COVID-19 (92 in the MSC group and 56 in the control group) were assessed at the four-year follow-up point, constituting the final long-term cohort from the two original trials (Fig. 1). This cohort accounted for attrition as a result of loss to follow-up (MSC group: n = 24; control group: n = 36) and death (n = 1 in each group). Crucially, despite this attrition over the extended study period, the two groups remained well-balanced across all the key demographic and clinical characteristics at the four-year mark (Table 1). More specifically, the groups were comparable in terms of the participants’ age (mean ± SD: 60.8 ± 8.8 vs. 60.9 ± 9.5 years; p = 0.9671), gender distribution (male: 58.70% vs. 46.43%; p = 0.1464), height (165.37 ± 7.46 vs. 164.09 ± 7.69 cm; p = 0.3229), weight (69.98 ± 10.57 vs. 67.37 ± 10.55 kg; p = 0.1520), and BMI (25.66 ± 3.33 vs. 24.89 ± 3.24 kg/m2; p = 0.1779). This sustained balance strengthened the validity of the subsequent long-term comparative analyses.

Long-term safety of MSC therapy

Over the four-year follow-up period, the numbers of new-onset comorbidities were low and comparable between the groups (Table 2). With regard to cardio-cerebrovascular diseases, hypertension occurred in 11.96% (11/92) of the MSC group versus 12.50% (7/56) of the control group, coronary artery disease was absent in the MSC group (0/92) but present in 3.57% (2/56) of the control group, and cerebral infarction was reported in 3.26% (3/92) of the MSC group versus 5.36% (3/56) of the control group. In terms of metabolic and nutritional diseases, the incidence of diabetes was 4.35% (4/92) (MSC) versus 10.71% (6/56) (control), while the incidence of hyperlipidemia was 8.70% (8/92) (MSC) versus 12.50% (7/56) (control). For malignancies (critical for cell therapy safety), the MSC group had rare cases (colon cancer, gastric cancer, and meningioma: 1/92 each), while the control group had one salivary gland tumor (1/56), and there was no significant between-group difference in this regard.

Comprehensive serum tumor marker testing confirmed there to be no increased oncogenic risk (Table S1). The alpha-fetoprotein, free-β-human chorionic gonadotropin, and squamous cell carcinoma antigen were normal in all the participants (0/92 MSC and 0/56 control). The abnormal rates of the other markers were as follows: carcinoembryonic antigen (9.78% [9/92] MSC vs. 7.14% [4/56] control), carbohydrate antigen 19–9 (1.09% [1/92] MSC vs. 5.36% [3/56] control), and neuron-specific enolase (50.00% [46/92] MSC vs. 53.57% [30/56] control), and all with p-values > 0.05, indicating there to be no significant between-group differences.

Efficacy of MSC therapy

Assessment of the objective clinical measures, including HRCT imaging (evaluated via the TSS and morphological features), pulmonary function tests (spirometry and diffusion capacity), and functional exercise capacity (6-MWD), did not reveal any statistically significant differences between the MSC and control groups at the four-year mark. With regard to the HRCT, the TSS (range: 0–25) was 4.7 ± 4.2 in the MSC group versus 5.9 ± 5.2 in the control group (mean difference: −1.28, 95% CI: −2.83–0.27; p = 0.1037), while the proportion of patients with completely normal HRCT (all lobes scored 0) was 27.17% in the MSC group versus 17.86% in the control group (OR = 1.72, 95% CI: 0.75–3.91; p = 0.1958). In terms of the pulmonary function and exercise capacity, the forced vital capacity (FVC) was 2.820 ± 0.743 L (MSC) versus 2.627 ± 0.749 L (control) (mean difference: 0.19, 95% CI: −0.07–0.46; p = 0.1520), the diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO) was 17.458 ± 5.381 versus 17.230 ± 5.730 mL/min/mmHg (mean difference: 0.23, 95% CI: −1.75–2.20; p = 0.8202), the 6-MWD was 434.8 ± 55.3 versus 439.5 ± 65.2 m (mean difference: −4.71, 95% CI: −24.70–15.27; p = 0.6418), and the incidence of LLN and modified mMRC dyspnea scores also did not differ significantly between the groups (p > 0.05) (detailed data are available in Table S2).

However, analysis of the patient-reported SF-36 data revealed a meaningful clinical pattern. While the MSC group showed consistent and favorable trends across most SF-36 domains, a statistically significant and specific benefit was observed in relation to the Role-Emotional domain, which assesses limitations in an individual’s usual role activities due to emotional problems (mean difference: 9.65, 95% CI: 0.50–18.81; p = 0.0389; Table 3). This finding indicates a potential positive impact of MSC therapy on the psychosocial dimension of patients’ long-term recovery.

The persistent burden of PCS in this cohort of survivors of severe COVID-19 was substantial, with an overall prevalence of 73.6% (109/148) (Fig. 2A) at the four-year post-infection assessment. Symptom analysis revealed fatigue (44.59%), alopecia (22.97%), and arthralgia (20.95%) to be the most frequent manifestations (Fig. 2B). Categorization into clinical subtypes (CFs, REs, CPs, NSs) established CFs to be the most common subtype of PCS, affecting 50.7% (75/148) of participants (Fig. S1). A significant reduction in the incidence of CFs was observed in the MSC group when compared with the control group (38/92 [41.3%] vs. 37/56 [66.1%]), whereas no marked differences were found in terms of the overall incidence of PCS and the other subtypes (REs, CPs, and NEs) (Fig. 2B). A pivotal finding emerged from the univariate logistic regression analysis adjusting for sex, age, and BMI—that is, prior MSC therapy was independently associated with a substantially and significantly lower likelihood of developing CFs (OR = 0.361, 95% CI: 0.181–0.722; p = 0.003; Table 4). By contrast, no significant associations were observed between MSC therapy and the other PCS subtypes (REs, CPs, and NSs), highlighting the existence of a specific relation with the fatigue-dominant manifestation of PCS.

Proteomic signature and integrated clinical–proteomic predictive modeling

The specificity of the association between MSC treatment and the CFs subtype of PCS provided a strong rationale to dissect the distinct molecular pathways underlying the different PCS manifestations. Using high-resolution serum proteomics, it was determined that each clinical subtype was underpinned by a unique protein signature (Fig. S2). Here, REs showed the most extensive proteomic derangement, with clear separation from the controls via principal component analysis, which is indicative of a profound systemic insult. By contrast, the CFs signature was more refined, involving key proteins such as PLEKHF1 (immunomodulation) and PGD (metabolism), indicating a potential disruption at the immunometabolic interface. Moreover, to evaluate the clinical translatability of these molecular findings, predictive models were constructed. The performance in relation to the REs subtype (AUC: 0.854 training, 0.85 validation; Fig. S4) suggested that a small serum protein panel (MAN1A2, HNRNPU, LTBP3, ENTPD5, SLC30A1) may help identify patients with persistent respiratory sequelae. In the case of CFs, the integration of the proteomic signature with the two key clinical covariates—namely, MSC treatment and age—yielded a model (AUC: 0.773; Fig. 3) that outperformed the protein-only model (AUC = 0.747; Fig. S3), highlighting the complementary relation between the molecular biomarkers and the clinical context for risk prediction.

Unsupervised molecular endotyping identifies a high-risk immunometabolic cluster

Finally, to move beyond supervised analyses tied to clinical symptoms and reveal the fundamental, data-driven biological stratification of patients, an unsupervised clustering approach was employed with regard to the entire proteomic dataset. This analysis revealed three robust molecular endotypes (Clusters 1–3; Fig. 4A, B) that cut across the conventional clinical definitions.3–6 The most striking discovery was the overwhelming enrichment of REs in Cluster 2, where 70.6% (12/17) of patients developed REs when compared with only 27.2% (22/81) and 26.5% (13/49) in the other clusters (adjusted α = 0.0167, p < 0.01; Fig. 4C). This association identifies Cluster 2 as a distinct high-risk endotype for pulmonary sequelae. Furthermore, a deep dive into the biology of this endotype using GSEA demonstrated a coherent pathological state of immunometabolic dissonance—that is, a combination of blunted adaptive immunity (as evidenced by the downregulation of the T-cell and immunoglobulin pathways), enhanced mitochondrial abundance, and persistent innate immune activation (Figs. 4D–I, S8, S9, and S10).

Discussion

Building on the findings of our previous three-year follow-up assessment, which indicated the promising safety profile and potential quality-of-life improvement of MSC therapy in survivors of severe COVID-19 [26–28], this four-year follow-up study provides substantial evidence concerning the long-term safety of MSC therapy and its specific association with a reduced risk of the most prevalent subtype of PCS—namely, CFs. Moreover, by integrating deep clinical phenotyping with high-resolution serum proteomics, this study moves beyond syndromic classification to delineate the distinct molecular signature underlying PCS’s heterogeneity, thereby offering a plausible biological context for the subtype-specific association observed with regard to MSC therapy.

The substantial burden of PCS in this cohort of survivors of severe COVID-19 remains considerable, with 73.6% of participants meeting the diagnostic criteria at four years after infection. The predominance of CFs in this population aligns with global epidemiological trends, underscoring its central role in the long-term experience of patients with COVID-19 [44, 45]. In this context, the observed association between prior MSC therapy and a lower likelihood of CFs is notable. Distinct from pathogen-specific antivirals or symptom-focused therapies, MSCs are believed to operate via systemic pathways, primarily through immunomodulation, anti-inflammation signaling, and mitochondrial metabolism regulation [21, 46]. This finding indicates the possibility that MSC infusion is associated with post-acute modulation of the immune and metabolic dysregulation implicated in cases of chronic fatigue. The improvement noted in the Role-Emotional domain of the SF-36 further suggests that the potential impact of MSC therapy may encompass psychosocial well-being, which is recognized as a critical dimension of recovery [47, 48].

The proteomic data provide a biological context for the above-mentioned clinical observations. The distinct protein signature associated with CFs, particularly the involvement of molecules related to energy metabolism such as PGD, is consistent with the growing body of evidence implicating mitochondrial dysfunction in post-viral fatigue syndromes [49]. Importantly, the predictive model we formulated, which integrates proteomic markers with clinical variables (e.g., MSC treatment and age), demonstrates reasonable performance for identifying individuals susceptible to CFs (AUC = 0.773). Beyond diagnosis, this exploratory model offers value for understanding COVID-19-related long-term sequelae. Unlike diagnostic tools, this model dissects the molecular heterogeneity of PCS, highlighting key proteins (e.g., PLEKHF1 and MMP1) that bridge immunomodulatory and metabolic pathways. This offers insights into the biological underpinnings of why only fatigue-dominant PCS appears associated with MSC therapy. Such integrative models, consistent with findings in other long COVID studies [18], help prioritize potential targets for subsequent mechanistic investigation and precision medicine strategies, rather than serving as clinical diagnostic tools. This alignment suggests a plausible framework for understanding the association with MSC therapy, given that the capacity of MSCs to modulate cellular metabolism and engage in mitochondrial transfer is a recognized aspect of their mechanism of action [46, 50]. Critically, while mitochondrial dysfunction has previously been implicated in PCS and MSC therapy has been reported to influence mitochondrial function [51, 52], the findings of this study suggest a potential link between these phenomena, indicating that MSC therapy may be associated with the alleviation of fatigue-related sequelae via modulation of the mitochondrial pathways.

Conversely, the absence of a significant association between MSC therapy and REs can be understood through the lens of the underlying molecular endotype, as identified by means of unsupervised clustering. This approach revealed a distinct patient subgroup characterized by a high prevalence of REs and marked by a complex biological signature. The pathophysiology of this endotype is multifactorial, involving several interconnected biological disturbances. First, it exhibits profound mitochondrial dysfunction, as reflected in the enrichment of pathways associated with the inner mitochondrial membrane and matrix, which disrupts cellular energy metabolism and so is consistent with the notion of “viral mitochondriopathy” [49]. Second, the epithelial barrier integrity is compromised, as indicated by the downregulated keratinization, impairing the respiratory tract’s physical defense and contributing to the persistent respiratory symptoms observed. Third, the endotype is defined by dysregulated complement activation—specifically reduced formation of the C4/C2 activators—a pattern previously linked to thrombo-inflammation and vascular injury in cases of PCS [13]. Finally, these features are accompanied by enhanced CD8+ T-cell dysfunction, which may perpetuate chronic tissue damage [53]. This multifaceted pathophysiology, which involves entrenched adaptive and innate immune dysregulation, energetic deficits, and structural damage, implies the existence of a more entrenched state of tissue remodeling that responds poorly to acute-phase MSC therapy. This likely explains the differential association observed across the PCS subtypes.

It must be acknowledged that this study has several limitations. First, this work is a long-term observational follow-up of prior interventional trials. While it reveals significant associations, the original trials were not statistically powered to detect PCS outcomes; therefore, causal inference cannot be definitively established. Unmeasured confounding factors—particularly relevant for subjective outcomes such as fatigue—may also influence the observed relationships. Second, our cohort consisted exclusively of severe COVID-19 survivors, potentially limiting the generalizability of these findings to broader clinical settings or milder disease phenotypes. Therefore, the predictive models developed from this specific population require external validation in broader post-COVID cohorts. Third, the cross-sectional proteomic design captured only a single time point, necessitating longitudinal studies to track the temporal dynamics of the identified molecular signatures. Finally, the sample sizes for the less prevalent PCS subtypes were limited, underscoring the need for larger cohort studies to fully characterize these groups.

In conclusion, this four-year follow-up study reinforces the long-term safety of MSC therapy and provides both clinical and molecular evidence of its sustained association with a reduced risk of CFs in survivors of severe COVID-19. The proteomic data identify mitochondrial dysfunction as a central driver of PCS’s heterogeneity, with distinct clinical correlates across the PCS subtypes. In terms of CFs, MSC therapy correlates with key metabolic regulators (e.g., PGD), suggesting that MSCs may be linked to fatigue modulation via immunometabolic mechanisms. Conversely, a distinct immune-metabolically dysregulated endotype—marked by enhanced mitochondrial pathways, innate immune activation, and adaptive immune impairment—strongly associates with REs. This molecular divergence may help explain the subtype-specific association observed with MSC therapy, underscoring the need for mechanism-based and subtype-targeted therapeutic development. Collectively, our findings position MSC therapy as a possible candidate for the treatment of fatigue-predominant PCS, paving the way for larger studies to validate the molecular endotypes and advance precision medicine for PCS.
