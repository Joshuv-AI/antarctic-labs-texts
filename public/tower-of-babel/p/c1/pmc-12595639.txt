Exploring factors associated with clinical competence among nursing students volunteering in Indigenous communities: a cross-sectional analysis
Authors: An-Chi O Yang, Shiou-Fang Lu, Yi-Ting Hu, Fang-Tsuang Lu, Jen-Hung Wang, Pei-Ru Hu, Shu-Fen Lo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough volunteer service in Indigenous communities provides valuable experiential learning for nursing students, its impact on clinical competence remains underexplored. This study investigated associations among volunteer motivation, self-efficacy, and clinical competence in nursing students serving Indigenous communities.MethodsA cross-sectional, descriptive study was carried out among nursing students enrolled in five-year vocational and two-year technical programs in eastern Taiwan. Data were collected between July 1, 2021, and June 30, 2023, using structured questionnaires: the volunteer motivation scale, volunteer self-efficacy scale, and clinical internship competence scale. Statistical analyses included descriptive statistics, Pearson correlation, and stepwise multiple linear regression.ResultsA total of 123 nursing students were initially recruited; ten participants were excluded due to incomplete responses or withdrawal, yielding a final sample of 113 students (92% response rate; aged 17–25). Participants exhibited elevated levels of volunteer motivation (M = 94.42, SD = 10.30) and self-efficacy (M = 86.58, SD = 9.13), with moderate clinical competence (M = 58.04, SD = 6.88). Significant positive correlations were observed between clinical competence and both volunteer motivation (r = .87) and self-efficacy (r = .85). Stepwise multiple regression identified self-efficacy (β = 0.58, p < .001), internship grade (β = 0.14, p = .001), and volunteer motivation (β = 0.31, p = .001) as significant predictors, explaining 79% of the variance in clinical competence.ConclusionStructured volunteer service in Indigenous communities appears to enhance nursing students’ clinical competence, likely through increased motivation and self-efficacy. Integrating such experiences into nursing education may better prepare students for diverse clinical settings and improve readiness to address community health disparities. Future curricula should consider systematically incorporating structured, culturally responsive volunteer programs. Longitudinal studies are recommended to assess the long-term impact on professional development and health outcomes in underserved populations.Keywords: Nursing students, Clinical competence, Volunteer motivation, Self-efficacy, Indigenous communities, Service learning

Background

Nurses play a pivotal role in addressing structural and systemic factors that drive health disparities, particularly in underserved populations [1]. As healthcare systems evolve globally, preparing future nurses to advance population health and equity has become imperative [1, 2]. Service-learning, an approach integrating volunteer service with reflective learning, is increasingly recognized as an effective strategy to cultivate these competencies [3]. Immersive volunteer experiences expose nursing students to social determinants of health—such as socioeconomic barriers, cultural norms, and community dynamics—while fostering collaboration with marginalized groups [3, 4]. Through activities such as health education and preventive care delivery, students help address immediate care gaps and develop empathy, cultural sensitivity, and social accountability [5].

Beyond clinical skill development, participation in service-learning enhances self-efficacy, intrinsic motivation, and professional identity formation [6, 7]. According to Self-Determination Theory (SDT), fulfilling psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness drives intrinsic motivation that supports persistence, adaptability, and clinical competence [8]. Positive volunteer experiences further strengthen students’ confidence in managing complex care scenarios, thereby bridging theoretical knowledge and real-world practice [9, 10]. These competencies are especially relevant in resource-limited settings where students often serve on the frontline during crises such as the COVID-19 pandemic [11], post-disaster relief [12], suicide-prevention programs [13], and palliative-care outreach [14].

Despite these benefits, substantial gaps in healthcare access persist for rural and Indigenous populations [1]. Globally, Indigenous peoples experience disproportionately poorer health outcomes, including higher burdens of chronic disease, maternal mortality, and shorter life expectancy than non-Indigenous groups [15, 16]. In Taiwan, 16 Indigenous tribes comprise about 2.5% of the national population, with 28.3% residing in the eastern region—primarily Hualien and Taitung Counties [16], the catchment area of Tzu Chi University. These communities, historically marginalized by colonization and constrained by rural mountain geography, face persistent socio-economic disadvantage and have a life expectancy 8–10 years shorter than the national average, with higher rates of chronic liver disease, diabetes, and kidney disease [17]. Geographic remoteness limits access to preventive and acute care, further compounding these inequities [16]. Volunteer initiatives led by nursing students in these regions aim to mitigate disparities by improving healthcare access and fostering cultural competence [18].

Taiwan’s nursing education comprises two pathways: a technical–vocational track—a five-year program after junior high school followed by a two-year advanced technical program—and a baccalaureate track offered by universities. Vocational-track students constitute the largest share of the nursing workforce and enter clinical practice earlier than their baccalaureate counterparts [19]; yet their service-learning experiences in Indigenous communities remain underexplored. Prior research on nursing volunteerism has largely examined general student populations [20], providing limited insight into how Indigenous service-learning affects students’ motivation, self-efficacy, and clinical learning outcomes. This issue is critical because culturally complex environments demand competencies—such as climate-related disaster care, community partnership, and cultural responsiveness—that are seldom emphasized in conventional nursing curricula.

To address this gap, we focused on vocational-track students to explore how early-stage learners develop volunteer motivation, self-efficacy, and culturally responsive clinical competence through structured Indigenous service-learning. Guided by SDT [8] and Bandura’s self-efficacy framework [21], we examined how volunteer motivation, self-efficacy, and demographic factors relate to students’ clinical-internship competence in these culturally complex settings. We anticipated that our findings would clarify how psychosocial factors shape students’ transition from classroom to clinical practice and provide evidence to inform curriculum design in foundational nursing education.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional descriptive survey was conducted using a structured questionnaire and administered to approximately 3,000 undergraduate nursing students in the department of nursing at Tzu Chi university, located in eastern Taiwan where many Indigenous communities reside. The university was selected as the study site because of its large enrollment of Indigenous students and its long-standing service-learning partnerships with local Indigenous communities. The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for observational research [22].

Participants

Participants were recruited from both the five-year vocational and two-year technical nursing programs. The inclusion criteria were: (1) current enrollment in a clinical practicum with prior completion of basic nursing internships; (2) experience in volunteer service within Indigenous communities; and (3) active enrollment in a nursing degree program. Participants were excluded if they (1) had no prior Indigenous community volunteer experience or (2) were short-term international exchange students. Engagement in the volunteer activities was entirely voluntary, outside of course requirements, and not imposed by the research team.

The sample size was calculated using G*Power version 3.0.10. Based on the results of a pilot study and an F-test for linear multiple regression (fixed model, R² deviation from zero, effect size f² = 0.15, and seven predictors), it was estimated that a minimum of 103 participants would be required to achieve 80% statistical power with a significance level of α = 0.05. Considering a 20% anticipated rate of ineligibility or non-response, the final sample size was adjusted to 123 participants.

Instruments

Three structured questionnaires were administered: the Volunteer Motivation Scale (VMS), the Volunteer Self-Efficacy Scale (VSES), and the Clinical Internship Competence Scale (CICS), along with a demographic survey. All instruments had been originally developed and psychometrically validated in Chinese for use with Taiwanese nursing students; thus, no additional translation or cross-cultural adaptation procedures were required.

Demographic form

Demographic information collected included age, gender, level of nursing education, duration of volunteer service, total volunteer hours, average internship grade, degree of interest in nursing, and other relevant background variables.

Volunteer motivation scale (VMS)

The VMS was used to assess nursing students’ motivation to participate in volunteer service within Indigenous communities. This instrument was based on SDT [8] and had been informed by the research of Lin and Tseng [9] and Lu [10, 23]. The scale consists of 21 items, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater motivation. The total score range is from 21 to 105. Exploratory factor analysis identified five underlying factors, which together accounted for 78.03% of the total variance. The overall Cronbach’s alpha was 0.91, with subscale reliability coefficients ranging from 0.75 to 0.90. The content validity index (CVI) was 0.96.

Volunteer self-efficacy scale (VSES)

The VSES was used to measure students’ confidence in their ability to perform volunteer-related tasks. This scale was based on Bandura’s theory of self-efficacy [21], as well as research by Lin and Tseng [9] and Lu [10, 23]. The scale consists of 19 items, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater perceived self-efficacy. The total score ranges from 19 to 95. Factor analysis revealed four factors, accounting for 82.85% of the variance. The overall Cronbach’s alpha was 0.92, with subscale values ranging from 0.87 to 0.90. The CVI was 0.95.

Clinical internship competence scale (CICS)

The CICS was designed to assess students’ perceptions of the impact of their volunteer experience on their clinical competencies. The scale was based on SDT [8] and prior studies by Lin and Tseng [9] and Lu [10, 23]. The scale consists of 13 items, each assessed using a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater clinical competence. The total score ranges from 13 to 65. Factor analysis identified three components, which together accounted for 79.31% of the variance. The overall Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.93, with subscale values ranging from 0.90 to 0.91. The CVI was 0.93.

Data collection and procedure

The volunteer activities were part of the “Indigenous Holiday Classroom,” an informal, community-based service project initiated by nursing faculty and students, not linked to course credit or financial incentives. Students typically participated on weekends or holidays and received a brief orientation on cultural sensitivity, communication, and basic health-screening skills before service. Core activities included health-promotion education, vital-sign assessments for older adults, and academic tutoring for children. The research team introduced the study and explained its objectives to potential participants during service sessions or in their free time. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and additional consent was secured from legal guardians for those under 20 years of age. The structured questionnaire (approximately 10–15 min to complete) was administered at the end of the semester, coinciding with the conclusion of the service project (July 1, 2021 – June 30, 2023).

Data analysis

The data were processed using SPSS version 30.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, percentages, and total scores, were used to summarize the demographic information and responses from the questionnaires. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to examine differences in CICS scores, with post hoc pairwise comparisons performed using the Scheffé test. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were computed to explore the relationships among key variables, including VMS, VSES, and CICS scores. Stepwise multiple linear regression analysis was used to identify significant predictors of self-assessed internship competence. A p-value of less than 0.05 (two-tailed) was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hualien Tzu Chi Hospital (IRB No. 110-115-B) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after a full explanation of the study’s objectives, procedures, potential risks and benefits, and their right to decline or withdraw at any time without any impact on their academic standing or personal rights. For participants under 20 years of age, consent was additionally obtained from their legal guardians. All identifiable information was removed; data were anonymized, encrypted, stored in access-restricted files, and made available only to the research team to ensure strict confidentiality.

Results

Participant demographics and characteristics

Of 123 distributed questionnaires, 113 were valid (92% response rate; 6 incomplete, 4 withdrawals). Participants (mean age = 19.4 ± 1.8 years; 17–25 range; male: female = 1:10.3) were predominantly enrolled in a 5-year vocational nursing program (73.5%). Mean volunteer duration was 1.99 ± 1.08 years (69.69 ± 50.36 total hours), with primary activities being blood pressure monitoring (89.4%), child accompaniment (96.5%), and health education (63.7%). Most (54.9%) expressed strong nursing career interest, with mean clinical internship grades of 82.58 ± 3.96 (Table 1).

Correlations among volunteer motivation, self-efficacy, and clinical competence

Significant positive correlations were found between CICS and VSES (r = .85, p < .001) and VMS (r = .87, p < .001). Nursing interest correlated with CICS and VMS (both r = .22, p < .05). VMS correlated with volunteer years (r = .19) and hours (r = .18) (both p < .05). VSES correlated strongly with VMS (r = .89, p < .001) and nursing interest (r = .23, p < .05) (Table 3).

Stepwise multiple linear regression analysis of clinical competence predictors

A stepwise multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to identify significant predictors of CICS, based on prior correlation and ANOVA results. All continuous variables were standardized before analysis to generate standardized regression coefficients (β), allowing for direct comparison of the relative influence of each predictor. Categorical variables were dummy-coded, and multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with all values below 10 indicating no issues with collinearity. The final model identified three significant predictors: VSES (β = 0.58, t = 6.09, p < .001), internship grade (β = 0.14, t = 3.41, p = .001), and VMS (β = 0.31, t = 3.27, p = .001). Together, these variables accounted for 79% of the variance in clinical competence scores (R² = 0.79), and the overall model was statistically significant (F = 10.70, p < .001). These findings highlight the critical role of motivation, self-efficacy, and academic performance in predicting clinical competence among nursing students (Table 4).

Discussion

The main finding of this study is that volunteer motivation, self-efficacy, and internship grade were significant predictors of clinical-internship competence among nursing students engaged in Indigenous community service. This highlights that both psychological readiness and academic performance substantially contribute to clinical learning outcomes in culturally complex service-learning settings. Unlike prior studies that primarily involved urban or university-track nursing students [12, 24, 25], our participants were younger vocational-track students—often from lower-income households—who enter clinical practice earlier and serve in remote Indigenous communities. These contextual differences may have contributed to the stronger intrinsic motivation and sustained engagement observed in our participants. By focusing on this underserved and under-studied group, the study addresses a critical gap in the literature, offering new evidence on how volunteer motivation and self-efficacy shape clinical readiness in Indigenous-focused service-learning—an area largely overlooked in previous research [9, 10].

Volunteer motivation among nursing students

This study examined nursing students’ volunteer motivation through the lens of Self-Determination Theory (SDT) [8]. Intrinsic motivation—autonomy, competence, and relatedness—was markedly stronger than extrinsic drivers such as social obligation or incentives, indicating that participation was guided by personal growth and meaningful service experiences. This aligns with SDT and prior evidence that intrinsic engagement clarifies personal values and long-term goals [26], whereas workload and career pressures often hinder volunteering [20]. Participants—mainly 17- to 20-year-old vocational-track students—often come from less-advantaged socioeconomic backgrounds, enter clinical practice earlier, and face fewer external constraints than university-track peers. These developmental and contextual factors may explain their stronger autonomy- and competence-driven engagement. Their average volunteer duration of 1.99 years also supports SDT’s principle that fulfilling intrinsic needs sustains long-term persistence [8].Unlike most prior research centered on undergraduate or graduate nursing students [12–14], this study focused on vocational-track students (73.5%) who received brief preparatory training in cultural safety, social determinants of health, and basic health-screening skills within a structured service-learning model including reflection sessions [27, 28]. Such preparation likely enhanced confidence, peer bonding, and professional development [27, 28]. Although volunteerism in Chinese society is often seen as a social expectation [26], socially driven motives were least influential; intrinsic goals—such as improving service competence, interpersonal skills, cultural understanding, and collaborative capacity—were dominant, consistent with Dyson et al. [29] and Németh et al. [27].This study contributes novel evidence to an underexplored area by revealing how intrinsic motivation drives vocational-track students’ sustained engagement in Indigenous service-learning, thereby extending the literature beyond predominantly studied university nursing populations. These findings offer preliminary insights into the motivational dynamics of younger, socioeconomically diverse nursing students and highlight the need for further research on how early-stage nursing education can cultivate intrinsic motivation to prepare socially engaged, culturally competent, and clinically ready professionals.

Volunteer self-efficacy among nursing students

According to Bandura’s theory, self-efficacy arises from mastery and vicarious experiences, verbal persuasion, and emotional states [21]. Personal accomplishments build confidence, while observing successful peers or receiving affirmation further strengthens perceived ability; reduced stress also enhances efficacy [21, 26, 30]. High self-efficacy in nursing is linked to better academic performance, resilience, and stress management [30]. In this study, vocational-track students aged 17–20—often from less-advantaged socioeconomic backgrounds and entering clinical practice earlier than university-track peers—reported generally high self-efficacy, especially in vicarious experience. This suggests that seeing classmates succeed in Indigenous service-learning boosted their confidence to deliver culturally appropriate care. Such findings complement Lumish et al. [30], who showed service-learning improves self-efficacy and empathy in underserved settings. Belief in one’s ability influences motivation, effort, and persistence [31]. Our results indicate that volunteer service strengthened students’ motivation and supported growth in cultural competence, teamwork, and professional identity—key attributes for community-based Indigenous care [20]. This study contributes new evidence by documenting how self-efficacy develops within vocational-track students engaged in Indigenous service-learning—an area largely underexplored in earlier nursing literature—highlighting its role in preparing a culturally competent frontline workforce.

Clinical internship competence and predictors among nursing students

According to Bandura’s self-efficacy theory, individuals with strong efficacy beliefs engage more proactively with challenges and learn more effectively, reducing stress and clinical uncertainty [21, 31]. Volunteer motivation and self-efficacy are well-recognized predictors of clinical competence in nursing students [31]. In this study, stepwise regression revealed that volunteer motivation, self-efficacy, and academic performance significantly predicted students’ clinical-internship competence. Vocational-track students—typically younger, from less advantaged socioeconomic backgrounds, and entering practice earlier than university-track peers—showed that higher intrinsic motivation and stronger perceptions of service value were positively associated with self-assessed clinical skills. This highlights the importance of targeted support for this underserved group. These findings align with prior evidence that structured service-learning enhances clinical reasoning, cultural awareness, and professional identity [12, 26, 28], yet contrast with Prashar & Sharma [32], who cited workload as a barrier to volunteering. Our results suggest that well-organized Indigenous service-learning can complement formal curricula by fostering altruistic values, teamwork, and humanistic care, while meeting unmet community needs. This study contributes novel insights by identifying how motivation and self-efficacy jointly predict clinical competence in vocational-track nursing students—a population rarely examined in Indigenous service-learning research—thereby filling a critical gap in the literature on preparing culturally responsive frontline nurses.

Limitations

This study has three main limitations. First, we used purposive sampling from a single nursing institution in eastern Taiwan. Although this school enrolls students from different regions, the sample may not fully capture the national demographic or cultural diversity of nursing students, especially given the narrow age range (17–25). Future research should adopt multi-site or stratified sampling to improve generalizability and reduce selection bias. Second, the cross-sectional, self-reported design limits causal inference and may be influenced by social-desirability bias. Longitudinal or mixed-methods studies are recommended to evaluate the long-term effects of volunteer experiences on clinical competence, motivation, and self-efficacy. Finally, the potential impact of institutional financial support on volunteer participation warrants further investigation. Future studies should explore how financial aid and socioeconomic background influence students’ motivation, access, and engagement in service-learning, especially in resource-limited settings.

Conclusions

This study fills a critical gap by focusing on vocational-track nursing students serving Indigenous communities—an understudied group in prior volunteerism research. We found that volunteer motivation, self-efficacy, and academic performance are key predictors of clinical internship competence, highlighting the pivotal role of intrinsic motivation in sustaining long-term engagement and professional development. By integrating Self-Determination Theory and Bandura’s self-efficacy model, the study provides a framework for understanding how culturally responsive service-learning fosters clinical readiness. Practically, the findings support embedding inclusive, community-attuned service-learning into nursing curricula to strengthen students’ clinical competence, cultural sensitivity, and social accountability. Nevertheless, the use of purposive sampling from a single institution and a cross-sectional design limits generalizability and causal inference. Future multi-site, longitudinal studies are recommended to examine how such experiences influence professional identity formation, skill progression, and career intentions over time. These findings underscore the importance of institutional support and well-structured volunteer programs in preparing socially responsible nursing professionals capable of addressing health inequities in underserved populations.
