Thrombogenicity and the No-Reflow Phenomenon in STEMI
Authors: Anastasios Apostolos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: clot strength, no-reflow, PCI, STEMI, thromboelastography

The no-reflow phenomenon remains a significant challenge in the management of ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). Despite the technical success of primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) in restoring epicardial coronary artery patency, a notable subset of patients experience persistent microvascular obstruction, resulting in inadequate myocardial perfusion. This complication is associated with larger infarct sizes, impaired left ventricular function, and increased long-term morbidity and mortality.1 Decades of research have associated multiple factors, such as distal embolization, ischemic injury, reperfusion damage, and microvascular susceptibility, in the pathogenesis.2 Up to now, effective prevention and treatment strategies remain suboptimal. Against this background, the study by Omar et al3 introduces a novel perspective by linking thrombogenicity, assessed via platelet-fibrin clot strength (P-FCS), to the incidence and prognostic implications of no-reflow in STEMI patients undergoing primary PCI.

In this issue of JACC: Asia, Omar et al3 conducted a prospective study involving 334 STEMI patients treated with primary PCI at 2 South Korean centers. The no-reflow group showed higher levels of neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio (5.6 ± 4.4 vs 3.8 ± 3.3; P = 0.004), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (5.2 ± 8.9 vs 2.9 ± 7.2 mg/L; P = 0.078), N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (30,11 ± 7,286 vs 1,107 ± 3,268 pg/mL; P = 0.006), creatine phosphokinase-myocardial band (16 ± 38 vs 35 ± 76 ng/mL; P = 0.012), and troponin I (2.8 ± 6.4 vs 5.9 ± 12.5 ng/mL; P = 0.016) than the normal-reflow group. The no-reflow group had similar levels of platelet reactivity (258 ± 54 vs 272 ± 65 platelet reactivity units [PRUs]; P = 0.218) and coagulation activity (eg, fibrinogen, prothrombin time and activated partial thromboplastin time) compared with the normal-reflow group (Table 2). Using thromboelastography, a global hemostasis assay, Omar et al3 measured P-FCS from preprocedural blood samples to evaluate thrombogenicity, along with conventional hemostatic measurements. The results revealed an interesting association: patients with no-reflow (11.1% of the cohort) had significantly higher P-FCS levels (70.4 ± 5.9 mm) compared with those with normal reflow (66.6 ± 6.7 mm, P = 0.001). Other thromboelastography indices were not different between the groups. After multivariable analysis, only 2 variables remained as significant independent predictors for “no-reflow phenomenon” occurrence: P-FCS (per 1-mm increase: OR: 1.065; 95% CI: 1.008-1.125; P = 0.025) and post-dilation (OR: 2.496; 95% CI: 1.146-5.437; P = 0.021). The incidence of no-reflow escalated across P-FCS quartiles, from 3.7% in the lowest quartile to 15.6% in the highest (P = 0.007). Receiver-operating characteristic analysis identified an optimal P-FCS cutoff of 68.8 mm for predicting no-reflow. The most interesting finding is that patients exhibiting both no-reflow and high P-FCS (≥68.8 mm) faced a 6-fold increased risk of major adverse cardiovascular events at 3 years (adjusted HR: 6.654; 95% CI: 2.678-16.530; P < 0.001) compared to those with normal reflow and lower P-FCS.

This study stands out for its innovative use of TEG to assess thrombogenicity in the acute STEMI setting. Unlike traditional hemostatic tests, which focus on isolated pathways, TEG provides a comprehensive view of clot formation dynamics, capturing the interplay between platelets, fibrinogen, and coagulation factors. Previous research has established no-reflow as a multifactorial phenomenon, with contributions from thrombus burden, inflammation, and microvascular dysfunction. However, the specific role of clot strength had been explored in association with coronary microvascular dysfunction but not with no-reflow phenomenon. Kang et al.4 showed that high P-FCS (≥68 mm) significantly increased the risk of postprocedural CMD (OR: 4.35; 95% CI: 1.74-10.89) in 116 patients with acute myocardial infarction. Patients with both CMD and high P-FCS had a higher rate of ischemic events compared to non-CMD patients with low P-FCS (OR: 5.58; 95% CI: 1.31-23.68).4 Against the same background, Scarsini et al.5 performed TEG analysis of infarct-related coronary artery blood in 25 consecutive STEMI patients before and after successful pPCI and explored CMD, showing an association between the prothrombotic state of blood and CMD. Nevertheless, the small sample and the lack of P-FCS limits the findings of this study.5 By demonstrating that heightened P-FCS independently predicts no-reflow, Omar et al.3 bridge this gap, offering a biomarker that aligns with the thrombotic underpinnings of STEMI. The additive prognostic value of combining P-FCS with no-reflow status further enhances its clinical relevance, supporting the connection between hypercoagulability to adverse outcomes in acute myocardial infarction.6

The implications of these findings for clinical practice are profound. Current STEMI management relies heavily on standardized antithrombotic regimens, typically aspirin, a P2Y12 inhibitor, and heparin, according to existing guideline. However, the persistence of no-reflow in approximately 10%–15% of patients suggests that a “one-size-fits-all” approach may be inadequate. Measuring P-FCS at presentation could enhance risk stratification, identifying patients vulnerable to no-reflow who might benefit from intensified therapy. For example, high P-FCS patients could be treated with intracoronary glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors, which have shown efficacy in reducing microvascular obstruction in bailout scenarios. Alternatively, novel anticoagulants or tailored P2Y12 inhibitor dosing could be explored to modulate clot strength.

Despite its strengths, the study has notable limitations. The sample size (n=334) is modest, and its two-center design limits generalizability. Conducted exclusively in East Asian patients, the findings may not fully apply to other ethnic groups, particularly given known differences in thrombotic and bleeding profiles, as East Asians often exhibit higher bleeding risks with potent P2Y12 inhibitors, influencing treatment choices.7 The study’s reliance on TIMI flow grades to define no-reflow, while practical, overlooks subtler microvascular dysfunction detectable by advanced imaging or physiology evaluation.1 The single-point measurement of P-FCS also fails to capture its dynamic evolution during PCI, potentially missing key therapeutic windows. Additionally, the lack of data on how antithrombotic therapies modify P-FCS or outcomes leaves a critical question unanswered. The selection of P2Y12 inhibitors in this study warrants further discussion, as clopidogrel predominated (91.3%), which is in contrast with the guidelines from European Society of Cardiology and American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association, that favor potent P2Y12 inhibitors like ticagrelor or in STEMI.8,9 This choice reflects increased bleeding concerns in East Asians, often termed the “East Asian paradox,” where lower ischemic events coexist with increased hemorrhage risks, as evidenced by trials like PHILO and TICA KOREA showing higher bleeding with ticagrelor versus clopidogrel.10,11 However, the 2020 Asian Pacific Society of Cardiology consensus recommends ticagrelor (180 mg loading, 90 mg twice daily) or prasugrel (60 mg loading, 10 mg daily) over clopidogrel in STEMI patients undergoing primary PCI, supported by high-level evidence from PLATO and TRITON-TIMI 38, despite the limited Asian-specific data.12,13 A real-world study in East Asian STEMI patients further demonstrated that ticagrelor reduced no-reflow incidence (11.8% vs. 21.3% with low-load clopidogrel) and in-hospital mortality, potentially via adenosine-mediated vasodilation and enhanced coronary flow.14 Although Omar et al. observed no link between platelet reactivity and no-reflow, this suggests that global thrombogenicity measures like P-FCS may be more relevant than isolated P2Y12 effects. Future investigations should assess whether P-FCS-guided selection of potent P2Y12 inhibitors could optimize outcomes and compare potent P2Y12 inhibitors with clopidogrel in the terms of P-FCS.

The forementioned limitations pave the way for future investigations. Larger, multicenter studies across diverse populations are essential to validate the P-FCS–no-reflow association and establish population-specific cutoffs. Randomized trials should test whether P-FCS-guided interventions, such as intensified antithrombotic regimens, can prevent no-reflow and improve outcomes. Serial TEG assessments during and post-PCI might depict better the temporal dynamics of thrombogenicity, exploring the optimal timing for interventions. Mechanistic studies exploring how high P-FCS contributes to microvascular obstruction could shed the lights on novel therapeutic targets. Finally, the combination of P-FCS with other biomarkers, like troponin or hs-CRP might further advance risk models with improved predictive accuracy.

The work by Omar et al3 marks an important advance in understanding the no-reflow phenomenon, highlighting thrombogenicity as a modifiable risk factor in STEMI and showing that leveraging TEG-derived P-FCS might provide a more personalized STEMI care. Further studies are required for validating these results in non-Asian populations and use pre-PCI thrombogenicity profiling to guide real-time treatment decisions, to reduce no-reflow incidence and its devastating sequelae. By pursuing these research avenues, we can transform no-reflow from an unpredictable foe into a manageable challenge, ultimately improving survival and quality of life for STEMI patients worldwide.
