The Beclin1‑Targeting stapled peptide suppresses colorectal cancer progression by disrupting EGFR/β-catenin axis
Authors: Mingyuan Huang, Rentang Chen, Yuying Wen, Minsheng Lan, Sining Lian, Jin Xie, Qi Liu, Yixi Liu, Wenjie Lu, Guangxian Xu, Yanxiang Zhao, Xianxiu Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) remains a leading global cause of cancer-related mortality, demanding innovative therapies. Beclin1, a key autophagy and endolysosomal trafficking regulator with tumor-suppressive properties, is frequently downregulated in CRC. Although Beclin1 downregulation correlates with poor clinical outcomes, its regulatory mechanism in CRC remains largely undefined, and effective Beclin1-targeting therapeutic strategies remain limited. Our group previously developed a Beclin1-binding stapled peptide i7-01s-20 for Beclin1 activation, yet its anti-tumor potential and underlying mechanism in CRC have not been elucidated.MethodsWe analyzed Beclin1 and β-catenin expression and clinical correlations across public transcriptomic, proteomic, and human CRC patient tissue cohorts. A scrambled peptide (ScraP) was used as negative control. In vitro functional assays including autophagic flux detection, EGFR degradation assays, dual-luciferase reporter analysis, ubiquitination and cycloheximide chase tests were performed in CRC cell lines. Beclin1 knockdown rescue experiments were performed to verify target dependency. In vivo anti-tumor efficacy was investigated using DLD-1 subcutaneous xenograft tumor models with gradient-dose peptide treatment. Quantitative DIA proteomics of xenograft tumor tissues were conducted to profile global protein alterations induced by the peptide.ResultsBeclin1 expression was significantly decreased in human CRC tissues and correlated with advanced tumor stage and unfavorable patient survival, while β-catenin was highly accumulated in tumors and negatively correlated with Beclin1 at protein level. In CRC cells, i7-01s-20 potently promoted EGFR endolysosomal degradation, suppressed its downstream AKT/GSK-3β pathway, and triggered ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation of β-catenin. Silencing endogenous Beclin1 completely abrogated these effects triggered by i7-01s-20. Functionally, i7-01s-20 dose-dependently inhibited CRC cell proliferation, colony formation and migration in vitro, whereas its scrambled peptide ScraP exerted negligible biological activity at identical concentrations. In xenograft models, systemic i7-01s-20 administration produced robust tumor-suppressive effects without obvious systemic toxicity, accompanied by repressed AKT/β-catenin signaling in tumor tissues.ConclusionsThis work uncovers a previously uncharacterized Beclin1-dependent EGFR/β-catenin regulatory cascade in colorectal cancer. The stapled peptide i7-01s-20 achieves simultaneous blockade of two core oncogenic pathways by activating Beclin1. Our study provides mechanistic evidence supporting the Beclin1-targeted stapled peptide i7-01s-20 as a promising therapeutic candidate against CRC by disrupting EGFR/β-catenin signaling.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08608-x.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) represents one of the most prevalent malignancies in the digestive system, with its incidence and mortality rates showing a steady increase worldwide [1]. While early detection and surgical intervention can achieve curative outcomes for many patients, approximately 25% of patients are diagnosed at advanced stages with distant metastases, missing the optimal window for surgical resection [2]. Current therapeutic strategies for these patients primarily rely on chemotherapy and radiotherapy, which are frequently hampered by dose-limiting toxicities and the inevitable emergence of acquired resistance, underscoring the critical need for novel, targeted therapeutic strategies [3].

Macroautophagy (hereafter autophagy), an evolutionarily conserved metabolic process in eukaryotes, plays a crucial role in cellular homeostasis. During this process, cytoplasmic components including damaged proteins and organelles are sequestered within double-membrane vesicles termed autophagosomes, which subsequently fuse with lysosomes for degradation [4]. As a vital protective mechanism, autophagy regulates fundamental physiological processes such as nutrient metabolism, oxidative stress response, and cellular turnover [5, 6]. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated the intricate involvement of autophagy in multiple aspects of tumorigenesis and cancer progression, including metastasis [7].

Beclin1 (Bcl2-interacting myosin-like protein 1) is a well-characterized tumor suppressor and master regulator of autophagy. Substantial evidence demonstrates that Beclin1-mediated autophagy plays crucial roles in various pathological processes, including tumorigenesis and aging [8–10]. Genetic studies reveal that homozygous deletion of Beclin1 results in early embryonic lethality in mice, while heterozygous deletion predisposes to spontaneous tumor development, underscoring its tumor-suppressive function [11–13]. Beyond its canonical role in autophagy regulation, Beclin1 participates in multiple signaling pathways and has been implicated in tumor progression, metastasis, and poor clinical outcomes, establishing it as a promising molecular target for cancer therapy [10, 14, 15].

As the scaffolding protein, Beclin1 orchestrates autophagy initiation through its coiled-coil domain (CCD), where it interacts with autophagy activators Atg14L (Atg14-like protein) and UVRAG (UV radiation resistance-associated gene) [16–18]. In mammalian cells, UVRAG forms a heterodimer with Beclin1 to activate the class III PI3K (PI3KC3) complex, thereby promoting autophagosome formation. Notably, the Beclin1-UVRAG complex is critical for promoting not only autophagosome maturation but also the endocytic sorting and degradation of activated receptor tyrosine kinases, such as EGFR (Epidermal growth factor receptor) and HER2, thereby attenuating their potent oncogenic signaling [19].

The EGFR signaling axis is a well-validated driver of CRC progression, fostering cell proliferation, survival, and metastasis [20, 21]. Consequently, EGFR-targeted therapies have been integrated into clinical practice. However, their efficacy is often transient, limited by primary and acquired resistance mechanisms [21]. Parallel to EGFR, the Wnt/β-catenin pathway is aberrantly activated in the majority of CRC cases, primarily through Adenomatous Polyposis Coli (APC) mutations, leading to nuclear accumulation of β-catenin and transcriptional activation of pro-tumorigenic genes like c-Myc and cyclin D1 [22]. Intriguingly, significant crosstalk exists between the EGFR and Wnt/β-catenin pathways. EGFR activation can phosphorylate and destabilize the E-cadherin/β-catenin adhesion complex, liberating β-catenin from the membrane and enhancing its nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity [23, 24]. This convergence suggests that co-targeting these pivotal pathways could yield superior therapeutic outcomes.

We previously demonstrated the potent Beclin1-UVRAG interaction is critical for endolysosomal trafficking of EGFR, and developed a series of hydrocarbon-stapled α-helical peptides that specifically target the coiled-coil domain of Beclin1. These peptides disrupt Beclin1 homodimerization and stabilize the Beclin1-UVRAG heterodimer, thereby potentiating PI3KC3 activity [19, 20]. Our lead optimized peptide, i7-01s-20, exhibits high binding affinity for Beclin1 and has demonstrated potency to enhance autophagic flux and promote EGFR degradation [19, 23]. In this study, we investigate the anti-tumor efficacy and molecular mechanism of i7-01s-20 in CRC. We demonstrate that this stapled peptide concurrently induces the endolysosomal degradation of EGFR and the proteasomal degradation of β-catenin, effectively disrupting two major oncogenic signaling pillars supporting CRC growth and progression. Our findings not only elucidate a novel regulatory axis linking Beclin1 to Wnt/β-catenin signaling but also position i7-01s-20 as a promising dual-pathway inhibitory agent for CRC treatment.

Materials and methods

Reagents and antibodies

Chloroquine (CQ; Sigma) was prepared in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Epidermal growth factor (EGF; Gibco) was reconstituted in sterile water, while MG132 (Beyotime Biotechnology) and cycloheximide (CHX; MCE) were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). The following primary antibodies were used: anti-LC3B (NB100-2220) and anti-p62 (H00008878–B01) from Abnova; anti-EGFR (sc-80543) and anti-β-actin (sc-58673) from Santa Cruz Biotechnology; anti-β-catenin (M24002M) and anti-Beclin1 (T55092M) from Abmart; anti-E-cadherin (3195S), anti-Vimentin (5741S), anti-N-cadherin (13116S), anti-c-Myc (9402S), anti-p-GSK3β (5558S), anti-GSK3α/β (12456S), anti-STAT3 (9139S), anti-p-STAT3 (9145S), anti-p-AKT (4060S), anti-AKT (4691S), anti-ubiquitin (3936S), anti-Flag (14793S), and anti-GAPDH (2118S) from Cell Signaling Technology.

Chemical synthesis of stapled peptides

The stapled peptide i7-01s-20 was chemically synthesized and purchased from GL Biochem (Shanghai) Ltd. as described previously [19, 23]. In accordance with our previous studies, a cell-penetrating Tat sequence (YGRKKRRQRRR) was conjugated to the N-terminus of this peptide to enhance cellular penetration. The final product was validated by high-resolution mass spectrometry (MS) and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), confirming a purity of >95% (Supplementary Figure S1 & S2). A scrambled control stapled peptide (ScraP) with identical amino acid composition but random sequence was also synthesized by GL Biochem (Shanghai) Ltd. The purity of the scrambled peptide was verified to be > 95% via HPLC and MS (Supplementary Figure S3 & S4). Both i7-01s-20 and ScraP peptide were prepared as 20 mM stock solutions by dissolving the powder in distilled water for subsequent cellular experiments.

Cell lines and cell culture

Human colorectal cancer cell line RKO, DLD-1 and SW1116 were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). RKO and SW1116 cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. DLD-1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium with 10% FBS. All cells were kept at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂incubator.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was measured using the trypan blue exclusion method. Briefly, RKO and SW1116 cells were seeded in 96-well plates. After 24 hours culturing, cells were treated with indicated concentrations of i7-01s-20 for 24 or 48 hours. Viable cells were manually counted, and IC50 values were calculated by fitting data to a concentration-response curve. Experiments were performed with three biological replicates.

EGFR degradation assay

SW1116 cells cultured in 6-well plates were serum-starved for 12 hours, washed with PBS twice, and then treated with EGF (200 ng/mL) in the presence or absence of stapled peptide i7-01s-20. Cells were harvested at specified time points after EGF stimulation, and lysed for immunoblot analysis of EGFR levels.

Immunofluorescence assay

Cells grown on coverslips in 6-well plates were treated with EGF for the indicated time in the presence or absence of i7-01s-20. After treatment, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 minutes, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 minutes, and blocked with 5% BSA for one hour at room temperature. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-EGFR antibody (Santa Cruz) in 0.1% BSA solution, followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 488- or Alexa Fluor 594- conjugated secondary antibodies. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (Beyotime Biotechnology; P0131) before imaging.

Colony formation assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 500 cells per well and cultured overnight in an incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. The medium was then replaced with fresh culture medium containing the indicated concentrations of i7-01s-20, and the culture medium was subsequently refreshed every three days. After 10 days, cell colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet, and manually counted using ImageJ software.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

RKO and SW1116 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well, and transfected with TOP-flash or FOP-flash luciferase reporter plasmids (Beyotime Biotechnology; D2501, D2503) upon reaching 70–80% confluence. TOP-Flash is a TCF-reporter plasmid, along with its negative control FOP-Flash enables quantitative measurement of the β-catenin/TCF signaling in cells transfected with these plasmids. After 24 hours, the cells were treated with stapled peptide i7-01s-20 at indicated concentrations for an additional 24 hours. Luciferase activity was assessed using the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (Beyotime). The relative Wnt/β-catenin signaling activity was calculated by normalizing firefly luciferase luminescence to that of Renilla, after subtracting background values from blank wells.

Wound healing assay and transwell assay

For wound healing assay, confluent monolayers in 6-well plates were scratched using a sterile pipette tip. Wound closure was monitored at 24-hour intervals. For transwell migration assays, 5 × 104 cells in serum-free medium were placed in the upper chamber, while the lower chamber contained medium with 10% FBS. After 48 hours, migrated cells were fixed with 4% methanol, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and counted from at least three microscopic fields.

Subcellular fractionation assay

Cytoplasmic and nuclear protein fractions were isolated using the Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Kit (Vazyme, E101-01) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were harvested after i7-01s-20 treatment, washed with pre-cooled PBS, and sequentially incubated with cytoplasmic and nuclear extraction buffers supplemented with protease inhibitors. Fractions were collected by differential centrifugation at 4 °C. GAPDH was used as a cytoplasmic marker to assess cytoplasmic contamination in nuclear fractions, and histone H3 served as the nuclear loading control.

Co-immunoprecipitation assay

Cell lysates were pre-cleared with protein A/G agarose beads (Santa Cruz Biotechnology; sc-2003) for 1 hour at 4 °C. Immunoprecipitation was performed overnight at 4 °C using 2 μg primary antibody and 50 μl of protein A/G beads. Beads were washed three times using ice-cold PBS or cell lysis buffer, and bound proteins were eluted by boiling in loading buffer for subsequent analysis.

In vivo tumor xenograft model

BALB/c nude mice (female, 4–6 weeks old) were purchased from Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Use and Care of Animals Committee at Guangdong Medical University. To establish xenograft models, 8 × 106 DLD-1 cells were subcutaneously injected into the flanks of mice. Tumor volumes and the body weights of mice were measured regularly. When the average tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3, mice were randomized into three groups and treated daily via intraperitoneal injection with PBS, i7-01s-20 (10 mg/kg), or i7-01s-20 (30 mg/kg). Tumor volume (mm3) was calculated using the following formula: (length × width2) /2, where length represents the longest axis and width is the measurement perpendicular to the length. Mice were sacrificed at the study endpoint, and tumors were excised for further analysis.

Proteomic and bioinformatics pathway analysis

Tumor tissues from five mice per group were lysed in 5% sodium dodecyl sarcosinate buffer containing 100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.5, with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails. Samples were homogenized, sonicated, boiled, and centrifuged, and protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay. For each sample, 100 μg protein was reduced with DTT, alkylated with IAA, and digested overnight at 37 °C with trypsin at a 1:50 enzyme-to-protein ratio. Peptides were purified using MCX solid-phase extraction columns, dried, reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid, spiked with iRT peptides, and analyzed by DIA-LC–MS/MS on an Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer coupled to a Vanquish Neo LC system. Peptides were separated on a 15-cm EASY C18 column with a 60-min 5–35% acetonitrile gradient. DIA data were acquired with an MS1 scan range of 380–980 m/z and 299 MS2 isolation windows, and raw data were processed using DIA-NN v1.8.1. Searches were performed with trypsin specificity allowing one missed cleavage, carbamidomethylated cysteine as a fixed modification, and methionine oxidation and protein N-terminal acetylation as variable modifications. Peptide- and protein-level FDRs were controlled at 1%.

Differentially expressed proteins were determined using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. Proteins with nominal p < 0.05 and |log₂FC| >0.26 were considered differentially expressed. GO enrichment analysis was performed using Fisher’s exact test followed by Benjamini–Hochberg correction, and terms with adjusted p < 0.05 were considered significant. GSEA was carried out in R using the clusterProfiler package and MSigDB Hallmark gene sets. Proteins were ranked according to log₂FC, and enriched gene sets were considered significant when |NES| >1.5, nominal p < 0.05, and FDR q < 0.25.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis and graphing were performed using the GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. All quantitative data are represented as mean ± SEM from at least three independent biological replicates, unless otherwise specified. Statistical significance was assessed by either two-tailed, unpaired Student’s t-test or ordinary one-way ANOVA for multiple cohorts. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

(1) Beclin1 expression is negatively correlated with β-catenin levels and its downregulation predicts poor prognosis in CRC

To elucidate the clinical relevance of Beclin1 in cancer, we first interrogated public transcriptomic and proteomic databases. Examination of Beclin1 expression across various tumor types in the TCGA cohort revealed differential Beclin1 expression across 14 cancer types, with colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) exhibiting significantly reduced Beclin1 expression (Fig. 1A). Analysis of the CPTAC dataset via UALCAN indicated a significant downregulation of Beclin1 protein levels in COAD tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 1B). The mRNA expression data from the TCGA database also showed significant decreased Beclin1 mRNA levels in COAD samples (n = 469) versus adjacent normal tissues (ANT; n = 41) (Fig. 1C). Besides, an overall decrease of Beclin1 mRNA expression was observed in COAD tissues in comparison with their paired ANT samples (Fig. 1D). This reduction in Beclin1 expression is clinically relevant, as downregulation correlated strongly with advanced tumor stages in COAD patients (Fig. 1E). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed that low levels of Beclin1 were closely related to poor overall survival (OS) probability in COAD patients (Fig. 1F). Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of our clinical cohort further confirmed the diminished Beclin1 protein expression in CRC specimens relative to matched normal mucosa (Fig. 1G). Given the established role of aberrant Wnt/β-catenin signaling in colorectal cancer pathogenesis, we then examined β-catenin levels in colorectal cancer and its correlation with Beclin1 expression. Analysis of TCGA datasets revealed that the mRNA levels of β-catenin were significantly upregulated in colorectal cancer tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues (ANT) (Fig. 2A), and its expression was positively correlated with higher tumor grades (Fig. 2B). An overall increase of β-catenin mRNA levels was observed in COAD tissues in comparison with their paired ANT samples (Fig. 2C). Kaplan–Meier survival plot revealed that high mRNA level of β-catenin was closely related to poor clinical outcomes in COAD patients (Fig. 2D). IHC staining of patient tissues revealed a mutually exclusive pattern: while Beclin1 was abundant in normal epithelia and lost in tumors, β-catenin was weakly expressed in normal tissues but markedly accumulated in CRC (Fig. 2E). In accordance with our IHC data, analysis of CPTAC proteomics database demonstrated that the β-catenin protein level was negatively correlated to that of Beclin1 (Fig. 2F). This inverse correlation at the protein level was further validated in a panel of five CRC cell lines (RKO, SW480, SW1116, LOVO, and HCT116), where Beclin1 and β-catenin protein levels exhibited a strong negative correlation (R = −0.9244) (Fig. 2G&H). Previous studies have demonstrated that β-catenin itself serves as a substrate for autophagic clearance, as it interacts with LC3 through its LC3-interacting region (LIR), enabling degradation by the autolysosomal pathway [20]. We hypothesized that the inverse Beclin1-β-catenin correlation might be mediated through an autophagy-dependent mechanism. Co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays was conducted to test whether Beclin1 interacts directly with β-catenin. However, our co-IP results failed to detect any physical interaction between Beclin1 and β-catenin (Supplementary Figure S5). This finding explicitly argues against the possibility that Beclin1 modulates β-catenin protein levels through the autophagy pathway, thereby redirecting our focus to alternative regulatory cascades.

(2)The designed Beclin1-targeting stapled peptide i7-01s-20 enhances autophagy activity and promotes EGFR degradation in colorectal cancer cells

To dissect the regulatory axis underlying the Beclin1-β-catenin inverse correlation, we leveraged hydrocarbon-stapled peptides that we previously engineered to target the coiled-coil domain of Beclin1, reducing Beclin1 homodimerization and promoting Beclin1-UVRAG heterodimer formation [19, 23]. These stapled peptides enhance autophagy and facilitate endolysosomal degradation of EGFR and HER2, resulting in significant antiproliferative effects in cancer models, including EGFR-positive non-small cell lung cancer cells and HER2-positive breast cancer cells [19, 23]. To enhance binding affinity for Beclin1, we had also further optimized the stapled peptides through the introduction of novel staple designs and residue substitutions. This effort resulted in the development of i7-01s-20, which exhibits an approximately 30-fold improvement in Beclin1 binding [23].

Herein, we examined the functional impact of our lead compound, i7-01s-20, on autophagy and EGFR degradation in two colorectal cancer cell lines, RKO and SW1116. Among 63 colorectal cell lines in the human protein atlas, SW1116 exhibited relatively higher level of EGFR expression while RKO showed lower level of EGFR (Supplementary Figure S6). The cellular autophagic activity was assessed by detecting the protein levels of LC3 and p62. EGFR is a transmembrane receptor that frequently overexpressed or aberrantly activated in colorectal cancer [24]. Given the Beclin1-UVRAG complex is known to modulate endolysosomal trafficking of receptor tyrosine kinases, we then examined the effect of i7-01s-20 on EGFR degradation. Western blot analysis demonstrated that i7-01s-20 significantly accelerated EGFR degradation following EGF stimulation. Treatment of i7-01s-20 at 10 μM resulted in a modest decrease in EGFR levels in SW1116 cells, with roughly 40% decrease at 180-min post-EGF stimulation (Fig. 3E&F). This EGFR-degradation promoting effect was particularly pronounced in RKO cells, where over 60% of EGFR was degraded within 180 minutes (Fig. 3G&H). We further visualized this process using immunofluorescence microscopy. Prior to EGF treatment, endogenous EGFR was predominantly localized at the plasma membrane as a well-defined thin layer. Upon i7-01s-20 treatment, approximately half of EGFR was internalized at 60 minutes, forming cytosolic puncta structures, and was nearly completely cleared from the cells within 90 minutes post-stimulation. In contrast, untreated cells retained strong EGFR fluorescence at the plasma membrane without significant internalization (Fig. 3I). These findings demonstrate that i7-01s-20 potently redirects EGFR from the membrane to the endolysosomal degradation pathway.

(3) Stapled peptide i7-01s-20 inhibits the EGFR downstream AKT/GSK3β pathway and the nuclear translocation of β-catenin

It has been reported that EGFR closely interacted with the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway in tumorigenesis, in particular, EGFR can activate β-catenin via receptor tyrosine kinase-PI3K/AKT pathway [25]. The canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway relies on the dynamic regulation of β-catenin protein stability. As a dual-function protein, β-catenin participates in both cell adhesion and transcriptional regulation. When anchored at the plasma membrane, β-catenin interacts with E-cadherin to form adherens junctions that mediate intercellular adhesion. EGFR activation is reported to induce phosphorylation of β-catenin, triggering its dissociation from the membrane and promoting translocation to the cytosol [22]. Accumulated cytoplasmic β-catenin subsequently enters the nucleus, where it associates with transcription factors of the TCF/LEF family to initiate the expression of downstream target genes, including c-Myc, cyclin D, and transcription factors like snail. The switch of β-catenin from the plasma membrane to the nucleus leads to its aberrant activation that is strongly correlated with cancer progression and poor prognosis, particularly in tumors like colorectal carcinoma where mutations in the Wnt pathway are common [26].

We hypothesized that enhanced EGFR degradation would impair its downstream signaling, i.e., PI3K/AKT signaling and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) pathway. Western blot analysis showed i7-01s-20 treatment at 20 μM markedly reduced phosphorylated AKT (Ser473) and STAT3 (Tyr705) levels (Fig. 4A). Crucially, we observed a significant decrease in the inhibitory phosphorylation of GSK3β at Ser9 (Fig. 4B), which could be a direct consequence of diminished AKT activity. Since active GSK3β phosphorylates β-catenin to target it for proteasomal degradation, we reasoned that reactivation of GSK3β would lead to degradation of β-catenin, a master regulator of the canonical Wnt pathway. Indeed, i7-01s-20 treatment resulted in a concentration-dependent reduction of total β-catenin protein across multiple CRC cell lines (Fig. 4C). This downregulation further resulted in the functional suppression of the β-catenin-mediated transcriptional process, as evidenced by decreased protein levels of its key oncogenic targets, c-Myc and cyclin D1 (Fig. 4D). The reduction in β-catenin signaling profoundly affected epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), a cellular process that significantly contributes to tumor aggression by promoting tumor cell migration and metastasis [27]. EMT is characterized by the so-called “cadherin switch”, where the epithelial marker E-cadherin is downregulated, while the mesenchymal marker N-cadherin is upregulated. Discussion

The pursuit of effective therapies for advanced colorectal cancer remains a pressing oncological challenge, particularly given the limitations of current targeted agents with acquired resistance and insufficient efficacy [28]. The EGFR and Wnt/β-catenin pathways represent two core oncogenic drivers in CRC [21]. Their aberrant activation fuels cell proliferation, survival, and metastasis, while crosstalk between these pathways further reinforces malignant progression [29]. In this study, we unveil a novel therapeutic strategy that simultaneously undermines these two cornerstone signaling pathways in CRC, through the targeted activation of the master regulatory node—Beclin1.

Our work first establishes the clinical relevance of Beclin1 as a pivotal regulator in CRC. Through analysis of public datasets and our own clinical cohort, we confirm that Beclin1 expression is significantly downregulated in CRC tissues compared to adjacent normal mucosa. This reduction correlates with advanced tumor stages and poor patient survival, reinforcing Beclin1’s well-recognized role as a haploinsufficient tumor suppressor [12]. Notably, we uncover a striking inverse correlation between Beclin1 and β-catenin protein levels in both clinical specimens and CRC cell lines. This observation suggests that Beclin1 may function as an upstream modulator of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, a relationship not fully delineated in prior CRC research. Although previous studies have suggested that β-catenin can be degraded through autophagy via the p62-LC3 axis, co-IP assays did not detect an interaction between Beclin1 and β-catenin in CRC cells. In line with this, blocking autophagy pharmacologically did not restore β-catenin levels after i7-01s-20 treatment, whereas proteasome inhibition led to a clear rescue of β-catenin accumulation. These results suggest that autophagy activation acts upstream in this process, rather than directly mediating β-catenin clearance in our system.

The hydrocarbon-stapled peptide i7-01s-20, designed to stabilize the Beclin1-UVRAG heterodimer, served as the key tool to dissect this relationship. This design is rooted in our previous work showing that Beclin1-UVRAG complex formation enhances endolysosomal trafficking of EGFR and potentiates autophagic flux [19, 23]. We demonstrated that i7-01s-20 effectively enhances autophagic flux and, more critically, potently accelerates the endolysosomal degradation of EGFR. This direct antagonism of EGFR signaling led to reduced phosphorylation of AKT, which in turn relieved AKT-dependent inhibitory phosphorylation of GSK3β. Reactivated GSK3β then triggered the ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of β-catenin, not its autophagic clearance, as confirmed by inhibitor studies. This cascade represents a key mechanistic insight: Beclin1 activation by stapled peptide i7-01s-20 indirectly but potently suppresses β-catenin stability through intercepting its upstream EGFR-AKT signaling axis.

The functional consequences of this dual-pathway inhibition induced by i7-01s-20 are profound. The degradation of β-catenin led to the suppression of its transcriptional program, evidenced by downregulation of c-Myc and cyclin D1. Furthermore, we observed a marked reversal of EMT, a key process in metastasis, characterized by restored E-cadherin expression and loss of N-cadherin and vimentin. These molecular changes culminated in significant inhibition of CRC cell proliferation, clonogenic survival, and migratory capacity in vitro. Critically, i7-01s-20 exhibited dose-dependent anti-tumor efficacy in a CRC xenograft model, reducing tumor volume by ~40% and tumor weight by ~50% at the highest dose, without causing systemic toxicity or organ damage. Quantitative proteomic profiling of treated tumors further validated the pathway-specific effects of i7-01s-20, highlighting downregulation of Wnt signaling pathway proteins, including β-catenin itself, as a central mechanism of action.

Our findings position the Beclin1-EGFR-β-catenin axis as a therapeutically vulnerable signaling circuit in CRC. They align with and extend previous work suggesting that the Beclin1-UVRAG complex can suppress Wnt/β-catenin signaling, potentially through mechanisms involving E-cadherin membrane localization [30]. By designing a stapled peptide that specifically reinforces the Beclin1-UVRAG interaction, we have leveraged this endogenous regulatory mechanism for therapeutic gain. While our previous work demonstrated the anti-tumor potential of Beclin1-targeting stapled peptides in non-small cell lung cancer and breast cancer [23, 31, 32], this study is the first to delineate their efficacy in CRC through the Beclin1-EGFR-β-catenin axis. This dual-action mechanism to simultaneously degrade an oncogenic RTK and destabilize a key transcriptional regulator offers a novel approach that may circumvent the resistance often encountered with direct kinase inhibitors or individual signaling component modulators. Hydrocarbon-stapled helical peptides have been widely used to modulate difficult protein–protein interactions, although most studies have focused on p53 or Bcl-2 family proteins [33]. Our group previously developed Beclin1-specific stapled peptides, including Tat-SP4 and i7-01s-31, which primarily inhibited single oncogenic receptor pathways in lung and breast cancer models, without addressing crosstalk with the Wnt pathway [32, 34]. In contrast, the optimized peptide i7-01s-20 exhibits approximately 30-fold higher affinity for Beclin1 and simultaneously suppresses EGFR and β-catenin signaling in CRC. By comparison, conventional small-molecule autophagy modulators, such as mTOR agonists or lysosomal activators, often lack specificity and may induce off-target toxicity or compensatory pro-proliferative signaling [10]. Notably, i7-01s-20 preferentially stabilizes the Beclin1-UVRAG complex, consistent with our clinical observations linking low Beclin1 expression to poor prognosis in CRC. Moreover, unlike previous reports describing direct p62-LC3-mediated autophagic degradation of β-catenin, our data indicate that peptide-induced autophagy functions upstream to suppress EGFR/AKT signaling, thereby promoting proteasome-dependent β-catenin degradation. Together, these findings define a regulatory axis linking Beclin1 to Wnt signaling in colorectal cancer.

Several limitations of our study warrant consideration. First, while our data strongly support the EGFR-AKT-β-catenin cascade as the primary mechanism, we cannot exclude the contribution of additional pathways to i7-01s-20’s efficacy. Moreover, future studies should evaluate the peptide’s performance in patient-derived xenograft models or genetically engineered mouse models to better recapitulate clinical CRC heterogeneity.

In conclusion, our study elucidates a previously unappreciated link between Beclin1-directed endolysosomal trafficking and the control of Wnt/β-catenin signaling in CRC. We identify i7-01s-20 as a promising dual-pathway inhibitor that effectively targets both EGFR and β-catenin through Beclin1 activation, demonstrating potent antitumor potency in vitro and in vivo. These findings validate the Beclin1-EGFR-β-catenin axis as a therapeutically vulnerable node in CRC and provide a strong rationale for further development of Beclin1-targeting stapled peptides. Future efforts to optimize this agent and explore its combination with standard chemotherapies or other targeted drugs may offer a new therapeutic avenue for patients with advanced CRC.
