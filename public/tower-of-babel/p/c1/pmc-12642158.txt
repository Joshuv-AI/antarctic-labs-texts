The synergy of therapeutic vaccination and timely diagnosis for TB control in high-burden settings: Nunavut as a case study
Authors: Elaheh Abdollahi, Seyed M. Moghadas, Alison P. Galvani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough the overall prevalence of Tuberculosis (TB) in Canada is relatively low, Indigenous communities are disproportionately burdened, with active TB rates exceeding those of Canadian-born non-Indigenous populations by more than 55-fold. In response, the Government of Canada and Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami have launched an initiative to eliminate TB among Inuit by 2030. We evaluated strategies underpinning this initiative, including the potential use of therapeutic TB vaccines that are currently undergoing clinical trials.MethodsWe developed an agent-based model of TB transmission dynamics that includes the demographic distributions and household structure of the Nunavut population, as well as TB progression at the individual level. We simulated the model with combinations of interventions, including treatment of active cases, testing, and contact-tracing. We further evaluated the impact of vaccinating active TB cases and those identified with latent TB infection (LTBI) to project the reduction of TB incidence over a 20-year time horizon starting from 2025. In scenario analyses, we considered different vaccine efficacies, diagnostic delays, and rates of contact-tracing.ResultsIn the absence of a therapeutic vaccine, we estimated a maximum reduction of 20.0% (95% Uncertainty Range [UR]: 15.5% – 24.8%) in active TB incidence with a 4-week diagnostic delay and 75% contact-tracing rate, over the simulated time horizon. Vaccination of active TB cases and those identified with LTBI was projected to decrease the incidence by 87.0% (95% UR: 68.8% – 97.5%) by 2045. Contact-tracing of non-household members exhibited a marginal effect on reducing TB incidence in the presence of vaccination, especially when diagnostic delay was shortened.ConclusionsOur findings highlight the importance of rapid TB diagnosis. The availability of therapeutic vaccines can substantially enhance TB control efforts towards achieving the elimination goal.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), remains one of the deadliest infectious diseases worldwide, responsible for over 1.3 million deaths annually [1]. Despite targeted efforts over the past three decades that have reduced TB-related mortality, the disease continues to impose significant health and socioeconomic burdens [1]. While high-income countries have a low incidence overall, the disproportionate burdens borne by marginalised populations are dramatic [2]. For example, Indigenous populations in the Canadian Arctic region have one of the highest TB incidence rates globally [3]. The Nunavut population, in particular, endures incidence rates of over 40 times higher than the overall rate in Canada [3].

The Global End TB Strategy formulated by the World Health Organization (WHO) aims to achieve a 90 % reduction in TB incidence and a 95 % reduction in deaths by 2035, compared to baseline levels in 2015 [4], However, progress towards these goals has been slow, with only an 11 % decline in TB incidence and a 9.2 % decrease in mortality achieved between 2015 and 2020 [5]. Aligned with the WHO TB strategy, the Government of Canada and Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami have jointly developed TB elimination action plans, aiming to reduce the incidence of active TB by 50 % (from approximately 200 to no more than 100 cases per 100,000 population) by 2025 and to eliminate the disease by 2030 in the Inuit Nunangat [6,7]. Our previous work demonstrated that elimination with current measures is not achievable within the timelines outlined in these action plans [8]. However, therapeutic vaccination could facilitate the goal of TB elimination by reducing treatment failure and preventing recurrence (relapse) following treatment.

Several TB vaccine candidates are currently in active clinical trials, utilising mycobacterial-live attenuated, protein/adjuvant, and RNA platforms, including H107/CAF10b (phase 1), MTBVAC (phase 2a, 2b, and 3), BNT164a1 and BNT164b1 (phase 2a), ID93 + GLA-SE (phase 2a), BCG (phase 3), GamTBvac (phase 3), and M72/AS01E (phase 3) [9]. In contrast to prophylactic vaccines, which aim to prevent initial infection or disease severity, therapeutic vaccines are designed to bolster the immune response and improve treatment success rates in TB patients, while reducing the risk of developing disease or relapse after initial cure [9]. The WHO preferred product characteristics (PPC) describe desired therapeutic vaccines with an efficacy of at least 50 % in reducing both treatment failure and TB recurrence following a standard course of drug treatment [10,11]. Examining the impact of introducing new tuberculosis vaccines in low- and middle-income countries when they become available, a recent study found that vaccination could significantly reduce TB cases and deaths [12]. While global TB control efforts using therapeutic vaccines are likely to prioritise high-burden settings, it is crucial to also consider underserved populations within lower-burden countries. Furthermore, evaluating the synergistic effects of vaccination in conjunction with interventions currently in practice will help improve targeted strategies for TB control.

In this study, we developed a simulation model of TB transmission dynamics to assess the impact of TB control measures individually and in combination with a therapeutic vaccine that meets the WHO PPC standards. Considering Nunavut as the study population with a high burden of TB, we determined timelines for achieving elimination goals. In particular, we quantified the effect of contact-tracing and testing with delays in diagnosis of active TB cases.

Methods

We extended a previously established agent-based simulation model in the context of Nunavut [8] to integrate various TB interventions currently in practice, including case finding, testing, contact-tracing, and drug treatment (Fig. 1). The natural history of the disease was implemented to include latent infection, active disease, treatment, and potential relapse. The model population was stratified into five age groups (0–4, 5–19, 20–49, 50–64, and 65 + years), with household distributions parameterised by the demographics of Nunavut [13,14]. Contact patterns between and within age groups were informed by empirical data on daily interactions both inside and outside households (Table A1),[15] with the proportion of inside versus outside contacts drawn from Canadian contact survey data (Table A2) [15]. Community contacts were further classified as frequent (80 %) representing repeated interactions such as those with coworkers or classmates and casual (20 %), representing occasional encounters in public spaces [15,16].Fig. 1Schematic structure of the model for the transmission dynamics of TB, including treatment and therapeutic vaccination.

Natural history and transmission dynamics of TB

We implemented the natural history of TB by considering the epidemiological statuses of susceptible, non-infectious latent TB infection (LTBI), and infectious active TB (Fig. 1). Susceptible individuals were defined as those who have never been infected or who had been previously infected and were successfully treated. Infected individuals enter the LTBI state with an age-dependent risk of progression to active TB throughout their remaining life expectancy [17,18]. We modelled the duration of LTBI by fitting a probability distribution to Kaplan-Meier estimates of tuberculosis-free survival, thereby capturing the wide range of clinical variability in latency, which can range from weeks to decades [19]. Latency times were sampled for each infected individual conditional on remaining life expectancy at the time of infection, preventing implausibly long periods among older individuals [18,19].

Disease transmission in the model occurs through contacts between susceptible individuals and those with active, smear-positive and/or pulmonary TB, with a per-contact transmission probability calibrated to observed epidemiological data. While contact patterns were parameterised (Appendix Tables A1, A2) using age groups (0–4, 5–19, 20–49, 50–64, and 65 + years), individuals retained their exact age within the model, and infectiousness was applied at the individual rather than group level.

Current interventions

We implemented an array of interventions that are currently in practice for TB control, including case finding and isolation of active TB patients, contact-tracing and testing, and drug treatment. As the baseline, we assumed a 4-week delay between the onset of active TB and diagnosis [20,21], before the start of treatment. Following the start of treatment, TB patients were home-isolated for two weeks, during which their daily contacts were limited to household members [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24]]. We considered directly observed therapy (DOT) of TB patients, with an 8-week initial phase of daily Isoniazid (INH), Rifampin (RMP), Pyrazinamide (PZA), and Ethambutol (EMB), followed by a 4-month continuation phase with INH and RMP (Appendix Table A3) [24]. Active TB cases were assumed to be non-infectious after completing two weeks of treatment, with a reduction of 50 % in their infectivity after the first week [25]. Treated patients became susceptible again with a probability that depends on the success rate of the treatment regimens [26]. In our model, treatment failure was defined as the persistence of viable bacilli after treatment of active TB, resulting in potential relapse, and as failure of preventive therapy for LTBI, resulting in progression to active disease.

We assumed that all household members of an active TB case under treatment were screened using a Tuberculin Skin Test (TST). For identification of active TB and LTBI among non-household members, we implemented contact-tracing and testing of individuals classified as frequent contacts within four weeks prior to identification. For treatment of individuals identified as LTBI, a 3-month course of weekly INH and RMP was implemented (Appendix Table A4).

Vaccination

We assumed that a therapeutic vaccination meeting the WHO PPC would become available in 2025, with a minimum efficacy of 50 % in reducing treatment failure and recurrence for active TB, and a duration of 20 years of protection [27]. In scenarios with LTBI vaccination, we considered a 50 % reduction in both treatment failure and risk of progression to active TB [10]. In our model, vaccination did not alter the risk of reinfection post-treatment. In primary analyses, therapeutic vaccination was offered to all active TB cases. In secondary analyses, coverage was extended to include individuals with LTBI identified through contact-tracing. In vaccination scenarios, we assumed all eligible individuals (i.e., diagnosed active TB cases and identified LTBI) were vaccinated alongside their treatment plan.

Model parametrisation

Disease progression and intervention effects were parameterised using published estimates and the model calibration was conducted to reproduce TB incidence in Nunavut. While the lifetime risk of developing active TB after new infection is often estimated at 5 %–10 % [28], the majority of this risk is concentrated within the first few years post-infection. The probability of developing active TB was 45 % within the first year of infection and increased to 98 % within twelve years [19]. Progression from LTBI to active TB without treatment ranged from 2 % to 40 % [17,19], with the timing of activation drawn from a survival distribution conditional on remaining life expectancy [18,19]. Children under 12 years of age with active TB were assumed to have a 95 % lower risk of transmitting the disease compared to adolescents and adults [20]. Consistent with clinical data, we incorporated a 3 % case fatality rate for active TB patients [29]. LTBI treatment was assumed to be 93 % effective, with a 95 % probability of completion. Scenario analyses

In the baseline scenario without vaccination, we used a 25 % contact-tracing rate of frequent contacts, in addition to testing all household members of an active TB case. We further assumed that TB active cases were identified within 4 weeks of developing symptoms. We then varied the rate of contact-tracing in the presence of vaccination with an efficacy of 50 % in reducing both treatment failure and TB recurrence, and simulated scenarios with a shorter delay of two weeks for active TB diagnosis. Additional analyses were performed when vaccine efficacy was increased to 80 % to project the reduction of TB incidence over a 20-year time horizon (Appendix Table A5).

Model implementation and calibration

The model was implemented in the Julia language and calibrated using annual incidence of TB in Nunavut from 2009 to 2018 [32]. We applied the least-squares method to determine the transmission probability per contact. The calibrated model was then simulated for seven additional years until 2025 without vaccination. We then introduced the vaccine in 2025 and ran the model for an additional 20 years, with a weekly timestep. Incidences of active TB cases on an annual basis were derived and scenarios were compared to determine the median and uncertainty range of reduction achieved over the study period using 500 independent simulations for each scenario. The computational model is available at https://github.com/Ellie-Abdollahi/TB.

Results

Baseline scenario with treatment

We found that maintaining the baseline interventions (i.e., a 4-week diagnostic delay of active cases, 25 % contact tracing of frequent contacts, and testing all household members) would result in a median of 5 % increase in TB incidence over a 20-year time horizon of simulations (Fig. 2). Increasing contact-tracing to 75 % of frequent contacts reduced active TB incidence by 20.0 % (95 % Uncertainty Range [UR]: 15.5 % – 24.8 %) compared to the baseline. Vaccination of active TB cases

Compared to the baseline scenario, vaccination of active TB cases reduced the incidence by a median of 22.2 % (95 % UR: 1.2 % – 54.9 %) over the simulated period without any contact-tracing outside households (Table 2). When contact-tracing increased to 75 %, the median reduction of TB incidence was more than twofold higher at 48.9 % (95 % UR: 6.6 % – 76.8 %). Vaccination of both active TB cases and LTBI

Extending the program to vaccinate individuals identified with LTBI in addition to those with active TB had a substantial effect on reducing the incidence (Fig. 1). For example, without contact-tracing and a 4-week diagnostic delay, the incidence of active TB was projected to decrease by a median of 65.0 % (95 % UR: 29.9 % – 84.7 %) over the 20-year simulated period. This reduction was even more pronounced, reaching 87.0 % (95 % UR: 68.8 % – 97.5 %) when the contact-tracing rate increased to 75 % (Table 2).

Shortening diagnostic delay to 2 weeks, vaccination resulted in median reductions of 92.3 % (95 % UR: 79.0 % – 100 %) without contact-tracing, and 96.3 % (95 % UR: 86.0 % – 100 %) with a 75 % contact-tracing rate (Table 2). While vaccination of LTBI showed a substantial impact on improving program effectiveness (Fig. 2), the marginal difference between the reductions achieved for incidence of active TB in scenarios without and with contact-tracing was only 4.0 %.

Additional analyses with higher vaccine efficacy (80 %) showed similar but slightly improved reductions in TB incidence, with detailed results reported in Table A5 of the Appendix.

Discussion

TB remains a major global health challenge, particularly in high-burden settings. We evaluated the synergistic effects of measures currently in practice and a potential therapeutic vaccine in Nunavut as a population disproportionately burdened by TB. Our analysis shows that without vaccination, rapid case identification is critical for reducing active TB incidence, but even with intensive contact-tracing, a 50 % reduction is not achievable before 2035 (Table 2). Vaccinating only active TB cases improves outcomes but still falls short of the Inuit Nunangat Action Plan target. In contrast, extending vaccination to include individuals with LTBI enables a 50 % reduction within 5 years of vaccine introduction, even with a 4-week diagnostic delay and no contact-tracing. Under diagnostic delays of less than 2 weeks, incidence reductions exceeding 90 % become achievable by 2045, though not before 2035. With a therapeutic vaccine meeting the WHO PPC requirements, vaccination of both active TB cases and household LTBI cases can achieve a > 50 % incidence reduction within 5 years, and when combined with rapid case detection, can suppress incidence by > 90 % within 20 years. Additional analyses assuming an 80 % vaccine efficacy showed similar but slightly greater reductions in TB incidence (Appendix Table A5), supporting the robustness of our findings. Importantly, in the presence of vaccination and timely treatment initiation, contact-tracing beyond households showed limited additional impact. Overall, while a potential therapeutic vaccine could considerably improve TB control in Nunavut, full elimination remains infeasible within the current action plan timelines.

Numerous studies have examined various TB control strategies, including treatment and vaccination, using transmission dynamic modelling [12,27,[34], [35], [36], [37], [38]]. Our findings are consistent with those presented in recent studies on the impact of therapeutic vaccination in low- and middle-income countries,[12] especially when combined with rapid diagnosis and effective treatment [12,37]. We demonstrated that vaccinating individuals with LTBI can substantially enhance program effectiveness by reducing progression to active disease, thereby diminishing reliance on costly and resource-intensive contact-tracing or community-wide screening efforts. While vaccinating only active TB cases improves treatment outcomes and lowers relapse, it does not address the much larger reservoir of latent infection. Extending vaccination to both active and latent TB therefore yields a stronger population-level effect by targeting the primary source of future disease.

Our analysis did not differentiate between vaccine types, and we recognize that a single product may not be suitable for vaccinating both LTBI and active TB cases. Considering the WHO PPC for new tuberculosis vaccines, we evaluated their effects in preventing progression from latent infection to active disease, and in reducing relapse or treatment failure among patients receiving treatment for active TB. Therefore, our modelling was framed as a scenario analysis to illustrate the potential benefits that could be achieved if either type of vaccine were to become available.

Our study has several limitations. First, our method considers transmission dynamics in a single synthetic population representing the demographics of Nunavut. However, the Arctic region of Canada comprises 53 distinct communities within Inuit Nunangat. Second, we relied on empirical data for daily interactions to generate contact matrix patterns, which may be imbalanced across age groups. Such imbalances have been shown to introduce bias in compartmental deterministic models [43]. Although our model employs agent-based processes, the impact of assuming balanced contacts between groups remains uncertain, as outcomes may be influenced by many other factors that are subject to stochasticity. Third, we only accounted for the age-specific risk of TB activation, neglecting pre-existing comorbidities such as diabetes, cancer, chronic lung disease, and smoking that may substantially influence the rate of disease progression. For example, the prevalence of tobacco use among adolescents in Nunavut is more than four times the national average [39]. Fourth, our baseline scenario was calibrated with current TB control practices, assuming a 25 % contact-tracing rate and an average TB diagnosis time of 4 weeks. However, contact-tracing may have fluctuated over time, and diagnostic delays can vary depending on community proximity to TB testing and laboratory facilities. Fifth, we used vaccine effectiveness for reducing both treatment failure and TB recurrence but did not account for the number of vaccine doses or the short durability of vaccine protection. Moreover, vaccination scenarios were implemented for all cases of active TB and/or LTBI, and differential vaccine targeting was not evaluated; however, vaccines may be approved for specific age groups in the population. Sixth, in the absence of Nunavut-specific treatment success rates, we relied on global estimates, yet we understand that outcomes vary by treatment regimens and patient adherence. Seventh, we did not account for residual immunity from prior TB infection, which may influence reinfection or relapse risk. Seventh, we assumed 100 % vaccination coverage of active TB cases and identified LTBI to provide upper-bound estimates of the potential impact of therapeutic vaccines, while recognizing that prioritisation, eligibility, and other individual-level factors would affect vaccine uptake. Finally, our study did not address programmatic aspects of TB vaccination, such as delivery logistics, dosing strategies, and vaccine acceptance.

Conclusions

Our study shows that improving current TB control strategies in Nunavut through rapid case diagnosis and enhanced contact-tracing will reduce the incidence, but will remain inadequate to reach the goals of action plans. Therapeutic vaccination can play a significant role in accelerating the progress towards the elimination goal, potentially reducing TB incidence by over 90 % within 20 years of implementation.
