Leukoglycemic index and inflammatory markers in acute abdominal pain: Comparison of non-specific pain and acute appendicitis severity
Authors: Muhammet Ali Erinmez, Erkan Güler, Rojhat Kurt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe diagnosis of acute appendicitis and the early identification of complicated disease remain challenging in emergency practice. This study aimed to evaluate the potential role of the leukoglycemic index (LGI) in distinguishing acute appendicitis from non-specific acute abdominal pain and to explore its performance in identifying complicated appendicitis, in comparison with commonly used inflammatory markers.MethodsThis retrospective study included adult patients presenting with acute abdominal pain between 2017 and 2024. Patients were categorized as controls with non-specific abdominal pain, non-complicated acute appendicitis, or complicated acute appendicitis. LGI, white blood cell count (WBC), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) were calculated at admission. Multivariable logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were performed.ResultsA total of 642 patients were analyzed, including 320 with acute appendicitis and 322 controls. Among patients with appendicitis, 179 (55.9%) had non-complicated and 141 (44.1%) had complicated disease. LGI was significantly higher in patients with acute appendicitis compared with controls (p < 0.001) and was independently associated with appendicitis in the adjusted multivariable model (OR 60.0, p < 0.001), with diagnostic performance comparable to WBC (AUC 0.844 vs. 0.867). In addition, age, sex, and comorbidity status were also significantly associated with appendicitis in the adjusted analysis. In patients with complicated appendicitis, LGI, NLR, WBC, and SII were significantly elevated compared with non-complicated cases (all p < 0.001). In ROC analysis for complicated appendicitis, LGI demonstrated good discriminative ability (AUC 0.857), while WBC showed an AUC of 0.826; NLR and SII showed the highest overall performance (AUC 0.951 and 0.931, respectively).ConclusionsLGI is independently associated with acute appendicitis and demonstrates diagnostic performance broadly comparable to white blood cell count. However, it does not outperform simpler, routinely available markers, and its clinical utility remains uncertain. In addition, its potential incremental value beyond established inflammatory markers was not formally assessed and therefore cannot be inferred from the present data. LGI may be considered as a complementary parameter requiring further validation.Keywords: Acute appendicitis, Complicated appendicitis, Inflammatory markers, Leukoglycemic index, Receiver operating characteristic analysis

Introduction

Acute appendicitis is one of the most common causes of acute abdominal pain requiring emergency surgical intervention. Early and accurate diagnosis is essential, as delayed diagnosis may result in disease progression, leading to perforation, abscess formation, sepsis, and increased morbidity. Although diagnostic imaging modalities such as ultrasonography and computed tomography have improved diagnostic accuracy, their availability may be limited in certain emergency settings due to institutional resources, patient-related factors, or time constraints. Current guidelines emphasize the need for a combined approach incorporating clinical evaluation, laboratory parameters, and imaging findings, highlighting the importance of accessible diagnostic tools, particularly in the emergency department setting [1].

Various laboratory parameters and inflammatory indices have been investigated to support the diagnosis of acute appendicitis. Among these, white blood cell count, C-reactive protein, and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio are the most frequently studied markers. Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, in particular, has been shown to have diagnostic value in distinguishing acute appendicitis from other causes of abdominal pain and in predicting disease severity, including complicated appendicitis. However, reported cut-off values and diagnostic performances vary considerably across studies, and none of these indices has demonstrated sufficient reliability to be used as a standalone marker for clinical decision-making, especially in differentiating complicated from non-complicated appendicitis [2].

The leukoglycemic index, calculated as the product of white blood cell count and serum glucose level, reflects both systemic inflammatory response and stress-induced hyperglycemia. Previous studies have primarily evaluated the leukoglycemic index in cardiovascular and critical care settings, where it has been shown to be a significant predictor of mortality and major complications, particularly in patients with acute myocardial infarction. Meta-analytic evidence suggests that the leukoglycemic index has acceptable prognostic performance for adverse outcomes in acute inflammatory conditions. Despite its theoretical relevance, the role of the leukoglycemic index in acute appendicitis has not been adequately investigated [3].

Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the leukoglycemic index in patients with suspected acute appendicitis and to compare its predictive value with other commonly used inflammatory indices. In addition, this study aimed to evaluate the performance of the leukoglycemic index in distinguishing complicated from non-complicated acute appendicitis, particularly in emergency settings where rapid and accessible decision-support tools are needed to guide clinical management.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective observational study was conducted at the Emergency Department of Mersin City Training and Research Hospital. Patients who presented between February 3, 2017, and January 1, 2024, and were diagnosed with acute appendicitis according to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) diagnostic codes were retrospectively identified using the hospital electronic medical record system.

Patients aged 18 to 65 years who were evaluated for suspected acute appendicitis based on physical examination, laboratory findings, and imaging studies, and who had complete clinical and laboratory data, were included. Patients younger than 18 years or older than 65 years were excluded to minimize age-related confounding. Additional exclusion criteria included a known history of diabetes mellitus, malignancy, or pregnancy, as well as missing or incomplete clinical or laboratory data. The control group consisted of patients presenting with acute abdominal pain during the same study period who underwent clinical, laboratory, and radiological evaluation and were discharged from the emergency department without a diagnosis requiring hospitalization or surgical intervention. All control patients were retrospectively reviewed through the hospital electronic medical record system, and those who re-presented and were subsequently diagnosed with acute appendicitis or another surgical pathology were excluded to minimize misclassification. From the eligible control population, the first patients meeting the inclusion criteria were included in the analysis, without additional matching or randomization.

The classification of complicated and non-complicated appendicitis was based on both intraoperative findings and histopathological reports, as documented in surgical notes and pathology records. Complicated appendicitis was defined by the presence of perforation, gangrene, periappendiceal abscess, or phlegmon/plastron formation. As this was a retrospective study, the classification relied on routine clinical documentation, and no independent reassessment or formal interobserver agreement analysis was performed. Some degree of interobserver variability between surgical and pathological assessments may therefore exist. However, this approach reflects real-world clinical practice, where classification is based on operative and histopathological reports. The patient selection process is summarized in Fig. 1.

Data collection

Demographic and clinical data were retrospectively collected from patient records using a standardized data collection form. Demographic variables included age and sex. Laboratory parameters obtained at the time of admission to the emergency department were recorded, including white blood cell count (WBC, ×10³/µL), neutrophil count (NEU, ×10³/µL), lymphocyte count (LYM, ×10³/µL), platelet count (PLT, ×10³/µL), serum glucose (mg/dL), and C-reactive protein (CRP, mg/dL). From these laboratory values, inflammatory indices were calculated as follows: the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was defined as the neutrophil count divided by the lymphocyte count, the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) as the platelet count divided by the lymphocyte count, and the leukoglycemic index (LGI) was calculated as (WBC [×10³/µL] × serum glucose [mg/dL]) / 1000.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome of the study was to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the leukoglycemic index and other inflammatory parameters in distinguishing patients with acute appendicitis from controls. Secondary outcomes included comparison of these parameters between complicated and non-complicated acute appendicitis subgroups and assessment of their predictive value for complicated disease.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. As most continuous variables were not normally distributed, data were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as number and percentage and compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Variables with potential clinical relevance and variables showing baseline imbalance between groups were considered for multivariable logistic regression analyses. Because of potential collinearity between composite inflammatory indices and their component variables, adjusted models were constructed by including LGI together with key clinical covariates, including age, sex, and comorbidity status. In the analysis of complicated acute appendicitis, WBC was excluded from the multivariable model to reduce collinearity with LGI. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The diagnostic performance of laboratory parameters and inflammatory indices was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. Exploratory optimal cut-off values were determined using the Youden Index. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Toros University Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Committee (Decision date: 17 April 2024; Decision No: 2024/80).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 642 patients were included in the study. Of these, 320 patients (49.8%) were diagnosed with acute appendicitis, while 322 patients (50.2%) constituted the control group. Among patients with acute appendicitis, 141 patients (44.1%) were classified as having complicated appendicitis and 179 patients (55.9%) as having non-complicated appendicitis.

The leukoglycemic index was significantly higher in patients with acute appendicitis compared with controls (median 1.34 [IQR 0.93–1.70] vs. 0.74 [0.60–0.91], p < 0.001). Similarly, white blood cell count was higher in the acute appendicitis group (12.75 [9.17–15.66] vs. 7.28 [6.17–8.59], p < 0.001). Predictors of acute appendicitis

Multivariable logistic regression analysis identified the leukoglycemic index (LGI) as an independent predictor of acute appendicitis (odds ratio 60.0, 95% confidence interval 29.8–121.0, p < 0.001) (Table 2). In the adjusted model including age, sex, and comorbidity status, LGI remained strongly associated with acute appendicitis, supporting the robustness of the observed association. Age was inversely associated with the likelihood of appendicitis (p = 0.009), while female sex was associated with lower odds compared with male sex (p < 0.001). The presence of comorbidity was also independently associated with increased odds of acute appendicitis (p = 0.027).

Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis demonstrated that the leukoglycemic index had good diagnostic performance for the prediction of acute appendicitis, with an area under the curve of 0.844 (95% CI 0.813–0.874). The ROC curve of LGI for the diagnosis of acute appendicitis is shown in Fig. 2. This performance was comparable to that of white blood cell count (AUC 0.867, 95% CI 0.839–0.895). In contrast, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (AUC 0.649, 95% CI 0.606–0.691) and systemic immune-inflammation index (AUC 0.651, 95% CI 0.609–0.693) demonstrated only moderate discriminative ability. Notably, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio demonstrated an AUC below 0.5 (AUC 0.333, 95% CI 0.292–0.375), suggesting a potential inverse association with acute appendicitis in this cohort rather than simply poor diagnostic performance. This finding may be explained by the higher PLR values observed in the control group, possibly reflecting the heterogeneous nature of patients presenting with non-specific abdominal pain. In addition, as a composite index influenced by both platelet and lymphocyte counts, PLR may exhibit context-dependent behavior across different clinical populations and should therefore be interpreted with caution. C-reactive protein also exhibited limited discriminative value (AUC 0.577, 95% CI 0.533–0.621). Optimal cut-off values were determined using the Youden Index. For the diagnosis of acute appendicitis, the optimal cut-off value for LGI was 1.11, yielding a sensitivity of 66.6% and a specificity of 90.7% (Youden Index: 0.57). For the prediction of complicated appendicitis, the optimal cut-off value for LGI was 1.34, with a sensitivity of 84.4% and a specificity of 76.0% (Youden Index: 0.60).

Comparison between complicated and non-complicated acute appendicitis

Comparisons of clinical and laboratory parameters between patients with complicated and non-complicated acute appendicitis are shown in Table 3. Patients with complicated appendicitis had significantly higher leukoglycemic index values than those with non-complicated appendicitis (1.66 [1.42–2.05] vs. 1.03 [0.77–1.33], p < 0.001). Similarly, the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio was markedly elevated in complicated cases (8.63 [6.65–11.98] vs. 2.84 [1.93–4.28], p < 0.001). In addition, white blood cell count and systemic immune-inflammation index were significantly higher in the complicated appendicitis group, whereas lymphocyte count was significantly lower. Predictors of complicated acute appendicitis

In multivariable logistic regression analysis excluding WBC to avoid collinearity, both LGI and NLR remained independently associated with complicated acute appendicitis. LGI was associated with a more than fourfold increase in the odds of complicated disease (OR 4.41, 95% CI 1.36–14.30, p = 0.013), while NLR also demonstrated strong independent predictive value (OR 2.78, 95% CI 2.00–3.87, p < 0.001). In contrast, PLR and SII did not retain statistical significance after multivariable adjustment, suggesting that their predictive contribution was largely explained by overlapping inflammatory components.

Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis further supported these findings. NLR showed excellent discriminative ability for predicting complicated acute appendicitis (AUC 0.951), followed by SII (AUC 0.931), while LGI demonstrated good predictive performance (AUC 0.857). C-reactive protein showed no meaningful discriminative value (AUC 0.511). Detailed diagnostic performances are summarized in Table 4.

Discussion

In this study, several routinely available inflammatory markers and indices were evaluated for their diagnostic and severity-related value in acute appendicitis, with particular emphasis on the leukoglycemic index (LGI). Patients with acute appendicitis had significantly higher LGI and white blood cell (WBC) values compared with the control group presenting with non-specific acute abdominal pain (LGI: 1.34 [IQR 0.93–1.70] vs. 0.74 [0.60–0.91], p < 0.001; WBC: 12.75 [9.17–15.66] vs. 7.28 [6.17–8.59], p < 0.001). LGI emerged as an independent predictor of acute appendicitis in the adjusted model (OR 60.0, 95% CI 29.8–121.0, p < 0.001).

In analyses stratified by appendicitis severity, patients with complicated acute appendicitis demonstrated significantly higher LGI, NLR, WBC, and SII values, accompanied by lower lymphocyte counts, compared with those with non-complicated disease (all p < 0.001). In multivariable analysis excluding WBC to address collinearity, both LGI (OR 4.41, 95% CI 1.36–14.30, p = 0.013) and NLR (OR 2.78, 95% CI 2.00–3.87, p < 0.001) remained independently associated with complicated appendicitis. Receiver operating characteristic analysis further demonstrated excellent predictive performance of NLR (AUC 0.951) and SII (AUC 0.931) for complicated disease, while LGI also showed good discriminative ability (AUC 0.857). However, the potential additive value of LGI beyond these established markers was not formally evaluated in this study and therefore cannot be inferred. Importantly, although LGI demonstrated good diagnostic performance, it did not outperform simpler or more established markers such as WBC for diagnosis or NLR and SII for predicting complicated appendicitis. This finding warrants careful interpretation. From a purely discriminatory perspective, the use of LGI alone may not provide incremental benefit over these markers. However, the clinical relevance of LGI may lie not in superior accuracy, but in its ability to integrate two biologically distinct yet clinically relevant processes—systemic inflammation and stress-induced hyperglycemia—into a single parameter. In this context, LGI may reflect an additional metabolic–inflammatory component of the acute physiological response. However, the potential additive or combined diagnostic value of LGI with established inflammatory markers was not formally evaluated in this study. Therefore, LGI should not be interpreted as a replacement for established markers, and any assumption regarding its incremental clinical role should be made with caution. Future studies are needed to determine whether LGI provides additional value when used in combination with other indices.

The concept of combining metabolic and inflammatory parameters into composite indices is not novel. Several markers, such as the triglyceride–glucose index combined with neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (TyG-NLR), TyG-SII, and CRP-TyG, have been investigated as indicators of inflammatory–metabolic burden in acute and chronic conditions, with some studies demonstrating improved predictive performance when used in combination with established markers [4–6]. However, these indices typically require additional biochemical parameters such as triglyceride levels, which are not routinely obtained in many emergency settings. In contrast, LGI is derived from parameters more commonly available during initial evaluation. Nonetheless, direct comparisons between LGI and these composite indices were not performed in the present study, and further research is needed to determine their relative clinical performance.

The diagnostic and severity-related performance of the leukoglycemic index (LGI) is biologically plausible because it combines two clinically relevant signals in acute illness: leukocytosis as a marker of systemic inflammatory activation and hyperglycemia as a reflection of metabolic stress. Experimental and clinical literature shows that hyperglycemia can promote a pro-inflammatory milieu and impair effective immune cell function, supporting the concept that elevated glucose during acute illness may track with a more dysregulated systemic response [7]. Within this framework, combining white blood cell count and blood glucose into a single composite index may offer an integrated snapshot of inflammatory burden plus stress response rather than relying on either parameter alone. In cardiovascular acute care populations, LGI has been directly evaluated and shown to have prognostic relevance in acute myocardial infarction cohorts, including both diabetic and non-diabetic patients, reinforcing that the composite signal carries clinically meaningful information [3, 8]. Similarly, more recent evidence from other acute thrombo-inflammatory conditions has applied the same LGI definition and reported associations with adverse outcomes, supporting the broader applicability of this composite marker across acute inflammatory states [9].

An important methodological strength of the present study is the inclusion of a control group consisting of patients presenting with non-specific acute abdominal pain, which allowed a more clinically realistic evaluation of diagnostic performance. In recent literature, systemic inflammatory indices—particularly the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII)—have been increasingly evaluated as adjunctive tools for both diagnosis and risk stratification in acute appendicitis. A contemporary systematic review specifically assessing SII reported that this index has potential diagnostic utility for acute appendicitis and may contribute to distinguishing complicated from non-complicated disease, although heterogeneity across studies and thresholds indicates that SII should be interpreted within the overall clinical context rather than as a standalone test [10]. Consistently, a large retrospective cohort examining the clinical significance of SII in acute appendicitis demonstrated that higher SII values were associated with greater pathological severity and were higher among surgically treated versus conservatively managed patients, supporting the concept that systemic inflammatory indices track with disease burden [11].

Against this background, our findings highlight the added value of evaluating diagnostic markers against a heterogeneous control population: LGI demonstrated robust discrimination between appendicitis and non-specific abdominal pain and retained independent significance in multivariable analysis, with performance comparable to WBC. In contrast, indices such as NLR and SII may behave differently depending on whether the endpoint is diagnosis or severity, as suggested by recent cohort data emphasizing that systemic inflammatory indices (including NLR, PLR, and SII) may be particularly informative for preoperative stratification and severity assessment, with age-related differences also influencing inflammatory profiles [12]. Taken together, these results reinforce that no single laboratory parameter is sufficient for definitive diagnosis. In this control-referenced diagnostic setting, LGI remained independently associated with acute appendicitis; however, whether it improves clinical decision-making beyond established markers remains uncertain. Most previous studies evaluating complicated acute appendicitis have focused on classical inflammatory markers and composite indices reflecting inflammatory burden, such as neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio and systemic immune-inflammation index. Large cohort data have shown that lymphocyte count, neutrophil count, white blood cell count, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, and CRP-based composite indices are associated with increasing histopathological severity of appendicitis, with the most pronounced abnormalities observed in gangrenous disease, although the overall explanatory power of these models remains limited [13]. Similar severity-oriented behavior of inflammatory indices has also been demonstrated in pediatric populations, where neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio, lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio, and systemic immune-inflammation index were associated with complicated appendicitis in an age-dependent manner, but with reduced discriminative performance compared with uncomplicated disease [14]. In this context, the present study extends the existing literature by demonstrating that the leukoglycemic index, a marker integrating leukocytosis with stress-related hyperglycemia, is independently associated with complicated appendicitis despite the absence of prior appendicitis-specific studies evaluating this index. Although LGI did not outperform established inflammatory indices such as NLR or SII, its independent association suggests that it captures an additional metabolic–inflammatory dimension not fully reflected by inflammation-based markers alone. These findings indicate that LGI should not be viewed as a replacement for established severity markers, but rather as an additional parameter that may provide contextual information in the early risk stratification of patients with suspected complicated acute appendicitis. C-reactive protein (CRP) is widely reported to be elevated in patients with acute appendicitis and is frequently used in clinical practice as an inflammatory marker. However, in the present study, CRP demonstrated no meaningful discriminative ability for distinguishing complicated from non-complicated appendicitis. This finding should be interpreted with caution. CRP is a time-dependent marker that typically increases 12–24h after the onset of inflammation, and early measurement in an emergency department setting may therefore underestimate its diagnostic value. In addition, the use of a single measurement rather than serial assessment may further limit its performance [15]. Previous studies have also suggested that CRP alone provides only moderate diagnostic accuracy and performs better when combined with other clinical or laboratory parameters [16]. Taken together, our findings do not necessarily indicate that CRP lacks clinical relevance, but rather that its standalone performance may be limited under specific clinical conditions, particularly in early presentations and heterogeneous patient populations.

The results of this study suggest that the leukoglycemic index (LGI) is associated with both acute appendicitis and complicated disease; however, its clinical role beyond established inflammatory markers remains uncertain. When assessed against a heterogeneous control population, LGI demonstrated diagnostic performance broadly comparable to white blood cell count and retained independent significance in multivariable analysis. For severity assessment, inflammatory indices such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio and systemic immune-inflammation index remained more informative for identifying complicated appendicitis. Therefore, LGI should not be interpreted as a standalone marker or as a replacement for established inflammatory indices. An additional practical consideration is that LGI requires serum glucose measurement, which may not be universally obtained alongside complete blood count parameters in all emergency settings. Although glucose testing is frequently included in routine evaluation of patients with acute abdominal pain, it is typically ordered as part of a separate biochemical panel. Therefore, the assumption of immediate availability should be interpreted with caution, and this may represent a practical limitation compared with indices derived solely from complete blood count.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the retrospective design and single-center setting may limit the generalizability of the results and introduce selection bias. Second, laboratory parameters, including the leukoglycemic index, were assessed at a single time point at presentation, and dynamic changes during the clinical course were not evaluated. Third, imaging findings and established clinical scoring systems were not incorporated into the analytical models, which may have influenced both diagnostic and severity assessments. Although exploratory Youden-derived cut-off values were calculated for LGI in the present study, these thresholds were not externally validated and should not be interpreted as clinically established cut-off values. Their diagnostic performance may vary according to clinical context, case-mix composition, including the use of non-specific abdominal pain controls, and pre-test probability. Therefore, the immediate clinical applicability of these cut-off values remains limited, and further prospective validation is required before they can be used for decision-making. Another important limitation relates to the use of serum glucose as a component of LGI. In emergency settings, glucose levels are influenced by multiple factors unrelated to appendicitis, including fasting status, recent oral intake, pain-induced stress response, intravenous fluid administration, and inter-individual metabolic variability. Although patients with known diabetes were excluded to reduce confounding, variability related to stress hyperglycemia remains substantial. In addition, patients older than 65 years were excluded to reduce clinical heterogeneity and the potential impact of comorbidities on inflammatory and metabolic parameters. However, this may further limit the generalizability of the findings, and the results should be interpreted with caution in older populations. This inherent instability may have attenuated the discriminatory performance of LGI and could partly explain why the addition of glucose to WBC did not improve, and in some analyses slightly reduced, diagnostic accuracy compared with WBC alone. In addition, although glucose testing is frequently performed in clinical practice, its simultaneous and universal availability alongside complete blood count parameters cannot be assumed across all emergency settings, which may limit the practical applicability of LGI. Furthermore, the potential additive or combined diagnostic value of LGI with other inflammatory markers was not formally evaluated in this study, precluding any conclusions regarding incremental benefit. Similarly, no direct comparison was performed between LGI and other metabolically integrated composite indices, such as TyG-based markers, which share conceptual similarities. Therefore, the interpretation of LGI should be made with caution, particularly in heterogeneous emergency populations. Finally, prospective multicenter studies with standardized diagnostic pathways are needed to validate these findings and to further define the role of LGI in both diagnostic and severity-oriented clinical decision-making.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the leukoglycemic index is independently associated with acute appendicitis and shows diagnostic performance broadly comparable to white blood cell count when evaluated against a heterogeneous control population presenting with acute abdominal pain. While established inflammatory indices such as neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio and systemic immune-inflammation index remain more informative for predicting complicated appendicitis, LGI did not demonstrate superiority over these markers. Therefore, its clinical utility beyond existing, routinely available parameters remains uncertain. LGI should be interpreted cautiously, and its incremental value beyond established inflammatory markers requires further validation in prospective multicenter studies.
