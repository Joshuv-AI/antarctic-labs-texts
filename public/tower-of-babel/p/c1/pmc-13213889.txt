Combined Effects of Race and Ethnicity With Rurality on Tonsillectomy Rates Among Children Enrolled in Medicaid
Authors: Colleen C McLaughlin, Jesse L Hawke, Emily F Boss, Guy M Brock, Meredith M Lind, Tasleem J Padamsee, Prasanth Pattisapu, Deena J Chisolm, Laura J Chavez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:To determine whether associations between race and ethnicity and tonsillectomy differ for urban and rural children.Methods:Tonsillectomy within 6 months of initial otolaryngology visit (January 1, 2017–June 30, 2019) for either throat infection (n = 64,972) or obstructive sleep–disordered breathing (oSDB) (n = 111,756) was examined for children on Medicaid ages 2 to 18 years using the Transformed Medicaid Statistical Information System Analytic Files from 15 States. Poisson regression was used to compare tonsillectomy rates for Black non-Hispanic (Black), Hispanic, and White non-Hispanic (White) children in urban and rural settings, controlling for age, sex, and clinical factors.Results:For children with throat infection at the time of otolaryngology visit, the highest adjusted rates of tonsillectomy were observed among White children (rural 31.9%; urban 31.0%), which were significantly higher than both Black (rural 29.1%, P = .0018; urban 25.1%, P < .0001) and Hispanic (rural 27.2%, P < .0001; urban 25.7%, P < .0001) children. Significant interaction with urban/rural was observed for Black children relative to White (P = .0101). For oSDB, the rate of tonsillectomy for rural White children (42.6%) did not differ significantly from rural Black children (42.8%, P = .8727), but was higher than urban Black (38.4%, P < .0001) and urban White (41.3%, P = .0008) children (interaction P = .003). The rates among rural White children were also significantly higher than for Hispanic children (rural 38.9% P < .0001; urban 36.1%, P < .0001; interaction P = .0124).Conclusions:These results indicate that racial and ethnic differences in tonsillectomy rates vary between urban and rural settings. These differences merit further exploration to ensure appropriate use of surgical procedures and limit unwarranted variation.Keywords: Children’s Health Insurance Program, ethnicity, Medicaid, race, rural health, tonsillectomy

Tonsillectomy is among the most common pediatric surgical procedures in the United States, with over half a million pediatric tonsillectomies per year.1 The most common indications for pediatric tonsillectomy, with or without adenoidectomy, are obstructive sleep–disordered breathing (oSDB) and recurrent throat infections.2 Poor sleep quality arising from oSDB is associated with adverse neurobehavioral/neurocognitive outcomes, including depression and poor school performance.3 Likewise, recurrent throat infections are associated with school absences, increased health care use, and risk of complications.4 Tonsillectomy can improve symptoms and quality of life for patients with oSDB and/or recurrent throat infections.3,4

Relative to Black non-Hispanic (hereafter referred to as Black) and Hispanic children, White non-Hispanic children (White) are more likely to undergo tonsillectomy.5-10 Tonsillectomy rates are also higher in rural communities and among children enrolled in Medicaid.6-10 There is no generally expected rate of tonsillectomy, so differences may reflect underuse or overuse. However, differences in surgical utilization that are not clinically driven are always a concern. The drivers of differences in tonsillectomy utilization have not been clearly elucidated. With respect to race-ethnicity and rurality, an outstanding question is whether the observed utilization patterns have been due to the racial and ethnic makeup of rural America (ie, residual confounding), to the effects of rurality independent of race-ethnicity, or to effect modification between race-ethnicity and rurality. Clarifying the relationship between race-ethnicity and rurality when it comes to tonsillectomy is necessary for understanding and addressing the previously identified differences.

Variation in tonsillectomy by rurality and by race-ethnicity is multifactorial, and it is difficult to separate differences related to underlying population health versus economic, geographic, and supply-related access to care. Studies that have examined population–level tonsillectomy rates were generally unable to control for otolaryngology referral, otolaryngology office visit rates, costs not covered by insurance, and severity of oSDB or frequency of throat infections.5-8 We utilized a 15-state cohort of children enrolled in Medicaid or Children’s Health Insurance Program (CHIP) with an otolaryngology specialist visit for oSDB or throat infection to examine the individual and joint associations between race-ethnicity and rurality in tonsillectomy rates from 2017–2019. We also examined the patterns of tonsillectomy in relation to the Child Opportunity Index (COI).11

Methods

Study Population

This study used the Transformed Medicaid Statistical Information System Analytic Files (TAF) to select Black, Hispanic, and White Medicaid/CHIP enrollees ages 2 to 18 years in 2017 to 2019 from 15 US States (California, Delaware, Florida, Indiana, Kentucky, Maine, North Carolina, New Hampshire, Nevada, Ohio, Pennsylvania, South Dakota, Texas, and Washington). These states were identified as having high or moderate usability of race-ethnicity data for children identified as Black, Hispanic, or White based on completeness of the TAF variable and on benchmarking race-ethnicity counts against the American Community Survey.12,13

Beneficiaries were eligible if they had a throat infection or oSDB/tonsillar hypertrophy diagnosed at the time of a new patient evaluation and management office visit or outpatient consultation with an otolaryngology specialist (see Supplement Table S1 for all diagnosis and procedure codes used in this study). The first eligible visit from January 1, 2017 to June 30, 2019 was designated as the index visit. To allow for the determination of prior medical history and follow-up, only beneficiaries who were enrolled for at least 11 out of 12 months before and 6 months following their index visit were included.

Several exclusions were made to improve the specificity of the selection of study subjects. To rule out otolaryngology visits related to follow-up for prior treatment, we excluded children who had otolaryngology surgery of any kind in the 12 months before the index visit.14 We excluded children with specific medical complexities associated with differences in the clinical practice guidelines for tonsillectomy, including Down syndrome, craniofacial abnormalities, neuromuscular disorders, sickle cell disease, or mucopolysaccharidoses.2 Children who had a tonsillectomy on the same date as the index visit were excluded under the assumption that the initial otolaryngology evaluation had been missed in the claims data. Finally, children were excluded if they were missing Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services assigned beneficiary identification number, sex, birth date, or ZIP Code.

Diagnosis Cohort and Outcome

The outcome of interest was defined as an inpatient or outpatient (adeno)tonsillectomy within 6 months following the index visit. Analyses were conducted on 2 separate cohorts based on diagnosis of throat infection or oSDB/tonsillar hypertrophy at the index visit. Beneficiaries with oSDB were reassigned to the throat infection group if both oSDB and throat infection were coded on the claim for their index visit or if there were 7 or more throat infection claims in the 12 months before the index visit. These 2 cohorts were separated to be consistent with treatment guidelines, which propose different thresholds for recommending tonsillectomy.2 Separating the analysis by indication may reveal differences in rates of tonsillectomy based on race-ethnicity or rurality that may be dependent on the availability of health care resources, such as polysomnography. To explore whether a 6-month follow-up for identifying tonsillectomies was adequate, we also extended the follow-up time to 12 months in a sensitivity analysis.

Independent Variables

Race-ethnicity in TAF, defined as Hispanic ethnicity of any race, and Black race or White race exclusive of Hispanic ethnicity, is self-reported at the time of Medicaid application and redetermination.15 The 2010 Rural/Urban Commuting Area codes for ZIP Codes were used to classify beneficiary residence as urban or rural.16 Urban was defined as metropolitan areas plus all other ZIP Codes with greater than 30% secondary commuting flow to metropolitan areas. Rural was defined as the remaining ZIP Codes. ZIP Code was also used to assign the COI 2.0 level, ranging from very high to very low.11

Claims data for the 12 months before the index visit were used to measure health history and health care utilization, including throat infections, obesity, asthma, attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder, autism spectrum disorder, and polysomnography. We also identified polysomnography after the index visit until either tonsillectomy or the 6-month maximum follow-up had elapsed, whichever came first.

Statistical Analysis

Because tonsillectomy was not a rare outcome, we used multivariable Poisson regression with robust error variance estimators to derive rate ratios (RR) and rate differences with associated 95% confidence intervals (CI). Poisson regression was also used to estimate adjusted tonsillectomy rates averaged across the covariate values (predicted population margins). All models were estimated separately by indication cohort and included age, sex, race-ethnicity, rurality, obesity, asthma, attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder, throat infections, and polysomnography before or after index visit as covariates. These covariates were chosen because they are potentially associated with the underlying risk of recurrent throat infection or oSDB or with provider tonsillectomy recommendations.2,5-8,17,18 Models were assessed for overdispersion using the deviance/degrees of freedom statistic.

Statistical multiplicative interaction between race-ethnicity and rurality was assessed using interaction terms in the Poisson regression model. Adjusted risk difference interaction contrasts (IC) for assessment of additive interaction were estimated with linear normal models with robust error variance. Because interpretation of interaction is easier when the direction of the effect is the same for all involved variables, we used White race-ethnicity and rural residence as the referent groups. SAS v9 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, North Carolina) was used for all analyses. This study was approved by the Nationwide Children’s Hospital Institutional Review Board.

Results

Subject Selection

We identified 229,348 Medicaid/CHIP beneficiaries who had an initial eligible otolaryngology specialist visit between January 1, 2017 and June 30, 2019, from 15 US states with usable race-ethnicity data (Table S2). After applying all inclusion and exclusion criteria, 176,728 beneficiaries were included, representing 77.1% of the beneficiaries identified as having an initial eligible otolaryngology visit. Diagnosis at the time of otolaryngology visit included 45,260 (25.6%) throat infection only, 111,756 oSDB (63.2%) only, and 19,712 (11.2%) with both throat infection and oSDB (Table 1). The latter group was combined with the throat infection cohort.

The median ages of the cohorts were 9.2 years for throat infection and 7.1 years for oSDB. The population was 51.7% female (Table 1). The urban-rural and US Census Region distributions of the study cohort were similar to the US population age 0 to 18 years (Figs. S1 and S2). The percent of the study population that was White (46.1%) was lower than the US population as a whole (56.8%), reflecting the racial and ethnic distribution of the Medicaid program coverage. The study population was 38.9% Hispanic and 15.0% Black, compared to 27.0% and 16.3%, respectively, for the US population. The majority of the study population lived in ZIP Codes in the very low (30.9%) or low (28.6%) categories of COI (Table 1, Fig. S4). The very low COI group had a higher percentage of Black and Hispanic, as well as urban, children compared to the other categories. The low category had the highest percentage of White children and children living in rural settings. Only 5.5% of the children in the study lived in ZIP Codes classified as having very high COI. Just under half (n = 86,453, 48.9%) of the full cohort had a tonsillectomy within 6 months of their index otolaryngology visit (Table 1). Throat Infection

Overall and by domain, lower COI level, representing reduced access to positive neighborhood resources, was statistically significantly associated with higher rates of tonsillectomy (Table 2). The magnitude of the RR, however, did not consistently increase with decreasing COI level. The highest RR were observed in the moderate COI level (adjusted RR = 1.17, 95% CI 1.13 −1.22).

Obstructive Sleep–Disordered Breathing

As with throat infection, in the oSDB cohort, lower overall COI was associated with higher rates of tonsillectomy, relative to the very high COI category (Table 2). The COI Health and Environment Domain was the exception, with no statistically significant differences in relative rates across the 5 levels.

For Black children relative to White, there was statistically significant multiplicative (ß = −0.08, P value = .0005) and additive (Table 3, Figure) interaction. Rural Black children had the same adjusted rate of tonsillectomy as rural White children (42.8% and 42.6%, respectively, RR 1.00 [95% CI 0.96–1.04]). However, in urban settings, the IC was −3.76% (P = .0030), indicating the rate difference between Black and White children (38.4% and 42.3%, respectively) was wider than expected when compared to the rural rate difference.

Hispanic children had lower adjusted rates of tonsillectomy compared to White children in both urban and rural settings (39.1% and 38.9%, respectively) but in contrast to what was observed for Black children, the gap between Hispanic and White children was greater in rural settings compared to urban (IC = 2.27, P = .0124) (Table 3, Figure).

Sensitivity Analyses

Using 12 months of follow-up to detect tonsillectomies reduced the total cohort size by 47,190 (26.7%), because of the loss of cohort members first seen in 2019. Among those eligible for 12-month follow-up, the crude tonsillectomy rate increased from 49.8% when 6 months was used to 53.5% when 12 months was used, an increase of 3.7%. There was minimal difference in the RR when using 12 versus 6 months (Table S4).

Discussion

In this large, multistate study of Medicaid-insured children who had an initial visit with an otolaryngologist, we observed increased rates of tonsillectomy associated with White non-Hispanic race-ethnicity and residence in rural ZIP Codes. These results are consistent with previous studies across a wide range of study designs,5-10 although ours is the first that we know of in the United States that specifically followed cohorts of children who had an evaluation by an otolaryngology specialist. Thus, the differences related to race-ethnicity and urban-rural residence observed previously persist, even among children who have succeeded in obtaining referrals and attending specialty care visits.

We also found evidence of effect modification of the race-ethnicity differences by rurality. For both the throat infection and oSDB cohorts, the differences in rates of tonsillectomy between Black children and White children were larger in urban settings than in rural settings. This effect modification indicates there may be some Black children living in urban areas who did not have a tonsillectomy but who, had they lived in rural areas, might have had one. The picture for Hispanic children was somewhat different. For the oSDB cohort, the Hispanic-White gap in rates was larger in rural settings, indicating some Hispanic children with oSDB living in urban areas had a tonsillectomy but, had they lived in rural areas, might not have had one. In the infection cohort, however, Hispanic children had the same degree of difference in rates compared to White children across urban and rural settings; this was the only comparison where no interaction was observed.

Although geographic variation in tonsillectomy rates has been reported in higher-income countries for almost a century,19 our results are the first to suggest that the associations of surgical use and race-ethnicity may vary based on whether the child lives in an urban or rural setting. Like other health care interventions with large geographic variation, the geographic variation for tonsillectomy has been interpreted as an issue of supply and demand, which itself is a combination of location-based and policy-based variation. Demand for tonsillectomy is driven by the prevalence of oSDB and recurrent throat infection, as well as screening and referral to otolaryngology by primary care. We were able to control for this by restricting our study population to children who were seen by an otolaryngologist with a diagnosis of either throat infection or oSDB. This suggests that there are factors influencing race-ethnicity and urban/rural differences in tonsillectomy over and above the demand related to underlying disease.

One possible explanation for lower rates of tonsillectomy among Black or Hispanic children could be that their families experience greater structural barriers to surgery after their consultation, relative to their White peers. Structural barriers to care may include parental time off work, inadequacies in patient-provider communication, and implicit racial bias.18,20-28 Studies have shown that marginalized populations wait longer for surgery in general and for tonsillectomies in particular, suggesting a role for loss-to-follow-up that can be associated with longer wait times.17,18,29 Because all children in the study had Medicaid/CHIP insurance, they presumably did not experience barriers related to insurance deductibles or copay costs, which may be an explanation for lower tonsillectomy rates among privately insured children in previous studies.23,27,30 By limiting our study to Medicaid/CHIP, out-of-pocket expenses are controlled for, but families living near the poverty level have a higher burden of indirect expenses, such as missed work time, and competing expenses, such as caregiving for other family members. Even within the Medicaid population, there are varying levels of income that qualify across the United States. It is possible that these indirect expenses and other barriers are experienced by children who are Black or Hispanic to a greater extent than their White peers. Further, Black children and Hispanic children are likely experiencing these inequities in different ways, related to differences in the manifestation of structural racism.

As with prior studies, we found higher utilization of tonsillectomy among children in rural communities relative to urban communities, with the exception of rural Hispanic children with oSDB.6-9,31 This would seem to conflict with expectations that the limited supply of specialists in rural communities could result in underuse of surgical procedures.18,32 Underuse or delays in rural areas have been reported by the majority of studies of surgical rates from North America.32 A recent review by Ebrahim et al, however, identified a subset of studies showing higher use or accelerated time to surgery in rural areas, particularly for low-value surgeries that are considered discretionary and/or have more advantageous nonsurgical treatments.32 Consistent with this, increased tonsillectomy rates in rural areas could be due to lack of access to polysomnography which could lead to increased otolaryngology referrals from primary care, as well as higher probability of surgery among children with lower acuity oSDB. Further, the structural barriers associated with race-ethnicity differ between urban and rural areas, although the relationships with health care have been shown to be complex.33-35 Parent preferences for “watchful waiting” or alternatives to surgery may be supported by greater availability of polysomnography in urban over rural areas. In the case of Hispanic patients, lack of polysomnography in rural areas may enable caregivers to pursue surgery for children with milder oSDB.

Rural America is also not a uniform concept, with important differences in racial diversity, poverty, and industries across the country. The distribution of race-ethnicity across the rural United States is one of the more important differences with respect to the comparisons made in this study. The rural Black population predominantly resides in the South and Mississippi Delta areas (represented in this study by North Carolina), while the rural Hispanic population is more concentrated in the Southwest and West (California, Nevada, and Texas). These regional differences introduce potential for confounding by health care access, agricultural and industrial pollutants, parental smoking, and other factors that vary across the rural areas of the United States.

It is unknown what the “correct” rates of tonsillectomies per pediatric population should be for rural or urban communities, and the observed patterns are likely a combination of underuse in some populations and overuse in others. Thus, it could be due to overuse of the procedure in rural areas related to differences in otolaryngology specialist practices and adherence to guidelines or to caregiver and patient preferences. Meanwhile, practice patterns and patient preference could also be driving underuse in urban populations in general, and urban minoritized populations in particular. Because we limited our cohorts to patients with an otolaryngology specialist visit, our results support possible differences in specialist practice as a driver of utilization, rather than differences in access or supply of specialists. While the observed rate differences are small in the states included in our analysis, added together they represent approximately 2.5% of tonsillectomies in the study. If we assume that the observed disparities from our analysis reflect disparities seen in all 50 states, this could mean that there are approximately 5000 tonsillectomies per year impacted nationally. Medicaid is the largest provider of children’s health insurance coverage in the United States. As one of the most common surgical procedures in childhood, even relatively small differences can have a large impact at the national level for Medicaid-enrolled children.

There are several potential limitations in the current analysis. First, our cohort design, which selects children who have had an initial otolaryngologist visit, offers advantages but there are also potential limitations. For example, if children in urban and rural settings seek care from specialists at different rates, it could be that those who seek care in rural settings have higher acuity, reflected in the higher rates of tonsillectomy observed in this study, as well as others that used differing cohort definitions.6,7,10 Second, Medicaid data quality and completeness vary by state. We attempted to mitigate this by selecting 15 states with higher quality, usable race-ethnicity data,12 and these states had adequate quality of data for other key variables for the years studied.13 Finally, controlling for oSDB disease severity, which could be higher among Black and Hispanic children, was not possible with claims data. For the race-ethnicity comparison, the direction of confounding by severity would be toward the null when using White non-Hispanic children as the reference group. Confounding by severity, however, could explain our findings with respect to COI, if the severity of disease at the time of otolaryngology visit is associated with living in communities with lower opportunity.

This study of pediatric tonsillectomy utilization is one of the largest to be reported for the United States. Limiting the cohort to children who were known to have a higher likelihood of undergoing tonsillectomy strengthens the assumption that prior findings of differences in tonsillectomy rates were not due to differences in the prevalence of underlying disease. Further exploration of the roles of race-ethnicity and rurality could include examination of differences across US regions, improved specification of the intersection of rurality, urbanicity, and socioeconomic disadvantage, and exploration of the role of supply of primary care and otolaryngology specialty care in tonsillectomy variation. More work is needed to understand the drivers of geographic differences in pediatric tonsillectomy utilization in the United States, particularly the possibility of overuse in rural populations. Incorporating patient and provider perspectives through qualitative research might provide insight into the possible drivers of differences in rural and urban tonsillectomy use, which could be further explored in future studies.

What’s New

This cohort study using Medicaid data showed lower tonsillectomy rates among Black children relative to White children, which were more pronounced in urban settings. However, Hispanic children, relative to White children, had lower tonsillectomy rates in both urban and rural settings.
