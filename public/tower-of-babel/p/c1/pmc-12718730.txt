Factors Affecting Visual Outcome after Small Incision Lenticule Extraction (SMILE) Pro
Authors: Muhammad Irfan Kamaruddin, Ucok Parlindungan Pasaribu, Tjahyono Darminto Gondhowiardjo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo report the factors affecting visual outcomes after small incision lenticule extraction (SMILE) in eyes treated with the Visumax 800.MethodsThis is a retrospective study with cross-sectional design of eyes underwent SMILE Pro using Visumax 800 at Jakarta Eye Center. Patients ≤40 years old with a corrected distance visual acuity of >20 / 25, manifest refractive spherical equivalent ≤10.0 diopters (D), and cylinder refraction <5.0 D were included. Evaluation of visual outcomes was conducted at 1 day and 1 month postoperatively.ResultsA total of 258 eyes from 132 patients were analyzed. Among the total, 94% achieved an uncorrected distance visual acuity (UDVA) of 20 / 20 at 1 month postoperatively. Of the eyes with high myopia, 81% achieved a UDVA of 20 / 20, followed by 97% and 99% in moderate and low myopic eyes, respectively. Spherical equivalent refraction (r = 0.273, p < 0.01), optic zone (OZ; r = 0.260, p < 0.01), cap thickness (r = 0.209, p < 0.01), and residual stromal thickness (RST; r = 0.256, p < 0.01) showed a significant but weak correlation with UDVA at 1 month postoperatively. There is increasing risk to get <20 / 20 UDVA in right eyes (odds ratio [OR], 3.101; p < 0.045), eyes with high myopia (OR, 9.964; p < 0.01), 100-μm cap thickness (OR, 4.578; p < 0.01), ≤6 mm OZ (OR, 10.556; p < 0.01), and <300-μm RST (OR, 9.100; p < 0.01) at 1 month postoperatively.ConclusionsSMILE Pro results in promising visual outcome in early postoperative period. Patients with low to moderate myopia have better visual outcomes than those with high myopia. A small OZ, thin cap thickness, and low RST could increase the risk of not achieving the maximum visual outcome after SMILE. A prospective study with a longer follow-up period that includes comprehensive variables is needed to fully understand the factors affecting visual acuity after SMILE.Keywords: Small incision lenticule extraction (SMILE), SMILE Pro, Visual outcome

Following the availability of femtosecond lasers, refractive lenticule extraction (ReLEx) has continued to be developed over the past few decades for minimally invasive keratorefractive surgery [1]. Small incision lenticule extraction (SMILE) was first performed in 2007 and published in 2011 by Sekundo et al. [2]. This technique was modified from the femtosecond lenticule extraction (FLEx). Unlike FLEx, which creates and lifts a flap to extract the intrastromal lenticule, the lenticule is manually extracted through a small corneal incision in the periphery of the cornea [2,3].

Several peer-reviewed studies have shown good and satisfying outcomes and long-term clinical results [4–6]. In terms of safety, efficacy, stability, and predictability, SMILE appears to be equivalent to femtosecond laser in situ keratomileusis (LASIK) for myopia correction [7]. This method eliminates flap-related complications, and some studies have demonstrated shorter duration of postoperative dry eye symptoms and faster recovery from corneal sensation [8–10]. Moreover, this technique enhances postoperative corneal strength and reduces the number of induced corneal aberrations [2,6,11,12].

Besides its advantages, SMILE has some limitations: it is still unable to correct higher-order aberrations, and some studies have reported cases of suction loss, which certainly affects the results [13–16]. Additionally, SMILE has been reported to have a slower visual recovery postoperatively, with the postoperative day 1 visual acuity (VA) reported to be lower than that of LASIK [10,17].

At the end of 2021, a second-generation femtosecond laser, Visumax 800 (Carl Zeiss Meditec), was launched. It is claimed to offer new advantages in surgical performance by reducing treatment time, and could lead to better patient experience. The new version runs at 2 MHz, which results in less time (approximately 10 seconds) required for lenticule creation and less time required for the patient to remain still, thus reducing the risk of suction loss [18,19].

This study aimed to report the visual outcome and the factors that could affect it in the early experience of treating eyes with Visumax 800 at our eye center.

Materials and Methods

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Faculty of Medicine, Hasanuddin University, which served as the central review board for the study (No. 198/UN4.6.4.5.31/PP36/2025). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. This study was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study design and patients

This retrospective study had a cross-sectional design. A total of 258 eyes from 132 patients who underwent SMILE for myopia correction between January 2023 and December 2023 at Jakarta Eye Center (JEC) Kedoya in Jakarta, Indonesia, were included in this study.

Patients 40 years old or younger with the corrected distance VA (CDVA) of >20 / 25, manifest refractive spherical equivalent (SE) ≤10.0 diopters (D), cylinder refraction <5.0 D were included. Patients with active ocular diseases, keratoconus, previous ocular surgery, connective tissue disorders, or immune diseases were excluded. Preoperatively, the patients were expected to stop wearing soft contact lenses for at least 2 weeks and rigid contact lenses for at least 4 weeks.

Clinical examination

Three examinations were performed: one preoperative and two postoperative follow-ups (1 day and 1 month postoperatively). These included uncorrected distance VA (UDVA), objective refraction, noncontact intraocular pressure, slit-lamp biomicroscopic examination, fundus examination, and corneal morphological examination. In this study, myopia was classified into high myopia (>6.00 D), moderate myopia (3.25 to 6.00 D) and low myopia (1.00 to 3.00 D). Corneal morphology was assessed using anterior segment tomography (Pentacam, Oculus). Multiple images were captured using a Scheimpflug camera to synthesize the three-dimensional images of the anterior segment. A series of corneal topographic data, such as central corneal thickness and mean keratometry, were automatically obtained. In this study, three primary surgical parameters (cap thickness, optic zone [OZ], and residual stromal thickness [RST]) were separated into two groups according to clinically and biomechanically significant thresholds. A cap thickness of 100 μm was selected as the threshold to determine the depth of stromal tissue preserved anteriorly; thicker caps (>100 μm) are thought to preserve the biomechanically stronger anterior stroma, which may reduce postoperative microdistortions and enhance visual recovery. A cutoff of 6.0 mm was used for the OZ because smaller OZs have been associated with higher-order aberrations and reduced mesopic visual quality, particularly in patients with larger scotopic pupil sizes. The RST was divided at 300 μm, a commonly accepted lower limit, to ensure postoperative corneal stability; thinner residual beds (<300 μm) may be more prone to biomechanical weakening and less than ideal refractive stability.

Surgical technique

All procedures in this study were performed individually by an experienced surgeon (UPP), with a postoperative target refraction of zero. A Visumax 800 femtosecond laser system with a laser pulse frequency of 2 MHz and pulse energy of 130 nJ (energy index, 26) was used to create a stromal refractive lenticule. The expected corneal cap thickness was between 100 and 120 mm, and the corneal cap diameter was 1.0 mm larger than the diameter of the optical zone. The expected diameter of the optical zone was varied between 5.8 and 6.7 mm, and the transition zone was 0.1 mm when the astigmatism was corrected. No nomograms were used for surgery. Preoperatively, two drops of lignocaine hydrochloride 2% (Xylocaine) were used for superficial anesthesia; the patients were then instructed to keep their eyes open.

The patient’s eye was positioned under a curved suction contact glass using a Visumax femtosecond laser (Treatment Pack). Once the eyes were attached properly, automatic suction, fixation, and laser treatment were initiated. The lenticule was scanned and segregated in the following sequence: the posterior surface of the lenticule (periphery to center), border, anterior surface of the lenticule (center to periphery), and a 3.5 mm side-cut incision with 90° angles was made located at 120° in both eyes. A blunt spatula was used to separate the anterior and posterior surfaces of the stromal lenticule through a small incision. We manually extracted the lenticule using forceps and rinsed the incision with a balanced salt solution after removing the lenticule (Alcon Laboratories).

Postoperative medications included 0.5% gatifloxacin hemihydrate (Giflox) four times a day for 3 days and combined neomycin-polymyxin-dexamethasone (Polydex) four times a day (tapered every 2 weeks). Artificial tears (Eye-fresh) were administered every 3 hours a day for 1 month.

Statistical analysis

Qualitative characteristics are expressed as frequencies and percentages, and quantitative characteristics are expressed as the mean and standard error of the mean. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess normality. Spearman rho correlation test was used to assess the relationships between postoperative UDVA, baseline characteristics, and other surgical parameters. Mann-Whitney and Kruskal-Wallis tests were performed to determine the association between UDVA and patient characteristics.

We also divided the cohort into halves according to the achievement of 20 / 20 UDVA 1-month postoperatively to compare postoperative visual outcomes between baseline characteristics and surgical parameters and calculated the odds ratio (OR) in achieving <20 / 20 UDVA 1-month postoperatively using the chi-square and Fisher exact tests.

All statistical analyses were performed using the IBM SPSS ver. 26.0 (IBM Corp). A p-value of <0.05 was used to reject the null hypothesis for two-tailed tests.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 258 eyes of 132 patients were analyzed. The mean age was 23.03 ± 5.02 years (range, 18 to 39 years). The preoperative SE refraction (SER) was −4.42 ± 2.48 D (range, −0.75 to −9.88 D), with spherical value of −3.79 ± 2.37 D (range, −1.00 to −10.00 D), and cylindrical value of −1.26 ± 0.84 D (range, −0.25 to −4.75 D). Central corneal thickness was 542.02 ± 33.93 μm (range, 487 to 617 μm). Cap thickness was 110.70 ± 6.19 μm (range, 100 to 120 μm). Cap thickness was 110.70 ± 6.20 μm (range, 100 to 120 μm), OZ was 6.56 ± 0.27 mm (range, 5.8 to 6.7 mm), and RST was 331.43 ± 38.55 μm (range, 273 to 416 μm). Preoperative UDVA was logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR) 0.89 ± 0.12 (range, logMAR 0.10 to 1.00), while postoperative UDVA at 1 month was logMAR 0.004 ± 0.050 (range, logMAR −0.10 to 0.20) (Tables 1, 2).

Visual outcomes after the SMILE Pro procedure

UDVA was equal to or better than CDVA preoperatively in 242 of 258 eyes (94%) and 20 / 20 or better in 242 of 258 eyes (94%) at the 1-month post-SMILE Pro follow-up (Fig. 1A). The patients’ UDVA also showed good stability from 1 day to 1 month postoperatively (Fig. 2). Regarding the degree of myopia, the high myopia group showed less efficacy than the moderate and low myopia groups; the UDVA was equal to or better than the CDVA preoperatively in 81% of the high myopia eyes, 97% of the moderate myopia eyes, and 99% of the low myopia eyes (Fig. 1B–1D).

Visual acuity stability at 1 month postoperatively (n = 258).

Based on the group of variables, there were significant differences in UDVA 1 month after surgery in the eye side, degree of myopia, cap thickness, OZ, and RST groups (p < 0.05) (Table 1). No differences were observed in terms of sex, age, or pachymetry. The left eye had better UDVA but high myopia group had lower UDVA at 1 month after surgery. For surgical parameters, cap thickness of >100 μm had better UDVA, but OZ ≤6 mm and RST <300 μm had lower UDVA, 1 month after surgery.

Correlation of baseline characteristics and surgical parameters with visual outcome

Some baseline characteristics and surgical parameters were significantly related to the UDVA 1 month after surgery. The UDVA, spherical size, and SER of the patients were significantly positively correlated with the VA 1 month after surgery. Surgical parameters such as cap thickness, OZ, and RST also positively correlated with UDVA at 1 month postoperatively. This means that the more positive the value of each characteristic, the better the UDVA achieved one month after surgery. However, all the correlations were still categorized as weak correlation (0.01< r <0.3).

OR for <20 / 20 visual outcome 1 month postoperatively

Each variable was divided into two groups to determine the odds of <20 / 20 UDVA at 1 month after surgery (Table 3). There were some significant results. The odds of having UDVA <20 / 20 at 1 month after surgery were 3.101 higher in the right eye group than in the left eye group ( p = 0.045). Patients with high myopia had 9.964 times higher odds of having UDVA <20 / 20 than those with low to moderate myopia (p < 0.001). Thin cap thickness (100 μm), small OZ (≤6 mm), and thin RST (<300 μm) also increased the odds of having <20 / 20 UDVA at 1 month postoperatively by 4.758 (p = 0.006), 10.556 (p < 0.001), and 9.100 (p < 0.001), respectively. Age and sex did not influence the odds of UDVA <20 / 20 at 1 month after surgery.

Multivariate analysis

Multivariate linear regression was used to identify independent variables that could forecast uncorrected distance VA (UCVA) at 1 month postoperatively, assessed in log- MAR (Table 4). The final model included 10 variables: age, sex, laterality, pachymetry, cap thickness, OZ size, RST, spherical power, cylindrical power, and degree of myopia. The model yielded an R2 of 0.084, indicating that only 8.4% of the variance in UCVA at 1 month postoperatively was explained by the included factors. The model demonstrated statistical significance (F(10, 247) = 2.278, p = 0.014), however its explanatory capacity remained constrained.

The degree of myopia was marginally significantly correlated with postoperative uncorrected VA (B = −0.010, p = 0.052), suggesting that higher degrees of myopia may be moderately associated with poorer UCVA outcomes. Additional variables, including age, sex, pachymetry, cap thickness, OZ size, and RST did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05). The standardized coefficients (β) indicate that all variables exhibit minimal effect sizes. The results indicate that no single variable exerts a significant independent influence on UCVA within this model. However, the degree of myopia approached statistical significance. The limited explanatory power of the model may have resulted from unmeasured variables (such as surgical concentration, tear film quality, or interface roughness) or measurement constraints.

Discussion

The second-generation femtosecond laser platform Visumax 800 showed outstanding outcomes. Our study results showed that the visual outcomes from treated eyes were comparable and could be superior to those of the previous laser generation, Visumax 500 (Carl Zeiss Meditec) [2,3,20,21]. In terms of efficacy, our results are comparable with previous study the by Reinstein et al. [18] and Saad et al. [19], who also performed SMILE Pro using Visumax 800. Three-month postoperative UDVA was 20 / 20 in 91% and 93% of the patients, respectively. In our study, we achieved a 1-month postoperative UDVA of 20 / 20 in 94% of treated eyes.

Based on the demographic results, no relationships or differences were found between age and sex and UDVA after SMILE in our study. Age above 40 years could give different results in visual and refractive outcomes [22]; therefore, our treated eyes, which were concentrated at 40 years of age or younger, could minimize the effect of age on a wide range of visual outcomes. Moreover, sex, which also did not show significant differences in visual outcomes, indicated that the effect of demographic characteristics on other variables in our study, including surgical parameters, could be minimized.

This study showed that the left eye had a better VA than the right eye. There were no data related to the association between eye laterality and visual outcomes after SMILE Pro using Visumax 800. There are two optional orders for operation in the Visumax 800: we can choose to finish the laser for both eyes (first and second eye) and continue to perform lenticule extraction in the second operated eye, and finally, we finish with lenticule extraction in the first operated eye. The second option is the same as the order of Visumax 500, in which we finish the laser and continue the lenticule extraction, and then continue to the second operated eye using the same procedure. Our study used the first option order in our treated eyes with right eye as first eye and left eye was second eye, meaning that the first eye experiencing laser process and the last eye experiencing dissection process is the eye that has less VA than another eye.

A previous study using Visumax 500 reported no differences in VA between the first and second operated eyes after SMILE; however, the first operated eye showed a smaller number of decentered displacements and demonstrated superior outcomes in terms of horizontal coma and modulation transfer function values compared to the second operated eye [23]. They speculated that the first operated eye experiences more stress and discomfort than the second eye, resulting in better centration and moreover resulting in better visual outcomes. Contrastingly, our results showed that the eye that underwent the dissection process first (second or the left eye) had a better visual outcome. The dissection process can influence microdistortions of the Bowman layer after SMILE surgery [23]. We speculated that the last lenticule dissection process (first or the right eye) was more challenging due to stress or discomfort of the patient, resulting in more distortion of the Bowman layer, which has a negative impact on the visual outcome. This result also emphasizes the advantages of the shorter treatment/laser time of Visumax 800, which may decrease patient anxiety and eye movement during the laser treatment process and have been linked to intraoperative complications [4,14,16,24].

Regarding the degree of myopia, a previous study of SMILE proved to provide good visual quality in high myopia [25,26]. Several studies reported that UDVA achieved 20 / 20 in 37% to 80% of highly myopic eyes, and less than 20% lost more than one line of CDVA [27–29]. The results of our study were not inferior to those of these studies; 81% of the eyes in the high myopia group achieved 20 / 20 at 1 month postoperatively.

Our other findings also showed a relationship between VA and SER, in which the highest SER was the lowest at 1-month postoperative UDVA. In addition, there were significantly higher odds of obtaining a UDVA <20 / 20 for high myopia than for low-moderate myopia. Donate and Thaeron [30] also found that patients with low myopia had significantly better UDVA than moderate and high myopia at 1 day and 1 month after SMILE.

Some studies have revealed that optical quality and visual recovery after SMILE are caused by microdistortions in Bowman membrane, which are dependent on refractive error and lenticule thickness [31,32]. Shroff et al. [33] quantified the distortion using the Bowmann Roughness Index (BRI). They found that BRI was related to the slow visual recovery after SMILE, which peaked at 1 week after surgery and stabilized in the further course of time. Luo et al. [32] reported that Bowman membrane microdistortions did not occur in low myopia, which is in line with our results in which low-moderate myopia had better UDVA compared to high myopia.

However, a recent study by Saad et al. [19], which also used Visumax 800, reported a different result from our study. They achieved very good results in highly myopic eyes; 100% of their treated eyes achieved 20 / 20 at 3 months postoperatively. The differences between the results of our study and those of previous studies can be attributed to several factors. Ganesh et al. [34] proposed a grading system called the Interface Roughness Index (IRG) to grade the roughness of an interface. This is because the interface is deeper than the Bowmann membrane and all manipulations are performed at the interface level; thus, interface healing also plays a role in visual recovery after SMILE. Their study found that IRG was significantly correlated with CDVA. Also based on IRG score, they postulated that it may take 3 months or more for the healing of the interface and hence complete stabilization of vision after SMILE. This finding could explain the difference between our findings in the high myopia group, which still had a low percentage of achieving 20 / 20 compared to the study by Saad et al. [19] (80% vs. 100%). A possible reason is the different follow-up time, which was longer in their study; therefore, their treated eyes may have had more stable interface healing than ours.

Our study showed a relationship between cap thickness and visual outcome 1 month after surgery, and increasing cap thickness resulted in better UDVA. Also, there is increasing risk not achieving 1.0 UDVA in eyes with cap thickness of 100 μm. Despite the planned cap thickness during treatment being established between 100 and 120 μm, postoperative assessments revealed certain eyes with a cap thickness measured precisely at 100 μm. All patients exhibited cap thickness values, whether measured or approximated, of at least 100 μm. In our dataset, cap thickness was not examined as a continuous variable; rather, it was categorized for statistical comparison, with eyes divided into two groups, according to the distribution and frequency of values: 100 and >100 μm. This classification corresponded to the clinical benchmarks and noted diversity in the results. Owing to the stronger tensile strength of the anterior cornea compared to the posterior cornea, thick cap thickness or deeper lenticule creation results in minimal change in the strongest part of the cornea and could lead to less effect on corneal biomechanics, which indirectly affects refractive and visual outcomes [35,36]. This theory supports our results in which thick cap thickness could lead to better UDVA after SMILE Pro. Another possible reason is that a thick cap results in a thicker anterior stromal portion of the SMILE cap below the Bowmann membrane. This condition provides more protection to the Bowmann membrane during interface manipulation, resulting in minimal microdistortion of the membrane.

Several previous studies with various cap thicknesses showed that there were no significant differences in visual and refractive outcomes between thin and thick caps [37–40]. Interestingly, the biomechanical effects of different cap thicknesses were inconsistent. Some studies have reported that a thick cap has a good effect on corneal biomechanics [37,40]; however, Jun et al. [41] reported that a thick cap resulted in a greater decrease in corneal stiffness, and Damgaard et al. [42] revealed no differences in corneal biomechanical strength between thin and thick caps. These inconsistent findings indicate that cap thickness is not an independent factor affecting corneal biomechanics and further affects refractive and visual outcomes. Eskina et al. [43] found that in patients with good RST, higher cap thickness could boost vision recovery. This finding could explain our different results with other studies regarding cap thickness and visual outcome, our good visual outcome in high cap thickness could possibly related to high RST in that group.

The present study also show relation between RST and visual outcome after SMILE Pro. Low RST (<300 μm) had poor UDVA than high RST; the more the RST, the better the UDVA, and eyes that have low RST were nine times at risk to have UDVA <20 / 20, 1 month after SMILE Pro. There is still lack of study that reported the relation between RST and visual outcome, a study by Ogasawara and Onodera [44] indicates that bed thickness correlates with regression of myopia after LASIK and enough bed thickness is important to maintain good UDVA in the long term.

Larger OZ diameter in our study proved to have better UDVA 1 month after SMILE Pro. The chance of obtaining a UDVA <20 / 20 increased more than 10 times in eyes with an OZ of 6 mm or less. It is well-known that a larger OZ diameter is required to maintain good visual quality. The diameter of the OZ affects postoperative refractive outcomes in SMILE; the larger the OZ diameter, the better the early postoperative visual quality in low to moderate myopia [45], and larger optical zones induce less undercorrection, especially in patients with high myopia [46]. The OZ was related to the pupil diameter; the larger the OZ, the greater the area of the pupil covered, thus increasing the visual quality. Zhou et al. [45] also indicated that the smaller the dark pupil diameter, the better the subjective visual quality in the early postoperative period.

Wu and Huang [47] reported that no differences in UDVA 1 month after SMILE with different OZ diameter (range, 6.1 to 6.8 mm) for moderate myopia. However, they also observed a slight effect on night vision when using a larger OZ. The different results from our study could be due to the range of OZ that we used, as we treated eyes with an OZ <6.1 mm, which increased the possibility of having a greater effect on visual quality.

Our study has some limitations. The retrospective design of our study limits the exploration of additional variables, such as laser setting, biomechanical parameters, and tear film stability. A study exploring more variables may be needed to provide a comprehensive view of the factors affecting VA after SMILE using Visumax 800. Our study also could not demonstrate safety and predictability due to the short follow-up period of 1 month. Moreover, we did not perform multiple analyses for each variable to explore the relationships between them. Further studies that thoroughly investigate how variables affect each other through multiple analyses are needed to confirm which variables have a greater effect on VA after surgery.

This study included both eyes from individual patients in the analysis. Future studies should include only one randomly selected eye per subject to ensure independent observations and enhance the methodological rigor of the statistical analyses. Another limitation is that this study only focused on visual outcomes and did not present post operative refractive data, which are critical endpoints for evaluating surgical success. Future research also should think about expanding visual acuityVA measurements to include values better than 20 / 20 (e.g., logMAR – −0.1 to – −0.2).

In conclusion, our study shows that SMILE Pro using Visumax 800 give promising visual outcome in early postoperative period. Patients with low to moderate myopia have better visual outcomes than those with high myopia. A small OZ, thin cap thickness, and low RST could increase the risk of not achieving the maximum visual outcome after SMILE. A prospective study with a longer follow- up period that includes comprehensive variables is needed to fully understand the factors affecting VA after SMILE.
