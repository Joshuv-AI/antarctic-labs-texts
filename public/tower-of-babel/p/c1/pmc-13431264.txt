Deciphering the Association of Fasting Blood Glucose With Gamma-Glutamyl Transferase and Uric Acid: A cross-sectional study in a tertiary care hospital in South India
Authors: Anbalagan Anithasri, Rajagambeeram Reeta, Velmurugan Hasini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryObjectives:Fasting hyperglycaemia (FHG) in insulin resistance is due to oxidative stress wherein gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) and uric acid (UA) are known to impair insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells due to their pro-oxidant action and their role in metabolism of antioxidant glutathione. This study aims to determine the association of GGT and UA with FHG and its role in predicting the onset of prediabetes.Methods:This cross-sectional study was conducted in Government Villupuram Medical College, Villupuram, India, from July to December 2023. Individuals in the age range of 25–65 years attending the master health check-up clinic were included. The subjects were classified as normoglycaemic (FBG <100 mg/dL) or hyperglycaemic (FBG ≥100 mg/dL). GGT, UA, glucose (Glu):GGT ratio, Glu:UA ratio, lipid profile and GGT: high-density lipoprotein (HDL) were compared between the two groups using Student's t test. The parameters were compared between the quintiles of glucose using ANOVA. Pearson's correlation was used to analyse the correlation between the study parameters.Results:A total of 500 adults were included in this study. Triglyceride (TG), TG:HDL ratio, GGT, UA and Glu:GGT, Glu:UA and GGT:HDL ratios were significantly high in fifth quintile compared to other glucose quintiles. Glu:UA and Glu:GGT ratios showed significant positive correlation with glucose levels. Receiver operating characteristic analysis showed that Glu:UA ratio was a good predictor of FHG (AUC = 0.808; P <0.0001) with a sensitivity of 70.1% and specificity of 79.8% at a cut-off value of 26.5 to diagnose insulin resistance.Conclusion:Glu:UA and Glu:GGT ratios are surrogate markers of underlying oxidative stress and insulin resistance and good predictors of fasting hyperglycaemia.Keywords: Gamma-Glutamyltransferase, Insulin Resistance, Oxidative Stress, Uric Acid, Prediabetic State

1. Introduction

Blood glucose (Glu) is regulated and maintained by hormones such as insulin, glucagon, adrenalin, growth hormone and thyroid hormones. Impaired fasting glucose (IFG) represents abnormal glucose regulation, which is an intermediate state between normal glucose balance and overt diabetes. As per American Diabetes Association Criteria, fasting blood glucose (FBG) level between 100–125 mg/dL is IFG while FBG ≥126 mg/dL is type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). IFG is a manifestation of underlying hepatic insulin resistance and decreased insulin release from beta cells of pancreas. Therefore, IFG may be considered as a surrogate marker of insulin resistance. In India, the overall prevalence of prediabetes in 15 states was 10.3% as per the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) –India Diabetes.[1]

Gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) is a membrane bound ectoenzyme involved in γ-glutamyl cycle in the synthesis and degradation of glutathione. Increase in oxidative stress leads to dysfunction of β-cells, thereby reducing insulin production and its action.[2] Hence, an increase in GGT level reduces antioxidant glutathione and promotes oxidative stress which is a risk factor for developing IFG and T2DM.[3] Wang et al.'s longitudinal study was conducted in China and showed that the incidence of prediabetes increases with the increase in the GGT level and that the hazard ratio for IFG increased by 28.9% for each unit increase in GGT levels.[4] Uric acid (UA) is associated with systemic inflammation and oxidative stress which also impairs insulin secretion by the pancreatic β-cells. Previous studies have shown UA levels are higher in patients with diabetes and cardiovascular disorders.[5,6,7] A meta-analysis found a 6–7% high risk of IFG for every 1 mg/dL increase in serum UA in adults.[8] Subjects with IFG have a significantly higher UA and GGT levels than those with normal fasting glucose. The odds ratio for IFG were high for higher UA and GGT quartiles compared to reference quartile.[9] The serum UA level was increased in youths who were overweight/obese and can be used as a biomarker of impaired glucose tolerance as concluded by an Italian study.[10]

Kanagasabai et al.'s study found that sleep duration-fasting insulin relationship was mediated by GGT and UA highlighting the fact that inflammation, oxidative stress and antioxidant status play a role on sleep-insulin and glycaemic control. The study proposed that maintaining good sleep hygiene and decreased inflammation with adequate antioxidants are crucial in the management of diabetes risk.[11] Molla et al. conducted a cross-sectional study in an adult Bangaladeshi population to analyse the relationship between UA and elevated liver enzymes; they found a strong and positive association between UA and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and GGT independent of confounding factors such as age, gender, body mass index (BMI), FBG, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure and lipid profile suggesting that increased UA may be an effective marker in predicting liver enzyme abnormalities, especially GGT.[12] Hyperuricemia decreases nitric oxide release causing endothelial dysfunction in addition to oxidative and inflammatory changes in adipocytes. Both these pathways result in insulin resistance culminating in metabolic syndrome and obesity.[13] Hence, this study aimed to determine the association of GGT and UA with FBG and the utility of Glu:GGT and Glu:UA ratios in predicting the onset of prediabetes and diabetes.

2. Methods

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the Department of Biochemistry, Government Villupuram Medical College, Villupuram, India, in collaboration with the master health check-up clinic as an ICMR Short Term Studentship project between July 2023 and December 2023. Consecutive participants were recruited from the master health check-up clinic. A detailed history regarding previous illness, comorbidities and medications was obtained. Subjects with liver disease, renal disease, chronic infections, alcohol use and malignancy were excluded. Demographic data and anthropometric indices were recorded.

A fasting blood sample was obtained from the subjects and analysed for Glu, urea, creatinine, GGT, UA, liver function tests and lipid profile. The ratios that were calculated included Glu:GGT, Glu:UA, GGT:BMI and triglyceride (TG):BMI. The biochemical parameters were estimated using automated chemistry analyser Beckman Coulter AU480 (Brea, California, USA) by standard methods. The subjects were classified as normoglycaemic (FBG <100 mg/dL) or hyperglycaemic (FBG ≥100 mg/dL). GGT >30 IU/L was considered to be high and UA >5.5 mg/dL was considered as hyperuricemia. The sensitivity and specificity of these cut-offs were calculated. The study participants were divided into quintiles based on glucose values and the mean glucose of quintiles were 66.4 mg/dL, 78.7mg/dL, 88.0 mg/dL, 103.3 mg/dL and 193.1 mg/dL for Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4 and Q5, respectively.

The distribution of all study parameters are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. The study parameters were compared between the normoglycaemic and hyperglycaemic groups as well as between the gender groups using independent student's t test. The study group was divided into quintiles of glucose and the parameters were compared between the quintiles using ANOVA. Pearson correlation was used to assess the linear relationship between the study parameters. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was done to derive the cut-off values for GGT, UA, Glu:GGT and Glu:UA to predict fasting hyperglycaemia in the context of insulin resistance. All statistical analyses were carried out at 5% level of significance using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) software, Version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA). A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

A total of 500 consecutive subjects, in the age group of 25–65 years, were included; 334 (66.8%) were male and 166 (33.2%) were female. The mean age of the study population was 44.1 ± 13.4 years. The normoglycaemic group was comprised of 346 (69.2 %) subjects and 154 (30.8%) were present in the hyperglycaemic group. Glucose, total cholesterol, GGT, UA, Glu:GGT, Glu:UA, GGT:HDL were significantly higher in the hyperglycaemic group compared to the normoglycaemic group (P <0.05 each). Total protein (P = 0.02) and albumin (P = 0.04) were significantly lower in the hyperglycaemic group compared to the normoglycaemic group [Table 1].

Gender based analysis showed that urea, TG, TG:HDL and GGT were significantly higher in males compared to females. In the current study, GGT was high (GGT >30 IU/L) in 202 (40.4%) and hyperuricemia (UA >5.5 mg/dL) was found in 122 (24.4%) subjects. GGT levels >30 IU/L had a sensitivity of 51.9% and specificity of 64.7%. The positive predictive value was 39.6% and the negative predictive value was 75.2%. The UA cut-off of >5.5 mg/dL had a sensitivity of 31.8% and a specificity of 78.9%. The positive predictive value was 40.2% and the negative predictive value was 72.2%.

The Glu:GGT (r = 0.482; P <0.001) and Glu:UA (r = 0.775; P <0.001) ratios had a significant positive correlation with glucose [Fig. 2]. Glucose showed a significant weak positive correlation with GGT:HDL ratio (r = 0.22; P <0.001), TG and total cholesterol. The remaining parameters did not show any significant statistic correlation.

ROC analysis of GGT, UA, Glu:GGT and Glu:UA ratios in predicting fasting hyperglycaemia showed that Glu:UA was a good predictor of fasting hyperglycaemia (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.808; P <0.0001) and Glu:GGT (AUC = 0.682; P <0.0001), while GGT and UA also had a significant AUC in predicting fasting hyperglycaemia (0.614 and 0.575, respectively) [Fig. 3]. 4. Discussion

The current study found that 30.8% of the participants were hyperglycaemic (impaired fasting glucose and fasting hyperglycaemia). GGT, UA, Glu:GGT and Glu:UA ratios were found to be significantly higher in subject with fasting hyperglycaemia which is suggestive of oxidative stress. This finding is synonymous with previous studies that have shown GGT as a predictor of IFG, prediabetes and T2DM;[3,4,9] however, the current study's high Glu:GGT ratio was not documented in these studies. Lin et al. conducted a cross-sectional study to address the association of dietary patterns with abnormal serum hepatic enzymes in adults with IFG. They documented that a Mediterranean dietary pattern had an inverse association with risk of high serum ALT and GGT; this is probably due to rich antioxidants, phenolic compounds and fibre in a Mediterranean diet that prevent elevation of liver enzymes by their protective effect on oxidative stress.[14] In addition, the significantly high total cholesterol levels in the current study's hyperglycaemic group accompanied by high GGT, UA, Glu:GGT and Glu:UA ratios in these individuals explains the implication of dyslipidaemia and oxidative stress in the pathogenesis of hyperglycaemia. The GGT:HDL ratio was high in hyperglycaemic subjects although the HDL levels did not show a significant difference. It is evident that ratios of these metabolic markers are better in predicting insulin resistance and hyperglycaemia than the individual markers themselves. TG:HDL is also proposed as an estimate of cardiometabolic risk associated with insulin resistance in adolescents. Kurniawan et al.'s results were similar; their study included Taiwanese adults where TyG index and TG:HDL ratio were positively associated with high GGT values.[15] Chronically high GGT levels initiate breakdown of extracellular glutathione and depletion of essential antioxidant. The resulting cellular distress leads to metabolic changes that increases UA production along with reactive oxygen species by the action of xanthine oxidase.[16] Both elevated GGT and high UA serves as markers of oxidative stress and metabolic syndrome and are individual predictors of cardiovascular risk. Thus, Glu:GGT and Glu:UA ratios provide a comprehensive picture of the inflammatory status and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (i.e., manifestation of metabolic syndrome; NAFLD) in relation to hyperglycaemia and serve as useful markers of cardiovascular risk.

Molla et al. found an increasing trend of ALT and GGT across quartiles of UA and a positive correlation of UA with GGT in Bangladeshi adults.[12] AlMuraikhy et al. found high GGT and UA levels in non-diabetic individuals with insulin resistance.[17] GGT and UA also have a strong correlation with BMI suggesting that abdominal obesity triggers hyperuricemia and hepatic involvement.[18] Additionally, sleep duration-fasting insulin relationship was mediated by GGT and UA, signifying that factors associated with inflammation, oxidative stress and antioxidant status play an important role in sleep-insulin and glycaemic control.[11] Liu et al. showed that insulin resistance had a significant association with high GGT levels among overweight/obese non-diabetic Chinese adults. GGT is known to be a predictor of diabetes. Insulin resistance drives liver dysfunction through a process known as the ‘two-hit hypothesis’. The ‘first hit’ is fatty liver or steatosis and the oxidative stress induced by high fat diet by the activation of CYP2E1 is the ‘second hit’. High intrahepatic free fatty acids leads to lipid peroxidation since the antilipolytic effect of insulin is lost in insulin resistance.[19]

After dividing the subjects into quintiles based on their glucose levels, GGT, UA, Glu:GGT and Glu:UA ratios were significantly higher in the 5th quintile compared to the other quintiles. Hyperglycaemia is associated with high GGT values and glucose lowering agents such as SGLT-2 inhibitors tend to decrease GGT levels.[20] Wang et al. compared the 5 quintiles of GGT and found a significant association of hyperglycaemia, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, creatinine, AST, ALT and UA with high GGT values; they found that people with high GGT levels were more likely to be male and obese.[21] In the current study, GGT but not UA was significantly higher in males compared to females which may be due to physiological and hormonal influences. However, GGT or Glu:GGT did not correlate with BMI. Lack of positive correlation between GGT and UA in the current study may be due to variation in population characteristics such as age, BMI, dietary and lifestyle factors. The low total protein and albumin levels in the hyperglycaemic group could be the response to underlying inflammatory status and oxidative stress as albumin is a negative acute phase reactant.

The high negative predictive value of GGT (75.2%) and UA (72.2%) indicates that hyperglycaemia is unlikely when GGT and UA levels are normal. This denotes that low oxidative stress prevails in normoglycaemic status and vice versa. Nevertheless, ROC analysis for predicting hyperglycaemia revealed that the AUC was good for the Glu:GGT ratio (0.682) and Glu:UA ratio (0.808) compared to GGT and UA alone. Hence, it is prudent to interpret these oxidative markers in relation with glycaemic status rather than as individual values. Glu:UA and Glu:GGT ratios showed a significant positive correlation with glucose levels in the correlation analysis. The current study did not find any positive correlation between GGT and UA as has been documented in other studies.[21,22] Yet both Glu:GGT and Glu:UA ratios were predictors of fasting hyperglycaemia. This is a bidirectional phenomenon whereby insulin resistance and hyperglycaemia trigger liver injury by inducing inflammatory pathways and endothelial dysfunction while NAFLD can interfere with insulin signalling pathways.[23] Hepatocyte injury increases UA and the release of GGT by enhancing purine breakdown and activating the HMP shunt pathway. UA, in turn, increases the risk of NAFLD and increases GGT.[21]

The prevalence of high GGT was 40.4% and hyperuricemia was 24.4% in the current study. Plausible reasons for the association of high GGT with hyperuricemia is the pro-oxidant effect of GGT and its role in insulin resistance. Decrease in insulin sensitivity decreases UA excretion by activating Na+H+ exchange in renal tubules thereby leading to hyperuricemia.[24] Caporaso et al.'s large population-based study found that the odds ratio for a quartile change in HOMA-IR for GGT was 1.4 which was broadly similar to that observed in prediabetes and diabetes, despite laboratory values being within normal limits across insulin quartiles.[25] Therefore, even if GGT and UA are within normal limits, using the ratio of Glu:GGT and Glu:UA could be better predictors fasting hyperglycaemia and surrogate markers of insulin resistance.

This study was subject to some limitations. A key limitation of this study is its cross-sectional design, which precludes the establishment of causal relationships between the examined parameters. Therefore, prospective studies are warranted to further investigate these associations.

5. Conclusion

GGT, UA, Glu:GGT and Glu:UA ratios were found to be significantly high in subjects with fasting hyperglycaemia which is suggestive of oxidative stress. Glu:UA and Glu:GGT ratios showed a significant positive correlation with glucose levels. The Glu:UA ratio is a good predictor of fasting hyperglycaemia in the context of insulin resistance. Glu:UA and Glu:GGT ratios can be considered as surrogate markers of underlying oxidative stress and insulin resistance leading to fasting hyperglycaemia even when GGT and UA are within the normal biological reference range.
