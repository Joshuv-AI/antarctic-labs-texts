Role of distance in the utilization of emergency departments for non-traumatic dental conditions in Maryland, USA, 2017–2021
Authors: Uma Kelekar, Shillpa Naavaal, Sidney Carl Turner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDisparities in access to oral health are evident in Emergency Departments’ (EDs) utilization for non-traumatic dental conditions (NTDCs) in the United States (US). While studies have examined the associations of socio-economic factors with ED utilization, there is little evidence of the role of distance on EDs use for NTDCs. To fill the gap, the main objectives of our study were to: (i) assess how geographic proximity to the nearest ED influences utilization of NTDCs (ii) examine how utilization varies with distance to nearest private dental clinics, community health centers or federally qualified health centers and urgent care clinics.MethodsWe used the State Emergency Department Database and The American Dental Association provider data files for 2017–2021 for Maryland. Following the Dental Quality Alliance (DQA) guidelines, we extracted NTDC visits using ICD-10 diagnoses codes. On using negative binomial regression models, we estimated the ED utilization rate for the overall population as well as for sub-groups defined by age, race/ethnicity and payer as a function of nearest distances between the patient zip code and an ED facility; as well as to dental providers, while controlling for confounders.ResultsThe average NTDC utilization rate declined (β= -0.299; 95% CI: [-0.411, -0.188) as distance to the nearest ED increased, while utilization was positively associated with distance to a nearest provider (β = 0.313; 95% CI: 0.200, 0.425). Distance decay effect amongst NH Blacks was the highest and different from that of NH Whites: there was a decline of 40.4% and 16.9% in ED visits with doubling of distance, respectively. Significant differences were reported between children (β = 0.236, 95% CI: 0.086, 0.386) and young adults aged 19–34 years (β = 0.395, 95% CI: 0.265, 0.526) in utilization as distance to a nearest provider increased. Lastly, a doubling of distance to Medicaid-accepting dentists or those affiliated with FQHCs was linked to an approximately 23% increase in ED use for NTDCs.ConclusionsOur findings show evidence of a distance decay effect with respect to ED utilization for NTDC visits, while also demonstrating a decline in ED visits when a dental provider was nearby suggesting an interplay of perceived need, cost, and access to dental care in the community.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13523-w.Keywords: Distance, Utilization, Emergency departments, NTDCs, Distance decay, Dental conditions, Dental clinics, Maryland

Introduction

Access to dental care and oral health services in the United States (US) is low, especially for individuals with low income, those covered by publicly-funded programs, and those living in rural areas [1]. Emergency Departments (EDs) are designed to treat traumatic and urgent conditions in a timely fashion. However, there is a notable increase in the utilization of EDs for Non-Traumatic Dental Conditions (NTDCs) nationally over the past decade [1, 2]. NTDCs refer to diseases affecting the teeth and supporting structures without a traumatic origin [3]. Studies focusing on NTDCs utilization in the EDs across the US have identified several socio-demographic characteristics to be strongly associated with ED use such as being female, racial-ethnic minority (e.g. Non-Hispanic (NH) Black), working young adults, residing in rural or low-income areas or those with public insurance coverage specifically Medicaid or the uninsured [4–6].

Based on the Anderson’s Model of Health Services Use framework [7], commonly used to study health utilization behaviors and patterns, individuals’ behaviors and outcomes are determined by personal and environmental characteristics related to need, predisposing and enabling factors. Previous research has also applied this framework to examine ED utilization of health services [8]. Access is a critical enabling factor known to influence utilization of ED services, particularly for non-urgent health services. Studies have identified geographic proximity, often used as a measure of access, to significantly influence the use of ED for non-urgent services both in US and internationally [8–10]. According to the Rosenstock’s Health Belief Model [HBM] [11], decisions on accessing care are determined by perceived severity and susceptibility to a disease on one hand, and perceived costs and benefits of care on the other. In conjunction, the phenomenon of ‘distance decay’ suggests that as the distance to an ED facility increases, the likelihood of utilizing ED services decreases, especially for non-urgent medical conditions. Empirical studies have tested and found evidence in support of this effect. For example, Chen et al. (2015) found shorter distances to be associated with higher non-urgent utilization among ED users in California [9]. Similarly, another 2007 US study found higher utilization of ED services among certain population groups such as African Americans, those below poverty level and residing within 5 miles of a hospital in the state of Mississippi [12]. These findings align with the HBM, which suggests that higher travel costs act as a tax-like deterrent, thus reducing the demand for health care services, especially if the medical condition is treatable in a primary care setting. Consequently, those individuals living further away from an ED with higher travel costs may forgo care, whereas those in close proximity, for whom the cost of accessing an ED is low, it may justify seeking non-urgent care in EDs [13].

Another area of research explores how proximity to other providers in addition to EDs can influence peoples’ decisions to use an ED. In the US, settings to provide dental care can include dental offices, Community Health Centers (CHCs) or Federally-Qualified Health Centers (FQHCs), and Urgent Care Clinics (UCCs). FQHCs are safety-net providers that receive government support to serve populations with limited healthcare access, offering comprehensive outpatient services, including dental care regardless of patients’ ability to pay [14]. UCCs, on the other hand, are walk-in clinics that typically handle minor illnesses and injuries. Although they do not typically provide comprehensive dental services, patients can receive pain medications at UCCs [15]. One US-based study found that when FQHCs are located within 0.5 miles of Medicaid patients, ED use for non-emergent medical visits reduced [9]. Similarly, another 2009 study found that children living further away from their PCPs had 13% greater ED use than those living within a mile of their PCP [16]. Another study from the United Kingdom (UK) found that with each additional kilometer from a minor injury unit, an alternative care setting to an ED, ED use increased for adults and children [17].

There is limited research that investigates the role of access in driving ED use for NTDCs. One study demonstrated distance as a significant barrier to NTDC use in EDs among the Medicaid population in Wisconsin [3]. They found that individuals living 3 miles or more from an ED had significantly lower rates of utilization, compared to those living closer. However, the study did not consider the location of other dental providers. Additionally, while they identified disparities among racial groups, particularly Hispanics, their findings were limited to Medicaid beneficiaries in Wisconsin. Thus, through this study, we aim to address this gap by examining whether a distance decay effect exists in ED utilization for NTDC across the state of Maryland, as well as among racial-ethnic minorities, age and payer groups, while taking into account the locations of dental providers in the area. To the best of our knowledge, no prior study has conducted a similar investigation.

The objectives of this study are to (i) assess how proximity to a nearest ED influences utilization for NTDCs at ED (ii) examine how ED utilization for NTDCs varies based on the distance to the nearest dental provider (including private dental clinics, CHCs or FQHCs, and UCCs) (iii) investigate differences in the relationship of distance with ED use for NTDCs across racial-ethnic, age and payer groups. We hypothesize that “as the distance between patient’s residence and a nearest ED increases, ED use for NTDCs will decrease”. Conversely, we hypothesize that “as the distance between patient’s residence and nearest dental providers increases, ED use for NTDCs will increase”. We also expect to observe differences among the different racial-ethnic minority, age and payer groups. The findings of this study will be crucial for policy-makers to better understand the interplay between ED locations and other dental providers in influencing ED use for NTDCs. Furthermore, they will inform the development of programs and interventions aimed at reducing unnecessary ED use for non-urgent dental visits.

Data and methodology

Data

We used Maryland’s State Emergency Department Database (SEDD) data for 2017–2021 to estimate ED utilization rates. The SEDD are a visit-level set of longitudinal state-specific emergency department databases that includes all emergency department visits in the state that did not result in an admission, along with patient zip codes and hospital identification numbers [18]. These unique hospital identifiers in the SEDD were matched with publicly-available Hospital Compare data [19] to obtain hospital addresses. The American Dental Association (ADA) Master Files, available for 2017–2021, were accessed for the addresses of dental clinics (that included private dental offices and dental colleges). The locations of permanent service-delivery FQHCs/CHCs (n ≈ 100 per year) were obtained for each year from publicly available data provided by Health Resources and Services Administration (HRSA) [20]. In Maryland, the ADA data contained 85% of dentists (n = 5,508) with a business address on record. This database was used in conjunction with the data from Maryland’s State Board of Dental Examiners [21] that included a list of currently licensed dentists in the state that enabled us to identify 297 additional dental active dental offices. These addresses were manually checked and retained if they had active business websites. Additionally, we obtained addresses of approximately 250 UCCs operating in the state from a licensed provider of UCCs. Demographic data from the 5-year American Community Survey (ACS) [22], including the total population, distribution by age, sex, race, payer-type, median household income and population density was aggregated to the zip-code level using a crosswalk file (linking the census tracts to zip codes) from the Department of Housing and Urban Development [23].

Study sample

Following the Dental Quality Alliance (DQA) guidelines [23, 24], NTDCs were identified using ICD-10 (K000-K089; M2600-M2769; R682, R859, R6884, A690, Z0120, Z0121, Z463, Z464) diagnoses codes. We limited the analysis to Maryland residents by excluding non-Maryland patient zip codes.

Outcome variable

Our primary outcome variable was ED NTDCs utilization rates (i.e. visits per 100,000 residents) estimated at the zip code level.

Explanatory variables

The primary explanatory variables were the average distance, calculated as the mean straight-line distance from patient’s zip code of residence to the nearest ED, dental provider and UCC along with age (0–18 years, 19–34 years, 35–64 years, 65 and over), and race/ethnicity (NH White, NH Black, Hispanic and other race (included Asian, Pacific Islander, Native American and other race) groups. Control variables included gender, age distribution, race and ethnicity, payer-type composition, along with median household income, and population density. To calculate the distance at the zip code level, distance between census tracts’ centroids and the nearest ED were computed. These distances were aggregated to the zip code level by taking a weighted average across all census tracts intersecting a particular zip code, where the weight was the fraction of the zip code’s residents residing in a particular tract. For the second hypothesis, locations of nearest private dental clinics, FQHCs and UCCs were geocoded and distances were computed. In order to obtain the nearest distance to other providers by zip code, the weighted average of distance of census tracts intersecting the zip code were computed. Demographic variables were aggregated in a similar way.

Statistical analyses

Our unit of observation is the demographic group-by-zip code-by-year, where demographic groups are defined by either race/ethnicity or age and group-zip code level data were pooled across multiple years. To account for potential confounders, we applied the multivariable regression methodology. Since the outcome (ED utilization rate) in this analysis has a distribution similar to a count variable, to account for influential observations that result from the skewed distribution of this dependent variable we estimated the negative binomial specification, an extension of the Poisson model, ideally suitable to model count data that allows for over-dispersion and has been used in prior studies [3, 13]. To account for potential heterogeneity across demographic groups, separate regression models were estimated for population sub-groups defined by age and race/ethnicity. Additionally, given that a majority of the ED visits for NTDCs in Maryland involved Medicaid beneficiaries, we conducted an additional sub-group analyses stratified by payer type (Results in Supplemental file). As a robustness check, we utilized the available ADA data on provider characteristics, specifically, whether dentists accepted Medicaid or were affiliated with FQHCs for the years 2017 and 2019. In the supplemental analysis, we used distance to these providers as an additional covariate in the regression analyses.

It was approved by Marymount University’s Institutional Review Board (IRB) under exempt category and data user agreement was completed by the researcher who analyzed the data. All analyses was completed using the Stata statistical package (Version 18) and statistical significance was reported at 95% confidence level unless stated otherwise [26].

Results

During 2017–2021, there were 185,132 NTDC-related ED visits. After omitting visits of non-Maryland residents (3,180, 1.72%), there were 181,952 total visits. Over half of these visits were among females (53.05%), working adults ages 19–64 years (83.41%), and NH Blacks (55.24%). Majority were Medicaid beneficiaries (55.67%) followed by those having private insurance (18.93%), individuals residing in metro areas with a population > 1 million (82.43%) and in low-income zip codes (73.89%). About one-third (29.95%) of these visits were made on a weekend.

The state-wide ED utilization rate was 692.60 NTDCs visits per 100,000 residents (See Fig. 1, for zip-code level variation in 2017). There were significant variations by the observed sub-groups of population based on age and race. The highest ED for NTDCs was amongst adults 19–34 ages, followed by the working adults aged 35–64 years. Non-Hispanic Blacks had nearly 2–5 times higher ED use for NTDC compared to other racial ethnic groups. On the other hand, the lowest utilization was reported amongst children 18 years and below, and Non-Hispanic others.

On an average, for the residents of Maryland the distance from a nearest ED was 4.17 miles (SD = 3.18). Among the other dental care providers in the state, FQHCs was the farthest of all (4.43 miles, SD = 4.09), followed by an UCC (2.64 miles, SD = 2.90) and then a dental clinic (1.00 miles, SD = 1.33). NH Blacks followed by Hispanics reported the shortest distance to a nearest ED, ranging between 3 and 3.5 miles. These groups also reported the shortest average distances to an UCC or community-based dental clinics including FQHCs compared to NH Whites.

Multivariable analysis

The regression analyses were conducted on a zip-code by year dataset comprising 2,142 observations across 472 zip codes. We first estimated the Poisson specification but a likelihood ratio test confirmed negative binomial as more appropriate than the Poisson specification. Furthermore, the Akaike’s information criteria (AIC) and Bayesian information criteria (BIC) [25] values were the smallest for the negative binomial specification indicating this model to be the best fit compared to the other count models we tested. We therefore report and interpret findings from the negative binomial model only. Table 2 displays the results for the overall population, after adjusting for covariates. The or the slope coefficient estimated presents the percent change in ED visits per 100,000 residents in response to percent change in the nearest distance to a facility. The effect size representing the distance decay effect on ED utilization for NTDC visits is with respect to the nearest ED, while the remaining three relevant effects are with respect to the nearest dental clinic, FQHC and UCC respectively. Table 3 presents regression results (3a-3d) for race and ethnicity groups and Table 4 presents results (4a-4d) for age sub-groups of populations. To interpret the distance effects for the sub-groups of population and observe the differences across the different categories, we interacted the nearest distance variable with the two sub-group dimensions of population – racial/ethnic (NH Whites, NH Blacks, Hispanic and NH Other) and age groups (Under 19 years, 19-34-year-old, 35-64-year-old and older adults 65 years and above.) in separate sets of regression models, while also controlling for the main effects of these variables. In the first model, (3a-3d), we compared each of the racial groups to the NH Whites, while in the second model (4a-4d), we compared each of the age-groups to children < = 18 years.

Distance effect for the total overall population

The regression results in Table 2 reveals that a statistically significant distance decay effect is present for the total population indicating that as distance to a nearest ED increases, the utilization of NTDCs declines. Specifically, a doubling of distance was associated with an approximate 29.9% decline in NTDC ED visits per 100,000 population. While there was no significant effect observed on NTDC ED use of distance from a FQHC or UCC for the overall population, a doubling of distance to a dental clinic was, however, associated with an increase of NTDC ED visits by 31.3%. Our findings were thus in support of hypotheses 1 and 2.

Distance effects for sub-groups

Racial group

The regression model results presented in Table 3 reveals the variations across sub-groups of race and ethnicity. Specifically, a distance decay effect was observed to be the largest amongst the NH Blacks (β = -0.235, 95% CI: -0.393, -0.076) and differed significantly from the baseline category of NH Whites. Consistently, the elasticity of NTDC ED utilization rate computed for each of these sub-groups, revealed that the distance decay effect amongst NH Blacks was the highest and differed statistically from that of NH Whites, indicative of a decline of 40.4% ED visits among NH Blacks, in comparison to NH Whites (16.9%) with doubling of distance; a difference of 23% change in ED utilization rate (Fig. 2, Panel A). Additionally, the elasticity of NTDC ED utilization rate with respect to distance for other races was 20.2% and statistically different from zero, although it didn’t differ significantly from other categories.

Panel A (left) represent elasticities of ED utilization rate with respect to distance from nearest ED facility for racial/ethnic minorities and age-groups; Panel B (right) represents elasticities of ED utilization rate with respect to distance from nearest dental clinic across racial/ethnic minorities and age-groups

Another key finding was that of ED utilization rate to be positively associated (β = 0.332, 95% CI: 0.208, 0.457) with the distance from a dental clinic suggesting that as distance to the dental clinic increased, the ED utilization rate for NTDCs also increased (See Table 3b). Specifically, the largest elasticity was observed amongst NH Blacks (35.8%), followed by Hispanics (35.4%), NH Whites (33.3%), and other races (27.7%) (Fig. 2, Panel B). However, while all the effects were statistically significant, there were not notably different from each other.

Age-groups

The regression model results (4a-4d) in Table 4 present variations across age-groups. Among children under 19 years, NTDC ED utilization declined as distance to a nearest ED increased (β = -0.262, 95% CI: -0.409, -0.114). The elasticities with respect to distance to a nearest ED, were the largest among working adults, and lowest amongst older adults 65 years and over, and statistically different from zero, albeit not statistically different from each other (Fig. 2, Panel A). Similar to the previous model, there were no significant effects observed on ED use of distance from either FQHCs or UCCs. However, ED use was found to be positively influenced by distance from a dental clinic. As distance to a dental clinic increased, ED utilization for NTDCs also increased (β = 0.236, 95% CI: 0.086, 0.386) (Table 4(3b)) with statistically significant differences in elasticities reported between children and adults aged 19–34 years respectively (23.6% vs. 39.5%) (See Fig. 2, Panel B).

Discussion

Our study found evidence of a reduction in ED utilization for NTDCs as distance to a nearest ED increased, for the state of Maryland from 2017 to 2021. Conversely, we found ED utilization to increase as distance from nearest private dental clinics increased while we found no significant relationship of ED utilization with distance to UCCs. Our study findings are compatible with the postulates put forth in Rosenstock’s Health Belief Model [HBM] [11], discussed earlier. When comparing racial groups, NH Blacks demonstrated the most pronounced distance decay effect, significantly different from other racial groups. For NH Blacks, who constitute a disproportionate share of ED users for NTDCs covered by Medicaid in Maryland (which did not offer any adult dental coverage until adult dental coverage until mid-June 2019 [27], the perceived cost of traveling to the ED to seek dental care plausibly outweighs the benefits of care, particularly amongst those that are living further away from an ED. Consequently, a long distance or high travel cost might discourage these users from seeking ED care, especially for NTDCs. In the long run, this could also imply forgoing dental care especially if ED serves as their sole safety net provider [3, 13] which may in turn have an unintended effect eventually leading to negative oral health outcomes, due to lack of treatment and progression of disease [28]. Thus, having affordable and easily accessible dental care through dental care settings is warranted for reducing ED dental visits. Although the effects didn’t differ across age-groups, younger working adults exhibited the most pronounced decrease in ED utilization for NTDCs with increasing distance, while older adults had the lowest distance decay effect, followed by children. This suggests that ED utilization declined more steeply with every additional unit of distance traveled for working adults as compared to older adults and children. For older adults, especially if they had a supplemental dental coverage through Medicare, a smaller distance decay effect may indicate a stronger sense of urgency to seek timely care, regardless of how far they have to travel to the nearest provider [29, 30]. Parents of young children, on the other hand, are likely to perceive EDs to be better equipped with resources and staff to diagnose their child’s condition in the quickest possible manner, thereby relieving their own anxiety [29–31].

Another factor contributing to the phenomenon of distance decay in non-urgent ED use is the accessibility and availability of community providers. A few previous studies [9, 29] have indicated a decline in ED use as local providers in the community were more accessible. Specifically, communities with better access to dentists tend to rely less on ED services. While our findings align with those of previous studies and we observed a positive association between ED use and distance to the nearest dental clinic, our analysis was limited by the lack of detailed provider-level characteristics such as whether they accepted Medicaid patients, practice hours or appointment availability. Moreover, we did not find a significant relationship between proximity to the nearest FQHC and ED visits for NTDCs. This result, however, should be interpreted with caution, as FQHCs are not the only source of dental care for Medicaid beneficiaries. In addition to them, the Medicaid population can seek dental services at dental clinics accepting Medicaid beneficiaries. Therefore, to account for this, we conducted a robustness check and incorporated information on whether dental providers accepted Medicaid patients or were affiliated with a FQHC for 2017 and 2019 (the two years for which data was available). Our results showed that proximity to a Medicaid accepting dentist/or those affiliated with a FQHC was indeed associated with reduced ED utilization among Medicaid beneficiaries.

Our findings further showed a significant effect among younger working adults, suggesting that if distance to a nearest dental clinic doubled, their use of EDs would increase more substantially compared to children. One possible explanation is the convenience factor: longer travel times to a dentist could imply time away from work, thereby prompting individuals to opt for a more accessible ED instead [32]. Additionally, these younger adults may not have an established regular dental care routine due to an overall good health status, and fewer healthcare needs.

NTDC-related ED visits are costly and often result in non-definitive treatments involving pain killers and antibiotics [33]. To reduce the unnecessary use of the EDs for NTDCs, therefore, it is critical to promote oral health education and increase public awareness about the role of FQHCs and community dental clinics as cost-effective alternatives to ED care [8]. Some challenges associated with accessing dentists in the community include medical mistrust of PCPs [34], limited dental practice hours, and reluctance on the part of dentists to accept Medicaid patients [35]. Our extended analysis reinforces the role of Medicaid-accepting dentists as viable alternatives to EDs, highlighting the importance of enhancing access to such providers. Policy-level changes, therefore, such as increasing Medicaid reimbursement rates might be needed to support and encourage dental providers to accept Medicaid patients and help close the access gaps and subsequently reduce unnecessary ED use. While UCCs can serve as alternatives to EDs for general oral care, they are often not equipped to manage dental conditions. Integrating dental professionals, such as hygienists or dentists, into UCCs could provide a feasible strategy for diverting NTDC cases away from EDs. Additionally, improving appointment availability—such as extending clinic hours or offering weekend services—may help overcome access barriers, particularly for working-age adults [36].

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the distance decay phenomenon for NTDCs while controlling for the location of other dental providers in the community and other socio-demographic zip-code level characteristics. However, our study is not without limitations. First, we used the straight-line distance between the patient’s residence and the nearest facility, instead of the street or travel distance over a road network. While straight line distance is highly correlated with street travel distance [37, 38], using street distance or travel time would also require making assumptions about what travel route options were available for residents of a particular zip in a given year, which might have changed over the study period. Nevertheless, this approach is consistent with several previous studies that have reported a high correlation between straight line distance and travel distance. Second, visits originating from a particular zip code were assigned to co-ordinates of its population-weighted census tract centroid. While population weighting adjusts for underlying disparities in population distribution but not demographic characteristics within areal units, this technique has been the convention in prior studies on healthcare access [3]. Next, our data did not allow us to determine if the patient visited the ED from any other location other than their residence. Finally, while distance to the ED and other dental clinics is one of the proxies of travel cost to seek care, this study did not assess factors such as means of transportation, or had access to information on office hours, wait times to and availability of dental appointments etc. Future research is warranted to examine the mechanisms guiding the distance decay phenomenon for overall populations and sub-groups, while taking into consideration more granular information on dentists’ characteristics, as well as testing it at larger regional levels.

Conclusion

ED utilization for NTDCs increased with shorter distances to a nearest ED, while decreased with shorter distances to nearest dental providers in the community. The largest distance decay effect was observed amongst NH Blacks while ED utilization was observed to decline when a provider in the community was available nearby with significant differences observed between children and young adults. Dental clinics accepting Medicaid patients were a viable alternative to EDs for the Medicaid beneficiaries. Our findings suggest an interplay of perceived need, cost, and access to dental care in the community and provide additional support for creating solutions to improve access to dental care.
