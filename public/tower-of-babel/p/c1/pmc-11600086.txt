Flavonoid intakes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, adult asthma, and lung function: a cohort study in the UK Biobank
Authors: Nicola P Bondonno, Benjamin H Parmenter, Alysha S Thompson, Amy Jennings, Kevin Murray, Daniel Bech Rasmussen, Anna Tresserra-Rimbau, Tilman Kühn, Aedín Cassidy
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGiven their antioxidative stress, anti-allergic, anti-inflammatory, and immune-modulating effects, flavonoids are hypothesized to play a role in preventing chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and asthma.ObjectivesThis cohort study aimed to examine associations between flavonoid intake and COPD, asthma, and lung function.MethodsAmong 119,466 participants of the UK Biobank, median [interquartile range] age of 60 [53, 65] y, we estimated intakes of flavonoids, flavonoid-rich foods, and a flavodiet score from 24-h diet assessments. Prospective associations with both incident COPD and asthma and cross-sectional associations with measures of lung function [%predicted forced expiratory volume in 1s (FEV1); and FEV1/forced vital capacity (FVC)] were examined using multivariable-adjusted Cox proportional hazards and linear regression models, respectively. We investigated mediation by inflammation––represented by the INFLA score––and stratified analyses by smoking status.ResultsCompared with low intakes, moderate intakes of total flavonoids, flavonols, theaflavins + thearubigins, and flavanones, and moderate-to-high intakes of flavanol monomers, proanthocyanidins, anthocyanins, flavones, and the flavodiet score were associated with up to an 18% lower risk of incident COPD {e.g., [hazard ratio (95% confidence interval) for total flavonoids: 0.83 (0.75, 0.92)]} but not incident asthma. Furthermore, compared with low intakes, higher intakes of all flavonoid subclasses (except theaflavins + thearubigins), and the flavodiet score were associated with better percent predicted FEV1 baseline. Associations were most apparent in ever (current or former) smokers. Flavonoid intakes were inversely associated with the INFLA score, which appeared to mediate 11%–14% of the association between intakes of proanthocyanidins and flavones and incident COPD.ConclusionsModerate-to-high flavonoid intakes were associated with a lower risk of COPD and better lung function, particularly among ever smokers. Promoting intakes of healthy flavonoid-rich foods, namely, tea, apples, and berries, may improve respiratory health and lower COPD risk, particularly in individuals with a smoking history.

Introduction

According to the latest Global Burden of Disease study, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) ranked sixth among the leading causes of disability-adjusted life-years in 2021 [1]. This pervasive health challenge is characterized by persistent respiratory symptoms and irreversible airflow limitations resulting from an abnormal inflammatory response to noxious gases and particles [2,3]. The projected increase in COPD burden in the coming decades, attributed to continued exposure to smoking and environmental risk factors such as air pollution and biomass fuel combustion, alongside an aging population [4], underscores the urgent need for preventive strategies. Asthma, a complex and heterogeneous airway disease marked by airway inflammation and bronchial hyper-responsiveness, presents recurrent episodes of dyspnea, wheezing, and cough [5]. It is estimated that >260 million people were living with asthma globally in 2019 [6]. Although traditionally associated with early childhood, adult-onset asthma introduces a different etiology influenced by environmental and lifestyle factors, including diet [7]. Given the antioxidative stress, anti-allergic, anti-inflammatory, and immune-modulating effects of dietary flavonoids, they are hypothesized to play a role in preventing both COPD [8] and asthma [9].

Flavonoids, a class of polyphenols with subclasses based on chemical structure, are abundant in fruits, vegetables, tea, cocoa, and other plant-based foods and beverages. These bioactive compounds have demonstrated efficacy in reducing oxidative stress and systemic inflammation [10]. Our previous research demonstrates that higher flavonoid intakes are associated with a lower risk of incident COPD among current and former smokers, with indications that flavonoid intake may partially mitigate the adverse effects of smoking intensity [11]. There is also preliminary evidence from animal and human intervention studies suggesting that flavonoids may ameliorate, in part, the systemic inflammation and oxidative stress induced by smoking [[12], [13], [14]]. Additionally, higher flavonoid intakes have shown favorable associations with pulmonary function parameters [15,16], less age-related decline in lung function [17], and a lower incidence of asthma in adults [18]. Despite these promising findings, a comprehensive investigation is lacking, examining associations between flavonoid subclass intakes and flavonoid-rich food intakes and both lung function measures and lung disease outcomes (COPD and asthma) in a large well-phenotyped cohort.

Therefore, the primary objective of the present study was to investigate associations between total flavonoid intake, flavonoid subclasses, and the flavodiet score with incident COPD and asthma. Secondary aims include examining associations between flavonoid-rich foods and beverages and incident COPD and asthma, evaluating associations between flavonoid intakes and lung function measures (percent predicted FEV1 and FEV1/FVC ratio), exploring potential mediation of the associations by inflammation, and examining the consistency of associations between never and ever (current/former) smokers.

Methods

Study population

The UK Biobank is a prospective cohort study of ∼500,000 men and women who were recruited from across the United Kingdom between 2006 and 2010 when they were 40–69 y old if they lived within a reasonable traveling distance of one of the 22 assessment centers located across England, Wales, and Scotland [19]. At baseline, participants attended one of 22 assessment centers where they completed a touchscreen questionnaire, which collected information on sociodemographic characteristics, and lifestyle, reproductive, and environmental factors. In addition, anthropometric and physical measurements, including spirometry, were taken using standardized procedures [19]. Following this, participants completed the Oxford WebQ 24-h dietary questionnaire, issued on 5 separate occasions between 2009 and 2012 [20]. Information on health outcomes after recruitment into the cohort was gathered prospectively via data linkages with hospital and death registries. After withdrawals, data from 502,359 participants were available for the present study. We then excluded participants who completed less than two 24-h dietary assessments, those who had implausible energy intakes (<800 or >4200 kcal/d for men and <500 or >3500 kcal/d for women) and those with missing data for exposures or covariates (only for covariates where <2% of data were missing as missing indicator categories were created for variables with ≥2% missing data; see Supplemental Table 1 for more information), leaving 119,466 participants eligible for inclusion in the present study (Supplemental Figure 1). For cross-sectional spirometry analyses, we further excluded participants with missing outcome data as well as those who had smoked or used an inhaler in the hour preceding the spirometry test leaving 90,194 participants for this analysis. For prospective analyses, participants who had prevalent COPD or asthma at baseline or who were diagnosed with these conditions before completion of their last 24-h dietary assessment were also excluded when the respective disease was the outcome of interest leaving 117,990 participants for incident COPD analyses and 105,078 participants for incident asthma analyses. For mediation analyses, participants with missing blood biomarker data and participants with prevalent COPD were excluded leaving 92,367 participants for these analyses. The UK Biobank received ethical approval from the NHS North West Multi-centre Research Ethics Committee (Ref. 11/NW/0382) and all participants gave informed consent.

Exposures

Dietary information was collected using the Oxford WebQ 24-h dietary questionnaire, which asked about the frequency of consumption of >200 types of food and >30 beverages over the previous 24-h period [21]. Calculations of flavonoid intake, estimated from the Oxford WebQ 24-h dietary questionnaire using United States Department of Agriculture databases [22,23] have been described in detail previously [24]. Flavonoid intakes (mg/d), from all completed questionnaires for which energy intake was deemed to be plausible, were averaged. Intakes of the following 7 flavonoid subclasses were derived: flavonols, flavan-3-ol monomers, proanthocyanidins, theaflavins + thearubigins, anthocyanins, flavanones, and flavones. Total flavonoid intake was estimated as the sum of all subclasses. Furthermore, we calculated a flavodiet score, details of which have been published previously [24]. In brief, the score was made up of 10 high-flavonoid food items: tea (black and green), red wine, apples, berries, grapes, oranges (including satsumas), grapefruit, sweet peppers, onions, and dark chocolate. A final flavodiet score, ranging from 0 to 30 points, was calculated by summing the total number of servings consumed across the 10 selected food items with tea intakes capped at a maximum of 4 servings/day. Due to the nature of 24-h diet assessment, those with a recorded consumption of “zero” are likely to be a mix of true nonconsumers and those who have a frequency of consumption too low to be detected by the dietary assessment. Thus, in food-based analyses, participants in the lowest quantile (Q1) of consumption will be referred to as “very low consumers.”

Study outcomes

COPD and asthma

Prevalent COPD and asthma at baseline were identified using self-reported physician diagnosis of disease at baseline and registrations, before baseline, in the hospital registers. Incident hospitalizations/deaths from COPD or asthma after baseline were identified using hospital and death registers. The International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision codes used to capture COPD and asthma were J44 and J45, respectively.

Baseline measures of lung function

Forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) and forced vital capacity (FVC) were measured at baseline as described previously [25]. Two blows were recorded for each participant and a third blow was administered if the differences between both the FVC and FEV1 from the first 2 blows were >5%; the highest values for both FVC and FEV1 from acceptable blows were used. Percentage predicted FEV1 was defined as FEV1 divided by the predicted FEV1 multiplied by 100, with the description of the calculation of predicted FEV1 described previously [26].

Covariates

Information on sociodemographic factors, lifestyle factors, diet, and medical history were obtained from the baseline assessment. Covariates used in this study are described in detail in Supplemental Table 1. We calculated the inflammation (INFLA) score, a composite measure reflecting low-grade inflammation, by combining measures of high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), white blood cell count, platelet count, and the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio as described previously [27]. These 4 biomarkers are established indicators of systemic inflammation, jointly contributing to proinflammatory processes within diverse immune response pathways [27]. A higher INFLA score signifies a heightened level of low-grade inflammation.

Statistical analysis

Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, with age as the underlying time scale, were used to investigate relationships between exposures (flavonoid intakes, the flavodiet score, and intakes of flavonoid-rich foods) and incident COPD and asthma, separately. Participants were followed up from the completion of the last diet questionnaire until diagnosis of COPD or asthma, death, loss to follow-up, or the end of follow-up, whichever came first. Follow-up for hospital inpatient data was available until October 31, 2022, for England (Hospital Episode Statistics for England), August 31, 2022, for Scotland (Scottish Morbidity Records), and May 31, 2022, for Wales (Patient Episode Database for Wales). Schoenfeld residuals were plotted to examine Cox proportional hazards assumptions, with no violation found. All continuous covariates, including the exposures of interest, were modeled with restricted cubic splines using the “rms” R package with the rcs() function [28] to allow associations to be nonlinear. Exposure variables were categorized and the median value of each category was calculated. Hazard ratios (HRs), obtained from the Cox proportional hazards models, are relative to the median intake in the lowest intake quantile (reference value); these were plotted against the exposure variable, with 95% confidence interval (CI) bands provided. In addition, HRs and 95% CIs at the median intake in each quantile were extracted from the fitted models and tabulated. For visual simplicity, the x-axis of all figures was truncated at 3 standard deviations above the mean. Three models of adjustment were used: (1) minimally adjusted: gender and region (entered as a strata variable); (2) multivariable-adjusted: gender, region, body mass index, income, education, ethnicity, physical activity, alcohol intake, smoking status, packyears, passive smoking status, deprivation index, prevalent asthma, or prevalent COPD (as relevant, depending on the outcome), and diet stability; (3) multivariable-adjusted including potential dietary confounders: all variables in Model 2 plus intakes (g/d) of wholegrains, refined grains, sugar-sweetened beverages, dairy, eggs, fish, and meat. Confounders were selected based on a priori knowledge [8,[29], [30], [31]]. To examine cross-sectional associations between exposures and spirometry measures we used linear regression models with exposures modeled using restricted cubic splines, as described above. To examine the consistency of the associations, all analyses were stratified by smoking status (ever compared with never). For flavonoid exposures for which clear inverse associations with inflammation (represented by the INFLA score) and incident COPD were observed, the extent to which the association between flavonoid intake and COPD was mediated by inflammation was quantified through natural direct and indirect effects [32]. These estimate how large an association we would observe if flavonoid intake had no impact on inflammation (the natural direct effect) and if flavonoid intake only had an impact upon COPD through its impact on inflammation (the natural indirect effect). Estimates were obtained from Cox proportional hazards models, with exposures included as categorical variables (quintiles) and adjusting for covariates in Model 2, using the Medflex package for R [33]. CIs for these estimates were obtained by bootstrapping (1000 iterations). Given that the distinction between asthma, COPD, and a combination of both diseases is not always clear, and that asthma is a risk factor for COPD, particularly in smokers [34], as a sensitivity analysis we excluded all prevalent cases of both asthma and COPD when examining the association between total flavonoid intakes and incident COPD. Similarly, to examine the influence of reverse causality bias on the associations, in a second sensitivity analysis, participants who were diagnosed with COPD within the first 2 y of follow-up were excluded. Thirdly, to explore whether associations observed for red wine are likely attributable to its high flavonoid content, we examined associations between intakes of low-flavonoid white wine and incident COPD. Finally, examination of associations between total flavonoid intakes and spirometry measures were restricted to participants who had ≤3 y between their assessment center visit (where spirometry measures were taken) and their final diet assessment. All analyses were undertaken using R statistics (R Core Team, 2023 [35]).

Results

In this population of 119,466 UK Biobank participants, with a median [IQR] age of 60 [53, 65] y, 2195 incident cases of COPD and 2142 incident cases of asthma were identified during a maximum of 11.7 y of follow-up. The median [IQR] flavonoid intake was 790 [450–1115] mg/d. Participants with higher total flavonoid intakes were more likely to be older, to be more physically active, and were less likely to be living in London and to be a current smoker when compared with those with lower total flavonoid intakes (Table 1). Incident COPD and asthma

For intakes of total flavonoids, flavonols, theaflavins + thearubigins, and flavanones, associations with incident COPD were U-shaped (Figure 1), with the lowest risks observed in the moderate intake quintiles [HRQ3vsQ1 (95% CI); total flavonoids: 0.83 (0.75, 0.92); flavonols: 0.88 (0.80, 0.96); theaflavins + thearubigins: 0.89 (0.78, 1.01); flavanones: 0.83 (0.73, 0.94); Model 3; Table 2]. For the proanthocyanidin subclass, inverse associations with incident COPD appeared to be more linear, with the lowest risks observed in the highest intake quintile [HRQ5vsQ1 (95% CI); proanthocyanidins: 0.82 (0.73, 0.92); Model 3; Table 2]. For intakes of the flavan-3-ol monomer, anthocyanin, and flavone subclasses, and the flavodiet score, inverse associations with incident COPD were nonlinear (Figure 1), plateauing around moderate intakes, after which HR’s remained stable [HRQ3vsQ1 (95% CI); flavan-3-ol monomers: 0.89 (0.79, 0.99); anthocyanins: 0.84 (0.74, 0.94); flavone: 0.84 (0.76, 0.92); flavodiet score: 0.88 (0.80, 0.96; Model 3; Table 2]. When examining associations at the food level, for intakes of tea, red wine, peppers, and dark chocolate, U-shaped associations with incident COPD were observed (Supplemental Figure 3); when compared with very low consumers, those who consumed 2–4 serves per day (s/d) of tea, those who consumed ½–1 s/d of red wine, and those who consumed ¼–½ s/d of peppers had the lowest risks of incident COPD [tea: 0.85 (0.76, 0.95); red wine: 0.85 (0.75, 0.96); peppers: 0.88 (0.77, 0.99); Model 3; Supplemental Table 3]. For intakes of apples and berries, inverse associations with incident COPD were linear (Supplemental Figure 3), with the lowest risks observed for those who consumed >2 s/d of apples [0.70 (0.50, 1.00)] and those who consumed >1 s/d of berries [0.71 (0.56, 0.90)] when compared with very low consumers (Model 3; Supplemental Table 3). No clear associations between intakes of either grapes, oranges, grapefruits, or onions and incident COPD were observed (Supplemental Figure 3).

Cross-sectional associations with lung function parameters

Total flavonoid intakes were positively associated with percent predicted FEV1; associations were nonlinear and were initially steeper up until flavonoid intakes of ∼500 mg/d (Figure 2). Compared with participants with the lowest total flavonoid intakes (Q1), those with higher intakes (Q2–Q5) had a significantly higher percent predicted FEV1, for example, 0.89% (95% CI: 0.60%, 1.18%) higher for Q3, after adjusting for demographic, lifestyle, and dietary confounders (Model 3: Table 3). Comparable positive associations with percent predicted FEV1 were seen for intakes of all individual flavonoid subclasses (Table 3) with the strongest association observed for proanthocyanidins where participants with the highest intakes had a 1.23% higher percent predicted FEV1. Total flavonoid intakes were not associated with FEV1/FVC ratio in the whole analytic population (Figure 2). Total flavonoid intakes were nonlinearly positively associated with percent predicted FEV1 and FEV1/FVC ratio in ever smokers only (Figure 2). Intakes of all subclasses (except flavonols and flavanones), and the flavodiet score were positively associated with FEV1/FVC ratio in ever smokers (Supplemental Table 4).

Mediation analysis

Participants with moderate intakes of total flavonoids, flavonols, and flavanol monomers and high intakes of proanthocyanidins, anthocyanins, flavanones, and flavones and the flavodiet score had significantly lower INFLA score values than those with the lowest intakes (Supplemental Table 5). Conversely, participants with the highest intakes of theaflavins + thearubigins had significantly higher INFLA score values. The INLFA score mediated 14%, and 11% of the association between intakes of proanthocyanidins and flavones, respectively, and incident COPD (Supplemental Table 6). Mediated proportions were not statistically significant for any of the other exposures.

Sensitivity analyses

Associations were unchanged after the exclusion of all prevalent cases of both COPD and asthma [total flavonoids; HRQ3vsQ1 (95% CI) for COPD: 0.81 (0.72, 0.91); HRQ5vsQ1 (95% CI) for asthma: 1.00 (0.89, 1.12); Model 3] as well as after the exclusion of participants who had a COPD diagnosis within the first 2 y of follow-up [HRQ3vsQ1 (95% CI): 0.82 (0.74, 0.93); HRQ5vsQ1 (95% CI): 0.92 (0.81, 1.06); Model 3]. White wine intake was not associated with incident COPD (Supplemental Figure 4). Associations between total flavonoid intakes and spirometry measures were unchanged when analyses were restricted to participants who had ≤3 y between their assessment center visit and their final diet assessment (Supplemental Figure 5).

Discussion

Among UK Biobank cohort participants, moderate intakes of total flavonoids, flavonols, theaflavins + thearubigins, and flavanones, and moderate-to-high intakes of flavanol monomers, proanthocyanidins, anthocyanins, flavones, and the flavodiet score were associated with up to an 18% lower risk of incident COPD but not incident adult asthma. Furthermore, intakes of total flavonoids, all flavonoid subclasses, and the flavodiet score were positively associated with up to a 1.23% higher percent predicted FEV1 at baseline. For most exposures, evidence of associations with incident COPD and spirometry measures were present only in participants who currently or formerly smoked. Intakes of most of the flavonoid subclasses were inversely associated with the INFLA score, which appeared to mediate 11%–14% of the association between intakes of proanthocyanidins and flavones and incident COPD.

Our finding of a lower risk of COPD with moderate-to-high flavonoid intakes is in line with our previous study in the Danish Diet Cancer and Health cohort where, during 23 y follow-up (5557 incident cases of COPD) participants with the highest total flavonoid intakes had a 20% lower risk of incident COPD [HRQ5vsQ1 (95% CI): 0.80 (0.74, 0.87)] and a 6%–22% lower risk for each flavonoid subclass [11]. Interestingly, in the present study, several of the observed associations were U-shaped with the lowest risks seen for moderate intakes of tea and red wine. In fact, the spline suggests that the risk of COPD was significantly higher for those consuming >7 serves (i.e., 7 cups) of tea per day and participants with the highest intakes of theaflavins + thearubigins––flavonoid compounds coming exclusively from tea––appeared to have higher levels of chronic inflammation, as indicated by the INFLA score. Although some studies suggest that tea protects against COPD [36,37] and tea flavonoids have numerous reported health benefits [38], concerns exist around the content of heavy metals, pesticide residues, and mycotoxins in tea [38]. Another possibility is that the flavonoids in tea may have a hormetic effect, providing benefits at low doses but becoming potentially harmful at very high doses [39] or that associations are due to reverse causation. Our finding of a U-shaped association with red wine and incident COPD are in line with similar findings from the Cohort of Swedish Men [40] and may be due to confounding by alcohol; Sisson [41] suggested that the impact of alcohol consumption on lung function depends on concentration and duration of exposure, with short exposure to moderate concentrations of alcohol potentially attenuating the airway inflammation and injury and prolonged and heavy exposure worsening lung function in patients with COPD. The apparent benefit of moderate red wine intakes is likely due to red wine’s high flavonoid content as no association between low-flavonoid white wine and incident COPD was observed. In the present study, participants with the highest intakes of apples [i.e.,those consuming > 2 serves (equivalent to 2 apples) per day] and berries [i.e., those consuming > 1 serve (equivalent to 1 handful) per day] had the lowest risk of incident COPD, but significantly lower risks were already observed in participants consuming ½ serves per day of both apples and berries. This mirrors findings from the Cohort of Swedish Men, where a higher apple intake (>1 serving per day) was associated with a 30% lower COPD risk [42]. Additionally, a higher intake of peppers, but not berries, was associated with a lower COPD risk in the Cohort of Swedish Men, whereas our study demonstrated a “U-shaped” association for peppers. These associations may be influenced by diverse eating and cooking practices across populations and time periods. The absence of any associations with incident adult asthma is consistent with existing literature, which indicates that diet-related associations with adult asthma are, at best, moderate [43]. Although some observational studies report “protective” associations for fruits and vegetables [43], we found no evidence that this could be attributed to flavonoids.

The initial exploration of the relationship between dietary flavonoid intake and lung function in 2001, in the MORGEN study cohort, revealed an inverse association between catechins (from nontea sources), flavonols, and flavones––the only flavonoid subclasses that could be estimated from diet records at the time––and FEV1 [44]. On the whole food level, intakes of apples and pears but not tea, were inversely associated with FEV1. More recently, the consumption of anthocyanins and anthocyanin-rich blueberries was reported to be inversely associated with age-related decline in FEV1 and FVC, in both current/former and never smokers [17]. Additionally, 2 cross-sectional studies reported protective associations between various flavonoid subclasses and pulmonary function parameters [15,16]. Our study, however, found that higher intakes of all subclasses (except theaflavins + thearubigins) were associated with better FEV1. Our study unveiled a nonlinear trend in the associations with percent predicted FEV1, indicating a steeper incline initially aligning with the nonlinear association seen with incident COPD. Although in the present study only proanthocyanidins and anthocyanins were positively associated with FEV1/FVC ratio in the whole cohort––a similar finding to that in the GA2LEN cohort [15]––intakes of total flavonoids, most subclasses, and the flavodiet score, were positively associated with a higher FEV1/FVC ratio in ever smokers. These collective findings highlight the potential respiratory health advantages linked to increasing dietary flavonoid intake, even just to moderate levels, particularly in those with low intakes.

Considering evidence indicating that flavonoids may mitigate the systemic inflammation and oxidative stress induced by smoking [[11], [12], [13], [14]] that is known to contribute to COPD development [45], we examined potential mediation by inflammation, represented by the INFLA score. In the present study, the INFLA score mediated 11%–14% of the association between intakes of proanthocyanidins and flavones, and incident COPD. Furthermore, our findings indicate that individuals who do not smoke may also experience benefits from increased flavonoid intake as, even though flavonoid intakes were only positively associated with FEV1/FVC ratio in ever smokers, evidence of a lower risk of incident COPD, albeit not statistically significant, was also seen in nonsmokers. This observation aligns with findings from 3 previous cohorts [16,17,44], which all report no effect modification by smoking status. As current smokers are at a higher COPD risk compared with never smokers, regardless of flavonoid intake [11], prioritizing smoking cessation remains paramount for COPD risk reduction. Nevertheless, these collective findings suggest that flavonoids may contribute to further diminishing the risk associated with smoking and other prevalent risk factors.

The current study has several strengths, including its substantial 11-y follow-up of a large adult population, facilitating examination of associations in various subpopulations. As the largest study to date focusing on flavonoids and spirometry analyses, it contributes significantly to the existing body of knowledge. However, being observational in nature, the study cannot establish causation and we acknowledge the potential for residual or unmeasured confounding. To enhance representation of habitual dietary preferences, we used dietary data from participants with at least two 24-h dietary assessments, supported by the reproducibility of the flavodiet score over time [24]. Although the Oxford WebQ dietary questionnaire exhibits reasonable validity [46], concerns about over- or under-reporting and recall bias persist. Misclassification of flavonoid subclass intakes due to grouping specific food items (e.g., berries) may occur. The reliance on primary care diagnosis for COPD may underestimate events, although any resulting misclassifications would likely bias associations toward the null. The use of a dichotomous definition for asthma, a highly heterogeneous and underdiagnosed disease, could introduce misclassification and bias estimates, potentially reducing the power to detect associations [43]. Finally, the UK Biobank populations lack of representativeness of the broader United Kingdom adult population, with a majority being White individuals of European ancestry with high education levels, raises concerns about generalizability. However, the large sample size in the UK Biobank suggests robustness in detecting causal relationships despite this nonrepresentativeness [47].

Our findings indicate that both current/former smokers and never smokers may benefit from moderate-to-high flavonoid intakes to lower risk of COPD. We did not observe similar benefits for asthma in this cohort. This protective association for COPD may be, in part, mediated by the alleviation of inflammation, contributing to improved lung function as represented by higher FEV1, FVC, and FEV1/FVC ratios. Notably, moderate tea consumption (1–2 serves per day) and an achievable intake of apples and berries (>½ a serve per day), prominent contributors to flavonoid intake, emerge as potential strategies associated with reduced COPD risk.
