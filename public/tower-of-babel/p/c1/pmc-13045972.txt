Introduction

Leiomyomas are benign tumors originating from smooth muscle cells throughout the body, primarily uterus and small bowel. Intestinal leiomyomas tend to be small and are often asymptomatic, usually incidentally identified during endoscopy or at autopsy[1,2]. The primary differential diagnosis for gastrointestinal leiomyoma is gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST), solitary fibrous tumors (SFT)[3], schwannoma, lymphoma, duodenal adenocarcinoma, and leiomyosarcoma. Leiomyomas may be differentiated from other intestinal bowel tumors by a low mitotic rate, absence of necrosis on microscopy, and positive desmin and smooth-muscle actin staining, while staining negative for KIT and CD34[3]. The duodenum is the rarest location for gastrointestinal leiomyomas, accounting for <5% of small intestine leiomyomas[2]. While the majority of duodenal leiomyomas are asymptomatic, the most common symptom is anemia due to upper gastrointestinal bleeding[2]. This case is reported following SCARE guidelines[4].

Presentation of case

A previously healthy 67-year-old male presented to the emergency department (ED) via ambulance following a syncopal episode, complaining of chest pain and shortness of breath on mild exertion. The patient reported a 48-hour history of melena. Vitals on presentation to the ED included blood pressure of 90/60, tachycardia 102, respiration rate of 18, O2 saturation of 97% on room air, and afebrile at 36.4°C. Suspecting an upper gastrointestinal bleed, fluid resuscitation, and pantoprazole bolus followed by IV infusion were initiated. Laboratory assessment revealed anemia with a hemoglobin of 96 g/L, WBC 4.2 × 109/L, platelets 253 × 109/L, creatinine 93 μmol/L, and International Normalized Ratio (INR) 0.9. Follow-up liver function tests and bilirubin were normal. An urgent upper endoscopy confirmed active bleeding from a submucosal lesion located in the second portion of the duodenum (Fig. 1A). Active hemorrhage was controlled with the placement of two endoscopic 16 mm micro-hemovascular clips (Fig. 1B). Two units of blood were given during endoscopy. Post-endoscopy hemoglobin of 73 g/L triggered a CT angiogram, which confirmed hemorrhage control with no active extravasation identified on imaging. Past medical history

The patient related a family history of myocardial infarction but denied a personal history of underlying cardiac disease. The patient had no history of gastrointestinal bleeding, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory and/or acetylsalicylic acid use. The patient is stated healthy, reporting only a history of “indigestion” described as chronic epigastric pain and a sensation of abdominal fullness, worsening over the last 6 months.

Investigations

The emergency department CT scan identified a multilobulated mass in the second portion of the duodenum with compression/involvement of the pancreatic head (Fig. 2A and B). The post-endoscopy CT angiogram performed to assess for bleeding confirmed the 9.0 × 7.0 cm hyperattenuating mass in the second portion of the duodenum involving the pancreatic head/uncinate process, without active extravasation of contrast to suggest active bleed (Fig. 2A and B). Subsequent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) confirmed the presence of a lobulated submucosal mass centered in the second portion of the duodenum, measuring 11.9 × 8.7 cm compressing the pancreatic head (Fig. 3A and B). The contrast imaging demonstrated a mildly arterial enhancing mass with increasing enhancement at 5 minutes delayed phase imaging (Fig. 4A-C), with no invasion of the surrounding structures and no extension beyond the duodenal serosa. The mass exhibited mild diffusion restriction (Fig. 5A), no evidence of tumor necrosis, and marked MRI T2 hypointensity suggesting mesenchymal origins, and an Axial Apparent Diffusion Coefficient (ADC) 0.78 to 10 × 10−3mm2/s (Fig. 5B). No loco-regional lymphadenopathy and no evidence of metastatic disease were present on chest/abdominal/pelvic imaging. Based on the MRI appearance, the impression favored the diagnosis of a GIST, with the differential including lymphoma, duodenal adenocarcinoma, or uncommon duodenal tumors such as leiomyoma. Therapeutic intervention

The case was discussed at multidisciplinary pancreas tumor conference, with consensus recommendations for surgical resection due to the large size of the duodenal mass. Endoscopic ultrasound guided interventions were deferred due to perceived risk of initiating recurrent hemorrhage and the limited utility of tissue sampling in a large duodenal mass. Due to the risk of recurrent hemorrhage and the concerns for duodenal malignancy, the patient underwent a laparoscopic staging followed by pancreaticoduodenectomy (Whipple). At the time of the Whipple procedure, an 11 cm mass was identified in the periampullary region of the duodenum. Limited resection of duodenum was considered intraoperatively; however, due to tumor size and involvement of the pancreatic head, the Whipple procedure provided the only safe option for R0 resection. The patient was discharged home on postoperative day 7, with no 90-day postoperative complications reported.

Pathology

Gross examination of the specimen revealed a well-circumscribed, 11 cm firm white solid mass arising from the second part of the duodenum, bulging intraluminally with mucosal ulceration. The mass demonstrated “whorled” architecture with areas of hemorrhage and associated ischemic necrosis (Fig. 6). The adjacent soft tissue and pancreatic parenchyma were compressed but uninvolved by the tumor. All surgical margins were free of tumor involvement (R0 resection), with no regional lymph nodes that demonstrated tumor spread (0/16 nodes). Histopathological examination demonstrated a sharply circumscribed leiomyoma arising from the duodenal muscularis propria, composed of cytologically bland smooth muscle cells arranged in interlacing fascicles (Fig. 7A). The focal necrosis observed was confined to the central tumor with preserved tumor cell outlines and without associated inflammation or mitotic activity, which is consistent with ischemic degeneration. The tumor Ki67 proliferative index is less than 1%. Immunohistochemistry demonstrated strong positivity for smooth muscle antigen and desmin, confirming smooth muscle differentiation and supporting the diagnosis of benign leiomyoma of the duodenum (Fig. 7B). Tumor cells were negative for DOG1, CD117, and S100, excluding GIST and neurogenic tumors such as schwannoma. Features indicative of leiomyosarcoma, including cellular pleomorphism, increased mitotic activity, or tumor necrosis, were absent.

Discussion

Duodenal leiomyoma is a rare tumor, originating in the smooth muscle of the bowel wall, making up <0.1% of all gastrointestinal tumors[1,2], and due to the small size, the majority are discovered incidentally on endoscopy or at autopsy[1,2]. Pathology studies reveal that duodenal leiomyomas develop from the muscularis mucosae or the muscularis propria smooth muscle cell layers. Larger lesions develop from the muscularis propria (2–4 cm), compared to the muscularis mucosae (0.1–0.2 cm)[3].

The largest case series reported from the Mayo Clinic reviewed 12 surgically treated patients with duodenal leiomyoma from 1975 to 1995. Patients were predominantly female (2:1 ratio), presenting for surgery at a mean age of 57 (age range 20–73), with anemia in 10 of the 12 patients[2]. The largest tumor in the case series measures 3.6 cm (mean 2.3 cm), with all leiomyomas demonstrating an absence of tumor necrosis and cellular atypia[2]. No patient in the Mayo Clinic series developed recurrent leiomyoma or evidence of malignancy development after surgical intervention (4–17 years of follow up)[2].

Our case reports a 67-year-old male who presented with life-threatening upper gastrointestinal hemorrhage from an 11.9 × 8.7 cm duodenal leiomyoma originating from the muscularis propria, the largest lesion to date in the scientific literature.

Excellent contrast resolution and multiplanar capabilities of MRI make it the ideal choice for visualizing gastrointestinal leiomyoma. MRI can distinguish the tumor’s relationship with the layers of bowel, allowing identification of site of origin and revealing distinct characteristics of the tumor’s smooth muscle content[5]. Leiomyoma has typical imaging characteristics of marked T2 hypointensity, similar in signal to skeletal muscle, gradual enhancement on post contrast images, and an absence of lesional necrosis. Gastrointestinal leiomyoma imaging characteristics differ from the more commonly found GIST, which tend to demonstrate T2 hyperenhancement and necrosis as lesional size increases[6]. Small bowel lymphoma displays features such as regional lymphadenopathy, aneurysmal dilation, and circumferential mucosal thickening – features not found in leiomyomas[7].

On MRI, our patient’s duodenal mass is markedly T2 hypointense, demonstrating mild early enhancement with increasing intensity on delayed phase images, without invasion of the surrounding structures nor extension through the serosal. The mass demonstrated mild diffusion restriction (Fig. 5A), absence of tumor necrosis, and no locoregional adenopathy. The ADC value of the mass is heterogeneous, ranging between 0.78 and 10 × 10−3mm2/s, confounded by the presence of intra-tumoral hemorrhage. The T2W signal and enhancement characteristics support the diagnosis of a submucosal mesenchymal tumor most suggestive of leiomyoma. Differentiating leiomyoma from leiomyosarcoma on imaging alone can be challenging due to the variety of appearances of atypical leiomyoma, including cellular type and those with cystic or hemorrhagic transformation of leiomyoma[8]. The imaging characteristics support the diagnosis of a submucosal mesenchymal tumor consistent with leiomyoma.

Leiomyomas are benign smooth muscle neoplasms characterized histologically by spindle cells with minimal cytologic atypia and very rare mitotic figures. In larger lesions ischemic coagulative necrosis may be present, resulting from tumor growth outpacing vascular supply. Leiomyomas can be differentiated from malignant leiomyosarcoma, which histologically display marked pleomorphism, brisk mitotic activity, and true tumor necrosis. Immunohistochemically, leiomyomas consistently express desmin and smooth muscle actin, indicative of smooth muscle lineage. The immunohistochemical profile assists in excluding other spindle cell neoplasms, notably GIST, SFT, and schwannoma. Histopathological evaluation is essential for establishing a definitive diagnosis.

Our patient is a 67-year-old male diagnosed with an 11.9 cm duodenal leiomyoma due to life-threatening upper gastrointestinal hemorrhage, with pathological confirmation after curative Whipple resection. While recurrence is exceedingly rare after complete resection of benign duodenal leiomyoma, surveillance imaging should be considered for tumors >10 cm due to the rarity of giant duodenal leiomyomas.

Conclusion

Reported is a rare case of life-threatening hemorrhage from giant duodenal leiomyoma which adds to the limited knowledge on small bowel leiomyoma, and provides the first comprehensive radiological-histopathological correlation for a duodenal leiomyoma exceeding 11 cm.
