Dietary supplementation of spirulina powder (Arthrospira platensis) enhances growth, antioxidant status, immune function, and cardiac health in Japanese quails
Authors: Arezoo Isazaei, Farzad Bagherzadeh-Kasmani, Mahmoud Ghazaghi, Mehran Mehri, Javid Esfandiari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study was conducted to evaluate the effects of dietary supplementation with different levels of Arthrospira platensis (Spirulina powder) on growth performance, antioxidant status, immune function, and lipid metabolism in Japanese quails. A total of 600 seven-day-old quail chicks were randomly assigned to four dietary treatments with five replicates of 30 birds each. The treatments included a control group (basal diet with no additive) and three experimental groups supplemented with 1,000, 3,000, and 5,000 mg/kg of Spirulina powder, respectively. Growth performance, antioxidant status, lipid peroxidation, and immune responses were assessed between days 7 and 35. Results demonstrated that Spirulina supplementation linearly improved body weight gain and feed conversion ratio, enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, GPx, and catalase) and reduced malondialdehyde (MDA) levels in serum, liver, and muscle (P < 0.001). Relative weights of immune organs (bursa of Fabricius, thymus, and spleen) increased significantly, and serum immunoglobulins (IgY, IgM, IgA) were elevated in Spirulina-fed groups, showing predominantly linear and occasionally quadratic responses. Innate immunity markers including complement C3, interleukin-10, and lysozyme also increased. Furthermore, Spirulina supplementation decreased serum LDL and improved HDL concentrations and the LDL/HDL ratio. In conclusion, dietary inclusion of Spirulina powder exerted clear linear and, in some cases, quadratic dose-dependent effects on growth, antioxidant defense, immune responses, and lipid metabolism in Japanese quails, supporting its potential as a functional feed additive in poultry nutrition.Keywords: Spirulina, Quail, Growth, Immunoglobulin, Lipoprotein

Introduction

The global rise in population and the resulting threat to food security have intensified the need to enhance animal nutrition strategies in poultry production. As a major source of affordable and high-quality animal protein in the form of meat and eggs, the poultry industry plays a critical role in addressing malnutrition and meeting human nutritional needs (Abd El-Hack et al., 2022). Consequently, the identification and implementation of effective feed additives to improve growth performance and feed efficiency have become a key focus.

For decades, antibiotic growth promoters (AGPs) have been widely used to enhance feed efficiency, prevent diseases, and improve poultry performance. However, their indiscriminate use has led to serious public health concerns, including the development of antimicrobial resistance and the transfer of resistant genes to human microbiota. Residual antibiotics in poultry products and the environment may enter the human food chain, increasing health risks and contributing to antibiotic resistance. As a result, the European Union has banned the use of AGPs (Mehdi et al., 2018). In light of these concerns and the increasing consumer demand for antibiotic-free and organic poultry products, there is a growing interest in natural alternatives to AGPs that are safe, sustainable, and effective.

In recent years, researchers have explored a wide array of natural feed additives, including phytogenics, probiotics, prebiotics, synbiotics, organic acids, amino acids, and microbial metabolites, which have shown promising effects on growth, immune response, and overall poultry health (Acharya and Barsila, 2025; Nechitailo et al., 2024; Wickramasuriya et al., 2024). Among these alternatives, microalgae–particularly cyanobacteria such as Spirulina–have received considerable attention due to their exceptional nutritional profile and abundance of bioactive compounds (Vasquez-Moscoso et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025).

Spirulina (Arthrospira platensis) is a filamentous, photosynthetic cyanobacterium found in freshwater and marine environments of tropical and subtropical regions. It is considered a highly nutritious and sustainable biomass, rich in high-quality protein, essential amino acids, polyunsaturated fatty acids such as linoleic and linolenic acids, vitamins, minerals, antioxidants, glycolipids, polysaccharides, and dietary fiber (Lestingi et al., 2024; Sahil et al., 2024). The major bioactive compounds in Spirulina powder–including C-phycocyanin (13.8 g/100 g), total carotenoids (625 mg/100 g), β-carotene (249 mg/100 g), γ-linolenic acid (1.07 g/100 g), selenium (31 μg/100 g), iron (218 mg/100 g), iodine (500 μg/100 g), magnesium (500 mg/100 g), and niacin (3.33 mg/100 g)–are responsible for a broad spectrum of health-promoting effects such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, hepatoprotective, and neuroprotective activities (Chwil et al., 2024; Dimopoulou et al., 2025; Sabat et al., 2025). Previous poultry studies have shown that effective responses can occur at relatively low dietary inclusion levels (1000-2000 mg/kg) for antioxidant protection and immunomodulation (Abdel-Wahab et al., 2023), whereas higher concentrations (3000-5000 mg/kg) are often required to optimize lipid metabolism and immune organ development (Sarker et al., 2022).

In poultry nutrition, Spirulina has been reported to improve growth performance, feed efficiency, and meat quality in broiler chickens (El-Shall et al., 2023; Abdelfatah et al., 2024), enhance antioxidant status and immune responses in quails (Abdel-Wahab et al., 2023), and support disease resistance and lipid metabolism in layers and broilers (Sarker et al., 2022; Omar et al., 2022). These findings highlight Spirulina’s potential as a functional feed additive in poultry. Its antioxidant capacity is largely attributed to pigments such as chlorophyll, β-carotene, phycocyanin, α-tocopherol, and γ-linolenic acid, which act either by directly scavenging free radicals or by enhancing the activity of endogenous antioxidant enzymes like superoxide dismutase, glutathione peroxidase, and catalase. Moreover, Spirulina positively influences gut microbiota, as it has been shown to promote the proliferation of beneficial bacteria such as Lactobacillus spp. in broiler chickens and quails, thereby improving intestinal health and nutrient utilization. It also modulates lipid and carbohydrate metabolism and may mitigate the adverse effects of toxic agents and drug-induced nephrotoxicity (Abdelfatah et al., 2024; Alghamdi et al., 2024; Kasmani et al., 2023; Omar, et al., 2022).

Importantly, Spirulina is an environmentally friendly resource. It has a relatively low ecological footprint, requires less land and water than traditional crops, and contributes to carbon capture through photosynthesis. Furthermore, its cultivation does not compete with food crops and can be integrated into waste-to-nutrient bioconversion systems, making it an attractive option for sustainable agriculture and circular bioeconomy models (Fernández-Ríos et al., 2023; Quintero et al., 2021).

Given its rich nutritional content, biological activities, and environmental benefits, Spirulina is a promising candidate for replacing synthetic additives and supporting antibiotic-free poultry production systems. Therefore, the present study evaluated the effects of Spirulina powder supplementation at 1000, 3000, and 5000 mg/kg on growth performance, antioxidant status, lipid peroxidation, immune responses, and key atherogenic and anti-atherogenic factors in Japanese quails.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

All animal care and experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with ethical guidelines and approved by the Research Animal Ethics Committee of the University of Zabol, Iran (Approval ID: AECUZ-2012-UP-2020-BR). The study adhered to the 3Rs principles (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement) to ensure animal welfare.

Dry matter was determined by oven-drying at 135°C for 2 h (AOAC, 2006; Method 930.15). Gross energy content was measured using an adiabatic bomb calorimeter (Model 6300, Parr Instrument Co., Moline, IL, USA). Crude protein, crude fiber, crude fat, and ash were measured according to AOAC methods 990.03, 978.10, 920.39, and 942.05, respectively (AOAC, 2006). The concentrations of selenium, iron, iodine, and magnesium were determined using atomic absorption spectroscopy. Niacin (vitamin B3) was measured using microbiological assay techniques.

Experimental design and animal management

A total of 600 seven-day-old Japanese quail chicks (Coturnix japonica) were randomly allocated to four dietary treatments in a completely randomized design (CRD). Each treatment consisted of five replicates with 30 birds per replicate. The dietary treatments were as follows:

Spirulina powder was added to the basal diet by replacing an equivalent amount of corn starch (Table 2). The basal diet was formulated to meet or exceed the nutritional requirements for growing quails (NRC, 1994). After incorporation of Spirulina powder into the diets, the diets were prepared in mash form without pelleting or heat processing. Diets were freshly prepared on a weekly basis and stored under controlled environmental conditions to minimize potential degradation. Therefore, the bioactive compounds analyzed in Spirulina powder (Table 1) were expected to remain stable and available in the final diets.

All groups were managed under uniform environmental and husbandry conditions. Birds were housed in floor pens with ad libitum access to feed and water. Environmental conditions were maintained at 32 ± 1.5°C and 60 ± 10 % relative humidity during the first week and gradually reduced to 20°C by the end of the experiment. Lighting was provided continuously at 50 lux in a 16 h light: 8 h dark cycle (Gharaoghlan et al., 2022; Shahraki et al., 2025). Feed was weighed and offered weekly, and feed residues were collected to calculate feed intake.

Performance parameters

To minimize handling stress, quail chicks were weighed at the beginning (day 7) and end (day 35) of the experimental period after a 3-hour feed withdrawal to ensure partial gastrointestinal clearance. Body weight gain (BWG) was calculated by subtracting the initial body weight from the final body weight. Feed was weighed and offered daily, and feed residues were collected and weighed. Feed intake per pen was calculated as the difference between the total feed offered and the feed remaining. Feed conversion ratio (FCR) was determined for each replicate as the total feed intake divided by total body weight gain over the experimental period.

Sample collection

At 35 days of age, one bird per replicate (n = 5 per group), representing the average body weight of each pen, was selected for blood and tissue sampling. Blood samples were collected from the wing vein without anticoagulant, allowed to clot, and centrifuged at ∼1,000 × g for 15 min to obtain serum. The bursa of Fabricius, thymus, and spleen were excised and individually weighed. Their relative weights were calculated as a percentage of the bird’s live body weight (g organ/100 g body weight). Liver and thigh muscle tissues were collected and used for malondialdehyde (MDA) analysis.

Antioxidant and lipid peroxidation assays

Serum concentrations of superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), catalase (CAT), and MDA were determined using colorimetric assay kits (Pars Azmun, Tehran, Iran) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols. Absorbance was read using a spectrophotometer (UNIKON 933; Kontron Co. Ltd., Milan, Italy). MDA content in liver and thigh muscle was also quantified as an index of lipid peroxidation and expressed in mEq/kg tissue (Botsoglou et al., 1994).

Immunoglobulin and innate immunity assays

Serum concentrations of IgY, IgM, and IgA were quantified using poultry-specific ELISA kits (FAR, Fermi, Verona, Italy). Assays were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions, including standard curve generation using provided calibrators. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, Thermo Scientific), and concentrations were determined based on standard curves. Innate immunity markers including complement component C3 (C3), interleukin-10 (IL-10), and lysozyme activity were measured using commercial kits (Pars Azmun, Iran).

Lipid profile analysis

Serum levels of low-density lipoprotein (LDL) and high-density lipoprotein (HDL) were measured using enzymatic kits (Pars Azmun, Iran), with absorbance readings taken using a UV-visible spectrophotometer. These parameters were used to evaluate the effect of dietary Spirulina on lipid metabolism.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the General Linear Model (GLM) procedure in SAS software (Version 9.1; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Normality of the residuals was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was checked using Levene’s test. Treatment effects were evaluated using orthogonal polynomial contrasts to determine linear and quadratic responses to increasing levels of Spirulina powder. In addition, a contrast comparison was performed between the control group (0 mg/kg) and Spirulina-supplemented groups (1000–5000 mg/kg). Results are presented as means with their pooled standard errors (SEM). Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Growth performance

Dietary Spirulina powder supplementation significantly influenced growth performance traits of Japanese quails (Table 3). Body weight gain increased with Spirulina inclusion, showing a significant linear response (P < 0.001) and a tendency for a quadratic response (P = 0.056). Feed intake decreased progressively with increasing Spirulina levels, which was confirmed by a strong linear reduction (P < 0.001), while the quadratic effect was not significant (P = 0.091). Feed conversion ratio (FCR) improved with Spirulina supplementation, also displaying a clear linear decrease (P < 0.001) and a tendency toward a quadratic effect (P = 0.072). The overall treatment effect (Model) was significant for all performance traits (P = 0.009 for body weight gain; P < 0.001 for feed intake and FCR). Furthermore, contrast analysis indicated that Spirulina-fed groups differed significantly from the control group (P < 0.05 for all three parameters).

Antioxidant status

Dietary Spirulina powder supplementation significantly influenced the antioxidant status of Japanese quails (Table 4). Serum SOD, GPX, and CAT activities all increased with Spirulina inclusion, showing strong linear effects (P < 0.001 for all). In addition, quadratic responses were observed for SOD (P = 0.038) and GPX (P = 0.044), while CAT tended to show a quadratic effect (P = 0.051). The overall treatment effect (Model) was highly significant for all three enzymes (P < 0.001). Contrast analysis further confirmed that Spirulina-fed groups had greater antioxidant enzyme activities compared with the control (P < 0.001 for all parameters).

Dietary Spirulina powder supplementation markedly reduced malondialdehyde (MDA) concentrations in serum, meat, and liver of Japanese quails (Table 4). A strong linear decrease was observed for all three tissues (P < 0.001). In addition, quadratic effects were detected for serum (P = 0.041) and liver MDA (P = 0.048), and tended to be significant for meat MDA (P = 0.052). The overall treatment effect (Model) was highly significant in all cases (P < 0.001). Furthermore, contrast analysis indicated that Spirulina-fed groups had consistently lower MDA concentrations compared with the control group (P < 0.001 for all parameters).

Immune organ development

Dietary Spirulina powder supplementation significantly affected the relative weights of immune organs in Japanese quails (Table 5). A clear linear increase was observed for spleen, bursa of Fabricius, and thymus weights (P < 0.001 for all). In addition, quadratic effects were detected for spleen (P = 0.047) and bursa (P = 0.038), while thymus weight tended to show a quadratic response (P = 0.051). The overall treatment effect (Model) was highly significant for all three organs (P < 0.001). Furthermore, contrast analysis indicated that Spirulina-fed groups had significantly greater immune organ weights compared with the control group (P < 0.001 for all parameters).

Humoral immune response

Dietary Spirulina powder supplementation significantly affected serum immunoglobulins (Table 5). There was a clear linear increase with dose for all three isotypes (IgY: P < 0.001; IgM: P < 0.001; IgA: P = 0.009). A quadratic response was significant for IgY (P = 0.042), tended to be significant for IgM (P = 0.066), and was not significant for IgA (P = 0.228). The overall treatment effect (Model) was significant for IgY (P = 0.001), IgM (P = 0.001), and IgA (P = 0.012). In addition, the control vs. Spirulina contrast confirmed higher immunoglobulin levels with Spirulina inclusion (IgY and IgM: P < 0.001; IgA: P = 0.018).

Innate immunity

Dietary Spirulina powder supplementation significantly affected innate immunity parameters in Japanese quails (Table 5). Serum C3, IL-10, and lysozyme activity all increased with Spirulina inclusion. Strong linear responses were detected for all three traits (P < 0.001 for C3 and IL-10; P = 0.001 for lysozyme). In addition, quadratic effects were significant for C3 (P = 0.041) and IL-10 (P = 0.038), while lysozyme showed a tendency toward a quadratic response (P = 0.055). The overall treatment effect (Model) was highly significant for C3 and IL-10 (P < 0.001) and significant for lysozyme (P = 0.001). Furthermore, contrast analysis confirmed that Spirulina-fed groups had higher levels of all three innate immunity indicators compared with the control group (P < 0.01 for all parameters).

Lipid profile

Dietary Spirulina powder supplementation significantly influenced serum lipoproteins of Japanese quails (Table 6). Serum LDL concentrations decreased progressively with increasing Spirulina levels, showing a strong linear reduction (P < 0.001) and a significant quadratic component (P = 0.039). Conversely, HDL concentrations increased in a dose-dependent manner, with both linear (P < 0.001) and quadratic (P = 0.046) responses. The LDL/HDL ratio was also markedly reduced in Spirulina-supplemented groups, with significant linear (P < 0.001) and marginal quadratic (P = 0.052) effects. The overall treatment effect (Model) was highly significant for all three parameters (P < 0.001). Contrast analysis confirmed that Spirulina-fed groups had lower LDL levels, higher HDL levels, and a more favorable LDL/HDL ratio compared with the control group (P < 0.001 for all).

Discussion

This study provides comprehensive evidence that dietary supplementation with Spirulina significantly modulates physiological, antioxidant, and immunological functions in Japanese quails. The observed improvements in performance and health markers can be mechanistically linked to the unique biochemical composition of Spirulina, including essential amino acids, pigments, trace minerals, fatty acids, and vitamins (Abdelfatah et al., 2024; El-Shall et al., 2023).

In the present study, Spirulina supplementation improved body weight gain and feed conversion ratio in a linear manner. These findings are in line with earlier studies in broiler chickens, where Spirulina supplementation improved growth performance and feed efficiency (El-Shall et al., 2023; Abdelfatah et al., 2024; Salah et al., 2025b). Similar results were reported in quails (Abdel-Wahab et al., 2023; Fawaz et al., 2025), supporting the growth-promoting potential of Spirulina across different poultry species.

The improvement in body weight gain and feed conversion ratio in Spirulina-fed groups may be attributed to several overlapping mechanisms. The high crude protein content (62 %) and balanced amino acid profile, especially lysine, methionine, and threonine, directly support muscle protein synthesis and cellular proliferation in intestinal tissues, leading to improved nutrient absorption and growth (El-Bahr et al., 2020). Moreover, Spirulina is rich in bioactive compounds such as C-phycocyanin, which has been reported to enhance digestive enzyme activity and modulate gut microbiota by increasing beneficial bacterial populations (e.g., Lactobacillus spp.), resulting in better gut health and nutrient digestibility (Abdel-Wahab et al., 2023; Alghamdi et al., 2024).

Quails fed Spirulina showed increased activities of SOD, GPx, and CAT, along with reduced MDA levels in serum, liver, and muscle. These results are consistent with previous studies in quails (Abdel-Wahab et al., 2023) and broilers (Omar et al., 2022; Salah et al., 2025a), which demonstrated Spirulina’s ability to reduce oxidative stress and enhance antioxidant enzyme activity.

The antioxidant enzymes SOD, GPx, and CAT play crucial roles in the detoxification of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Ighodaro and Akinloye, 2018; Mates, 2000). Spirulina-fed quails exhibited enhanced activity of these enzymes, indicating upregulation of endogenous antioxidant defense mechanisms. These improvements in antioxidant defense may be attributed to the combined action of several key bioactive compounds in Spirulina. For instance, selenium, a vital cofactor for the GPx enzyme, plays a central role in the enzymatic reduction of hydrogen peroxide and lipid hydroperoxides into harmless molecules, thus protecting cellular components from oxidative damage (Santi et al., 2013). In parallel, β-carotene and other carotenoids act as potent non-enzymatic antioxidants by neutralizing singlet oxygen and peroxyl radicals through electron transfer mechanisms, effectively terminating lipid peroxidation chain reactions (Edge and Truscott, 2018). Additionally, C-phycocyanin, a unique blue pigment-protein complex found abundantly in Spirulina, exerts strong antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties by inhibiting NADPH oxidase activity and downregulating the expression of pro-oxidant enzymes such as inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), thereby limiting intracellular ROS production (Bannu et al., 2019; Shih et al., 2009).

The reduced MDA levels observed in serum, liver, and muscle reflect lower lipid peroxidation, which may preserve the integrity of cellular membranes and prevent oxidative damage to lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids (Yekti et al., 2018).

The relative weights of lymphoid organs (bursa, thymus, spleen) and serum immunoglobulins (IgY, IgM, IgA) increased in Spirulina-fed birds, together with higher C3, IL-10, and lysozyme levels. These results indicate enhanced humoral and innate immunity, which is in agreement with findings in broilers (El-Shall et al., 2023; Abdelfatah et al., 2024; Omar et al., 2022). Qureshi et al. (1996) also reported that Spirulina supplementation enhanced both humoral and cell-mediated immune functions in chickens.

The increased relative weights of the bursa of Fabricius, thymus, and spleen suggest enhanced lymphoid tissue development. This may be a downstream effect of γ-linolenic acid (GLA) and iron present in Spirulina. GLA is a precursor of dihomo-γ-linolenic acid (DGLA), which is converted to prostaglandin E1 (PGE1), a known immunomodulator that supports T-cell differentiation and thymic growth. Iron is essential for DNA synthesis and mitochondrial respiration in proliferating lymphocytes. Additionally, Spirulina's arginine content can serve as a substrate for nitric oxide (NO) synthesis, a key molecule in immune signaling and pathogen defense (Kwak et al., 1999; Lestingi et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2016).

The elevation in serum immunoglobulins (IgY, IgM, IgA) in Spirulina-fed birds indicates activation of both T-helper 2 (Th2)-mediated B cell maturation and class-switch recombination. Spirulina polysaccharides have been shown to act as pattern recognition receptor (PRR) ligands, particularly binding to TLR2 and TLR4, leading to the upregulation of IL-4 and IL-6 expression, which are critical for B-cell differentiation and antibody production (Srivastava et al., 2024).

The increase in innate immune markers such as C3, IL-10, and lysozyme highlights Spirulina’s role in priming innate immune responses. Complement C3 activation enhances opsonization and phagocytosis, while lysozyme contributes to lysis of gram-positive bacterial walls via muramidase activity (Aragão et al., 2022; Bjanes and Nizet, 2021; Khalil et al., 2017). IL-10, an anti-inflammatory cytokine, plays a regulatory role by inhibiting excessive inflammatory responses and maintaining immune homeostasis. Its elevation may reflect the immunoregulatory action of phycocyanin, which inhibits NF-κB translocation and reduces pro-inflammatory cytokine expression (e.g., IL-1β, TNF-α), shifting the immune response toward a balanced anti-inflammatory state (Kubo and Motomura, 2012; Matin et al., 2024; Tabarzad et al., 2020). Trace elements like magnesium and iodine in Spirulina further support cytokine receptor signaling and oxidative burst functions in macrophages and neutrophils (Aceves et al., 2021; Alghamdi et al., 2022; Ashique et al., 2023).

In the current experiment, Spirulina supplementation reduced serum LDL, increased HDL, and improved the LDL/HDL ratio. These effects are consistent with previous reports in layers, where Spirulina improved lipid profile and reduced serum cholesterol (Sarker et al., 2022), and in rabbits, where Spirulina prevented hypercholesterolemia (Cheong et al., 2010).

The observed improvements in lipid metabolism–specifically the reduction in LDL levels, elevation of HDL concentrations, and a lower LDL/HDL ratio–can be attributed to the presence of several lipid-modulating bioactive compounds in Spirulina. Notably, niacin (vitamin B3), present at appreciable levels (3.33 mg/100 g), is known to exert hypolipidemic effects by inhibiting hepatic diacylglycerol acyltransferase-2 (DGAT-2), a key enzyme in triglyceride synthesis (Julius, 2015). This inhibition leads to reduced very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) secretion and, consequently, a decrease in circulating LDL cholesterol (Serban et al., 2016). In addition, GLA contributes to the modulation of hepatic lipid metabolism through the activation of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPAR-α). This nuclear receptor promotes the transcription of genes involved in fatty acid β-oxidation while downregulating lipogenic pathways, thereby shifting hepatic metabolism toward lipid catabolism (Choopani et al., 2016; Samuels et al., 2002; Takala et al., 2023). Furthermore, C-phycocyanin, beyond its antioxidant role, may support reverse cholesterol transport by enhancing the activity of lecithin–cholesterol acyltransferase (LCAT). This enzyme is responsible for the esterification of free cholesterol in HDL particles, which is essential for the maturation of HDL and efficient removal of cholesterol from peripheral tissues (Ama Moor et al., 2017).

The present study demonstrated clear linear responses of antioxidant enzymes (SOD, GPx, and CAT) and reductions in MDA levels in serum, liver, and muscle. Quadratic components were detected for some traits, indicating modest curvilinear effects at higher Spirulina inclusion levels. Similarly, humoral immune parameters (IgY, IgM, IgA) and innate immune indicators (C3, IL-10, and lysozyme) showed predominantly linear improvements, with quadratic effects evident for selected traits. Lipid metabolism also responded linearly, as LDL decreased and HDL increased, leading to a lower LDL/HDL ratio.

Although the present study did not evaluate economic parameters, it should be noted that Spirulina powder is increasingly cost-competitive with other feed additives due to improvements in large-scale production. Future research under commercial settings is recommended to include cost–benefit analysis to further clarify its economic viability in poultry feeding.

Conclusion

Dietary inclusion of Spirulina powder significantly enhanced growth performance, antioxidant defense, humoral and innate immune responses, and lipid metabolism in Japanese quails. These effects are attributed to the rich nutritional composition of Spirulina, including high-quality protein, essential amino acids, antioxidants (e.g., carotenoids, phycocyanin, selenium), and functional lipids. The effects of Spirulina powder were dose-dependent, with linear responses predominant across most traits, while quadratic effects were evident for selected parameters. Overall, Spirulina powder can be effectively supplemented within the tested range (1000-5000 mg/kg) to achieve improvements in performance, antioxidant capacity, immunity, and lipid profile in Japanese quails.
