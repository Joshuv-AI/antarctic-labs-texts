A liver abscess caused by Aeromonas dhakensis after the consumption of freshwater fish: A case report and literature review
Authors: Huiling Zhang, Sheng Ye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAeromonas dhakensis, is increasingly recognized as a pathogen capable of causing a variety of infections, particularly in individuals with underlying malignancies. However, detailed pediatric data concerning this organism remains scarce. We describe a 16-year-old male patient with aplastic anemia and leukemia who developed persistent fever and abdominal pain following the consumption of partially cooked freshwater fish. Initial antibiotic therapy proved ineffective, and imaging studies revealed a liver abscess. Although initial pus culture misidentified the pathogen as Aeromonas hydrophila, mNGS subsequently confirmed the presence of A. dhakensis in blood and pus samples. After adjusting the antibiotic regimen and performing abscess drainage, the patient’s condition improved significantly, leading to his eventual discharge. A. dhakensis, while an uncommon cause of bacteremia, is often misidentified as A. hydrophila. This case highlights the importance of accurate species identification and underscores the need for further research to fully understand the distribution and clinical implications of A. dhakensis infections.Keywords: Aeromonas dhakensis, Bacteremia, Liver abscess

Background

The bacterium Aeromonas dhakensis (A. dhakensis) was first reported by Huys G et al. [1] in 2002 as a strain isolated from children with diarrhea in Dhaka, Bangladesh, and was initially named Aeromonas hydrophila subsp. dhakensis. In 2008, Martinez-Murcia et al. [2] isolated a new species from the skin of an ornamental fish in Portugal, Aeromonas aquariums. Beaz-Hidalgo et al. [3] later confirmed that these two strains belonged to the same species using rpoD and gyrB gene sequencing and reclassified them as A. dhakensis. The biochemical similarities between A. dhakensis and A. hydrophila make distinguishing between them a challenging task. While A. dhakensis has been detected in diverse environments, including river water, fishponds, marine shrimp, dolphins, etc., reports detailing clinical infections and associated fatalities caused by this organism remain relatively limited. This report details a case of A. dhakensis bloodstream infection leading to a liver abscess in a patient with hematological disease, thereby emphasizing the necessity for further investigation into this emerging pathogen.

Case report

A 16-year-old male patient who was admitted to the Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, complaining of fever. One week before admission, the patient got a fever one day after consumption of snakehead (takeout food, boiled fish slices), without vomiting, diarrhea, or cough or dyspnea. Notably, the patient had not engaged in activities like hot springs bathing or swimming in the weeks preceding his illness. His condition did not improve significantly after treatment with intravenous ertapenem (1.0 g per day, for 7 days) piperacillin-sulbactam (2.5 g twice daily, for 2 days), methylprednisolone (0.04 g per day, for 5 days), with oral linezolid (0.6 g every 12 h, for 2 days). One day before admission, the patient’s symptoms became exacerbated and got abdominal pain. He had a known history of aplastic anemia and Bechcet’s disease. He refused bone marrow transplantation, only received red blood cell and platelet transfusion on demand. The patient’s parents refused to have any prior antibacterial or antifungal prophylaxis as per standard of care. His vital signs were as follows: respiratory rate, 24 breaths/minute; pulse rate, 72 beats/minute and regular; blood pressure, 120/61 mmHg; and temperature, 36.9°C. His heart sound and breath sound were normal. He appeared pale with cyanotic lips and showed extensive ecchymoses and petechiae on the extremities. Abdominal examination revealed tenderness in the hepatic region without rebound tenderness.

Laboratory findings on admission were as follows: white blood cell (WBC) count of 750/mm3 (normal value 4000–12000/mm3); neutrophil granulocyte 210/mm3 (normal value 1500–7800/mm3); hemoglobin 72 g/L; thrombocyte 14000/mm3 (normal value 100000–400000/mm3); alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 436 IU/L (normal value 7–43 IU/L); aspartate transferase (AST) 127 IU/L (normal value 12–37 IU/L); C-reactive protein (CRP) 23.8 mg/dL

(normal value 0–0.8 mg/dL); Procalcitonin (PCT) 0.52 ng/ml (normal value 0–0.46 ng/ml). HIV antibody and rheumatological work-up were negative. Hepatitis B virus, hepatitis C virus infection and live cirrhosis were not detected. Arterial blood gas analysis revealed pH 7.43, PaCO2 46.3 mmHg, PaO2 37.9 mmHg, HCO3 30.2 mmol/L, Lac 1.6 mmol/L, SaO2 78.1 % with the patient breathing room air. Abdominal computed tomography (CT) scanning performed on the same day indicated a possible liver abscess (Fig. 1A) and pneumonia.

Abdominal Computed Tomography (CT) Scan of the Patient. 1 A: Abdominal CT performed on August 1st revealed that a patchy low-density shadow under the capsule of segment V of the right liver. The shadow had blurred edges and measured approximately 22 mm*15 mm in size. The central density of the lesion was lower than that of the edge, and the CT value was approximately 21 HU. 1B: The abdominal contrast-enhanced CT on August 8th showed a rounded hypoattenuating lesion surrounded by a patchy edema band in the right lobe of the liver, measuring approximately 40.1 mm*26.8 mm*39.5 mm, with rim enhancement, consistent with a liver abscess.

Empirical antimicrobial treatment with vancomycin (0.5 g every 6 h), meropenem (0.98 g every 8 h) and methylprednisolone sodium succinate (0.4 g per day) for Bechcet’s disease and adjunct for infection, were intravenously administered after admission. Blood culture and metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) were obtained at the same time. Despite treatment, the patient continued to have a high fever, without any cough or dyspnea. Three days after admission, mNGS of a blood sample suggested A. dhakensis, with 107 reads identified as Aeromonas genome sequences, of which 42 (39.3 %) reads belonged to A. dhakensis. Blood and sputum cultures were normal. Blood fungal G test and GM test were negative. An enhanced CT scan revealed an enlarged liver abscess (Fig. 1B) and the chest X-ray showed slight improvement in the left lower pneumonia compared to previous images. Due to poor response followed by persistent high fever and CRP levels fluctuating between 8.7 and 19.7 mg/dL, vancomycin was replaced with linezolid (0.6 g every 12 h). Meropenem was continued for pneumonia, and tigecycline was initiated. Initially, the patient's parents opposed percutaneous drainage, and they only agreed to sign for platelet transfusion if the platelet count was below 10,000/mm3. Not until twelve days later, we finally reached an agreement to proceed with the procedure after platelet transfusion. Two days post-drainage, the patient’s temperature normalized, and the CRP level decreased to 4.8 mg/dL. mNGS of the pus also suggested A. dhakensis, with a total of 171164 DNA and RNA reads mapped to the A. dhakensis genome reported. Additionally, mNGS detected the AQU-2 gene. However, we did not perform gene sequencing to verify the strain’s identification as A. dhakensis or carry out genotypic testing for carbapenems’ genes. Concurrently, the isolate from the pus culture was classified as A.hydrophila using the VITEK 2 system for identification. The patient was discharged on oral levofloxacin (0.25 g every 12 h) and follow-up examination revealed a CRP level < 0.8 mg/dL and a decreased liver abscess size (6*5*5 mm), with the patient reporting no discomfort. The comprehensive antibiotic treatment course was completed over a span of 34 days. Informed consent was obtained for this case report. The test for A. hydrophila susceptibility to various antimicrobial agents was performed by the microbroth dilution method according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) 2016 guidelines M45, which revealed that A. hydrophila was resistant to cefoxitin, piperacillin tazobactam, imipenem, cefuroxime, ceftriaxone, cefoperazone /sulbactam, ceftazidime, and sensitive to amikacin, levofloxacin, tigecycline.

Discussion and conclusion

A comprehensive literature review was conducted, involving a search of PubMed for case reports on A. dhakensis, revealed a paucity of data, with only 5 case reports [4], [5], [6], [7] (Table 1, Table 2). In congruence with the current case, three of these reports detailed instances where parients suffered from fever, skin petechiae, and hemorrhage following the consumption of undercooked fish. Of the five cases, four were fetal, with a brief and aggressive disease course. This case report represents a rare instance of a liver abscess caused by A. dhakensis in a patient with hematological disease. A. dhakensis is increasingly acknowledged as common human pathogen prevalent in tropical and subtropical climates, though it can also be found in temperate climates. Seasonal trends suggest that infections tend to peak during warmer months [8], which corresponds to the onset of illness in our patient. Human exposure to A. dhakensis usually results from the consumption of contaminated water or food, especially seafood, or from open wounds that have been exposed to polluted water [9], [10]. In this instance, the patient’s consumption of boiled fish slices prompts further inquiry into the pathogen’s viability and infectivity in seafood.

A.dhakensis has been recognized as an emergent human pathogen associated with gastroenteritis, skin and soft tissue infections, bacteremia, and necrotizing fasciitis [11], [12], [13].The bacteremia caused by A. dhakensis, although rare, particularly in pediatric populations, is associated with significant morbidity and mortality, especially in individuals with underlying conditions such as cirrhosis, diabetes mellitus, and malignancies [14]. In our patient, the rapid progression of the liver abscess and subsequent bacteremia underscored the aggressive nature of A. dhakensis infection.

The distinction between A. dhakensis and A. hydrophila is crucial due to the former's suspected increased virulence and cytotoxicity [11], [15]. This differentiation is essential for guiding antimicrobial therapy, as highlighted by the misidentified of A. dhakensis and A. hydrophila in our patient’s blood culture by the VITEK-2 system, which was corrected by the mNGS assay. Given that A. dhakensis and A. hydrophila exhibit comparable biochemical phenotypes, traditonal methods such as bacterial culture is often insufficient for accurate species differentiation. mNGS technology has been widely applied in various aspects of clinical pathogen identification, surveillance and prevention of emerging infectious diseases and hospital infection outbreaks, detection of pathogen resistance gene mutations, and vaccine development. NGS contributes significantly to the precise diagnosis and treatment of infectious disease [16].

Genotypic methods remain the gold standard for definitive species identification. While A. dhakensis infection can affect individuals of any age, it appears to pose a greater risk of severe disease in children, the elderly, and immunocompromised individuals. Due to the unknown etiology and severity of the infection, a broad-spectrum antibiotic meropenem and vancomycin were initially administered to our patient. Despite immediate and intensive therapy with antibiotics, the patient’s fever persistent. The subsequent pus culture antibiotic susceptibility test showed a resistance to meropenem. A. dhakensis isolates frequently demonstrate resistance to a broad spectrum of antibiotics, including amoxicillin, ceftriaxone, and imipenem [12], [17]. The presence of multiple β-lactamase genes, such as TEM, SHV, MOX, CepH, CphA, and aac (6′)-Ib-cr, contributes to resistance against β-lactam antibiotics [18]. Studies by Murata et al. [19] have documented high rates of meropenem resistance in A. dhakensis isolates. Our findings identified the AQU-2 gene, which is linked to cephalosporin resistance. Effective treatment options include cefepime, piperacillin/tazobactam, minocycline, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, sulfonamides, and tetracyclines[8], [11], [20]. However, prudent antibiotic use is essential to prevent the development of resistance. This study emphasizes the importance of accurate diagnosis in guiding appropriate antimicrobial therapy. While antibiotics remain a cornerstone of treatment, our findings suggest that alternative approaches, such as abscess drainage, can yield promising outcomes.

This study has limitations. The single patient case design may not be representative of all A. dhakensis infections. Additionally, the study did not explore all potential risk factors or treatment methods, which could influence the assessment of the disease’s impact. Despite these limitations, our findings underscore the need to reevaluate the significance of A. dhakensis as a serious pathogen. Further research with larger prospective cohorts is necessary to validate these preliminary results. Further studies should also characterize the virulence mechanisms and optimal treatment approaches for A. dhakensis infections.
