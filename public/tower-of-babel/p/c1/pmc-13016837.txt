Enhanced clinical trial stratification in spinal cord injury: the value of electrophysiology
Authors: Paulina S Scheuren, Martin Schubert, Michèle Hubli, Catherine R Jutzeler, Rüdiger Rupp, Rainer Abel, Doris Maier, Klaus Röhl, Michael Baumberger, Margret Hund-Georgiadis, Marion Saur, Jesús Benito-Penalva, Kerstin Rehahn, Mirko Aach, Andreas Badke, Jiri Kriz, Patrick Freund, Norbert Weidner, Martin E Schwab, John L K Kramer, Armin Curt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThere are no approved interventional therapies, aside from neurorehabilitation, that enhance neurological recovery after acute traumatic spinal cord injury. A key challenge is the lack of biomarkers surpassing clinical standards for optimal stratification. We evaluated electrophysiological markers of preserved neuronal function to improve enrichment strategies over clinical measures. We hypothesized that participants with preserved electrophysiological markers would achieve greater neurological and functional recovery in response to a plasticity-inducing intervention.We conducted a retrospective stratification analysis of data from the recently completed randomized, placebo-controlled, phase 2b Nogo Inhibition in spinal cord injury (NISCI) trial (NCT03935321) investigating the efficacy of NG101, a recombinant human antibody that neutralizes the neurite outgrowth-inhibiting protein Nogo-A. Participants aged 18–70 years with acute (4–28 days) cervical spinal cord injury were eligible. At screening, all participants underwent clinical neurological examination and electrophysiological recordings (i.e. somatosensory evoked potentials). Treatment effect sizes for the recovery of upper extremity motor scores and spinal cord independence measure of self-care (6-month change) between NG101 and placebo groups were compared for stratification based on clinical versus electrophysiological criteria. Power analyses were conducted to estimate the required sample sizes needed for each method.The cohort included 116 participants (45.5 ± 16.8 years old, 74 NG101 and 41 placebo). Clinical stratification showed greater functional recovery in motor-incomplete participants treated with NG101 versus placebo [estimate 0.02 (95% confidence interval: 0.006–0.038), P = 0.007]. Electrophysiological stratification revealed greater functional recovery in participants with preserved somatosensory evoked potentials treated with NG101 versus placebo [0.04 (0.015–0.054), P < 0.001]. Effect sizes were large for electrophysiological stratification (Cohen’s d = 0.94) but small for clinical stratification (Cohen’s d = 0.46). Power analyses demonstrated smaller required sample sizes for electrophysiological stratification (required n = 32) versus clinical stratification (required n = 120).This study shows the value of electrophysiology in comparison to clinical measures for biomarker-driven enrichment and improved power in acute spinal cord injury trials. We emphasize the importance of functionally spared neuronal pathways in promoting recovery in response to plasticity-inducing interventions, such as anti-Nogo-A antibodies.Keywords: spinal cord injury, clinical trial, electrophysiology, biomarker, stratification, anti-Nogo-A, enrichment

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) is a devastating neurological condition that significantly and adversely affects quality of life.1 No therapeutic strategies, aside from neurorehabilitation, have been shown to enhance neurological function after acute injury beyond the natural course of spontaneous recovery. Emerging from ‘failed’ attempts at translation are promising trends towards functional improvements in specific subgroups of individuals with acute SCI.2-4 For example, although both the riluzole neuroprotective3 and anti-Nogo neuroregenerative4 trials missed their primary end point, subgroup analyses revealed improvements in participants with motor-incomplete injuries. These findings underscore the need for improved participant stratification to identify those most likely to benefit from specific interventions prior to clinical trial enrolment.

Traditional stratification methods in the field of SCI are based solely on neurological impairments assessed by way of the International Standards for Neurological Classification of Spinal Cord Injury (ISNCSCI) and the associated American Spinal Injury Association Impairment Scale (AIS) grade.5-7 However, this approach lacks the sensitivity to detect subtle variations in recovery, which is a task it was never designed to accomplish.5 As a result, significant unexplained variability remains in individual recovery outcomes.8 Given these limitations, biomarkers are urgently needed to better detect and predict neurological recovery after injury, improve participant enrichment strategies for clinical trials and, most importantly, to identify individuals who are most likely to respond to intervention.

A growing body of evidence supports the use of electrophysiological markers, such as somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEPs), as objective measures of spinal cord function in spinal disorders.9-13 Importantly, these markers can help to identify individuals with preserved neuronal pathways, which might serve as key substrates for plasticity-inducing therapies.14,15 However, their potential for predicting treatment response and optimizing participant stratification in acute SCI clinical trials remains largely unexplored.

The objective of this study was to examine the value of electrophysiological assessments as predictive biomarkers of functional recovery in comparison to clinical measures in the phase 2b Nogo Inhibition in Spinal Cord Injury (NISCI) trial. We hypothesized that participants with preserved SSEPs (indicative of sufficiently spared sensory axons to bridge the injured spinal cord) would exhibit the greatest response to treatment with the plasticity inducing anti-Nogo-A antibody therapy.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This study is a secondary analysis of the recently completed randomized, placebo-controlled, multicentre, multinational phase 2b NISCI clinical trial (NCT03935321) conducted between May 2019 and July 2022.4 We analysed electrophysiological data that were collected as part of the NISCI trial. The NISCI trial was approved by the Ethics Committee at Heidelberg University Hospital for all study sites located in Germany (Afmu-815/2018), the Kantonale Ethikkommission Zurich for all sites located in Switzerland (2016-01042), the Comité Ético de Investigación de la Fundació Unió Catalana Hospitals for the site located in Spain (2016-001227-31) and the Ethics Committee for Multi-Centric Clinical Trials of the University Hospital Motol for the site in Czechia (EK-1367/19).

Individuals between the ages of 18 and 70 years with acute traumatic cervical SCI were eligible for this study. Inclusion criteria comprised a neurological level of injury between C1 and C8, with time since injury between 4 and 28 days. Participants were included based on predicted upper extremity motor scores (UEMS) at 6 months assessed using unbiased recursive partitioning.16,17 Study participants were randomized as described previously using computerized randomization.4 To control for recovery potential, participants were allocated into unbiased recursive partitioning nodes based on their UEMS screening assessment, then randomized according to a 2:1 (NG101:placebo) allocation ratio. Both study participants and study staff/investigators were blinded to the intervention. Administration of NG101 and placebo was performed by qualified study staff at each study centre as described previously.4 Briefly, intrathecal bolus injections (3 mL) were administered at the L3/L4 level using bevelled needles (diameter 0.5–0.9 mm; Spinocan® Quincke, B. Braun, Germany). Initial administration of NG101 and placebo was performed within 4–28 days post-injury, and a total of six repeated injections were administered every 5 days, over 25 days. The verum group received NG101 (45 mg per injection), a human recombinant antibody blocking Nogo-A activity (donated by Novartis Pharma, Basel, Switzerland)18 dissolved in PBS. The placebo group received PBS only.

Electrophysiological assessments for stratification

All participants underwent bilateral electrophysiological assessments in a clinical neurophysiology unit at screening. These assessments were performed by trained technicians using clinically certiﬁed conventional electrophysiology equipment. To ensure consistency, all participating study centres completed extensive electrophysiological training to standardize testing procedures across sites prior to the study. Testing was performed according to clinical standards and followed a strict protocol established within the European Multicentre Study about Spinal Cord Injury (EMSCI) (NCT01571531) prior to the NISCI trial (Supplementary material, ‘Appendix 1’).

Tibial SSEPs and C8 dermatomal SSEP (dSSEP) were performed to assess the integrity of ascending spinal pathways as reported previously.12,19 Bilateral tibial SSEPs were recorded at the Cz′ electrode (2 cm posterior to Cz position) referenced to Fz based on the 10–20 electrode configuration system (impedance maintained at <5 kΩ) in response to repetitive electrical stimulation (0.2 ms, 3.1 Hz, 300 stimuli) of the right and left tibial nerve dorsal to the malleolus medialis. Stimulation intensity was set to produce a muscular response (maximum 40 mA). Tibial SSEP amplitudes were defined as the magnitude of the P40/N50 complex, which were detected manually as a clear deflection from baseline EEG (i.e. signal was reproducibly distinguishable above noise level in independent averages from odd and even traces) by experienced examiners. Tibial SSEPs were classified as either ‘abolished’ (no clear deflection, P40/N50 amplitude was set to 0 μV) or ‘preserved’ (P40/N50 amplitude >0 μV) to identify the percentage of participants with spared spinal pathways. Bilateral C8 dSSEPs were recorded from contralateral electrodes (C3′/C4′) according to the 10–20 system referenced to Fz (impedance maintained at <5 kΩ) in response to repetitive electrical stimulation (0.2 ms, 3.1 Hz, 300 stimuli) of the right and left C8 dermatomes (palmar fifth digit), respectively. Stimulation intensity was adjusted to 3- to 4-fold the electrical perception threshold. C8 dSSEP amplitudes were defined as the magnitude of the first negative/positive (N1/P1) complex, which were detected manually by experienced examiners. C8 dSSEPs were classified as ‘abolished’ (P1/N1 amplitude = 0 μV) or ‘preserved’ (P1/N1 amplitude > 0 μV).

Tibial and C8 dSSEP recordings from the ‘better’ side [i.e. the side (right or left) with the highest response amplitude] were used for further analyses. If SSEP recordings from one side were missing (i.e. not available/not performed) and the available recording on the other side was classified as ‘preserved’, SSEPs from this participant were classified as ‘preserved’ (because at least one side was known to be preserved). If recordings from one side were missing (i.e. not available/not performed) and the available recording on the other side was classified as ‘abolished’, SSEPs from this participant were classified as ‘not available/NA’.

Clinical assessments for stratification

All participants underwent clinical neurological examination according to the ISNCSCI6 at screening. Clinical stratification was performed based on injury severity at screening: motor-complete (AIS grades A and B) or motor-incomplete (AIS grades C and D), defined by the AIS grade.

As a supplementary analysis, we performed clinical stratification based on the clinical equivalent to tibial SSEPs: light touch sensation at dermatomes L4–S3 that are innervated by the tibial nerve. For this, bilateral light touch scores were assessed based on the ISNCSCI (0 = no sensation, 1 = impaired sensation and 2 = normal sensation) at segments L4–S3. For the analysis, L4–S3 light touch scores were classified as ‘absent’ if bilateral light touch scores (L4–S3) were equal to zero. L4–S3 light touch scores were classified as ‘preserved’ if the right and/or left light touch scores (L4–S3) were larger than zero.

Clinical outcome measures

The primary end point of the NISCI trial was the delta change in UEMS assessed according to the ISNCSCI6 from baseline (1 day before the intervention) and 6 months post-baseline. ISNCSCI was also assessed at 1 and 3 months post-baseline. This study also included the spinal cord independence measure (SCIM)20 III with its self-care subscale for the analysis to focus on functional recovery of the upper limbs, which were part of the secondary outcome measures in the NISCI trial. The SCIM self-care score ranges from 0 to 20. The SCIM was performed at baseline and at 1, 3 and 6 months post-intervention.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software (version 4.3.1, Mac OSX). Linear mixed-effect models were performed to test the effect of time (days post-baseline) and treatment group (NG101 versus placebo) on: (i) UEMS; and (ii) SCIM self-care (interaction effect Time × Treatment group), using the lmer() function from the lme4() package in R. Participant was included as a random effect. This was performed for clinical stratification only, electrophysiological stratification only and combined clinical + electrophysiological stratification. Clinical stratification was performed based on injury severity at screening (motor-complete versus motor-incomplete). Electrophysiological stratification was performed based on tibial SSEPs (abolished versus preserved) and C8 dSSEP (abolished versus preserved) at screening, respectively. Supplementary stratification analyses were performed for clinical L4–S3 light touch sparing (absent versus preserved) at screening.

We then calculated effect sizes and the power achieved (post hoc) for each stratification method when comparing UEMS and SCIM self-care recovery [Δ(6 months − baseline)] between treatment groups (NG101 versus placebo). For normally distributed data (ΔUEMS), effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were classified as negligible (d < 0.20), small (0.20 ≥ d < 0.50), medium (0.50 ≥ d < 0.80) and large (d ≥ 0.80). For non-normally distributed data (ΔSCIM self-care), effect sizes (Cliff’s δ) were classified as negligible (δ < 0.15), small (0.15 ≥ δ < 0.33), medium (0.33 ≥ δ < 0.47) or large (δ ≥ 0.47).

A priori power analyses were performed to compute the total required sample size needed to detect a treatment effect with 80% power (significance level α = 0.05) for each stratification method. This was performed based on the effect sizes calculated for each stratification method.

Finally, to estimate the trial enrolment rate for each stratification method, we calculated the estimated sample size for prescreening, screening and the trial enrolment period for each stratification method based on the enrolment rate observed in the original NISCI trial.

Results

Participants

A total of 463 individuals with acute traumatic cervical SCI were assessed for eligibility to participate in the NISCI trial between 20 May 2019 and 20 July 2022.4 All inclusion criteria were met by 140 participants, all of whom provided written informed consent to participate in the trial. Eleven participants withdrew consent prior to randomization and two after randomization. One participant was excluded after randomization because they presented with spontaneous improvement prior to baseline assessments. The final sample consisted of 126 participants with a mean age of 46 ± 17 years (85% male), of whom 78 were randomized to NG101 and 48 to placebo.

Stratification based on tibial SSEPs at screening revealed that participants with preserved responses in the NG101 group experienced significantly higher recovery of SCIM self-care [95% CI (0.02, 0.05); Fig. 2B(ii)], with a large effect size compared with the placebo group (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1). Stratification based on C8 dSSEPs at screening revealed that participants with preserved responses in the NG101 group showed higher recovery of UEMS [95% CI (−0.0004, 0.04); Fig. 2A(ii)], with a small effect size and SCIM self-care [Fig. 2B(ii)], with a large effect size compared with the placebo group (significant Time × Treatment group interaction; Fig. 3A, Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1). Stratification based on light touch sparing at segments L4–S3 (i.e. clinical equivalent to tibial SSEPs) revealed that participants with preserved light touch (L4–S3) sensation in the NG101 group experienced significantly higher recovery of UEMS with a small effect size and SCIM self-care with a small effect size compared with the placebo group (Supplementary Table 2). The total sample size required to achieve 80% power was Nreq = 316 for UEMS and Nreq = 412 for SCIM self-care recovery (Supplementary Table 2). There was no difference in recovery between treatment groups in participants with absent light touch sensation.

Combined clinical and electrophysiological stratification

Combined stratification based on clinical and electrophysiological measures revealed that participants with motor-incomplete injuries and preserved tibial SSEPs in the NG101 group experienced significantly higher recovery of SCIM self-care with a large effect size compared with the placebo group (significant Time × Treatment group interaction; Supplementary Table 1). In this subgroup of participants (motor-incomplete + preserved SSEPs), the difference in UEMS recovery in the NG101 group compared with the placebo group yielded a medium effect size but did not reach significance. The total sample size required to achieve 80% power in this subgroup was Nreq = 48 for UEMS and Nreq = 24 for SCIM self-care recovery (Supplementary Table 2). There was no difference in recovery between treatment groups in participants with motor-incomplete injuries and abolished tibial SSEPs.

Participants with motor-incomplete injuries and preserved C8 dSSEPs in the NG101 group experienced significantly higher recovery of UEMS and SCIM self-care with large effect sizes compared with the placebo group (significant Time × Treatment group interaction; Supplementary Table 1). The total sample size required to achieve 80% power in this subgroup was Nreq = 42 for UEMS and Nreq = 26 for SCIM self-care recovery (Supplementary Table 2). There was no difference in recovery between treatment groups in participants with motor-incomplete injuries and abolished C8 dSSEPs.

Electrophysiological stratification shortens clinical trial enrolment periods

The enrolment period from the original NISCI trial was 39 months, with an enrolment rate of 3.23 participants per month (Ntotal = 126). Based on real-time data from the NISCI trial, electrophysiological stratification or the combined clinical and electrophysiological stratification revealed that biomarker-driven enrichment strategies would have reduced the enrolment period to achieve 80% power in detecting a significant difference in SCIM self-care recovery between the treatment groups in comparison to clinical stratification alone (Fig. 3B). The number of participants needed for prescreening (i.e. cervical SCI) and screening (i.e. primarily eligible) would have been reduced with electrophysiological or combined clinical and electrophysiological enrichment strategies (Fig. 4). Discussion

We performed a secondary analysis of the phase 2b, randomized, placebo-controlled NISCI trial4 to investigate electrophysiological measures as predictive biomarkers of treatment effects. Our main finding was that electrophysiological stratification based on preserved SSEPs of upper and lower limbs (a technique that assesses spinal cord conduction across the lesion site) increased treatment effect sizes for functional outcomes (SCIM self-care) in comparison to conventional clinical stratification. A subsequent power analysis demonstrated that targeting treatment to participants with preserved tibial SSEPs reduced the total required sample size by a factor of 3.7 in comparison to conventional clinical stratification (Nreq = 32 versus Nreq = 120). Enhanced stratification based on electrophysiological measures not only reduced the total required sample size but would also have substantially shortened the time required to complete trial enrolment (41 months shorter in comparison to clinical stratification based on motor-incomplete injuries). Our findings demonstrate, for the first time, the clinical value of electrophysiological assessments to improve biomarker-driven enrichment of SCI clinical trials. Notably, incorporating electrophysiology does not impose additional burden on participant recruitment; instead, it offers a tangible benefit. Finally, we provide in vivo evidence highlighting the mechanistic concept of functionally spared neuronal pathways in facilitating recovery, particularly in response to plasticity-inducing interventions, such as anti-Nogo-A antibodies.

Objective in nature, SSEPs offer several distinct advantages over clinical standards (e.g. ISNCSCI), including acquisition in unresponsive patients.6 They are also less prone to subjective judgements introduced by both the participant and the examiner. Although previous studies have demonstrated the value of SSEPs in prognosing recovery in acute SCI,9,12,13 our observations are the first to demonstrate utility to enhance the likelihood of identifying participants who will respond best to plasticity-enhancing therapies. Electrophysiological stratification based on preserved tibial SSEPs yielded significant treatment effects. This is attributable to the inclusion of a small number of participants assessed as clinically motor-complete (by means of the AIS classification) into the electrophysiologically incomplete group (i.e. n = 12/57)21,22 and the exclusion of participants who were clinically motor-incomplete but did not show reliable SSEP responses (electrophysiologically complete, n = 29/58). Electrophysiological stratification thus reduced heterogeneity in recovery in the anti-Nogo-A-treated group, yielding increased treatment effects. This discrepancy between electrophysiological and clinical measures of completeness is not unexpected in acute SCI.21-23 Although sparing within spinal pathways might suffice for being classified as clinically incomplete (i.e. participants experiencing some sensation), the recording of preserved SSEPs requires a level of synchronized cortical responses.21-23 It is important to note that stratification based on tibial SSEPs also outperformed its direct clinical equivalent, i.e. light touch sparing at levels L4–S3. From a biological perspective, our observations correspond to the notion that a certain condition of functional spared fibres traversing the lesion site is crucial to support plasticity-inducing interventions.14,15,24,25

Electrophysiological stratification greatly enhanced predictions of SCIM self-care recovery but, to a lesser extent, UEMS recovery. This raises a crucial question regarding which outcomes are best suited as primary end points in SCI clinical trials. Traditional end points, such as UEMS recovery, might not capture treatment efficacy fully in terms of neurological improvements that translate adequately into functional recovery of independence.26 In this context, our findings show the potential value of SCIM self-care as a valuable clinical trial end point.

Recruitment challenges in SCI, particularly in acute-phase trials, have been well documented.2,27 These challenges are often driven by low incidence (orphan disorder) and restrictive inclusion and exclusion criteria, which have contributed to the early termination or abandonment of numerous promising interventions.28 Our goal was to ensure that implementing new enrichment criteria would not prolong trial durations. Indeed, stratification based on electrophysiological measures yielded a substantial reduction of the estimated sample size required to detect improvements in neurological and functional outcomes with high statistical power, allowing for trial completion within a feasible time frame (33 months), which was shorter than the original NISCI trial. Comparatively, the modest effect of anti-Nogo-A antibodies and corresponding larger sample size (Nreq = 120) needed to detect significant effects on recovery of SCIM self-care in participants with clinically motor-incomplete injuries dramatically increased the estimated trial duration (74 months).

Optimizing clinical trial designs with predictive markers (e.g. electrophysiology, imaging or biological samples) allows trials to focus resources on a smaller, responsive sample, accelerating the discrimination of effective therapies. The inclusion of electrophysiological markers can also be applied following recursive partitioning to refine subgroup classification (e.g. maintaining balanced cohorts and to exclude participants who are predicted to reach a ceiling effect), thereby improving both clinical relevance and pathophysiological insight. Additionally, improved enrichment strategies that inform inclusion/exclusion criteria shorten enrolment periods and encourage trials to ‘fail fast’, reducing both costs and participant burden.2 This approach could be particularly valuable in adaptive trial designs, enabling rapid attainment of sample sizes for interim analyses and facilitating timely adjustments.29 In the context of platform trials, recruitment of smaller, well-stratified cohorts optimizes resources to identify promising treatments and discontinue ineffective ones.29

Although we demonstrate the feasibility and substantial value of electrophysiological markers in an acute SCI, multicentre clinical trial, implementation challenges remain. The most obvious one is that electrophysiological testing requires advanced recording equipment and expertise that is often available only at specialized centres. Moreover, there is a need to standardize the acquisition and interpretation of electrophysiological outcomes. To the benefit of the NISCI trial, these issues were already largely addressed by way of longstanding protocols put in place as part of the EMSCI network.13 Future trials that are run outside the EMSCI network will be likely to face the additional costs and barriers of developing the necessary electrophysiology infrastructure. Although retrospective stratification by means of preserved SSEPs would not have prolonged the enrolment period in the NISCI trial within the established EMSCI network, this might differ in other clinical trial networks. Nevertheless, this study demonstrates the feasibility of SSEP examinations in acute SCI clinical trials that can be adopted at early time points and do not require participant engagement (e.g. can even be performed in sedated or unconscious participants). This represents an important advantage in comparison to other electrophysiological assessments, such as the motor evoked potential elicited by transcranial magnetic stimulation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the integration of electrophysiological measures into clinical trial design represents a substantial step forwards in the field of SCI. We propose an optimized framework that integrates electrophysiological markers of preserved neuronal pathways, serving as substrates for regenerative processes, to achieve a more comprehensive characterization of injury and recovery potential. By adopting such an approach, and the possible expansion to other predictive markers, future trials might better account for SCI heterogeneity beyond clinical testing, thereby increasing the likelihood of progressing successfully into the next clinical trial phase. Stratification based on preserved SSEPs not only enhanced the robustness of trial outcomes through increased treatment effect sizes, but also has important practical implications for future trials, i.e. shorter trial durations. As the field continues to evolve, the adoption of objective and predictive electrophysiological markers into clinical trials will be essential for advancing therapeutic development and improving outcomes for individuals living with SCI.
