Population Pharmacokinetic Analysis of MIT‐001, a Novel Ferroptosis Inhibitor, for Dose Optimization
Authors: Sungyeun Bae, Eunwoo Kim, Su‐jin Rhee, Soonha Kim, Kyung‐Sang Yu, SeungHwan Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMIT‐001 is a novel ferroptosis inhibitor that is a potent mitochondrial‐targeting antioxidant targeting various inflammatory diseases. Utilizing pharmacokinetic (PK) and safety data from previous clinical trials in healthy individuals with preclinical efficacy data from the animal disease models, we aimed to develop a population PK model for MIT‐001 to provide evidence for selecting optimal dosage in clinical trials with oral mucositis (OM) patients. The PK model was developed using data from three phase 1 clinical trials with NONMEM software. Covariate analysis included demographic characteristics and clinical laboratory tests. Models were validated using objective function value, goodness of fit plots, bootstrap analysis, and prediction‐corrected visual predictive check. PK profiles of MIT‐001 across various doses were simulated using the final population PK model. A three‐compartment model with linear elimination and first‐order absorption without a lag time best described the PK profile of MIT‐001. Weight and ALT were significant covariates for the peripheral compartment and clearance, respectively. The simulated PK profile showed that 97.4% of patients are expected to reach effective exposure after twice weekly 30 mg SC dose in 3 weeks. Furthermore, 93.6% of patients are expected to be within a tolerable range after 13 weeks of 80 mg SC dosing. A 30 to 80 mg twice weekly SC dose is expected to be both effective and tolerable for OM patients.Keywords: antioxidant, oral mucositis, pharmacokinetic‐pharmacodynamic model, phase 1 clinical trial, phase 2 clinical trial, subcutaneous

Introduction

Oral mucositis (OM) is a common and severe complication in cancer patients undergoing radiotherapy or chemotherapy, characterized by inflammation or ulceration of the mucosa in the oral cavity. 1 , 2 The primary pathogenesis involves oxidative stress induced by reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated during therapy. ROS activates pro‐inflammatory signaling pathways, leading to the mucosal epithelial cell damage, which is accompanied by the necrotic ulcerative lesion of grade 2/3/4 in OM. 3 OM significantly impacts patients' quality of life, increases the duration of hospitalization and readmission rates, exacerbates complications, and imposes substantial economic burdens. 4 Management strategies include antimicrobial mouth rinses, analgesics, cryotherapy, low‐level laser therapy, and parenteral glutamine supplementation; however, therapeutic options remain limited.

MIT‐001, also known as NecroX‐7 or LC28‐0126, is a novel small‐molecule ferroptosis/necrosis inhibitor acting as a mitochondria‐targeted ROS scavenger, with therapeutic potential for treating oxidative stress‐ or ferroptosis‐related diseases (data on file). It is well established that excessive ROS generation occurs in cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy or radiotherapy. 5 Radiotherapy is known to alter mitochondrial functions, including membrane permeability, ATP synthesis, and metabolism, which leads to excessive ROS generation and creates a vicious cycle of oxidative stress. 6 MIT‐001 inhibits both ferroptosis and excessive generation of ROS and prevents abnormal iron and calcium accumulation, thereby blocking cell necrosis and the production of pro‐inflammatory cytokines. Through these mechanisms, MIT‐001 may help maintain normal mitochondrial function and homeostasis, potentially mitigating cellular damage induced by excessive inflammation.

MIT‐001 demonstrated potent antioxidant activity comparable to that of Vitamin E when total antioxidant capacity was analyzed using the 2,2‐diphenyl‐1‐picrylhydrazyl assay (data on file). Furthermore, flow cytometry analysis of rat cardiomyocytes treated with hydrogen peroxide revealed that MIT‐001 maintained significantly higher mitochondrial membrane potential compared with conventional antioxidants or apoptosis inhibitors. 7 MIT‐001 also effectively suppressed mucositis induced by 5‐fluorouracil (5‐FU) alone or in combination with irradiation in mice, as well as mucositis caused by 5‐FU administration in hamsters (data on file).

The pharmacokinetic (PK) profile of MIT‐001 after single and multiple doses has been investigated in several clinical trials. 8 , 9 , 10 After a single intravenous (IV) dose, MIT‐001 exhibited a multi‐compartmental profile with an elimination half‐life (t1/2) ranging from 27 to 45 h. The exposure and maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) of MIT‐001 increased dose‐proportionally over the single IV dose range of 3 to 200 mg. After multiple once‐daily IV doses, the accumulation ratio ranged from 2.58 to 2.79, and the t1/2 was similar to that observed after a single IV dose. Similar PK characteristics were observed following subcutaneous (SC) dosing, with a time to reach Cmax (Tmax) of approximately 2 h, a t1/2 of 27 to 40 h, and an accumulation of 2.75 to 2.77 after multiple once‐daily doses. The bioavailability calculated based on the area under the curve (AUC) after a 40 mg SC dose relative to a 40 mg IV dose was estimated to be 94%.

To support rational dose selection for MIT‐001 in patients with OM, a population PK modeling and simulation approach was applied using PK and safety data from phase 1 clinical trials with preclinical efficacy exposure. Therefore, this study aimed to develop a population PK model based on PK data obtained from previous clinical trials with healthy volunteers. Using the final population PK model, SC dosing regimen capable of maintaining the expected efficacious concentration in OM patients was identified.

Methods

Clinical Studies

The plasma concentration–time data obtained from three clinical trials conducted in healthy Korean adults were used for population PK model development (Table S1, Supporting Information). Study 1 (NCT01737424) was a randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, single ascending dose study with two separate cohorts. 8 Participants received a single IV dose of MIT‐001, 3 to 200 mg for 30 min in cohort 1 and 0.3 to 200 mg for 5 min in cohort 2. Plasma samples were collected up to 48 h post‐dose in both cohorts. Study 2 (NCT03196804) was a randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, and multiple ascending dose study. 9 Participants received an IV dose of MIT‐001, 3 to 30 mg once daily for seven consecutive days. Plasma samples were collected up to 12 h after the first dose and up to 144 h after the last dose, and pre‐dose before the second to sixth dose. Study 3 (NCT05389696) was a randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, dose escalation study with two separate cohorts. 10 In cohort 1, participants received a single SC dose of MIT‐001 10 to 40 mg. For the 40 mg dose group, an additional single IV dose of 40 mg was administered following a two‐week washout period to assess bioavailability. In cohort 2, participants received multiple SC dose of MIT‐001 20 and 40 mg once daily for seven consecutive days. Plasma samples were collected up to 144 h after the SC dose and 48 h after the IV dose in cohort 1 and 24 h after the first dose and 216 h after the last dose in cohort 2. Pre‐dose samples were additionally collected before the fifth and sixth doses in cohort 2.

Safety assessments were based on the results of physical examinations, vital signs, 12‐lead electrocardiograms, and laboratory tests.

Measurement of Plasma Concentration and Data Handling

Plasma MIT‐001 concentrations were determined using validated liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC‐MS/MS) assays. The calibration range for MIT‐001 was 1‐2000 ng/mL for Studies 1 and 2, and 2‐2000 ng/mL for Study 3. The accuracy and precision (CV) were within ±15% and 20%, respectively, and met the criteria recommended by regulatory agencies for the acceptance of bioanalytical method validation.

All available plasma concentrations were used for model development. Concentrations below the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) values prior to reaching the Cmax were treated as zero, while LLOQ values thereafter were handled as missing data.

Population Pharmacokinetic Model Development

To initially characterize the PK characteristics of MIT‐001, concentration–time profiles following IV and SC administration were evaluated graphically. Additionally, noncompartmental analysis was performed after single‐ and multiple‐dose using WinNonlin software (Version 8.3, Certara, Princeton, NJ, USA).

The nonlinear mixed effects method was used to develop the population PK model of MIT‐001 using NONMEM (Version 7.5.1, ICON Development Solutions, Ellicott City, MD, USA). All parameters were estimated using the first‐order conditional method with interaction (FOCEI). Each model was evaluated using Perl‐speaks‐NONMEM (PsN, Version 5.3.0, Uppsala University, Sweden). Models were evaluated using numerical and visual assessments, including the goodness‐of‐fit (GOF) plots, bootstrap analysis, and prediction‐corrected visual predictive check (pcVPC). A decrease in objective function value (OFV) more than 3.84 (p < .05) was considered a statistically significant improvement within the nested models.

The base model structure was developed, including an evaluation of 1‐, 2‐, and 3‐compartment models. For SC administration, absorption models were tested, including first‐order absorption with or without lag time, zero‐order absorption with or without lag time, and combined zero‐ and first‐order absorption. Interindividual variability (IIV) in each parameter was evaluated using an exponential error model. Proportional, additive, and combined error models were evaluated for residual variability.

Following the establishment of the base model, covariate analysis was conducted. Candidates for potential covariates included demographic characteristics such as height, weight, body mass index, sex, and age, as well as clinical laboratory tests such as aspartate transaminase (AST), alanine transaminase (ALT), serum creatinine, and albumin. Simulation

Using the final population PK model, we simulated 1000 virtual subjects for each dosing regimen, incorporating both the IIV and residual error. The simulation was conducted under a scenario where MIT‐001 was administered subcutaneously at doses of 10 to 100 mg twice weekly (day 1 and day 4) for 13 weeks. A virtual population of 1000 patients was generated with the assumption of a body weight of 60 kg and an ALT level of 40 IU/L. The PK parameters of simulated patients were estimated using the ncar package in R (Version 4.3.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The average concentration (Cavg) in weeks 1, 3, and 13 was estimated considering the time to reach steady state (week 3) and the overall treatment duration (week 13) by AUC in weeks 1, 3, and 13 divided by 168 h.

The efficacy data obtained from animal disease models and the safety margin established in previous clinical trials were compared to the simulated PK profiles of MIT‐001 to provide evidence for selecting optimal dosage in clinical trials with OM patients. As the pharmacodynamic (PD) marker, the minimum effective concentration was selected based on Cavg (87 ng/mL) estimated in the C57BL/6N mouse model, in which MIT‐001 3 mg/kg successfully modulated the inflammatory process and effectively prevented the formation of OM. 11 The safety thresholds were derived from the observed Cmax after a 200 mg single dose (Study 1, 10,777.2 ng/mL) and AUC within a dosing interval (AUCtau) after 30 mg once daily multiple‐dose (Study 2, 16,167.4 ng/mL h). The probability of target attainment (PTA) in efficacy and safety was calculated as a percentage of patients achieving above the Cavg of 87 ng/mL or below the Cmax of 10777.2 ng/mL and AUCtau of 16167.4 ng/mL h in weeks 1, 3, and 13. The target dose was set based on the criterion that at least 90% of the population should reach the therapeutic concentration after three weeks and be within the safety margin after 13 weeks of dosing. The reason for evaluating safety margins by PK simulations at 13 weeks was to assess whether MIT‐001 exposures over the 13‐week period remained within the safety margins established in previous clinical studies.

Results

Plasma concentration–time profiles from 95 individuals who received IV administration and 30 individuals who received SC administration were included in the population PK model development (Table 1). The number of concentration–time data points in study 1, study 2, and study 3 was 1185, 1038, and 636, respectively (Table S1, Supporting Information). Across the three clinical trials, the majority of participants were male, and demographic characteristics and clinical laboratory test results were similar among the trials.

Pharmacokinetic Model

A three‐compartment model with linear elimination best described the PK profile of MIT‐001 (Figure 1). Absorption after SC administration was adequately characterized by first‐order absorption without a lag time. The final model included IIV for clearance (CL), central volume of distribution (V1), peripheral volume of distribution (V2), additional peripheral volume of distribution (V3), intercompartmental clearance between V1 and V2, intercompartmental clearance between V1 and V3, and bioavailability. The residual error was best explained by proportional error model. PK parameters were generally estimated with precision and were concluded to be physiologically plausible (Table 2).

Inclusion of weight as a covariate for V2 and V3 reduced the OFV by 7.85 and 24.07, respectively. As this was deemed physiologically plausible, it was incorporated into the final population PK model. Additionally, ALT was selected to be a significant covariate on CL of MIT‐001. When ALT was added as a covariate for CL, the OFV decreased by 7.15. Given that reduction of clearance with higher ALT levels were physiologically plausible, it was also selected as a covariate.

The population predicted concentrations and individual predicted concentrations were both well‐aligned with the observed concentrations (Figure 2). The conditional weighted residuals (CWRES) were evenly distributed regardless of time or concentration values, with the majority falling within the range of −2 to 2. A slight trend was observed in the CWRES versus concentration plot, which appeared to be driven by a small number of observations at higher concentrations where the model tended to underpredict the observed values (Figure 2). However, this trend was not considered to exert a meaningful impact on overall model performance. In the pcVPC, most of the observed values were distributed within the 95% confidence interval, regardless of the route of administration or infusion duration (Figure 3; Figure S1, Supporting Information).

Simulation

PK profiles of MIT‐001 were simulated across various SC doses using the final population PK model (Figure 4). With a SC dose of 20 mg, 75.9% of patients were predicted to achieve the target concentration at week 3, whereas with a 30 mg SC dose, 97.4% of patients were anticipated to reach the target concentration (Table 3). From a safety perspective, it was predicted that up to 50 mg twice weekly SC dosing would not reach the safety thresholds observed in the phase 1 studies. On the other hand, with 60 mg twice weekly dosing, participants were beginning to exceed the thresholds at week 3. Under a 90 mg twice weekly regimen, 13.4% of participants were expected to exceed the thresholds at week 13 (Table 3).

Discussion

The overall PK profile of MIT‐001 was well explained by the three‐compartment model with linear elimination and first‐order absorption without lag time. This was consistent with the PK characteristics shown in previous studies, where the AUC and Cmax of MIT‐001 increased dose‐proportionally. 8 , 9 The bioavailability of SC formulation was estimated to be 82% in this model, which was comparable to that shown in a previous clinical trial. 10

According to the final population PK model, ALT and weight were adopted as the significant covariates for CL, V2, and V3. AST and ALT are commonly used as markers representing liver function and have frequently been reported as significant covariates for clearance in PK models. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 In the previous phase 1 clinical trials, the fraction of MIT‐001 excreted in urine following single and multiple once daily IV administration was marginal, with 1%‐3%, suggesting that MIT‐001 is predominantly eliminated by hepatic metabolism. 8 , 9 Therefore, adopting ALT as a significant covariate for CL in the MIT‐001 PK model is considered physiologically plausible. However, since the analysis was based solely on clinical trial data from healthy participants with a limited range of ALT values, further assessment will be necessary in future trials. In general, the central compartment often represents intravascular volume or rapidly distributing tissues, while the peripheral compartment typically reflects other volumes, such as fatty tissue. Based on this interpretation, evaluating weight as a significant covariate for V2 and V3 is considered physiologically plausible. Furthermore, it suggests the possibility of increased peripheral volume of distribution in obese patients, which may potentially lead to altered disposition of MIT‐001. In the current PK model, no significant covariates were identified for V1. However, when MIT‐001 was administered intravenously to patients with myocardial infarction (NCT02070471, NCT02770664), significantly lower concentrations were observed compared to healthy individuals. This suggests that the disease state may influence the PK profile, especially by changing the central volume of distribution of MIT‐001. Therefore, further analysis of covariates significantly affecting the central compartment will be necessary in future clinical trials conducted in the target population. Moreover, as the majority of the population included in the model was male, further analysis is warranted to assess the potential impact of sex on the PK characteristics of MIT‐001.

By performing simulations under various scenarios considering the final PK model, effective concentration obtained from the animal disease model, and the safety margins established from previous studies in healthy individuals, we tried to determine the appropriate dosing regimen for OM patients. The goal was to identify the dosing range in which over 90% of the simulated population would exceed the predefined effective concentration after 3 weeks, while maintaining the safety margin after 13 weeks. PTA is a clinically meaningful metric that quantifies the probability that a given dosing regimen will achieve predefined PD and safety targets across a patient population. By integrating IIV in drug exposure, PTA provides a direct link between PK variability and PD efficacy or safety thresholds. 16 , 17 , 18 With 30 mg of twice‐weekly SC dosing, more than 90% of simulated patients were expected to reach the effective exposure after 3 weeks. Under the 80 mg twice weekly dosing regimen, exposures remained within the predefined safety threshold. However, simulations predicted that with 90 mg twice weekly dosing, more than 10% of virtual patients would exceed the safety threshold after 13 weeks. Therefore, based on the simulation result, twice weekly SC dose of MIT‐001 ranging from 30 to 80 mg is recommended for clinical trials with OM patients.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the PK profile of MIT‐001 was adequately explained by a three‐compartment model with linear elimination and first‐order absorption without lag time. Based on simulations using the final population PK model, preclinical efficacy data, and clinical trial data on safety, 30 to 80 mg twice weekly SC dosing of MIT‐001 is recommended for OM patients.
