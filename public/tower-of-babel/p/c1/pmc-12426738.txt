Nodal surgery in early-stage cervical cancer: the PHENIX trial and the road ahead
Authors: Connor C Wang, Emma L Barber
Published: 2025

For decades, the surgical management of early-stage cervical cancer has included a radical hysterectomy or trachelectomy along with pelvic lymphadenectomy (PLND) for nodal staging [1,2]. However, full PLND carries substantial morbidity, including lymphocyst formation, lymphedema, neuropathy, and prolonged recovery [1,2,3]. Of patients who undergo PLND, only 10%–20% will have nodal metastases, potentially leading to overtreatment of a majority of patients with early-stage cervical cancer [4,5]. To that end, sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) has emerged as a promising de-escalation strategy. It offers accurate nodal staging with lower morbidity, and it enables ultrastaging to detect micrometastases and isolated tumor cells (ITCs) that standard pathology may miss [2,3]. Although SLNB has been widely adopted as a standard of care across other tumor types, its adoption in cervical cancer has been slower, largely due to the prognostic weight of nodal status. The presence of positive pelvic lymph nodes shifts five-year survival from approximately 90% in node-negative stage IB1 disease to around 40%–50% in node-positive IIIC1 disease, and withholding adjuvant chemoradiotherapy in node-positive patients risks compromising survival outcomes [1,5].

Prior to PHENIX, several prospective studies established the foundation for SLNB in cervical cancer. The SENTIREC trial demonstrated that SLNB achieves high diagnostic accuracy (96.3% sensitivity and 98.7% negative predictive value) when a sentinel node algorithm is strictly followed, requiring bilateral mapping or side-specific lymphadenectomy in cases of mapping failure, particularly for tumors ≤20 mm [6]. SENTICOL I confirmed that bilateral sentinel node detection yields a 100% negative predictive value, with no false negatives, supporting the diagnostic reliability of SLNB when mapping is successful [7]. SENTICOL II, a randomized trial, demonstrated that SLNB alone reduced lymphatic morbidity without compromising three-year disease-free survival, which furthers supports the oncologic safety of SLNB when performed according to protocol [8]. The SENTIX trial further validated relatively high bilateral detection rates in experienced centers and emphasized the importance of ultrastaging, as nearly half of nodal metastases would be missed by routine histopathology alone [9,10]. Meta-analyses estimate SLNB’s sensitivity at approximately 90% and negative predictive value at >98% [11]. Importantly, SLNB identifies atypical drainage patterns, such as parametrial, presacral, and paraaortic nodes, not systematically removed by PLND, enhancing diagnostic precision. The European Society of Gynaecological Oncology and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network now cautiously endorse SLNB alone in well-selected patients, provided strict adherence to the sentinel node algorithm and use of ultrastaging, but until recently, definitive survival data were lacking [12]. The PHENIX trial now provides the long-awaited prospective evidence for the oncologic safety of SLNB as a standalone staging procedure in this setting.

The PHENIX trial, a multicenter, randomized, non-inferiority study, enrolled over 900 patients with FIGO 2009 stage IA1 (with lymphovascular space invasion), IA2, IB1, and IIA1 cervical cancer and tumors ≤3 cm. All patients underwent SLNB with intraoperative frozen section. Sentinel node-negative patients were randomized to SLNB alone or SLNB plus PLND. Both groups benefited from SLNB, which allowed identification of nodes beyond traditional pelvic boundaries and provided detailed evaluation, although ultrastaging was not mandatory. The primary endpoint was three-year disease-free survival. With a median follow-up of 52 months, SLNB alone achieved noninferior three-year DFS compared to SLNB plus PLND (96.8% vs. 94.5%; hazard ratio [HR]=0.61) and superior cancer-specific survival (100% vs. 97.8%; HR=0.21). Recurrence rates were lower in the SLNB arm (3.5% vs. 5.7%), and notably, all retroperitoneal nodal recurrences occurred in the SLNB plus PLND group. SLNB alone was associated with reduced operative time, blood loss, antibiotic use, and lower rates of lymphocyst, lymphedema, pain, and intraoperative complications. Approximately 75% of SLNB plus PLND were performed laparoscopically; in this subgroup, disease-free survival was worse compared to SLNB alone, raising questions about potential technical or biological factors, though this observation requires further investigation [13].

Despite its strengths, PHENIX had limitations. The high adjuvant treatment rate, approaching 50%, in both arms may have mitigated potential survival differences between groups. This raises the possibility that adjuvant chemoradiation, rather than the omission of PLND, may have driven the favorable oncologic results. The early closure of the PHENIX-II arm, which was designed to study patients with sentinel lymph node-positive disease, leaves unresolved questions regarding the optimal management of micrometastatic and macrometastatic nodal disease as well as the survival differences between SLNB and SLNB + PLND among node positive cervical cancer patients. The prognostic significance of micrometastases and the role of completion lymphadenectomy versus adjuvant therapy in this subgroup remain unclear. The lack of mature, adequately powered data from this cohort limits conclusions about safety of SLNB in node-positive patients. The predominance of single tracer mapping (85% methylene blue, 14% indocyanine green) with a bilateral detection rate of 83%, allowance of unilateral sentinel node detection, and the lack of mandated ultrastaging may reduce sensitivity for low-volume metastases and deviate from more standardized protocols used in prior trials. The use of frozen section is also a less accurate determination of nodal status evidenced by the 1.7% of patients randomized SLNB alone in PHENIX-I who were frozen section node negative, but reported to have positive lymph nodes. Frozen section may also compromise the ability to perform additional pathologic staging and further decrease sensitivity. These factors may impact both the detection of nodal disease and the reproducibility of results in settings with different mapping and pathology standards [13].

SENTICOL III offers a complementary and structured approach. This ongoing international, randomized, non-inferiority trial compares SLNB alone to SLNB plus PLND in sentinel node-negative patients with FIGO 2018 stage IA1 (with lymphovascular space invasion) to IB2 (≤4 cm) disease. The trial is designed with co-primary endpoints of disease-free survival and health-related quality of life. It follows the Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center sentinel node protocol and has strict requirements for bilateral mapping. Mapping techniques permitted include isotope alone, isotope with blue dye, isotope with ICG, or ICG alone; blue dye alone is explicitly excluded due to inferior detection rates. Ultrastaging is also mandated across all centers, which provides granular data on micro-volume disease as well as ITCs and their prognostic relevance [14].

Key differences and controversies between the PHENIX and SENTICOL III trials center on SLNB protocols, tracer techniques, mapping requirements, surgical standardization, and the impact of adjuvant therapy rates on survival outcomes [13,14]. PHENIX predominantly used single-tracer mapping (vast majority methylene blue), allowed unilateral sentinel node detection, and did not mandate ultrastaging, reflecting a pragmatic, real-world approach that enhances generalizability but introduces heterogeneity and lower sensitivity. The use of intraoperative frozen section in PHENIX introduces another deviation from commonly used sentinel lymph node mapping protocols. In contrast, SENTICOL III requires dual tracer mapping (radioisotope plus blue dye or indocyanine green), mandates bilateral SLN detection for randomization, and enforces standardized ultrastaging and surgical protocols across all centers. This approach maximizes diagnostic accuracy and minimizes false negatives. SENTICOL III’s rigorous quality assurance and credentialing reduce variability due to surgeon experience or institutional practices, whereas PHENIX’s less standardized approach may allow such factors to possibly influence oncologic outcomes. PHENIX reported a high rate of adjuvant therapy use, which could potentially confounding the impact of surgical extent on survival, while SENTICOL III is designed to better isolate the effect of nodal surgery by standardizing adjuvant therapy indications. These differences highlight ongoing controversies regarding the optimal SLNB protocol, the trade-off between real-world applicability and methodological rigor, and the interpretation of oncologic safety in the context of adjuvant therapy use.

From these two studies, additional unresolved questions remain. Regarding the optimal tracer technique, while dual tracer mapping and high bilateral detection rates are associated with improved sensitivity, real-world practice often relies on single tracers, which may compromise diagnostic accuracy and consistency across centers. Although ultrastaging increases detection of micrometastases and ITCs, its necessity and standardization remains controversial as there is no universally accepted protocol, and the clinical significance of low-volume metastases, particularly ITCs in cervical cancer specifically, is not fully defined [3,15]. The optimal management of sentinel lymph node-positive patients, especially those with micrometastases or ITCs, remains unknown, as PHENIX-II was underpowered to inform practice and SENTICOL III does not randomize these patients, leaving uncertainty about the need for additional lymphadenectomy or adjuvant therapy in this subgroup. The inferior disease-free survival observed with laparoscopic PLND in PHENIX raises the possibility that minimally invasive approaches introduce risk in complete nodal dissection for cervical cancer that are not present in laparoscopic SLNB alone, echoing findings from other gynecologic oncology trials [16], and warrants prospective evaluation. As the field evolves, incorporating patient-reported outcomes alongside traditional oncologic endpoints, such as designed in SENTICOL III, will be essential to defining what constitutes meaningful clinical benefit. PHENIX’s pragmatic findings move the field forward by reflecting real-world practice, while SENTICOL III’s forthcoming results will provide the rigor needed to solidify SLNB’s place in international practice. The generalizability of trial results to less-experienced centers also remains uncertain, given variability in mapping protocols, surgeon experience, and institutional resources.

In conclusion, the PHENIX trial marks a pivotal advance in cervical cancer care, demonstrating that “less can be more” in terms of oncologic outcomes and reduced morbidity. However, several important questions remain and require further prospective evaluation. SENTICOL III is expected to provide critical data to define the role of SLNB as a new standard of care and will clarify for whom, under what conditions, and with what tools SLNB should be implemented. While precision surgery is evolving rapidly to become standard of care, its optimal application will continue to require careful patient selection and surgical expertise.
