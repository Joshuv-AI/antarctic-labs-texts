The effect of barberry supplementation on components of metabolic syndrome: a grade assessment systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Xiu Zhang, Qion Lou, Sho Liou, Xiaoshan Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePolyphenol-rich foods, such as barberry, improve cardiovascular risk factors, but evidence remains inconsistent. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis were carried out to assess the effects of barberry supplementation on components of metabolic syndrome in adults.MethodA comprehensive literature search of Web of Science, Embase, PubMed, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library was conducted up to July 2025. We included randomized clinical trials that examined the effect of barberry on one of the components of metabolic syndrome in this meta-analysis. Weighted mean differences (WMD) were pooled using a random-effects model.ResultsEleven studies published between 2012 and 2023 were included, comprising 718 participants. Barberry supplementation significantly reduced weight (WMD = − 1.48 kg; 95% CI: − 2.94 to − 0.02), systolic blood pressure (WMD: − 9.84; 95% CI: − 17.36 to − 2.31), diastolic blood pressure (WMD: − 11.31; 95% CI: − 19.63 to − 3.00), total cholesterol (WMD = − 17.90 mg/dL; 95% CI: − 32.99 to − 2.81), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (WMD = − 11.38 mg/dL; 95% CI: − 21.35 to − 1.40), and triglyceride (WMD = − 23.63 mg/dL; 95% CI: − 35.58 to − 11.68), FBS (WMD = − 16.69 mg/dL; 95% CI: − 30.46 to − 2.93), HOMA-IR (WMD = − 0.95; 95% CI: − 1.79 to − 0.12), insulin (WMD = − 1.70 mU/L; 95% CI: − 2.33 to − 1.08). It also increased high-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels (WMD = 2.46 mg/dL; 95% CI: 0.61 to 4.31). However, no significant effect was found for glycated hemoglobin (WMD = − 0.72%; 95% CI: − 1.85 to 0.40) or body mass index (WMD = − 0.28 kg/m2; 95% CI: − 0.59 to 0.03).ConclusionBarberry supplementation elicits modest but significant improvements in key components of metabolic syndrome, including blood pressure, weight, lipid profile, and glycemic indices. These results support its potential as an adjunctive dietary intervention for managing metabolic health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-025-01067-y.Keywords: Barberry, Obesity, Glycemic indices, Lipid profile, Blood pressure, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Cardiometabolic syndrome encompasses a group of interrelated metabolic abnormalities, including central obesity, dyslipidemia, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), hypertension, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [1]. These disorders are strongly associated with the development and progression of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, which remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide [2]. According to the World Health Organization, cardiovascular diseases account for approximately 32% of global deaths, translating to nearly 19.7 million fatalities each year [3]. In this context, dietary interventions, including functional foods, specific food groups, and overall dietary patterns, have been investigated for their cardioprotective potential. Herbal medicines have also gained increasing attention due to their affordability, accessibility, and generally lower risk of adverse effects compared with conventional pharmacological therapies [4].

Barberry (Berberis vulgaris L., Berberidaceae), native to Asia and Europe, is widely used as a food additive and for its medicinal properties. Its high content of berberine, an isoquinoline alkaloid, is believed to drive most of its biological activities [5]. Experimental and clinical studies suggest that berberine exerts multiple beneficial effects, including anti-inflammatory, lipid-lowering, and vascular-protective actions [6, 7]. Mechanistically, barberry may enhance glycemic control by activating AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), a central regulator of energy metabolism that facilitates glucose uptake and reduces hepatic gluconeogenesis [8, 9]. Additionally, it may modulate lipid metabolism by promoting cholesterol excretion and inhibiting lipogenesis [10].

Although some findings are encouraging, the evidence remains inconsistent, with substantial heterogeneity in sample size, dosage, treatment duration, and outcome measures, which limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions. Furthermore, meta-analyses of randomized clinical trials (RCTs) have reported conflicting results [8, 11, 12]. This meta-analysis aims to systematically review and synthesize the existing evidence on the effects of barberry on metabolic syndrome components. By synthesizing data from multiple studies, we aim to clarify the potential effects of these interventions on cardiovascular risk factors and provide a comprehensive summary to inform future research and clinical practice.

Materials and methods

The reporting of this meta-analysis adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [13]. The protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews.

Search strategy

A systematic search was conducted across several electronic databases up to July 2025 to identify relevant controlled trials that examined barberry's effect on metabolic syndrome components. The databases searched included Scopus, PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library. The search terms used in the query included a combination of keywords (Supplementary Table 1). We also screened the reference lists of relevant systematic reviews and meta-analyses to ensure comprehensive coverage. We employed the Cochrane-recommended PICO framework to guide our search strategy, defining: (1) participants as adults, (2) intervention as supplementation with Berberis vulgaris, (3) comparison as control or placebo groups, and (4) outcomes as components of metabolic syndrome.

Study selection

The following criteria were applied to include clinical trials: (1) randomized controlled trials (parallel or crossover design); (2) adult participants aged ≥ 18 years; (3) publications in English; (4) studies reporting mean ± standard deviation (SD) or sufficient data to calculate these values; and (5) studies evaluating the effects of Berberis vulgaris supplementation on one or more of the following outcomes: fasting blood sugar (FBS), insulin, homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), body weight, body mass index (BMI), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP). Exclusion criteria included non-clinical studies, in vitro or animal studies, case reports, observational studies, letters, trials with short duration of follow-up (< 2 weeks), commentaries, and conference abstracts without full-text availability.

Data extraction

Two researchers independently selected the study, with the lead researcher available to resolve disagreements. Where the necessary data were not reported, the corresponding authors were contacted by e-mail in order to obtain the information requested. The following data were extracted from each study included: first author, year of publication, location of the study, study design, characteristics of participants, sample size, duration of the intervention, dose of Berberis supplementation, health status of participants and reported outcome measures.

Assessment of the quality of studies and meta-evidence

The methodological quality of the included studies was independently assessed by two reviewers, with discrepancies resolved through discussion. Risk of bias in eligible trials was evaluated using the Cochrane Collaboration Risk of Bias tool [14]. The GRADE framework was utilized to appraise the certainty of evidence for each outcome [15].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 15.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA) to evaluate the effects of Berberis on components of metabolic syndrome. Extracted data included means, standard deviations (SDs), and sample sizes at baseline and post-intervention. When necessary data were missing, corresponding authors were contacted to obtain the relevant information. To allow for comparisons across different measurement scales, weighted mean differences (WMDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated [16]. Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the I2 statistic and interpreted according to established thresholds [17]. Subgroup analyses were conducted according to health condition, dosage of barberry supplementation, and intervention duration. Sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness of the findings. When fewer than 10 studies reported results, a Begg test was reported for publication bias [18].

Results

Study selection and study characteristics

A systematic literature search identified 2,279 records. After removing 787 duplicate entries, 1,492 records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 1471 were excluded due to irrelevance to the study objectives, population, or outcomes. The full texts of the remaining 21 articles were retrieved for further assessment. During the full-text assessment, ten studies were excluded. Finally, 11 studies were included in the current meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of the literature search and study selection process

Table 1 shows the characteristics of the included studies. All of the included RCTs published between 2000 and 2020 had a parallel design, were all conducted in Iran (n = 11) [19–29]. The study populations exhibited diverse clinical profiles, including type 2 diabetes patients (T2DM) [19, 22, 23, 25, 29], and the other included non-alcoholic fatty liver disease patients (NAFLD) [27], metabolic syndrome (MetS) [21, 26, 28], benign breast disease [24], and hypertension [20]. The mean age of participants across the included studies ranged from 36 to 57 years. The duration of interventions ranged from 3 to 12 weeks. Barberry doses ranged from 75 mg/day to 10 g/day for the fruit and from 200 mL/day to 480 mL/day for the juice.

Barberry administration and anthropometric indices

The pooled analysis demonstrated a significant weight reduction (WMD = − 1.48 kg; 95% CI: − 2.94 to − 0.02; p = 0.047; I2 = 0.0%; p = 0.854) (Fig. 3a), but not BMI (WMD = − 0.28 kg/m2; 95% CI: − 0.59 to 0.03; p = 0.079; I2 = 0.0%; p = 0.441) (Fig. 3b) following barberry supplementation. These findings remained robust in sensitivity analyses, and no publication bias was observed (Begg’s test: p˃ 0.05).

Forest plot in relation to effect of Barberry on weight (A), and BMI (B)

Barberry administration and blood pressure

Analysis revealed that barberry supplementation significantly reduced both SBP and DBP, with pooled estimates showing a decrease of − 9.84 mmHg (95% CI: − 17.36 to − 2.31; p = 0.01; I2 = 85.6%; p < 0.001) for SBP (Fig. 4a) and − 11.31 mmHg (95% CI: − 19.63 to − 3.00; p = 0.008; I2 = 93.2%; p < 0.001) for DBP (Fig. 4b). Sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of SBP and DBP results. Begg’s test indicated no evidence of publication bias for either SBP or DBP (p˃ 0.05).

Forest plot in relation to effect of Barberry on SBP (A), and DBP (B)

Barberry administration and lipid profile

The pooled analysis demonstrated that barberry supplementation significantly reduced TC (WMD = − 17.90 mg/dL; 95% CI: − 32.99 to − 2.81; p = 0.02; I2 = 90.9%; p < 0.001) (Fig. 5a), LDL-C (WMD = − 11.38 mg/dL; 95% CI: − 21.35 to − 1.40; p = 0.025; I2 = 85.3%; p < 0.001) (Fig. 5b), and TG (WMD = − 23.63 mg/dL; 95% CI: − 35.58 to − 11.68; p < 0.001; I2 = 55.7%; p = 0.027) (Fig. 5c). It also increased high-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels (WMD = 2.46 mg/dL; 95% CI: 0.61 to 4.31; p = 0.009; I2 = 78.6%; p < 0.001) (Fig. 5d). Subgroup analyses identified treatment dosage, underlying health conditions, and intervention duration as potential sources of heterogeneity. Barberry supplementation in patients with T2DM, administered for ˃8 weeks at a dosage of ≥ 750 mg/day, resulted in a higher reduction of TC, TG, and LDL-C levels (Table 3). Also, Barberry supplementation in patients with MetS, administered for ≤ 8 weeks at a dosage of < 750 mg/day, resulted in a higher increase in HDL-C levels (Table 3). Begg’s test revealed no sign of publication bias across outcomes. Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the lipid profile findings.

Barberry administration and glycemic indices

Barberry supplementation showed a significant effect on FBS (WMD = − 16.69 mg/dL; 95% CI: − 30.46 to − 2.93; p = 0.017; I2 = 96.6%; p < 0.001) (Fig. 6a), HOMA-IR (WMD = − 0.95; 95% CI: − 1.79 to − 0.12; p = 0.025; I2 = 89.1%; p < 0.001) (Fig. 6b), insulin (WMD = − 1.70 mU/L; 95% CI: − 2.33 to − 1.08; p < 0.001; I2 = 8.6%; p = 0.350) (Fig. 6c), but not effect on HbA1c (WMD = − 0.72%; 95% CI: − 1.85 to 0.40; p = 0.209; I2 = 92.7%; p < 0.001) (Fig. 6d). Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the findings, while Begg’s test (p > 0.05) showed no indication of publication bias.

Discussion

This meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials demonstrates that barberry supplementation confers significant benefits on several cardiometabolic parameters, most notably blood pressure, lipid profile, and insulin resistance. While modest effects were observed on body weight and FBS, the findings suggest that barberry may be a valuable adjunctive intervention in MetS, T2DM, and related conditions.

Our results are consistent with previous meta-analyses examining Berberis vulgaris and its primary bioactive compound, berberine, which have repeatedly demonstrated favorable effects on lipid metabolism, glycemic control, and blood pressure [11, 30]. For instance, a recent meta-analysis of berberine in T2DM reported reductions in LDL-C and TG comparable in magnitude to those observed in our pooled estimates, supporting the biological plausibility of our findings [31]. Notably, the reductions in systolic and diastolic blood pressure observed in our analysis (− 9.84 mmHg and − 11.31 mmHg, respectively) exceed those reported in earlier reviews, potentially reflecting the inclusion of studies with higher baseline blood pressure or longer treatment durations. The absence of a significant effect on BMI aligns with several prior reports, although some studies have observed modest decreases, likely attributable to differences in population characteristics and intervention protocols.

Subgroup analysis revealed that higher doses (≥ 750 mg/day) administered for longer durations (> 8 weeks) produced greater reductions in total cholesterol, LDL-C, and triglycerides [32]. This is consistent with a dose–response relationship observed in pharmacological studies of berberine, where higher exposure is associated with greater AMPK activation and lipid-lowering efficacy. Conversely, shorter-duration, lower-dose regimens (< 750 mg/day, ≤ 8 weeks) yielded more pronounced HDL-C increases. This pattern may reflect differences in lipid metabolism adaptation—initial HDL-C elevation may occur early in treatment, whereas sustained lipid-lowering effects require prolonged intervention. These findings underscore the importance of optimizing dosage and treatment duration for maximal therapeutic benefit.

Barberry, primarily through its bioactive alkaloid berberine, exerts comprehensive effects on glycemic control, lipid metabolism, blood pressure, and body composition in adults. Activation of AMPK enhances insulin sensitivity, promotes peripheral glucose uptake, and suppresses hepatic gluconeogenesis, thereby improving glycemic indices [33]. Simultaneously, barberry modulates lipid metabolism by upregulating hepatic LDL receptor expression, inhibiting lipogenesis, and reducing TG synthesis, resulting in lower total cholesterol, LDL-C, and triglycerides, while short-term or lower-dose interventions may transiently increase HDL-C through enhanced reverse cholesterol transport. Vascular benefits include improved endothelial function, increased nitric oxide bioavailability, and attenuation of oxidative stress, contributing to modest reductions in blood pressure [34]. In addition, barberry influences body weight and adiposity by modulating adipocyte differentiation, enhancing energy expenditure, and regulating appetite-related hormones. Collectively, these interconnected mechanisms underscore the potential of barberry as a holistic intervention for metabolic disorders, with efficacy strongly dependent on dosage and duration of therapy [35].

The magnitude of change observed in this meta-analysis is clinically meaningful. Reductions of ~ 10 mmHg in SBP and DBP are associated with a 20–30% lower risk of major cardiovascular events [36]. Similarly, the lipid-lowering effects—particularly LDL-C reduction of > 10 mg/dL—translate into a substantial decrease in atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk [37]. Improvements in HOMA-IR and fasting insulin suggest better insulin sensitivity, which may delay progression from prediabetes to T2DM and reduce associated cardiovascular risk [38].

Strengths and Limitations

The strengths of this study include the exclusive inclusion of RCTs, the comprehensive assessment of multiple metabolic endpoints, the subgroup analysis to explore heterogeneity, and the lack of evidence for publication bias. Limitations include substantial heterogeneity for specific outcomes, variability in barberry formulations and dosages, and relatively short intervention durations in most trials, which may underestimate long-term effects on BMI, HbA1c, and clinical outcomes. The modest sample sizes and differences in background diet and medication use may also have influenced the results. Although the parallel-group design employed in all included studies minimizes carryover effects, it may also increase between-group variability and lower the accuracy of estimated effects, especially in smaller trials. All included studies were conducted in Iran, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations due to potential differences in genetics, diet, lifestyle, and healthcare access. The absence of eligible studies from different regions could reflect a lack of RCTs evaluating this intervention elsewhere or stricter inclusion criteria in this review. Future research in diverse populations is warranted to confirm the applicability of these findings globally.

Conclusions

The pooled results indicate that Barberry exerts favorable effects on anthropometric indices, particularly weight, while also contributing to improvements in blood pressure, lipid parameters (TC, LDL-C, HDL-C, and TG), and insulin resistance markers (FBS, insulin, and HOMA-IR). These findings confirmed its potential role as an adjunctive therapy in managing MetS, T2DM, and related cardiovascular risk factors. Future large-scale, long-term trials with standardized formulations across diverse populations are needed to validate these effects, assess inter-individual variability, and determine optimal dosing strategies.
