Comparison between ultrasound guided pericapsular nerve group (PENG) block and interscalene brachial plexus block for postoperative analgesia following shoulder arthroscopy: a randomized controlled trial

Introduction

Shoulder arthroscopy is among the most commonly performed orthopedic procedures and is often associated with significant postoperative pain, especially in the first 24 h. Effective pain control is critical not only for patient comfort but also for early rehabilitation and functional recovery [1]. Regional anesthesia techniques have become the cornerstone of multimodal analgesia strategies in shoulder surgeries, with the interscalene brachial plexus (ISB) block long regarded as the gold standard. However, ISB block use is often limited by the well-documented profile of adverse effects, most notably phrenic nerve palsy, hemidiaphragmatic paralysis, Horner's syndrome, and motor blockade of the upper limb. These complications, while transient, are especially problematic in patients with compromised pulmonary function or when early upper limb mobility is desired [2].

In response to these limitations, anesthesiologists and pain specialists have explored alternative diaphragm sparing approaches e.g. suprascapular nerve and axillary nerve blocks [3]. Ultrasound-guided pericapsular nerve group (PENG) block, originally developed for hip surgeries is now being investigated for shoulder procedures [4]. Use of PENG in the shoulder region was originally evaluated by the cadaveric study of Yamak Altinpulluk et al. [5].

The anatomical rationale for adapting the PENG concept to the shoulder is based on the clustered distribution of articular sensory branches around the glenohumeral capsule. The capsule is primarily supplied by articular branches of the suprascapular and axillary nerves, with variable contributions from the lateral pectoral and subscapular nerves. These branches typically course along the glenoid neck and periosteal surfaces before capsular penetration. Interfascial planes, including the rotator interval and subscapularis–capsular plane, may permit injectate spread across multiple capsular nerve entry zones, supporting the feasibility of a pericapsular injection strategy targeting shoulder joint sensory innervation. Unlike conventional shoulder interfascial blocks such as suprascapular, axillary nerve, or combined suprascapular–axillary techniques that target individual terminal nerves, the modified PENG approach is designed to target multiple articular sensory branches at their pericapsular course before capsular penetration [6–9]. The recent report of Küpeli and Yazici Kara [4] suggested that the modified PENG block may offer targeted analgesia without diaphragmatic or upper extremity motor involvement.

Despite increasing clinical interest in diaphragm-sparing regional anesthesia for shoulder surgery, there is scarcity of published randomized controlled studies that have directly compared the analgesic efficacy and complication profile of PENG block versus ISB block in patients undergoing shoulder arthroscopy. This represents a significant gap in the literature, particularly as the surgical and anesthesia communities seek safer and more selective nerve blocks tailored to patient-specific risks and recovery goals.

The present study aimed to compare the clinical outcomes between ultrasound-guided PENG and ISB blocks in the setting of shoulder arthroscopy.

Patients and methods

Setting, ethics, selection and randomization

The present randomized study was conducted following approval by the Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in line with the recommendations of Helsinki Declaration on clinical research involving human subjects and CONSORT guidelines for reporting the results of randomized controlled trials.

The sample size was calculated using G Power Software (Kiel University, Germany). Based on data from Aksu et al. [10], the mean 24-h VAS score after ISB was 2.0 ± 1.7. To detect a clinically meaningful difference between groups, corresponding to a standardized effect size (Cohen’s d) of 0.8, a sample size of 25 patients per group was calculated with a two-sided alpha of 0.05 and 80% power.

Patients were selected to participate in the study on the basis of the inclusion criteria: 18 to 60-year-old, classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I or II, and scheduled for elective unilateral shoulder arthroscopy under general anesthesia. Exclusion criteria were: body mass index (BMI) above 35 kg/m2, pregnancy or lactation, neurological or psychiatric disorders such as epilepsy or stroke, known coagulopathy or anticoagulant therapy, infection at the block site, known allergy to local anesthetics, or chronic hepatic, renal, or respiratory disease that could interfere with block safety or analgesic response.

Among the 64 consecutive patients assessed for eligibility, 50 patients were enrolled. They were randomly and equally allocated to one of the study interventions: ISB (n = 25) or PENG (n = 25) blocks using a computer-generated randomization sequence and sealed envelope technique. None of the studied patients was lost to follow up (Fig. 1).

Preoperative assessment

All patients underwent a comprehensive preoperative assessment, including complete medical history, thorough physical examination, and routine laboratory investigations. Additionally, patients were instructed on how to use the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) to rate postoperative pain, with 0 representing no pain and 10 representing the worst pain imaginable. This training ensured uniform understanding of the pain assessment tool across all participants.

Anesthetic technique and surgical procedure

General anesthesia was standardized for all participants. Induction was performed using propofol (2 mg/kg), fentanyl (1–2 μg/kg), and atracurium (0.5 mg/kg) to facilitate endotracheal intubation. Anesthesia was maintained using isoflurane (1–2 MAC) in an air-oxygen mixture (50%) and supplemental doses of atracurium as needed. Intraoperative analgesia was augmented with fentanyl if heart rate or mean arterial pressure increased by more than 20% from baseline. Granisetron (3 mg IV) was administered prophylactically for postoperative nausea and vomiting. At the end of surgery, neuromuscular blockade was reversed using neostigmine and atropine.

Surgical procedures were performed by the same orthopedic team, following a standardized arthroscopic technique. Both blocks (PENG and ISB) were administered after induction of general anesthesia to maintain patient blinding and comfort.

Regional block techniques

In the ISB block group, the block was performed using a 15 mL injection of 0.25% bupivacaine. A high-frequency linear ultrasound probe (SonoSite M-Turbo C®) with high frequency (6 −13 MHz) was used to visualize the roots of the brachial plexus between the anterior and middle scalene muscles at the level of C6. In the PENG block group, under similar ultrasound guidance, a modified pericapsular nerve group block targeting the sensory innervation of the shoulder joint capsule was performed under ultrasound guidance. With the patient's arm in abduction and external rotation, 15 mL volume of 0.25% bupivacaine was injected between the deltoid muscle and subscapularis tendon using an in-plane approach under sterile conditions. The probe was positioned transversely over the anterior shoulder to identify the anatomical landmarks [4] (Fig. 2). Both blocks were conducted by the same experienced general anesthesiologist with typical training in regional anesthesia to ensure consistency and safety.

Postoperative monitoring and analgesia

After surgery, patients were transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) for continued monitoring. Hemodynamic parameters including heart rate, mean arterial pressure, and oxygen saturation were recorded at 15-min intervals for the first hour. Pain was assessed using the VAS scale at 0, 2, 4, 6, 12, and 24 h postoperatively. If VAS score exceeded 3 at any time, rescue analgesia in the form of pethidine 50 mg IV was administered. Additional doses were given every 6 h as needed. No other analgesics were given until discharge.

Outcome assessment

All outcome assessors were blinded to the allocated interventions. The primary outcome is the postoperative pain levels at 24 h postoperative. Other outcome measures included time to first rescue analgesic request, total pethidine consumption over 24 h, and any side effects. These included nausea, vomiting, motor weakness (assessed using the Medical Research Council scale [11]), phrenic nerve palsy (assessed clinically by dyspnea or decreased air entry), Horner’s syndrome, hoarseness, or local anesthetic systemic toxicity.

Statistical analysis

The collected data were coded, tabulated, and statistically analyzed using IBM SPSS statistics (Statistical Package for Social Sciences) software version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Chicago, USA). Quantitative data were described as mean ± SD (standard deviation) and compared using independent t-test. Qualitative data described as number and percentage and compared using Chi square test as well as Fisher’s Exact test. The level of significance was taken at p-value < 0.05.

Results

The present study was conducted on 50 patients allocated to PENG (n = 25) or ISB (n = 25) block. Both groups were comparable regarding age (41.5 ± 4.6 years versus 42.2 ± 5.7, p = 0.64), male/female distribution (16/9 versus 17/8, p = 0.77), BMI (28.4 ± 2.9 kg/m2 versus 27.8 ± 3.1, p = 0.49), ASA status I/II distribution (15/10 versus 16/9, p = 0.77), intraoperative fentanyl dose (84.8 ± 25.1 μg versus 82.7 ± 19.0, p = 0.74) and operative duration (83.4 ± 11.8 min versus 85.4 ± 16.3, p = 0.62) (Table 1).

Discussion

The present study aimed to compare ultrasound guided PENG and ISB blocks for postoperative analgesia following shoulder arthroscopy. Given the novelty of the block, the perioperative analgesic regimen was intentionally restricted to intraoperative fentanyl, the block intervention, and rescue opioids to allow rigorous assessment of block-specific analgesic efficacy while ensuring adequate patient pain control. The study findings suggest that PENG block appears to have effective analgesic performance with adequate safety profile.

In fact, previous reports had demonstrated the analgesic effectiveness of both techniques. The case series of Küpeli and Yazici Kara [4] demonstrated that the PENG block can be effectively and safely applied under ultrasound guidance in selected shoulder surgery cases for postoperative analgesia. Also, Karaman et al. [12] study on 62 patients scheduled for arthroscopic shoulder surgery found that supraclavicular and ISB blocks had comparable postoperative pain scores, timing of first analgesic requirement, additional analgesic requirements, procedure time and time to onset of sensory blockade. However, ISB block was associated with more complications as Horner’s syndrome.

The relation between ISB block and such complications was also highlighted by the meta-analysis of White et al. [13] that compared the analgesic effectiveness and complications rates for anterior suprascapular nerve block compared to ISB block for arthroscopic and outpatient shoulder surgery and revealed that use of the ISB block resulted in an increase in unintended blocking of other nerves as demonstrated by significantly higher rates of Horner’s syndrome, hoarseness of voice and impaired respiratory function tests. Horner’s syndrome can occur as a result of paralysis of the ipsilateral sympathetic cervical chain by the local anesthetic [14, 15]. Most of the studies that have compared ISB with supraclavicular blocks reported that Horner’s syndrome developed more frequently in ISB block [16]. Similar findings were noted in studies comparing ISB block to other diaphragm sparing blocks e.g. suprascapular block [17].

The findings of our study appear to contrast with those reported by Çam et al. [18] in a recent randomized controlled trial comparing interscalene block (ISB) with an anterior shoulder capsule block. In that study, the authors demonstrated significantly superior analgesia with ISB across all measured postoperative time points (1–24 h), concluding that the anterior capsule approach is inadequate as a standalone regional technique. These results differ from our observations, where the anterior approach provided comparable analgesia under the study conditions. Important methodological and clinical factors may explain this discrepancy.

First, differences in local anesthetic dosing and concentration likely played a pivotal role. Çam et al. [18] utilized relatively high volumes and concentrations (20 mL of 0.5% bupivacaine for both ISB and anterior capsule block), which would favor broader neural spread and more consistent blockade—particularly for ISB, where dense coverage of the brachial plexus roots is expected. In contrast, our study employed lower concentrations and/or volumes, which may have attenuated the relative advantage of ISB while still allowing the anterior approach to provide sufficient capsular analgesia. Reduced anesthetic spread in ISB could diminish its superiority, especially in less extensive surgical trauma settings. Of note, use of lower concentrations is in line with the recent trends of using lower concentrations of analgesic agents to reduce the related side effects. One randomized study showed that both 10 and 20 ml of levobupivacaine 0.25% had comparable pain scores and rescue analgesia requirements with better hemi-diaphragmatic function in the lower dose [19].

Second, postoperative analgesic strategy differed substantially between studies. The trial by Çam et al. [18] incorporated a more comprehensive multimodal analgesia regimen, enhancing baseline pain control and potentially accentuating differences between regional techniques. In contrast, our protocol relied primarily on rescue pethidine without a structured multimodal regimen. This approach may have increased variability in pain perception and analgesic consumption, thereby reducing the detectable superiority of ISB and allowing the anterior approach to appear comparably effective in clinical practice.

Results of the present study should be cautiously interpreted. In fact, patients in the PENG group had noticeably larger total 24 h pethidine dose as compared to their counterparts in the ISB group. While the difference wasn’t statistically significant, this may have meaningful clinical implications. It is important to note that this finding is consistent with the expected mechanistic differences between the two techniques. The ISB provides dense proximal blockade of the brachial plexus, including reliable involvement of the suprascapular nerve, resulting in more comprehensive shoulder analgesia. In contrast, the modified PENG approach is a distal fascial plane technique that primarily affects accessible anterior and periarticular articular branches, and does not consistently influence posterior contributors to shoulder innervation. Accordingly, the slightly higher opioid requirement observed in the PENG group may reflect a lower overall analgesic density rather than a lack of efficacy per se.

Conclusions

The present study concludes that the ultrasound-guided PENG block appears to provide effective management of postoperative pain following shoulder arthroscopy. Importantly, the PENG block offers this potential benefit with adequate safety profile.

Limitations

These conclusions may be limited by the relatively small sample size of the study groups, raising the possibility of a Type II error, as well as by the single-center design, which may limit generalizability. Also, ultrasound or spirometric confirmation wasn’t used to exclude phrenic nerve palsy. However, this approach reflects common clinical practice and allowed for consistent assessment across all patients. Moreover, it should be emphasized that the anatomical and clinical rationale for the use of the PENG block in shoulder analgesia is still evolving and not yet as well established as for other regional techniques. There is a need for further anatomical and adequately powered clinical studies to clarify its role. Importantly, anterior approach misses the suprascapular nerve, which is responsible for providing up to 70% of the sensory innervation to the shoulder joint. However, it should be noted that the present study introduces a selective anterior pericapsular approach as a potential phrenic-sparing alternative for shoulder analgesia. While it does not aim to replicate the dense and comprehensive blockade achieved with interscalene techniques, the observed clinical outcomes suggest that modulation of accessible anterior and periarticular sensory inputs may be sufficient to achieve acceptable postoperative analgesia in selected patients. These findings may support the concept of functionally targeted analgesia rather than complete neural blockade. Notably, multimodal analgesia was lacking in the present study. For research purposes, our study design intentionally aimed to isolate the analgesic effect of the investigated blocks as much as possible, minimizing potential confounding from additional analgesic modalities. While performing both blocks after induction of general anesthesia precludes pre-incision sensory testing, it reflects real-world clinical performance rather than idealized block efficacy.
