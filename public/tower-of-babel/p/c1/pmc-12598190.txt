Prevalence and Correlates of Hyperglycemia Among People Living With HIV and TB on Dolutegravir‐Based Antiretroviral Therapy in Zimbabwe: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Tafadzwa Zireva, Cuthbert Musarurwa, Arthur Mazhandu, Brenda Nherera, Shungu Munyati, Justen Manasa, Danai Tavonga Zhou, Donald Vhanda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsIn low‐ and middle‐income countries, dolutegravir‐based antiretroviral therapy regimens are the preferred first‐line treatment for adults, adolescents, and children with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). Dolutegravir exhibits a higher genetic barrier to resistance, improved tolerability, and reduced potential for drug‐drug interactions. However, emerging reports suggest a possible association between dolutegravir usage and hyperglycemia. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the hyperglycemia risk in dolutegravir‐based antiretroviral therapy among people living with HIV and tuberculosis (TB) in Zimbabwe.MethodsAn analytical cross‐sectional study was conducted on 162 participants aged 18 and older, recruited from April to July 2024 in Harare, Zimbabwe. Participants were divided into three groups based on their HIV and TB status and dolutegravir dosage. Participants' glycated hemoglobin levels were analyzed to determine the hyperglycaemic risk. A questionnaire was also administered to assess the risk factors associated with hyperglycemia. The R statistical software (version 4.3.2, Vienna, Austria) was used for data analysis. A p‐value of < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.ResultsThe median (interquartile range) ages for these sub‐groups were 44 [36–56], 44 (29.3–54.8), and 45 [35–56] years, respectively, and the age range was 20‐80 years. The group taking 100 mg of dolutegravir had a 40% prevalence of impaired glucose regulation, and a hyperglycemia prevalence of 31%. In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, taking 100 mg of dolutegravir was associated with a 5.17 (95% Confidence Interval: 1.21–27.82) fold risk of developing hyperglycemia.ConclusionThe study findings indicate that the prevalence of hyperglycemia and impaired glucose regulation is high in patients taking dolutegravir‐based antiretroviral therapy in Zimbabwe. People living with both HIV and TB taking a double dose of dolutegravir are at a higher risk of hyperglycemia and impaired glucose regulation than those taking lower doses. This emphasizes the necessity for clinical and public health interventions to mitigate this emerging hyperglycaemic risk.

Introduction

In low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMIC), dolutegravir (DTG)‐based antiretroviral therapy (ART) regimens are the preferred first‐line treatment for adults, adolescents, and children living with the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) [1, 2]. Dolutegravir, a second‐generation integrase strand transfer inhibitor, exhibits a higher genetic barrier to resistance, improved tolerability, and a reduced potential for drug‐drug interactions [3, 4, 5]. However, emerging reports suggest a possible association between DTG use and hyperglycemia [6, 7, 8, 9].

A retrospective cohort study by Duga et al. (2022) found that the incidence of hyperglycemia was higher in patients receiving DTG‐based ART compared to those on other ART regimens [10]. The study reported a 1.5‐fold increased risk of hyperglycemia among DTG users [10]. In a survey by Namara et al. (2022), patients with prior DTG use had seven times greater odds of a subsequent diagnosis of hyperglycemia compared to those who had been on non‐DTG‐based regimens. The study further demonstrated a strong association between prior DTG exposure and the subsequent diagnosis of hyperglycemia [6].

The African region, with its disproportionately high burden of HIV, tuberculosis (TB) and diabetes mellitus (DM), necessitates a comprehensive and nuanced assessment of hyperglycemia risk across diverse programmatic settings [11]. When co‐administered with rifampicin‐containing anti‐TB treatment, the standard dose of DTG is increased to counteract the reducing effect of rifampicin on DTG plasma concentrations, thereby ensuring therapeutic efficacy, but potentially elevating the risk of hyperglycemia [12]. Furthermore, HIV and TB are independent risk factors for hyperglycemia, likely due to chronic inflammation, immune dysregulation, and cytokine‐mediated insulin resistance associated with both conditions. This is particularly concerning, as hyperglycemia can, in turn, increase the risk of TB threefold, perpetuating a vicious cycle of disease progression and complicating patient management [13, 14].

Africa faces a substantial DM burden, with 54% of cases undiagnosed, affecting millions. Limited healthcare resources, inadequate screening, and insufficient awareness exacerbate this issue, leading to poor health outcomes, increased morbidity, and mortality [10]. The global prevalence of impaired glucose regulation (IGR), also known as “pre‐diabetes,” is alarmingly high and escalating, with millions of individuals at risk of progressing to type 2 DM [13]. Impaired glucose regulation is defined by plasma glucose levels that fall within an intermediate range, exceeding normal thresholds but not meeting the diagnostic criteria for DM. Individuals with IGR are at elevated risk of progressing to DM, underscoring the importance of early detection and intervention [15].

Hyperglycemia can occur in individuals with DM, pre‐diabetes, or those experiencing stress, illness, or taking certain medications [16]. Early detection and management of IGR can prevent or delay progression to DM. Furthermore, patients with DM are disproportionately susceptible to infections, and hyperglycemia during infectious disease episodes can predict increased morbidity and mortality, underscoring the critical importance of effective glucose management in mitigating adverse outcomes [17, 18].

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends monitoring ART across different programmatic settings, and targeted screening for non‐communicable diseases, including hyperglycemia, is essential in Africa [19]. Despite the increasing burden of hyperglycemia, there is a paucity of information on the relationship between DTG usage and hyperglycemia, particularly among people living with HIV and TB. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the hyperglycemia risk in DTG‐based ART among people living with HIV and TB in Zimbabwe.

Methods

Study Design, Population, and Setting

This analytical cross‐sectional study was conducted on participants visiting the Parirenyatwa Group of Hospitals (PGH) Opportunistic Infections Clinic (OIC), and Beatrice Road Infectious Diseases Hospital (BRIDH) in Harare, Zimbabwe. Data collection was done from 16 April 2024 to 4 July 2024.

Consenting and eligible participants were stratified into three groups based on their HIV and TB status and associated DTG dosages as follows:

Group 1: HIV‐negative, TB‐positive, and DTG‐naive patients.

Group 2: HIV‐positive, TB‐negative, and on 50 mg DTG patients.

Group 3: HIV‐positive, TB‐positive, and on 100 mg DTG patients.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Consenting and consecutive study participants aged 18 years or older, who consecutively presented at the PGH OIC and BRIDH, were enrolled. Eligible participants were also required to meet the following criteria: HIV‐negative and TB positive, HIV‐positive and TB negative, and HIV‐positive and TB positive. For those who were HIV‐positive, a minimum of 6 months on DTG therapy, and a willingness to provide blood samples for analysis. The following patients were excluded from the study: those who were breastfeeding, pregnant, critically ill, known anemic, patients with kidney disease, those who had a pre‐existing diagnosis of DM, were taking psychiatric medications, or were using medications known to interact with DTG, including antacids containing magnesium and aluminum, and certain anticonvulsants (phenytoin, phenobarbital, and carbamazepine).

Sample Size Determination

Using the Cochran's formula, the sample size for the study was calculated taking into account the following parameters: Confidence Interval (CI) = 95%, prevalence of persistent hyperglycemia in HIV/TB coinfection (p) = 11.9% (0.119), Z‐score = 1.96 and a margin of error = 5% (E = 0.05). Cochran's is widely employed in health research for determining appropriate sample sizes when estimating proportions. The formula is expressed as: n = (Z2 * p * (1–p))/E2. A total sample size of 162 was obtained from this calculation.

A combination of random sampling and stratified sampling was employed. Random sampling ensured that each HIV/TB‐positive adult seeking treatment at the study recruitment sites had an equal chance of being enrolled into the study. Stratified sampling was used to allocate the population into subgroups. Within each stratum, random sampling was employed to select participants, ensuring representation from different groups and allowing for the investigation of specific associations and risk factors. Thus, the total sample size of 162 study participants was recruited into the three subgroups detailed above.

Data Collection Procedure

The data collection procedure involved the completion of an interviewer‐administered questionnaire. Following the study participants' informed consent, researchers collected socio‐demographic data such as marital status, educational attainment, occupational status, level of physical activity, and family history of DM. Information about the duration of HIV and TB, current ART and TB regimens and duration of treatment was obtained from the patients' medical records. Furthermore, data on anthropometric parameters were collected from the study participants by clinical nurses under the supervision of the investigators. Weight was measured in kilograms and height in meters using a nonautomatic weighing device with a height rod (Model: Micro M301; Sasco Africa, Benoni, South Africa). BMI was calculated as: weight/height2. The BMI was classified based on the WHO criteria [20]: Underweight: < 18.5 kg/m²Normal: 18.5–24.99 kg/m²Overweight: 25–29.9 kg/m²Obese: ≥ 30 kg/m²

The blood sample (3 milliliters) was drawn by venipuncture from the consenting participant into a purple top tube containing tri‐potassium ethylene diamine tetra acetic acid (EDTA). The blood samples were stored at 2–8°C and transported to the laboratory for testing. The study assessed glycaemic risk in study participants using glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c). Measurements of HbA1c levels were done on the Snibe automated immunoassay analyzer (Shenzhen New Industries Biomedical Engineering Co. Ltd, Shenzhen, China) [21]. The assays were carried out according to the manufacturer's protocol and following good clinical laboratory practice principles.

Data Management and Analysis

Data were checked for completeness and consistency in the hard copy format and double‐entered into a password‐protected Excel database before being exported to R statistical software (version 4.3.2, Vienna, Austria https://www.R-project.org/).

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics. Continuous variables were described using median and interquartile range (IQR), while categorical variables were described using frequency and proportions with 95% CIs.

The Shapiro‐Wilk test was used to determine whether the data were approximately normally distributed. The Kruskal‐Wallis test and Mann–Whitney U‐test were used to test differences between medians among the participants stratified according to DTG dosage status. Fisher's exact test was used to assess the association between categorical variables among the DTG sub‐groups [22].

Regression analysis was conducted both with and without parameter elimination to determine predictors of hyperglycemia. The Akaike Information Criterion was employed for parameter elimination. The Spearman correlation test was used to assess the strength and direction of the association between HbA1c and DTG dosage [23]. A p‐value of < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Ethical Approval

Ethical approval was also obtained from the Medical Research Council of Zimbabwe (Approval number: MRCZ/B/2709). Each participant gave written informed consent before enrollment and was given full rights to continue or withdraw from the study. Participant data were anonymised by assignment of unique participant identifiers.

Discussion

Our study determined the risk of hyperglycemia in patients receiving DTG‐based ART in Harare, Zimbabwe. The prevalence of IGR in people living with both HIV and TB who were receiving 100 mg of DTG daily was 40% (95% CI: 27.1–52.9), while the prevalence of hyperglycemia was 31% (95% CI: 18.7–43.2). In the multivariable logistic regression analysis with variable elimination, taking 100 mg of DTG was associated with over a five‐fold increased risk of developing hyperglycemia (Table 3).

Our findings are consistent with other studies that reported a higher hyperglycemia prevalence of 15%–30% in sub‐Saharan Africa, where both HIV and TB are endemic [24, 25]. Jemal et al. (2023) reported a hyperglycemia prevalence of 17.2% in DTG use [26]. This finding was higher than that observed in this current study, which found a hyperglycemia prevalence of 14.5% in single‐dose DTG intake. Variations in prevalence may be influenced by differences in geographical regions, genetic factors, and DTG dosages across settings.

Other studies have documented DM rates ranging from 17% to 35% in people living with HIV and TB, which are comparable to the findings of this study [24, 25]. Similarly, studies from diverse geographic regions support our findings. In high‐burden TB regions such as India and Southeast Asia, the prevalence of DM among people living with HIV and TB has been reported to range between 19% and 36% [27, 28]. Findings by Waritu et al. (2024) align with our study, reporting a 35.9% prevalence of hyperglycemia in DTG‐based ART usage. Body mass index and DTG‐based ART were identified as the determinants of hyperglycemia [9].

The prevalence of IGR, often referred to as pre‐diabetes, in the group taking 100 mg of DTG daily was 40% (95% CI: 27.1‐52.9) in the current study. Impaired glucose regulation is characterized by an abnormally elevated plasma glucose level that remains below the diabetic threshold [13]. Tesfaye et al. (2018), in a study conducted in Ethiopia, reported an IGR prevalence of 32% [29]. Among individuals acquiring both HIV and TB, IGR prevalence has been reported to range between 30% and 45% and the findings of the current study fall within that range [6, 25, 30]. However, a lower IGR prevalence of 25% was reported by Tamuhla et al. (2021) in a South African study [31]. In Asia, where TB is also endemic, studies have reported lower IGR prevalence rates ranging from 20% to 30% in HIV and TB coinfected populations, with some studies indicating higher prevalence among individuals on ART for longer durations [16, 27].

Contrary to our findings, some previous studies have reported lower prevalences of hyperglycemia and IGR. Byereta et al. (2024) in a cross‐sectional study conducted in Uganda reported a hyperglycemia prevalence of 12.8% and a pre‐diabetes prevalence of 10.3% [7]. Variations in reported prevalence rates may be attributed to differences in geographical regions, the burden of HIV and TB, programmatic approaches to the comanagement of HIV and TB, and possible genetic variances.

Table 3 shows that a daily DTG dosage of 100 mg was a statistically significant predictor of hyperglycemia, with participants on that dose having over five times greater odds of developing hyperglycemia compared to those who were DTG naive. This finding aligns with a study by Namara et al. (2022) in which individuals on DTG‐based ART had seven times greater odds of developing hyperglycemia than those on non‐DTG ART regimens [6]. Consistent with the current study, Berkowitz et al. (2018) reported that individuals coinfected with TB and HIV who were receiving rifampicin and a double dose of DTG were twice as likely to be hyperglycaemic compared to their mono‐infected (HIV only or TB only) counterparts on treatment [32]. Other reports suggest that prolonged exposure to DTG may disrupt glucose metabolism, although the specific mechanisms remain unclear [10, 20]. Some studies have hypothesized that magnesium ion chelation by DTG leads to hypomagnesaemia, which may contribute to the dysregulation of glucose homeostasis [10, 33].

Risk Factors for Hyperglycemia

Consistent with previous studies by McLaughlin et al., and Lamorde et al., (2020), DTG dosage and lack of awareness of a familial history of DM emerged as statistically significant risk factors for the development of hyperglycemia [33, 34]. Contrary to findings from a study by Bosch et al (2023) [20], the duration of DTG treatment was not a significant predictor of hyperglycemia in this current study [20].

The body mass index was not a significant predictor of hyperglycemia, even though over 50% of the study participants were classified as either obese or overweight according to WHO criteria. This finding contrasts with previous studies by Masuku et al. (2019) in which BMI was identified as a significant predictor of hyperglycemia [35]. However, findings from a study by Namara et al. (2022) were consistent with the current research, showing that BMI was not significantly associated with the risk of hyperglycemia in multivariable regression analysis after controlling for other factors [6].

The limitations of BMI may explain such contrasting findings in its ability to accurately reflect body composition and fat distribution. The metric does not differentiate between fat, muscle, and bone mass. Therefore, BMI may not be an ideal predictor of hyperglycemia, as factors beyond weight alone influence glucose regulation [36]. There was also no significant difference in the distribution of participants by gender across the three groups. This finding aligns with studies by Zvinoera et al. (2021) and Gwitira et al. (2021), who similarly reported no significant gender‐based difference in the distribution of participants across HIV and TB acquiring cohorts [37, 38].

Zimbabwe has made significant progress in implementing the WHO‐recommended transition to DTG‐based therapy for people living with HIV. However, there remains a need for close monitoring of patients with comorbidities to manage unintended consequences that may increase the risk of non‐communicable diseases. Coinfections are associated with drug‐drug interactions and an elevated risk of drug toxicity. These study findings should prompt further research to elucidate the potential causes of the observed associations between DTG use and hyperglycemia to inform context‐specific interventions that enhance treatment outcomes.

Strengths of the Study

The study has demonstrated a high prevalence of hyperglycemia associated with DTG‐based therapy use in Zimbabwe, in a context with a high HIV and TB burden. The study, therefore, provides useful baseline data for future studies. The study also offered a comparison between three groups receiving different levels of DTG, and the groups were fairly constituted, making comparisons easier and more meaningful.

Limitations of the Study

Our study has some limitations, including its cross‐sectional nature, which precludes the drawing of causal associations between study variables. In addition, the study participants were restricted to those aged 18 years and above and were recruited from two centers in Harare, the capital city of Zimbabwe, both of which are referral and teaching hospitals. These limitations may curtail the generalizability of the findings. Larger sample sizes may yield more precise estimates and further validate our results. Comparing DTG‐based regimens with those not containing DTG regimens could also provide additional insights.

Conclusion and Recommendations

The study findings highlight a high prevalence of hyperglycemia and IGR among patients receiving DTG‐based ART in Zimbabwe, with individuals on higher DTG doses, particularly those living with both HIV and TB, exhibiting a greater risk of developing hyperglycemia and IGR. These findings underscore the urgent need for comprehensive clinical and public health interventions to address this emerging metabolic risk. Policymakers from Zimbabwe and other countries with similar contexts of DTG‐based therapy are encouraged to develop and implement context‐specific screening programmes to inform tailored interventions that reduce the hyperglycaemic risk among individuals on DTG therapy. Such programs should prioritize enhanced screening and management of hyperglycemia, especially before patients progress from prediabetes to the DM stage. Finally, given the observed association between DTG dosing and hyperglycemia, we recommend conducting further longitudinal studies to elucidate the temporal relationship and potential causality between DTG exposure and the development of hyperglycemia.
