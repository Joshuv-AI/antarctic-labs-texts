Trabecular Bone Score and Bone Turnover Markers Unmask Early Skeletal Fragility in Children and Adolescents With Type 1 Diabetes
Authors: Vinod Ullatil, Piyas Gargari, Mainak Banerjee, Pradip Mukhopadhyay, Sujoy Ghosh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCompromised bone health is a recognized consequence of type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM), particularly in adults. Studies that comprehensively assess dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry parameters, trabecular bone score (TBS), and bone turnover markers (BTMs) are scarce in children and adolescents with T1DM. Additionally, the relationship between bone health and corneal nerve abnormalities assessed by corneal confocal microscopy, a surrogate of diabetic microangiopathy, remains unexplored.MethodsFifty children/adolescents with T1DM and 46 healthy siblings (aged 11-18 years) were enrolled. Participants underwent assessment for relevant biochemical parameters, including 25-hydroxyvitamin D, and dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry for bone mineral content, bone mineral density (BMD) Z-score, total body less head Z-score, TBS, and BTMs, including osteocalcin, procollagen type 1 N-terminal propeptide, and C-terminal telopeptides. Multivariable general linear models adjusted for age, sex, pubertal status (Tanner stage ≥ II), body mass index Z-score, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and 25-hydroxyvitamin D assessed group differences.ResultsBone mineral content, BMD Z-score, total body less head Z-score, and height-adjusted BMD were similar between groups, but TBS was significantly lower in T1DM vs controls (adjusted mean 1.37 [95%CI 1.35–1.39] vs 1.41 [95%CI 1.39–1.43]; P = 0.011) even after multivariable adjustment. Likewise, osteocalcin (P < 0.001) and C-terminal telopeptides (P = 0.013) were significantly reduced in T1DM vs controls, while procollagen type 1 N-terminal propeptide showed a nonsignificant downward trend (P = 0.086). Impaired TBS correlated with longer diabetes duration, poorer glycemic control, and corneal confocal microscopy abnormalities on exploratory analysis.ConclusionBone microarchitecture and turnover are compromised early in T1DM, preceding detectable changes in conventional BMD. Incorporating TBS and BTMs into bone health assessments may improve early identification of skeletal fragility in this population.

Clinical Relevance

Routine evaluation of bone microarchitecture using trabecular bone score alongside bone turnover markerss should be integrated into clinical care of pediatric type 1 diabetes mellitus to enable timely interventions aimed at fracture risk reduction.

Introduction

Alterations in skeletal health represent a significant microvascular complication of type 1 diabetes (T1DM), demanding increased scrutiny, particularly within the pediatric and adolescent cohorts. Strong epidemiological evidence indicates that a long duration of diabetes and suboptimal glycemic control serve as independent risk factors for fragility fractures in T1DM.1 This bone fragility occurs not only due to compromised areal bone mineral density (aBMD) and diminished mineralization, but also from structural defects in skeletal microarchitecture and perturbations in dynamic bone turnover.2, 3, 4

The evidence of reduced BMD is well-established in adults with long-standing T1DM,5, 6, 7 while dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA)-based aBMD measurements in children and adolescents have yielded conflicting results. This discrepancy is often attributed to the inherent limitation of aBMD as a 2-dimensional measure that fails to account for three-dimensional bone size and geometry in growing skeletons,8 necessitating the use of size-adjustment metrics like bone mineral apparent density (BMAD) or BMDZHAZ.9 Furthermore, T1DM is associated with abnormal bone turnover, typically marked by suppressed bone formation, evident through low serum osteocalcin.10 Given that bone strength depends fundamentally on its quality, the trabecular bone score (TBS), a validated index of lumbar spine microarchitecture derivable from standard DXA scans, presents a highly relevant, non-invasive tool.11 TBS’s ability to assess bone quality independently of size makes it crucial for evaluating fracture risk in the young population, even when aBMD is preserved.12 While low bone turnover state is a recognized entity in the adult population with diabetes, studies providing a comprehensive and simultaneous assessment of microarchitecture (TBS), size-adjusted BMD (BMDZHAZ), and BTMs in children with T1DM are scarce.

Therefore, the primary objective of this cross-sectional study was to evaluate the early effects of T1DM by comparing TBS, DXA parameters, and BTMs between children and adolescents with T1DM and their healthy siblings. We also aimed to explore the relationship between compromised bone parameters and early diabetic complications, specifically using corneal confocal microscopy (CCM) as a sensitive marker of diabetic neuropathy,13,14 to provide insight into the potential interplay between microangiopathy and skeletal health.

Methods

Participant Selection

This was a single tertiary care center, hospital-based cross-sectional study. After obtaining the necessary ethical approval from the institutional ethics committee (Memo No: Inst/IEC/2018/383), children and adolescents aged 11 to 18 years with T1DM attending the “type 1 diabetes clinic” were recruited consecutively.

The diagnosis of T1DM was based on the diagnostic criteria of diabetes proposed by the American Diabetes Association (2018), with documentation of insulinopenia (low C-peptide level in a euglycemic patient on insulin therapy and following a standard protocol) with or without antibody positivity (Anti GAD65, IA-2, ICA, ZnT8 antibodies) at presentation or diagnosis.

Siblings of the study participants of a similar age range were invited to participate voluntarily and were included in the control/comparator group. We chose siblings as controls to mitigate the potential influence of shared genetic and environmental factors on bone metabolism. Informed consent/assent from the participants and/or informed consent from the legal guardians (where applicable) were obtained after a detailed explanation of the study protocol.

Participants suffering from any bone disease or systemic illnesses, or those on drugs/conditions known to affect bone health (eg, smoking, celiac disease, chronic kidney, or liver disease), or those unwilling to give consent/assent were excluded from the study. All participants had a complete set of measurements done for the outcomes reported in the tables.

Clinical Assessment

Upon recruitment, relevant clinical history was recorded for all participants. Height was measured using a wall-mounted Harpenden stadiometer with a fixed footplate and movable wooden headboard (precision up to 1 mm). Weight was recorded using an electronic weighing machine (precision of 10 gms).

Pubertal stage was assessed by an experienced endocrinologist according to Tanner and Marshall. Tanner stage 2 was considered the cut-off for classifying the prepubertal and pubertal groups. The development of breast buds in females and a testicular volume of 4 mL or more in males were categorized as Tanner stage 2. Participants were considered to have attained puberty if they had reached Tanner stage II or above (peri- or postpubertal stages).

HbA1c was measured by high-performance liquid chromatography using the BioRad D10 platform. While the bone assessment was cross-sectional, we used a mean cumulative HbA1c from past HbA1c records of up to 3 years to better represent chronic glycemic exposure rather than a single point in time. The HbA1c of the T1DM participants was assessed at a 6-monthly interval for a duration of up to 3 years (47% of the T1DM group had a duration less than 3 years, necessitating the modification of the assessment period) followed by averaging their results to express mean cumulative HbA1c. The HbA1c of the control cohort was recorded at the entry-point of the study. Urine albumin creatinine ratio was performed at an interval of 3 months. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the CKD-EPI equation. Retinopathy status was screened using digital fundal photography (Topcon nonmydriatic camera) and graded using the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) classification.15

Laser scanning corneal confocal microscopy (CCM) was performed by trained personnel blinded to the T1DM diagnosis. The central sub-basal plexus was analyzed using the Rostock Cornea module of the Heidelberg Tomograph III (Heidelberg Engineering, Smithfield, RI) as per standard protocol. Images were analyzed using custom-designed manual nerve analysis software (CCMetrics Image Analysis Tools software version 1.1) as per protocol. Corneal nerve fiber length (CNFL) in mm/mm2, corneal nerve fiber density (CNFD) in number/mm2, and corneal nerve branch density (CNBD) in number/mm2 were evaluated as an average value from 6 images (3 from each eye).

Laboratory Analysis

Serum samples were collected in a fasting state in the morning for: serum total calcium, inorganic phosphate, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), intact parathormone, 25-hydroxy vitamin D (25-OHD), as well as BTMs including Osteocalcin, PINP, and CTX.

Calcium, phosphate, and ALP were assessed with an auto-analyzer. 25-OHD was assessed by electrochemiluminiscence (Roche Cat# 09038078, RRID: AB_2909604) using the Roche Cobas e411 platform (measuring range of 3-100 ng/mL), as was insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) (measuring range: 7-1600 ng/mL). Osteocalcin (Roche Cat#12149133122, RRID: AB_2915903), PINP (Roche Cat#03141071190, RRID: AB_2782967), and CTX (Roche Cat# 12149133122, RRID: AB_2915903) were also assessed by ECLIA on the Roche Cobas e411 platform. The lower detection limit of osteocalcin was 0.5 ng/mL, upper detection limit 300 ng/m, and coefficient of variation (CV) 2.0%. For P1NP, lower detection limit was 5 μg/L, upper detection limit 1200 μg/L, and CV 4.1%. The lower detection limit of CTX was 0.01 ng/mL, upper limit 6.0 ng/mL, and CV 5.7%.

Bone Densitometry

Participants underwent bone mineral density assessment using a GE Lunar Prodigy Advance 2000 DXA machine with pediatric BMD assessment software and the BMDCS (Bone Mineral Density in Childhood Study) reference data. The scans were performed by a single operator with a CV for the lumbar spine and total body of 1.2% to minimize investigator bias.7 BMD was assessed at the L1-L4 lumbar vertebrae and total body less head (TBLH) according to ISCD position statements.16

Height adjustment for BMD was performed as per the ALPHABET study protocol, and BMAD for the lumbar spine was calculated for each participant.17 BMAD was calculated by dividing the sum of the bone mineral content (BMC) of L1-4 vertebrae by the sum of their volumes, as defined in the aforementioned study: BMAD=L1BMC+L2BMC+L3BMC+L4BMCL1V+L2V+L3V+L4V, where V = volume of the individual vertebrae and BMC= Bone Mineral content. Volume (V) of individual vertebrae is calculated by raising area of individual vertebra to 1.5 ie L1V=L1A1.5, where A is the area of the vertebra. Z-score was calculated by subtracting obtained BMD from mean bone mineral density of same age and sex and dividing by standard deviation (using the combined NHANES/Lunar reference population database). BMD Z-score height adjusted (BMDZHAZ) was calculated from height Z-score (HAZ) and BMD from each participant.18

Trabecular bone score (TBS) analysis for the L1-4 vertebra was performed using a research trial version of TBS iNsight software (GE HealthCare; Medimaps group, Geneva). TBS was classified into 3 groups: degraded (TBS≤1.2), partially degraded (TBS >1.2 to <1.35) and normal (TBS≥1.35).Based on the data from Kalkwarf et al derived from BMDCS study, TBS Z scores were calculated by subtracting individual data points from median and dividing it by standard deviation.19

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analyses were carried out using IBM SPSS software version 21.0 (IBM). The sample size was calculated based on a previous study by Krishnan et al, taking a mean difference of TBS as 0.05 and SD as 0.08, which yielded a required sample size of 40 in each arm.20

Continuous variables were assessed for normality in distribution using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Variables with a normal distribution were compared using the independent sample t test or one-way ANOVA as applicable. Variables not showing normal distribution were compared using Mann–Whitney test or Kruskal–Wallis test as applicable. Categorical variables were expressed as proportions and analyzed with the chi-square test. Multivariable general linear models compared group differences between T1D and controls, adjusting for age, sex, pubertal status (Tanner stage ≥ II), BMI Z-score, eGFR, and 25-OHD levels. Adjusted group means and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using estimated marginal means with least significant difference adjustment. Model significance was assessed via type III F-tests, that is, partial sums of squares test. Model fit was assessed via R2 diagnostics.

Results

A total of 50 participants with T1DM, aged between 11 and 18 years of age, were recruited in this study. Forty-six siblings of the participants of same age range were included as controls. While the majority (n = 41) were direct siblings of the T1DM cohort to control for shared genetic and environmental factors, 5 were age and sex-matched healthy peers from similar socio-economic backgrounds.

Median age was 13 years (IQR 12-15) for the control group and 13 years (IQR 11-15) for the T1DM group, respectively. Out of 50 subjects with T1DM, 19 (38%) were males and 31 (62%) were females. Out of 46 controls, 25 were males (58.7%) and 21 were females (41.3%). 80% of the T1DM group and 71% of the control had attained puberty i.e. Tanner stage 2. Diabetes duration of the T1D patients was 6.1 ± 2.9 years (minimum 1 year, maximum 12 years). Mean HbA1c of T1DM and control subjects were 9.1% and 4.7%, respectively. The BTM levels (osteocalcin, PINP, CTX) were significantly lower in T1DM patients compared to controls, as detailed in Table 2. However, after multivariable adjustment, P1NP showed a minor trend toward lower levels in T1DM vs controls (404.6 [332.9-476.3] μg/L vs 498.0 [421.2-574.8]; difference −93.4 [-200.2 to 13.5], F = 3.020, P = 0.086); whereas osteocalcin was lower in T1DM (adjusted mean 58.2 [48.6-67.7] ng/mL vs 95.8 [85.6-106.0]; difference −37.7 [−51.8 to −23.5], F = 27.878, P < 0.001). CTX was significantly lower in T1DM (1.24 [1.08-1.40] ng/mL) vs controls (1.54 [1.37-1.71]; difference −0.30 [−0.53 to −0.06], F = 6.424, P = 0.013).

The impact of glycemic status (HbA1c) on bone mineral content and BMD Z-Score at L1-L4 and TBLH, TBS and bone turnover markers in subjects with T1DM has been presented in Supplementary Table 1. Individuals with impaired bone microarchitecture i.e. TBS <1.35 (n = 17) had higher HbA1c (9.8 ± 1.7%) compared to those with normal architecture i.e. TBS ≥1.35 (n = 33, 8.7 ± 1.5%) (P = 0.03).

We also found that in addition to the BMD Z-score at L1-L4 and TBLH, TBS and bone turnover markers were significantly lower in T1DM subjects with longer diabetes duration (≥10 years). This has been presented in Supplementary Table 2.

Regarding CCM parameters, lower values of CNFD, CNFL and CNBD were seen in the T1DM cohort (Table 1). A longer duration of diabetes (≥10 years) was associated with lower values of CNFD, CNBD and CNFL. Similarly, higher cumulative mean HbA1c (≥7.5%) was associated with lower CNFD and CNFL. It was also found that the subjects with impaired TBS in T1DM had lower CNFL and CNFD in comparison to those with normal TBS. This is summarized in Supplementary Table 3. Mild background retinopathy was seen in 2 patients with T1DM; hence, the numbers were too small for any further analysis.

While exploring the relationship of bone parameters with 25-OHD level, we found that L1-4 BMD Z score, TBLH Z score and L1-4 TBS were significantly lower in those with 25-OHD deficiency (25OHD <30 ng/mL) compared to 25-OHD sufficiency (25OHD ≥30 ng/ml). This has been summarized in Supplementary Table 4.

Discussion

Osteopenia, typically manifested by low BMD and BMC, along with decreased bone quality and strength, is a recognized entity in T1DM.21,22 Previous studies have reported diminished bone density at the hip, femoral neck, and spine in adult males with T1DM compared to age-matched controls. However, in our cohort of children and adolescents, we did not find any difference in BMC at L1-L4 and TBLH, or in BMD Z-score at L1-L4 and TBLH between the T1DM and control groups. When comparing bone parameters, we accounted for potential confounders such as sex, weight, pubertal status, and 25-OHD level through statistical adjustments. Similar findings of preserved BMD have been demonstrated by Pascual et al in a cohort of children and adolescents with recent-onset, non-complicated T1DM versus control subjects.23 The study by Pascual et al did not assess TBS or BTMs, and defined recent onset as duration <2 years, whereas our study includes participants with a minimum duration of 1 year and a mean duration of 6.1 years. The lack of significance in BMD difference in our study, despite the longer average duration, may suggest that the effects of T1DM are predominantly on bone quality (microarchitecture and turnover) before they manifest as overt changes in aBMD Z-scores. In our study, we observed that the trabecular microarchitecture was significantly deranged in children and adolescents with T1DM, with a significantly lower TBS score, even in the group with shorter disease duration (<10 years). This effect was observed despite the absence of a significant difference in aBMD or height-adjusted BMD (BMAD/BMDZHAZ) between the 2 groups, and without significant differences in baseline demographic or anthropometric parameters, including 25-OHD status. These findings underscore the paramount importance of assessing bone microarchitecture in T1DM patients.24

While direct assessment of bone microarchitecture is possible using tools like micro-CT or high resolution peripheral quantitative computed tomography, these are costly and not widely available. In contrast, DXA-based tools like TBS offer a more widely accessible method to gain information regarding microarchitectural integrity, albeit without the high level of detail provided by 3D imaging.25 Although, the difference in TBS appears to be numerically small; in the context of TBS (which has a narrow physiological range), it represents a significant shift causing a deviation toward the impaired microarchitecture category for a substantial subset of our T1DM cohort. Furthermore, based on adult data, a 0.06 unit decrease in TBS has previously been associated with presence of fractures in T1DM.25 However, caution must be exercised when extrapolating these adult-derived fracture thresholds to pediatric and adolescent populations, as the clinical significance of specific TBS values in growing skeletons is not yet fully established. The association between the impaired trabecular microarchitecture and prevalent fractures has been observed in T1DM, even with normal 25-OHD levels.25 Similarly, Wagh et al demonstrated that TBS was significantly lower in T1DM children, around 13 years of age.26 Conversely, Thangavelu et al did not find any difference in TBS in adults with T1DM versus controls who had comparable 25-OHD levels.27

Bone turnover markers have emerged as a tool for evaluating the underlying biology of bone disease and treatment response for osteoporosis. Hamilton et al reported that a state of low bone turnover, primarily driven by impaired bone formation, may occur in adults with T1DM.5 Meta-analyses in adults have consistently shown a decrease in both bone formation and resorption markers.28,29 A meta-analysis that included over 1000 adults and children with T1DM from 26 studies concluded that the bone formation marker osteocalcin is consistently lower. Regarding CTX and PINP, the meta-analysis did not demonstrate a clear alteration. However, the nonsignificant results for these resorption markers may be attributable to a lack of statistical power due to the low number of studies included.30 Madsen et al reported that all markers of bone formation and resorption (osteocalcin, PINP, and CTX) were decreased in children and adolescents with T1DM.31 In partial agreement with the evidence of suppressed turnover, we also demonstrated that both bone formation (osteocalcin) and resorption (CTX) markers were significantly low in our T1DM cohort compared to controls, indicating an overall lower bone turnover state in T1DM and pointing towards compromised bone health.

In addition to the main findings, we were also able to demonstrate that bone mineral content, BMD Z-Score, TBS, and BTMs were lower in T1DM subjects with a longer diabetes duration (≥10 years) and with higher HbA1c (≥7.5%), in consistency with previous literature.1,32,33 In our cohort, the cumulative mean HbA1c was 9.1%, a level that is representative of the real-world challenges frequently encountered in clinical practice when managing T1DM in adolescents. This is particularly relevant in our setting, where the uptake of advanced diabetes technologies, such as insulin pumps and continuous glucose monitoring, remains suboptimal due to high out-of-pocket costs and a lack of government support. The observed range of suboptimal glycemic control facilitated a more robust analysis of the relationship between hyperglycemia and bone health, allowing us to detect the early adverse effects of suboptimal glucose control on trabecular microarchitecture. Long-term poor glycemic control is indeed hypothesized to be the underlying mechanism for poor bone health.34,35 Diabetic microvascular complications are also known to contribute to impaired bone quality and increased fracture risk in both T1DM and T2DM.28 In our study, we explored the potential correlation between diabetic complications and bone changes. Since overt diabetes complications are uncommon in children, we used CCM parameters, an emerging tool for assessing early diabetic neuropathy,36 with respect to the TBS. Though the numbers were small, we found that subjects with a lower TBS score had worse CCM findings in the T1DM group, indicating a possible relationship between diabetic microangiopathy with bone architecture.

A key strength of this study is the choice of siblings as controls for the T1DM population, which helped to mitigate the risk of genetic and shared environmental bias on the assessment of bone metabolism. Nevertheless, this research has certain limitations as well. Firstly, this is a single center study and the results might not be generalizable to all ethnic populations. Secondly, the technique used for calculating the BMDZHAZ was derived from a study that employed a Hologic DXA machine, while our study used a GE Lunar machine. It may introduce a potential discrepancy due to the different calibration and algorithms used by the manufacturers.37 However, since the same calculation technique was applied consistently to both the T1DM and sibling control groups, the methodological consistency suggests that the conclusion concerning observed differences between the groups is unlikely to be affected. Third, we focused our statistical power on TBS as it was our primary outcome of interest. While we acknowledge that formal adjustments for multiple comparisons were not applied, the consistent directionality of the results across BTMs and TBS indicates a biological pattern rather than random chance. Additionally, the secondary objectives such as the relationship with microvascular complications were exploratory and lacked sufficient statistical power. Furthermore, the bone geometry section was largely unexplored, necessitating further research in this area. Direct tools for the assessment of 3D bone microarchitecture (e.g., high resolution peripheral quantitative computed tomography) were not employed, which could have provided more detailed insight. Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes any conclusions regarding causality. A longitudinal follow-up through large-scale, multicenter studies would be able to provide more clarity on the temporal changes in bone parameters.

Conclusion

Traditional measures of bone health, such as bone mineral content and BMD/TBLH Z-scores, were preserved in children and adolescents with T1DM. However, bone microarchitecture (assessed by TBS) was significantly compromised, along with an alteration in bone turnover markers e.g. CTX and Osteocalcin, indicating a low bone-turnover state in children and adolescents with T1DM compared to healthy controls. The impairment in bone microarchitecture was also likely to be associated with other microvascular complications of diabetes, as suggested by the correlation with CCM findings. These findings suggest that adverse bone changes in T1DM occur relatively early in the natural history of the disease, even before any discernible changes manifest in traditional DXA assessment. TBS should therefore be considered as an integral part of the bone health assessment in children and adolescents with T1DM.
