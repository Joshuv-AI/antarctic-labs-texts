Recent Advances in Pyrimidine Molecular Hybrids, Green Synthetic Strategies, and Diverse Biological Applications
Authors: Mlis Belete, Yadessa Melaku, Endale Mulugeta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pyrimidine-based molecular hybrids have emerged as a privileged class of bioactive scaffolds owing to their structural versatility and broad pharmacological potential. This review comprehensively covers recent advances in green synthetic strategies, including solvent-free, microwave-assisted, ultrasound-assisted, ionic liquid-mediated, and nanocatalyst-promoted Biginelli-type reactions, synthesis, and the biological applications of pyrimidine hybrids incorporating pharmacophores such as chalcones, quinolines, triazoles, pyrazoles, and coumarins. These hybrids demonstrate potent antibacterial, antifungal, anticancer, anti-inflammatory, and antiprotozoal activities through mechanisms including kinase inhibition, apoptosis induction, COX-2 selectivity, and NF-κB/MAPK modulation. Structure–activity relationship analysis highlights the critical influence of substituent electronic properties, substitution position, and linker design on biological potency and selectivity. Current challenges, including poor solubility and metabolic instability, are discussed alongside future directions emphasizing computational drug design, target expansion, and clinical translation.

Introduction

Nitrogen-containing heterocyclic scaffolds are fundamental to the modern medicinal chemistry arena, forming the core segments of numerous pharmaceuticals and biologically active molecules. − These compounds have also gained significant attention in material and analytical chemistry. In particular, nitrogen heterocycles are widely utilized in the design of highly sensitive turn-off fluorescence probes for the detection of metal ions, anions, and biologically relevant species. , Pyrimidine (1) is a fundamental six-membered nitrogen-containing heterocyclic scaffold widely found in nucleic acids and various drugs. , The pyrimidine nucleus is an integral component of nucleic acid bases such as cytosine (2), thymine (3), and uracil (4), underscoring its essential role in genetic processes and cellular function (Figure ). , This inherent biological relevance makes pyrimidines a highly attractive scaffold for drug design.

Nitrogenous bases in DNA and RNA (2–4) and FDA-approved pyrimidine drugs (5–7).

Structurally, pyrimidine is an electron-deficient aromatic ring with two nitrogen atoms, enabling diverse substitution patterns and strong interactions with biological targets through hydrogen bonding, π-π stacking, and electrostatic forces. − These properties contribute to biological activities that exhibit a broad pharmacological spectrum, for instance, antimicrobial, antiviral, anticancer, anti-inflammatory, antimalarial, and antioxidant effects (Figure ). As a result, pyrimidine-based compounds are widely employed in the development of therapeutics across multiple disease areas.

Recent research has revealed a shift from conventional pyrimidine synthesis toward hybrid molecular design, representing a key advancement in medicinal chemistry. Molecular hybrids involve combining pyrimidine with other bioactive scaffolds to generate multifunctional derivatives for targeting multiple biological pathways simultaneously. , This strategy addresses critical challenges such as drug resistance, limited selectivity, and poor pharmacokinetics, making pyrimidine hybrids promising candidates for various diseases, including cancer, infections, neurodegenerative disorders, and inflammation. ,

Notably, pyrimidine has been successfully integrated with diverse pharmacophore scaffolds such as chalcones, triazoles, quinoline, coumarins, oxadiazole, indoles, and thiazoles. These molecular hybrid systems usually exhibit enhanced biological activities due to synergistic effects between pharmacophores (Figure ). As illustrated in Figure , these bioactive pyrimidine hybrids span major therapeutic categories such as antibacterial, conjugates benzimidazole (i), triazole (ii), and oxazolidin (iii) moieties have demonstrated improved membrane penetration and multitarget inhibitory potential, while anticancer hybrids incorporating chalcone and thiazole (iv), pyrazole (vi), and quinolone (v) interact with key oncogenic targets through hydrogen bonding and π-π stacking interactions. Antioxidant, hybrids integrating thiazo (vii), indole (viii), and pyrazole (ix) exhibited notable radical scavenging capacity attributable to electron-donating nitrogen atoms, and anti-inflammatory conjugates featuring pyridine (x), pyrazole (xi) and triazole (xii) pharmacophores have shown promising cyclooxygenase inhibitory activity, underscoring the versatility of the pyrimidine core as a privileged scaffold in drug design.

Chemical structure of bioactive pyrimidine molecular hybrids.

To prepare pyrimidine derivatives, synthetic strategies have advanced significantly, with a strong emphasis on effectiveness, sustainability, and structural diversity. Multicomponent reactions (MCRs) remain key tools for rapid scaffold construction, enabling the one-step assembly of complex molecules from three or more building blocks with high atom economy, reduced waste generation, and operational simplicity. , Modern techniques such as microwave-assisted, ultrasound irradiation, mechanochemistry, and solvent-free reaction conditions have further improved efficiency and environmental compatibility. − In parallel, ionic liquids (ILs) have emerged as efficient green catalysts and media for MCRs, owing to their tunable properties such as dual Brønsted/Lewis acidity, recyclability, negligible vapor pressure, and ability to act as both solvent and catalyst. These features enhance the sustainability and efficiency of pyrimidine synthesis, including Biginelli and related reactions. Additionally, nanoparticle-catalyzed microwave-assisted MCRs provide a powerful approach for heterocycle synthesis, offering improved reaction rates, yields, and selectivity due to the high surface area and catalytic activity of nanoparticles. Magnetic nanocatalysts further enable easy recovery and reuse, making these strategies highly aligned with green chemistry principles such as energy efficiency, waste reduction, and catalyst recyclability. −

Several review articles have addressed different facets of pyrimidine chemistry. Earlier studies primarily focused on the general pharmacological properties of pyrimidine derivatives, whereas others examined heterocyclic hybrids incorporating selected pharmacophores or systematically covered green synthetic approaches without integrating biological applications. − However, these studies lack a focused treatment of molecular hybridization strategies, do not systematically address green synthetic approaches (e.g., nanocatalysis, solvent-free, microwave-assisted, and mechanochemical methods), and fail to integrate diverse biological applications within a single framework. Moreover, recent advances reported since 2023 remain insufficiently consolidated. To the best of our knowledge, this Review is the first to comprehensively integrate pyrimidine-based molecular hybridization, green and sustainable synthesis, and diverse biological applications, with particular emphasis on literature from 2023 to 2026.

Despite these advancements, challenges such as poor solubility, metabolic instability, toxicity, and limited target specificity continue to hinder clinical translation. Future efforts should focus on rational scaffold modification and hybrid optimization with integration of computational and experimental approaches to improve therapeutic efficacy. ,

Classical Synthetic Approaches

The advantages of classical methods include operational simplicity, the precursors are readily available, and the reaction procedure can be scaled up for industrial purposes.

Modern Green Synthetic Strategies

Solvent-Free Synthesis of Pyrimidine Derivatives

Solvent-free protocols are achieved by directly combining precursors with little or no added solvent, fully adhering to the green chemistry principles. These methods minimize waste generation, lower the ecological footprint, reduce energy demands, simplify purification, and accelerate reaction kinetics through higher reactant concentrations. Also, it promotes safety, cost-effectiveness, and product selectivity and purity.

Through a one-pot solvent-free Biginelli condensation, 3,4-dihydropyrimidin-2­(1H)-ones/thiones (18) were synthesized from methyl acetoacetate (9), diverse aldehyde derivatives 15, and urea or thiourea (8) to afford 72–95% yields.

The protocol tolerates both electron-rich and electron-poor groups effectively, with minor yield drops only from ortho steric hindrance; meta/para patterns, aliphatic aldehydes (95%), and furfural (93%) performed excellently. A further sustainable approach utilizing l-arabinose, a renewable, biodegradable sugar, as an organocatalyst under neat conditions afforded pure DHPM products 18 in 77–95% yields (Scheme ).

Microwave-Assisted Synthesis of Pyrimidine Derivatives

Microwave-assisted synthesis is a rapid and sustainable approach that uses versatile precursors to afford targeted products. The rapid and uniform heating of the reaction mixture significantly reduces reaction times and energy consumption while affording good to moderate yields and improving selectivity. This method is a convenient procedure for reactions that follow solvent-free and green chemistry strategies.

Malik et al. reported a catalyst-and base-free microwave-assisted tandem Kornblum oxidation and Biginelli reaction for the synthesis of dihydropyrimidones. In this work, benzyl halide derivatives 20 are oxidized in situ with aldehyde, then immediately condensed with urea/thiourea derivatives 8 and ethyl acetoacetate (9) at 80 °C (300 W) to produce dihydropyrimidones (21) in excellent yields (Scheme ). The reaction is tolerant to and suitable for a wide variety of substituents on benzyl substrate, with both electron-donating and electron-withdrawing groups affording high yields.

Faizan et al. developed a reusable acid-functionalized mesoporous polymer (AFMP) catalyst for a solvent-efficient microwave platform. Under solvent-free conditions, aldehyde (15), ethyl acetoacetate (9), and urea (8) reacted in the presence of AFMP at 80 °C and 50W to yield 3,4-dihydropyrimidin-2­(1H)-ones in 89–98% yield (Scheme ).

Changmai et al. described a mild, PTSA-catalyzed parallel synthesis of 1,4-dihydropyrimidines (25). The one-pot cyclocondensation of o-methyl acetoacetanilide (24), with various aldehydes (15), and urea/thiourea (8) proceeds using microwave assistance (500 W, 15–20 min) and affords derivatives 25 in 68–98% yields. Ultrasound-Assisted Synthesis of Pyrimidine Derivatives

Sonochemistry provides a sustainable reaction route to organic pharmaceuticals using ultrasound-induced acoustic cavitation with accelerated reaction rates, enhanced selectivity, and improved yield while minimizing waste and energy consumption.

Handore et al. introduced the use of polyindole catalyst under solvent-free conditions using ultrasound-assisted Biginelli reactions to prepare compound 26. Employing the catalyst with aromatic aldehydes (15), ethyl acetoacetate 9 in the presence of urea/thiourea (8) underwent efficient one-pot cyclocondensation at 45 °C under sonication for 25–35 min, affording compound 26 in 86–97% yield (Scheme ). The reaction protocol scope tolerates versatile derivatives in both electron-donating and electron-withdrawing substituents. Introduction of this reaction protocol to the green ultrasound method, Fuse et al. developed the magnetic Fe3O4@l-Arg@NiO nanocatalyst, aromatic aldehydes (15), urea (8), and ethyl acetoacetate (9) afford 3,4-dihydropyrimidin-2­(1H)-ones (26) in 85–95% yield (Scheme ).

Ionic Liquid (IL)-Assisted Synthesis of Pyrimidine Derivatives

Ionic liquids (ILs) are composed of organic cations and anions and are liquid at room temperature, displaying exceptional characteristics, such as high thermal stability and negligible vapor pressure. Their strong solvating capabilities facilitate reactions without volatile organic solvents. Moreover, they are easily recovered and reused across multiple cycles without loss of activity, making them sustainable and environmentally friendly catalysts.

Le et al. developed a magnetically recoverable Brønsted acidic IL catalyst, Fe3O4@O3Si­[PrMIM]­HSO4, by immobilizing an imidazolium-based IL on nanomagnetite. This catalyst efficiently promoted solvent-free Biginelli-type reactions at 100 °C, to produce derivatives of compound 29 in 57–93% yield. They found that electron-donating groups enhanced nucleophilicity, resulting in higher yields (81–93%), while electron-withdrawing groups reduced reactivity (Scheme ).

Imidazolium-Based Ionic Liquid-Assisted Synthesis of Pyrimidines 29

Expanding the scope of ionic liquid catalysts, Kumar et al. developed a solid dicationic IL, [BCMAB]­[Cl], for the green synthesis of 1,2,3,4-tetrahydropyrimidine-5-carboxylates (31) under green conditions while reflux in ethanol. This catalyst proved reusable for six cycles, achieved yields of up to 98%, and demonstrated effectiveness with a range of heterocyclic aldehydes (15) (Scheme ). The green chemistry performance of this Biginelli condensation was further assessed through five key metrics: E-factor (7.7), PMI (8.7), RME (63.9%), Atom Economy (75.1%), and Carbon Efficiency (77.8%), collectively indicating moderate sustainability, with the majority of reactant atoms and carbon content successfully incorporated into the desired product. While these values reflect a reasonably efficient process, further improvements in reagent stoichiometry, solvent reduction, and reaction pathway optimization remain viable strategies to minimize waste and enhance overall process efficiency. Similarly, Zhang et al. reported in 2025 that the Brønsted acidic ionic liquid Et3N+(CH2)3SO3H·H2PO4 – effectively catalyzed the one-pot Biginelli synthesis of 3,4-dihydropyrimidin-2­(1H)-ones (32). The catalyst minimized byproduct formation, exhibited recyclability for five cycles, and demonstrated tolerance toward both electron-donating and electron-withdrawing substituents, in 61–93% yield (Scheme ). However, sterically hindered or strongly electron-withdrawing aldehyde derivatives resulted in slightly diminished yields.

Nanomaterial-Assisted Pyrimidine Synthesis

Nanomaterials exhibit superior catalytic performance compared with conventional catalysts due to their high surface area, unique electrical properties, and enhanced catalytic activity. Consequently, their application in organic reactions improves both the selectivity and efficiency. This approach also aligns with green chemistry principles, resulting in a more environmentally friendly and commercially viable synthetic procedure.

Several catalytic strategies have been explored to enhance the environmental compatibility of the Biginelli reaction. In 2025, Sead et al. reported an environmentally conscious Biginelli synthesis using magnetically recoverable AlFeO3 nanoparticles in glycerol. This method produced 3,4-dihydropyrimidin-2­(1H)-thiones (33) in high yield (89–97%) within remarkably short reaction times (Scheme ). The reaction rate was accelerated by electron-donating substituents on the aldehyde, and heteroaryl aldehydes also proved to be effective substrates.

Synthesis of 3,4-Dihydropyrimidin-2­(1H)-Thiones 33 Using AlFeO3 Nanoparticles-Assisted Reaction

In a complementary approach, Taravati et al. developed a magnetically separable Fe3O4@PEG@CPTMS@dithizone-Ag nanocatalyst for one-pot Biginelli reactions in aqueous ethanol. This protocol similarly affords the target compound 34 in high yields (86–97%), and the catalyst was recovered and reused for six cycles without significant deactivation (Scheme ). In this method, aldehyde derivatives bearing electron-withdrawing groups play a key role in accelerating the reaction, with slightly higher yields.

More recently, efforts have focused on developing more sustainable catalyst designs. Aziza et al. reported a plant-mediated synthesis of nickel oxide (NiO) nanoparticles as a catalyst. The optimally calcined NiO-300 sample catalyzed the three-component Biginelli condensation reaction of aromatic aldehyde derivatives (15), urea (8), and acetylacetone (9) in ethanol, delivering 3,4-dihydropyrimidin-2­(1H)-ones (35) in 62–91% yields (Scheme ). Comparative Analysis of Synthetic Methodologies

A comparative evaluation of the reported methodologies highlights clear trade-offs among efficiency, sustainability, and practicality (Table ). Classical Biginelli and cyclocondensation reactions offer operational simplicity and scalability but are limited by prolonged reaction times (4–8 h), corrosive acid catalysts, and significant waste generation. In contrast, microwave-assisted and solvent-free approaches substantially improve reaction efficiency (15–30 min) and eliminate hazardous solvents, although their reliance on specialized instrumentation may limit accessibility. Ultrasound-assisted methods provide high yields (85–98%) under mild conditions but face scale-up challenges.

Ionic liquid-mediated systems function as both solvent and catalyst, affording high yields (up to 98%) and recyclability (5–6 cycles), albeit with higher preparation cost. Nanocatalyst-mediated strategies, particularly those employing magnetically recoverable materials (e.g., AlFeO3, Fe3O4 composites, NiO-300), offer an optimal balance, combining good to excellent yields (62–97%), reduced reaction times (0.5–3 h), and facile recovery with reusability. Mechanistic Aspects of Pyrimidine Synthesis

The Biginelli reaction is generally accepted to proceed via an iminium-mediated pathway, which has been supported by both experimental and computational studies (Figure ). Initially, acid-catalyzed condensation of the aldehyde with (thio)­urea affords intermediate (I), which upon protonation of the carbonyl group, nucleophilic addition, and subsequent dehydration, generates the highly electrophilic iminium ion (II). In parallel, the β-ketoester undergoes keto–enol tautomerization to furnish the corresponding enol species III, which serves as the active nucleophile. Subsequent C–C bond formation occurs via nucleophilic attack of the enol (III) on the iminium carbon of intermediate II, leading to the open-chain ureide intermediate IV. This intermediate then undergoes intramolecular cyclization through nucleophilic attack of the second amino group of the (thio)­urea moiety onto the carbonyl carbon, affording the cyclic intermediate V. Finally, dehydration accompanied by structural reorganization yields the thermodynamically stable 3,4-dihydropyrimidin-2­(1H)-one/thione scaffold (VI). This iminium pathway is considered the most favorable due to its lower activation energy relative to alternative enamine or Knoevenagel mechanisms, thereby accounting for its predominance under a wide range of catalytic conditions.

Iminium mechanism for the Biginelli reaction.

The proposed mechanism for the Biginelli condensation in the one-pot multicomponent synthesis of 1,2,3,4-tetrahydropyrimidine derivatives catalyzed by BC­[2Cl] ionic liquids (ILs) is presented in Figure . In the initial step, the catalyst activates the heterocyclic aldehyde (15), thereby facilitating its condensation with urea or thiourea to generate intermediate I, which is commonly described as an N-acylimine species. Subsequently, intermediate I undergoes a Michael-type addition with the enol or enolate form of ethyl acetoacetate (9) to afford intermediate II, an open-chain ureide derivative. Thereafter, nucleophilic attack by the amino group of (thio)­urea on intermediate II promotes intramolecular cyclization, followed by dehydration to furnish tetrahydropyrimidinone or tetrahydropyrimidinethione framework III. Finally, under an air atmosphere, oxidative aromatization yields desired derivatives IV.

Plausible mechanism of the IL-mediated Biginelli reaction.

The catalytic mechanisms reported across the nanomaterial-based Biginelli reaction systems converge on two principal pathways, both facilitated by Lewis acid active sites on the nanoparticle surface. In the imine-enamine pathway (Figure ), Lewis acid centers, most commonly exposed metal ions such as Al3+, Ni2+, or Ag+, on the nanoparticle surface first activate the aldehyde by coordinating to its carbonyl oxygen (I), making the carbon more electrophilic for attack by urea/thiourea to form an imine intermediate (II). Simultaneously, the NPs activate the β-ketoester (ethyl or methyl acetoacetate) via coordination, facilitating enolate formation. This enolate then attacks the activated iminium ion to form intermediate III, followed by intramolecular cyclization (IV) and dehydration to release the final 3,4-dihydropyrimidin-2­(1H)-one/thione product (V). Alternatively, the reaction can proceed via a Knoevenagel-type mechanistic pathway (Figure X). In this route, the activated aldehyde (I) initially undergoes condensation with the active methylene component to furnish an α,β-unsaturated olefinic intermediate (II) through a classical Knoevenagel condensation. The resulting conjugated intermediate, characterized by enhanced electrophilicity, subsequently undergoes nucleophilic addition by urea or thiourea to afford intermediate (III). This is followed by intramolecular cyclization via nucleophilic attack of the amino group onto the carbonyl functionality, leading to the formation of intermediate (IV). Subsequent proton transfers and rearrangement steps generate intermediate (V), which upon final dehydration yields the desired 1,2,3,4-tetrahydropyrimidine derivative (VI). Regardless of the specific pathway operative, the surface Lewis acidity of the nanomaterial plays a key role in stabilizing polar transition states and lowering activation energy, translating mechanistic efficiency into the high yields and short reaction times consistently reported across these systems. Additional factors, including nanoparticle size, surface area, and calcination history, modulate the density and accessibility of active sites, while the heterogeneous nature of these catalysts, confirmed by negligible metal leaching and sustained recyclability over multiple cycles, underscores their practical viability. Collectively, these mechanistic insights highlight how rational nanomaterial design, particularly the tuning of surface Lewis acidity within magnetically recoverable or green-synthesized frameworks, can simultaneously advance catalytic performance and align with the broader principles of sustainable chemistry.

Pyrimidine Hybrids

Currently, the design and synthesis of hybrid molecules have become a key strategy for modern drug development, particularly with pharmacophore molecules. This approach covalently links two or more bioactive moieties into a single molecule, aiming to enhance potency, selectivity, and pharmacokinetic properties while simultaneously reducing toxicity, resistance, and off-target effects. Hybrid molecules provide improved efficacy and patient compliance through multitarget action compared with traditional combination therapies, particularly in the treatment of cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, and infectious diseases. − Pyrimidines commonly synthesized using Biginelli multicomponent reactions are often covalently linked with pharmacophores such as chalcones, quinolines, triazoles, pyrazoles, coumarins, or isatins to generate multitarget drugs with enhanced potency, selectivity, and expanded biological profiles. The order of presentation of pyrimidine hybrids is intended to provide representative examples from the literature and does not imply any specific hierarchy or classification.

Chalcone-Pyrimidine Hybrids

Chalcone-pyrimidine hybrids are readily synthesized from either the incorporation of chalcone moieties with an aldehyde component during the Biginelli reaction or through postcondensation click chemistry. These compounds exhibit potent antimicrobial and anticancer activities due to synergistic electrophilic and antioxidant mechanisms. −

Gohil et al. reported an alternative approach using direct condensation of preformed chalcone precursors (36) bearing various aryl substituents with urea (15) in a solution of sodium hydroxide in methanol at room temperature for 24 h (Scheme ). This method provides chalcone-pyrimidine hybrid derivatives (37) in 63–79% yields. Notably, all substituents were well tolerated.

Building on similar chalcone-pyrimidine scaffolds, Abdelaziz et al. developed a concise three-linear-sequence step that completely avoided the Biginelli reaction. Initially, chloroacetamide derivatives (38) were prepared via Claisen-Schmidt condensation and subsequently acylated using chloroacetyl chloride. Then, S-alkylation of 4,6-dimethyl-2-thioxopyrimidine/2-thiouracil derivatives (39) with chloroacetamide derivatives 38 under reflux afforded target chalcone 2-thiopyrimidine conjugates 40 in 62–82% yields (Scheme ).

The product 2-amino-4-(2-fluoro-4-methoxyphenyl)-6-arylpyrimidine derivatives (43) were isolated in good yields.

Quinoline-Pyrimidine Hybrids

Quinoline-pyrimidinone hybrids were synthesized using tandem or optimized Biginelli reaction with significant promising activities as cytotoxic and antimalarial agents. Studies from 2025 indicate that these derivatives exhibit enhanced antiproliferative activity and effectively inhibit P. falciparum glutathione reductase. The quinoline component is hypothesized to facilitate DNA intercalation. ,

Mansour et al. described the synthesis of quinoline-3,4-dihydropyrimidin-2­(1H)-one hybrids (49) using the classical Biginelli reaction. This method involved refluxing a mixture of quinoline aldehyde (48), 1,3-dicarbonyl compound (9), and urea (8) in the presence of concentrated HCl for 5–6 h. The desired hybrids 49 were obtained in 65–88% yields (Scheme ). The study explored variations at the α-position (R group; ketone or ester moieties such as acetyl, methyl, ethyl, isopropyl, tert-butyl, isobutyl), with the isopropyl ester yielding the highest yield (88%).

Trivedi et al. reported the synthesis of quinoline-bearing 1,6-dihydropyrimidine azo dye derivatives (51) using the classical Biginelli strategy. The key intermediate, ethyl 2-amino-4-(2-chloroquinolin-3-yl)-6-methyl-1,6-dihydropyrimidine-5-carboxylate (50), was prepared via condensation of 2-chloroquinoline-3-carbaldehyde (48), ethyl acetoacetate (9), and guanidine (8) under reflux conditions. Diazotization of the amino group (50), followed by coupling with phenol derivatives, afforded azo derivative compound 51 in 72–79% yields (Scheme ).

In a complementary approach using green catalysis, Sharma et al. reported a green synthesis of 2-chloroquinoline-dihydropyrimidine derivatives 54 and 55, utilizing a magnetically recoverable ZnAg0.4Fe1.6O4 catalyst. The Biginelli reaction of 2-chloroquinoline-3-carbaldehyde (48), 1,3-dicarbonyl compounds/β-ketoesters (9/53), and urea/thiourea (8) was conducted in water under reflux for 2 h with the ferrite catalyst to afford the target compounds in 95–99% yields (Scheme ). Pyrano-Fused Pyrimidine Hybrids

Pyrano-fused pyrimidine hybrids, particularly pyrano­[2,3-d]­pyrimidine derivatives 63, are fused pyran and pyrimidine ring-containing compounds. Bioactivities of Pyrimidine Molecular Hybrids

Pyrimidine derivatives have attracted much attention in the heterocyclic chemistry arena due to their versatile chemical structure and diverse biological activities. Structurally similar to purines, these heterocycles exhibit a broad spectrum of pharmaceutical activities, including antibacterial, antifungal, anticancer, and anti-inflammatory activities.

Antibacterial Activities

Many heterocyclic compounds, particularly nitrogen-containing heterocycles such as pyrimidine derivatives, exhibit broad-spectrum antibacterial activity against both Gram-positive and -negative bacteria. They often demonstrate antifungal effects, especially against resistant strains. − Antibacterial potency is commonly assessed by using agar disk diffusion, agar well diffusion, and microdilution assays.

Recently, several research groups reported promising results with pyrimidine-based scaffolds (Figure ). For example, Nima and Shtnaif synthesized novel pyrimidine and chalcone derivatives and evaluated their activity against Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli using agar diffusion. The most active compounds, 147a–c, produced zones of inhibition (ZOIs) 15 ± 1.9–20 ± 2 mm. Structure–activity relationship (SAR) analysis indicated that pyrimidine-thione and hydroxy/methoxy groups were beneficial, while nitro substitution modulated Gram selectivity. In another study, Chouhan and Maheshwari prepared thiophene-fused triazolopyrimidines and tested them at 1000 μg/mL using the cup-plate method. Compound 148, with electron-withdrawing difluoro substituents, achieved broad ZOI, 22–24 mm, while Cipro 28–30 mm. Further expanding the series, Sroor et al. synthesized benzo­[4,5]­imidazo­[1,2-a]­pyrimidines (149a–c), which demonstrated minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of 10–60 μg/mL against S. aureus and S. typhimurium, comparable to those of ciprofloxacin (5–10 μg/mL). Thiophene and morpholine substituents were found to be particularly advantageous.

Pyrimidine hybrids with reported potent antibacterial activities, 147–154.

Building on these findings, researchers in 2026 achieved even greater potency through further structural refinements. Reshma et al. synthesized 2,4,6-trisubstituted pyrimidines (50) and, using disc diffusion at 50 μg/mL, recorded ZOIs of up to 22 mm against E. coli and 26 mm against S. aureus, while Cipro showed 32 and 27 mm, respectively, at 10 μg/mL. Nitro substitution at C-6 significantly enhanced activity. Salem et al. evaluated pyrazolopyrimidines against resistant strains using microdilution. Compound 151a–c (MIC of 0.5–64 μg/mL), comparable to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole due to glutamic acid conjugation, improved dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) inhibition. , El-Essawy and Odah prepared pyridopyrimidine derivatives with MICs ranging from 0.48–3.91 μg/mL. The tricyclic analogue 152 (MIC 0.48–2.50 μg/mL) rivaled ciprofloxacin (0.25–0.50 μg/mL), with tricyclic fusion and N-substitution identified as key potency enhancers. Suggala et al. assessed dihydroisobenzofuran-imidazole-pyrimidine hybrids using both disc and broth-dilution methods at 250 μg/mL against E. coli and S. aureus. Compounds 153a and 153b exhibited MICs of 6.25 μg/mL and 11.5–12.0 μg/mL, respectively, equaling or exceeding those of chloramphenicol MICs of 12.25 μg/mL. Finally, Jafarian et al. developed chromenopyrimidine sulfonates and compounds 154a and 154b achieved MICs of 62.5 μg/mL against multidrug-resistant S. aureus and E. coli, equivalent to ciprofloxacin (MIC = 62.5 μg/mL). The p-tolyl sulfonate group enhanced lipophilicity and Gram-negative potency, while the chromenopyrimidine core facilitated essential hydrogen bonding.

SAR analysis of pyrimidine hybrids (147–154) highlights the pyrimidine-thione scaffold as superior to oxo analogs due to enhanced hydrophobic interactions from the CS bond. Electron-withdrawing groups (e.g., difluoro, nitro) improved broad-spectrum activity by enhancing membrane permeability and enzyme binding, whereas electron-donating groups (hydroxy, methoxy) favored Gram-positive selectivity via hydrogen bonding. Thiophene and morpholine substituents balanced lipophilicity and solubility, while rigid fused systems (152) yielded the lowest MIC values by strengthening topoisomerase binding. Additional features such as p-tolyl sulfonate improved Gram-negative penetration, and glutamic acid conjugation optimized DHFR inhibition. Overall, optimal activity requires a thione or fused core, electron-withdrawing groups for Gram-negative activity, electron-donating groups for Gram-positive selectivity, and structural rigidity.

Antifungal Activities

Pyrimidine derivatives demonstrate promising antifungal effects in addition to their antibacterial properties, particularly against clinically relevant fungal strains. , Recently, studies have highlighted antifungal and antimicrobial properties of structurally diverse pyrimidine derivatives. For instance, Azzouzi et al. reported significant activity of imidazo­[1,2-a]­pyrimidine-Schiff bases against Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. albedinis (IC50 = 2.6–4.7 μg/mL), with compound 155 exhibiting the highest potency. This effect was attributed to its thiophene moiety and favorable nitro substitution, where the meta position conferred greater activity compared to the para or ortho positions. Building upon this scaffold, Saini et al. developed fluorinated pyrimidines (156a–d) that demonstrated strong antifungal activity, significantly inhibiting adhesion even at lower concentrations (30 μg/mL), surpassing fluconazole, which required 64 μg/mL to achieve weaker effects. SAR analysis underscored the importance of fluorophenyl substitution.

Similarly, Ren et al. synthesized novel pyrimidine derivatives incorporating a 1,1,2-trifluoro-1-butene moiety and evaluated their antifungal efficacy against four fungal strains via mycelial growth rate assays. Compound 157a demonstrated inhibition of Aspergillus niger by 64.55% and 48.85% at concentrations of 100 and 50 μg/mL, respectively, exhibiting activity comparable to that of prochloraz (62.2 and 46.91%, respectively). Furthermore, Compound 157b displayed enhanced nematocidal activity against C. elegans, with corrected mortality rates of 89.65% and 79.47% at 100 and 50 μg/mL, respectively, outperforming ethoprophos (79.26% and 66.43%, respectively). Finally, Manoharan et al. synthesized a series of cyclopenta­[d]­pyrimidine derivatives and evaluated their antifungal activity against A. niger and Candida albicans using the disk diffusion method. Their findings indicated that several compounds 158a–f exhibited promising activity, displaying a MIC of 11.2–13.5 μg/mL. Further structural analysis suggested that the presence of benzylidene rings, in conjunction with electron-withdrawing groups, significantly enhanced the compounds’ antimicrobial properties (Figure ).

Structure of pyrimidine-based antifungal hybrids, 155–158.

For antifungal hybrids (155–158), activity was governed by substitution pattern, halogenation, and heterocyclic auxiliaries. Meta-nitro substitution provided optimal potency by balancing electronics and sterics, while ortho substitution reduced activity. Thiophene was critical for hydrophobic and π-interactions. Fluorinated phenyl groups enhanced activity through improved lipophilicity and CYP51 binding, and polyfluorinated side chains increased membrane disruption. Electron-withdrawing groups on benzylidene rings further improved potency. In summary, effective antifungal agents require halogenated or electron-withdrawing substituents, meta positioning, and sulfur-containing heterocycles.

Anticancer Activities

Cancer remains a major global health challenge, with current treatments often causing serious side effects and facing increased drug resistance. The pyrimidine heterocyclic component of nucleic acids involved in vital biological processes is also linked to cancer development, making it a promising scaffold for designing new anticancer agents. , A recent review highlighted the strong anticancer potential of pyrimidine-based hybrid compounds, demonstrating enhanced potency, selectivity, and clear structure–activity relationships across diverse cancer cell lines.

Abolicida et al. synthesized a series of 1,3-thiazolyl-pyrimidine hybrids and evaluated in vitro cytotoxicity against HepG2 liver cancer cells using the MTT assay. Five compounds displayed promising activity with IC50 values surpassing that of doxorubicin (IC50 = 6.18 μM). Among them, compound 159a was the most potent (IC50 = 2.39 μM), followed by 159b (3.27 μM), 159c (3.81 μM), 159d (4.04 μM), and 159e (5.02 μM). SAR analysis revealed that electron-donating groups, such as methyl or methoxy, generally enhanced activity, whereas electron-withdrawing substituents reduced it.

Lakshmi et al. synthesized a series of amide derivatives of 1,3,4-oxadiazol-pyridine-pyrimidine and tested them against MCF-7 (breast), A549 (lung), Colo-205 (colon), and A2780 (ovarian) cells using the MTT assay. Most derivatives showed remarkable potency, with five compounds 160a-e outperforming etoposide. Compound 160a, a 3,4,5-trimethoxy aryl substitution, emerged as the most promising candidate, with IC50 values of 0.12–0.33 μM across the cell lines, surpassing etoposide’s 0.17–3.34 μM. SAR studies highlighted that both the nature and position of aryl substitutions critically influence activity.

Exploring fused pyrimidine systems, Ait Elmachkouri et al. developed N2- and C5-substituted pyrazolo­[4’,3′:5,6]­pyrano­[2,3-d]­pyrimidine derivatives and screened them against the NCI-60 panel at a single 10 μM dose. The N2-benzyl derivative compound 161a demonstrated the broadest and most potent activity, achieving complete (100%) growth inhibition in HL-60­(TB) and MOLT-4 leukemia lines, as well as HOP-62 lung cancer cells, along with 98.4% inhibition of MDA-MB-468 breast cancer cells. Compound 161b showed moderate effects, while others were less active, underscoring the critical role of the N2-benzyl group in broad-spectrum efficacy.

Anwer et al. synthesized pyrazole/pyrimidine derivatives incorporating azobenzene linkers and evaluated them against HepG2, MCF-7, HCT-116, and A549 cells via MTT assay. HCT-116 and A549 were particularly sensitive. Compound 163c was the most potent (IC50 = 5.12–6.77 μM), outperforming erlotinib and approaching sorafenib, while analogs 163a and 163b also showed strong activity (IC50 ≈ 6.0–8.25 μM). SAR findings suggested that pyrimidine cores were generally more effective than pyrazoles, with the azobenzene moiety essential for activity.

Similarly, Aziz et al. reported novel pyrazolo­[3,4-d]­pyrimidine derivatives tested against HCT-116, MCF-7, HepG2, and A549 cells. Compounds 164a and 164b were most effective (IC50 = 5.00–6.40 μM), often surpassing or matching erlotinib and sorafenib (IC50 = 4.00–13.91 μM), confirming the pyrazolo­[3,4-d]­pyrimidine core as a key pharmacophore for dual VEGFR-2 and EGFR inhibition.

In prostate cancer studies, Garmabi et al. developed pyrido­[2,3-d]­pyrimidine derivatives evaluated against PC-3 cells via MTT. Compound 165 showed the greatest potency, with IC50 values of 35.60 ± 1.14, 14.02 ± 1.26, and 9.39 ± 1.23 μM at 24, 48, and 72 h, respectively, although less potent than doxorubicin (≈2 μM). Extended treatment improved both cytotoxicity and selectivity.

Finally, Iliev et al. investigated seven types of 4-amino-2-substituted tetrahydrobenzothieno­[2,3-d]­pyrimidine derivatives on HSC-3 and SCC-9 oral squamous carcinoma cells. Compound 166 (IC50 58.96 μM on HCS-3 and IC50 48.54 ± 3.047 μM on SCC-9) demonstrated the highest potency and selectivity, inducing S-phase arrest and apoptosis while sparing nontumorigenic keratinocytes. SAR studies emphasized the importance of specific 2-position substitutions in balancing anticancer activity with reduced toxicity (Figure ).

Structure of strong anticancer potential pyrimidine-based hybrid, 159–166.

SAR of anticancer hybrids (159–166) revealed that electron-donating aryl substituents enhance cytotoxicity, while precise substitution patterns (e.g., 3,4,5-trimethoxy) maximize kinase binding. N-benzyl substitution improved allosteric interactions, and planar linkers such as azobenzene facilitated π-stacking with kinase residues. Fused bicyclic systems provided conformational rigidity for dual-target inhibition, and substitution position influenced selectivity. Overall, activity is driven by electron-donating groups, rigid fused cores, planar linkers, and optimized N-substitution geometry.

Anti-Inflammatory Activities

Recent studies highlighted the therapeutic potential of pyrimidine-based scaffolds as anti-inflammatory agents, acting through COX inhibition, cytokine modulation, nitric oxide suppression, and oxidative stress reduction. For example, Ali et al. synthesized a series of 2-oxo-1,2,3,4-tetrahydropyrimidine derivatives and evaluated their multitarget anti-inflammatory activity in LPS-stimulated WI38 lung fibroblast cells using LPS as standard. Among them, compound 167 showed significant suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β, with IC50 = 0.4 μM for IL-1β. It also strongly downregulated NF-κB, iNOS, and MAPK signaling pathways (IC50: 0.2–0.62 μM). SAR analysis pointed to the benzothiophene group at position-4 as a key contributor to its high potency.

Expanding on hybrid approaches, Shaker et al. developed pyridine-pyrimidine hybrids and tested their anti-inflammatory activity both in vitro and in vivo. In the carrageenan-induced rat paw edema model, most compounds significantly reduced inflammation, with 168a and 168b showing the strongest effects (83% and 74% inhibition at 1 h), outperforming celecoxib (74%). In COX assays, compound 9d demonstrated potent and selective COX-2 inhibition (IC50 = 0.54 μM), exceeding celecoxib (IC50 = 1.11 μM). SAR findings highlighted the importance of the 4-methoxyphenyl group in enhancing both activity and selectivity.

Selective COX-2 inhibition was also reported by Tylinska et al. (2024), who studied pyrimidine derivatives for their effects on cyclooxygenase enzymes and oxidative stress. Compounds 169a and 169b showed strong COX-2 selectivity (IC50 = 74.6 and 76.8 μM, respectively), outperforming piroxicam (IC50 = 80.1 μM) and approaching the efficacy of meloxicam (76.4 μM).

Dual targeting of inflammatory mediators was demonstrated by Abdel-Maksoud et al. with pyrimidine-pyrazole hybrid derivatives. These compounds were tested in LPS-activated RAW 264.7 macrophages for inhibition of nitric oxide and PGE2 production. Compounds 170a and 170b showed strong inhibition of iNOS (IC50 = 1.55 and 2.54 μM, respectively) and COX-2 while sparing COX-1. Compound 170b also stood out for PGE2 inhibition (IC50 = 6.31 μM). SAR analysis revealed that a 3-hydroxyphenyl group in the series provided greater potency than the corresponding 3-methoxyphenyl analogs.

Although structurally different, camphor-derived thiocarbamates studied by Abd El-Azeem et al. also showed strong and selective COX-2 inhibition (171a and 171b: IC50 = 7.655 and 8.823 μM, respectively), comparable to celecoxib (IC50 = 0.685 μM), along with high COX-2/COX-1 selectivity. The type and position of phenyl ring substitutions were identified as key factors influencing activity. Finally, Keshk et al. synthesized pyrimidine and pyrimidopyrimidine derivatives and evaluated their anti-inflammatory activity using a membrane stabilization assay. Compounds 172, 173a–d showed strong effects, with 173a achieving 72.62% stabilization, comparable to aspirin (76.33%). SAR analysis highlighted the role of the pyrimidine core, amino groups, hydrazino moiety, and pyrazole ring in enhancing activity (Figure ).

Antiprotozoal Activities

Recent studies have highlighted the therapeutic potential of pyrimidine-based scaffolds and their hybrids as antiprotozoal agents, acting against a broad range of parasites including Plasmodium, Leishmania, Trypanosoma cruzi, and Eimeria species. Lusardi et al. designed and synthesized a series of trisubstituted 2-aminopyrimidine derivatives, evaluating their antiplasmodial activity against CQ-sensitive D10 and CQ-resistant W2 P. falciparum strains, and antileishmanial activity against L. infantum and L. tropica promastigotes, using chloroquine (IC50 = 0.04 μM against D10; 0.45 μM against W2) and amphotericin B (IC50 = 0.22 μM against L. infantum; 0.29 μM against L. tropica) as reference drugs. Pyrimidine 173 emerged as the sole broad-spectrum analog, displaying micromolar inhibition against both P. falciparum strains (IC50 = 16.69 μM against D10; IC50 = 4.18 μM against W2), with notably stronger activity against the CQ-resistant W2 strain, alongside potent antileishmanial effects against L. infantum (IC50 = 7.31 μM) and L. tropica (IC50 = 9.14 μM), and no cytotoxicity against normal human embryonic fibroblasts GM-6114 (cell viability = 67% at 10 μM).

Building on the antiprotozoal potential of pyrimidine-based molecular hybrids, Mass et al. synthesized a series of quinazoline-dihydropyrimidinone hybrids and evaluated their activity against T. cruzi and four Leishmania species, using benznidazole (IC50 = 294.86 μM against epimastigotes; 235.8 μM against trypomastigotes) and miltefosine (IC50 = 14.68–19.75 μM) as reference drugs. Compounds 174a and 174b demonstrated exceptional antichagasic potency against T. cruzi epimastigotes (IC50 = 2.15 and 1.94 μM) and trypomastigotes (IC50 = 3.22 and 2.20 μM), significantly outperforming benznidazole, while also displaying potent antileishmanial activity against Leishmania braziliensis (IC50 = 1.58 and 1.12 μM), surpassing miltefosine. Their favorable selectivity profiles (SI > 60 for T. cruzi; SI > 170 for L. braziliensis) were attributed to low cytotoxicity against Vero cells and RAW 264.7 macrophages, with molecular docking studies further revealing strong interactions with cruzain, sterol 14-α demethylase, and dihydroorotate dehydrogenase, supporting a multitarget mechanism of action.

Extending the antiprotozoal scope of pyrimidines to coccidial infections, Elsayed et al. synthesized novel pyrimidine carbonitrile derivatives under eco-friendly conditions and evaluated their efficacy against Eimeria spp. oocyst sporulation, using toltrazuril (97.7 ± 1.2% sporulation inhibition at 25 ppm) as the reference. Azomethine derivative 176 and pyrazole-fused pyrimidine 175 achieved near-complete sporulation inhibition (98.7 ± 0.5% and 98.3 ± 0.5%, respectively) at 200 ppm, with compound 20 exhibiting the highest potency as reflected by the lowest LC50 and LC90 values (100 ppm/<1 h and 100 ppm/5 h), indicating both superior efficacy and rapid onset of action.

Further studies have highlighted the therapeutic potential of pyrimidine-based molecular hybrids as antiprotozoal agents, which act by inhibiting essential parasite heat shock proteins. Kayamba et al. designed and synthesized a series of novel (E)-1-(4-(4,6-diphenylpyrimidin-2-yl)­piperazin-1-yl)-3-phenylprop-2-en-1-one derivatives. This was achieved through a molecular hybridization strategy, combining a 4,6-diphenylpyrimidine core with a cinnamoyl pharmacophore via a piperazine linker. The researchers evaluated their antiplasmodial activity against the NF54 chloroquine-sensitive P. falciparum strain, using chloroquine (IC50 = 0.01 μM) and quinine (IC50 = 0.11 μM) as reference drugs. Compounds 177b and 177a emerged as the most potent derivatives (IC50 = 0.18 ± 0.02 and 0.21 ± 0.001 μM, respectively). They demonstrated favorable selectivity indices (SI = 18.59 and 16.75) against HEK293 cells and showed activity specifically directed against the trophozoite stage.

Across these studies, SAR analysis consistently identified key structural features governing antiprotozoal potency. In the pyrimidine series, the 3-(dibutylamino) propylamino chain at position-4, combined with retention of the 3-phenyl and 5-cyano substituents and expansion to the six-membered pyrimidine nucleus, was critical for broad-spectrum activity, while replacement with arylalkyl or cycloalkyl chains abolished antileishmanial effects. In the DHPM hybrids, molecular hybridization of the quinazoline and DHPM pharmacophores proved indispensable, as individual parent scaffolds were inactive (IC50 > 50 μM), underscoring the synergistic contribution of both heterocyclic components. For the pyrimidine carbonitrile anticoccidials, fused heterocyclic systems and electron-withdrawing substituents such as cyano and nitro groups enhanced lipophilicity, facilitating oocyst wall penetration, collectively establishing both functional-group modification and scaffold hybridization as productive strategies for antiprotozoal lead optimization.

Conclusion

This Review comprehensively summarizes recent advances in the synthesis and biological evaluation of pyrimidine-based heterocyclic compounds, affirming their significance as a privileged scaffold in medicinal chemistry. A major finding is the remarkable progress in sustainable synthetic methodologies, including solvent-free conditions, microwave-assisted reactions, ultrasound irradiation, ionic liquid-mediated protocols, and nanocatalyst-promoted transformations, which collectively enhance reaction efficiency and reduce environmental impact. Molecular hybridization of the pyrimidine nucleus with pharmacophoric units such as chalcones, quinolines, triazoles, and pyrazoles has yielded hybrid structures with synergistic biological profiles, notably broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against drug-resistant strains, potent anticancer effects via kinase inhibition and apoptosis induction, anti-inflammatory activity through selective COX-2 inhibition and cytokine regulation, and antiprotozoal activity. Structure–activity relationship analyses consistently demonstrate that the nature and position of substituents on the pyrimidine ring are critical determinants of potency and selectivity. Nevertheless, challenges including poor aqueous solubility, metabolic instability, and limited clinical translation remain to be addressed through rational drug design, computational tools, and deeper multitarget mechanistic investigations. Overall, the convergence of green synthesis and hybrid molecular design firmly positions pyrimidine derivatives as promising candidates for next-generation therapeutics with improved efficacy and safety profiles.

Future Scope

Despite notable progress in pyrimidine-based molecular hybrids, several key areas require further attention. Future research should emphasize the rational design of multitarget hybrids aimed at emerging targets such as CDK4/6, PARP, HDAC, and IDH1/2, moving beyond the commonly explored EGFR, VEGFR-2, and COX-2 pathways.

The early integration of computational tools, including machine-learning-based QSAR models, molecular dynamics, and generative AI, can accelerate lead optimization and reduce synthetic inefficiencies. Addressing pharmacokinetic challenges, particularly poor solubility and metabolic instability, through bioisosteric design, prodrug approaches, and nanoparticle-based delivery systems will be critical to clinical advancement.

In addition, green synthetic strategies should be adapted to scalable continuous-flow systems to support sustainable production. Expanding biological evaluations into underexplored areas such as antiviral, antileishmanial, neuroprotective, and antidiabetic applications is also warranted. Crucially, greater focus on in vivo validation, supported by comprehensive pharmacokinetic and toxicity studies, is essential to establishing the clinical relevance of these promising compounds.
