Physiotherapist-led education and exercise for patients with MRI-verified hip abductor tendon pathology: a protocol for a prospective cohort study
Authors: Mathias Høgsholt, Signe Kierkegaard-Brøchner, Jeppe Lange, Kristian Thorborg, Marie Bagger Bohn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLateral hip pain due to hip abductor tendon pathology (ie, gluteal tendinopathy or tendon rupture) is a relatively new diagnosis. This patient group has previously been described as a clinical dilemma due to the often-short-lived effects of treatment. In the process of identifying the underlying pathology, however, more targeted treatment strategies have been explored. Recent research recommends exercise as a first-line treatment for this patient group along with patient education. However, patients seen in a hospital setting (secondary healthcare) often present recalcitrant lateral hip pain, tendon ruptures and multiple comorbidities. Whether patient education and exercise therapy are beneficial for patients in secondary healthcare has only been sparsely investigated. Therefore, this study aims to investigate changes in lateral hip pain following a 3 month physiotherapist-led patient education and exercise intervention in patients with MRI-proven hip abductor tendon pathology seen in a hospital setting.Methods and analysisIn this prospective cohort trial, 60 patients, presenting lateral hip pain and MRI-verified hip abductor tendon pathology at the orthopaedic outpatient clinic at Horsens Regional Hospital, Denmark, a public teaching hospital, are included. Hip abductor tendon pathology will be diagnosed by clinical tests and MRI. The intervention consists of seven physiotherapist-led patient education and exercise sessions at the hospital during 3 months. The instructions will guide the patients in their daily home-based exercise sessions.The primary outcome is change in lateral hip pain measured by the ‘pain’ subscale from the patient-reported outcome measure: the revised Copenhagen Hip And Groin Outcome Score (HAGOS). Secondary outcomes are changes in the remaining subscales of the revised HAGOS, Oxford Hip Score, Victorian Institute of Sports Assessment-Gluteal Questionnaire, European Questionnaire-Five Dimensions Five-Level, lateral hip pain (numeric rating scale score, 0–10), hip muscle strength and the 30 s chair stand test. Also, patient-reported Global Rating of Change in hip condition, as well as adherence and harms, will be reported.Ethics and disseminationThis trial has been accepted by the Central Denmark Regions Ethics Committee (1-10-72-136-22). The study is registered at the Central Denmark Region List of Research Projects (Journal No 1-16-02-180-24) and clinicaltrials.gov (NCT06418217).All results from this study, regardless of the direction, will be published in a peer-reviewed journal and presented at national and international congresses.Trial registration numberNCT06418217.Keywords: Exercise, Hip, Physical Therapy Modalities, Patient Reported Outcome Measures

Introduction

Background and rationale

During the last decade, hip abductor tendon pathology, encompassing both tendinopathy and tears of the tendinous insertion of the gluteus medius and minimus muscles on the greater trochanter, has been established as the primary pathology associated with lateral hip pain.1,3 This challenges the previously held hypothesis that trochanteric bursitis constitutes the primary cause of lateral hip pain and how this patient group should be treated.1 2 4 5

Interestingly, two recent systematic reviews conclude that exercise has the best evidence for managing the impairments experienced by patients with lateral hip pain.6 7 In addition to exercise, the review by Bremer et al recommends patient education as a core approach.7 Patient education which focuses on behavioural changes, load and pain management, as described by Mellor et al.4 The effect of patient education alone has not been investigated for this patient group.8

In the study by Mellor et al, patients with varying degrees of MRI-verified hip abductor tendon pathology (tendinopathies, partial and full-thickness tendon ruptures) are included.4 Whether the patients with tendon ruptures obtain a long-lasting pain reduction and improvement in physical function after an exercise and patient education intervention is, however, not well investigated.9 On the other hand, surgical reconstruction of gluteal tendon ruptures of varying size (partial and full-thickness) has shown promising subjective and functional outcomes in short- and long-term follow-ups.10,12

Our own data and clinical experience with patients seen in a hospital setting (secondary healthcare) with lateral hip pain and MRI-verified hip abductor tendon pathology indicate that varying degrees of tendon damage (i) react differently to exercise treatment in terms of changes in pain and strength and (ii) present different baseline pain profiles.9 11 13

Objectives and hypothesis

The primary aim of this study is to investigate changes in lateral hip pain during daily activities, following 3 months of physiotherapist-led patient education and exercise in patients with MRI-verified hip abductor tendon pathology, including a spectrum of tendinopathies and tears, seen in secondary healthcare (teaching hospital).

We hypothesise that lateral hip pain measured with the revised Copenhagen Hip And Groin Outcome Score (HAGOS) pain subscale14 will improve by at least eight points from baseline to 3 month follow-up.

Furthermore, we will explore changes in both primary and secondary outcomes across the total cohort as well as different patient subgroups based on their MRI findings, Trendelenburg test, exercise progression and pain profiles. Also, adherence and harms will be reported.

Methods and analysis

This study will investigate a combined patient education and exercise intervention, inspired by Mellor et al,4 and the main focus is on changes in hip-related outcomes.

Trial design

This study protocol describes a prospective cohort study.

This study protocol will adhere to the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials 2025, which comprises a guideline for the minimum content of a clinical trial.15 The intervention is described in accordance with the Consensus on Exercise Reporting Template (CERT).16 The described study will comply with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.17 Enrolment of patients started in May 2024, and the last patient is expected to be enrolled during September 2025. Hence, the end of the study is expected to be during November 2025, when the last patient has completed the 3 month follow-up assessment.

Participants

Patients are included from an orthopaedic outpatient clinic at Horsens Regional Hospital, Denmark, a public teaching hospital. In Denmark, patients are referred from their general practitioner (primary healthcare) to further treatment at a hospital (secondary healthcare). In the orthopaedic outpatient clinic, consultants in orthopaedic hip surgery will perform the initial screening and inclusion. The screening involves a cluster of clinical tests used to raise suspicion of hip abductor tendon pathology.18 Subsequently, an MRI scan is performed to verify hip abductor tendon pathology. The MRI scan is evaluated by an orthopaedic surgeon and a musculoskeletal radiologist. Eligible patients will be given written project information. Eligible and interested patients will fill out an informed consent form at the baseline test (online supplemental file 1).

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

(1) Age 18–75 years, (2) MRI-verified hip abductor tendon pathology, (3) lateral hip pain duration >6 months and (4) ability to read and understand Danish.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Corticosteroid injection in the affected lateral hip within the last 6 weeks prior to the intervention, (2) previous bone-related surgery to the affected hip, (3) signs of bilateral hip abductor tendon pathology, (4) X-ray verified hip osteoarthritis (Kellgren Lawrence>2) and (5) pregnancy.

Intervention

The physiotherapist-led intervention will have an overall duration of 3 months. The contents of the intervention are both patient education and exercises. Throughout the 3 months, seven physiotherapist-led exercise sessions will take place in a hospital setting. Once a week, for the first 2 weeks of the intervention, the patients will be supervised by a physiotherapist at the hospital. From week 2 forward, the supervision will be performed fortnightly. The rest of the exercise sessions will be performed at home. The physiotherapists received training in both patient education content and the exercise protocol from one of the study’s authors (MH). The trainer (MH) has been working specifically with this patient group for the past 5 years. The physiotherapists at the supervising exercise sessions will not be involved in the test assessments. Further, the physiotherapists have at least 2 years of clinical experience with musculoskeletal conditions. Figure 1 illustrates timepoints for the intervention.

Patient education

During the exercise sessions at the hospital, patient education will be provided by the supervising physiotherapist. The overall focus of the patient education is to provide the patient with a better understanding of the condition, why the pain occurs and how to accommodate the pain through load management. The patient education is inspired by Mellor et al.4 More specifically, the education will contain information on the anatomy of the hip with a particular focus on the functions of the affected muscles and why certain activities and/or movements will probably provoke the pain. The education will introduce the different mechanisms driving the pain, for example, anatomical morphology, mechanical loading, tensile load and compressive tendon load through excessive adduction. In addition, patients will be guided on appropriate sitting, standing and resting positions to avoid excessive hip adduction; for example, avoid sitting with crossed legs, to stand with equal weight-bearing on the legs, and when resting, to have some sort of cushioning between the knees to keep the hip in a neutral position. Moreover, patients are provided with a folder containing guidance on how to perform the exercises, including instructions on what to pay attention to both during execution of the exercises and their activities of daily living and criteria for discontinuing the exercises, such as the onset of intolerable pain. Further, the folder contains an exercise diary. The patients are encouraged to note in their exercise diary if they have done additional physical activity and what kind of activity. This approach is intended to provide patients with insights into their daily activity levels, enabling them to identify potential correlations between physical activity and fluctuations in their pain. The supervising physiotherapist will guide the patients if needed to modify their activity level if it, by clinical reasoning, seems to affect the pain.

Exercise programme

The patients will be prescribed a daily exercise programme. The exercise protocol has been developed by physiotherapists and hip surgeons with documented clinical expertise in managing hip abductor tendon pathologies and is inspired by Mellor et al.4

The exercises will emphasise hip abductor engagement by incorporating both isometrics and dynamic movements to control hip adduction while also targeting the surrounding hip and thigh muscles for functional strengthening. Briefly, the patients will be instructed to perform four exercises consisting of (1) non-weight-bearing isometric hip abduction with a belt around the legs, (2) weight-bearing hip abductions, (3) supine bridging and (4) squats. Progression in the exercise protocol will be criterion-based. Hence, to progress in either the choice of exercise or volume of an exercise, lateral hip pain must be tolerable and acceptable for the patient. The supervising physiotherapist will guide the patients through both pro- and regression of the exercise intensity.

The intervention is described using the CERT in online supplemental file 1.16 The exercise protocol is provided in online supplemental file 1.

Patient and public involvement

Patients with gluteal tendinopathy who had participated in an exercise feasibility study filled out a questionnaire regarding their satisfaction with the intervention.19 The responses of the patients were taken into account when designing this study (ie, a longer duration of the intervention, an adequate number of supervised exercise sessions and the load of the exercises).

Patient characteristics

At baseline, sex, age, weight, height, educational level, civil status, employment status and usage of pain medication will be registered, as well as MRI findings and hip abductor function. The MRIs of individual patients (conducted during the screening) will be transcribed by one of the authors (JL). This will be based on our previous publication.2 The MRI findings will be dichotomised into cases with clear rupture (partial and full) of either the medius or minimus or tendinopathies (including uncertainty of rupture), as this best represents clinical translation. This will be done based on the presence of high-intensity signals cranial to the top of the greater trochanter, osteophytes at the anterolateral footprint with disruption of the gluteus minimus tendon and/or presence of clear fatty infiltration.

Further, at the baseline assessment, the Trendelenburg test will be used to examine hip abductor function.11 The patient stands on the affected leg in a single-leg stance for up to 30 s. The assessor stands behind the patient and places their hands on the iliac crests on either side of the pelvis. From here, the assessor observes if the pelvis stays level during the single-leg stance. A positive Trendelenburg sign is when the pelvis drops on the unaffected side.20

Primary outcome

The revised Copenhagen Hip And Groin Outcome Score

The primary outcome is change in lateral hip pain from baseline to 3 month follow-up, measured with the subscale ‘pain’ from the patient-reported outcome measure, the revised Copenhagen Hip And Groin Outcome Score (HAGOS).14

The revised HAGOS is validated for patients with hip and groin pain.14 Additionally, its psychometric validity has been specifically established for individuals with lateral hip pain.21 The revised HAGOS consists of six subscales (pain, symptoms, physical function in daily living, physical function in sport and recreation, participation in physical activities and hip and/or groin-related quality of life) and for each subscale, the score range is 0 (worst) to 100 (best).

Secondary outcomes

Patient-reported outcomes

Hip-related disabilities

The revised Copenhagen Hip And Groin Outcome Score

The subscales: symptoms, physical function in daily living, physical function in sport and recreation and hip and/or groin-related quality of life.14

Oxford Hip Score (OHS)

OHS consists of 12 items. OHS is intended for patients undergoing total hip replacements to assess pain and function. It is a composite score ranging from 0 (worst) to 48 (best).22 23

Victorian Institute of Sports Assessment-Gluteal Questionnaire (VISA-G)

VISA-G measures the severity of disability associated with gluteal tendinopathy. VISA-G is an eight-item questionnaire that results in a single score from 0 to 100, where a higher score indicates lower pain and less disability.24 25

Lateral hip pain

Pain measured using a numeric rating scale (NRS)

Lateral hip pain will be assessed by NRS: range 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst imaginable). The patients are asked to rate their average pain in the preceding 7 days on five rating scales (at rest, at the beginning of activity, during activity, 2 hours after activity and worst pain). The responses will be used for generating pain profiles for the subgroup analysis.

Overall health status

European Questionnaire-Five Dimensions (EQ-5D-5L) and EQ-visual analogue scale (EQ-VAS)

The EQ-5D-5L is a standardised, generic instrument for describing and assessing generic health status. It contains two main elements: a descriptive profile and the EQ-VAS.26 The descriptive profile comprises five dimensions, with each dimension having five levels of severity. This produces a five-digit code which, through an index calculator, can be converted into a numerical value (EQ index). Using a Danish value set, the Danish tariff ranges from −0.757 to 1, where 1 is depicted as the best possible health state.27 28 The EQ-VAS is a vertical line on which the patients score their perception of their overall health from 0 to 100 (worst to best).29

Improvement of hip condition

Global Rating of Change (GRoC)

GRoC is an 11-point scale, where the patient rates the perceived overall change of the hip condition from ‘very much better’ to ‘very much worse’.30 Responses on GRoC are considered successful if patients score themselves ‘moderately better’ to ‘very much better’. Global improvement will be reported as the percentage of successful reports.

Strength

Isometric hip muscle strength

Maximal voluntary isometric contraction will be measured using a fixed dynamometer (JTech Commander Echo Muscle Tester, USA) in four directions of the hip (abduction, adduction, extension and flexion). A full test protocol for the muscle strength tests is provided in online supplemental file 1. For each test direction, the patient will have at least three attempts to generate as much force into the dynamometer as possible. If the highest force is recorded at the third attempt, the test will continue until a decrease in force is recorded or with a maximum of seven attempts. The test assessor will instruct the patient to apply as much force as possible into the dynamometer at the given test direction and further that each contraction should have a duration of 5 s. During each attempt, the physiotherapist will verbally guide the patient through the test, to encourage and motivate the patient and to indicate the start and end of the 5 s.

Performance-based outcome

30 second Chair Stand Test (30s-CST)

The 30s-CST is a test of functional capacity.31 From a chair with a seat height of 45 cm and with their feet on the floor placed shoulder-width apart with the arms crossed across the chest, the patient is instructed to complete as many sit-to-stands as possible for 30 s. The tester will demonstrate the test prior to the patient’s attempt. A full test protocol for the 30s-CST is provided in online supplemental file 1.

Other intervention-related outcomes

Adherence

In the exercise diary, the supervising therapist will note the prescribed number of sets and repetitions. After every exercise session, the patients will fill out how many sets and repetitions they have performed.

To grade the adherence level, the cut-offs from Jacobsen et al (hip exercise for patients with hip dysplasia) will be used.32 To elaborate, a high adherence will be defined as completion of 75% or more of the prescribed repetitions (both supervised and home-based), medium adherence with completion of 50–74% and low adherence with completion of less than 50%.32

Adverse events

All adverse events or serious adverse events will be registered throughout the period of the study and will be reported to the local research ethics committee. The definition of an adverse event is any instant flare-ups of pain related to the hip, apart from muscle soreness in the muscles targeted with the exercises and other parts of the body.

Throughout the intervention, the supervising physiotherapists will report any adverse event that occurred during or in relation to the supervised exercise sessions or at the assessments of outcomes. If further medical evaluation or advice is needed, a referral to the hip surgeon involved in the inclusion will be established, for the surgeon to appraise the continuation in the study. Any adverse event occurring during the trial will be reported to the ethics committee of the Central Denmark Region.

Data collection methods and data management

Baseline and follow-up assessments will be conducted at the hospital by two physiotherapists. One of whom will also administer the physiotherapist-led sessions during the intervention, if needed, due to pragmatic reasons in the department. Data collected from the strength test and functional test will be entered into Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap)33 34 by the test assessor. The patients will fill out the PROMs on a laptop directly into REDCap at the baseline and 3 month follow-up testing.

Halfway through the intervention (6 weeks), PROMS will be sent out by email, and the patients will fill out their responses from their home computers. Data regarding patients will be pseudo-anonymised in REDCap by an assigned record ID to the individual patient.

Statistics

Sample size

The number of patients needed is based on a sample size calculation for changes in the subscale ‘pain’ in the patient-reported outcome measure of the revised HAGOS.14 The minimal important change for the HAGOS pain subscale is estimated to be around eight points, based on half the SD35 of the baseline values of these patients, as previously piloted and tested in our clinic.36 Based on a pilot follow-up study at our clinic, we also estimated the SD of the change score to be around 17 points.19 Hence, with a change score in the HAGOS pain subscale of at least eight points from baseline to follow-up, and a SD of 17 points, setting the significance level at 0.05 and the power of 90%, 50 patients would be required. When a dropout rate of 20% is also accounted for, we need to include 60 patients. The cohort will be analysed as one group, independent of sex.

Statistical methods

Both descriptive statistics and statistical tests will be reported in line with the guidelines composed in the STROBE statement.17 The distribution of data will be assessed using QQ-, scatter and Bland–Altman plots. Normally distributed, continuous data will be analysed using parametric statistics, employing paired t-tests, as differences from baseline to 3 month follow-up. Non-normally distributed data will be analysed using non-parametric statistics (Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney rank sum test).

Both primary and secondary outcomes will be analysed on the total cohort. Further, subgroup analysis by stratification on behalf of the severity of the hip abductor tendon pathology (MRI verified), Trendelenburg sign, pain profiles and exercise progression will be performed on all outcomes. The level of significance will be set at 0.05.

Ethics and dissemination

This study will adhere to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki II. Further, the study has been accepted by both the ethics committee of the Central Denmark Region (Journal No 1-10-72-136-22) and the Central Denmark Region List of Research Projects (Journal No 1-16-02-180-24). The study is registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT06418217). This study will not be monitored by a data monitoring committee, given that the study population is not in a critical health condition at the time of participation. Further, the exercise programme is not a high-risk intervention. Thus, the study is not required to undergo data monitoring or auditing.

Personally identifiable information on the patients will be removed from the datasets and the results. All outcomes will be presented and published in an international peer-reviewed scientific journal.

Discussion

Current literature recommends rehabilitation and patient education on load and pain management as the first-line treatment for all individuals with lateral hip pain, regardless of the severity of hip abductor tendon pathology.6 7 Surgical reconstruction is recommended when rehabilitation fails to alleviate lateral hip pain.36 37 As shown by Mellor et al, patients with gluteal tendinopathies achieve a long-term benefit from exercise and patient education.4 Interestingly, their cohort also comprises tendon ruptures.4

Our clinical experience from a hospital setting is, however, that not all patients benefit from an exercise and patient education intervention, that is, they do not experience pain reduction nor improved physical function. Exercise as the first-line treatment is nonetheless pragmatic, as this treatment strategy may assist in identifying patients with more severe tendon injuries.38 Especially, as clinical tests and MRI protocols to differentiate between hip abductor tendinopathies and tendon ruptures are not yet well described. Our experience from the secondary healthcare is that tendinopathies and tendon ruptures can be difficult to differentiate based on patient history and clinical tests. However, the degree of pelvic instability and time to known pain sets in, as tested with especially the single-leg-stance test/Trendelenburg test, might guide the differentiation. Moreover, our clinical experience is that pain profiles differ in patients with lateral hip pain and might be of a more ‘inflammatory’ character when a tendinopathy is present (pain reduction when active).

In the Danish healthcare system, patients typically receive treatment in primary care before referral to the hospital (secondary healthcare). Thus, patients referred to a hospital setting often have more severe symptoms, longer symptom duration and multiple coexisting conditions39 and might respond differently to exercise interventions compared with those in primary care. Whether rehabilitation should be the first-line treatment and is well tolerated in patients with longstanding lateral hip pain, and possibly gluteal tendon tears, has only been sparsely investigated.4 40 Thus, conducting a prospective physiotherapist-led education and exercise study for patients with MRI-confirmed hip abductor tendon pathologies in the secondary care (hospital setting) is therefore crucial to address this evidence gap.

There are several strengths of the study: first, to optimise the diagnostic certainty in this study, MRI scans are a part of the screening process. MRI scans offer the ability to stratify patients according to the degree of hip abductor tendon pathology. Second, the primary outcome has recently been shown to have high structural validity in patients with hip abductor tendon pathology seen in the secondary sector and includes pain on loading and pain at night.14 41 When asked, patients with lateral hip pain have reported that they especially worry about their way of living and their sleep because of the pain.5 42 43 Hence, investigating changes in pain seems to be the most clinically relevant outcome to investigate.21

Limitations of the current cohort study should be addressed in future research. Notably, the study is conducted at a single site, which may limit the generalisability of the findings. However, patients from all over Denmark are, as of now, referred to the specific teaching hospital where the study takes place. Nonetheless, future studies should consider involving multiple inclusion and exercise sites to enhance external validity. Additionally, the effects of patient education and the exercise intervention cannot be separated in this study. A future study would benefit from (i) multiple groups in order to clarify the individual contributions of each component and (ii) a biopsychosocial approach, which aims to investigate relevant psychological elements in this patient group (fear-avoidance, pain catastrophising and pain self-efficacy). These psychosocial variables may influence exercise engagement in patients with tendinopathy.44 Such a design would also provide a better understanding of the role of the therapeutic alliance between patients and physiotherapists, which could affect adherence and proposedly influence outcomes.
