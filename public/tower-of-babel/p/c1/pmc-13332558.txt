Ultrasound‐Confirmed Median Nerve Pseudoconduction Block due to Anatomical Variation
Authors: Yajing Ye, Bashar Katirji, Robert D Adams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction/AimsConduction block (CB) at non‐entrapment sites is an important electrodiagnostic (EDX) feature supporting acquired demyelinating neuropathies. However, anatomical variation may produce pseudoconduction block, mimicking true CB. Introduction

Conduction block (CB) is a key electrodiagnostic (EDX) finding indicating impaired action potential propagation along a nerve, usually due to nodal dysfunction or segmental demyelination from compression and/or inflammation. CB at non‐entrapment sites, such as the median nerve in the forearm, is an important supportive feature of acquired demyelinating neuropathy, including chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP).

“Pseudoconduction block” describes an EDX pattern mimicking CB that is not caused by demyelination or nodal dysfunction. It may occur with: (1) acute axonal injury when EDX is performed within 5–10 days before Wallerian degeneration is completed [1, 2] (2) anatomical variations [3]; or (3) technical factors. When pseudoconduction block is suspected and technical factors have been excluded, anatomical variations should be explored to avoid misdiagnosis.

Ultrasound can demonstrate nerve enlargement supportive of demyelinating neuropathies and directly identify anatomical variants responsible for pseudoconduction block. It also has been increasingly used to guide nerve conduction studies when nerves demonstrate high interpersonal variability and/or poor reproducibility [4], post‐surgical displacement [5], or technically challenging examinations [6].

We encountered a patient with pseudoconduction block of the median nerve in the forearm due to anatomical variation confirmed by NMUS. We found that practical guidance for EDX localization of an atypically positioned median nerve is limited. We therefore present this patient and a subsequent retrospective ultrasound review addressing three clinically relevant questions:

Methods

Index Patient

A 66‐year‐old man presented to another health care facility with acute‐to‐subacute onset of imbalance, proximal and distal weakness, and distal numbness in all extremities. EDX findings were consistent with a peripheral neuropathy, with markedly slowed conduction velocities and prolonged F‐waves. He was diagnosed with Guillain–Barré syndrome and treated with intravenous immunoglobulin with substantial improvement, leaving only mild imbalance and distal sensory loss.

Five years later, he presented to our neurology outpatient center with mild hip flexion weakness in the setting of mild hyperthyroidism and a normal creatine kinase level. EDX study of the left upper and lower extremities showed features consistent with a predominantly axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy; however, bilateral median motor CBs were noted in the forearm segments despite repeated optimization of stimulation by a senior technologist and a senior EDX medicine specialist. Notably, median nerve F‐wave latencies were borderline prolonged (35.6 ms on the right, 34.6 ms on the left, persistent). CIDP was suspected, but treatment was deferred given mild symptoms and patient preference.

Ten years after his initial presentation to our institution, he returned with frequent falls, increased leg numbness, and gait difficulty largely attributed to advanced degenerative joint disease. Neurological examination showed an antalgic gait, stable distal sensory loss, and areflexia, with no objective weakness. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and laboratory evaluations were unremarkable. Repeat EDX study again demonstrated axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy with persistent, unchanged bilateral median motor CBs in the forearms (Figure 1A), with prolonged F‐wave latencies (39.3 ms) and 80% persistence. This was again confirmed by a senior technologist and a senior EDX medicine specialist.

Given the long‐standing, isolated CBs without clinical progression or supportive features of CIDP, NMUS was performed. Except for bilateral nerve enlargement at the wrists consistent with carpal tunnel syndrome, ultrasound revealed borderline median nerve enlargement in the forearms (cross sectional area was 12.6 mm2 on the right and 12.4 mm2 on the left; normal value < 11 mm2) with normal echogenicity; and enlarged right median nerve in mid‐arm (18.9 mm2; normal value < 14 mm2) with normal echogenicity, likely related to his history of Guillain–Barré syndrome [7]. Notably, both median nerves followed an atypical course at the antecubital fossa, lying approximately 26 mm medial to the brachial artery and underneath the superficial head of the pronator teres (PT). When nerve conduction studies were repeated with ultrasound guided stimulation (Figure 1C,D), no CB was observed (Figure 1B).

Medical Record Review

We conducted a retrospective review of ultrasound images at University Hospitals Cleveland Medical Center over a 9‐month period from Oct 2021 to June 2022. Institutional Review Board approval was waived. Ultrasound studies were identified by including all of the studies performed by a single EDX medicine physician (to minimize measurement variability and ensure standardization) if they studied at least one arm with available images of the median nerve at the antecubital fossa. When both arms were imaged, one side was arbitrarily selected for analysis to avoid statistical oversampling. Because the index patient demonstrated bilateral atypical median nerve courses, it is likely that the median nerve courses in each arm are not completely independent, and therefore we did not include measurements from both arms in this data sample. The only exclusion criterion was prior surgery involving the median nerve or brachial artery.

The ultrasound images were obtained by a GE LOGIQ P9 ultrasound machine (Wauwatosa, Wisconsin, USA) with a 15–6 MHz matrix linear transducer. The median nerve was imaged in the transverse plane at the antecubital fossa with the elbow extended and the transducer oriented perpendicular to the forearm. The coronoid process was used as a standardized landmark by the EDX medicine physician when taking the image at the antecubital fossa (Figure S1). The distance between the midpoint of the median nerve and the midpoint of the brachial artery was measured.

After assessing the distribution of nerve‐artery distances using a histogram, available EDX studies were reviewed for patients with greater nerve‐artery separation than the majority of the cohort. CB was defined as a ≥ 50% reduction in median nerve CMAP amplitude or area across the forearm segment [8]. The frequency of CB among these patients was calculated.

Results

A total of 68 patients were included. No patients met exclusion criteria. In all patients (100%), the median nerve was located medial to the brachial artery, with distances ranging from 2.5 to 26.0 mm (Figure 2).

Histogram of the distance of median nerve from brachial artery in the antecubital fossa.

Because we could identify no established normative cutoff from literature, a cutoff of 12 mm was selected to define extreme values based on the observed data distribution and practical experience, as separations greater than approximately 10–20 mm may fall outside the effective range of standard stimulating electrode placement and increase the likelihood of failing to capture the median nerve.

In 61 of 68 patients (89.7%), the median nerve was located within 12 mm of the brachial artery. In the remaining seven patients (10.3%), the median nerve lay more than 12 mm medial to the artery. These seven patients were reviewed for available EDX studies. Two of the seven did not undergo EDX evaluation because studies were not ordered by the referring provider; in these patients, the median nerve was located 14.2 and 16.7 mm medial to the artery. The remaining five patients underwent median nerve conduction studies and three (60%) reported CB on EDX, with nerve positions of 16.0, 14.9, and 26.0 mm medial to the artery. The patient with 26.0 mm of nerve separation is the index patient (Figure 1), and the reported CB was shown to be pseudoconduction block. Because of the retrospective design, EDX studies of the other two patients were not repeated with ultrasound‐guided stimulation, and therefore the nature of the reported CB could not be determined.

Discussion

Although limited by small sample size, our case report and retrospective data review highlight the clinical importance of anatomical variation of the median nerve at the antecubital fossa as a cause of pseudoconduction block. Though the median nerve typically courses just medial to the brachial artery before passing between two heads of the PT, the nerve‐artery distance can vary substantially. Reliance on the brachial artery pulse as the sole landmark may lead to submaximal stimulation of the median nerve when the nerve follows an atypical course.

When CB is observed without temporal dispersion, with normal F‐wave persistence and latencies, or in an atypical clinical context, pseudoconduction block due to anatomical variants should be considered after excluding technical reasons, particularly submaximal stimulation of the median nerve in obese or heavily muscled individuals. NMUS is a valuable adjunct in such cases, allowing direct visualization of the nerve course and can improve diagnostic accuracy [9]. Based on our findings, pseudoconduction block of the median nerve when stimulating at the antecubital fossa may result from a markedly medial nerve position, a deep course beneath the PT (in the majority, at the level of trochlea/coronoid process, the median nerve is located lateral to the superficial head of PT, then passes underneath the superficial head of PT [10]), or both.

When pseudoconduction block of the median nerve in the forearm is suspected, we recommend stimulating 15–25 mm medial to the brachial artery pulse or slightly more medial and proximal between the biceps tendon and brachialis muscle, with caution to avoid ulnar nerve co‐stimulation.
