Enhancing the Study of Long-Term Pulmonary Sequelae After COVID-19: Methodological Perspectives on Imaging and Clinical Integration
Authors: Weikai Dong, Zhikang Lv, Qiang Wang
Published: 2026

We have read with great interest the recent publication by Song et al. [1], entitled “Long-Term Pulmonary Function and Radiologic Abnormalities Up to 3 Years After COVID-19: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis.” The authors’ comprehensive evidence shows that when fibrotic changes are stable, the diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide, forced vital capacity, and ground-glass opacities gradually improve. This work helps to understand the long-term pulmonary sequelae after coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) infection. However, there are several methodological considerations that need to be discussed to strengthen future research in this field.

First, the study used qualitative computed tomography (CT) descriptors rather than quantitative imaging markers. Most modern chest imaging techniques use quantitative CT to examine subtle interstitial abnormalities. A reconstruction algorithm based on deep learning can improve the quality of CT images and enhance the visualization of subtle lung structures, which may help detect fine parenchymal abnormalities [2]. In the future, addition of standard quantitative CT analysis to meta-analyses may improve the accuracy and repeatability of long-term image evaluation.

Second, the trends in studying and evaluating structure and function were not associated with clinical outcomes. Machine learning models that combine CT features with clinical variables can predict the severity and critical progression of COVID-19, demonstrating the prognostic value of integrating imaging and clinical data [3]. Correlating radiological findings with clinically significant endpoints (such as symptom burden, risk of hospitalization, and long-term respiratory morbidity) can enhance the clinical relevance of follow-up imaging studies.

Third, differences in CT interpretation among various studies may have an impact on the summary of the imaging results. Deep learning can automatically extract lung and cardiovascular indicators from chest CT without manual or visual evaluation, quickly standardize the quantification of imaging features, and predict the management of COVID-19 patients [4]. Standardized AI-driven tools in multicenter studies can reduce the differences among readers and enhance comparability among institutions.

Finally, persistent radioactive abnormalities related to COVID-19 may reflect heterogeneous pathological processes, rather than a single form of fibrosis. A systematic review of prolonged severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection shows that its imaging manifestations are diverse, including inflammation and organized pneumonia-like changes, which may evolve over time [5]. A more detailed classification of these radioactive phenotypes may help clarify the mechanism of long-term pulmonary abnormalities and their reversibility.

In conclusion, Song et al. [1] provided valuable evidence of long-term pulmonary function recovery after COVID-19. Future studies adopting quantitative imaging biomarkers, stratified variation, standard reporting frameworks, and clinically integrated results may be used to better understand the post-COVID-19 pulmonary sequelae. We offer suggestions for refining an already exceptional article and anticipate future insightful contributions from the authors.
