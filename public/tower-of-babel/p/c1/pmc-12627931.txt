Physical Functioning Report and Pulmonary Function Deficit in Childhood and Adolescent Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia Survivors: A National Cross‐Sectional ALL‐STAR Lungs Study
Authors: Sonja Izquierdo Riis Meyer, Mette Tiedemann Skipper, Birgitte Klug Albertsen, Ruta Tuckuviene, Peder Skov Wehner, Thomas Leth Frandsen, Kjeld Schmiegelow, Liv Andrés‐Jensen, Kim Gjerum Nielsen, Sune Leisgaard Mørck Rubak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundChildhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) survivors face an increased risk of pulmonary function deficit (PFD), which may affect physical functioning. We aimed to evaluate parent proxy‐ and self‐reported physical functioning and the association with PFD and abnormal bronchodilator response ( + BDR) among survivors versus controls.MethodsThis cross‐sectional study (February 2019–May 2024) included N = 184 ALL survivors (aged 5–17.9 years) from a national NOPHO ALL2008 protocol‐treated cohort (eligible, N = 295) ( ≥ 1 year after treatment cessation) and 207 controls. Physical functioning was assessed using the Paediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL) questionnaire. Pulmonary function tests (PFTs) identified PFD (abnormal PFT) and +BDR according to ERS/ATS criteria. Ordinal logistic regression and t‐test compared groups.ResultsALL survivor parents proxy‐reported significantly poorer physical functioning than controls (mean difference [MD] −13.3 95% CI [−16.3; −10.3] to MD −10.3 [−17.2; −3.3], p < 0.001 to p = 0.004), but clinically significant impairments were restricted to parent proxy‐reports from survivors treated with high‐risk (HR) chemotherapy (MD −19.9 [−29.0; −10.7] to −11.5 [−17.5; −5.6], p < 0.001) and stem cell transplantation (HR‐SCT) (MD −24.7 [−30.1; −19.4] to −16.9 [−27.4; −6.5], p < 0.001 to p = 0.002) as well as self‐reports from HR (MD −11.2 [−18.9; −3.4], p = 0.006) and HR‐SCT survivors (MD −14.5 [−24.1; −4.8], p = 0.004). Among 5–15‐year‐olds, PFD/ + BDR was associated with significantly poorer parent proxy‐reported physical functioning in survivors versus controls (MD −14.3 [−21.2; −7.3], p < 0.001). Significant deficits in these groups related to running, engaging in sports/exercise and lifting heavy objects. PFD/ + BDR was not associated with poorer physical functioning reports within survivors (MD −2.5 [−9.0; 3.3] to 0.1 [−13.7; 13.8], p = 0.36–0.99).ConclusionsPhysical functioning in survivors compared to controls varies with intensity of treatment, most evident in HR and HR‐SCT survivors, with impairments linked to PFD and +BDR in the youngest. Among survivors, physical functioning did not align with PFD and +BDR status, highlighting the need for parallel assessment of objective and subjective measures during follow‐up. Baseline PFT and tailored follow‐up are essential for high‐risk groups, to detect PFD early and intervene.Keywords: bronchodilator response, leukemia survivors, physical functioning, pulmonary function deficit, pulmonary function test in infants/children

Introduction

Over the past decades, acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), the most common childhood cancer, has become highly treatable, achieving some of the highest survival rates among pediatric malignancies in developed countries [1, 2]. Despite advances, long‐term survivors face late effects [3] impacting their quality of life (QoL) [4]. Thus, pulmonary function deficit (PFD) (abnormal pulmonary function) is a concern due to its association with reduced QoL [5, 6] and its potential to compromise physical functioning [7, 8, 9].

Health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) is well explored in pediatric ALL patients, with several reviews addressing the topic [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. Some studies report impaired physical functioning during treatment compared to healthy peers [9, 16], while stem cell transplantation (SCT) has been associated with lower QoL in ALL survivors [17, 18, 19]. Furthermore, severe chronic health conditions strongly predict physical health in childhood leukemia survivors [20]. Specifically, decreased physical QoL has been associated with PFD among pediatric lymphoma survivors [6]. A recent study in ALL survivors reported poorer proxy‐ and self‐reported physical functioning among 5–7‐year‐old survivors than in reference data [21]. The study also found that among 8–25‐year‐old ALL survivors, a higher parent‐rated toxicity burden (relative to other children with ALL) was associated with poorer self‐reported physical functioning [21]. Most previous studies provide insights into pulmonary late effects [22, 23], QoL [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 21, 24, 25], or physical capacity [16, 26, 27]. However, the studies do not agree on the overall HRQoL and physical functioning deficit among ALL survivors. Furthermore, none of these studies have explored late effects such as PFD and the association with the QoL subdomain physical functioning in childhood ALL survivors. With the growing population of ALL survivors and the potential long‐term impact on physical functioning, understanding the association between PFD and perceived physical functioning is crucial for improving follow‐up care. Notably, this association has not been studied in childhood ALL survivors treated with a contemporary protocol. Because treatment intensity and associated late effects differ, risk stratification of ALL survivors (e.g., treated with standard‐, intermediate‐, high‐risk chemotherapy or high‐risk chemotherapy followed by SCT) provides important context when evaluating pulmonary and physical outcomes.

This study aimed to examine and compare proxy‐ and self‐reported physical functioning among childhood ALL survivors to control subjects, overall and across risk groups. A secondary aim was to explore the association between PFD and poorer physical functioning—defined as lower scores in the Paediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL) Physical Functioning subdomain—and between abnormal bronchodilator response ( + BDR), as airway hyperresponsiveness may reflect asthma‐like changes or treatment‐related airway injury in ALL survivors, and poorer physical functioning in ALL survivors versus control subjects. We hypothesized that (I) ALL survivors report poorer physical functioning than controls, (II) survivors with PFD/ + BDR report poorer physical functioning than those without, and (III) among those with PFD/ + BDR, survivors report poorer physical functioning than control subjects.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

We conducted a national cross‐sectional study as part of the Danish Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia Survivor Toxicity And Rehabilitation (ALL‐STAR) study that assesses subjective and objective treatment‐related morbidity in children and young adult ALL survivors [28]. The study included ALL survivors treated according to the Nordic Society of Paediatric Haematology and Oncology (NOPHO) ALL2008 protocol at all four Danish pediatric oncology treatment centers [2]. Participant recruitment and clinical examinations across all organ systems were performed at Copenhagen University Hospital and Aarhus University Hospital between February 2019 and May 2024. The study report adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [29]. A research team of 1‐2 trained research nurses, assistants and a physician at both sites collected the data. Data were managed with Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) tools hosted at The Capital Region of Denmark [30, 31].

Study Population

The eligible study population included children and adolescents, ALL survivors and healthy community control subjects < 18 years. Eligible ALL survivors were treated according to the NOPHO ALL2008 protocol from July 2008 to October 2018 and recruited from the NOPHO registry. They included relapse‐treated ALL survivors, treated for philadelphia chromosome‐negative B‐cell precursor (BCP) or T‐cell ALL enrolled ≥ 1 year after completing treatment (N = 295) [28]. Eligible community control subjects included age‐ and gender‐matched 1:1 chemotherapy‐naive individuals who were not first‐degree relatives to survivors, recruited through participating survivors and social media. Participants aged 5–17.9 at the time of examination were included if they completed ≥ one valid pulmonary function test (PFT) and the proxy‐ and/or self‐reported physical functioning questionnaire.

Outcomes

Participant Characteristics

ALL treatment data were collected from the NOPHO registry: final treatment risk‐stratified subgroups, that is, standard risk (SR), intermediate risk (IR), high risk (HR) chemotherapy and HR chemotherapy followed by stem cell transplantation (HR‐SCT). Please refer to Supporting Information S1: E‐Figure 1, Frandsen et al. 2014 [32], for a NOPHO ALL2008 overview. Survivors treated according to the same risk‐stratified subgroup (SR, II, HR, HR‐SCT) were referred to as “SR survivors” and so forth. Height and Body Mass Index (BMI) were collected at examination, and age‐ and gender‐adjusted z‐scores were calculated using published Danish reference data [33, 34].

Questionnaire‐Reported Outcomes

An electronic questionnaire was sent to participants shortly before their ALL‐STAR study examination date and was completed by the examination day. The questionnaire included proxy‐ and self‐reported current smoking status and the physical functioning domain of the PedsQL generic Core Scales version 4.0 questionnaire completed by participants before their examination date [35]. PedsQL is also validated in pediatric cancer patients and survivors [36, 37]. The physical functioning domain consisted of eight items (questions, referred to as “item(s)”) addressing the extent of a problem the child had experienced over the past month (e.g., “Problems with running,” details in Supporting Information S1: E‐Table 1a) [35]. The items utilized a 5‐point Likert scale for both parent proxy‐report and child self‐report (range 0–100, referred to as “item(s) score(s)”): 0: Almost always, 25: Often, 50: Sometimes, 75: Almost never, 100: Never. The physical functioning summary score (referred to as “physical functioning”) was calculated as the mean score of completed items, with a higher score indicating better physical functioning (maximum score 100) and a lower score indicating poorer physical functioning (minimum score 0). Measures included two modes (proxy‐ and self‐report) and two age groups based on the age at the examination: 5–15 years (proxy‐report) and 15–17.9 years (both proxy‐ and self‐report).

Pulmonary Outcomes

A blood sample for hemoglobin concentration (to correct hemoglobin pulmonary diffusing capacity) and PFTs were collected during the examination (Supporting Information S1: E‐Table 1a) [28]. All PFTs were conducted, quality assessed in real time and validated by a trained operator according to the European Respiratory Society (ERS) and the American Thoracic Society (ATS) recommendations [38, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45]. The tests included the nitrogen multiple breath washout test (N2MBW) for lung clearance index (LCI) [38, 39, 40, 41], spirometry assessing forced expiratory volume in the first second (FEV1) [42], impulse oscillometry (IOS) for airway resistance at a frequency of 5 Hz (R5Hz) [43], diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO) [44] and nitric oxide (DLNO) [45] and bronchodilator response (BDR) [42, 43]. PFTs underwent post‐hoc validation by another member of the research team. Standard reference population and equations used for z‐score calculations are detailed in Supporting Information S1: E‐Table 1b. Included participants were categorized as “PFD” if they had ≥ one valid abnormal PFT defined as LCI, R5Hz z‐score > 1.645 and/or FEV1, DLNO/DLCO z‐score < ‐1.645 from the predicted value [46, 47, 48, 49, 50, 51]. Those with a valid BDR were categorized as “+BDR” (abnormal BDR) if FEV1 BDR ≥ 10% and/or R5Hz BDR < ‐40% [43, 52] (details in Supporting Information S1: E‐Table 1b). Information on participants’ current medication use was not collected.

Statistical Methods

Statistical tests were two‐sided (α = 0.05), employed a noncausal, descriptive approach with unadjusted estimates and conducted using Stata/SE 18 (StataCorp LP, College Station, Texas, USA).

Descriptive statistics were used to compare survivor and control demographics. Continuous variables were expressed as means with standard deviations (SD) and medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) depending on whether normality could be assumed. Dichotomous variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Unpaired two‐sample t‐test or Wilcoxon rank‐sum/Mann‐Whitney U‐test and one‐way ANOVA or Kruskal‐Wallis test were used for continuous variables depending on whether normality could be assumed. Fisher's exact test was used for dichotomous variables. Physical functioning summary scores and item scores were evaluated across (I) Survivors versus control subjects, (II) Survivors stratified by final risk groups (SR, IR, HR, HR‐SCT) and control subjects, (III) control subjects with and without PFD, and control subjects with and without +BDR, respectively, (IV) Survivors with and without PFD and survivors with and without +BDR, respectively and (V) Survivors and control subjects with PFD and +BDR, respectively. The physical functioning item score scale was reversed before ordinal logistic regression was applied to compare them to simplify the odds ratio (OR) interpretation. A positive OR indicated higher odds—and thus higher risk—of a poorer item score than the reference group. An unpaired t‐test was used to compare physical functioning summary scores. A negative mean difference (MD) signified a poorer mean physical functioning summary score than the reference group. Analyses were adjusted using PFT z‐scores for age, gender and height. MD and OR were reported with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). A sub‐analysis compared the characteristics of included survivors with those with invalid PFTs, no PFT performed (including those who declined) and ALL‐treated patients who were deceased or lost to follow‐up (LTFU). To validate control representativness, a two sample unpaired t‐test was applied to compare control subjects’ physical functioning scores to those of healthy controls from the original PedsQL study [35].

Ethics

The ALL‐STAR study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Regional Ethics Committee for The Capital Region of Denmark (H‐18035090/H‐20006359) and by the Danish Data Protection Agency (VD‐2018–519). Oral and written informed consent was obtained from all participants’ parents or legal guardians.

Results

Participant Demographics

The study included 184 survivors and 207 control subjects (Figure 1), with a survivor recruitment rate of > 62% (184/295). Eight survivors and six control subjects were excluded due to invalid PFTs or incomplete questionnaires. Survivors were shorter (mean 151.5 ± 17.2 cm vs. 155.7 ± 16.8 cm, p = 0.015 and mean z‐score −0.15 ± 1.14 vs. 0.36 ± 1.04, p < 0.001) and had higher BMI z‐scores than control subjects (0.41 ± 1.28 vs. 0.15 ± 0.99, p = 0.025) (Table 1). Gender, smoking, age and BMI were similar across groups. The risk group distribution was similar between included survivors (SR 42.4%, IR 38.0%, HR 9.2%, HR‐SCT 10.3%) and those who were excluded or who declined participation (p = 0.108) (Table 4). Compared with excluded survivors, included survivors were older at examination (11.92 ± 2.95 vs. 8.06 ± 2.12 years, p = 0.001), taller (in cm) (151.49 ± 17.22 vs. 132.93 ± 11.72 cm p = 0.005) and had a longer follow‐up time since ALL diagnosis to their examination day (7.15 ± 2.37 vs. 5.20 ± 1.50 years, p = 0.032) (Supporting Information S1: E‐Table 4).

Questionnaire‐Reported Physical Functioning

Proxy‐Reported Physical Functioning, 5–15 Years

We included 157 survivors and 173 control subjects in this age group. Survivors reported significantly poorer physical functioning than control subjects (80.4 ± 18.4 vs. 93.7 ± 8.0, MD −13.3 95% CI [−16.3; −10.3], p < 0.001) (Table 2) and across all risk‐stratified subgroups (SR, IR, HR and HR‐SCT) (82.7 ± 15.5, 80.0 ± 19.4, 82.2 ± 22.9, 69.0 ± 22.2 vs. 93.7 ± 8.0, MDs −11.0 [−14.0; −8.0] to −24.7 [−30.1; −19.4], p < 0.001) (Supporting Information S1: E‐Tables 2, 3a, Figure 2). HR‐SCT survivors reported significantly poorer physical functioning than SR survivors (MD ‐13.8 [‐23.5; ‐4.0], p = 0.006) (Supporting Information S1: E‐Table 3b). For individual item scores, survivors overall and across all risk‐stratified subgroups reported significantly poorer outcomes than control subjects in most areas, for example, walking > 100 meters, running, sports/exercise engagement and heavy lifting (OR 6.2 [3.1; 12.4] to 50.1 [8.5; 295.3], p = 0.000–0.018). Control subjects reported significantly higher physical functioning than healthy controls from the original PedsQL study [35] (93.7 ± 8.0 vs. 89.32 ± 16.32, MD = 4.38 [1.87; 6.89], p = 0.0006).

Proxy‐Reported Physical Functioning, 15–17.9 Years

This age group included 27 survivors and 32 control subjects with completed proxy‐reports. Survivors reported significantly poorer physical functioning than control subjects (81.0 ± 16.9 vs. 91.3 ± 9.1, MD −10.3 [−17.2; −3.3], p = 0.004) (Table 2), particularly among HR survivors (71.4 ± 17.2 vs. 91.3 ± 9.1, MD −19.9 [−29.0; −10.7], p < 0.001) and HR‐SCT survivors (74.4 ± 18.9 vs. 91.3 ± 9.1, MD −16.9 [−27.4; −6.5], p = 0.002) (Supporting Information S1: E‐Tables 2, 3a, Figure 2). HR and HR‐SCT survivors had significantly poorer item scores than control subjects for running and sports/exercise engagement (OR 9.9 [1.4; 70.7] to 37.6 [5.0; 283.3], p = 0.000 to 0.023). Additionally, HR‐SCT survivors reported poorer scores for heavy lifting (OR 29.3 [3.4; 252.8], p = 0.002) and energy level (OR 15.5 [1.5; 158.1], p = 0.020). Control subjects reported similar physical functioning compared to healthy controls from the original PedsQL study [35] (91.3 ± 9.1 vs. 89.32 ± 16.32, MD = 1.98 [−3.73; 7.69], p = 0.4959).

Self‐Reported Physical Functioning, 15–17.9 Years

We included 26 survivors and 34 control subjects in this age group with completed self‐reports. Survivors and control subjects reported similar physical functioning overall (86.2 vs. 91.4, MD −5.2 [−10.9; 0.5], p = 0.072) (Table 2). HR and HR‐SCT survivors reported significantly poorer physical functioning than control subjects (80.2 vs. 91.4, MD −11.2 [−18.9; −3.4], p = 0.006 and 76.9 vs. 91.4, MD −14.5 [−24.1; −4.8], p = 0.004) (Supporting Information S1: E‐Tables 2, 3a, Figure 2). HR survivors reported significantly poorer item scores than control subjects for pain/aches (OR 6.9 [1.0; 46.4], p = 0.046) and running (OR 7.4 [1.3; 40.5], p = 0.021). In comparison, HR‐SCT survivors reported poorer item scores than control subjects for sports/exercise engagement (OR 13.4 [1.6; 113.3], p = 0.017) and heavy lifting (OR 13.4 [1.8; 101.3], p = 0.012). Control subjects reported significantly higher physical functioning than healthy controls from the original PedsQL study [35] (91.4 ± 7.8 vs. 84.41 ± 17.26, MD = 6.99 [1.12; 12.86], p = 0.0198).

Discussion

Our study confirmed that across all risk groups, ALL survivors aged 5–15 reported poorer physical functioning than control subjects, based on proxy reports. Furthermore, HR and HR‐SCT survivors aged 5–17.9, along with their parents, reported significantly poorer physical functioning than control subjects. Additionally, parents of 5–15‐year‐old survivors with PFD/ + BDR reported significantly poorer physical functioning than control subjects. We observed significantly poorer item score reports, especially problems regarding running, sports/exercise engagement and heavy lifting. These are all physical tasks that require endurance and strength. However, other factors, such as dyspnea and obesity in survivors, can be associated with physical functioning impairment [53, 54]. Indeed, survivors had higher BMI z‐scores than controls, reflecting differences in body composition or posttreatment lifestyle patterns, which may have contributed to differences in perceived physical functioning. Interestingly, physical functioning among survivors was comparable regardless of PFD/ + BDR status. This was unexpected, given that PFD is typically associated with diminished physical capability [55], as evidenced by self‐reports from 15 to 17.9‐year‐old control subjects. This suggests that objective pulmonary impairments and subjective physical functioning may not correlate directly. One possible explanation could be that survivors with PFD/ + BDR unintentionally adopt compensatory strategies [56], such as pacing or resting during activities. Similar strategies have been observed in pediatric brain tumor survivors [57]. Another explanation suggests that exertional dyspnea, may persist subclinically until a significant decline in pulmonary function becomes detectable [58, 59]. Our results therefore underline the importance of combining objective PFTs with patient‐reported outcomes, since symptoms alone may fail to capture subclinical impairments and potential long‐term risk. As expected, parents of survivors aged 5–15 years with PFD or +BDR reported poorer physical functioning than control subjects.

Our findings align with prior reports of impairments in ALL [21] and HR‐SCT survivors [11, 17, 18, 19]. Moreover, our findings are consistent with objective impaired physical capacity in ALL survivors [60].

Parents of younger survivors tended to report poorer functioning than controls, whereas adolescent survivors reported functioning closer to peers. These observations emphasize the value of including both parent proxy‐ and self‐reports. Proxy‐reports may capture subtle limitations that children themselves underreport, while self‐reports increasingly gain validity with age. Future studies should examine trajectories and consider directly comparing proxy and self‐reports with objective measures of physical functioning to better understand their clinical meaning and to guide follow‐up strategies.

We observed that 5–15‐year‐old HR‐SCT survivors (proxy‐report: 69.0), 15–17.9‐year‐old HR survivors (proxy‐report: 71.4) and HR‐SCT survivors (self‐report: 76.9) reported almost similar or poorer physical functioning than chronically ill children (proxy: 73.28, self: 77.36) in the PedsQL 4.0 validation study [35]. In addition, the MDs in physical functioning between HR/HR‐SCT survivors and control subjects, both proxy‐ (−24.7 to −16.9) and self‐reported (−14.5 to −11.2), exceeded the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) reported in the validation study (proxy‐report: −16, self‐report: −7) [35]. Thus, these differences in HR and HR‐SCT survivors can be considered clinically meaningful. Notably, SR and IR survivors, comprising the majority of ALL survivors ( ~80%), did not exhibit clinically significant deficits compared to control subjects. These findings highlight that clinical relevance is confined to HR and HR‐SCT survivors, which has important implications for risk‐adapted follow‐up and intervention and the value of coupling proxy‐ and self‐reported outcomes with PFTs to capture physical functioning impairments in these subgroups. This supports the need for screening and early interventions in these subgroups to improve long‐term pulmonary and physical outcomes [61, 62, 63].

Interestingly, physical activity initiated during in‐hospital treatment has demonstrated positive long‐term effects on muscle strength and cardiorespiratory fitness among childhood cancer patients [64] and sustained physically active lifestyles among childhood leukemia survivors [65]. Similar approaches may be effective in pediatric ALL survivors. Implementing physical activity programs during treatment, alongside medical intervention, should be considered for HR and HR‐SCT patients and those with respiratory symptoms, PFD and/or +BDR. For HR and HR‐SCT patients we recommend baseline PFT and tailored follow‐up monitoring with novel PFTs (LCI, DLCO, and DLNO) to enhance the timely detection of PFD and +BDR [66]. The NOPHO ALL Together protocol, implemented in 2019, incorporated reduced HR and HR‐SCT stratification enabled by biological and precision treatment advancements. This can potentially reduce the risk of pulmonary and physical impairments. However, future studies should evaluate this and the effect of the approaches discussed above on perceived and objective physical functioning and pulmonary function.

Strengths and Limitations

This study's strengths include its large, uniformly treated ALL survivor cohort, robust questionnaire and PFT data collection, and the use of matched control subjects. Including both proxy‐ and self‐reports provided a comprehensive perspective on physical impairments. The survivor participation rate ( > 62%) exceeded other study accomplishments [21], minimizing selection bias. The validated PedsQL questionnaire ensured reliable measurement of physical functioning [35], while PFT height‐, gender‐ and age‐adjusted z‐scores mitigated confounding and improved interpretability [46, 47, 48, 49, 50, 51]. Control subjects reported similar or slightly better physical functioning than healthy controls from the PedsQL normative data [35], potentially reflecting differences in sample composition or response tendencies. This may have slightly overestimated survivor impairments; however, alignment with control adolescent proxy reports suggests limited impact. Limitations include the cross‐sectional design, which limits causal inferences between PFD and physical functioning impairments. Participation decline and incomplete questionnaires from survivors may have introduced selection bias, which could have over‐ or underestimated our findings. Moreover, recruiting control subjects via social media may likely have attracted more health‐conscious participants, introducing self‐selection bias and potentially overestimating differences in physical functioning. The known challenge in obtaining valid PFTs in younger children could have limited our findings' generalizability due to their underrepresentation in the analyses. Confounding factors like activity level, socioeconomic status and medication use may have influenced results and small sample sizes in the 15–17.9 age group likely reduced the statistical power. Finally, our findings reflect physical functioning outcomes at a mean of 7 years post‐ALL diagnosis, highlighting the need to consider long‐term survivorship trajectories. Although cross‐sectional by design and with a relatively short follow‐up time that may not acknowledge the full burden of ALL and treatment, our results stress that physical impairments may persist for many years after treatment, underscoring the clinical value of combining PFTs and patient‐reported outcomes in follow‐up care to guide timely interventions. These aspects underscore the need for longitudinal future studies to strengthen the validity and generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

This study shows that physical functioning in childhood ALL survivors varies with treatment intensity, with HR and HR‐SCT survivors experiencing evident impairments, including associations with PFD and +BDR in the youngest survivors. Importantly, physical functioning among survivors was not consistently aligned with pulmonary outcomes (PFD or +BDR), underscoring that pulmonary objective and subjective outcomes do not necessarily overlap and should be assessed in parallel during follow‐up. Our findings underscore the need for baseline PFT and tailored follow‐up in HR and HR‐SCT survivors to detect PFD and physical activity intervention during treatment to mitigate functional decline.
