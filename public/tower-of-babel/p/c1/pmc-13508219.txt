Psychological distress and quality of life in women with infertility due to polycystic ovarian syndrome versus unexplained infertility: a cross-sectional study with phenotype-based analysis
Authors: Kamna Kataria, Garima Kapoor, Rahul Saha, Abhishek Lachyan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInfertility is associated with significant psychological distress and impaired quality of life (QoL) in women. Polycystic Ovarian Syndrome (PCOS), a common cause of infertility, is frequently associated with metabolic and psychological symptoms. However, limited evidence directly compares psychological symptom burden and QoL between women with PCOS-related infertility and unexplained infertility, including across PCOS phenotypes.MethodsThis cross-sectional observational study included 240 women aged 18–40 years with infertility due to PCOS or unexplained causes, conducted at a tertiary care center between October 2019 and March 2021. PCOS was diagnosed using revised Rotterdam criteria, and unexplained infertility was defined after exclusion of known causes. Psychological outcomes were assessed using validated screening instruments (PHQ-9, BDI, BAI, and GAD-7), representing symptom severity rather than clinical psychiatric diagnoses. QoL was assessed using WHOQOL-BREF. Statistical analysis included group comparisons using appropriate parametric and non-parametric tests, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.ResultsWomen with PCOS demonstrated significantly higher screening-based depressive symptom burden (60% vs. 14%, p < 0.001) and anxiety symptom burden (63% vs. 21%, p < 0.001) compared to women with unexplained infertility. QoL scores were significantly lower in the PCOS group (66.8% vs. 85.1%, p < 0.001), particularly in psychological and social domains. Among PCOS phenotypes, hyperandrogenic phenotypes (B and C) showed relatively higher psychological symptom burden and lower QoL scores compared to other phenotypes. The PCOS group also demonstrated higher BMI and androgen levels.ConclusionInfertility associated with PCOS is linked to higher screening-based psychological symptom burden and reduced quality of life compared to unexplained infertility. Hyperandrogenic PCOS phenotypes appear to be more affected. These findings support the integration of routine psychological screening and multidisciplinary support into infertility care for women with PCOS.

Introduction

Infertility is a major public health concern affecting both men and women worldwide. Infertility is defined as the inability to achieve pregnancy after 12 months or more of regular unprotected sexual intercourse. Globally, infertility affects nearly one in four couples in developing countries, highlighting its significant clinical and psychosocial burden [1]. In India, the prevalence of primary infertility among women of reproductive age is estimated at approximately 8.9%, reflecting a substantial healthcare challenge [2].

Beyond its reproductive implications, infertility is strongly associated with psychological distress, particularly among women. Societal expectations, cultural pressures, and personal aspirations related to motherhood contribute significantly to emotional vulnerability in this population. Multiple studies using validated screening instruments have reported increased depressive and anxiety symptom burden and reduced quality of life among infertile women compared to fertile controls. A systematic review reported prevalence rates of depression and anxiety symptoms of 44.32% and 36.17%, respectively, among infertile women in low- and middle-income countries [3]. Importantly, these estimates are primarily based on screening instruments rather than structured clinical diagnostic interviews and therefore reflect symptom burden rather than confirmed psychiatric disorders.

In the Indian context as well, studies have demonstrated considerable psychological morbidity among infertile women, with depressive and anxiety symptoms ranging from mild to severe severity levels [4, 5]. These psychological symptoms may negatively influence treatment adherence, interpersonal relationships, and overall quality of life. Conversely, strong social support has been shown to reduce psychological distress in this population [6]. Recent reviews have further highlighted the complex psychosocial and emotional burden associated with infertility and assisted reproductive care, emphasizing the importance of holistic and multidisciplinary support approaches [7].

Polycystic Ovarian Syndrome is one of the most common endocrine disorders affecting women of reproductive age and is a leading cause of infertility. The prevalence of PCOS varies widely depending on diagnostic criteria and population characteristics, with reported global estimates ranging from 6% to 26%, while Indian prevalence estimates range between 3.7% and 22.5% [8, 9]. PCOS is characterized by hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and polycystic ovarian morphology and contributes to infertility in approximately 70–80% of affected women [10, 11]. In addition to reproductive dysfunction, PCOS is associated with metabolic disturbances and psychological symptoms, including depression and anxiety.

Emerging evidence suggests that women with PCOS exhibit higher levels of psychological distress compared to the general population, with reported anxiety and depressive symptom prevalence of approximately 38.6% and 25.7%, respectively [12]. These psychological manifestations are believed to be influenced by hormonal imbalance, metabolic dysfunction, body image disturbances, and infertility-related stress.

However, despite increasing recognition of the psychological impact of PCOS, limited studies have directly compared psychological outcomes between women with PCOS-related infertility and those with unexplained infertility. Moreover, PCOS is a heterogeneous disorder with distinct phenotypes based on combinations of hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and ovarian morphology, and the psychological differences across these phenotypes remain insufficiently explored.

Therefore, the present study was undertaken to compare depression, anxiety symptom burden, and quality of life between women with infertility due to PCOS and unexplained infertility. Additionally, differences across PCOS phenotypes were evaluated to better understand phenotype-specific psychological burden. All psychological outcomes were assessed using validated screening instruments representing symptom severity rather than formal psychiatric diagnoses. Understanding these associations may help identify high-risk subgroups and support development of multidisciplinary infertility care models integrating reproductive, metabolic, and psychological assessment.

Materials and methods

This cross-sectional observational study was conducted in the Department of Obstetrics and Gynaecology, VMMC and Safdarjung Hospital, between October 2019 and March 2021. The study included women aged 18–40 years diagnosed with infertility due to polycystic ovarian syndrome or unexplained infertility. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional ethics committee, and confidentiality of participant data was strictly maintained throughout the study.

Eligible participants were recruited consecutively from women attending the infertility clinic at our tertiary care center during the study period to minimize selection bias. Most participants were evaluated during initial infertility assessment prior to initiation of assisted reproductive treatment. Women with previously diagnosed psychiatric disorders or severe systemic illnesses were excluded to minimize confounding influences on psychological outcomes.

Participants were divided into two groups. Women in the PCOS group were diagnosed according to the revised Rotterdam criteria, requiring at least two of the following: oligo- or anovulation (defined as menstrual cycles shorter than 21 days, longer than 35 days, fewer than eight cycles per year, or primary amenorrhea by 15 years of age); clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism (including hirsutism, acne, alopecia, or elevated serum testosterone levels); and polycystic ovarian morphology on ultrasonography (follicle number per ovary > 20 and/or ovarian volume > 10 mL). Women in the unexplained infertility group were diagnosed after exclusion of all identifiable causes of infertility through standard evaluation, including ovulation assessment, tubal patency testing, and semen analysis of the male partner.

Data collection included detailed clinical history covering demographic characteristics, menstrual and obstetric history, psychosexual factors, medical and psychiatric history, and prior infertility treatments. A comprehensive physical examination was performed, including body mass index (BMI) calculation, assessment of clinical hyperandrogenism, and gynecological examination. Biochemical evaluation included day-2 luteinizing hormone, serum testosterone, dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate, lipid profile, and fasting blood glucose levels. Male partners underwent semen analysis and routine infectious disease screening.

Psychological assessment

Psychological outcomes were assessed using validated self-report screening instruments. These tools were used to measure symptom severity and psychological distress and not to establish formal psychiatric diagnoses based on DSM or ICD criteria.

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), with scores > 9 indicating clinically significant depressive symptomatology. Anxiety symptoms were assessed using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scale, with scores > 7 indicating clinically significant anxiety symptoms. Severity of depressive symptoms was further evaluated using the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), and anxiety severity was assessed using the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI). These instruments were used in combination to capture different dimensions of psychological distress and improve measurement robustness.

Quality of life assessment

Quality of life was assessed using the World Health Organization Quality of Life-BREF (WHOQOL-BREF) questionnaire, which evaluates physical, psychological, social, and environmental domains. Higher WHOQOL-BREF scores indicate better quality of life.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 21.0. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test as appropriate. Normality of continuous variables was assessed prior to selection of parametric or non-parametric tests. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages and analyzed using chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Psychological symptom burden (screening-based outcomes)

Women with PCOS demonstrated significantly higher screening-based psychological symptom burden compared to women with unexplained infertility. Depressive symptom burden (PHQ-9 > 9) was significantly higher in the PCOS group (60.0% vs. 14.0%; p < 0.001). Similarly, anxiety symptom burden (GAD-7 > 7) was more frequent in the PCOS group (63.3% vs. 21.0%; p < 0.001), as shown in Table 3.

Other screening-based symptom patterns, including somatoform-related, panic-related, and eating-related symptoms, were also more frequently observed in the PCOS group.

Discussion

The present study demonstrated significantly higher screening-based depressive and anxiety symptom burden and lower quality-of-life scores among women with polycystic ovarian syndrome-related infertility compared to women with unexplained infertility. Hyperandrogenic PCOS phenotypes also demonstrated relatively greater psychological burden and poorer quality of life. These findings highlight the multidimensional impact of PCOS-related infertility on psychological well-being.

PCOS was diagnosed according to the revised Rotterdam criteria, requiring the presence of at least two of three features: oligo/anovulation, hyperandrogenism, and polycystic ovarian morphology [12]. This widely accepted criterion ensured uniformity in case selection. Women with unexplained infertility were diagnosed after standard evaluation excluded ovulatory dysfunction, tubal factor infertility, and male factor infertility [13].

Psychological outcomes in this study were assessed using validated screening instruments evaluating symptom severity rather than formal psychiatric diagnoses. The Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) [14], Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI) [15], and WHOQOL-BREF questionnaire [16] were used to comprehensively evaluate depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and quality of life. Findings should therefore be interpreted as reflecting screening-based psychological symptom burden rather than clinically confirmed psychiatric disorders.

The demographic profile of participants in this study is consistent with previous literature. The slightly higher mean age observed in the PCOS group is comparable to findings reported by Evans-Hoeker et al. [17]. The significantly higher BMI in women with PCOS reflects the well-established association between PCOS and obesity, insulin resistance, and metabolic dysfunction [18].

Biochemically, elevated LH, testosterone, and DHEAS levels in the PCOS group are consistent with the classical endocrine profile of PCOS described in prior studies [19, 20]. These hormonal and metabolic alterations may contribute to emotional vulnerability through complex neuroendocrine and psychosocial mechanisms.

The present study demonstrated a higher screening-based burden of depressive and anxiety symptoms among women with PCOS compared to those with unexplained infertility. Similar trends have been reported in previous studies by Deeks et al. [21] and Evans-Hoeker et al. [17], which highlighted increased psychological symptom burden in PCOS populations. However, variability in assessment tools and the use of screening instruments rather than structured clinical interviews across studies may influence the reported prevalence of psychological symptoms.

Previous literature, including studies by Peterson et al. [22] and Holley et al. [23], has also reported elevated depressive symptoms among infertile women in general, suggesting that infertility itself is a major contributor to psychological distress irrespective of etiology.

Quality-of-life impairment observed in women with PCOS in this study is consistent with findings by Santoro et al. [24], who reported lower fertility-related quality-of-life scores in PCOS compared to unexplained infertility. The greater impairment in psychological and social domains observed in the present study further supports the multidimensional impact of infertility-related emotional burden.

The phenotype-based analysis revealed greater screening-based psychological symptom burden and lower quality-of-life scores in hyperandrogenic phenotypes (B and C). This finding is consistent with Veltman-Verhulst et al. [25], who suggested that hyperandrogenism may play a key role in emotional distress through its association with body image concerns, metabolic dysfunction, and hormonal imbalance. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously due to smaller subgroup sizes.

Differences in quality-of-life scores compared to Huppelschoten et al. [26] may be explained by differences in study populations, particularly treatment status. Participants in the present study were newly evaluated infertility patients and had not yet undergone assisted reproductive techniques, which are known to influence psychological well-being.

Recent literature has further emphasized the broader psychosocial complexity of infertility and assisted reproductive care. Ribeiro Neto et al. highlighted the emotional, relational, and ethical challenges faced by individuals undergoing infertility treatment and emphasized the importance of holistic multidisciplinary care approaches integrating psychological support into reproductive medicine [27].

The lower quality-of-life scores observed among women with PCOS-related infertility may have important implications for treatment adherence, interpersonal relationships, emotional well-being, and overall patient-centered infertility care. The findings of the present study support the integration of routine psychological screening and multidisciplinary support within infertility clinics, particularly for women with PCOS and hyperandrogenic phenotypes. Early identification of psychological distress may improve emotional well-being, treatment adherence, and overall patient-centered reproductive care.

Due to limited subgroup sample sizes, particularly within phenotypes B and C, detailed correlation analyses between biochemical markers and psychological outcomes were beyond the scope of the current study. Future studies with larger phenotype-specific cohorts may help clarify these associations. Further longitudinal and multicenter studies are warranted to better understand temporal relationships between metabolic dysfunction, infertility, and psychological outcomes in women with PCOS.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design limits causal inference between polycystic ovarian syndrome, infertility, and psychological outcomes. Second, psychological outcomes were assessed using validated screening instruments rather than structured clinical psychiatric interviews; therefore, findings reflect screening-based symptom burden rather than clinically confirmed psychiatric disorders. Third, potential residual confounding from unmeasured psychosocial factors cannot be excluded despite observed differences in BMI and biochemical parameters between groups. Additionally, the use of self-report instruments may introduce reporting or social desirability bias. Due to limited subgroup sample sizes, particularly within phenotypes B and C, phenotype-specific findings should be interpreted cautiously. As this study was conducted at a tertiary care center, the findings may not be fully generalizable to community or primary-care populations.

Recommendations

Based on the findings, routine psychological screening for depressive and anxiety symptoms may be considered in women presenting with infertility, particularly those diagnosed with PCOS. A multidisciplinary approach involving reproductive medicine, endocrinology, and mental health support may help address the combined psychological and metabolic burden observed in these patients.

Special attention may be warranted for women with hyperandrogenic PCOS phenotypes, who appear to have a higher screening-based psychological symptom burden and reduced quality of life. Future longitudinal and multicenter studies are recommended to better understand temporal relationships and to evaluate the effect of psychological and lifestyle interventions on fertility-related outcomes and quality of life.

Strengths

This study has several strengths. It utilized validated and widely accepted instruments (PHQ-9, BDI, BAI, GAD-7, and WHOQOL-BREF) to assess psychological symptom burden and quality of life. The inclusion of a well-matched comparison group of women with unexplained infertility strengthened the comparative analysis. Additionally, the phenotype-based subgroup analysis of PCOS provided clinically relevant insights into heterogeneity within PCOS populations.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that infertility associated with polycystic ovarian syndrome is linked to a higher screening-based psychological symptom burden and lower quality of life compared to unexplained infertility. Women with PCOS showed higher levels and greater severity of depressive and anxiety symptoms as assessed by validated screening instruments.

Among PCOS phenotypes, hyperandrogenic phenotypes (B and C) demonstrated relatively higher psychological symptom burden and poorer quality of life compared to other phenotypes. These findings suggest that certain clinical subtypes of PCOS may be more vulnerable to psychological distress in the context of infertility.

Routine psychological screening and multidisciplinary support may help improve overall reproductive and psychosocial outcomes in women with PCOS-related infertility.
