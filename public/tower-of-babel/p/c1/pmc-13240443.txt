Omega‐3 DHA as a blank canvas: A molecular approximation to how lipid mediators modulate LGR6 in inflammation resolution
Authors: Álex Pérez‐Sánchez, Mireia Garcia‐Viloca, Àngels González‐Lafont, José M Lluch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe leucine‐rich repeat‐containing G protein‐coupled receptor 6 (LGR6) has a potential role in inflammation resolution and different cancer processes. In this study, we employed computational techniques, including molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations of mammalian membrane‐embeded systems, to understand the activation mechanism of LGR6 upon binding of the specialized pro‐resolving lipid mediator maresin 1. Additionally, we investigated the interaction and the dynamics of other relevant lipid mediators (leukotriene B4, protectin D1, and docosahexaenoic acid) to understand their influence on receptor activity. Our findings reveal different mediator‐specific conformational changes in LGR6, suggesting hydroxyl positions at these mediators as the key factor by which maresin 1 and other lipid mediators modulate receptor activation and contribute to inflammation resolution.Keywords: AlphaFold, docosahexaenoic acid, GPCRs, inflammation, leukotriene B4 , LGR6, lipid mediators, maresin 1, molecular dynamics, omega‐3 fatty acids, protectin D1

INTRODUCTION

Inflammation‐based diseases are responsible for over 50% of the world's deaths (Furman et al., 2019). When the body is physically damaged or invaded by microbes, the immune response activates to defend it. The main goal is to preserve the health of the organism. This response triggers acute inflammation, which is characterized by five common symptoms: heat, pain, tissue swelling, redness, and impaired function (Chiang & Serhan, 2017). During this process, enzymes such as cyclooxygenase‐2 (COX‐2) and 5‐lipoxygenase (5‐LOX) produce pro‐inflammatory lipid mediators like prostaglandins and leukotrienes, both mainly derived from arachidonic acid (AA) (Serhan & Petasis, 2011). This self‐defense process must be regulated because if inflammation does not resolve, it can become chronic (Chen et al., 2018). Hence, the resolution phase of inflammation helps control and limit the first stage of the overall inflammation process by producing specialized pro‐resolving mediators (SPMs) like lipoxins, maresins, protectins, and resolvins (Buckley et al., 2014). SPMs contribute to the resolution of inflammation by reducing neutrophil infiltration, enhancing macrophage‐mediated phagocytosis and downregulating pro‐inflammatory cytokines (Freitas & Campos, 2019). They all show potential in suppressing inappropriate inflammation in various diseases, including cancer (Narasimha Das, 2013). These SPMs are mainly derived from the well‐known omega‐3 (ω‐3) polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), such as docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) that produces maresins, protectins, and D series resolvins or eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) that leads to E series resolvins (Buckley et al., 2014). AA is an omega‐6 (ω‐6) PUFA that produces the SPMs known as lipoxins but also leads to pro‐inflammatory mediators. For that reason, it is the intake of ω‐3 fatty acids that is highly recommended as the main responsible to produce the lipid mediators which resolve inflammation, whereas ω‐6 PUFAs are mainly identified as precursors of pro‐inflammatory lipid mediators. Recently, large‐scale studies on ω‐6/ω‐3 ratios associate high values of that ratio with increased mortality risk, highlighting Western diets as potentially responsible of chronic diseases (Zhang et al., 2024). This supports the health benefits of ω‐3 PUFAs and their role on promoting longevity (Asher et al., 2021).

Maresins are a class of SPMs that were first found in macrophage exudates. They are extremely effective lipid mediators that decrease the inflammatory response. The first maresin discovered was maresin 1 (MaR1) (Freedman et al., 2020). MaR1 derives from DHA after a series of reactions primarily mediated by 12‐lipoxygenase (12‐LOX) or 15‐lipoxygenase‐1 (15‐LOX‐1) (Freedman et al., 2020), followed by the action of an unknown hydrolase enzyme. The production of MaR1 has been clearly associated with the activation of the inflammation resolution mechanism. MaR1, produced by human macrophages, is a key limiting effector for polymorphonuclear neutrophils infiltration and promotes their apoptosis. Moreover, it contributes to tissue regeneration and pain reduction, particularly in the context of stroke recovery (Jadapalli & Halade, 2018).

In line with this, López‐Vales and co‐workers have demonstrated that MaR1 reduces neuroinflammation and promotes regulatory immune responses, emphasizing its therapeutic promise in multiple sclerosis (Sánchez‐Fernández et al., 2022). Given the increase of inflammatory diseases, many investigations remark the potential of MaR1 as therapeutic agent and identify the molecule as an advantageous option to replace current anti‐inflammatory therapies (Saito‐Sasaki et al., 2022; Tezcan et al., 2024). Furthermore, MaR1 has been also discovered to be an inhibitor of oxidative stress promoted by ultraviolet B irradiation skin's exposure (Cezar et al., 2019). Finally, MaR1 has been shown to resolve chronic inflammation causing diseases and inhibit the growth of some cancer cells (Leite et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2023).

Recently, MaR1 has been identified as an stereoselective agonist for the human leucine‐rich repeat‐containing G protein‐coupled Receptor 6 (LGR6), stimulating the pro‐resolving function of phagocytes (Chiang et al., 2019). However, a more recent study has not been able to reproduce this reported agonistic activity of MaR1 on LGR6 (Alnouri et al., 2024). Other studies suggest the possibility of this receptor to be tumor suppressor in combination with R‐spondin‐2 (Gong et al., 2012). Experimental evidence from Serhan and co‐workers (Chiang et al., 2019) support the specific binding of MaR1 to LGR6 by promoting the β‐arrestin recruitment and efferocytosis. In the same work, the behavior of other lipid mediators, such as Protectin D1 (PD1) and Leukotriene B4 (LTB4) that have the same dihydroxyl and conjugated triene structures as MaR1, was studied (see structures in Figure 1). The rank order potency of these three lipid mediators for LGR6 was fundamentally attributed to differences in their overall structure. Other studies have also related MaR1 interaction with LGR6 to the attenuation of neuroinflammation (Li et al., 2024).

2D representation of the 3D structure of docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), maresin 1 (MaR1), Leukotriene B4 (LTB4), and Protectin D1 (PD1).

Given the central role of MaR1 and its receptor LGR6 in resolving inflammation, it becomes urgent to study the molecular mechanism of this signaling process. G protein‐coupled receptors (GPCRs) are key players on transducing signals from extracellular to intracellular medium. Hence, it is not surprising to have them implicated in the mechanism of action of inflammatory processes. Recent studies reveal that some GPCRs connect SPMs and the resolution phase of inflammation (Chiang et al., 2019; Im, 2020; Park et al., 2020). Understanding this connection can be beneficial to modulate the balance of pro‐inflammatory responses and resolution.

GPCRs, also known as seven‐transmembrane receptors (7TM), play key physiological roles in most living organisms. They contribute to the cellular responses to external stimulus (for instance, light, odors, hormones, peptides, lipids, and growth factors). These types of proteins are characterized by seven α‐helices embedded in the cellular membrane (TM1–TM7), three extracellular loops (ECL1–ECL3), three intracellular loops (ICL1–ICL3), an extracellular N‐terminus and an α‐helix‐8 connected to a final intracellular C‐terminal tail (as shown in Figure S2a). The structural characteristics of these systems allow them to transduce the extracellular signals into intracellular responses assisted by the well‐known G‐proteins or β‐arrestins (Hilger, 2021; Klauer et al., 2024; Weis & Kobilka, 2018).

The transmembrane α‐helix domain creates the main ligand‐binding pocket, known as orthosteric site, which accommodates diverse type of molecules (effectors) that allosterically promotes the binding of other partners (Weis & Kobilka, 2018). Binding of this important number of molecular compounds is known to produce the rearrangement of the transmembrane domain. Despite general trends described for those rearrangements, the fact remains that these helix movements are closely tied to the specific characteristics of each receptor. In this line, several recent studies on GPCRs have appeared (Aranda‐García et al., 2025; Calderón et al., 2023), each of them showing different patterns of activation. However, in all of them activation shares a common feature: the creation of a cavity by the opening of a space between the seven transmembrane helices. General GPCR studies (Weis & Kobilka, 2018) indicate that TM6 and TM7 undergo the biggest outward movement relative to TM3, promoting the creation of a large cavity near the cytoplasmatic site that enables the recruitment of G‐proteins or β‐arrestins. However, the activation process is not binary, meaning that GPCRs do not adopt only two states, an inactive and an active conformational state. Due to their dynamic nature, agonist‐bound GPCRs can present a set of intermediate structures between the inactive and active conformations. Moreover, many GPCRs can induce some amount of signaling even in the absence of agonists, which explains the basal activity of GPCRs. This equilibrium is influenced by agonists and receptor's environment (Kasai et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2021). Apart from agonists there is a diversity of compounds modifying the behavior of the receptor's structure and activity. As Ma et al. (2021) explain in their review article, ligands affecting receptor activity can generally be classified as agonist, partial agonist, antagonist, and inverse antagonist. The effect of these ligands on receptor function is closely linked to the complex allosteric behavior of GPCRs (Alnouri et al., 2024). Hence, the study of the molecular mechanisms of this type of receptors is essential for the understanding of their molecular action. This knowledge will help on the challenging task of drug design to target the action of GPCRs (Congreve & Marshall, 2010).

In this study, we have built for the first time an in silico model of the LGR6 receptor (represented in Figure S2b), using an AlphaFold initial structure embedded in an asymmetric and biologically realistic mammalian membrane comprising more than 11 lipid types. We have run more than 100 μs of molecular dynamics simulations with MaR1, PD1, LTB4, and DHA to elucidate their binding modes and exhaustively analyze the different structural rearrangements of the receptor for each of the molecules. We have chosen those three lipid mediators because their experimental rank order potency has recently been obtained for human recombinant LGR6 (Chiang et al., 2019). Similarly, docking simulations and molecular dynamics have previously been employed to investigate the binding and activation mechanisms of other SPMs, such as lipoxin A4 (LXA4) and resolvin D3 (RvD3), at the ALX/FPR2 receptor. (Nunes et al., 2023, 2025) The short‐term objective of this research is to shed light on the activation of the regulating inflammation LGR6 receptor by ω‐3‐derived lipid mediators, as MaR1. In the long term, the goal of this research is to establish new possibilities for the development of exogenous drugs that mitigate the effects of acute inflammation as efficiently as endogenous mediators trigger resolution.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Active and inactive apo‐LGR6 states

Recent advances in artificial intelligence have given computational chemists and biochemists the opportunity to study systems whose structure is not determined by experimental methods (e.g., x‐ray, Cryo‐electron microscopy). We have used the G protein‐coupled receptors database (GPCRdb) (Pándy‐Szekeres et al., 2018) together with the potent multistate modeling approach (Heo & Feig, 2022), a modified protocol that enables the generation of accurate multiple states of GPCRs using AlphaFold2 (AF2), to model active and inactive structures of the LGR6 receptor. The obtained structures for both states have predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) score values higher than 90%, which gives confidence in their accuracy.

With these initial receptor structures, we have constructed membrane‐embedded systems for the apo modes of inactive (apoIN) and active (apoAC) states of LGR6. To study the stability of these two systems, we have run 10 μs molecular dynamics (MD) simulations for each, divided into five replicas of 2 μs. As shown in Figures 2 and S3, the apoIN and apoAC structures of the final MD receptor's conformations are quite similar to the corresponding initial models, despite some differences in the ICLs and ECLs of the apoAC system. In Figure 2, the location of the TM6 helix at the end of the MD simulations of apoIN and apoAC is highlighted in comparison with its position in the initial AF2‐multistate structures.

Structural comparison of AlphaFold2 inactive (pink) and active (blue) states of Leucine‐rich repeat‐containing G protein‐coupled Receptor 6 with the final MD representative frame (gray) (a) for the apo modes of inactive system and (b) for the apo modes of active system. TM6 helices have been highlighted by giving transparency to all the other helices.

Due to the clear implication of TM6 and TM2 in the activation process of this receptor and many others (Aranda‐García et al., 2025; Dalton et al., 2015), we have chosen a distance related to two specific residues at the intracellular end of these α‐helices: Val601 and Asp768, respectively, to monitor the receptor opening in all systems. Although D768 is located at the boundary between TM6 and ICL3, our structural analysis confirms that this residue maintains helical continuity with TM6 in the LGR6 receptor context, serving as the intracellular anchor of this helix and effectively capturing the lateral displacement associated with receptor activation. TM2, in turn, provides a stable and well‐established reference frame for monitoring inter‐helical conformational changes. Furthermore, this distance has been selected because it exhibits the largest variation in the transmembrane helices involved in activation. The TM6‐TM2 distance analysis throughout the MD simulations is given in Figure 3 for the apoIN and apoAC systems.

The distance values for apoIN (Figure 3a) remain fluctuating between 10 and 25 Å. The most probable distance for apoIN is approximately 17 Å, near the 14.5 Å value of the AF2‐inactive structure. These values ensure that LGR6 activation is not occurring in the apoIN system, despite the evident fluctuations. The distance values for the apoAC system (most of the time above the 27.5 Å value of the AF2‐active structure) presented in Figure 3b are enough differentiated from those of the apoIN system to establish the inactive‐active difference as a function of this frequency histogram. The apoIN system presents more conformational flexibility than the apoAC one and samples a greater number of intermediate conformations. This detectable population of intermediate inactive–active conformations during the MD simulations initiated from the apoIN free‐energy basin can be attributed to some degree of basal activity of LGR6. Basal activity has been observed in many GPCRs (Seifert & Wenzel‐Seifert, 2002), but the level of basal receptor activity is highly receptor specific. In turn, some population of apoAC also appears in the intermediate region initiated from the apoAC free‐energy basin. Therefore, we have structural evidence from our simulations of a population fluctuation of inactive and active conformations, which corresponds to the well‐separated apoIN and apoAC systems, respectively, indicating a breathing motion.

The backbone root mean square deviation (RMSD) (see Figure S3) for the transmembrane domain shows the strongest stability in the receptor. The C‐terminal domain in the intracellular region exhibits the largest values of the backbone RMSD because of its length and intrinsic disorder characteristics. The C‐terminal domain mobility is needed for β‐arrestin recruitment. Usually, GPCR receptor's experimental determination lacks enough resolution to establish this structural part due to the movement of the region during measurements. This agrees with our computational models describing LGR6 dynamics.

Binding mode analysis of possible effectors of the LGR6 receptor

To our knowledge, LGR6 has not been previously studied in terms of structure and binding modes of possible effectors. Serhan and co‐workers have stated that MaR1 is the main agonist of the LGR6 receptor (Chiang et al., 2019). Similarly, they have shown that PD1 and LTB4 are also effectors of this receptor with the following relative potencies: MaR1 > LTB4 > > PD1.

As no structure has been determined for any effector: LGR6 complex, we first produced a set of binding modes for MaR1 using a blind docking approach with AutoDock Vina. The binding mode prediction locates the ligand in the region between the leucine‐rich and transmembrane domains in the upper binding site (see Figure S4). This first solution is not consistent with most results obtained for other GPCRs in which the agonist is bound to the orthosteric cavity (Weis & Kobilka, 2018). At this point, AlphaFold3 (AF3) was released, including new interesting features, such as ligand binding prediction. Hence, we predicted the binding mode of MaR1 with the Protenix Server (Chen et al., 2025). The orthosteric site, a pocket between the seven transmembrane α‐helices, resulted in the first predicted binding cavity for the MaR1:LGR6 system, in agreement with standard GPCR binding modes. After analyzing the systems with MaR1 in both binding sites (shown in Figure S4) we selected the orthosteric cavity for running GOLD molecular docking calculations with the four molecular compounds: MaR1, PD1, LTB4, and DHA. We generated 100 solutions for each molecule into the orthosteric cavity. In what follows, all presented results were obtained from simulations based on these orthosteric binding‐site configurations.

Among the solutions, two structural families can be distinguished. On the one hand, we have named head‐first (hf) solutions to those configurations in which the carboxylate (the head) is directed to the end of the cavity, near the intracellular transmembrane hole, and the long aliphatic tail (the tail) is at the top of the receptor's cavity (the receptor entrance), near the extracellular region. On the other hand, tail‐first (tf) solutions refer to configurations in which the carboxylate is at the top transmembrane part and the ligand's tail is pointing toward the bottom region of the transmembrane cavity. We have analyzed in detail the possibilities of these two binding modes for all four effectors. The summary of those results is presented in Figure 4. Results in Figure 4a show that hf binding modes present higher score values for MaR1 than tf configurations. Moreover, hf solutions are more frequent (95 poses) than tf configurations (only 5 poses). This result suggests the possible contribution of hf binding modes in the LGR6 receptor activation process. MaR1 binding modes have higher scores than those for the other ligands (see Figure 4b), in agreement with the highest potency of MaR1 as an LGR6 agonist.

Similarly, LTB4 binding analysis (see Figure 4) assigns higher score values to hf solutions, also more abundant (88 hf poses vs. 12 tf), indicating their probable importance on the activation process. Score values for LTB4 are smaller than for MaR1, in agreement with experimental evidence of the lower potency of LTB4 in LGR6 activation.

In contrast, PD1 binding analysis (see Figure 4) shows higher scoring values for tf poses. Also, the values for the hf solutions are more confined in terms of distribution. In this case, the hf solutions are 25 and the tf count is 75, a proportion that could correlate with the lowest capacity of PD1 to activate LGR6 if hf configurations are those that induce the receptor conformational changes.

Finally, we have evaluated the scores of DHA binding modes as shown in Figure S5. The results differ from the other three compounds. For DHA, the scores of the hf and tf configurations are similar to each other. These results align with the fact that DHA does not have the two hydroxyl groups as the three lipid mediators: likewise, reducing the hydrogen‐bond contribution to zero for all solutions.

Maresin 1 as an agonist of the LGR6 receptor

MaR1 has been stated as an agonist of LGR6 in several research articles. (Chiang et al., 2019; Elder et al., 2021; Im, 2020; Yin et al., 2024) After running five 2‐μs MD replicas for the IN‐hfMaR1 and IN‐tfMaR1 systems, we observed that, only upon binding of hfMaR1 to the orthosteric cavity, the receptor moves toward the AF2‐active state conformation. Figure 5a presents a comparative image of a final representative frame of the IN‐hfMaR1 simulations with respect to the AF2‐inactive and AF2‐active structures. The TM6 and TM5 α‐helices have moved away from TM2 and TM3, demonstrating the capacity of hfMaR1 to activate the LGR6 receptor. To assess the receptor's activation process, we have chosen the TM6‐TM2 distance, defined above, as a distinctive metric. Figure 5b shows that the distribution of distance values for the IN‐hfMaR1 system is between 20 and 30 Å, which corresponds to intermediate structures of the activation pathway, but with a relevant population approaching the TM6‐TM2 distance of AF2‐active. These results align with the rearrangements depicted in Figure 5a and the possibility for β‐arrestin recruitment (see below). The tendency is not reproduced by one of the replicas, in which the distance values move to the 10–15 Å range. For the IN‐tfMaR1 model, there are more distance frequencies below 15 Å than for IN‐hfMaR1 (see Figure S6a), reinforcing the idea that not the tf orientation but the hf one promotes activation.

This type of GPCR receptor must recruit G‐proteins or β‐arrestins to activate the necessary biological mechanisms. The LGR6 receptor promotes β‐arrestin recruitment (Chiang et al., 2019), which initiates the resolution phase of inflammation and inhibits the elongation of the acute phase of inflammatory processes. (Chiang et al., 2019) As shown in Figure S13a, β‐arrestins are two‐domain proteins that couple to the same LGR6 binding site as G‐proteins. As mentioned, β‐arrestins need to be bound in between the seven transmembrane helices upon receptor activation. We have analyzed whether β‐arrestin can enter the orthosteric cavity by modeling β‐arrestin inside one of the final MD snapshots (defined as an intermediate state in Figure 5b) of the IN‐hfMaR1 simulation. Our modeling has been based on experimental evidence (Lee et al., 2020) of the protein binding for other GPCRs, whereby we superimposed LGR6 simulation frames on to experimentally resolved GPCR‐arrestin complex structures, assessing volumetric compatibility of β‐arrestin within the intracellular cavity of LGR6 and determining whether the receptor's conformational state, as captured in the last simulation frames, was structurally accessible for β‐arrestin engagement. As a reference, we have taken the β‐arrestin structure co‐crystallized with the highly studied β1‐adrenoceptor.

Figure S13a shows that binding β‐arrestin in the last MD snapshot of the IN‐hfMaR1 system is viable, making recruitment possible. When the receptor is in an inactive conformation, the distances between transmembrane segments are not big enough to accommodate the two‐domain protein: that is, the binding pocket is too small. As seen in Figure S13a, the β‐arrestin's loop (marked with broken lines), which must be introduced in the binding cavity, fits into the intracellular hole of IN‐hfMaR1, but undergoes clashes with the TM6 helix of the inactive conformation.

Up to this point, we have referred to the IN‐hfMaR1 final MDs structures as intermediate states based on apoIN and apoAC (from AF2‐multistate) references. However, the viability of β‐arrestin recruitment demonstrated here lets us define the equilibrated IN‐hfMaR1 system as active‐like.

Finally, the analysis of the selected TM6‐TM2 distance for AC‐MaR1 revealed distance frequencies (shown in Figure S6b) centered close to the apoAC band. The values of this system fluctuate less than those of the apoAC (Figure 3b). Thus, there is an increase in the active configuration stability promoted by MaR1 orthosteric binding. Despite this fact, we have previously verified that structures that do not reach this distance range can be considered active. Therefore, the results lead us to believe that, due to the multistate method's ability to predict active states, the structures of the AC‐MaR1 system are fortuitously shifted toward values that, although being active, are not strictly necessary for β‐arrestin recruitment.

Other studies also suggest that the maintenance of the active state is ligand‐dependent and varies according to the chemical nature of agonists. Nunes et al. (2024) observed that different BLT1 agonists stabilized the receptor's active conformation differently despite sharing the same binding pocket, while Wang et al. (2022) showed that structural features of the active conformation that enable the G protein coupling do not necessarily imply β‐arrestin recruitment. Altogether this supports our interpretation that, intermediate conformation's populations, ligand binding orientation and its chemical nature critically determine the degree of active‐state stabilization. (Nunes et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2022).

Leukotriene B4 and Protectin D1 bound to the LGR6 receptor

To investigate whether MaR1 is the unique LGR6 agonist, we decided to evaluate two molecules, LTB4 and PD1, experimentally studied by Serhan and co‐workers (Chiang et al., 2019). LTB4 and PD1 have been reported to present medium and low effects on the LGR6 activation process, respectively.

The IN‐hfPD1, IN‐tfPD1 IN‐hfLTB4, and IN‐tfLTB4 models were built in the same manner as those studied with MaR1. The experimental reported values for the impedance change induced by PD1 are under the vehicle values in accordance with very low receptor activation. Our simulations confirm that the orthosteric cavity does not open upon binding of hfPD1. Moreover, as can be observed from Figures 6a and 6b, the receptor dynamics suggests that hfPD1 produces a certain closing of the cavity in comparison with the inactive receptor conformation. The highlighted TM6 helices in Figure 6 for the different MD replicas show that, in the presence of PD1, this helix undergoes some bending, disrupting the LGR6 molecular structure. This deformation contributes to pocket closure, hampering the viability of β‐arrestin recruitment (as shown in Figure S13b).

On the other hand, LTB4 produces an impedance change higher than PD1 and lower than MaR1. These reported experimental values support the possibility of LTB4 acting as a partial agonist. The theoretical results nicely agree with these values. Figure 6c shows, in comparison with AF2‐inactive and AF2‐active structures, that there is a slight aperture of the orthosteric cavity of the receptor upon the binding of hfLTB4. Comparing the results with the AF2‐active state, we can observe that the IN‐hfLTB4 movement is on the path to achieve this activated structure but is still near the initial inactivated system in some cases. Our results suggest that the ligand can activate the receptor, but not to its full capacity. LTB4 can probably be classified as a partial agonist of LGR6.

If the TM6‐TM2 distance, as defined in previous systems, is monitored (see Figure 7), we can observe that IN‐hfPD1 values (Figure 7a) are more scattered compared to those of IN‐hfLTB4 (Figure 7b), making it more difficult to understand the IN‐hfPD1 system. For IN‐hfLTB4, the distance frequencies are located between the inactive region and the results for MaR1, suggesting what is experimentally observed: LTB4 has possibilities to act as a partial agonist for LGR6. For the IN‐tfLTB4 model, the most frequent distances are in the inactive region (see Figure S6c), reinforcing the idea that not the tf orientation but the hf one participates in the LGR6 activation.

The results of IN‐hfPD1 in Figure 7a, which correspond to a significant population of structures with high distance values, could apparently suggest a stronger potential for activation of this molecule in contrast to experimental evidence. However, when considering the actual molecular docking solutions, the balance between hf and tf orientations is not the same for the three molecules, as already commented. For MaR1 and LTB4, the hf:tf ratio is 95:5 and 88:12, respectively. This ratio is 25:75 for PD1, between 57 and 22 times smaller than the values for the other two compounds. Since the ratios are inverted, tfPD1 is the most representative binding mode of PD1. Therefore, we must develop further our analysis of the IN‐tfPD1 system.

The results demonstrate strong agreement with experiments (Chiang et al., 2019), characterizing the molecule as a possible antagonist. The distance pattern shows, with insignificant differences between replicas, that the IN‐tfPD1 system presents most values below the inactive distance region.

Hydroxyl pattern and anchoring zones

To provide further insights into LGR6 functional selectivity to the different ligands studied in this work, we summarize in this subsection the role of hydrogen bonds between the ligand and the receptor to explain how hf orientations for MaR1, LTB4, PD1, and DHA affect the LGR6 activation process.

As mentioned above, we have described three main anchoring zones in the LGR6 receptor. The first region (zone A) is located between the extracellular upper part of TM6 and TM7 (coinciding with part of the conserved NPXXY motif). The hydroxyl O2 atoms can interact with residues of zone A for molecules with the adequate spatial placement of their atoms. The second region (zone B) is in the lower pocket between TM3, TM5, and TM6. This second binding site is associated with the interaction with the hydroxyl O1 atom. Finally, the third region (zone C) is located at the intracellular end of TM7 (coinciding with part of the conserved NPXXY motif) providing anchoring for the carboxylate O3 and O4 atoms via polar residues in this region. This interaction pattern is followed by hfMaR1 and hfLTB4 at 100%.

To unravel the activation mechanism, it has been necessary to observe how the ligands interact with these three zones (zones A–C) during the production period of the MD simulations. Figure S27a–c depict the time evolution of the hfMaR1, hfPD1, and hfLTB4 binding modes, respectively, throughout an MD trajectory.

The carboxylate group of hfMaR1, hfPD1, and hfLTB4 starts having non‐specific interactions near zone B of the receptor. During the simulations, the diffusive path of those molecules starts to differ. The interactions of O3 and O4 atoms move to zone C for all compounds and displace the two hydroxyl groups. The difference in the molecular evolution of hfMaR1 and hfLTB4 from hfPD1 begins here. The hfMaR1 and the hfLTB4 effectors can locate the hydroxyl groups interacting with O2 and O1 in zones A and B, respectively, by moving their long tails. In the case of hfMaR1, the hydroxyl O2 group begins interacting with Leu816 to end in zone A, whereas the hydroxyl O1 group begins interacting with Asp782 to end in zone B. This movement, together with the strong anchoring of the OH groups, provokes the displacement of TM6 away from TM2. Hence, we see that the hydrogen bonds formed by the hydroxyl groups of the ligands determine the role of MaR1 and LTB4 as agonist and partial agonist, respectively.

In contrast, the hfPD1 hydroxyl group location does not allow a strong enough anchoring of the hydroxyl oxygens to maintain the binding modes. As a result, the molecule diffuses more freely to finally be inserted between TM5 and TM6. This molecular behavior of hfPD1 does correlate with its role as an antagonist of LGR6.

The representation in Figure 9 illustrates the three‐dimensional (3D) hydrogen‐bond pattern of the three lipid mediators bound to LGR6 in a two‐dimensional (2D) plot. At the same time, we can clearly see how the stereochemistry of the ligands affects which zones they bind to.

Allosteric communications

As commented in previous sections, the activation process could be modulated by allosteric communication within the receptor. Figure S28 shows the allosteric communities for the three molecules hfMaR1, tfPD1, and hfLTB4. The communities for the initial MD frame show clear interconnections (represented with red lines) with high correlation factors between hfMaR1 or hfLTB4 and residues of the TM3‐TM5‐TM6 α‐helices (see Figures S28a,c).

In contrast, the tfPD1 communities (shown in Figure S28b) predict more correlations of the ligand with a higher number of residues. The representation shows an elevated and more diffuse number of red lines (correlated residues) for the IN‐tfPD1 complex in comparison with the more localized correlations of the other two systems. This phenomenon supports the idea that allosteric communication paths regulate activation in GPCRs, as noted in numerous other research articles (Ma et al., 2021; Shen et al., 2023; Shpakov, 2023).

Figures S29–S31 represent the communication communities for hfMaR1, tfPD1, and hfLTB4 systems in their final states. We have concluded that the divergence of the results among the replicas indicates that after performing their molecular function, the different systems exhibit a more diverse pattern of communications. For the case of tfPD1, Figure S30 shows that in all replicas, the ligand moves to the top part of the receptor, generating a final movement of its communities to this part of the system.

Is DHA an agonist of LGR6?

We have studied how the DHA molecule interacts with the LGR6 receptor to verify that only lipid mediators, such as MaR1 or LTB4, can activate LGR6, in contrast to their parent PUFAs from which they are biochemically derived.

We have built a hf DHA: LGR6 system for this molecule (IN‐hfDHA), and we run up to 10 μs of MD production simulation in five replicas. During the simulations, we observe that the DHA molecule exits the receptor in a lateral way. As reported in other articles (Aranda‐García et al., 2025; Jakowiecki et al., 2020; Szlenk et al., 2019), this entrance/exit mechanism allows lipids in the membrane to bind to the receptors or vice versa. This way of leaving the orthosteric cavity is facilitated by the chemical structure of the DHA molecule. When embedded in the membrane, DHA maximizes its hydrophobic interactions because the fatty acid has no hydroxyl groups in its structure. The carboxylate group remains exposed to the intracellular medium. At the end of the simulation, the DHA molecule is fully embedded in the plasma membrane, as shown in Figure S32. The anchoring points of the receptor to DHA are almost non‐existent. In some of the simulations, the lipid molecules have fully exited the cavity in the first 100 ns of the MD production, reinforcing the idea that DHA does not activate the LGR6 receptor.

CONCLUSIONS

Nowadays, the crucial health benefits of ω‐3 fatty acids, especially DHA, are widely recognized. It is accepted that their potent anti‐inflammatory properties help reduce chronic inflammation. Although some debate (Schebb et al., 2022; Serhan & Petasis, 2011) exists about the biosynthesis of biologically‐relevant amounts of SPMs in vivo and their enzymatic pathways, we have assumed here that in the human body DHA is converted into different SPMs (maresins, resolvins, and protectins) under catalysis by some enzymes. We can wonder whether DHA by itself has anti‐inflammatory properties or if it is simply a precursor to the SPMs. It has been shown that the DHA‐derived SPMs exert their anti‐inflammatory and pro‐resolving effects through allosteric modulation of the EP4 receptor (Alnouri et al., 2024). On the other hand, the human LGR6 also has been identified as a receptor that plays a key role in inflammation resolution, with MaR1 being a stereoselective agonist of it.

In this paper, we have employed computational methods to elucidate the molecular mechanism by means of which MaR1 activates LGR6. We have observed that MaR1 binds in a hf configuration into the orthosteric cavity, the pocket between the seven transmembrane α‐helices of LGR6. The rather rigid structure of MaR1 anchors to LGR6 through the formation of hydrogen bonds with a number of residues placed in 3 different zones of the receptor: the two hydroxyls in carbon atoms 14 and 7 and the carboxylic oxygen atoms of MaR1 interact with some residues in zones A (between the extracellular part of TM6 and TM7), B (in the lower pocket between TM3, TM5, and TM6) and C (at the intracellular end of TM7), respectively, of LGR6. As a result of this MaR1‐LGR6 interaction, the TM6 and TM5 of the receptor move away from the TM2 and TM3, creating a space between the seven transmembrane α‐helices at the intracellular side of the orthosteric cavity that is wide enough to allow β‐arrestin binding into this cavity, thus activating LGR6. In the inactive conformation of LGR6, a β‐arrestin loop undergoes clashes with TM6, and the β‐arrestin recruitment is hindered.

We have also confirmed the experimental evidence on the role of MaR1, LTB4, and PD1, ranking their potency in the following order: MaR1 (agonist) > LTB4 (partial agonist) >> PD1 (antagonist). LTB4 also binds into the orthosteric cavity in a hf configuration, with formation of hydrogen bonds between the two hydroxyls in carbon atoms 12 and 5 and the carboxylic oxygen atoms with a number of residues placed in the zones A–C, respectively, of the receptor. However, LTB4 provokes a smaller opening of that cavity than in the MaR1 case, only acting as a partial agonist. Conversely, the quite different regiochemistry of the hydroxyls (in carbon atoms 17 and 10) of PD1, although also generated from DHA, leads to a tf binding where the two hydroxyls do not form hydrogen bonds with residues in zones A or B. The tail of PD1 is inserted between TM5 and TM6; the opening of the orthosteric cavity does not happen, and LGR6 is not activated.

On the other hand, our molecular dynamics simulations indicate that DHA leaves the orthosteric cavity of LGR6 to establish hydrophobic interactions with the membrane, although maintaining its carboxylate group exposed to the intracellular medium.

From the results obtained in this paper, we can extract some general interesting points:

DHA, one of the most efficient and recommended ω‐3 fatty acids that provide human health benefits (Li et al., 2021), is unable by itself to resolve inflammation by activating the suitable G protein‐coupled receptors, as LGR6. This is because DHA lacks the hydroxyl groups required to interact with the residues from the receptor that act as anchoring points. As a consequence, DHA is expelled from the receptor toward the plasma membrane, without having any effects.

DHA is a quite rigid molecule that contains six carbon–carbon double bonds, which form five bisallylic methylene groups. Instead, their precursor DHA (humans can synthesize DHA from alpha‐linolenic acid, but very inefficiently) can be obtained from good dietary sources, with the advantage that the single intake of that blank canvas is enough for the endogenous production of a variety of SPMs. This is the reason for the well‐known health benefits of ω‐3 fatty acid intake, showing the simplicity and complexity of inflammation resolution signaling. COMPUTATIONAL METHODOLOGIES

Protein structure preparation

The protein structure of the receptor was obtained from GPCRdb (Pándy‐Szekeres et al., 2018), which provides models of GPCRs based on the multistate AF2 approach (Heo & Feig, 2022). We selected the active and inactive conformation states of the receptor, to initiate the simulation from both models. The downloaded structures were inspected using University of California, San Francisco Chimera (Pettersen et al., 2004) to assess the different structural inconsistencies prior to start the modeling. The location of the C‐terminal region of the receptor was modified from its non‐trustable original coordinates (as colored in Figure S2b) to correctly assemble the membrane in the subsequent steps of the process. Protonation states of titratable residues were predicted using the PropKa (Olsson et al., 2011; Søndergaard et al., 2011) code within the PlayMolecule (Torrens‐Fontanals et al., 2024) suite. The pKa values were calculated at a pH of 7.4. The predicted protonation pattern was applied to the receptor to reflect physiological conditions of the mammalian cells.

Molecular docking predictions

Ligands were prepared using GaussView6 (Dennington et al., 2019), optimized with Gaussian16 (Frisch et al., 2016), and docked to predict binding modes within the LGR6 receptor. AutoDock Vina (Trott & Olson, 2010) was used for a blind docking of MaR1. The rest of molecular docking calculations were performed with the standard protocol of GOLD2022 (Jones et al., 1997). Two different α‐carbon atoms were chosen as the search zone center. The first selected residue was Ser796, the most convenient to represent the upper binding site of the receptor, which was initially suggested for this type of leucine‐rich receptors by Serhan and co‐workers (Chiang et al., 2019). However, after performing some predictions with AF3 (Abramson et al., 2024) via the Protenix Server (Chen et al., 2025) (a PyTorch (Ansel et al., 2024; PyTorch Team, 2024)) reproduction of AF3), we introduced a new residue center, the Pro819 residue, for defining the well‐known orthosteric binding site. Top‐ranked configurations were selected based on their binding score. The best‐scoring pose was used as the initial structure in the setup and building of the model system prepared to perform MD simulations. In general terms, the poses were clustered in two main groups: hf and tf. We selected one top‐ranked solution from each cluster to correctly study these two different binding modes of the compounds.

Ligand parametrization with CGenFF

The structure of the different ligands (MaR1, PD1, LTB4, and DHA) were submitted to CHARMM‐GUI (Jo et al., 2008) (in mol2 format) which uses the CGenFF (Vanommeslaeghe et al., 2010) (CHARMM General Force Field) to determine, based on atom types, the force field parameters and topology files necessary for incorporation into the membrane system, as detailed in the following section.

Membrane system setup

To replicate a biologically realistic environment, the receptor‐ligand complex was inserted into an asymmetric mammalian lipid bilayer using CHARMM‐GUI (Jo et al., 2008) Membrane Builder (Brown et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2023; Gee et al., 2024; Jo et al., 2009; Lee et al., 2019; Park et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2014). The membrane composition consisted of the following lipid ratios in the inner and outer leaflets: 1‐palmitoyl‐2‐oleoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphocholine at 16:7, 1‐palmitoyl‐2‐linoleoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphocholine at 22:11, 1‐palmitoyl‐2‐arachidonoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphoethanolamine at 3:12, 1‐palmitoyl‐2‐oleoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphoethanolamine at 3:14, 1‐palmitoyl‐2‐oleoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphoinositol at 0:5, 1‐palmitoyl‐2‐arachidonoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphoserine at 0:11, 1‐palmitoyl‐2‐oleoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphate at 0:1, N‐stearoyl‐sphingomyelin at 11:5, N‐nervonoyl‐sphingomyelin (NSM) at 11:5, Ceramide monohexoside at 4:0, and cholesterol at 37:29, based on the recommended ratios available in the CHARMM‐GUI biomembrane archive (Jo et al., 2008; Pogozheva et al., 2022). The bilayer was constructed to reflect leaflet‐specific asymmetry consistent with mammalian plasma membranes, with a total XY area of approximately 24,025 Å2 (155 Å × 155 Å). The system was solvated with explicit TIP3P (Jorgensen et al., 1983) water molecules, and physiological ionic strength was achieved by the addition of Na+ and Cl− at a concentration of 0.15 M. After the inclusion of all the necessary molecules, the system parameters were generated with the CHARMM‐GUI input generator (Brooks et al., 2009; Lee et al., 2016) to be run on AMBER package. The final size of each system is around 750,000 atoms. Finally, new parameter files for the systems were generated with hydrogen mass repartitioning (HMR) using the parmed tool from AmberTools24 (Case et al., 2024).

Molecular dynamic simulations

All molecular dynamics simulations were performed using AMBER24 (Case et al., 2024), employing periodic boundary conditions and the CHARMM36m (Huang et al., 2017; Lee et al., 2016) force field for proteins and lipids. Temperature and pressure were maintained at 310.15 K and 1 atm, respectively. Initial velocities for each replica were randomly assigned from a Maxwell‐Boltzmann distribution to ensure uncorrelated trajectories. The simulation protocol followed the membrane protein workflow described in the recommended AMBER membrane tutorial (Dickson, 2023). The system underwent a two‐step energy minimization to relieve steric clashes (first minimization step was performed using the central processing unit architecture, with all subsequent steps carried out using the GPU‐accelerated pmemd.cuda code). They use energy minimization algorithms that switch from steepest descent to conjugate gradient while maintaining a 10.0 Å cutoff for electrostatic and van der Waals potentials under periodic boundary conditions at constant volume. A two‐phase heating schedule from 0 to 310.15 K via a Langevin thermostat and a Berendsen barostat with anisotropic scaling follows the minimization. From now on, a time step of 0.002 ps, SHAKE constraints for hydrogen bonds and velocity Verlet algorithm for integration of motion equations are used. After it, equilibration proceeded in several stages under semi‐isotropic pressure conditions to scale independently membrane plane and vertical axis with long‐range electrostatic interactions (Particle Mesh Ewald method is used): first with positional restraints on the protein backbone, then limited to Cα atoms. Next, after removing all restraints, a short 100‐ns simulation was performed. Finally, long‐production simulations were conducted with HMR enabled, allowing a 4‐fs time step, 2 ps−1 collision frequency and Monte Carlo barostat. HMR was used by assigning a mass of three times that of hydrogen (3.024 Da) to hydrogen atoms. Based on several studies, it has been verified that the HMR method does not affect the structural or thermodynamic properties of the membrane, maintaining consistency with standard 2‐fs simulations (Balusek et al., 2019; Hopkins et al., 2015). A total of five independent 2‐μs replicas of long‐production simulation were conducted, resulting in an aggregate simulation time of 10 μs for each of the systems.

Docking and trajectory analysis

Docking clustered results were analyzed with a custom Python (van Rossum & Team, 2001) pipeline based on pandas (McKinney, 2010; The Pandas Development Team, 2020), seaborn (Waskom, 2021) and matplotlib (Hunter, 2007). Analysis of the molecular dynamics trajectories was performed using cpptraj tool of AmberTools24 (Case et al., 2024), including calculations of intermolecular distances, hydrogen‐bond analysis, lifetime estimations, and other structural and dynamical descriptors.

Protein structure network (PSN) analysis

Protein communication networks within the receptor were analyzed using webPSN (Felline et al., 2020, 2022; Seeber et al., 2015). This tool analyses the interactions of atoms inside proteins structures to extract communication pathways in the systems. The software allows to filter the different interactions of the global communication generated path by selecting extremities. We have selected the MaR1, PD1, and LTB4 molecules as the first extremity to analyze all possible communications from those ligands.
