Immunotherapy with a short-lived anti-PD-L1 antibody in Alzheimer’s disease: a phase 1b, randomized, double-blind trial
Authors: Tommaso Croese, Catherine J Mummery, Noa Bregman, Dalia Bracha, Kuti Baruch, Alexander Kertser, Sharona Raveh, Eliezer Shochat, Michal Schwartz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is initiated by amyloid plaque accumulation, its progression involves local neuroinflammation that the brain cannot resolve when age-related dysfunction of the systemic immune system limits peripheral immune support. Preclinical studies using rodent models showed that transient systemic blockade of programmed death-ligand 1 is associated with reduced neuroinflammation, neuroprotection and attenuation of disease progression. Based on the underlying mechanism, a new short-lived anti-programmed death-ligand 1 antibody with Fc-effector silencing and reduced FcRn binding (IBC-Ab002) was engineered. Here, we report a randomized, double-blind, phase 1b first-in-human trial in early AD, with safety and tolerability as the primary endpoint. Forty participants were enrolled across five ascending dose cohorts (1–30 mg kg−1), with dosing administered four times at 3-month intervals. Treatment was well tolerated, with no treatment-related serious adverse events or evidence of amyloid-related imaging abnormalities. Exploratory analyses at week 48 showed directional changes in cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers of neuronal and synaptic damage favoring the 30 mg kg−1 dose, although no doses reached statistical significance given the limited sample size. The safety and tolerability profile supports further clinical development of systemic, intermittently administered IBC-Ab002 in early AD. Main

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is defined pathologically by the accumulation of amyloid-β (Aβ) plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles, yet the onset and rate of clinical progression are only partly explained by these proteinopathies. Progression is increasingly understood to involve chronic neuroinflammation which, together with other processes such as metabolic and vascular dysfunction, contributes to neurodegeneration and may itself be fueled by age-related decline of the systemic immune system1–6. Although immune aging is not the primary cause of AD, evidence suggests it acts as a catalyst for symptom manifestation and disease progression, a concept that helps reconcile the temporal gap between amyloid accumulation and cognitive decline7,8. In aging, and increasingly in AD, this peripheral immune support becomes compromised, leading to a self-perpetuating cycle in which exhausted, inflammation-promoting cells in the brain are no longer counterbalanced by an effective protective systemic immune response9–12.

In preclinical models of amyloidosis and tauopathy, transient blockade of the inhibitory programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) immune checkpoint pathway has been shown to trigger a cascade of immunological events that includes the homing of monocyte-derived macrophages and regulatory T cells to the brain, with consequent reduction of neuroinflammation and slowing of disease progression13–16. These effects require pulsatile treatment rather than continuous antibody exposure; a single dose initiates a process that leads to effects within the central nervous system (CNS) that persist long after the antibody has been cleared from the circulation. However, maintaining efficacy over time requires repeated intermittent dosing at long intervals17. These response characteristics prompted the engineering of IBC-Ab002, a fully human anti-PD-L1 antibody with Fc-effector silencing and reduced FcRn binding designed to deliver short systemic exposure, while preserving the durable CNS effects observed in rodent models.

Following preclinical proof-of-concept, toxicology and pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic modeling in rodents and nonhuman primates, we initiated a clinical study in participants with early-stage AD across 11 centers in the UK, Israel and the Netherlands. In this article, we report the outcome of this first-in-human study, in which 40 early-stage patients were enrolled and assigned to five cohorts treated with escalating doses (1–30 mg kg−1). Each participant received four infusions administered 12 weeks apart, with six active and two placebo participants per cohort, providing a total of ten placebo participants. The primary endpoint was safety and tolerability, while pharmacokinetics, immunogenicity, target engagement and exploratory fluid biomarkers were also evaluated. We show that IBC‑Ab002 was well tolerated across all tested doses, with a pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profile that is consistent with its engineered design, supporting further clinical development of systemic, intermittently administered anti‑PD‑L1 therapy in early AD.

Results

Participant disposition and baseline characteristics

Of the 75 individuals screened, 41 met the eligibility criteria and 40 were enrolled across 5 dose-escalation cohorts (Fig. 1). All participants received at least one infusion, completed the single-dose observation period and continued into the multiple-dose phase. Two participants (5%) did not complete all scheduled study visits: one placebo-treated participant discontinued because of progressive cognitive decline consistent with progression of disease, and one IBC-Ab002-treated participant (cohort 3) discontinued because of multiple adverse events (AEs), both related and unrelated to the study drug, all mild or moderate in severity (Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) grade 1 or 2). Of the 38 participants who completed the 48-week observation period, 2 were unable to receive all 4 intended infusions: one because of an immune-related rash (cohort 1) and the other because of elevated liver enzymes (cohort 4).

CONSORT flow diagram.

Of 75 individuals assessed for eligibility, 41 met the inclusion criteria and 40 were enrolled and randomly assigned 3:1 within each of 5 sequential dose-escalation cohorts to receive intravenous IBC-Ab002 (n = 6 per cohort, at doses of 1, 3, 6, 15 or 30 mg kg−1) or matching placebo (n = 2 per cohort). All participants received at least one infusion and completed the single-dose observation period. Two participants discontinued the study early: one participant who was administered placebo (cohort 2) because of progressive cognitive decline, and one participant who was administered IBC-Ab002 (cohort 3) because of multiple AEs. Of the 38 participants who completed the 48-week observation period, 2 did not receive all 4 intended infusions—a cohort 1 participant because of an immune-related rash and a cohort 4 participant because of elevated liver enzymes.

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were broadly balanced across dose cohorts and the pooled placebo group (Table 1). The overall study population had a mean age of 69.1 years (range: 51–81), was 57.5% female and predominantly white (92.5%). Based on the Clinical Dementia Rating–Global Score (CDR-GS), 30 of 40 participants (75%) had a score of 0.5, which is consistent with mild cognitive impairment because of AD; ten (25%) had a score of 1.0, which is consistent with mild AD dementia. The median Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score at screening was 25 (range: 20–28) and 31 of 40 (77.5%) were apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 carriers.

Safety and tolerability

Treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) were reported in all 40 participants across both active and placebo groups, reflecting intensive longitudinal safety monitoring. TEAEs considered related to the study drug were reported in 22 of 30 (73.3%) active participants and 6 of 10 (60.0%) placebo participants. The most common AEs among active participants were fatigue (8 of 30 (26.7%)), headache (7 of 30 (23.3%)) and infusion-related reactions (6 of 30 (20.0%)) (Table 2). All infusion-related reactions were grade 1 or 2 in severity and did not preclude further dosing.

Three serious adverse events (SAEs) occurred during the study: two in the placebo group (one each, seizure and syncope) and one in an active participant treated with 15 mg kg−1 (multiple fractures after a mechanical fall). Immune-related AEs (irAEs), predefined as AEs of special interest, were observed in 10 of 30 (33.3%) active participants, and 1 of 10 (10.0%) placebo participants. The corresponding odds ratio was 4.5 (95% confidence interval (CI) 0.5–40.7), an interval too wide to provide a meaningful conclusion regarding the effect on risk. Approximately half of these events were identified through routine laboratory monitoring rather than presenting clinically. All irAEs were mild or moderate in severity except for one grade 3 event. Asymptomatic thyroid dysfunction was the most common irAE, observed in four participants (cohorts 2, 3 and 5), which is consistent with the known class effects of immune checkpoint inhibitors. Three resolved spontaneously and one developed persistent hypothyroidism managed with thyroid hormone replacement therapy. The only severe irAE (CTCAE grade 3) was an asymptomatic elevation in liver enzymes (aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), gamma-glutamyl transferase and alkaline phosphatase) in a participant in cohort 4 after the third dose, which is consistent with a mixed hepatocellular and cholestatic pattern with no change in total bilirubin; it was detected through routine laboratory monitoring and resolved spontaneously. In accordance with a protocol-defined individual stopping rule, this participant discontinued the study drug. This was the only grade 3 event among the three active participants who discontinued the study drug because of AEs (Table 2); all such events resolved or were ongoing without sequelae. No placebo participants discontinued because of AEs. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) performed at weeks 7 and 48 revealed no new study-drug-related abnormalities; no cases of amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA) were observed. Overall, IBC-Ab002 was generally well tolerated; the nature of the observed AEs was in line with the known class effects of anti-PD-(L)1 antibodies18, but no severe or life-threatening events were reported.

Pharmacokinetics

IBC-Ab002 was engineered with two types of Fc modifications: one to eliminate antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity and complement-dependent cytotoxicity, and a second to reduce FcRn binding and accelerate systemic clearance. Noncompartmental and population pharmacokinetic analyses (30 participants, 756 observations) confirmed that IBC-Ab002 exhibited a short mean elimination half-life of approximately 4 days across all dose cohorts (range: 3.5–4.6 days), with a low volume of distribution (~3.5–4 l) and rapid clearance (~0.7–0.8 liters per day). Serum exposure (maximum serum concentration Cmax and area under the curve (AUC)) increased proportionally with dose across the 1–30 mg kg−1 range; a two-compartment linear pharmacokinetic model adequately described the data at dose levels ≥ 3 mg kg−1. Serum concentrations of anti-PD-L1 at all dose levels fell below the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) (19.54 ng ml−1) before each subsequent infusion (Fig. 2a). No accumulation was observed with repeated every 12 weeks; Cmax values remained consistent across four dosing cycles. At the highest dose (30 mg kg−1), mean Cmax was approximately 670 μg ml−1 (Fig. 2b). IBC-Ab002 exhibited moderate between-participant variability (~20–30%) in clearance and volume of distribution, which is comparable to other clinically available anti-PD-L1 antibodies. No covariate effect (body weight, body mass index, body surface, sex) on the pharmacokinetic parameters could be identified within the data.

Peripheral target engagement was assessed by measuring PD-L1 receptor occupancy (RO) on circulating T cells using a validated flow cytometry assay. High RO (80–100%) was observed across all dose levels when serum concentrations exceeded approximately 1 μg ml−1, with a strong coupling between drug concentration and percentage RO. RO returned to baseline levels within approximately 2 months after each dose in the two highest dose cohorts, which is consistent with the intended intermittent exposure profile (Fig. 3b). Levels of soluble PD-L1 (sPD-L1), a pharmacodynamic marker of target engagement, showed a dose-proportional increase after IBC-Ab002 infusion, and similarly returned to baseline before subsequent dosing (Fig. 3c).

Peripheral immune system engagement

Blockade of the PD-1–PD-L1 pathway was associated with activation of peripheral T cells, an effect described with anti-PD-L1 antibodies used in cancer immunotherapy19,20 and recapitulated in preclinical models of neurodegeneration after anti-PD-L1 treatment. In the present study, a single dose of IBC-Ab002 induced a dose-dependent increase in the frequency of circulating PD-1+ICOS+ on CD4+ memory T cells, as measured using flow cytometry. The increase in effector memory T cells coexpressing PD-1 and ICOS represents a population of recently activated T cells released from the PD-L1-mediated suppression by the blocking antibody. This response peaked approximately 7 days after administration and was transient, which is consistent with the brief systemic exposure to the antibody (Fig. 3a). Levels of activated T cells returned close to baseline before each subsequent dose at week 12. This is consistent with the design rationale, grounded in the preclinical understanding of the mechanism of action that intermittent dosing is required to initiate an immune response with each administration.

Cognitive assessments

Longitudinal cognitive function was assessed using the MMSE and the Cognitive Functional Composite (CFC), a composite measure integrating components of memory, executive function and functional ability, designed to detect early cognitive changes in prodromal and mild AD21–23. These two tests were assessed at multiple time points throughout the 48-week study period. The study was designed to evaluate safety and was not powered for clinical efficacy endpoints. Importantly, no signs of rapid or unexpected cognitive deterioration were observed in any active treatment group (Fig. 4e,f); however, no efficacy conclusions can be drawn from these exploratory cognitive observations. One placebo participant (cohort 2) discontinued before the third dose because of progressive cognitive decline attributed to disease progression and comorbid factors.

Discussion

In this clinical evaluation, treatment with IBC-Ab002, a newly engineered short-lived anti-PD-L1 antibody, was well tolerated across all tested doses in participants with early symptomatic AD.

Consistent with its engineered design, IBC-Ab002 showed a short mean half-life of approximately 4 days across all dose cohorts, with serum trough concentrations well below the RO threshold (~0.1 μg ml−1) before each subsequent infusion. Peripheral target engagement, measured as PD-L1 RO on circulating T cells, was high (80–100%) when serum concentrations exceeded approximately 1 μg ml−1, and returned to baseline within approximately 2 months of each dose at the two highest dose levels. Peripheral immune activation, measured as the frequency of circulating PD-1+ICOS+ on circulating CD4+ memory T cells, peaked approximately 7 days after administration and resolved before subsequent infusion. Short systemic exposure, transient immune activation and return to baseline before each dose are the key pharmacological features distinguishing this approach from anti-PD-L1 antibodies used in oncology, which require continuous exposure to maintain their effect20. ADAs were detected in 19 of 30 active participants (63.3%), with no appreciable impact on Cmax, AUC, clearance or volume of distribution at any dose level.

The safety profile observed in this study supports the intended design. No treatment-related SAEs were observed, and no cases of ARIA were detected. The latter is consistent with the mechanism of action of IBC-Ab002, which does not target vascular amyloid. irAEs occurred in 10 of 30 (33.3%) active participants, with asymptomatic thyroid dysfunction being the most common; approximately half of the irAE events were identified through routine laboratory monitoring rather than presenting clinically. All irAEs were mild or moderate in severity and transient, with the exception of one asymptomatic grade 3 (per CTCAE v.5.0) elevation in liver enzymes that resolved spontaneously. All irAEs were managed per protocol; all events resolved with the exception of one case of hypothyroidism on stable replacement therapy. The irAE pattern was consistent with the known effects of PD-1–PD-L1 blockade. The brief systemic exposure and 12-week dosing interval would be expected to limit the duration and severity of these events relative to oncology anti-PD-(L)1 regimens, although this will require confirmation in larger studies.

Exploratory CSF biomarker analyses at 48 weeks showed directional reductions in neurogranin (−23.1%), tTau (−12.4%) and pTau181 (−11.0%) at the 30 mg kg−1 dose relative to pooled placebo, with intraindividual consistency across the three markers in treated participants. The study was not powered for biomarker efficacy analyses; all biomarker analyses should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating without adjustment for multiplicity. No CSF biomarker reached statistical significance at any dose. With 6 active and 2 placebo participants per cohort across 11 sites, it was not possible to model site effect or between-participant heterogeneity, which may contribute to the observed intercohort differences (Extended Data Table 2). The analyses involved five active 30 mg kg−1 recipients, and six pooled placebo participants with paired CSF available from baseline and end of study; the end-of-study lumbar puncture was optional. Therefore, the observed signals require confirmation in an adequately powered study. The direction of changes is consistent with preclinical findings in models of amyloidosis and tauopathy13–16, in which anti-PD-L1 treatment was associated with reduced neuroinflammation and downstream changes in markers of neuronal and synaptic integrity. Unlike the reductions observed in the CSF, treatment-related changes in plasma GFAP, neurofilament light (NfL), pTau181 and Aβ1–42 were not detected over the 48-week treatment period. This may reflect the narrower dynamic range and slower kinetics of plasma versus CSF biomarkers, dilution of CNS-derived analytes in the peripheral compartment and the limited statistical power of the exploratory subsets analyzed in this study24. Larger studies with longer follow-up are required to determine whether the central pharmacodynamic signals observed in CSF ultimately translate into detectable plasma changes.

Mechanistically, the approach evaluated in this study differs from anti-amyloid immunotherapies in that it does not depend on direct amyloid binding or plaque clearance25,26. Preclinical studies suggest that the observed CNS effects reflect an immune-mediated process driven by the recruitment of peripheral immune repairing cells that can remove damage-associated molecular patterns14 and senescent microglia27, and reduce local inflammation16. Accordingly, the earliest detectable effects would be expected in biomarkers reflecting neuronal and synaptic injury rather than in markers of amyloid deposition. This is consistent with the findings of the present study, in which changes were observed in CSF neurogranin, tTau and pTau181. In contrast, no changes were detected in amyloid biomarkers, which would be expected to change only after a longer period of treatment or follow-up.

The absence of an NfL signal might be considered consistent with the disease stage, and interpreted in this context knowing that in early AD CSF NfL is only modestly elevated relative to cognitively unimpaired individuals and shows substantial interindividual variability, unlike the markedly elevated, more dynamic levels seen in rapidly progressing neurodegenerative diseases such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis28,29. The directional consistency across these markers is compatible with a genuine pharmacodynamic effect; however, because only five treated participants at the highest dose contributed paired CSF, these findings are best regarded as a hypothesis-generating pharmacodynamic signal requiring confirmation in an adequately powered study. In addition, the primary effect of this therapy is expected to be on local brain inflammation, which is difficult to demonstrate directly in humans because the available fluid biomarkers are nonspecific and variable. Therefore, the CSF changes reported in this study should be viewed as indirect, downstream readouts rather than direct evidence of reduced brain inflammation.

Cognitive assessments showed no signs of unexpected deterioration in any active treatment group; however, the short follow-up and the small sample size limit any conclusions regarding clinical efficacy. An adequately powered evaluation of cognitive endpoints is planned for an upcoming phase 2 study.

Therapies targeting inhibitory immune checkpoints have been primarily developed in the context of cancer immunotherapy19. The approach evaluated in this study differs from cancer immunotherapy with respect to both the target population and the underlying mechanism of action. Whereas cancer immunotherapy relies on sustained PD-1–PD-L1 blockade to maintain cytotoxic (CD8-dependent) antitumour immunity, the mechanism described in this study is hypothesized to require only short, transient CD4-dependent immune activation that initiates a cascade of downstream antibody-free events within the brain. This rationale motivated the engineering of a short-lived antibody administered intermittently. The combination of a short-lived antibody with intermittent administration is expected to contribute to a favorable safety profile in neurodegeneration, although direct comparison between indications would require larger studies. Because IBC-Ab002 acts primarily through the peripheral immune system, its activity is not expected to depend on antibody penetration into, or persistence within, the brain. Furthermore, its mechanism relies on transient rather than continuous immune checkpoint blockade. This distinguishes it from CNS-targeted monoclonal antibodies whose effect is constrained by limited brain accessibility. Consistent with this, in preclinical studies blocking monocyte homing to the brain abrogated the beneficial effects of anti-PD-L1 treatment in mouse models14–16. In addition, the recruited monocyte-derived macrophages express anti-inflammatory activity and scavenger receptors with the capacity to clear Aβ14,30.

Overall, this clinical evaluation of IBC-Ab002 in early symptomatic patients with AD demonstrates an acceptable safety and tolerability profile across the dose range tested, with pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic behavior consistent with the engineered design. Based on its mechanism of action, IBC-Ab002 is intended to transiently re-engage the peripheral immune system, whose ability to support the brain declines with aging, thereby restoring its capacity to protect the brain and dampen brain inflammatory processes. Because this strategy does not rely on directly targeting a specific proteinopathy, it has broader applicability across other chronic neurodegenerative conditions in which neuroinflammation contributes to disease progression. The safety, pharmacokinetic and central and peripheral pharmacodynamic signals observed at the highest dose provide a basis for further clinical development of this newly engineered anti-PD-L1 therapy in AD.

Methods

Study design and participants

IBC-01-01 was a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, single and multiple ascending dose phase 1b trial of IBC-Ab002 in participants with early symptomatic AD (NCT05551741). The study was conducted across 11 sites in the UK (5), Israel (5) and the Netherlands (1) between April 2023 and December 2025. Eligible participants were aged 50–80 years and met National Institute on Aging–Alzheimer’s Association criteria for early AD31, including a CDR-GS of 0.5 or 1.0 (memory box ≥ 0.5), an MMSE score of 20–28 and an amyloid-positive and tau-positive (A+T+) CSF biomarker profile. Body weight was 45–120 kg. Participants on stable doses of symptomatic AD treatments were eligible. Key exclusion criteria included serious neurological diseases other than AD, a history of autoimmune disease, prior immunotherapy or MRI findings suggestive of alternative diagnoses or extensive ARIA with hemosiderin/hemorrhage.

Forty participants were enrolled across five dose cohorts (eight per cohort) and randomized 3:1 to IBC-Ab002 or matching placebo within each cohort. Randomization was computer-generated. Both participants and study staff were blinded to treatment assignment. Each cohort followed a sentinel dosing scheme in which the first two participants (one active, one placebo) were dosed at least 1 week before the remaining cohort members, with blinded safety review before proceeding.

IBC-Ab002 was administered intravenously over approximately 120 min at doses of 1, 3, 6, 15 or 30 mg kg−1 in cohorts 1–5, respectively. The single ascending dose portion consisted of one infusion followed by a 12-week observation period. In the multiple ascending dose portion, three additional doses were administered, every 12 weeks for a total observation period of approximately 48 weeks. Dose escalation between cohorts was guided by cumulative safety, tolerability and pharmacokinetic data reviewed by the Study Monitoring Team and an independent Data Monitoring Committee.

No formal sample size calculation was performed. Sample size was determined using conventional phase 1b dose-escalation methodology; with 30 participants receiving active treatment, there was a probability of approximately 80% of observing at least one event occurring at a true rate of ≤5%.

Trial data were captured using an electronic data capture system (TrialMaster v.5.0 update 8).

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was safety and tolerability, assessed through TEAEs, SAEs, AEs of special interest (irAEs), vital signs, clinical laboratory parameters, electrocardiography and brain MRI, including surveillance for ARIA. Secondary endpoints included characterization of serum pharmacokinetics and immunogenicity (ADAs). Exploratory endpoints included PD-L1 RO on circulating T cells, sPD-L1, frequency of PD-1+ICOS+CD4+ memory T cells, CSF biomarkers and cognitive assessments (MMSE and CFC).

MRI

Brain MRI was performed at screening, week 7 and week 48 (end of study) for safety surveillance and detection of ARIA32. Each examination included three-dimensional T1-weighted, two-dimensional (2D) axial fluid-attenuated inversion recovery, 2D axial T2-weighted, 2D axial T2*-weighted (gradient-echo) and 2D axial diffusion-weighted imaging sequences, enabling detection of ARIA with edema or effusion (on fluid-attenuated inversion recovery) and ARIA with hemosiderin/hemorrhage (on T2*-weighted imaging). Image acquisition was standardized across sites, with central quality control and image management by Clario (central imaging core laboratory). All scans were reviewed by central neuroradiology blinded to treatment assignment, using prespecified criteria for ARIA classification and severity.

CSF and plasma biomarker measurements

CSF was collected using lumbar puncture at screening (approximately 6 weeks before the first dose), at week 8 and at end of the study (week 48). Participation in the end-of-study lumbar puncture was optional. Plasma samples for AD-related biomarkers were collected before each dose and every 12 weeks in the multiple ascending dose portion of the study. CSF biomarkers included pTau181 (Elecsys, Roche), tTau (Elecsys), Aβ 1–42 (Aβ1–42) (Elecsys), GFAP (Simoa), pTau217 (Simoa), soluble TREM2 (ELISA), NfL (Simoa) and neurogranin (ELISA). Plasma biomarkers included pTau217, pTau181, Aβ1–42, Aβ1–40, GFAP and NfL (all measured by Simoa). All results reported in this article were assayed in a single laboratory (VU University Medical Center, Amsterdam), using validated protocols.

Pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic assessments

Serum samples were collected at predefined time points (cycle 1 rich sampling and cycles 2–4 sparse sampling) for pharmacokinetic analysis. Serum IBC-Ab002 concentrations were quantified on a Meso Scale Discovery platform using a tailor-made, validated method (Synexa). Noncompartmental analysis using the rich C1 samples was performed with the ncaR package (R v.4.5). Population pharmacokinetic modeling using the full (C1–C4) pharmacokinetic dataset utilized the nonlinear mixed-effects modeling nlmixr package (R v.4.5). A small subset of Cmax values (<5% of total Cmax data) was manually corrected after being traced to incorrect data entry or infusion-hand sampling. PD-L1 RO on circulating T cells was measured using a validated flow cytometry assay (Synexa). Binding ADAs were assessed using a validated, tiered approach including a qualitative screening assay, a qualitative confirmatory (specificity) assay and a quasi-quantitative titer assessment for confirmed ADA+ samples.

Statistical analyses

All enrolled participants who received at least one dose of the study drug were included in the safety and pharmacokinetic analyses. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic data. Continuous variables are presented as the median with interquartile, or as mean ± s.d. or s.e., as specified; categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. AEs were coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities v.27.1 and graded according to CTCAE v.5.0.

The primary end-of-study CSF biomarker comparison (Fig. 4) was based on the 30 mg kg−1 cohort versus the pooled placebo. CSF biomarker analyses included all participants who consented to the optional end-of-study lumbar puncture (n = 19 of 40, 47.5%). No outliers were removed from any analysis. The CSF dose–response was assessed using linear regression on dose and post hoc using a linear mixed model with a per-participant random intercept (Extended Data Table 6). AE rates were compared with pooled placebo, according to cohort and for all IBC-Ab002 participants, using Fisher’s exact tests (Extended Data Table 7). Post hoc dose–response biomarker analyses were further assessed: cohorts were pooled on the basis of systemic exposure and target engagement into subtherapeutic (1, 3 and 6 mg kg−1; cohorts 1–3) and therapeutic (15 and 30 mg kg−1; cohorts 4–5) doses (Extended Data Fig. 2). Population pharmacokinetic modeling established that the IBC-Ab002 pharmacokinetic profile in dose levels ≥3 mg kg−1 is most compatible with a two-compartment linear model with first-order elimination. ADA status was determined using a validated tiered assay.

Data Availability Statement

Individual de‑identified participant data that underlie the results reported in this article, together with a data dictionary, will be made available, along with the study protocol and statistical analysis plan. Access will be granted to qualified researchers who submit a methodologically sound proposal for analyses aimed at achieving the objectives set out in that proposal; requests should be directed to the corresponding author and will be reviewed by the sponsor, ImmunoBrain Checkpoint Inc; requests will be reviewed and responded to within a 1-month period. Data will be shared after execution of a data‑sharing agreement and in accordance with applicable data protection regulations, including the EU/UK General Data Protection Regulation.

All analyses were performed with standard, widely used software (SAS v.9.4, Prism v.11 and R version 4.5, the latter with the ncaR and nlmixr packages). No custom code central to the study’s conclusions was generated; any analysis scripts are available from the corresponding author under the same conditions as the data.
