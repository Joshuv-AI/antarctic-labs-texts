Area-Selective Low-Pressure Thermal Atomic Layer Deposition of Gallium Nitride
Authors: Bernhard Y. van der Wel, Kees van der Zouw, Antonius A.I. Aarnink, Alexey Y. Kovalgin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This work reports on the successful intrinsic area-selective deposition of GaN films by thermal atomic layer deposition at back-end-of-line compatible temperatures. Using sequential pulses of trimethylgallium and ammonia (NH3) at a substrate temperature of 673 K, polycrystalline GaN is selectively formed onto AlN on substrates consisting of patterned AlN on thermal SiO2. Before the deposition of GaN, a thin film of AlN is deposited selectively by atomic layer deposition on the patterned AlN to enable the deposition of GaN on AlN. The interfaces and layer thicknesses were examined by transmission electron microscopy, spectroscopic ellipsometry, and atomic force microscopy.

Introduction

Group III nitrides (III-nitrides) are essential in high-power and high-performance electronics and optoelectronics. They are implemented in applications such as light-emitting diodes (LEDs), high-electron-mobility transistors, photodetectors, and thin-film transistors. Conventionally, these materials are grown on mismatched substrates involving thick buffer layers and large thermal budgets with temperatures well above the back-end-of-line (BEOL) limit of 673 K for silicon processing. , A suitable candidate to investigate thin film deposition of III-nitrides with a reduced thermal budget is atomic layer deposition (ALD). Being a subset of chemical vapor deposition (CVD), ALD involves sequential self-limiting vapor–solid reactions, resulting in thin film deposition with sub-nm precision, high conformality, and considerable area uniformity. ,− Initially introduced as atomic layer epitaxy (ALE) by Suntola, ,, the technique has been adopted in countless fabrication processes of devices with feature sizes below 10 nm. − ,, Thanks to its characteristics, ALD is essential in accommodating the demand for ever-decreasing feature size in the semiconductor industry. Along with the implementation of ALD, the critical alignment requirements have been increasing, igniting interest in methods to reduce the number of necessary lithography steps. One strategy to achieve this, which has taken much attention recently, is area-selective deposition (ASD). , While advanced ASD involves the periodic introduction of passivation, activation, or etching agents, conventional ASD centers on the initial conditions of the substrate surface. Apart from passivation or activation techniques (e.g., by surface functionalization using a polymer, a self-assembled monolayer (SAM), or small-molecule inhibitors (SMIs)), the most rudimentary conventional approach is that of intrinsic or inherent ASD. , This involves clean surfaces and surfaces with native terminal groups such as hydride, hydroxyl, and amino groups. With ALD being inherently surface-sensitive, selectivity between different surfaces can be exploited to deposit III-nitrides selectively. This enables further down-scaling of device dimensions and expands the applications area, facilitating novel device architectures and fabrication processes. ,, In this study, we show the intrinsic thermal AS-ALD of GaN films on patterned thin film AlN on SiO2 below the BEOL temperature limit of 673 K. ,,,

Considerable research into III–V thin films has already been performed. GaAs was the first III–V compound deposited with ALD (labeled “molecular layer epitaxy”, MLE) by Nishizawa et al. in 1985. , A few years later, the first III-nitride, AlN, was deposited by ALD in 1991. Shortly after, explorations were initiated into the ALD of GaN on sapphire substrates by Asif Khan et al., referred to as “switched atomic layer epitaxy” (SALE), using metalorganic chemical vapor deposition (MOCVD) equipment. These first investigations used triethylgallium (TEG) as the metal precursor, ammonia (NH3) as the nitrogen precursor, and H2 as a carrier gas at a substrate temperature range of 723–973 K. − An attempt to decrease the deposition temperature was made by Sumakeris et al. by leading the NH3 to flow over a tungsten filament heated to 1473 K, resulting in successful deposition at a substrate temperature ranging from 393 to 823 K. It is likely that under these growth conditions, a substantial fraction of the supplied NH3 decomposed into reactive NH2 radicals, enabling the growth at reduced temperatures. , Recently, Deminskyi et al. investigated the ALD of GaN using these precursors in combination with a myriad of energetic and reactive species, concluding that a purely thermal process without radical enhancement at substrate temperatures ranging from 433 to 623 K does not lead to film formation. As a methylated surface would favor reactions with NH3, , TEG was soon replaced with TMG (trimethylgallium). − Depositions using TMG and NH3 at temperatures below 773 K and without additional activation methods were reported much later, resulting in polycrystalline films on Si and TiN substrates. − Plasma was used to enhance the reactivity of the nitrogen-containing precursor, which was introduced by Adelmann et al. with N2 plasma in the early 2000s. , Still, this method did not get traction until the 2010s. At that time, the N2 plasma (often combined with an additional flux of H2 − or Ar , ) and NH3 plasma ,,,− became the common nitrogen-delivering species. The increased reactivity of plasmas enabled deposition at lower substrate temperatures. Apart from TEG and TMG, alternative metal-delivering precursors used are Ga, , GaCl, , GaCl3, , TDMAG, and Ga­(triaz)3, the latter being specifically developed to accommodate ALD. This work uses the precursor combination of TMG and NH3, as they are readily available, well-studied, and low-cost.

ALD of strongly oriented metastable cubic GaN has been established using buffer layers on GaAs at 673 and 623–823 K. More commonly, ALD of strongly oriented hexagonal GaN using buffer layers has been reported on GaAs at 773 and 773–923 K and on sapphire at 723–1173, , 823, 973, and 1073–1173 K. Epitaxial growth without the use of buffer layers has been achieved on sapphire 548 K , and on SiC at temperatures as low as 523, 543–923, and 623–673 K. However, these films have been reported to exhibit an increase in defect density after the critical thickness of 5–6 nm and are considered polycrystalline after that. ,,, While most authors retain self-limiting growth during deposition of crystalline GaN, ,,,,,− this does not hold for all ,,, or is not reported. Sumakeris et al. report that self-limiting growth occurs only at reduced temperatures, but this results in polycrystalline films (below 523 K) or even amorphous films (below 443 K) on α­(6H)-SiC(0001) substrates. To obtain a strongly oriented thin film, the substrate lattice and temperature are crucial. Exploiting hot filaments, plasmas, or precursor engineering to generate energetic and reactive species provides a viable route to reduce the thermal budget and deposit the material below the BEOL limit. Still, considering a penalty in parameters like mobility or quantum efficiency, polycrystalline films can be a cost-effective alternative to their monocrystalline equivalent, and crystallinity may improve as research progresses. −

ASD of hexagonal GaN was first observed during the fabrication of p–n junction type blue/ultraviolet (UV) light emitting diodes (LEDs) with an AIGaN/GaN double heterostructure by Akasaki et al. in 1992, where a GaN film was formed on an AlN buffer layer while merely islands were formed on the exposed sapphire substrate with metalorganic vapor phase epitaxy (MOVPE). , ASD of cubic GaN was first reported in 1994 by Nagahara et al., employing SiO2 as masking material, a method swiftly adopted for the ASD of hexagonal GaN by Kato et al., both using MOVPE. After that, selective area growth (SAG) has been widely used to fabricate waveguides, field emitters, facet lasers, and low-dimensional quantum structures and to reduce the defect density in GaN films − using several deposition methods: MOVPE/MOCVD, ,,,− hydride vapor phase epitaxy (HVPE), ,,− molecular beam epitaxy (MBE), − the sublimation method, − and even magnetron sputter epitaxy (MSE). A common technique to reduce the defect density mentioned earlier is epitaxial lateral overgrowth (ELO or, alternately, ELOG, LEO). It is based on the epitaxial growth of GaN on the exposed surface of lattice-matched substrates inside windows opened in a masking material (such as SiO2, SiN x , W, or WN x ) without nucleation on the masking material, followed by lateral extension and coalescence. The technique relies on the combined effects of selective epitaxy and growth anisotropy, and its use has played a significant role in breaking the bottleneck concerning GaN laser diode lifetimes. By altering the growth conditions, lateral growth can be suppressed, while promoting growth in other crystallographic directions. Both lateral and nonlateral growth can be used in techniques akin to ELO, such as selective area epitaxy (SAE), selective area growth (SAG), and pendeo epitaxy (PE). This has been utilized by Akasaka et al. to grow GaN hexagonal micro prisms of 5–16 μm in diameter on a sapphire substrate, where the relatively low total pressure (∼100 mbar) and partial pressure of precursors were deemed crucial in obtaining highly oriented, micrometer-sized crystals with smooth and flat facet surfaces.

The thermal ALD of GaN below the BEOL limit without additional activation (e.g., using NH3 plasma) has proven challenging. Earlier work demonstrated the thermal ALD of GaN, exploiting high NH3 dosages at reactor pressures of 1–10 mbar on H-terminated Si, OH-terminated SiO2, and in situ deposited seed layers of AlN and GaN. , The growth on Si and SiO2 exhibited a nucleation delay, while that on AlN and GaN did not, indicating intrinsic selectivity toward the thermal ALD of GaN. Reducing the reactor pressure regime to 10–3–10–2 mbar (concurrently reducing the NH3 dosage) did not result in growth on H-terminated Si or in situ deposited seed layer of AlN but did indicate that nucleation occurred on AlN. Recently, we managed to initiate the ALD of AlN at these conditions below the BEOL limit and reported on the intrinsic thermal area-selective atomic layer deposition (AS-ALD) of AlN on patterned AlN/SiO2 and proposed that it might be a pathway for subsequent intrinsic AS-ALD of GaN. ,

AS-ALD is based on a difference in surface reaction kinetics between the growth surface and the nongrowth surface under certain growth conditions. The kinetics must be controlled such that, to achieve selectivity, the GPC on the GS becomes significantly larger than that on the NS. The self-limiting nature of ALD implies that the reactions stop after the formation of at most 1 monolayer. Whether the process runs in a full saturation or subsaturated regime is a consequence of the growth conditions, among which pulse time is crucially important. However, increasing this time, to achieve full saturation of GPC, can lead to a loss of selectivity. This is because film growth on the NS can still continue slowly, while that on the GS cannot (self-limitation). To maintain selectivity, performing the process in a subsaturated regime may be necessary.

In this work, we demonstrate that the AS-ALD of AlN, as proposed in our previous work, can be a precursor for the AS-ALD of GaN. We use TMA (trimethylaluminum), TMG, and NH3 to deposit AlN and, subsequently, GaN. AlN would be a good candidate for the growth surface (GS) enabling GaN deposition because of their low lattice mismatch (2.4%) and common functional surface groups (primary, secondary, and tertiary amino groups: −NH2, =NH, and N, respectively). For the nongrowth surface (NS), thermal SiO2 is used. AlN and SiO2 surfaces are expected to exhibit a significant difference in nucleation of GaN precursors due to the amorphous character of thermally grown SiO2 and the difference in the functional groups terminating their surfaces (i.e., amino versus hydroxyl, respectively). , Here, both AS-ALD of AlN and GaN were likely operating within a subsaturated regime with a penalty in GPC and increased the O share within the films. The purge durations were long to ensure little to no precursor mixing (PM). Albeit under significantly different conditions, the combination of GS/NS with ASD of AlN and GaN used here has been reported before by MOCVD at 1373 K and 267 mbar. To our knowledge, this work is the first demonstration of the intrinsic ASD of GaN by ALD. An attempt to explain the origin of selectivity based on the literature is presented in the discussion. This work does not elaborate on the deposition of AlN and GaN being ALD, as this was the subject of prior studies. ,,

Methods

A home-built hot-wall single-wafer reactor (wafer chamber volume of 32 cm3) having a base pressure of <10–8 mbar is used. The AS-ALD of AlN and GaN by ALD is performed using TMA (electronic grade, N6.0) or TMG (electronic grade, N6.0) as a precursor, together with NH3 (N5.0) as the coreactant and Ar (argon, N5.0) as the purge/carrier gas (caution: TMA and TMG are pyrophoric, exposure to air may result in spontaneous ignition). The reactor is equipped with spectroscopic ellipsometry (SE) for in situ film growth monitoring. Additional ex-situ analysis is performed using SE, atomic force microscopy (AFM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and scanning TEM (STEM) accompanied by energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis. See the Supporting Information for more details on the equipment used.

A prerequisite for intrinsic AS-ALD is a significant difference in the nucleation delay of a specific ALD process on at least two surfaces. To demonstrate AS-ALD, SiO2 is taken as NS, and AlN is taken as GS. The selectivity S ranges from 0 to 1, varies with the number of cycles, and is approximated by employing the film thickness ratio (determined with SE); see our previous work and references. ,,,

A substrate containing both NS and GS is needed to establish AS-ALD on a single substrate. We start with a Si wafer with thermal SiO2. Next, a layer of AlN is grown on top of the SiO2, after which the AlN is patterned. Preparing the AlN pattern involves reactive sputtering of polycrystalline AlN and conventional lithography. OPD4262 (developer containing TMAH - tetramethylammonium hydroxide) is used to both develop the photoresist (293 K, 60 s) and selectively etch the exposed AlN (90 s, about 20 nm/min), generating circular patterns of AlN on top of the thermal SiO2. As AlN is prone to oxidation in the ambient atmosphere, an ex situ pretreatment is employed just before the substrate is loaded into the reactor load lock (being evacuated to <10–5 mbar). During the pretreatment, surface organics are removed with HNO3 (99 wt %, 293 K), and the native AlO x N y formed on the AlN pattern is stripped with H3PO4 (85 wt %, 293 K). The substrate preparation and pretreatment are discussed in great detail in our previous works. , After that, the substrate is transferred from the load lock into the reactor (<10–7 mbar) and kept at approximately 473 K for approximately 14 h. Before the start of actual deposition experiments, the substrate temperature is increased to the desired process temperature. We found that the ALD of GaN does not initiate on AlN without in situ ALD of AlN. Here, the ALD of AlN exhibits characteristics of substrate-inhibited growth and goes through a maximum in the GPC around 4–8 nm. We hypothesize that at the maximum GPC of ALD of AlN, the ALD of GaN can proceed with the highest initial GPC, while the least amount of AlN has been deposited on the NGA. Therefore, AlN is grown by AS-ALD until it reaches the thickness at which the GPC goes through a maximum, after which we proceed with in situ AS-ALD of GaN, resulting in a minimal added layer of in situ grown AlN. Process Flow That Is Used for Preparing the GS and NS for AS-ALD of GaN

To enable GaN deposition, AlN was deposited in situ by AS-ALD at a reactor pressure of 1 × 10–3 mbar and 623 K using TMA and NH3. The ALD cycle consists of the TMA pulse (t 4) and Ar purge (t 2) and the NH3 pulse (t 3) and Ar purge (t 4), with {t 1/t 2/t 3/t 4} = {0.1/30/0.1/30} s. Next, the reactor is prepared for AS-ALD of GaN by increasing the reactor pressure to 10 mbar using an Ar flow and the temperature to 673 K, well below the decomposition temperature of TMG in the gas phase. − As determined in our previous study, these parameters enable ALD of GaN with a feasible GPC with little to no carbon contamination. , Establishing these conditions after terminating AlN deposition is necessary as increasing the temperature or reactor pressure during ALD of AlN leads to loss of the self-limiting nature of TMA chemisorption and covers the SiO2 with AlN, resulting in a substrate incapable of sustaining selective growth of GaN. In contrast, after stabilizing these conditions for 1 h without deposition of AlN, the AS-ALD of GaN was enabled. Here, the ALD cycle consisted of the TMG pulse (t 5) and purge (t 6) and the NH3 pulse (t 7) and purge (t 8). The standard ALD cycle was chosen as {t 5/t 6/t 7/t 8} = {0.1/30/30/60} s based on results from our previous work to ensure sufficient purging and a feasible growth rate. , During deposition, the total purge flow of Ar was set to 21 sccm, which additionally functions as a carrier gas for both precursors.

Results

To demonstrate the AS-ALD of GaN, the growth of GaN needs to be established on the GS without significant growth on the NS. Before combining the GS and NS on a single substrate, a difference in the nucleation delay is first demonstrated on separate AlN- and SiO2-coated substrates.

The growth of ALD-GaN on separate AlN- and SiO2-coated substrates is presented in Figure . It demonstrates that no significant growth initiates on SiO2 and sputtered AlN, both behaving as NS. In our previous work, we observed a long nucleation delay during ALD of AlN on sputtered AlN, which we attribute to oxynitride formation upon exposing sputtered AlN to oxidizing ambients (air, liquid chemicals, etc.) before, during, and after the patterning process. , In the case of AS-ALD of AlN, the ex situ oxynitride strip employing H3PO4 (85 wt %, 293 K) was sufficient to reduce the nucleation delay and deposit ALD AlN selectively on the sputtered AlN. This work used the same procedure to strip the oxynitride from the sputtered AlN. Nevertheless, a long nucleation delay was observed during ALD of GaN on the sputtered AlN directly. However, after having deposited about 6 nm or more of AlN by ALD onto the sputtered AlN, growth of GaN by ALD does commence (provided there was no vacuum break after the ALD of AlN) and reveals that the ALD of AlN can form a suitable GS in situ onto sputtered AlN. This demonstrates the critical influence of functional surface groups of the AlN surface on the enhancement of GaN growth. Previous work also shows that preferential growth occurs on the sputtered AlN (acting at GS) while SiO2 acts as NS for AS-ALD of AlN. , Provided that the AlN has obtained suitable functional surface groups (e.g., by coating it in situ by AS-ALD with AlN), the results indicate that SiO2 and sputtered AlN can be used as NS and GS, respectively, for the AS-ALD of GaN.

Next, moving away from deposition on separate substrates, we demonstrate selective growth on a single substrate containing the GS and NS, prepared as described in Scheme . We use the conditions described in the previous section to deposit 6 nm of AlN by AS-ALD at a reactor pressure of 10–3 mbar and substrate temperature of 623 K. After that, the temperature set point is increased to 673 K, and the reactor pressure is increased to 10 mbar using a throttle valve, reducing the effective pumping speed of the turbomolecular pump. After stabilizing the substrate temperature, 6 nm of GaN is deposited onto the GS by AS-ALD. The growth and growth per cycle (GPC) as a function of time for AlN and GaN are displayed in Figure , both deposited by AS-ALD. Previously, we determined the type of growth of AlN to be substrate-inhibited growth of type 2. ,, The subsequent growth of GaN seems to be a combination of two growth modes. At first, the growth appears to be substrate-enhanced, as the GPC in the first 15 cycles starts high and decreases to a nonzero value. Note that this effect could also be caused by the inaccuracy of the SE model for very thin layers. After that, the GPC increases again, stabilizing without going through a clear maximum, indicative of substrate-inhibited growth of type 1. Assuming island growth for both depositions, coalescence initiates where the slope of the GPC has reached its maximum, giving about 150 cycles for AlN and about 60 cycles for GaN. Here, the growth exits the initial regime and enters the transition regime. The AS-ALD of GaN on AlN seems substrate-inhibited, as an initial delay in the GPC is observed. However, it is difficult to reliably determine the growth type on this surface and whether island formation occurs.

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was used to image the circular AlN pattern before and after AS-ALD, as depicted in Figure . Before AS-ALD, the NS was flat, and only a few islands were visible, presumably a result of incomplete removal of the sputtered AlN during the patterning process and pretreatment. Just before AS-ALD, the GS has a root-mean-square (RMS) roughness of 0.7 nm and the NS has an RMS roughness of 0.9 nm. The initial surface roughness of the AlN surface is a consequence of the sputtering process (increasing roughness) and the H3PO4 pretreatment (reducing roughness). After AS-ALD of both AlN and GaN, islands appeared on the NS. The RMS roughness of the GS increased to 2.3 nm and that of the NS increased to 3.8 nm. We attribute the increase in roughness on the GS to the initial growth phase being substrate-inhibited. This is caused by partial oxidation of the GS during substrate cleaning and transfer into the process chamber, leading to island formation and increased surface roughness. On the NS, the increase in roughness is more prominent, indicating that the growth on the NS had not surpassed the point of coalescence. − Considering the trend in GPC depicted in Figure b, the lower roughness increase on the GS is indicative that growth indeed had surpassed the point of coalescence on the GS. An average height profile across the pattern reveals the relative increase in film thickness of about 10 nm, see Figure c. This is close to the total thickness added onto the GS of 12 nm (6 nm of AlN and 6 nm of GaN) as determined by in situ SE. The difference of about 2 nm can originate from the deposition on the NS or from the optical model used during SE analysis.

Starting at position x = 0 nm only the C capping layer is visible, which decreases sharply at the thin-film stack, after which it slowly diminishes. Subsequently, the Ga content decreases, coinciding with an increase in the Al content and a slight rise in N content. However, additional oxidation of the lamella sidewalls (occurring in time between lamella preparation and TEM analysis) could result in an artificially increased average oxygen content measured during the analysis. Similarly, the carbon present could be a remainder from the lamella preparation process and/or adventitious carbon deposited between ex situ preparation of the lamella and TEM measurements.

Discussion

The following section discusses insights into the possible origins of the selective growth mechanism by analyzing the relevant literature. The possible mechanisms considering the selective growth of AlN on a patterned AlN/SiO2 substrate are not discussed here, as it has been treated elaborately in our previous work.

Let us examine the precursors, TMG and NH3. TMG is monomeric and shows little tendency to dimerize. ,− The onset of dissociation in an N2 environment lies between 673 and 723 K. Still, it may depend on the reactivity of the carrier gas, as dissociation in a H2 environment reportedly occurs at temperatures as low as 573 K. ,, Complete disassociation into DMG (dimethylgallium) occurs above 823 K, − but no methyl radicals are created in the gas phase between 373 and 843 K. TMG and DMG form a stable reversible adduct with NH3 in the gas phase. − ,− Similar to the gas phase, adducts may form on the substrate surface, with the difference that evolving gaseous byproducts are pumped away, making the reaction irreversible. For a Lewis adduct to be formed at the surface, a surface atom must have a free electron pair or lack an electron pair, the former allowing adduct formation with a free TMG molecule and the latter with a free NH3 molecule.

Continuing with the growth of GaN on AlN, we observed that the ALD of GaN does not initiate on sputtered AlN without in situ ALD of AlN on it. In contrast with the ALD of GaN, the ALD of AlN (on sputtered AlN) does initiate. We propose that this originates in TMA’s ability to form a functional surface group lacking an electron pair, such as bridged or terminal MMA (monomethylaluminum). This allows further reaction with NH3, which can form a functional surface group with a free electron pair, such as terminal primary amino groups (−NH2). The hindrance of GaN nucleation can arise from incomplete removal of oxidized AlN or introduction of hydroxyl surface groups during preparation and loading of the substrate, as visible as an increase in O content in Figure , a situation similar to the suspected origin of the nucleation delay of ALD AlN on exposed-to-air AlN, observed in our previous work. For GaN, as ab initio calculations state, the phase transitions between the main dominating surface terminations occur at lower temperatures than for AlN. AlN’s stable termination at 600 K and 1 mbar consists of a total coverage of terminal primary amino groups (−NH2) located directly above a metal site. In comparison, for GaN at 600 K and 1 mbar, the surface is covered with hydrogen and bare sites, having already lost all of the amino surface groups at this temperature. Increasing the pressure can, to some extent, prevent the loss of primary terminal amino groups. At the deposition conditions for GaN used in this work, 10 mbar and 673 K, the GaN surface is at the line of transition between a phase where the surface is covered with H and bare sites and a phase where the surface is covered with NH2, H, and bare sites. This might explain the difficulties of growing ALD GaN once TMG reacted with the AlN surface. The reaction of TMG with the amino groups on the AlN surface consumes the primary terminal amino groups present at the AlN surface. Supplementary exposure to NH3 will hardly form new amino groups on the Ga surface sites, leading to a net decrease in the reactive surface sites needed for sustaining future deposition cycles.

Next, considering the growth of GaN on SiO2, it is reported that TMG reacts in a self-limiting fashion with silanol and siloxane to form gallium sites with two methyl ligands. The reaction occurs pairwisely, as the first reaction of TMG forms a DMG surface group (−Ga­(CH3)2), which coordinates with an adjacent silanol, increasing that silanol’s acidity, making it more reactive toward the second TMG. Both DMG surface sites form a dimer containing significant Lewis acidity. Exposure to NH3 leads to their coordination with a DMG surface group by disrupting the bridging interaction without evolving methane. This generates a four-coordinate Ga site that is no longer Lewis acidic. This may explain the lack of growth of GaN by ALD on the NS. However, in this case, one would expect the SiO2 surface to exist of groups containing Ga and N. This is not confirmed with STEM-EDX and might be explained by the SiO2 surface already being modified by TMA during AlN deposition. Adsorption of TMA onto SiO2 leads to methylation of the surface, which is left occupied with Al- and Si-containing groups that are no longer acidic. This is also indicated by the Al measured at the surface in Figure . The combination of selective deposition of both AlN and GaN has been reported before in an MOCVD process. Here, the nucleation of AlN on SiO2 is observed. Still, the selectivity of subsequent GaN deposition is conserved, which is attributed to the reduced reactivity of polycrystalline AlN on the NS compared to the monocrystalline GS. The similarity between these results and those presented here may indicate that the conditions at the surface responsible for selective growth in the case of MOCVD and ALD are much alike.

Conclusions

Employing AlN as GS and SiO2 as NS, we have demonstrated the successful AS-ALD of GaN on patterned substrates. In situ AS-ALD of AlN was crucial to obtaining the required surface conditions to enable AS-ALD of GaN. Hexagonal AlN and GaN layers were confirmed from both lattice spacings and EDX measurements, showing oxygen enrichment between the two AlN layers. GaN crystals tend to grow epitaxially on AlN crystals. Limited deposition is observed on the NS, revealing small islands (2–7 nm) with lattice spacings and elemental content of GaN on top of a thin Al-rich layer that covers the entire NS.

We propose that the lack of growth on the NS mainly arises from the (lack of) Lewis acidity of the chemisorbed surface groups. The methylation of the SiO2 surface causes this through TMA adsorption during AlN deposition and by generating four-coordinate Ga sites on SiO2. This results in surface groups that are no longer Lewis acidic and less reactive to NH3 and prevents further chemisorption of TMG during GaN deposition.

In contrast, primary terminal amino groups at the AlN surface are expected to play a crucial role in successful ASD, as they are very susceptible to reacting with TMA or TMG. A reduction in the surface coverage of primary terminal amino groups by oxidation may explain the necessity for in situ ASD of AlN. Additionally, surface reactions with TMG also lead to less surface coverage of primary terminal amino groups when compared to TMA, which may explain the limited growth rate observed for the AS-ALD of GaN compared to that of AlN.
