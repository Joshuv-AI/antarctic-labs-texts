Gut microbiota profiles associated with persistent anal HPV infection in men who have sex with men
Authors: Heng Yang, Juyuan Bian, Wenhui Yu, Shulipan Asilibieke, Tian Tian, Wenjie Zhang, Henry S Lynn, Jingjing He, Jianghong Dai, Guozhen Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore the differences in the composition of the anorectal microbiota in the context of known anorectal HPV infection, providing scientific evidence for early prevention and intervention of persistent HPV infection in the MSM population.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed based on an existing prospective cohort study conducted between 2016 and 2023, which provided rectal swab samples for microbiota sequencing. After providing consent, 58 participants with more than 8 months of follow-up were divided into two groups on the basis of their HPV infection status during their last two follow-up visits: the HPV-negative group (negative) and the persistent HPV-positive group (positive). Rectal swab samples were collected from both groups, and 16 S rRNA sequencing was performed to analyze the microbiota composition.ResultsNo significant differences were observed in the α- or β-diversity of the gut microbiota between MSM with persistent HPV infection and those in the HPV-negative group. However, the relative abundance of Bacteroides fragilis was significantly higher in the persistent HPV group (p = 0.002, bh_p = 0.016). Additionally, taxa such as Victivallis, Negativibacillus, Victivallaceae, and Eggerthella were enriched in individuals with persistent infection. In contrast, Prevotella disiens, Gardnerella, and Negativicoccus sp. showed higher relative abundances in the HPV-negative group, suggesting their potential as microbial markers associated with HPV clearance.ConclusionThis study indicate that the gut microbiota is altered in MSM with persistent HPV infection. Although no significant differences were observed in overall α and β diversity, taxa such as Bacteroides fragilis, Victivallis, and Eggerthella were enriched in the persistent group, while Prevotella disiens and Gardnerella were more abundant in the negative group. These findings are exploratory, and future longitudinal studies with HPV genotyping and confounder control are warranted to clarify their relevance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11624-9.Keywords: Human papillomavirus, Men who have sex with men, HIV-negative, Gut microbiota, Persistent infection

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is one of the most common sexually transmitted viruses in both men and women [1, 2]. Owing to specific sexual behaviors, anorectal HPV infection is highly prevalent among men who have sex with men (MSM) [3–5]. A meta-analysis revealed that the prevalence of anorectal HPV infection among HIV-negative MSM is 64% [6]. The incidence of HPV-related anal cancer has been reported to be increasing globally [7], with HPV detected in the anal environment in 88.3% of anal cancer patients [8]. MSM are approximately 20 times more likely to develop anal cancer than heterosexual men are [9, 10].

Most HPV infections do not exhibit obvious clinical symptoms and may be spontaneously cleared, but persistent HPV infection can lead to various benign and malignant lesions [11]. High-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (HSILs) of the anus are considered precursors to anal squamous cell carcinoma (ASCC), and their development is closely associated with persistent infection with high-risk HPV (HR-HPV) [12, 13]. Additionally, low-risk HPV (LR-HPV) can also cause genital warts [14, 15].

The occurrence and carcinogenic mechanisms of persistent HPV infection are thought to be related to changes in the local microbiota. Multiple studies have shown that dysbiosis not only leads to inflammation but also promotes persistent HPV infection and the development and progression of HSIL. Some studies have shown that bacterial metabolites can activate inflammasomes in epithelial cells, thus expanding the inflammatory response and enhancing the activity of HPV oncogenic proteins [16], which is closely associated with persistent HPV infection. One study divided HIV-positive MSM with HR-HPV infection into an HSIL group and a control group and reported significant differences in the abundance of 28 bacterial genera in the anorectal microbiota, further revealing a proinflammatory and carcinogenic microenvironment specific to persistent HPV infection [17]. These studies suggest a close relationship between changes in microbiota composition, persistent HPV infection, and tumorigenesis. As the terminal part of the digestive tract, the anorectum harbors a large number of resident microbiota.

This study aimed to explore the association between gut microbiota composition and persistent anal HPV infection in MSM. While previous research has investigated the role of microbiota in cervical HPV infection or the anal microbiome in cross-sectional HPV-positive populations, few studies have focused on persistent HPV infection in MSM—a population at disproportionately high risk for HPV-related anal dysplasia and cancer. Our study is novel in its use of a follow-up design to identify microbiota profiles linked to persistent infection, rather than transient detection, and adds to the limited body of evidence regarding the microbial environment that may influence HPV persistence at the anal site.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This study focused on MSM community organizations in Urumqi, Xinjiang. The participants were primarily volunteers from MSM community organizations in Urumqi. To ensure the scientific rigor and representativeness of the study, the following strict inclusion criteria were set: (1) aged 18 to 65 years; (2) had had sexual intercourse with at least one same-sex partner in the past six months; and (3) agreed to provide anorectal specimens. All participants signed a written informed consent form, and the study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University (Batch Number: 20160512-11).

Recruitment was conducted through a partnership with the Xinjiang Dream Health Service Center, a local nongovernmental organization recognized by health authorities. Multiple channels, including website advertisements, WeChat, and peer referrals, were used for recruitment. At baseline and during follow-up visits every six months, participants completed an electronic questionnaire that collected demographic and sexual behavior data.

To minimize the potential influence of confounding factors on gut microbiota composition, all participants included in the analysis were HIV-negative, had not used antibiotics or probiotics within one month prior to sampling, and reported no use of pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP). These criteria were applied during data quality control to ensure sample consistency and analytical validity.

Before the study began, clinical physicians provided training to volunteers, who then assisted participants in self-collecting anorectal specimens. Self-collection was chosen to enhance participant privacy and compliance while reducing discomfort or embarrassment, particularly in MSM populations. Previous studies have demonstrated that this method is both acceptable and reliable in similar contexts [18, 19]. This procedure involved rotating a swab inside the anal canal and placing it in 3 ml of transport medium for HPV testing and microbiome sequencing. The samples were stored at 4 °C for no longer than 24 h and were transported in foam boxes with ice packs to the laboratory for further processing.

The participants received a reimbursement of 40 RMB (approximately 6.6 USD) for their travel and time costs for each visit, including both the initial and subsequent follow-ups every six months. This study is a retrospective secondary analysis using data and biospecimens collected from a prospective cohort of MSM enrolled between 2016 and 2023. A total of 58 participants whose follow-up exceeded 8 months were divided into two groups on the basis of their anal HPV infection status during the last two follow-up visits: the HPV-negative group (negative) and the persistent HPV-positive group (positive).

Anorectal swab samples were then collected from these participants for microbiome composition sequencing analysis.

Persistent HPV infection was defined as the detection of the same HPV type in the anorectal region during at least two consecutive follow-up visits.

Variable measurement

The questionnaire consisted of the following sections:

Demographic information

Basic demographic data, including age, household registration, ethnicity, education level, marital status, and income, were collected.

Sexual behavior characteristics

Sexual history from the past six months was collected, including sexual orientation, the number of sexual partners, sexual roles (passive, active, and versatile), condom use, drug use, the gender of sexual partners, whether participants seek sexual partners in public places such as baths, and the frequency of anal intercourse in the past week. The behavioral data used in this study were extracted from the questionnaire administered at the same follow-up visit when the anal swab samples were collected.

The questionnaire used in this study was adapted from a previously published version [20].

HPV DNA detection and typing

The Hybribio 37 HPV GenoArray Diagnostic Kit (Hybribio Biotech, Chaozhou, China) was used to detect and genotype HPV, following the methodology previously described. This diagnostic tool uses a flow-through hybridization and gene-chip technique, where the low-density gene chip is precoated with 37 type-specific oligonucleotides. Genotype analysis was conducted via the HybriMax system (Hybribio Biotech), with the results discerned through a colorimetric shift on the membrane, which was observed directly. Positive and negative controls were systematically integrated within the GenoArray test kit for each polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis and throughout the hybridization phase to ensure quality control. This assay facilitates the concurrent detection of 37 HPV genotypes: HPV6, 11, 16, 18, 26, 31, 33–35, 39, 40, 42–45, 51–59, 61, 66–73, and 81–84.

Laboratory measurements

The 16 S rRNA gene sequencing methods were adapted from the Human Microbiome Project and Earth Microbiome Project [21]. The swab samples were stabilized via BuccalFix DNA Stabilization Solution (Isohelix, BFX-25) within 1 h of collection and stored at − 80 °C until DNA isolation. The bacterial 16 S rRNA gene V3-V4 region was amplified and sequenced by Shanghai Tianhao Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Quality control, read processing, and taxonomic assignment

After removing potential adapter sequences and primers via the cutadapt plugin in QIIME2, the quality of the raw data was assessed. The DADA2 plugin in QIIME2 was used for quality filtering, denoising, merging, and chimera removal, generating a feature table and representative sequences.

After quality control with DADA2 in QIIME2, amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were generated directly. Each ASV/OTU representative sequence was compared with the database for taxonomic assignment. Taxonomic annotation of the ASV/OTU sequences was performed via QIIME2 software (confidence level: 0.8; ITS: 0.6; annotation method: classify-sklearn).

Statistical analysis

For the sexual behavior and demographic variables, statistical comparisons were performed in RStudio 4.3.1. The t test was used for normally distributed continuous variables, the Wilcoxon test was used for nonnormally distributed continuous variables, and the chi-square test was used for categorical variables.

To evaluate alpha diversity, we calculated five complementary indices: Observed species and Chao1 (richness estimators, with Chao1 sensitive to rare taxa), Shannon and Simpson (which reflect both richness and evenness, with Shannon giving more weight to abundant taxa and Simpson emphasizing dominant ones), and Good’s Coverage (assessing sampling completeness) [22]. For beta diversity, Bray–Curtis dissimilarity was used to quantify between-sample compositional differences, considering both presence and abundance of taxa. Group-level differences were visualized via PCoA and tested with ADONIS. These metrics are widely recommended in microbiome studies to comprehensively capture within- and between-sample diversity features [23].

Wilcoxon tests and Metastats analysis were used to compare differences in specific taxon abundances between groups, with the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) method applied to correct for the false discovery rate (FDR) due to multiple comparisons. The BH procedure adjusts the p-values to control the expected proportion of false positives among the set of significant results, thereby improving the reliability of the findings in high-dimensional microbiome data. The linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size method (LEfSe) was used to identify the species most likely to explain group differences. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In this study, we performed anorectal swab microbiome sequencing on MSM with different HPV infection statuses to explore an unresolved issue—the association between the anorectal microbiota and persistent anal HPV infection in MSM. Previous studies have indicated that in the vaginal environment, HPV-positive women typically exhibit increased microbial diversity and reduced levels of key lactobacilli; such alterations are also linked to persistent HPV infection and a greater risk of cervical precancerous lesions [24–28]. In contrast, our study did not observe differences in the alpha or beta diversity of the gut microbiota among MSM with different HPV infection statuses, a finding that is consistent with that of Cristina et al. [17], who compared the gut microbiota of MSM with HSIL versus those without SIL. Furthermore, no significant difference in lactobacilli abundance was noted between the two groups. These discrepancies may be attributed to the unique ecology of the male gut microbiota, whereas the vaginal environment is predominantly dominated by lactobacilli [29], the gut microbiota is far more diverse and complex and is regulated by various local factors, such as pH, nutrient availability, and immune barriers [30, 31]. Consequently, the mechanisms underlying the response to HPV infection may differ fundamentally. Although some microbial abundance differences presented p values below 0.05, these differences were no longer significant after controlling for the false discovery rate, possibly due to the limited sample size (n = 58). Nevertheless, these differential taxa may still play a potential role in intergroup microecological changes, warranting further investigation and functional validation.

Our study revealed that the relative abundance of Bacteroides fragilis was greater in the gut microbiota of MSM with persistent HPV infection. B. fragilis is known to affect the host immune system by producing regulatory molecules, such as polysaccharide A (PSA), which can modulate CD4⁺ T cell responses and inflammatory pathways [32]. These immunomodulatory effects—particularly in the anorectal region—might influence local immune tolerance and potentially reduce antiviral responses, although such mechanisms in the context of HPV persistence remain hypothetical. In addition, enterotoxigenic B. fragilis (ETBF) secretes fragilysin, which disrupts epithelial barriers and induces IL-8-mediated inflammation, potentially contributing to a mucosal environment that favors viral persistence [33, 34]. Previous studies, such as Cristina et al. [17], have also reported increased B. fragilis abundance in MSM with HSIL, suggesting a possible association between this species and HPV-related disease progression. While these findings are intriguing, direct mechanistic evidence linking B. fragilis to HPV persistence is currently lacking. Therefore, our results should be interpreted with caution and considered hypothesis-generating, warranting further investigation in future mechanistic and longitudinal studies.

Our Metastats analysis revealed a significant result for the Eggerthellaceae family in the negative group, whereas the Wilcoxon test revealed that its subordinate taxon, the genus Eggerthella, was more abundant. Additionally, both Gardnerella and Negativibacillus were found to be significant in both analyses; specifically, Gardnerella was significantly enriched in the negative group, whereas Negativibacillus was enriched in the persistent infection group. Eggerthellaceae can metabolize plant polyphenols and flavonoids to produce metabolites with antiviral and immunomodulatory effects, thereby providing protection in both the gut and systemic immunity [35]. These metabolites may increase T-cell activity or natural killer (NK) cell function, strengthening the resistance of the host to HPV infection. Chronic low-grade inflammation typically impairs mucosal barrier function [36, 37], offering an opportunity for pathogens such as HPV to infect; the anti-inflammatory properties of Eggerthellaceae may help maintain a low-inflammatory state and enhance immune defense, thereby preventing HPV infection. Although Gardnerella is considered an important risk factor for bacterial vaginosis (BV) in women [38] and has been found to be highly prevalent among HPV-positive women [39], our results revealed that Gardnerella was more abundant in the gut microbiota of the negative control group, suggesting that it may be involved in inhibiting persistent HPV infection. The underlying mechanisms, however, require further exploration.

The abundance of Negativibacillus, another bacterium with increased abundance in individuals with persistent HPV infection, has been reported in liver cirrhosis studies to be positively correlated with the gut injury biomarker diamine oxidase (DAO) [40]. The specific mechanism by which Negativibacillus is involved in HPV infection remains unclear; it may modulate the gut microecological environment, affect intestinal barrier function, and alter immune responses, thereby potentially modifying the host’s immune reaction to the virus. These effects might contribute to HPV immune evasion and persistence.

In recent years, advances in molecular immunology have led to the emergence of immunointervention strategies—such as modulating the host immune microenvironment or targeting microbial metabolic pathways—as new approaches for treating clinical diseases [41, 42]. In the MSM population, persistent HPV infection is a major risk factor for anal precancerous lesions (e.g., HSIL), making it crucial to explore the underlying immune‒microbiota interactions for early intervention. A clinical study published in 2023 revealed that changes in the concentrations of anorectal microbiota metabolites (e.g., succinyl-CoA and cobalamin) were closely associated with HPV-related lesions: these metabolites were significantly elevated in individuals with persistent HPV infection and were positively correlated with the enrichment of 12 oncogenic microbial proteins. A diagnostic model based on these two metabolites achieved an 88% detection accuracy for HPV-related lesions, which was markedly greater than that of conventional anal exfoliated cell cytology (60%) [43]. This finding suggests that dysregulation of microbial metabolism may contribute to the malignant transformation process of persistent HPV infection by activating oncogenic pathways (such as microbial protein synthesis), providing a new perspective for understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying persistent HPV infection in MSM. Future research may further explore intervention strategies targeting microbial metabolites (e.g., metabolite antagonists or probiotic modulation) to block persistent HPV infection at an early stage and prevent its progression to precancerous lesions.

Conclusions

Our findings indicate that the gut microbiota is altered in MSM with persistent HPV infection. No significant differences in overall α and β diversity were observed between the persistent infection group and the negative control group, but a higher relative abundance of Bacteroides fragilis was observed in individuals with persistent infection. Additionally, certain taxa such as Victivallis, Negativibacillus, Victivallaceae, and Eggerthella appeared enriched in the persistent group, while Prevotella disiens, Gardnerella, and Negativicoccus were more abundant in the negative group, potentially serving as candidate microbial markers.

Limitations and strengths of the study

This study draws upon data from a longitudinal cohort of men who have sex with men (MSM) in Urumqi, providing novel insights into the potential associations between the gut microbiota and persistent anal HPV infection. The regional focus enhances our understanding of HPV epidemiology and microbial ecology in underrepresented populations and offers foundational data for informing public health strategies targeting MSM communities. The integration of behavioral, clinical, and microbiological data, together with the use of rigorous statistical methods, strengthens the internal validity of the findings. Moreover, the long-term nature of the cohort supports the exploration of persistence-related outcomes, which is a key clinical concern in HPV-related disease progression.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size (n = 58), although common in microbiome studies involving high-dimensional data, limits statistical power and increases the risk of both Type I and Type II errors. Second, microbiota sampling was conducted only at the final follow-up visit, precluding assessment of temporal changes and causal inferences. Third, although behavioral data were collected longitudinally, only the final time point was included in the analysis, and time-varying factors such as changes in sexual behavior or diet were not accounted for in the statistical models. Fourth, co-infections with other sexually transmitted infections (e.g., Chlamydia trachomatis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae), which are common in high-risk populations and may alter the gut microbiome [44, 45], were not assessed. Fifth, the absence of HPV genotyping limited our ability to differentiate oncogenic from non-oncogenic subtypes, affecting the biological interpretation of the findings. Finally, although anal swab samples were collected under staff supervision, the HPV assay used (Hybribio GenoArray) lacked an internal human DNA control, leaving open the possibility of insufficient cellularity in some HPV-negative cases.

While these limitations affect the interpretation of our findings and underscore the exploratory nature of the study, the hypothesis remains of notable clinical interest. Future research should employ longitudinal designs with repeated microbiota sampling, HPV genotyping, and robust adjustment for clinical and behavioral confounders to clarify the role of the gut microbiota in persistent anal HPV infection among MSM.
