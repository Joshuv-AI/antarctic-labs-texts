Sleep deprivation impairs gastric ulcer healing and induces anxiety-like behavior in rats
Authors: Omosola Fisayo Anifowose, Moshood Abiola Folawiyo, Temitope Elizabeth Olabiyi, Oluwabunmi Grace Benson, Durotioluwa Lois Olu-Agbede, Gbenga Sunday Adeleye, Oluwatomisin Olalekan Faniyan, Kehinde Samuel Olaniyi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study investigated the effects of total sleep deprivation (SD) on gastric ulcer (GU) healing, anxiety-like behavior, neuro-oxidative stress, antioxidant defenses, and prefrontal cortical histoarchitecture in male Wistar rats.ResultsSD exacerbated weight loss, elevated corticosterone levels, ulcer area, and delayed GU healing. Ulcer+sleep-deprived rats exhibited increased thigmotaxis, reduced center exploration, locomotion, and rearing. Biochemical analyses revealed sustained malondialdehyde elevation and decreased antioxidant activity. Histology demonstrated progressive prefrontal cortex neurodegeneration, most severe in ulcer+sleep-deprived group by day 14. These findings demonstrates that SD during GU healing intensifies neuro-oxidative stress, disrupts antioxidant defenses, aggravates anxiety-like behavior and promotes neurodegeneration, collectively delaying gastric-mucosal repair.

Introduction

Sleep deprivation (SD), defined as insufficient, fragmented, or poor-quality sleep, is increasingly common. It may be total, with no sleep for a period, or partial, with reduced or disrupted duration impairing recovery [1]. SD disrupts the balance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) and antioxidant defenses [2], causing oxidative damage in neural regions [3, 4]. In contrast, sleep clears oxidants and supports brain, endocrine, immune, and gastrointestinal functions [5, 6]. Even partial disruption impairs cognition, emotion, behavior, and gut integrity [7]. SD is strongly linked to pathological anxiety [3]: about 70% of individuals with anxiety disorders experience sleep loss.

Gastric ulcer is a global health burden [8], marked by mucosal erosion from imbalance between aggressive factors (acid, pepsin, NSAIDs, stress) and protective defenses (mucus, bicarbonate, prostaglandins, blood flow) [9]. Beyond these risks, oxidative stress and inflammation are key drivers of mucosal injury. Ulcer formation involves excess reactive oxygen/nitrogen species and neutrophil proteases [10], overwhelming antioxidants and causing lipid peroxidation. Malondialdehyde (MDA) reflects this damage [11], while depletion of glutathione (GSH) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) weakens defenses [10], impairing gastric function, promoting ulceration, and delaying healing. SD triggers oxidative stress, disrupts neuroendocrine and gastrointestinal function, and weakens stomach defenses [12] by elevating ROS, reducing antioxidants, raising gastric acid, reduces blood flow, decreases prostaglandins and impairing mucosal protection [13].

These findings suggest that SD may impair gastric ulcer healing by exacerbating oxidative stress; yet, how its combined effects with gastric ulceration may influence behavior during ulcer-healing process is unclear. Evaluating behavior alongside oxidative stress markers, antioxidant defenses, and tissue changes can clarify mechanisms of behavioral modulation and ulcer healing under sleep loss. This study therefore assessed whether SD during the gastric ulcer healing process is associated with specific alterations in brain oxidative stress levels, antioxidant enzyme activities, and histoarchitecture, as well as changes in behavior, particularly anxiety-like behavior, in rats.

Materials and methods

Animals

Sixty [14] male Wistar rats (100–120 g) were supplied from the central animal house of Ekiti State University, Nigeria, housed in groups of five in plastic cages and kept in the animal house on a 12 h light/dark schedule with pellet chow and water available ad libitum. Rats were acclimatized for 2 weeks before any experiment was conducted.

Experimental design

Rats were randomly divided into 4 groups (n = 5) for day 3, 7 and 14 of the experiment. Groups are:

Group I–Control (CNT)

Group II–Gastric ulcer alone (UA)

Group III–Sleep deprived (SD)

Group IV–Ulcer+sleep deprived (USD)

Daily weights of each animal were measured and recorded.

Gastric ulcer induction

Ulcer was induced using the acetic acid model as described by Ajayi and Olaleye [15] with minor modification. Rats were fasted but given water for 24 h before ulcer induction. Under anaesthesia (Ketamine 80 mg/kg-Xylazine 5 mg/kg), laparotomy was performed, the stomach exteriorized and clamped, 0.2 ml of 30% acetic acid was injected into the sub-serosa to induce gastric ulcer [16–18]. The serosa was rinsed with saline, the stomach returned, and the abdomen sutured. Animals recovered with free access to air and water.

Sleep deprivation method

Rats were totally sleep-deprived using a gentle-handling method that minimizes stress and excess locomotion [19]. From 7:00a.m. to 1:00p.m., handlers prevented sleep with gentle stimulation methods such as touching the tails or whiskers, brushing fur, adding novel objects and softly shaking cages [20, 21] thereby providing effective arousal without forced activity.

Open-field test (OFT)

Open-field test was conducted immediately after SD in a 72 × 72 cm arena with 36 cm high plywood walls. Blue lines divided the floor into 18 × 18 cm squares [22], with a central square marked in the middle. Each rat was placed individually at the centre and behaviour was recorded for 5 min by an overhead camera. Parameters assessed included time in centre vs. periphery, rearing frequency, grooming, and total distance travelled. The arena was cleaned with 70% ethanol after each trial to remove olfactory cues [23].

Assessment of gastric ulcer score

Rats were anesthetized with ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (5 mg/kg) before cervical dislocation [18]. Stomachs were excised, rinsed, dried, and ulcer dimensions measured under a magnifying lens. Ulcer Index (UI) was the sum of vertical and horizontal diameters, UA (mm2) = π x (sum of vertical diameters / 2) x (sum of transverse diameters / 2) and Percentage Healing (%H) was calculated relative to day 3 as [(UI day 3 – UI day X) / UI day 3] × 100, and similarly for day 14 [24].

Biochemical assays

Before cervical dislocation, blood was collected via the retro-orbital sinus for corticosterone estimation using competitive ELISA [25]. After dislocation, brains were excised, rinsed, weighed, and placed in phosphate buffer. Homogenates were centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C, and supernatants analyzed for lipid peroxidation [26], reduced glutathione [27], superoxide dismutase [28], and catalase [29].

Histology

A section of prefrontal cortex was fixed in phosphate-buffer formalin, processed and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Morphological evaluation and microphotographs were captured to reveal pathology or microscopic changes.

Statistical analysis

Results were analyzed with GraphPad Prism 8 and expressed as Mean ± SD. Group differences were assessed by means of analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by post hoc Tukey’s tests, with significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Total SD in rats with gastric ulcer decreased body weight gain

On day 3, Ulcer Alone group (UA: 130.7 ± 1.16) and Ulcer+Sleep-Deprived group (USD: 126 ± 1.53) showed significantly reduced body weights compared with Control group (150 ± 3.06). By day 7, USD group exhibited a further significant decrease (124 ± 1.00), while the SD group remain increased (148.7 ± 3.06) compared with UA group (132.3 ± 2.52). Despite these changes, all groups remained significantly lower in body weight compared with Control. By day 14, weights increased across all groups relative to their earlier measurements; however, the USD group (124 ± 1.00) showed no weight gain (Fig. 1).

Total SD sustains larger ulcer area and delays healing in rats with gastric ulcer

On day 3, both UA and USD groups showed a significant increase in ulcer area compared with the control. Ulceration in the USD group was significantly severe than in UA group. By day 7, no significant difference was observed between UA and USD groups; however, both exhibited significantly larger ulcer areas and reduced healing compared with control. Sleep deprivation for seven days led to marked changes in the gastric mucosa, with persistent ulcerations. By day 14, healing was evident, as both UA and USD groups showed significant reduction in ulcer area compared with days 3 and 7. Nevertheless, USD group maintained significantly larger ulcer areas and lower healing rates compared with UA group and control (Table 1).

Discussion

Adequate sleep is essential for maintaining overall physiological and psychological well-being. SD is a multifaceted condition that disrupts brain neurochemistry, alters behavior and cognition, while also weakening gastric mucosal defenses which can increase the risk of wound-related complications, serving as an indicator of impaired healing processes [30, 31].

Previous studies have shown that SD triggers catabolic responses resembling protein malnutrition, leading to weight loss [32, 33]. However, its impact during gastric ulcer healing has received limited attention. In this study, sleep loss beyond 3 days significantly reduced body weight in rats, with or without ulcers. SD activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA)-axis, elevating corticosterone and may disrupt appetite regulation contributing to weight loss [34, 35]. It can also impair hippocampal and prefrontal function, reducing memory, motivation, and emotional regulation, which may lower feeding behavior which warrants further investigation [36]. Gastric ulceration alone can cause stress-related weight loss via gastrointestinal dysfunction, pain-induced hypophagia and oxidative stress [37]. Combined with total SD, it appears to intensify HPA-axis hyperactivation, oxidative stress, and gut–brain dysregulation, amplifying weight loss in the ulcer+sleep-deprived group [38].

Gastric ulcer healing involves coordinated inflammation, cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and tissue remodeling [17]. SD, however, causes cellular damage and metabolic disruption, leading to replication errors and impaired repair [39]. It activates the HPA-axis and increases pro-inflammatory cytokines, worsening inflammation and oxidative stress at ulcer sites, thereby hindering healing [40]. This study shows that progressive SD (3–14 days) significantly delays gastric ulcer recovery by impairing mucosal restoration.

The OFT assessed anxiety-like behavior in sleep-deprived rats with gastric ulcers. Key measures included thigmotaxis, rearing, and locomotor activity [3]. Greater time at the arena periphery (thigmotaxis) indicates higher anxiety, while reduced rearing reflects diminished exploration and anxiety. Locomotor activity, measured by distance traveled, serves as an index of exploration and lower anxiety [23]. Behavioral assessment using the OFT revealed that 3 days of SD decreased time in the periphery while increasing time in the center and distance traveled. This behavioral profile aligns with Tartar et al. [41], who reported reduced fear-related behavior under short-term sleep disruption, suggesting a transient anxiolytic-like effect. Sleep-disturbed rats exhibited elevated plasma corticosterone levels, indicating activation of the HPA-axis. However, exercise controls also showed similarly elevated corticosterone without corresponding increases in exploration [41], suggesting that while HPA axis activation contributes to anxiety regulation, it is not the sole pathway. Additional mechanisms, such as circadian rhythm disruption and corticosterone dysregulation, possibly interact to shape the behavioral changes observed in the open field test. In contrast, sleep-deprived rats with gastric ulcers stayed longer in the periphery, less in the center, reduced locomotion, and had fewer rearing events across days 3–14. These changes suggest cumulative stress, fatigue, and delayed healing, consistent with evidence that prolonged SD and stress enhances anxiety-like behavior [42, 43]. Overall, anxiety induced by combined SD and ulceration evolves dynamically during healing phases [43].

Serum corticosterone is often linked to anxiety [44]. In this study, levels were elevated across all groups, showing gastric ulceration and SD as potent physiological stressors [44]. Glucocorticoids help maintain mucosal integrity, but prolonged exposure can impair repair and delay healing [45], while prolonged elevation is associated with anxiety [46]. Consistent with this dual role, corticosterone declined in ulcer alone group as healing progressed, but remained high in sleep-deprived rats, corresponding with larger ulcers, slower healing, and greater anxiety-like behavior. These findings suggest persistent HPA-axis activation under SD disrupts ulcer repair and promotes anxiety-like behavior.

SD is thought to affect brain regions through oxidative stress [47]. An imbalance between ROS and antioxidant defenses contributes to numerous pathological conditions [48], including gastric ulcers [8]. Evidence supports sleep’s antioxidant role and shows oxidative stress after both total and paradoxical SD in rodents [20, 47]. While behavioral effects such as anxiety, mood changes and cognitive impairment are well documented [49, 50], less is known about its impact on specific pathologies, particularly gastric ulcer healing, and the molecular mechanisms involved. SD–induced oxidative stress and inflammation are considered key drivers of pathologies linked to insufficient sleep [40, 51].

Lipid peroxidation compromises tissue barriers, promotes inflammation, and increases ROS, intensifying oxidative stress and tissue damage [48, 52]. Accordingly, MDA levels reliably indicate lipid peroxidation and oxidative stress [53]. Elevated brain MDA across all groups paralleled corticosterone increases, indicating that SD and gastric ulceration act as strong stressors. These activate the HPA-axis, raising corticosterone, ROS, and lipid peroxidation, reflected by increased MDA levels [44, 54]. Furthermore, biochemical analyses revealed declining MDA levels in ulcerated rats as healing improved. Conversely, SD in rats with ulcer sustained elevated MDA, enlarged ulcer areas and reduced healing rates, underscoring oxidative stress as a key factor in recovery. SD further impaired healing [8]. Prior studies showed elevated MDA and cortisol to behavioral disturbances [54–56]. Consistently, sleep-deprived ulcerated rats displayed persistent anxiety-like behavior throughout the experiment.

The brain is particularly vulnerable to oxidative damage due to limited antioxidant defenses, abundant lipids prone to peroxidation, and redox-active metals like iron and copper that catalyze free radical reactions. Its high oxygen demand further drives continuous ROS production [57]. Physiologically, tissues are protected by enzymatic antioxidants: SOD converts superoxide to hydrogen-peroxide and oxygen, while catalase and glutathione peroxidase detoxify hydrogen-peroxide [8].

In this study, antioxidant enzyme activity declined by day 3 in sleep-deprived rats, with or without ulcers, suggesting increased use of antioxidant defenses under stress. The sustained drop in SOD and GSH through day 14, especially in sleep-deprived ulcerated rats, coincided with persistent corticosterone and MDA elevations. This shift toward a pro-oxidant state [58] likely contributed to anxiety-like behavior and delayed ulcer healing observed [59, 60]. However, the increase in CAT activity through day 14 suggests a compensatory attempt to restore equilibrium and limit oxidative stress, reflecting an adaptive antioxidant response [8].

The clearest evidence of neural injury is neurodegeneration, marked by neuronal loss with functional and behavioral deficits [14, 61, 62]. SD beyond 3 days caused neuronal degeneration in rats, regardless of ulcers, with the ulcer+sleep-deprived group showing the greatest damage. Prolonged dual stressors overwhelm antioxidant defenses, sustaining oxidative stress and activating redox-sensitive pathways like IL-10, which drive neuroinflammation and anxiety-like behavior. Persistent inflammation may trigger autophagy and apoptosis, leading to neuronal loss [62], though these mechanisms need further study.

Conclusion

The present findings demonstrate that sleep deprivation significantly impairs gastric ulcer healing and exacerbates anxiety-like behavior in experimental rats. These effects are accompanied by sustained neuro-oxidative stress, disruption of endogenous antioxidant defense, and progressive neurodegenerative changes within the prefrontal cortex. Collectively, the results suggest that sleep deprivation compromises both neural and gastrointestinal functions during ulcer repair, thereby delaying gastric mucosal healing.

Limitations

The present study is not without limitations in such that the molecular mechanisms underlying the impairment of gastric ulcer healing by sleep deprivation were not investigated, and the gentle-handling technique, though widely used but not validated by electrophysiology, and might introduce additional stress that could independently influence corticosterone levels, oxidative status, and behavioural outcomes. In addition, anxiety-like behavior was assessed only using the open-field test, which may not fully capture the complexity of anxiety-related behaviors without complementary tests.
