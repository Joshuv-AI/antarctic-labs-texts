Clinical tools to differentiate behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia from phenocopies
Authors: Joshua Flavell, Soohyun Lee, Caitlin A T McElligott, Felicia M Coleman, Amir Fazlollahi, Emily G McCann, Duy D Nguyen, Peter J Nestor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONBehavioral variant frontotemporal dementia (bvFTD) is often misdiagnosed, particularly in psychiatric phenocopies of the disease (phFTD). We compared the top‐performing bvFTD‐specific behavioral tools, identified in a prior systematic review, for differentiating bvFTD from phFTD.METHODSIn this prospective diagnostic accuracy study, consecutively recruited participants with suspected bvFTD by a specialist physician completed nine bvFTD‐specific tools at baseline with a blinded assessor. Final diagnoses were assigned by multidisciplinary consensus after follow‐up. Diagnostic performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic analyses.RESULTSFifty‐one participants were included (25 bvFTD, 26 phFTD). The Frontotemporal Dementia versus Primary Psychiatric Disorder (FTDvsPPD) checklist showed the best discrimination (area under the curve [AUC] 0.92), with 80.0% sensitivity and 96.2% specificity. The Social Behavior Observer Checklist (SBOC) performed next best (AUC 0.85). The Informant Based Questionnaire and Pick's Disease Scale showed moderate discrimination and other tools performed poorly.DISCUSSIONTools focused on clinician observation outperformed informant‐reliant measures. The FTDvsPPD checklist showed the strongest rule‐in and rule‐out utility, while the SBOC provided useful rule‐in support.Keywords: diagnostic accuracy, differential diagnosis, Frontotemporal dementia, mental disorders, phenocopy

BACKGROUND

Standardized clinical tools are crucial to the assessment of behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia (bvFTD), particularly early in the disease when behavioral change can be subtle and diagnosis is most uncertain. 1 In a recent systematic review of clinical tools, many tools with promising but variable diagnostic performance were identified. 2 Unfortunately, there were two major limitations in the evidence base: (1) most studies validated their tools using comparators that do not reflect real‐world differential diagnosis, most commonly healthy controls or Alzheimer's disease (AD) rather than mixed psychiatric cohorts, and (2) no study conducted a head‐to‐head comparison of these tools. As a result, it remains unclear which instruments best support clinical decision making when bvFTD is suspected.

Diagnostic confusion is common in both directions for bvFTD; it is frequently misdiagnosed as a psychiatric disorder, and conversely, psychiatric disorders can be misdiagnosed as bvFTD. 1 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 The latter problem is particularly challenging in false‐positive patients, often referred to as psychiatric ‘phenocopies’ of bvFTD (phFTD), in which patients appear to manifest a behavioral profile suggestive of bvFTD but do not show evidence of progressive neurodegeneration on longitudinal follow‐up. 8

In the absence of disease‐specific biomarkers for bvFTD and its primary differential (psychiatric disorders), current recommendations emphasize the use of bvFTD‐specific clinical tools as part of the diagnostic assessment. 9 , 10 Although several such tools have been proposed, they have never been compared head to head in clinically realistic cohorts of patients referred with suspected bvFTD; therefore, it remains unclear which of these tools offers the greatest diagnostic utility in clinical practice. 2 , 9 Accordingly, this prospective diagnostic accuracy study compared the best‐performing bvFTD‐specific behavioral tools as identified in a previous systematic review, 2 in a consecutively recruited cohort of participants suspected to have bvFTD that either had the disease or a psychiatric disorder on follow‐up.

METHODS

Study design

This prospective diagnostic accuracy study recruited consecutively referred participants with suspected bvFTD by a specialist physician (neurologist, psychiatrist, or geriatrician). Recruitment occurred over a 4‐and‐a‐half‐year period, from January 2022 to May 2026. All participants underwent comprehensive baseline evaluation including clinical interview, cognitive testing, psychiatric assessment, and application of bvFTD‐specific clinical tools by a psychogeriatrician (J.F.).

Participants were followed up a minimum of every 12 months for up to 2.5 years, or until diagnostic consensus was reached by a cognitive neurologist (P.J.N.) and psychogeriatrician (J.F.), whichever occurred first. Final diagnoses were determined based on longitudinal outcome taking account of clinical features, neuropsychological assessments, neuroimaging results (including computerized tomography [CT], magnetic resonance imaging [MRI], and fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography [FDG PET]), and additional neurological investigations when clinically indicated, such as genetic testing and AD biomarkers.

Neuroimaging data were collected from participants’ medical records and the research setting. A research 3 Tesla MRI was conducted on all eligible participants (e.g., all those with no contraindication for an MRI), and a research FDG PET was conducted on all participants that had not previously had an FDG PET scan or their prior scan was equivocal (i.e., reported as having focal frontotemporal hypometabolism but on visual inspection by the authors the imaging was representative of artifact).

To improve further diagnostic certainty, the 0.8 mm isotropic resolution T1‐weighted MRI scan data was processed through a volumetric processing pipeline that has been shown to have superior precision for identifying cortical atrophy (full details of the acquisition and processing steps have been reported previously 11 ), including cortical thickness (Destrieux atlas 12 ). Subcortical volumes were analyzed by FreeSurfer ASEG. 13 Cortical thickness was analyzed in native units (mm), and subcortical volumes were normalized by estimated total intracranial volume (eTIV).

Participants with a family history of frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD) (e.g., bvFTD, motor neuron disease), defined as a Goldman score of ≤ 3, were screened for genetic causes of young‐onset dementia. 14

Participant eligibility

All participants were included in the study if they met three or more of the Rascovsky behavioral criteria for bvFTD. 15 Exclusion criteria included participants without a reliable informant and those who did not speak English. Participants with a documented psychiatric history were included only where the referring specialist considered the new‐onset behavioral disturbance to be more consistent with bvFTD than the pre‐existing psychiatric disorder.

Recruitment and setting

The study was conducted at the Queensland Brain Institute, The University of Queensland, Queensland, Australia between 2022 and 2026. Patients were recruited from two main sources: (1) The Mater Memory and Cognitive Disorders Clinic, a tertiary clinic focused on FTLD in Brisbane, Queensland and (2) multiple psychiatric services in Queensland, including both inpatient and outpatient psychogeriatric and generalist psychiatric settings.

Diagnostic classification

Participants were initially classified as possible, probable, or definite bvFTD, or had the diagnosis excluded, based on the Rascovsky criteria (the reference standard). 15 Those with possible and probable bvFTD at initial assessment, and no clear alternative diagnosis, were followed up longitudinally to confirm their status as either true bvFTD or a false‐positive psychiatric phenocopy of the disease (phFTD). Participants were excluded from the analysis if they had insufficient follow‐up to establish a diagnosis or if an alternative neurological disorder, such as AD, was identified on follow‐up to explain their symptoms. Psychiatric evaluations were conducted using the extended version of the Mini‐International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI), 16 supplemented by a psychiatric assessment to capture disorders not covered by the MINI. Participants who described a history consistent with a neurodevelopmental disorder underwent gold‐standard structured clinical interviews, behavioral questionnaires, and neuropsychological testing. 17 , 18 , 19 For autism spectrum disorder the assessment involved the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, Module 4 20 and Autism Diagnostic Interview‐Revised. 21 For attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) the assessment process involved the Diagnostic Interview for ADHD in Adults 22 and collateral history from a childhood informant and/or review of school reports. For intellectual disability the assessment process involved standardized intelligence testing and corroborated evidence of adaptive functioning impairment. 23 , 24 Participants were subcategorized into specific psychiatric diagnoses by a psychogeriatrician (J.F.) in accordance with the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM‐5) criteria. 18 Participants in the phFTD cohort with a family history of FTLD (i.e., Goldman score ≤ 3) were excluded from the analysis as an unidentified genetic cause of bvFTD could not be excluded.

Final cohort

The final cohort included a bvFTD group and a phFTD group. The bvFTD group comprised: (1) definite bvFTD, defined by a clinical phenotype consistent with bvFTD together with a known pathogenic gene mutation; (2) probable bvFTD, defined by the clinical phenotype of bvFTD accompanied by evidence of progressive focal frontal or temporal degeneration on serial neuroimaging; (3) bvFTD+, referring to participants who initially presented with bvFTD but subsequently developed another FTLD syndrome, reflecting the recognized clinical and pathological overlap whereby motor or language features may emerge over time. 3 , 25 The bvFTD+ cohort included progressive supranuclear palsy frontal variant (PSP‐f), 26 corticobasal syndrome frontal behavioral‐spatial variant (CBS‐fbs), 27 and semantic variant primary progressive aphasia (svPPA). 28 The one bvFTD+svPPA participant who was left handed also met criteria for semantic behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia (sbvFTD) 29 and the two that were right handed also met criteria for right temporal variant frontotemporal dementia (rtvFTD). 30 The phFTD group comprised participants who did not have evidence of FTLD and had a psychiatric disorder to explain their symptoms.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

1.Systematic review: The authors had previously conducted a systematic review of the literature to identify studies evaluating bedside clinical tools for distinguishing behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia (bvFTD) from psychiatric disorders and phenocopy presentations. This review identified that behavioral tools outperformed most cognitive tools. However, most tools had been validated in small samples and without direct comparison to other instruments in cohorts where bvFTD was not a genuine clinical suspicion.

2.Interpretation: In this prospective diagnostic accuracy study of individuals with suspected bvFTD, behavioral tools focused on clinician observation showed stronger diagnostic discrimination than informant‐based measures. The Frontotemporal Dementia versus Primary Psychiatric Disorder checklist demonstrated the best overall performance, while the Social Behavior Observer Checklist provided useful rule‐in utility.

3.Future directions: Larger multicenter validation studies are needed to confirm the diagnostic performance of these tools and assess their integration with neuroimaging and emerging biomarkers to improve early diagnosis of bvFTD.

Ensuring diagnostic accuracy

To ensure diagnostic accuracy in the sample, all participants were assessed for objective evidence of FTLD (or its absence) based on: (1) quantitative focal frontotemporal volume reduction on research MRI volumetric analysis, (2) qualitative review of serial structural neuroimaging ≥ 2 years apart for progressive focal frontotemporal atrophy by a cognitive neurologist (P.N.), (3) unequivocal focal frontotemporal hypometabolism on FDG PET, and (4) functional decline on clinical follow‐up. Particular attention was paid to probable bvFTD and phFTD classification: participants with probable bvFTD were required to demonstrate focal frontotemporal atrophy on either (1) or (2), whereas those with phFTD were required to show no evidence of atrophy on either measure. A summary of investigations and results are provided in the supporting information (Table S1).

Sample size

No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed, as this was a prospective diagnostic accuracy study embedded within an ongoing cohort of consecutively referred patients with suspected bvFTD. Sample size was therefore determined pragmatically by recruitment over the study period, and an evaluation of estimate precision was included in the data analysis.

BvFTD‐specific tools evaluated

Nine bvFTD‐specific behavioral tools identified by the previous review that showed the most promising diagnostic performance were evaluated. 2 One tool relied solely on behavioral observation by the clinician and no informant report: the Social Behavior Observer Checklist (SBOC). 31 The disorganization subscale was used for the SBOC. Three tools incorporated information obtained through an interview of the patient and an informant, clinical files, and behavioral observation: the Frontotemporal Dementia versus Primary Psychiatric Disorder (FTDvsPPD) checklist, 32 Middelheim Frontality Score (MFS), 33 and Pick's Disease Scale (PDS). 34 Five tools rely solely on informant report: the Disinhibition, Apathy, Perseverations, Hyperorality, Personal Neglect and Loss of Empathy tool (DAPHNE); 35 Edinburgh Cognitive and behavioral Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis Screen, Behavioral Component (ECAS‐B); 36 Frontal Behavioral Inventory (FBI); 37 Frontotemporal Behavioural Scale (FBS); 38 and Informant Based Questionnaire (IBQ; 39 Table 1).

Blinding of assessors

At the time of index test administration, members of the research team involved in data acquisition (J.F., S.L., F.C., E.M., A.F.) were blinded to the reference standard diagnosis and to all alternative diagnostic information, other than the fact that participants had been referred with suspected bvFTD by a specialist physician. Knowledge of the reference standard diagnosis was obtained only after follow‐up, when final diagnoses were established by multidisciplinary consensus. Index tool responses were collected at baseline but were not scored, processed, or reviewed by the multidisciplinary panel during the consensus diagnostic process. Final diagnosis of bvFTD was made strictly in accordance with the Rascovsky criteria. 15 Tool scores were generated only at the point of data analysis, after all consensus diagnoses had been finalized. Statistical analyses were performed with knowledge of diagnostic group.

Data collection

Demographic and clinical data included age, sex, symptom duration, and the Addenbrooke's Cognitive Examination‐III (ACE‐III). All index clinical tools were administered according to standard instructions by a psychogeriatrician (J.F.) based on a clinical assessment, informant interview, direct behavioral observation, and neurological examination (when appropriate). Follow‐up assessments were used only to establish diagnostic stability and were not used to inform baseline scores.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in accordance with Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (STARD) guidelines for diagnostic accuracy studies. 40 Statistical analyses were performed in R Statistical Software using the pROC package. 41 Analyses were performed on complete cases; no missing data were present for any of the tools or the reference standard diagnosis in the final sample.

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were summarized as mean and standard deviation and compared between groups using the independent‐samples t test, while non‐normally distributed variables were summarized as median and interquartile range and compared using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. Categorical variables were compared using a Fisher exact test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 (two‐sided) throughout.

Diagnostic performance of each clinical tool was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), with uncertainty quantified using stratified bootstrap resampling with 2000 replicates that preserved the original numbers of participants with bvFTD and phFTD within each resample. Bootstrap 95% confidence intervals were derived using the percentile method (2.5th and 97.5th quantiles of the bootstrap distribution). At the pre‐specified cut‐offs, sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values, positive and negative likelihood ratios, and balanced accuracy were calculated for each instrument and reported with 95% Clopper–Pearson exact binomial confidence intervals. Likelihood ratios were reported with log‐transformed 95% confidence intervals. Balanced accuracy, defined as the mean of sensitivity and specificity, was reported to provide a prevalence‐independent summary of diagnostic performance.

The cut‐off scores used for each test were as per the original thresholds proposed. 31 , 32 , 33 , 35 , 38 , 39 , 42 , 43 For the FBI, multiple cut‐off scores have been proposed; the threshold proposed to differentiate bvFTD from psychiatric disorders was used. 44 Calibrated cut‐off scores were derived for the four top‐performing tools by AUC (FTDvsPPD checklist, SBOC, IBQ, and PDS). Two prespecified thresholds were calculated for each: a rule‐out threshold targeting sensitivity ≥ 90%, and a rule‐in threshold targeting specificity ≥ 90%. When calibrated thresholds yielded fractional values, cut‐offs were rounded to the nearest integer, choosing whichever value was closest to the intended cut‐off target.

Given the specific purpose of the FTDvsPPD checklist in distinguishing bvFTD from psychiatric disorders, post hoc pairwise comparisons of the FTDvsPPD checklist AUC against each alternative instrument were conducted using paired DeLong tests, accounting for the correlated nature of ROC curves derived from the same participants. Family‐wise error was controlled using Holm correction for multiple comparisons.

Data availability

Anonymized data not published within this article will be made available on reasonable request to p.nestor@uq.edu.au after publication and for 5 years to qualified investigators with appropriate ethics approval.

RESULTS

Participants

In total, 61 participants were recruited. Ten participants were excluded from the final analyses: six for only meeting criteria for possible bvFTD due to insufficient follow‐up (median follow‐up 0.7 years, range 0.4–1.2) and four who had an alternative neurological diagnosis. No adverse events occurred related to performance of the index tests or the reference standard.

The final cohort comprised 51 participants, including n = 25 bvFTD and n = 26 phFTD. Within the bvFTD group, 5 participants had definite bvFTD (n = 3 C9orf72, n = 1 MAPT, and n = 1 GRN), 11 had probable bvFTD (mean follow‐up 1.7 ± 0.7 years), and 9 were classified as bvFTD+. The phFTD group included 6 participants with a single psychiatric diagnosis and 20 participants with multiple psychiatric diagnoses (median follow‐up 2.0 years, range 0.9–2.5). For participants with multiple psychiatric disorders, only a primary psychiatric diagnosis was assigned based on the disorder considered most explanatory of the presenting symptoms. Seven of the phFTD participants did not have a pre‐existing psychiatric diagnosis but all had a psychiatric disorder identified through the structured assessments. Of these participants, three were referred by a geriatrician, two by a neurologist, and two by a psychiatrist (Table 2).

Baseline characteristics

The bvFTD and phFTD groups were similar in age and sex. Symptom duration tended to be longer in the bvFTD group than in the phFTD group, although this difference did not reach statistical significance. In contrast, ACE‐III scores were significantly lower in the bvFTD group compared to the phFTD group (Table 3).

Descriptive statistics of the clinical tools

The raw scores on the tools were generally higher in the bvFTD group than in the phFTD group, but the magnitude of separation varied substantially between instruments. The FTDvsPPD checklist, SBOC, IBQ, and PDS showed significant group differences, whereas scores on the DAPHNE, ECAS‐B, FBI, FBS, and MFS overlapped substantially and did not differ significantly between groups. Floor effects were evident for the SBOC, IBQ, and PDS in the phFTD group, and the FBS was restricted at the upper end of its scoring range in both groups (Table 4).

Estimates of diagnostic accuracy

The FTDvsPPD checklist demonstrated the strongest overall discrimination between bvFTD and psychiatric phenocopies, with an AUC of 0.92 at the pre‐specified cut‐off of ≥ 11 (scoring range 0–17). At this threshold, sensitivity was 80.0% and specificity was 96.2%, yielding a high balanced accuracy of 88.1% and strong likelihood ratios indicative of good rule‐in performance.

The SBOC showed the next best overall performance with an AUC of 0.85. At a cut‐off of ≥ 2 (scoring range 0–9) its sensitivity was poor (60.0%) but specificity was high (96.2%), resulting in good positive predictive value (0.94) and a balanced accuracy of 78.1%.

The IBQ and PDS demonstrated reasonable discrimination with AUCs of 0.78 and 0.76. The IBQ's clinical utility was limited by an extreme imbalance between sensitivity and specificity at the pre‐defined cut‐off of ≥ 0 (scoring range –25 to 17). While sensitivity was 100% (all bvFTD cases screened positive), specificity was low (38.5%), resulting in limited rule‐in value despite a balanced accuracy of 69.2%. In contrast, the PDS showed a more balanced diagnostic profile at the cut‐off of ≥ 6 (scoring range 0–12), with sensitivity of 80.0% and specificity of 73.1%, yielding moderate predictive values and a balanced accuracy of 76.5%.

Across the remaining tools, diagnostic performance was generally poor, with several tools showing only marginal discrimination against chance (AUCs 0.57–0.64) and clinically unhelpful trade‐offs between sensitivity and specificity at their respective pre‐specified cut‐offs (Table 5).

Subgroup analysis restricted to the 16 participants with pure bvFTD (definite and probable bvFTD only, excluding the bvFTD+ cohort) yielded essentially the same pattern of findings. Comparison of the FTDvsPPD checklist versus other clinical tools

Paired ROC analyses using the DeLong test, with Holm adjustment for multiple comparisons demonstrated that the FTDvsPPD checklist showed significantly superior discriminative performance compared to most comparator tools. Specifically, the checklist demonstrated significantly higher AUCs than the DAPHNE, ECAS‐B, FBI, FBS, and MFS scales, with moderate to large differences in AUC (ΔAUC range 0.28–0.34; all Holm‐adjusted p ≤ 0.002), indicating significantly improved discrimination between bvFTD and psychiatric phenocopies.

In contrast, comparisons to the IBQ, PDS, and SBOC revealed smaller differences in AUC (ΔAUC range 0.07–0.15) that did not reach statistical significance after Holm adjustment (P = 0.093–0.325). Calibrated thresholds for leading clinical tools

The FTDvsPPD checklist supported both rule‐out and rule‐in use with a comparatively small indeterminate zone, consistent with its strong performance at the prespecified cut‐off. In contrast, the IBQ and PDS achieved the screening target but required wider indeterminate zones and exhibited limited confirmatory sensitivity at rule‐in thresholds (positive predictive value = 0.60–0.78). The SBOC did not achieve the prespecified rule‐out sensitivity target of ≥ 90%; the closest attainable rule‐out threshold yielded sensitivity of 88.0%, but with specificity of only 38.5%, whereas its rule‐in performance was similar to that of the FTDvsPPD checklist (positive predictive value = 0.89; Table 6).

Post hoc evaluation of sample size

The post hoc evaluation indicated that the final cohort size was sufficient to estimate diagnostic accuracy with adequate precision. For the FTDvsPPD checklist (AUC 0.92), the 95% confidence interval half‐width was ≈ ± 0.085. Confidence intervals were wider for lower performing instruments, reflecting greater uncertainty in their discrimination estimates. Overall, the cohort size supported the head‐to‐head comparison of the included tools.

DISCUSSION

In this prospective diagnostic accuracy study of patients referred with suspected bvFTD, the FTDvsPPD checklist achieved excellent 45 overall discrimination. It had high specificity and clinically meaningful likelihood ratios at its prespecified cut‐off score. The SBOC showed moderate overall discrimination but had relatively low sensitivity for detecting bvFTD at its prespecified threshold. This likely reflected differences in tool development and content, as the SBOC was validated against healthy controls and the disorganization subscale used in this study focuses on a restricted set of observable behaviors. For example. the SBOC does not incorporate other observable features of FTLD, such as neurological and language signs. In contrast, these features are captured in the FTDvsPPD checklist, possibly explaining the slightly better positive predictive value. The next best performing instruments, the IBQ and PDS, also demonstrated moderate discrimination. Notably, these tools, similar to the FTDvsPPD checklist, place substantial emphasis on patient insight, with insight‐related items comprising 14% of the IBQ and 11% of the PDS.

All remaining instruments showed weak to modest discriminative ability and clinically unhelpful trade‐offs between sensitivity and specificity, frequently misclassifying psychiatric disorders as bvFTD. The DAPHNE, ECAS‐B, FBI, FBS, and MFS rely predominantly on informant report of behavioral symptoms. Given that the bvFTD phenocopy is largely defined by the presence of bvFTD‐like behavioral features in the absence of neurodegeneration, instruments that rely almost exclusively on informant endorsement of such behaviors are, by definition, going to be limited in their ability to distinguish phFTD from bvFTD. More broadly, reliance solely on informant report, without systematic integration of other information sources, limits diagnostic specificity when informant accounts do not distinguish between phenotypic subgroups. None of the tools evaluated in this study provide explicit guidance on how clinicians should reconcile discrepancies between informant endorsement and directly observed behavior or other sources of information. In contrast, clinician‐rated instruments that emphasized directly observed behavior demonstrated superior overall performance, with the SBOC showing the closest performance to the FTDvsPPD checklist.

In the original validation study for the FTDvsPPD checklist, a cut‐off score of ≥ 11 showed high specificity (93.9%) but more modest sensitivity (71.1%). 32 In the present diagnostic accuracy study, the same cut‐off demonstrated comparable specificity (96.2%) with higher sensitivity (80.0%) and positive predictive value. The improved sensitivity in this study is likely attributable to design, as the original study relied largely on retrospective data, which may be subject to incomplete documentation and hindsight bias. 46 In contrast, prospective application at the first assessment required systematic, item‐by‐item evaluation of checklist features, reducing reliance on retrospective recall and increasing the likelihood that subtle early clinical features were identified and recorded. Differences in cohort composition and the use of standardized administration by a single assessor may have also influenced performance. Importantly, overall diagnostic accuracy was maintained when the FTDvsPPD checklist was applied prospectively to a consecutively recruited cohort with blinding to the final diagnosis, a setting in which test accuracy is typically reduced. 47

Unlike the FTDvsPPD checklist, the other tools were developed and validated using comparison groups that did not reflect the clinical context in which they are commonly applied. In routine practice, the key differential is between bvFTD and a heterogeneous mix of psychiatric disorders in patients presenting with late onset behavioral change. In many cases, validation samples contrasted bvFTD with healthy controls or with amnestic presentations of AD and other dementias without behavioural symptoms. The few tools that contrasted bvFTD with psychiatric disorders used only limited sampling, predominately mood disorders. This is likely to explain the discordance between the results of the initial validation studies for these tools, which generally showed excellent test performance, to the results of this study. 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 Together, these findings indicate that the preliminary test performance and thresholds for these tools may not generalize to diagnostically complex clinical populations, and as with other clinical tools, require recalibration when used to differentiate bvFTD from its psychiatric differentials. 48 This study showed that two‐threshold calibration supports the FTDvsPPD checklist as both a screening and confirmatory tool. Rule‐in performance closely matched the original validation study at the same cut‐off, indicating good external consistency in this prospective cohort. In contrast, the SBOC showed comparable confirmatory utility but could not achieve a high‐sensitivity rule‐out threshold, suggesting it is best positioned as a clinician‐observed rule‐in instrument when informant history is limited, rather than as a screening tool.

Before considering the broader implications of these results, it is important to clarify the definition of phenocopy bvFTD, which remains inconsistently applied in the literature. 8 , 49 , 50 , 51 Some authors reserve the term for individuals with no alternative diagnosis, 49 , 52 whereas others permit the presence or alternative explanation of a psychiatric disorder. 8 We adopted the latter approach because it reflects the common clinical scenario in which patients present with behavioral features consistent with bvFTD and are considered likely to have the disease by specialist clinicians, yet ultimately show no evidence of FTLD or another neurological disorder on follow up. Caution is warranted when interpreting cohorts reported to lack psychiatric diagnoses in this context, as the absence of a documented diagnosis does not exclude underlying psychiatric illness, particularly when structured psychiatric assessment by a psychiatrist has not been performed. In contrast, all participants in the present study underwent standardized specialist psychiatric evaluation, and notably, seven individuals in the phFTD cohort had no prior psychiatric history (including some referred by a psychiatrist) but were subsequently diagnosed with a psychiatric disorder. Although phFTD has also been proposed to include slowly progressive forms of bvFTD, particularly C9orf72‐associated disease, 53 , 54 such cases were excluded through family history, genetic testing, quantitative MRI analysis, and serial neuroimaging review. Ultimately, the clinical importance lies in avoiding the misdiagnosis of potentially treatable psychiatric conditions as neurodegenerative disease, and vice versa. 3 , 4 , 7

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The sample size was modest, and although bootstrap methods were used to quantify statistical uncertainty, confidence intervals for some instruments remain wide, although post hoc analysis showed that the sample size was sufficient. In addition, positive and negative predictive values are dependent on disease prevalence, and the estimates reported here reflect the specialist referral context of the present cohort, not generalist settings with substantially different proportions of bvFTD and phFTD. Final diagnoses were mostly based on expert multidisciplinary consensus with longitudinal follow‐up rather than histopathological confirmation, introducing potential reference standard misclassification, although this is mitigated by diagnostic stability over time and evidence of unequivocal focal frontal/temporal degeneration in all probable bvFTD cases. Although the assessor who administered the index tools (J.F.) also participated in the multidisciplinary consensus, the tool responses themselves were not scored or reviewed during the diagnostic process, and final diagnoses were made strictly against the Rascovsky criteria with longitudinal and biomarker support. The risk of incorporation bias was therefore minimized, although clinical impressions formed during tool administration cannot be entirely separated from the diagnostic process. All index tools were administered by a subspecialist with expertise in FTLD, which ensured consistency of administration but precluded assessment of inter‐rater reliability. Accordingly, generalizability may be restricted to specialist contexts and not applicable to primary care, given that all participants were referred with suspected bvFTD by a specialist and assessments were administered by a psychogeriatrician.

Despite these limitations, the results indicate that when differentiating bvFTD from psychiatric phenocopies of bvFTD, clinicians should prioritize tools that capture objective assessments of patient insight and indicators of FTLD rather than relying exclusively on informant‐reported behavioral change. The FTDvsPPD checklist is well suited to this role and is recommended as a bedside tool in patients with suspected bvFTD. Its reliance on informant and historical information is a limitation, however, because there would likely be instances when such data were unavailable or unreliable. In such cases, tools that rely on clinician observations such as the SBOC appeared to offer an acceptable substitute. The SBOC disorganized subscale, however, focuses primarily on a narrow set of observable behaviors such as disinhibition, impulsivity, stimulus‐bound behavior, perseveration, apathy, ecological executive dysfunction, and emotional blunting. It does not include the full cluster of observable features previously shown to differentiate bvFTD from psychiatric phenocopies, such as lack of insight, neurological signs, and language changes. 3

CONCLUSION

In a cohort of patients referred with suspected bvFTD, tools that focus on patient insight and capture objective, observable features of FTLD clearly outperformed informant‐reliant behavioral measures. The FTDvsPPD checklist and SBOC demonstrated the highest diagnostic accuracy and appear most promising for specialist clinical assessment. Other measures that heavily focused on patient insight also had good diagnostic accuracy. Tools that were not originally validated against mixed psychiatric cohorts generally performed worse in this cohort. Future studies should focus on improving clinician‐rated observational tools, assessments of insight, and integrating behavioral and other clinical markers into concise clinical tools to enhance diagnostic accuracy in this challenging clinical context.
