Ultrasound-assisted phosphorylation cantaloupe seed protein isolate-curcumin complexes prepared by pH shift treatment: Structural and functional properties
Authors: Jiankang Lu, Ruihua Zhang, Mingyan Ai, Chunlan Zhang, Shuting Li, Ruili Zhang, Weihua Wang, Zhiqiang Zhou, Yiming Jia, Zuoli Zhang, Shugang Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The efficient loading of proteins on bioactive substances had been a research hotspot in the fields of food science and industry in recent years. The complex of ultrasound-assisted phosphorylated cantaloupe seed protein isolate (UP-CSPI) and curcumin (CUR) was prepared by pH shift method to investigate the effects of different concentrations of CUR on the structure and functional properties of UP-CSPI. The results showed that with the increase of CUR concentration, the zeta potential, the antioxidant activity and emulsifying performance of the complex improved. The interaction between CUR and UP-CSPI was a spontaneous reaction driven by Gibbs free energy (ΔG<0). The enthalpy change (ΔH) and entropy change (ΔS)>0, indicating mainly hydrophobic interaction. Furthermore, CUR caused changes in the secondary structure of the UP-CSPI, β-turns increased from 38.33 % to 45 %–47 %, random coils decreased from 25.85 % to 13 %–14 %. In conclusion, the binding of CUR to proteins through pH shift had solved the problem of CUR instability and also provided new insights for the development and application of plant protein-based delivery systems.

Introduction

Turmeric rhizome contains curcumin (CUR), a polyphenolic substance renowned, which has the ability to inhibit tumor development, antioxidation and reduce inflammation. Furthermore, it demonstrates considerable potential for application in food science (Graef et al., 2023, Zhou et al., 2023). However, its aqueous solubility is extremely low (log P = 3.29), it is highly vulnerable to harsh environmental conditions, and its bioavailability is poor, facing substantial obstacles to widespread use in food products (Aditya et al., 2015). To overcome these challenges, it is critical to build bio-based polymer delivery systems, especially for proteins, and elucidate their underlying mechanisms.

Due to consumers' demand for healthier, more ethical and more sustainable food, the food industry is increasingly interested in using plant-based proteins. Cantaloupe seeds are by-products of Cucumis melo L. processing, usually discarded or underutilized, causing environmental pollution, economic losses and low resource efficiency (Qadeer et al., 2025). The raw material cost is low and it is easy to obtain. Cantaloupe seed protein isolate (CSPI) is rich in many essential amino acids and can be used as a source of vegetable protein. The chemical stability of curcumin can be enhanced by forming curcumin-protein complexes. Therefore, it is possible to create new bioactive functional components based on these complexes. On the other hand, the antioxidant capacity of proteins to protect phenols is receiving increasing attention.

Previous studies have reported that proteins can serve as an effective carrier for the delivery of active substances such as CUR. We previously modified cantaloupe seed protein isolate through ultrasound-assisted phosphorylation to obtain complexes (UP-CSPI) (Zhang et al., 2025). Due to the cavitation effect produced by ultrasound and the synergistic effect of phosphorylation, the protein structure unfolds,the hydrophobic group of CUR can be embedded into the hydrophobic region of the protein through "hydrophobic interaction", so we loaded CUR basedon UP-CSPI. Currently, researchers have also prepared protein-based CUR nanocomplexes using the pH shift method, enhancing the stability of CUR. For UP-CSPI, the mechanism of its binding to CUR through the pH shift method, as well as the specific impact of this binding process on the structural characteristics of the protein. At present, there are no literature reports, and there is a clear research gap.

Therefore, the purpose of this study is to investigate the conformational changes of UP-CSPI with different curcumin contents base on pH shift treatment. With the pH shift method, UP-CSPI was engineered to serve as a vehicle for the delivery of bioactive agents like CUR, which enhanced the possibility of the application of this method in industry (Halavach, 2024) and promoted environmental sustainability (Al-Muhe and Abdul-Wahid, 2024).

Materials and methods

Experimental materials

The cantaloupe seeds were purchased from Korla Prefecture, Xinjiang. Curcumin (AR), 1,1-Diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) and 2,2′-azinobis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid (ABTS+) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (China), Potassium bromide (Kbr, SP) was purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., LTD. All the other reagents used were of analytical grade.

Preparation of UP-CSPI-CUR

UP-CSPI were prepared using the same procedure re-ported in our previous study (Zhang et al., 2025). Added 1 % Sodium tripolyphosphate, ultrasonic power 400 W, ultrasonic time 20 min, throughout the process, the temperature of the ultrasonic bath was maintained at approximately 25 °C (Ren et al., 2024, Shang et al., 2023). Preparation of UP-CSPI solution(10 mg/mL), using 3 mol/L NaOH to adjust pH to 12.0, then magnetic stirring for 90 min. Added CUR to achieve 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 mg/mL concentration (Pan et al., 2014). Magnetically stir the mixture away from light for 1 h. Used 3 mol/L HCl to bring the pH of the mixture back to 7.0. Then solution was centrifuged (8000 r/min, 30 min) after continuing to magnetically stir for 2 h to remove the unbound CUR, the supernatant was the UP-CSPI-CUR complex.

Determination of load capacity (LC)and encapsulation rate (EE)

Referring to the research methodology of Chen et al. (2024), 4 mL of ethyl acetate was taken, and 0.2 mL of UP - CSPI - CUR was added to it. The resulting mixture was vortexed for 1 min. The UV–Vis spectrophotometer (UV-2450, Shimadzu, Japan) was utilized to measure absorbance at a wavelength of 420 nm. It was inserted into the standard curve formula (Y = 0.1489X + 0.0308, R2 = 0.9991).(1)LC(g100gprotein)=WAWB×100%(2)EE(%)=WAWC×100%where WA was the amount of CUR, WB was the mass of protein and WC represented the initial addition of CUR.

Determination of particle size distribution and zeta potential

The prepared sample (0.1 mg/mL) was equilibrated at room temperature for 10 min. The particle size distribution of the sample was determined using a Malvern particle size analyzer (ZEN3700, Malvern, Britain). 1.5 mL of the pre-treated sample solution was taken and placed in the sample cell. The measurement parameters were set as follows: temperature 25 °C, scattering Angle 90°, and equilibrium time 120 s.

Determination of Zeta Potential: 1 mL of the pre-treated sample solution was taken, injected into a dedicated folded capillary cell, and placed in the Zeta potential measurement slot. The parameters were set as follows: temperature 25 °C, equilibrium time 120 s. Each sample was measured three times. The average Zeta potential of the three measurements was taken as the final result.

Determination of the content of -SH and S-S groups

For the quantification of free sulfhydryl groups, 0.5 mL sample solution (3 mg/mL) was combined with 2.5 mL Tris-Gly buffer and 50 μL Ellman's reagent. To measure total sulfhydryl groups, 0.5 mL the sample solution (3 mg/mL) was treated with 2.5 mL Tris-gly-8 M Urea-0.5 % SDS buffer solution and 50 μL Ellman's reagent. This mixture underwent a reaction at 25 °C for 15 min. Subsequently, the absorbance of the sample was recorded at a wavelength of 412 nm (Ma et al., 2022).(3)R−SHcontent(μmol/g)=73.53×A412C(4)S−Scontent(μmol/g)=totalsulfhydryl‐freesulfhydryl2where A412 was absorbance; 73.53 = 106/(1.36 × 104), 1.36 × 104 was the molar extinction coefficient of Ellman's reagent; C was the concentration of the protein sample solution, mg/mL.

Determination of surface hydrophobicity (H0)

The H0 of the samples was determined by 1-aniline-8-sodium phthalate (ANS) fluorescence probe method (Han et al., 2023). The protein sample was dissolved in a phosphate buffer (10 mM, pH 7.2) at a final concentration of 0.05–0.25 mg/mL 4 mL protein solution was added into 30 μL 8 mM ANS solution and placed at 25 °C away from light for 15 min. The fluorescence intensity of the solution was measured using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (Lumina, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The excitation wavelength was set at 390 nm and the emission wavelength at 470 nm as the parameters. H0 was computed from the slope of the curve between the fluorescence intensity and the protein concentration.

FTIR and secondary structure analysis

1 mg of freeze-dried protein powder and 100 mg KBr, uniformly ground and pressed into transparent slices. FTIR (Frontier, PerkinElmer, USA) was used to scan 32 times in the range of 400–4000 cm−1 (Yan and Zhou, 2021). The area between 1700 and 1600 cm−1 was typically used to assess the changes in protein secondary structure. Baseline correction, second derivative, and curve fitting were performed using Peakfit 4.12 software to integrate the area of each fitted peak. The relative proportion was calculated as "peak area of a certain secondary structure/total peak area of amide I band × 100 %"

Fluorescence quenching analysis

UP-CSPI solution was mixed with equal volumes of CUR at different concentrations to yield a final UP-CSPI concentration of 0.125 mg/mL and CUR concentrations ranging from 0 to 60 μM. The mixtures were incubated in a water bath at 293K, 303K, and 313K for 20 min. Fluorescence spectra were determined at λex 280 nm and λem 300–500 nm. With binding parameters calculated from the 334 nm (Trp) intensity and fluorescence quenching parameters derived from the Stern-Volmer equation (Okagu et al., 2021).

Thermodynamic study determination

The thermodynamic parameters of the UP-CSPI-CUR complex were determined through fluorescence spectroscopy. The thermodynamic parameters, including enthalpy change (ΔH), entropy change (ΔS), and Gibbs free energy change (ΔG), were obtained at the corresponding temperatures of 293 K, 303 K, and 313 K (Liu et al., 2024).

Synchronous fluorescence measurement

The analysis of synchronous fluorescence spectra was measured, setting the wavelength scanning ranges at 260–340 nm (λ = 15 nm) and 220–360 nm (λ = 60 nm), with a voltage set at 700 V (Zhou et al., 2023).

Thermodynamic analysis

Differential scanning calorimeter was used for DSC determination (Setline DSC, French). Weighed 5 mg freeze-dried powder in a standard aluminum pot and heated it in the reaction chamber at a temperature of 30–180 °C, with a heating rate of 5 °C/min and a nitrogen rate of 50 mL/min.

Weighed 5 mg of freeze-dried powder in the crucible and measured it according to the following test conditions using TGA (Setline TGA, French): the initial temperature was 30 °C, the end temperature was 600 °C, the heating rate was 10 °C/min, and the whole experiment process was filled with nitrogen.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM)

Applied an appropriate amount of freeze-dried powder on the double-sided tape of the conductive plate and spray gold. The coating was observed by scanning electron microscopy (Apreo S, Thermofisher, USA) at an accelerated voltage of 3.0 kV (Wang et al., 2022).

Determination of CUR stability

1 mL of CUR-ethanol solution with a concentration of 10 mg/mL was taken and added to 9 mL of distilled water, then mixed evenly to obtain the free Cur solution. The freeze-dried powder of UP-CSPI-CUR was taken and the sample solution was prepared in the same way. The stability of CUR and UP-CSPI-CUR solutions at room temperature (25 °C) and 65 °C was characterized by the variation in absorbance values at 426 nm. The absorbance of the freshly prepared solution is designated as 100 %, and the ratio of the absorbance values at other times points to that of the freshly prepared solution indicating the retention rate of CUR.

Emulsifying activity (EAI) and emulsifying stability (ESI)

15 mL sample solution (1 mg/mL) and 5 mL of soybean oil were added. Then homogenized for 2 min at 12600 r/min in the homogenizer. Immediately after homogenization, 50 μL emulsion and 5 mL 0.1 % SDS solution were to vortex mix. The 0.1 % SDS solution was blank. Determined the absorption value (A0) at the ultraviolet absorption wavelength of 500 nm, and determined the absorption value (A30) by the same method after the protein emulsion was left for 30 min (Wang et al., 2025).(5)EAI(m2/g)=2×2.303×A0×N10000×C×φ(6)ESI(%)=A0A0‐A30×Twhere N represented the dilution ratio, C represented the protein concentration, and φ indicated the fraction of the oil phase: 0.25, T represented the time difference.

Determination of antioxidant activity

4 mL sample solution were combined with 4 mL 0.2 mM DPPH ethanol solution. The resulting mixture was incubated 30 min, subsequently, the absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 517 nm. 7 mM ABTS and 2.45 mM potassium persulfate were combined in equal volumes and kept in the dark for 12 h. At 734 nm, the mixture was diluted to an absorbance of 0.7 ± 0.002. 50 μL sample and 5 mL of the diluted ABTS solution. Incubate the mixture for 30 min and measure the absorbance at 734 nm (Wang et al., 2024).(7)Radicalscavengingactivity(%)=(A0−AS)/A0×100where AS was the absorbance of the CUR, UP-CSPI-CUR complex, A0 was the absorbance of the PBS control solution.

Molecular docking

Pumpkin seed globulin (PDB ID: 2evx) was downloaded from the PDB database to replace cantaloupe seed globulin for molecular docking (Dai et al., 2017). Water molecules and ions were removed using PyMOL (http://www.pymol.org/pymol), and the protein was retained for subsequent docking. The MMFF94 optimization of CUR molecules was carried out using Avogadro software.

Autodock Tools-1.5.6 was used to pretreat the protein and CUR molecules, and DoGSite3 (Graef et al., 2023) was used to predict the binding site of the protein. The central coordinates of the binding box for the protein docking were (13.9, −50.27, 30.54). The number of upward grid points of XYZ parties was 60 Å × 60 Å × 60 Å, the spacing of grid points was set to 0.375, and the docking number was set to 20. The docking algorithm was Lamarck genetic algorithm, and Autodock 4.2.6 (Morris et al., 2009) was used for molecular docking.

Data processing

Calculated the mean and standard deviation of each sample three times. Duncan's significance difference analysis was conducted using SPSS 21.0 software. A significance test standard of P < 0.05 was adopted for the evaluation.

Results and discussion

Load capacity and encapsulation rate analysis

Fig. 1A showed the visual appearance of UP-CSPI-CUR complexes. As the CUR concentration increased, the color deepened continuously. When the CUR addition amount was 2.0 mg/mL, the turbidity was the highest, indicating that larger aggregates were formed in the solution at this time. Fig. 1B showed the encapsulation rate and load of the UP-CSPI-CUR complex with different CUR concentrations. When the CUR content was 0.5 mg/mL, the encapsulation efficiency reached 90.79 %, which was distinctly higher compared to other content levels (P < 0.05). With the gradual increase of the CUR content, its load showed a significant rising trend (P < 0.05), reaching 11.67 g/100g protein, which had the same trend as the encapsulation rate and load studied by (Dai et al., 2018). When the CUR content increased, it would exceed the loading capacity of proteins, and could not fully bind to the hydrophobic sites, causing the encapsulation efficiency to diminish.Fig. 1Morphology (A), Load capacity and Encapsulation rate (B) of UP-CSPI-CUR complex.Fig. 1

Morphology (A), Load capacity and Encapsulation rate (B) of UP-CSPI-CUR complex.

Analysis of particle size distribution

From Fig. 2A, with the increment of CUR concentration, the particle size of UP-CSPI grew significantly (P < 0.05) (Shang et al., 2023). Among them, the particle size when the CUR concentration was 2.0 mg/mL was significantly larger than that at other concentrations. This was because when a large amount of CUR was added, the loading capacity of UP-CSPI was insufficient, and some CUR was not completely encapsulated, resulting in the self-aggregation of unbound or loosely bound CUR molecules, which then adsorbed onto the complex surface and formed larger aggregates. This was consistent with the experimental results of the encapsulation rate.Fig. 2Particle size distribution (A) and Zeta potential (B) of UP-CSPI-CUR complexes.Fig. 2

Particle size distribution (A) and Zeta potential (B) of UP-CSPI-CUR complexes.

Analysis of zeta potential

In Fig. 2B, after adding CUR, the absolute potential value of the UP-CSPI complex showed an increasing trend (P < 0.05), which was consistent with the research results of (Ren et al., 2024), the absolute value of Zeta potential increased, the phenolic hydroxyl groups and other groups contained in CUR would partially dissociate during the pH shift process, causing CUR molecules to carry a certain amount of negative charge. Under alkaline conditions, the hydrophobic groups of proteins were exposed. When proteins and CUR were combined through hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonds, the negative charge introduced by CUR would directly change the surface charge composition of the complex and increase the absolute value of the zeta potential (Li et al., 2023), indicating that the binding between UP-CSPI and CUR was closer. However, with the further increase of CUR concentration, the absolute value of the potential of the UP-CSPI complex did not change significantly (P > 0.05), which may be due to the supersaturated load of UP-CSPI on CUR, there was a reduction in the strength of intermolecular interactions (Liu et al., 2024). However, compared with the particle size analysis (Fig. 2A), it showed an increase in aggregation. This might have been because the hydrophobic interaction and hydrogen bonds formed between UP-CSPI and CUR were much greater than electrostatic repulsion, which led to an increase in absolute potential and particle size (Dai et al., 2022).

Content analysis of sulfhydryl group and disulfide bond

It is acknowledged that the sulfhydryl (-SH) group represents a vital functional component within proteins, which impacts protein's structural and functional properties (Liu et al., 2024). In Fig. 3A, the sulfhydryl group content decreased significantly with the increase of CUR concentration. The reduction in the content of free -SH groups was more likely due to protein aggregation, during the aggregation process, the -SH groups originally exposed on the molecular surface were buried inside the aggregate (Sui et al., 2018). CUR bound to proteins, the secondary structure (such as an increase in the proportion of β-turns) underwent unfolding, causing the disulfide bonds originally hidden within the molecule to be more easily broken by the high pH environment due to conformational expansion. Therefore, the number of disulfide bonds had decreased.Fig. 3Content of sulfhydryl group and disulfide bond (A) and H0 (B) of UP-CSPI-CUR complexes.Fig. 3

Content of sulfhydryl group and disulfide bond (A) and H0 (B) of UP-CSPI-CUR complexes.

Surface hydrophobicity analysis

As can be seen from Fig. 3B, after adding CUR, its hydrophobicity was significantly reduced, which may be due to the fact that the CUR molecular structure contains polar groups such as hydroxyl. When the CUR interacted with proteins, the hydroxyl groups it introduced can increase the hydrophilic environment around the protein, for example, forming hydrogen bonds with water molecules. The hydrophobic region on the protein surface was often one of the factors that led to protein aggregation and other phenomena (Liu et al., 2024). The increase of the hydrophilic environment made the water molecular layer around protein molecules more stable, and a weakening of the hydrophobic interaction among protein molecules occurred, thus reducing the hydrophobicity of proteins to a certain extent and making proteins more stable and dispersed in solution.

FTIR analysis

From Fig. 4A, the principal absorption bands of UP-CSPI consisted of the amide I band within the 1700-1600 cm−1 range and the amide II band spanning 1600-1500 cm−1. The amide I band primarily originated from the C=O stretching vibration of peptide bonds. Meanwhile, the amide II band encompassed both N-H bending vibration and C-N stretching vibration (Cui et al., 2023). Under the action of CUR, the peak values of the amide I band and the amide II band showed a redshift phenomenon, indicating that the CUR affected the protein structure and chemical microenvironment (Zhang et al., 2021).Fig. 4FTIR (A) and secondary structure content (B) of UP-CSPI-CUR complex.Fig. 4

FTIR (A) and secondary structure content (B) of UP-CSPI-CUR complex.

The secondary structure content of the protein was calculated by amide I band (1600-1700 cm−1) in Fig. 4B. The content of random coils structures decreased significantly from 25.85 % to 13 %–14 %, while at the same time, the contents of other structures all increased significantly. Among them, the content of β -turns structure increased particularly significantly, rising from 38.33 % to 45 %–47 %. This might be due to the hydrogen bond interaction and hydrophobic interaction between CUR and UP-CSPI, which led to the rearrangement of the secondary structure of the complex (Ren et al., 2024).

With the increase of CUR concentration, it's UP-CSPI fluorescence intensity decreased significantly, indicating that CUR quenched UP-CSPI fluorescence intensity in a concentration-dependent manner. Thermal stability analysis

The DSC denaturation temperature of protein was shown in Fig. 7A. CUR could bind to proteins through hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions or electrostatic interactions, the denaturation temperatures of the complexes were all higher than those of the 0 mg/mL CUR group (125 °C), which indicated that the binding of CUR to proteins enhanced the stability of the protein structure (Wang et al., 2024).Fig. 7DSC (A) and TG (B) of UP-CSPI-CUR complex.Fig. 7

DSC (A) and TG (B) of UP-CSPI-CUR complex.

Thermogravimetric (TG) analysis was employed to evaluate the thermal stability of proteins by detecting the change of heat flow signal during the temperature rise (Tarahi et al., 2024). The TG curve of the complex was shown in Fig. 7B. Both UP-CSPI-CUR complexes experienced two instances of weight loss. Among these, the first stage was probably caused by the evaporation of moisture and residual solvents in the system and the second stage was caused by protein decomposition. Consistent with the DSC curve, the UP-CSPI-CUR complex had higher thermal stability.

SEM analysis

The micromorphology of the UP-CSPI-CUR complex was shown in Fig. 8. The samples that had not been treated with CUR showed an irregular lamellar structure, a sizeable lamellar structure, and a rough surface. After CUR was embedded, the lamellar structure decreased, and the CUR particles were distributed on the structure, and an irregular porous structure began to appear. This might have been because after CUR occupied the hydrophobic points on the protein surface, the H0 of the protein surface decreased, resulting in a weakened ability of protein molecules to directly aggregate through hydrophobic interaction.Fig. 8SEM of UP-CSPI-CUR complex.Fig. 8

Stability analysis

Extensive research had indicated that the active methene and β-diketone constituents of CUR had been responsible for its suboptimal bioavailability, stability, and solubility (Song and McClements, 2021), and the degradation rate of free CUR aqueous solution can reach 70 % after 30 min at room temperature. This greatly limits the development and utilization of CUR. In Fig. 9A and B, the retention rates of CUR and UP-CSPI-CUR (0.5 mg/mL, highest encapsulation rate) at room temperature (25 °C) and high temperature (65 °C) were studied. The advantages of 65 °C over higher temperatures (such as 100 °C) lie in: avoiding severe protein denaturation (such as aggregation and precipitation), ensuring the authenticity of interactions, reducing the thermal degradation loss of polyphenols, and maintaining the chemical equilibrium of the system. At room temperature, the CUR retention rate decreased, and high temperature (65 °C) accelerated the degradation of CUR. However, after combining with UP-CSPI, the CUR retention rate increased, indicating that the protein had a certain protective effect on the CUR.Fig. 9The stability of the CUR and UP-CSPI-CUR complex at room temperature (25 °C) (A), 65 °C (B).Fig. 9

The stability of the CUR and UP-CSPI-CUR complex at room temperature (25 °C) (A), 65 °C (B).

EAI and ESI analysis

Modification with polyphenols can enhance the emulsification capacity of plant proteins through the augmentation of their water solubility and the modification of surface characteristics (Djuardi et al., 2020). The practical implementation of proteins depends vitally on the Emulsifying Activity Index (EAI) and Emulsifying Stability Index (ESI) (Xu et al., 2023). The EAI and ESI of UP-CSPI and UP-CSPI-CUR composites were shown in Fig. 10. The results showed that the addition of CUR significantly increased EAI and ESI (P < 0.05), which was consistent with (Sui et al., 2018) who found that soybean protein isolate-anthocyanin non-covalent and covalent complexes had higher EAI and ESI than SPI. The enhancement of protein emulsification performance was caused by the addition of CUR inducing changes in protein conformation through hydrophobic interactions, aromatic amino acid residues entering a more hydrophobic microenvironment, resulting in higher affinity of proteins for the oil-water interface (Nor Afizah and Rizvi, 2014). With the further increase of CUR addition, the emulsification and emulsification stability of CUR did not change significantly, which may be because the adsorption capacity of CUR at the oil-water interface had a certain limit.Fig. 10EAI and ESI of UP-CSPI-CUR complex.Fig. 10

EAI and ESI of UP-CSPI-CUR complex.

Antioxidant activity analysis

The antioxidant ability represents a significant property among those of the protein-polyphenol complex (Wang et al., 2024). In Fig. 11A and B, As the concentration increased, both CUR and UP-CSPI-CUR exhibited a growing scavenging capacity. At identical concentrations, UP-CSPI-CUR consistently demonstrated a higher free-radical scavenging efficiency than CUR. This disparity might be attributed to CUR's low aqueous solubility, which causes it to aggregate readily. Moreover, the large size of CUR aggregates restricts its interaction with free radicals (Zhong et al., 2023), so its free radical scavenging rate was low. The use of UP-CSPI encapsulation improves the physicochemical stability of the CUR, which may allow the CUR to interact more effectively with free radicals.Fig. 11DPPH (A) and ABTS+ (B) free radical scavenging activity of CUR, UP-CSPI-CUR complex.Fig. 11

Conclusion

In this study, the UP-CSPI-CUR complex was prepared by the pH shift method. Firstly, the pH shift method was utilized to increase the exposure of the charged side chains of protein molecules, effectively encapsulating CUR through hydrophobic interactions. The encapsulation effect of UP-CSPI enhanced the stability of CUR at different temperatures and significantly improved its scavenging ability for DPPH and ABTS free radicals. Furthermore, the thermal stability and emulsification performance of the complex have been enhanced. These findings reveal the potential of UP-CSPI as a CUR transfer carrier, providing experimental evidence and theoretical support for the development of low-cost and highly stable plant protein-based polyphenol transfer systems. However, this study lacks in vitro experiments and reports on its allergen components and sensitization thresholds. Further work is needed to transform it from "laboratory preparation" to "industrial-scale production".
