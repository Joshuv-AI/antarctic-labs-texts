The crustal juvenile and reworking in eastern Tibet due to tectonism and magmatism
Authors: Tianyu Zheng, Yumei He
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCrustal thickening in Tibet results from the collision of India and Eurasia. The fine structure of the crust is vital information to detect the tectonic genesis of crustal growth and reworking. Here, we constructed the crustal structure of eastern Tibet and its neighboring areas using depth-domain receiver function imaging. The imaging results reveal widespread low-velocity layers in the mid-crust, alternating high- and low-velocity structures in the lower crust, and regional variations in the velocity at the bottom of the lower crust and the thickness of the entire crust. We propose that the lower crust is juvenile crust, formed by multiphase magmatism and tectonic activity during continental collision. The regional variations in crustal structure can be attributed to differences in the tectonic position of subduction-collision system and the distinct crustal-derived materials that contribute to the formation of the juvenile crust. The low-velocity layers in the mid-crust are more likely to be the product of arc magmatism during the assembly of micro-continents. Based on the constraints of the unique crustal structure, we suggest that tectonic compression and magmatism caused the juvenile and reworking of the Tibetan crust.Subject terms: Solid Earth sciences, Seismology, Tectonics

Introduction

The Tibetan Plateau is a product of the Cenozoic collision of the Eurasian and Indian continents. It is the largest continental collision zone in the world, with the highest terrain and the thickest crust. Since the collision of the Indian and Eurasian continents, the crust has been thickening and the surface has been uplifting. The thickened crust formed during the collision provides information on the unique tectonic genesis of the Tibetan Plateau. The ongoing evolutionary process has preserved fresh records in the crust, providing an opportunity to study complex processes.

Based on the studies of the properties and genesis of exposed lower and middle crustal rocks, geologic features, and deep structures, various models of crustal evolution on the Tibetan Plateau have been proposed, including thickening of the crust by compression1,2, thrusting of the underlying crust3, lithospheric eastward extrusion4,5, and crustal channel flow6,7. Tectonic and magmatic processes experienced by the crust are used to study thickening mechanisms in Tibet 8–10. Previous seismological studies have revealed structure patterns of subduction of the Indian plate11–14, crustal thickening15, significant low-velocity anomaly in the mid-crust16,17, and underthrusting of the Indian lithosphere18. The results provide a general understanding of the crustal structure in Tibet, but it is difficult to identify the formation process and mechanism of crustal thickening.

This study uses depth-domain receiver function imaging19 to construct the fine internal structure of the crust in the eastern part of the Tibetan Plateau. We explore the role of tectonic and magmatic activity in shaping the thickened crust of the Tibetan Plateau from the perspective of crustal structural features and patterns of regional variability.

Geological setting

Unlike most collisional orogenic belts, the Tibetan Plateau underwent the Tethys evolutionary process of drifting of rifted continental blocks, closure of micro-oceanic basins and gradual assembly of micro-continental blocks prior to its formation. At the convergence of the Eurasian and Indian continents, the Songpan-Ganzi, Qiangtang and Lhasa terranes constitute the main plateau. The Jinsha River suture zone, the Bangong Nujiang suture zone and the Yarlung Zangbo River suture zone are the corresponding amalgamation boundaries20. Our study area includes the eastern part of these blocks (Fig. 1).

By the Early Triassic, the Qiangtang terrane had already separated from the Gondwana continent and drifted northwards. In the Late Triassic, the Qiangtang terrane collided with the southern margin of the Eurasian continent along the Jinsha River suture zone. The Lhasa terrane had already split from the northern margin of Gondwana in the Early Permian, drifted northward, and collided with the Qiangtang terrane in the Early Cretaceous, leading to the closure of the Bannu Ocean. The closure of the Yarlung Zangbo Ocean and the collision of the Indian and Asian continents occurred around 65–50 Ma. The continental collision events produced syn-collisional magmatic rocks widely distributed in the Lhasa terrane. Since the Mesozoic, magmatism in the Lhasa terrane exhibits two peaks in intensity, 113 Ma and 52 Ma, respectively21–23.

The Songpan-Ganzi terrane was formed during the Late Triassic orogeny of the converging North China, Tarim, Yangtze Craton, and Qiangtang block. The Songpan-Ganzi terrane is characterized by the development and preservation of a flysch basin and limited outcrops of Precambrian basement and Paleozoic strata in the eastern region. After uniform thickening of the sedimentary system, there was no Mesozoic exhumation or significant magmatic activity. It is suggested that the Songpan-Ganzi terrane did not undergo a complete continent–continent collision. This terrane may be a back-arc basin formed by the subduction of the Paleo-Tethys Ocean, or it may be a tectonic accretionary wedge with the remnant Paleo-Tethys oceanic crust at its base24,25.

Since the collision of the Lhasa block with the Indian plate, the Tibetan plateau has undergone severe crustal shortening. With the Eastern Himalayan Syntaxis as its axis, eastern Tibet rotates clockwise by about 90°26.

Seismic imaging of crustal structure

We use depth-domain receiver function (DRF) imaging to construct the S-wave velocity models of the crust, utilizing data from 133 permanent and temporary seismic stations in eastern Tibet and adjacent areas (Fig. 1). The DRF imaging is based on the interface depth and velocity variation information carried by the Ps-converted seismic wave phase to construct a crustal S-wave velocity model. Positive values of DRF indicate a downward increase in velocity and vice versa (Fig. S2). The imaging results revealed unique crustal structural characteristics and their regional variations. The imaging models and DRF waveform comparisons of representative stations in different areas are shown in Fig. 2. The crustal S-wave velocity models and DRF waveform comparisons for all stations can be found in the supporting materials (Figs. S3-S12). Based on the differences in crustal thickness (Fig. 3a) and velocity structure (Fig. 2), we divided the study area into five sub-regions from west to southeast: A, B, C, D, and E (Fig. 1). The eastern parts of the Lhasa and Qiangtang blocks are located in Zone A. The Songpan-Ganzi block in the northeast of the plateau is located in Zone C, connected to the Eastern Himalayan Syntaxis through a narrow zone to the south. Zone B is located in the southeastern part of the plateau. Zone D is the western part of the Yangtze craton, adjacent to Tibet. Zone E is located south of 25ºN in Yunnan Province, adjacent to southeastern Tibet. As shown in Fig. 2, the crust of Zones A, B, and C can be divided into upper and lower parts based on the distinct low-velocity layer at depths of 30–45 km. We refer to the bottom layer of upper crust and the bottom layer of lower crust as UCB and LCB, respectively. The crustal thickness (Fig. 3a) and the S-wave velocity of LCB (Fig. 4a) clearly show the regional difference in crustal structure.

Zones A, B and C are the main areas of crustal thickening on the eastern Tibetan Plateau.

Discussion

We have used seismic imaging to study the crustal structure of eastern Tibet and have identified unique structural features of the plateau crust described above. We discuss the genesis of these unique structures in conjunction with the results of geological studies of tectonic and magmatic activity on the Tibetan plateau.

Formation of the UCB

The Tibetan Plateau has undergone a series of tectonic processes, including micro-continental assembly, oceanic plate subduction, and continental collision, with crustal thickening of up to 20–40 km. These processes may have been accompanied by arc magmatism, subduction zone magmatism, and slab-fracture-magmatism. Our seismic imaging revealed the presence of a low-velocity layer UCB, at depths of 30–45 km and some high- and low-velocity alternating layers in the lower crust at depths of 40–80 km. The decrease in crustal viscosity due to radioactive heating or water addition is difficult to correlate with the structure of spaced multiple low-velocity layers.

From 110 to 100 Ma, large-scale magma eruptions occurred in the central and northern parts of the Gangdise Belt, which were products of the closure of the Bangong-Nujiang Ocean and the collision of the Qiangtang and Lhasa terranes. During the closure of micro-oceanic basins and the assembly of micro-continental blocks, felsic crustal material can form the lower part of the crust by relamination27,28. Changes in crustal components can also result from the addition of mantle-derived magamas29. The low velocity of UCB is more likely to be a consequence of arc magmatism. The depth of the UCB is 30–45 km, comparable to the depth of the paleo-crust30. Therefore, we consider the part above the base of the UCB to be reworked paleo-crust, and the crust beneath the UCB was a juvenile crust formed during the India-Eurasia collision at approximately 65–50 Ma, and its high- and low-velocity alternating structures are responses to co-collision and post-collision magmatism between the Eurasian and Indian continents.

Alternating high- and low-velocity structure in juvenile crust

The material and heat sources of forming juvenile crust can come from subduction zone magmas and plate rupture magmas formed by subduction of the Indian plate. In addition to material from the mantle, crust derived material is needed in the mantle to form the andesitic composition of continental crust, as well as isotopic and trace element information on crustal rocks31–33. Detachment and breakup of oceanic plates are sources of oceanic crustal material. During continental collision, detachment of the underplating crust, orogenic collopse34, and exhumation of deep subducting continental crust35 can lead to partial melting and metamorphism of the crust derived material in the mantle. Solutions of crustal derived material react with mantle material to produce crustal constituents, which then differentiate and crystallize to form new continental crust.

The above processes can be realized in the magmatic and tectonic activities occurring in Tibet, thus providing material and heat sources for the formation of juvenile crust. The Lhasa Block is the core of the collisional orogenic belt on the Tibetan Plateau. The closure of the Yarlung Zangbo Ocean and the collision event between the Indian and Asian continents resulted in the widespread distribution of syn-collisional magmatic rocks in the southern part of Gangdise21–23. There are Linzizong volcanic series (ca. 65–40 Ma) and Gangdise granite series (ca. 49–51 Ma). After collision, magmas (ca. 26–10 Ma) appeared mainly in the southern part of the plateau and extended northwards to Songpan-Ganzi terrane after 13 Ma. Multiphase magmatism is a response to multiphase tectonic activities, suggesting that crustal formation and transformation occurred intermittently as multiple processes, resulting in a multilayered, superimposed juvenile crust. The imprints left in the Ordos crust reveal that interlocking high- and low-velocity crustal structures contributed to the long-term stability of the craton28. The interlayering of high- and low-velocity crustal structures in the juvenile crust of Tibet may have contributed to the preservation of previous formed low-velocity layers in the mid-crust.

Under strong continental collision, the newly formed crust is thickened by strong thrusting, which may have led to the burial of the upper part of the juvenile crust, thus changing the order of stratigraphic accumulation and leading to spatial variations in crustal components18. This may also be a factor in the formation of alternating high- and low-velocity structures. The imaging results show that the LCB velocity in the central part of Zone A is lower than those on either side (Fig. 4a). This can be explained by the mantle wedge magmatism between the Indian and Tibetan subduction lithosphere beneath the Lhasa block 35.

Structure difference in Zone C

The juvenile crust in Zone C is about 7 km thinner than in Zones A and B, and the LCB layer has a relatively low velocity of 3.0 to 3.4 km/s. These features suggest that there are differences in tectonic and magmatic processes between Zone C and Zones A and B. Special tectonic and geodynamic environment during the evolution of Tethys prevented the complete collision of the Songpan-Ganzi terrane with main continental blocks23. Thus, most of the UCB in Zone C was formed under a thinner paleo-crust at depths of 25–35 km. In addition, the Songpan-Ganzi terrane is a remnant flysch basin, which together with the thick sedimentary layer of Assam Basin provides sedimentary dominated derived materials, resulting in lower LCB velocities in Zone C.

Tomographic imaging results indicate that the upper mantle in the eastern Songpan-Ganzi region is dominated by a low-velocity anomaly36–38, suggesting that Zone C is influenced by mantle magmatism caused by subduction of the northeast corner of the Indian plate to the north and east. Subduction of the northeast corner of the Indian plate occurred after the collision of the Lhasa block with the Indian continent39. The southwestern extremity of Zone C has distinct tectonic evolution. By combining anomaly positions derived from seismic tomography with paleogeographic reconstructions of India, Replumaz et al.5 proposed two successive episodes of southeastward extrusion followed by subduction around the Eastern Himalayan Syntaxis. However, it is difficult to detect a crustal structure related to this distinct tectonic evolution from the station CHY alone (Fig. S1). We expected further dense seismic observations to image the fine crustal structure of the Eastern Himalayan Syntaxis, providing significant information on the unique tectonic genesis.

The thickening mechanism of the extremely thick crust of Tibet is a scientific issue of great interest. Our seismic imaging results reveal the complex internal structure of the crust in the eastern Tibetan Plateau. Based on the tectonism and magmatism of the Neo-Tethys evolution and the India-Eurasia subduction-collision, we have explained the alternating high- and low-velocity structure of the juvenile crust and the genesis of its regional variations. We conclude that Tibetan crustal thickening was caused by both tectonic compression and magmatism.

Methods

Depth-domain receiver function imaging

We used DRF imaging to construct a crustal S-velocity model of the eastern Tibetan Plateau. The velocity model is obtained based on the best fit of the theoretical DRF and the observed DRF in DRF imaging19.

The main imaging steps are as follows:

(1) Construct the receiver function by time-domain maximum entropy deconvolution of the vertical components of teleseismic records from the radial component42,43.

(2) Time-to-depth conversion is used to convert the time-domain waveforms of the receiver functions at each station into amplitude-depth distributions, which are then stacked at the common conversion point to generate DRF. In the time-to-depth conversion, acceptable models obtained from previous search are used as conversion models for the current search.

(3) The theoretical DRF is calculated using the model parameters obtained from the best fit. The fit between the theoretical DRF and the observed DRF is taken as the objective function, and the simulated quenched simplex global inversion method44,45 is used to search for the optimal solutions of the model parameters. The layer-stripping inversion is performed from shallow to deep to reduce multiple wave interference.

Seismic data

The seismic data used in this study were recorded from 133 stations, including 90 permanent stations of the China National Seismic Network (CNSN) for the period 2020–2024 and 43 temporary stations of the Namche-Barwa project, which was operated from June 2003 to October 2004 by Lehigh University. These seismic stations provide a valuable dataset covering eastern Tibet and neighboring areas (Fig. 1). Three-component seismograms recorded from teleseismic events of magnitudes ≥ 6.0 with epicentral distances of 70º- 90° and narrow back azimuths of 107º- 122° were converted into radial receiver functions. By applying a zero-phase Butterworth filter to the computed receiver function, the frequency range of the receiver function is 0.05 to 4 Hz.

Reliability of the velocity model

Receiver function imaging uses the converted wave signals from seismic discontinuities to obtain information on the interface depth and the velocity contrasts across the discontinuities and to construct a model of the stratified structure of the crust. In the wave signals, shallow multiple waves (PpPs, PsPs, PpSs, etc.) overlap with Ps waves at the interface to be imaged, which poses a challenge for identifying the true interface and imaging complex structures within the crust. In DRF inversion19, each search step uses the acceptable model obtained in the previous step for the time-to-depth conversion of the current step, thus overcoming the bias caused by using only the initial model for time-to-depth conversion. The layer-stripping inversion uses the segmented weighting method from shallow to deep, which can effectively reduce the multi-wave interference.

In DRF inversion, O(z) is the observed value of the DRF, Y(z) is the theoretical value of the DRF, and z refers to the depth. We find the best fit between Y(z) and O(z) for each station by maximizing the objective function.

Fobj = [2Σi=1,n O(zi) • Y(zi)]/[Σi=1,n O2(zi) + Σi=1,n Y2(zi)].

The range of objective function values for the inversion results of 133 stations in this study is 0.78—0.95. Results with objective function values greater than 0.80 were considered reliable, and 95% of stations have credible results. Of these, 67 stations had objective function values greater than 0.90 and 59 stations had objective function values between 0.80 and 0.90.
