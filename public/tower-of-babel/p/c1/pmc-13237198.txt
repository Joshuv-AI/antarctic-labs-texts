The safety margin of small-scale tree cover loss in global fragmented forests
Authors: Jingrui Wang, Chaoqun Zhang, Yixuan Pan, Yongxian Su, Philippe Ciais, Taylor Smith, Jiali Shang, Jane Liu, Jing M Chen, Alessandro Cescatti, Yongguang Zhang, William F Laurance, Cornelius Senf, Zhiyan Zuo, Jinbao Liao, Raffaele Lafortezza, Kai Yan, Peng Zhu, Xiaobin Guan, Xing Li, Meimei Xue, Wenping Yuan, Xiuzhi Chen, Weiqi Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClimatic and anthropogenic disturbances have led to intense small-scale tree cover loss in global forests. However, it remains unclear when forest attributes at a large scale (e.g., 0.05° resolution) will decline in response to such sub-grid (e.g., 30-m) tree cover losses within forest ecosystems. Utilizing global maps of forest attribute proxies, we discover that vegetation greenness, canopy structure, composition, and photosynthesis function can all increase under limited tree cover loss, indicating a widely existing safety margin in global forests that is primarily buffered by a positive edge effect of landscape fragmentation within forest ecosystems. The safety margin varies across biomes (tropical: 7.7%; temperate: 3.7%; boreal: 1.0%) and is often positively correlated with ecosystem resistance. In addition, about 35.7% of the remaining global forests have exceeded the safety margin. Our finding contrasts with the conventional perception that sub-grid tree cover losses are inevitably associated with declines in forest attributes and functions. It provides quantitative information for mitigating forest degradation and has strong implications for sustainable forest management practices.Subject terms: Climate-change ecology, Climate-change ecology, Forest ecology

Introduction

Climate-driven disturbances and anthropogenic activities such as wildfires, urban expansion, agriculture, and forest management cause intense small-scale tree cover loss in global intact forests1,2, affecting a range of ecosystem attributes that are essential for human society and beneficial to all other organisms on Earth3–5. Yet, a fundamental and urgent question remains: when does such sub-grid tree cover loss become harmful to forest attributes at the ecosystem level?

At present, the effects of worldwide sub-grid tree cover loss on global forests remain insufficiently explored and are still debated across scientific frameworks6. For instance, disturbance theories suggest that small-scale tree cover loss fragments forest areas7,8. Landscape fragmentation intensifies the environmental stresses within forests9,10 and brings negative impacts on forest structure, composition, and function11–13. However, there are still disputes over the theory of the edge effect concept14. Fragmentation caused by tree cover loss can reduce competition within forests and promote a positive edge effect, enhancing forest structure, composition, and function15,16. In principle, the balance between the negative effects of tree cover loss and the positive effects from mitigated competition leads to overall negative12,13, neutral14, or even positive effects on forest ecosystems15,17. These highly variable results highlight the complexity of small-scale tree cover loss effects on forest ecosystems, underscoring the need for further investigations.

To address these knowledge gaps, we explore three key research questions: (1) Is there a safety margin (fsafety) when sub-grid small-scale tree cover fractional loss (floss) within forests leads to an overall degradation of forest ecosystem, i.e., declines in vegetation greenness, ecosystem structure, composition, and function? (2) If so, what is the key driver of the fsafety in fragmented forests, and how does this fsafety vary across biomes? (3) Has the tree cover loss in global fragmented forests reached or even exceeded the fsafety? Answers to these questions will provide important context for sustainable forest management and offer solutions for nature-based measures to mitigate the impacts of climate change.

In this work, we focus on the effects of 30-m resolution tree cover fractional loss on 0.05° resolution vegetation greenness, ecosystem structure, composition, and function in global forests that do not experience land-use change during the period 2000–2012 (Supplementary Fig. 1). Notably, the sub-grid gross tree cover fractional loss is quantified using 30-m resolution tree cover maps from Global Forest Watch (GFW)1. We use the enhanced vegetation index (EVI)18, the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI)18, and the near-infrared reflectance of vegetation (NIRv)19 as proxies of vegetation greenness, the leaf area index (LAI) as a proxy of canopy structure20, the biodiversity index (BDI) as a proxy of ecosystem composition21, and gross primary production (GPP)22–24 and solar-induced chlorophyll fluorescence (SIF)25 as proxies of ecosystem photosynthetic function (Supplementary Table 1). We then quantify anomalies (e.g., denoted as ΔEVI) of such forest attributes (e.g., EVI) before and after tree cover fractional loss to characterize the response of 0.05° resolution forest attributes to 30-m resolution sub-grid tree cover loss (see Methods). We show that the above-mentioned forest attributes all increase under limited floss, indicating a widely-existed safety margin of floss (denoted as fsafety) in global forests. Then, we quantify the fsafety across global 0.05° resolution forest grid cells and find that fragmentation-induced edge effect is the key factor in determining fsafety using landscape metrics such as edge density (ED), patch density (PD), and Euclidean nearest neighbor (ENN) index3. Finally, we show that about 35.7% of the remaining global forests have exceeded the fsafety.

Results

The safety margin of sub-grid tree cover fractional loss

The response of vegetation greenness is negatively modulated by floss (Fig. 1). Based on the EVI metric, we find a strong negative correlation between ΔEVI and floss (Fig. 1a–d, g). Interestingly, we detected an empirical threshold of floss—the safety margin (fsafety) below which ΔEVI can still increase with limited tree cover losses (Fig. 1e, g). This finding contrasted with the common understanding that tree cover loss was systematically associated with a decrease in greenness. The positive responses of EVI to tree cover loss were observed across 28.58% of the studied forests. It was most evident in tropical forests (north Amazon: 41.00%; central Africa: 41.51%; tropical Asia: 42.86%), characterized by a higher fsafety (7.76 ± 0.15) (Fig. 1e, h). Temperate forests (central China: 40.00%; eastern USA: 30.09%) had a moderate fsafety (3.69 ± 0.14). In these two biomes, anthropogenic activities such as the commodity-driven deforestation and agricultural expansion were the major drivers of sub-grid tree cover losses (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). In contrast, forests with positive ΔEVI were sporadically distributed in the boreal region (19.06%) (Fig. 1e, h). Their tree cover losses were mainly driven by wildfires and forestry, showing the lowest fsafety (0.99 ± 0.02) (Supplementary Fig. 2c). Notably, boreal tree cover can take a decade or more to regenerate after wildfire, especially in the northern part of the boreal biome. We thus took into account the factor of tree regeneration (i.e., tree cover gain) and re-quantified the fsafety across those 0.05° resolution boreal forest grid cells where more than 69.97% of 30-m resolution tree cover loss was driven by wildfire (see Methods). The results showed that the fsafety in the boreal region is 1.37 on a regional average, which is 0.68 higher than that without considering the tree regeneration factor (Supplementary Fig. 3).

The positive edge effect determines the magnitude of fsafety

The edge effect of fragmentation within forest ecosystems was assumed to be the key mechanism through which ecosystem attributes might be promoted under limited tree cover loss due to mitigated competition. This deduction was inferred on the grounds of the correlations between changes in ecosystem attributes (i.e., ΔEVI) and changes in forest landscape (e.g., edge density, ΔED) (Fig. 2). Specifically, ΔEVI was positively correlated with ΔED when floss was below fsafety, whereas the correlations between the two variables became negative once floss exceeded fsafety (Fig. 2a, c; Supplementary Fig. 12). The contrasting correlations between ΔEVI and ΔED below and beyond the fsafety were also supported by the partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) analyses (inset plots, Fig. 2a, c). In forests with limited tree cover loss (floss <fsafety), two main paths promote EVI (Fig. 3a; Supplementary Fig. 13). An increase in ED significantly (P < 0.001) cools local surface temperature (LST), decreases vapor pressure deficit (VPD), and raises soil moisture (SM), which jointly lead to an increase in EVI. Meanwhile, ED in intact forests may increase sunlight exposure and enhance absorbed photosynthetically active radiation (APAR), benefiting EVI increment. In contrast, when floss > fsafety, three paths may result in a negative effect on EVI (Fig. 3b; Supplementary Fig. 14): increases in ED due to intense tree cover loss significantly (P < 0.001) warm LST, increases VPD, and ultimately reduces EVI; ED also lowers SM and decreases APAR, producing a negative impact on EVI.

Overall, the balance between the negative effects of tree cover losses and positive effects from the edge effects collectively determines the direction and magnitude of changes in ecosystem attributes (i.e., ΔEVI). This finding was further validated using other alternative edge metrics (Supplementary Figs. 15, 16), all of which showed consistent patterns.

The fsafety is strongly associated with forest resistance

The fsafety is closely related to forest resistance (Fig. 4). Globally, fsafety is positively correlated with forest resistance (P < 0.001) across the majority of boreal (70.56%), temperate (66.86%), and tropical forests (66.44%). Temperate forests show the strongest correlation between fsafety and forest resistance (regionally averaged R = 0.29; P < 0.001). Tropical forests exhibit a moderate fsafety-resistance relationship (regionally averaged R = 0.20; P < 0.001). In contrast, boreal forests show the weakest correlation (regionally averaged R = 0.10; P < 0.001). The biome-dependent relationships between fsafety and forest resistance indicate that forests with higher fsafety often exhibit stronger resistance. In comparison, forests with lower fsafety are likely to be more vulnerable to climatic and anthropogenic disturbances.

Global tree cover loss budget

Finally, we evaluated the budget of tree cover fractional loss, defined as the differences (fbudget = fsafety-floss) between actual floss and fsafety, in global forests (Fig. 5; Supplementary Fig. 20). We found that fbudget had been overspent (fbudget = -0.12 ± 0.001) in approximately 35.7% of the studied forests. In contrast, the remaining 64.3% of the forests still had a fbudget underspending (fbudget = 0.07 ± 0.000). This asymmetry between overspent and underspent fbudget was typically evident in temperate and boreal zones. On a regional average, floss is overspent by more than 0.10 in degraded forests, while the remaining undegraded forests had the smallest quota left (fbudget < 0.03). In the tropical region, the situation differed across continents. Amazon retained the world’s largest quota in undegraded forests (82.3%; fbudget = 0.22 ± 0.000), and underwent a moderate degradation in the degraded forests (17.7%; fbudget = -0.08 ± 0.002). In contrast, tropical Asia showed the largest degraded area (43.8%) with the most severe degradation (fbudget = −0.21 ± 0.003). Congo forests experienced the lowest degradation (20.8%; fbudget = −0.04 ± 0.002) and meanwhile maintained a moderate fbudget in the remaining undegraded forests (79.2%; fbudget = 0.09 ± 0.000).

Discussion

One of the most important findings in this study is the quantification of a global safety margin of sub-grid tree cover loss below which forest attributes do not necessarily decline immediately. Instead, when tree cover loss remains below this safety margin, vegetation greenness, structure complexity, forest composition, and ecosystem function may be maintained or even enhanced, thereby partially mitigating forest degradation. This discovery has important theoretical implications, as it challenges the previous knowledge that tree cover loss inevitably leads to a decline in forest attributes and subsequent degradation11–13. Our results provide strong empirical evidence supporting the well-known edge effect theory14, which has long been difficult to quantify and thus largely overlooked to date. The safety margin has important implications for forest stability. Tropical and temperate forests with higher fsafety are more resistant to climatic and anthropogenic disturbances26,27. In contrast, boreal forests often show smaller fsafety and are more vulnerable to climatic and anthropogenic disturbances26,28. However, once the widespread post-fire tree regeneration is taken into account29, the fsafety of boreal forests will be largely promoted.

Equally importantly, we have mapped the global budget for tree cover loss to maintain current ecosystem attributes, such as vegetation greenness, structure, forest composition, and ecosystem function. This finding has strong implications for assessing the sustainability of current forest management practices. Regenerating tree cover in deforested areas is regarded as one of the most feasible and cost-effective nature-based solutions for climate mitigation30. However, to date, there is no quantitative information on the extent of tree cover fraction that should be regenerated in globally degraded forests. This knowledge gap hinders policymakers from formulating precise forest management strategies31. Our study has identified areas where the tree cover budget has been overspent and quantifies the minimal quota that needs to be reforested. This can avoid excessive future reforestation and the related potential negative environmental impacts (e.g., depletion of water resources) and maladaptation32. In addition, we have quantified the tree cover quota underspending in undegraded forests. This can serve as an early warning against excessive forest management activities (e.g., harvesting)1,33.

In summary, this study reveals that vegetation greenness, canopy structure, biodiversity, and photosynthetic function generally exhibit positive responses to limited sub-grid tree cover loss, and quantifies the global safety margins for avoiding forest degradation. This safety margin is mechanically modulated by the fragmentation edge effect within forest ecosystems and is of great significance to the stability of forest ecosystems. Using the estimated safety margins, we finally mapped the tree cover loss budget for global forest vegetation greenness, canopy structure, biodiversity, and photosynthesis function. Our findings not only supplement the classic ecology theories (i.e., disturbance and edge effect theories) but also hold important implications for guiding forest management practices. By providing both the safety margin and the budget of global tree cover loss, this study addresses a longstanding global concern that has not yet been fully explored.

Methods

Methods summary

The objective of this study is to evaluate whether and when sub-grid small-scale tree cover fractional loss (floss) within forests leads to overall declines in ecosystem-level vegetation greenness, ecosystem structure, composition, and function (Supplementary Fig. 1). To examine this, we first identified 0.05° forest grid cells that had experienced small-scale (30-m) gross tree cover fraction losses (floss > 0.02) within forests (defined as sub-grid tree cover loss) while having not induced a land use change (i.e., deforestation), based on the 30-m resolution tree cover maps from Global Forest Watch (GFW)1 and 0.05° resolution Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) land cover products34 (Methods: Select target forest pixels). We then used multiple global maps of vegetation greenness, canopy structure, composition, and function metrics (resampled to 0.05° resolution) (Supplementary Table 1) to calculate their anomalies for each 0.05° resolution forest grid cell before and after its sub-grid tree cover loss, with removal of impacts from climate variability, to assess the impacts of sub-grid fractional tree cover losses (floss) on such forest ecosystem attributes (Methods: Quantify impacts of sub-grid tree cover loss on forest attributes). Next, we identified the safety margin (fsafety) of floss beyond which vegetation greenness, canopy structure, composition, and function declined (Methods: Quantify the safety margin of sub-grid tree cover loss). After that, we explored the potential influences of the edge effect of fragmentation within the forest, demoted as the changes in edge density (ED) of the 0.05° forest grid cell associated with sub-grid 30-m resolution tree cover loss (Methods: Calculate sub-grid tree cover losses), on the fsafety in global forest (Methods: Metrics for representing the edge effect of sub-grid fragmentation, Quantify impacts of sub-grid fragmentation on forest attributes). Finally, we calculated the difference between actual floss and fsafety to derive the global tree cover loss budget (fbudget = fsafety - floss).

Notably, in the main text, we presented analyses based on the enhanced vegetation index (EVI) from the MOD13Q1 Version 6.1 dataset18. We also tested the result robustness based on other indicators and presented these analyses in supplementary materials, which include: the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) from MOD13Q1 Version 6.1 dataset18 and near-infrared reflectance of vegetation (NIRv)19 as proxies of vegetation greenness, the leaf area index (LAI) from MOD15A2H Version 6.1 dataset20 as a proxy of canopy structure, the biodiversity index (BDI) from projections of biodiversity intactness21 as a proxy of ecosystem composition, the gross primary production (GPP) from Penman-Monteith-Leuning Evapotranspiration Version 2 dataset22–24 and solar-induced chlorophyll fluorescence (SIF) retrievals from Orbiting Carbon Observatory-2, MODIS, and reanalysis data25 as proxies of ecosystem photosynthesis function.

Additionally, the edge effect was quantified in the main text using the edge density (ED) metric3. To further examine the robustness of the results, the supplementary materials also included analyses based on alternative edge effect metrics such as patch density (PD)35 and Euclidean nearest neighbor (ENN)36,37.

Select target forest pixels

The target 0.05° resolution forests should have ever experienced small-scale (e.g., 30-m resolution) tree cover loss within forest ecosystems, but should never experience a land use change and consistently remain as forest land during the study period. To ensure this, we first identified grid cells that were consistently classified as ‘forest lands’ from 2000 to 2012 in the MODIS MCD12C1 Version 6.1 land cover images34. Then, we overlaid the 30-m high-resolution global tree cover change maps from Global Forest Watch (GFW)38 onto the selected 0.05° resolution MODIS forest grid cells. For each 0.05° forest grid cell, we calculated the 30-m resolution tree cover fraction and selected those with the tree cover fraction consistently greater than 50% from 2000 to 2012 for analysis. To isolate the impact of tree cover fraction loss (floss) on forest attributes while minimizing the influence of tree cover fraction gain (fgain), we focused on those 0.05° resolution grid cells that exhibited a considerable tree cover loss (floss > 0.02) while maintaining small tree cover gain (fgain < 0.01) over the same period, based on the 30-m resolution GFW tree cover maps6.

Quantify the impacts of sub-grid tree cover loss on forest attributes

In this study, we selected multiple commonly used metrics representing vegetation greenness, canopy structures, composition, and function to evaluate the impacts of sub-grid tree cover losses on forest attributes. The vegetation greenness metrics include the enhanced vegetation index (EVI) and the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI). The data for these two indicators are sourced from the MOD13Q1 Version 6.1 dataset18, which has a spatial resolution of 250 meters, a temporal resolution of 16 days, and spans from 2000 to 2012. Additionally, the near-infrared reflectance of vegetation (NIRv) from 2000 to 2012 was included, which was estimated using the method proposed by Badgley et al.19. The canopy structure is characterized by the leaf area index (LAI) from the MOD15A2H Version 6.1 dataset20, which provides data with a spatial resolution of 500 meters and a temporal resolution of 8 days from 2000 to 2012. The ecosystem composition is depicted by the biodiversity index (BDI) from projections of biodiversity intactness21, with a spatial resolution of 100 meters and a time span from 2017 to 2020. The photosynthetic function is quantified by the gross primary production (GPP) from the Penman-Monteith-Leuning Evapotranspiration Version 2 dataset, which offers data with a spatial resolution of 500 meters and a temporal resolution of 8 days from 2000 to 2012. Meanwhile, the solar-induced chlorophyll fluorescence (SIF) retrieved from Orbiting Carbon Observatory-2, MODIS, and reanalysis data25 is also adopted as a proxy of photosynthetic function. It has a spatial resolution of 0.05°, a temporal resolution of 8 days, and a time span from 2000 to 2012. Notably, to match the spatial resolution of the target forest grid cells, all these metrics were aggregated annually and resampled to a spatial resolution of 0.05°.

In the main text, we used EVI as a metric for illustrating the effect of sub-grid tree cover losses on vegetation greenness. For each 0.05° resolution grid cell, in a given year with 30-m resolution tree cover loss within forest, we calculated the anomaly in the three-year averaged EVI (ΔEVIanomaly) before and after the year with tree cover loss. ΔEVIanomaly reflects a combination of background climate variability (ΔEVIbackground) and changes induced by sub-grid tree cover loss (ΔEVI). To isolate the effects solely attributed to sub-grid tree cover losses, we adjusted ΔEVI using a time-series analysis approach developed by Alkama and Cescatti2 (Eq. 4). Following the criteria of Su et al.6, we identified reference grid cells that had experienced minimal sub-grid tree cover change (fgain < 0.01 & floss < 0.01) and maintained a tree cover fraction consistently above 50%. The three-year averaged EVI anomalies before and after the year of tree cover loss for reference grid cells were attributed solely to background climate variability (ΔEVIbackground). For the target forest grid cells with substantial tree cover losses (Methods: Select target forest pixels), reference grid cells were selected from neighboring undisturbed forests within a 50 km radius2, assuming similar climate conditions within this spatial scale39. The ΔEVIbackground within 50 km was averaged using inverse distance weighting to minimize spatial biases6. To ensure statistical robustness, only target grid cells with at least five reference grid cells within a 50 km radius were included in the analysis.

where ΔEVI represents the EVI changes driven solely by sub-grid tree cover loss; ΔEVIanomaly denotes the total observed EVI changes, and ΔEVIbackground represents the portion influenced by climate variability. This adjustment ensures that the changes in the EVI solely reflect the impact of sub-grid tree cover loss, excluding confounding effects from climatic factors. For each given pixel, this approach was applied to all the years with tree cover loss. The ΔEVI of all those years with tree cover loss was averaged for this given pixel and analyzed against floss to explore the response of EVI to sub-grid tree cover losses.

Quantify the safety margin of sub-grid tree cover loss

In this study, we identified the safety margin of sub-grid tree cover losses (fsafety) as those where ecosystem attribute anomaly (e.g., ΔEVI) shifts from positive to negative values as floss increases. To achieve this, we classified the samples into different floss bins and selected those samples with ecosystem attribute anomaly located within the 5th percentiles for each floss bin, as these data were more likely to exhibit a strong edge effect. Then, we calculated the average ecosystem attribute anomaly in different floss bins. The fsafety was identified as the floss when the fitting line of ecosystem attribute anomaly in different floss bins crosses the zero line. Notably, we explored a nonlinear fitting approach to generate the fitting curve between floss and the ecosystem attribute anomaly. The ΔEVI-estimated fsafety was shown in Fig. 1g. Using this approach, we mapped global fsafety for each 4∘×4∘ moving window, as shown in Fig. 1e. The regionally averaged fsafety for boreal, temperate, and tropical zones was presented in Fig. 1h. To examine the variations in fsafety due to different drivers of sub-grid tree cover loss, we calculated the average values of fsafety across five key tree cover loss drivers, including commodity-driven deforestation, shifting agriculture, forestry, wildfire, and urbanization40. These fsafety values were further averaged under different tree cover fraction bins (e.g., 50–60%, 60–70%, 70–80%, and 80–100%) and across various climate zones, as summarized in Supplementary Fig. 2. In addition, post-fire tree regeneration frequently occurs in the boreal forests. Thus, tree cover gain was explicitly considered to re-quantify the fsafety in forest grid cells. To do this, only grid cells, where tree cover loss was attributed to wildfire according to the global tree cover loss driver maps40, were retained for subsequent analysis. Then, the safety margin (fsafety) by versus without consideration of post-fire regeneration was quantified for comparison (Supplementary Fig. 3).

We tested the robustness of the results by using other metrics of vegetation greenness (NDVI and NIRv), canopy structure (LAI), ecosystem composition (BDI), and photosynthesis function (GPP or SIF) to estimate the global pattern of fsafety. Results closely aligned with that of EVI-based fsafety (Supplementary Figs. 4–9). Additionally, we also selected those pixels with data within the 4th and 6th percentiles for each floss bin and applied the same method to identify fsafety. Spatial patterns of fsafety were shown in Supplementary Figs. 10, 11, consistent with those in Fig. 1e.

Quantify the impacts of sub-grid fragmentation on forest attributes

To assess the sensitivity of ∆EVI to ΔED induced by sub-grid tree cover loss, we categorized the forest grid cells into two groups: those with floss below and those beyond fsafety. Within each group, we calculated the slope of the linear correlations (SEDEVI) and the correlation coefficient (R) between ΔED and ΔEVI within 4°×4° moving windows for different tree cover bins (e.g., 50–60%, 60–70%, 70–80%, and 80–100%), as depicted in Fig. 2a–d. The linear fitting curves between the two variables for each forest grid cell were presented in Supplementary Fig. 12. We also calculated the average SEDEVI for forest grid cells affected by different tree cover loss drivers (commodity-driven deforestation, shifting agriculture, forestry, wildfire, and urbanization)40 across various tree cover bins (e.g., 50–60%, 60–70%, 70–80%, and 80–100%) in boreal, temperate, and tropical forests, respectively. We further stratified SEDEVI by both tree cover fraction bins and climate zones. These SEDEVI values for grid cells below and beyond fsafety were summarized in Supplementary Figs. 13, 14, respectively. The sensitivities of ΔEVI to fragmentation were also evaluated using other fragmentation metrics like PD and ENN (Supplementary Figs. 15, 16).

The partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) is a powerful statistical approach for estimating complex cause-effect relationships in path models involving latent variables42. Unlike traditional structural equation modeling, which relies on maximum likelihood estimation43 and assumes large sample sizes with multivariate normality, PLS-SEM imposes fewer restrictions, making it suitable for smaller datasets and non-normally distributed data44. Additionally, PLS-SEM can effectively handle missing data. The GoF index in PLS-SEM was primarily used to optimize the predictive accuracy of dependent variables45. In this study, we employed PLS-SEM to compare the effects of ΔED versus floss on ΔEVI. Specifically, we applied PLS-SEM analysis within each 4° × 4° moving window to mitigate the influence of other environmental factors. The PLS-SEM analysis was applied for 0.05° forest grid cells with floss below and beyond fsafety separately (inset plots in Fig. 2a,c). Only windows with a goodness of fit (GoF) index greater than 0.5, as determined by PLS-SEM analysis, were included in the evaluation. The PLS-SEM analyses based on other metrics, such as the PD and ENN, (Supplementary Figs. 15, 16) were similar to the analysis method for ED.

Depict the paths of sub-grid fragmentation impacts on forest attributes

First, we calculated the differences in land surface temperature (LST), soil moisture (SM), vapor pressure deficit (VPD), and absorbed photosynthetically active radiation (APAR) between 2012 and 2000 (denoted as ΔLST, ΔVPD, ΔSM, and ΔAPAR) to quantify their responses to sub-grid tree cover losses. These variables were resampled to a spatial resolution of 0.05° to match the 0.05° resolution forest grid cells. LST was derived from the MOD11C3 Version 6.1 land surface temperature products46. VPD and SM were sourced from the TerraClimate dataset47, and APAR was calculated based on the PAR and FAPAR from the global land surface satellite (GLASS) products48,49.

Subsequently, to accurately isolate the biophysical consequences of sub-grid tree cover loss, we assumed that adjacent forest and non-forest areas shared similar background climatic conditions. Based on this assumption, we could consider the intrinsic biophysical processes as localized perturbations superimposed on a stable climatic background50. For each forest grid cell, a space-for-time analogy39 was employed to estimate the local biophysical effects of sub-grid tree cover on key environmental variables (LST, SM, VPD, APAR). Taking LST as an example, ΔLST was quantified as the temperature difference between forest areas and nearby open areas (grassland and cropland) (Eq. 8). Forest and open land pixels were delineated using the MCD12C1 Version 6.1 land cover images34. These differences were calculated separately for different tree cover bins (e.g., 50–60%, 60–70%, 70–80%, and 80–100%) for the years 2000 and 2012.

Finally, we utilized the PLS-SEM approach to disentangle how changes in ΔED altered environmental conditions (ΔLST, ΔVPD, ΔSM, and ΔAPAR) and influenced forest attribute anomalies (e.g., ΔEVI) within each 4° × 4° moving window. It is noteworthy that only grid cells with a GoF index greater than 0.5 were included in the analysis. PLS-SEM analyses were conducted separately for forests with floss below and beyond fsafety, respectively (Fig. 3a, b). This method was also applied to other landscape metrics (ΔPD and ΔENN) to depict the pathways through which the edge effect of landscape fragmentation influenced forest attributes (e.g., ΔEVI) (Supplementary Figs. 17, 19).

Calculating ecosystem resistance

Resistance is commonly defined as the capacity of an ecosystem to maintain its normal levels during a climate disturbance event51. In this study, we quantified forest resistance using EVI as an illustration, calculated as a dimensionless ratio between the baseline vegetation condition and the magnitude of vegetation greenness decline during extreme disturbance events52,53 (Eq. 10).
