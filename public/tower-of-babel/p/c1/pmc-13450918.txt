Evaluation of the immunohistochemical expression of DNA damage marker 8-OHdG in different histopathological grades of oral epithelial dysplasia
Authors: Khadijah Mohideen, Krithika Chandrasekaran, Nadeem Jeddy, Sulaiman S. Alqahtani, J Bhuvaneswarri, Mutaz Ali-Hassan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:The present investigation assessed the advanced oxidative stress end product, 8-hydroxydeoxyguanosine (8-OHdG), in oral epithelial dysplasia using immunohistochemistry (IHC) on tissue samples.Materials and methods:The study used tissue blocks from 40 patients with epithelial dysplasia. The diagnosis was confirmed by reviewing the hematoxylin-eosin slides. Normal mucosa tissue samples (n = 10) formed the control group. The intensity of immunohistochemical reactions was evaluated using light microscopy. The collected data were analyzed statistically using SPSS 26.0 to determine differences between the sample groups at a 5% significance level using Fisher’s exact test.Results:Of the 40 leukoplakia with dysplasia samples examined, 5% showed high staining for 8-OHdG antigen, 25% had moderate staining, 37.5% had mild staining, and 43.33% showed no staining. Increased 8-OHdG antigen expression was found in dysplastic tissue samples (P < 0.001) compared to normal tissue samples. Of the mild dysplasia samples, 30% showed mild expression. The remaining 70% had negative 8-OHdG expression. Moderate dysplasia had 13.3% moderate expression, 60% mild expression, and 26.6% showed negative 8-OHdG expression. Of the severe dysplasia samples, 53.3% showed moderate expression, 20% showed mild expression, and 13.3% showed negative 8-OHdG staining. The 8-OHdG marker is intensely expressed in 13.3% of severe dysplasia samples. Compared to mild and moderate dysplasia tissue samples, severe dysplasia had higher 8-OHdG expression (P < 0.01).Conclusion:The current investigation suggests that detecting 8-OHdG in potentially malignant oral disorders may be a reliable diagnostic indicator of oxidative DNA degeneration.

INTRODUCTION

Oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs) are oral mucosal tissue diseases that are more susceptible to malignant transformation than other oral mucosal diseases.[1] Typically, these lesions are accompanied by genetic alterations that may lead to the development of cancer of the oral cavity.[2] Reactive oxygen species (ROS) cause oxidative stress (OS), and the enhanced antioxidant system functions as a free radical scavenger to prevent ROS-induced oxidative damage. Antioxidants are generated as a result of internal or external factors, such as glutathione peroxidase (GPx), reduced glutathione (GSH), glutathione reductase (GR), catalase (CAT), and superoxide dismutase (SOD), which are the most fundamental components of the system of antioxidants. Additionally, protein carriers of metals such as ceruloplasmin, lactoferrin, transferrin, and various micronutrients, including vitamins C, E, provitamin A, bilirubin, selenium, and uric acid, are also essential antioxidants.[3]

OS is produced when there is an excess of free radicals on one side and a deficiency of antioxidants on the other. Biomolecules, including DNA, lipids, and proteins, are subjected to OS, which disrupts their functions.[4] Consequently, OS is implicated in various pathological processes, including inflammation, potentially malignant tumors, mutation, and cancer progression. The ROS reaction with guanine bases in DNA impairs purines, pyrimidines, and deoxyribose.[5] Pyrimidine degeneration compounds include glycol, 5-hydroxy-deoxyuridine, 5-hydroxy-deoxy-cytidine, hydantoin, thymine, uracil glycol, and urea residue. Formamidopyrimidines, 8-hydroxydeoxyadenosine, 8-hydroxydeoxyguanosine (8-OHdG), and various purine-oxidized end products are produced by purine degradation.[6] The main product of oxidative degradation of nuclear and mitochondrial DNA is 8-OHdG. The 8-OHdG evaluation assesses oxidative DNA damage, including various carcinoma samples.[7]

The cellular biomembrane readily absorbs 8-OHdG and is typically detected in several biological tissues exposed to OS. The detection of 8-OHdG typically indicates mutagenesis due to oxidative stress.[8] Despite improved accessibility to the oral cavity, there has been considerable mortality related to oral cancer, as they are often diagnosed. Nevertheless, there are no dependable biomarkers to identify the process of malignant transformation in OPMDs. Diagnosing oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) at an early stage improves survival and prognosis.[9] The need to identify potential biomarkers for early detection, improved prognosis, and personalized treatment is underscored by these variables. Lipid peroxidation markers and antioxidant enzymes, presumably CAT, SOD, GPx, and GSH, are among the numerous OS-antioxidant-related markers documented in published studies.[1011]

Salivary biomarkers, such as IL-6, IL-8, IL-1β, basic fibroblast growth factor, and OS-related bioindicators, are widely recognized as indicators of oral pre-cancer and cancer.[1213] However, they have not yet been tested for predicting the likelihood of malignancy, identifying malignancies, or developing prevention strategies in a clinical setting.[14] ROS and nitrogen species may be essential in developing carcinogenic and mutagenic effects on DNA through oxidative DNA damage.[1516] The formation of 8-OHdG results from the Fenton reaction, UV light, radiation, ROS, nitrogen species, and DNA exposure.[16] Given all these facts, evaluating the genetic variations underlying tumor behaviour and tumorigenesis is crucial. To determine whether 8-OHdG could serve as a potential indicator of dysplastic traits in cells, the immunohistochemical expression assay is deemed the most suitable tool for future cancer diagnosis, prognosis, and assessment of treatment response.[1718] The present investigation assessed the oxidative DNA end product 8-OHdG in oral dysplasia lesions using the immunohistochemistry (IHC) procedure with formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded archival tissue specimens.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The retrospective experimental study was conducted after obtaining prior ethical approval from the Institutional Human Ethics Board. This investigation employed archival sections from tissues embedded in paraffin and maintained in 10% formalin. The tissue specimens were obtained from the Department of Oral Pathology of tertiary healthcare institutions. The control group consisted of tissue specimens from normal mucosa (n = 10) near the third molar extraction or reactionary lesional site. The study tissue samples comprised histopathologically confirmed 40 epithelial dysplasia tissue blocks. Histological grading was performed according to the World Health Organization classification.[1] Of the 40 dysplasia samples, 10 were mild, 15 moderate, and 15 severe, all of which underwent IHC analysis. Both smokers and non-users were represented in the histopathologically confirmed biopsy specimens of subjects aged 28 to 70 years, who were not under any systemic diseases, chronic immunocompromised conditions or under prescription drugs.

Individuals who were aged 28 years or older, those who had been on immunosuppressive medications or antioxidants, those who underwent treatment for the conditions, those with concurrent oral lesions or systemic diseases, and those with a previous history of malignancies aside from oral cancer were excluded. Additionally, tissue samples with limited epithelial components were excluded. Patient demographics, including age, gender, medical history, lesion site, clinical assessment of conditions, and histopathological reports, were obtained from hospital records. A thorough patient history was recorded, which included the consumption of betel nuts, nicotine, and alcohol.

Tissue blocks that were paraffin-embedded and fixed with formalin (10%) were cut to 5 μm, mounted on polylysine-coated positively charged slides, and incubated at 60-70°C for one hour. Paraffin-embedded specimens were deparaffinized in xylene for 10 minutes (twice) and, consequently, well-hydrated with progressively lower concentrations of isopropyl alcohol in the following order: 100% purified isopropyl alcohol (1 alteration spanning 5 minutes), then transferred to 90% isopropyl alcohol for 5 minutes, followed by 70% isopropyl alcohol immersion for 5 minutes.

After heat-induced epitope retrieval with antigen retrieval reagent-basic (Catalog #CTS013) for 15 to 20 minutes (for two pressure cooker whistles), the samples were rinsed with distilled water for 2 minutes (2 changes). After 30 minutes of cooling, the samples were rinsed twice with purified water. For two minutes, the samples were cleansed in sodium citrate buffer or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The liquid-repellent PAP pen was employed to mark the components with rings. The tissue sections were incubated with H2O2 (3%) in methanol for 30 minutes at room temperature to conduct endogenous peroxidase quenching. Tissue segments were incubated at room temperature for 40 minutes with Proteinase K (10 μg/mL in PBS at pH 7.4). The specimens were then incubated with normal serum (10%) in TBS for 10 minutes at room temperature, followed by diluted primary antibody to 8-OHdG (1:200) for 16 hours at ambient temperature. The primary antibody employed was the 8-OHdG Antibody [Goat polyclonal antibody; Novus Biologicals - A Biotechne Brand (NB600-1508) (IHC-P-1:200)]. In addition to the dysplasia tissue samples, colon carcinoma tissue was used as a positive control and subjected to primary antibody staining for IHC analysis of 8-OHdG expression. The negative control sample was processed identically to the dysplasia group, except that non-immune serum was used instead of the primary antibody. It was subsequently rinsed in a wash buffer (PBS) for 5 minutes and rinsed three times.

The poly-excel target binder reagent was then used to incubate the segments at room temperature for 12 minutes. The tissue samples were cleaned using three variations of wash buffer (PBS) for five minutes. The elements were treated with anti-mouse IgG poly-excel HRP antibody at room temperature for 30 minutes. The 3’ 3’- diaminobenzidine chromogen reagent was prepared by thoroughly combining one milliliter of DAB buffer with a single drop of DAB chromogen. The reagent was then washed in PBS for 5 minutes, with three washes. The complexes of antigens and antibodies were preserved by rinsing the segments with filtered water after incubating them with an active DAB chromogen for 2 to 5 minutes. Counterstaining all tissue sample segments was performed using hematoxylin for 30 seconds. Then, the sections were subjected to running water for 5 minutes, desiccated with successive administrations of isopropyl alcohol, and rinsed with xylene. They were later dried and cover-slipped using the aqueous mounting solution dibutyl phthalate xylene (DPX). Afterward, the slides with tissue sections were air-dried at room temperature in preparation for microscopic examination.

The extent of immunohistochemical reactions was assessed using light microscopy. The membranes, cytoplasm, or nuclei of the positive cells displayed significant brown staining. Slides exhibiting heterogeneous staining were examined for additional staining. The DNA degradation marker anti-8-OHdG-specific staining was detected in both the cytoplasm and the nucleus. The number of positive cells was counted in five randomly selected fields at 40x magnification. The staining intensity of the sections was evaluated as none, mild, moderate, or intense.[19] The specimens were assessed independently by two experts in the domain, who were unaware of each patient’s clinical observations. They achieved an exceptionally high degree of concordance. Statistical analysis was conducted by averaging the assessments.

Statistical analysis

The data were compiled and analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26.0 to determine differences between the two groups using Fisher’s exact test. Statistical significance was determined using a probability value of <0.05.

RESULTS

There were 25 males and 15 females, with a mean age of 45. Of these, 20 (50%) were reported to have been smokers for two to 15 years. The study includes tissue samples from the cheek (n = 30; 75%), tongue (n = 5; 12.5%), gingiva (n = 4; 10%), and lips (n = 1; 2.5%). Of the 40 leukoplakia with dysplasia samples analyzed, 2 (5%) demonstrated intense staining for the 8-OHdG antigen, 10 (25%) moderate staining, 15 (37.5%) mild staining, and 13 (43.33%) absent staining. Substantial upregulation of 8-OHdG antigen expression was observed in oral dysplasia tissue samples (P < 0.001) in comparison to the normal tissue specimens [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

Free radicals such as HO• attack cellular and mitochondrial DNA strands, adding new radicals to DNA bases and producing a diverse array of oxidation byproducts.[20] The nitrogen base guanine is preferred for DNA strand liaison to HO• due to its lower oxidation propensity. This interaction synthesizes the radical adducts C8-hydro guanine (8-OHGua) or its nucleoside form, deoxyguanosine (8-hydroxy-2´-deoxyguanosine), as a result of the HO• addition to the DNA bases. Subsequently, 8-OHdG is produced through one-electron abstraction. The radical adducts have promutagenic potential because they can couple with adenosine rather than cytosine during replication, thereby promoting GC/TA transversion mutations. Consequently, 8-OHdG serves as an indicator for carcinogenesis, aging, and oxidative stress.[21]

In the present study, 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) expression was significantly upregulated (P < 0.001) in oral leukoplakia with epithelial dysplasia, with positivity mainly localized to the basal layers, consistent with Ma et al.[22] This pattern highlights the susceptibility of proliferating basal cells to oxidative DNA damage from ROS, generated endogenously by oxygen metabolism or exogenously through risk behaviours. Hydroxyl radicals (HO•), in particular, are potent mediators of oxidative injury to DNA, proteins, and lipids, contributing to genomic instability and disease progression.[2324]

A gradual and significant increase in cytoplasmic 8-OHdG expression from normal oral mucosa to higher grades of epithelial dysplasia was observed, suggesting progressive oxidative DNA damage during oral carcinogenesis. Barros et al.[25] also demonstrated a positive association between oxidative DNA damage and increasing dysplasia severity. Similarly, Varela et al.[26] reported enhanced 8-OHdG expression in dysplastic oral epithelium, supporting the role of oxidative stress in early malignant transformation. Medeiros et al.[27] further observed strong 8-OHdG immunoreactivity across different grades of epithelial dysplasia, indicating that oxidative DNA injury may persist throughout the dysplastic process rather than being restricted to advanced lesions.

Comparable findings have been documented in other studies. Zimnoch et al.[28] noted intense nuclear 8-OHdG expression in gallbladder mucosa with chronic cholecystitis, supporting the role of chronic inflammation as an amplifier of oxidative stress. Paredes- Sánchez et al.[29] similarly demonstrated elevated salivary 8-OHdG levels in chronic periodontitis, which correlated with inflammatory burden and disease course.

Oxidative stress has been widely implicated in the pathogenesis and progression of oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs) and oral cancer, with numerous studies highlighting the role of oxidative DNA damage biomarkers such as 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG). In support of this observation, Roy et al.[30] reported elevated oxidative stress biomarkers in potentially malignant oral disorders, suggesting their potential utility as indicators of disease progression and carcinogenic risk. Totan et al.[31] reported significantly elevated salivary 8-OHdG levels in patients with oral lichen planus compared to healthy controls, while Chaiyarit et al.[19] demonstrated mutagenic DNA lesions, including 8-nitroguanine and 8-OHdG, in oral lichen planus tissue samples. Similarly, Kaur et al.[14] observed substantially higher salivary 8-OHdG in lichen planus, oral submucous fibrosis (OSMF), leukoplakia, and oral cancer when compared to normal controls, further supporting the potential of 8-OHdG as a biomarker across a range of OPMDs.

Leukoplakia has been a particular focus of salivary 8-OHdG studies, with Tarrad et al.[32] and Babiuch et al.[11] reporting elevated levels in affected patients compared to controls.[3] Rai et al.[33] also found significantly higher salivary 8-OHdG in OPMD group patients, while Subash and Jayanthi detected increased 8-OHdG in lymphocyte and urine samples from leukoplakia with dysplasia and oral cancer cases.[34] Elevated serum and salivary levels of 8-OHdG have also been documented in OSMF and OSCC patients, suggesting oxidative stress as a shared pathogenic mechanism among diverse OPMDs.[35363738]

In oral cancer, Hamidavi et al.[39] demonstrated significantly increased tissue 8-OHdG levels compared to normal gingiva, consistent with findings from other malignancies such as hepatocellular carcinoma, papillary thyroid carcinoma, and prostate adenocarcinoma.[404142]

The present study demonstrates that 8-OHdG nuclear expression in the basal layer of a few dysplasia samples suggests that oxidative nuclear DNA damage in basal layer cells may represent a frequently observed early event during carcinogenesis. Furthermore, the aggressive nature of the lesion may be linked to the nuclear localization of 8-OHdG. It can potentially increase the migratory and invasive nature of dysplastic cells. Furthermore, the severity of the pathologic process in the dysplastic samples was positively correlated with 8-OHdG expression. These results align with the concept that tumor cells, due to their elevated metabolic rates and mitochondrial dysfunction, produce excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), which, when combined with impaired DNA repair mechanisms, facilitate genomic instability and tumor progression.[43]

These results suggest that 8-OHdG is a promising biomarker for oxidative DNA damage in oral potentially malignant disorders, with progressive elevation reflecting disease severity. Its consistent association with chronic inflammation and epithelial transformation across different tissues strengthens its potential value in early detection, risk assessment, and monitoring of oral leukoplakia. Targeting oxidative stress pathways may offer preventive and therapeutic opportunities to mitigate the risk of malignant transformation.

The current study has a few drawbacks. The subjects of normal tissue sections were not age-matched, as it is challenging to identify age-matched healthy individuals with no evidence of lesions in their oral mucosa and without systemic disease. A more comprehensive investigation of age- and gender-matched study and control samples, considering other influencing factors, is necessary for more in-depth evaluations.

CONCLUSION

The current investigation suggests that detecting 8-OHdG in potentially malignant oral disorders may be a reliable diagnostic indicator of oxidative DNA degeneration. The extent of the disease shows a positive association with 8-OHdG staining in OPMDs. The level of 8-OHdG expression was significantly higher in severe dysplasia than in the mild and moderate dysplasia tissue samples.
