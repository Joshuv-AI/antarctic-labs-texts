Therapeutic Delivery of Bone Marrow Mesenchymal Stem Cell-Derived Exosomal miR-143-3p Inhibits Myocardial and Systemic Inflammation and Attenuates Sepsis-Related Myocardial Injury
Authors: Yuyang Qiu, Lian Liao, Tian Zhang, Yiming Ma, Jianyu Fu, Jiakai Wang, Xu Liu, Wei Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sepsis-related myocardial injury (SRMI) is a major contributor to mortality in septic patients, driven by uncontrolled inflammation and macrophage dysregulation. Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (BMSC-Exos) possess immunomodulatory properties, but their cardioprotective mechanisms remain unclear. Here, we investigated whether BMSC-Exos deliver microRNA-143-3p (miR-143-3p) to reprogram macrophages and attenuate SRMI. Exosomes were isolated from murine bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells and characterized by electron microscopy, nanoparticle tracking analysis, and immunoblotting. In lipopolysaccharide-stimulated macrophages, BMSC-Exos promoted a shift from pro-inflammatory M1 to reparative M2 polarization, and reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion. In a mouse model of endotoxemia, BMSC-Exo administration improved seven-day survival, preserved cardiac function, decreased circulating myocardial injury markers, and increased the proportion of cardiac M2 macrophages. MicroRNA sequencing identified miR-143-3p as highly enriched in BMSC-Exos but downregulated in circulating exosomes from septic mice. Delivery of miR-143-3p mimics recapitulated the protective effects of BMSC-Exos, while inhibition of miR-143-3p exacerbated injury. Mechanistically, miR-143-3p directly targeted Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) and suppressed the downstream myeloid differentiation primary response 88/nuclear factor-κB (MyD88/NF-κB) signaling pathway. Furthermore, TLR4 knockdown phenocopied the anti-inflammatory and M2-polarizing effects of miR-143-3p. These findings indicate that BMSC-Exos attenuate SRMI by transferring miR-143-3p to macrophages, where it inhibits TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling and promotes M2 polarization, highlighting a potential therapeutic strategy for septic cardiac injury.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10753-026-02555-x.

Background

Sepsis, defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction arising from a dysregulated host response to infection, continues to be a vital cause of mortality in intensive care units worldwide [1, 2]. Despite advances in supportive management, sepsis-associated mortality remains unacceptably high, largely owing to the progression of multiple organ dysfunction, with the heart representing a particularly susceptible target [3]. Sepsis-related myocardial injury (SRMI), also termed septic cardiomyopathy, manifests as reversible cardiac dysfunction accompanied by elevated myocardial biomarkers and is an independent predictor of adverse outcomes in septic patients [4, 5]. The pathogenesis of SRMI entails a complex interplay between systemic inflammation and local perturbation of the myocardial immune microenvironment, wherein overactivation of the innate immune system serves as a primary initiator and driver of myocardial damage [6, 7].

Upon microbial invasion, pathogen-associated molecular patterns—exemplified by lipopolysaccharide (LPS)—elicit a robust inflammatory cascade. Macrophages, as central orchestrators of innate immunity, are pivotal to this process [8, 9]. These cells display remarkable plasticity and can adopt functionally distinct phenotypes in response to microenvironmental cues: classically activated M1 macrophages exert pro-inflammatory and microbicidal functions, whereas alternatively activated M2 macrophages promote anti-inflammatory responses, efferocytosis, and tissue restoration [10, 11]. During early sepsis, appropriate M1 polarization is indispensable for pathogen eradication; however, excessive or protracted M1 activation aggravates tissue injury. Conversely, a timely phenotypic transition toward the M2 state is essential for curbing inflammation, facilitating repair, and reestablishing homeostasis [12, 13]. Notably, an imbalance in the myocardial M1/M2 macrophage ratio has been documented in both SRMI patients and preclinical models, with M1 predominance directly linked to impaired cardiac performance [14–16]. Hence, steering macrophage polarization from an M1 toward an M2 phenotype represents a compelling therapeutic avenue for SRMI [9, 17].

In recent years, stem cell-based interventions have opened new horizons for the management of inflammatory disorders. Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) have garnered considerable interest owing to their robust immunomodulatory and tissue-reparative capabilities [18]. Nevertheless, mounting evidence indicates that the beneficial effects of BMSCs are predominantly mediated by paracrine mechanisms rather than by direct differentiation and engraftment [19, 20]. Exosomes—nanoscale extracellular vesicles—carry a diverse cargo of bioactive molecules, including proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids (most notably microRNAs), thereby serving as key conduits of intercellular communication and paracrine signaling [21, 22]. BMSC-derived exosomes (BMSC-Exos) inherit the therapeutic potential of their parental cells while circumventing the risks inherent to cell transplantation, positioning them as promising cell-free therapeutic entities [18, 19]. Indeed, BMSC-Exos have been shown to confer protection in various cardiac disease models, an effect closely associated with modulation of macrophage function [23, 24]. For instance, BMSC-Exos were reported to deliver specific miRNAs such as miR-182 to target TLR4, thereby fostering macrophage M2 polarization and mitigating myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury [24]. Despite these advances, the specific molecular cargo and precise mechanistic underpinnings by which BMSC-Exos regulate macrophage polarization in the context of SRMI remain to be systematically delineated.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) constitute a class of small non-coding RNAs that post-transcriptionally modulate gene expression and play instrumental roles in immune and inflammatory networks [25, 26]. miR-143-3p is an evolutionarily conserved miRNA that, beyond its established functions in oncogenesis and cardiovascular development, has recently been implicated in inflammatory regulation. Notably, its expression is diminished in the serum of septic patients and correlates with disease severity [27]. In models of acute lung injury, miR-143-3p has been demonstrated to dampen inflammatory responses [28]. Moreover, bioinformatic prediction identifies TLR4 as a putative target of miR-143-3p, raising the possibility that miR-143-3p may modulate macrophage polarization through direct TLR4 suppression.

Collectively, these observations underpin the central hypothesis of the present study: BMSC-Exos attenuate SRMI by conveying miR-143-3p to cardiac macrophages, wherein this miRNA directly targets TLR4, represses downstream NF-κB signaling, and thereby propels macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype. To interrogate this hypothesis, we employed complementary in vitro and in vivo approaches to systematically characterize the protective actions of BMSC-Exos and delineate the functional contribution of miR-143-3p. This investigation aims to elucidate a novel mechanistic axis underlying BMSC-Exo-based therapy for SRMI and to furnish a theoretical framework for the development of exosome- or miRNA-directed precision immunotherapeutics.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Animals

Specific pathogen-free (SPF) female C57BL/6 mice (8–10 weeks old, 20–25 g) were purchased from Double Helix (Guizhou) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guizhou, China). All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Guizhou Medical University (Approval No. 2503017) and were conducted in strict accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Female C57BL/6 mice were used because male mice develop a more severe inflammatory response to LPS with high early mortality [29, 30], which would have hindered therapeutic evaluation over the 7-day observation period. Mice were housed under controlled environmental conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 50 ± 10% humidity, 12 h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to standard chow and sterile drinking water.

Isolation, Culture, and Identification of BMSCs

Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) were isolated from the femurs and tibiae of mice. Bone marrow cavities were flushed with α-MEM (Gibco, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. Cells were cultured at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. Non-adherent cells were removed after 72 h, and the medium was replaced every 3 days. Upon reaching 80–90% confluence, cells were detached with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco) and passaged. Fourth-passage BMSCs were used for phenotype identification. For flow cytometry, cells were stained with APC-CD29, FITC-CD44, PE-CD73, PerCP-Cy5.5-CD105, PE-CD34, FITC-CD45, and APC-CD11b antibodies (all from BioLegend, USA), along with appropriate isotype controls. Data were acquired on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA). Positive markers (CD29, CD44, CD73, CD90, CD105) were expressed in > 90% of cells, while negative markers (CD34, CD45, CD11b) were < 2%, confirming BMSC identity.

BMSC-Exo Isolation

BMSCs at passages 3–5 were cultured for 48 h in α-MEM supplemented with 10% exosome-depleted FBS (System Biosciences, USA). Conditioned medium was harvested and subjected to sequential centrifugation at 300 × g for 10 min and 2,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C to eliminate cells and debris. The resulting supernatant was passed through a 0.45 μm filter (Merck Millipore, USA) and subsequently ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 70 min at 4 °C using an Optima XPN-100 ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter) equipped with a Type 70 Ti rotor. The pellet was washed once with pre-cooled PBS under identical ultracentrifugation conditions. The final exosomal pellet was resuspended in PBS, and protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, China). The stock concentration was adjusted to 200 µg/mL, and aliquots were stored at − 80 °C.

BMSC-Exo Characterization

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

A 10 µL droplet of exosome suspension was applied to a Formvar-coated copper grid and allowed to adsorb for 1 min at room temperature. Excess liquid was removed with filter paper. Negative staining was performed with 10 µL of 2% phosphotungstic acid (pH 7.0) for 1 min, after which the grid was air-dried for 5 min. Images were captured using an HT 7700 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Japan) operated at 80 kV.

Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA)

The exosome suspension was diluted 100- to 1,000-fold in sterile PBS to achieve a particle concentration within the optimal range of 1 × 10⁷–1 × 10⁹ particles/mL. NTA was performed using a NanoSight NS300 instrument (Malvern Panalytical, UK) equipped with a 405 nm laser. Three 30 s videos were recorded for each sample, and particle size distribution and concentration were analyzed using NTA 3.2 software.

Western Blot for Exosomal Markers

Approximately 30 µg of exosomal protein was mixed with 5× SDS loading buffer, denatured at 95 °C for 10 min, separated by 10% SDS-PAGE, and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD63, CD9, CD81, TSG101, HSP70, and Alix (all 1:1000; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000; Jackson ImmunoResearch, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (Millipore) and imaged with a ChemiDoc MP system (Bio-Rad, USA).

Cell Culture, Transfection, and Treatment

RAW 264.7 macrophages (ATCC, USA) were cultured in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. To induce M1 polarization, cells were stimulated with 1 µg/mL LPS (from E. coli O111:B4, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). For exosome or miRNA intervention, BMSC-Exos (final concentration 50 µg/mL) or miRNA mimics/inhibitors (final concentration 50 nM) were added 30 min after LPS stimulation. For transfection, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 3 × 10⁵ cells/well (≈ 60–70% confluence). miRNA mimics or inhibitors (50 nM) and Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher) were diluted separately in Opti-MEM, mixed, incubated for 15 min, and then added to cells. After 6 h, the medium was replaced with complete DMEM, and cells were cultured for an additional 48 h. Transfection efficiency was verified by qPCR for miR-143-3p expression.

Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay

The wild-type (WT) TLR4 3’-untranslated region (UTR) fragment containing the predicted miR-143-3p binding site (seed-matching sequence: 5’-…AUAUCCU…-3’) was synthesized and cloned into the pmirGLO dual-luciferase vector (Promega, USA). The mutant (MUT) construct was generated by replacing the seed-matching sequence with 5’-…CGGCGCAG…-3’ to disrupt miR-143-3p binding. Both constructs were verified by sequencing, and the complete insert sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S1. HEK293T cells (ATCC, USA) were co-transfected with 0.5 µg of the wild-type or mutant reporter plasmid and 50 nM miR-143-3p mimic or negative control (NC) mimic using Lipofectamine 3000. After 48 h, cells were lysed with Passive Lysis Buffer (Promega). Firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) on a GloMax 20/20 luminometer (Promega). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity.

Sepsis-Related Myocardial Injury Model and In Vivo Interventions

SRMI was induced by intraperitoneal injection of LPS (5 mg/kg body weight, dissolved in sterile saline). Sham-operated mice received an equivalent volume of sterile saline. For BMSC-Exo treatment, mice were randomly allocated to Sham, LPS, and LPS + BMSC-Exo groups (n = 6–8 per group). At 24 h after LPS challenge, mice in the LPS + BMSC-Exo group received a tail-vein injection of 500 µL BMSC-Exo stock solution (200 µg/mL, equivalent to 100 µg exosomal protein per mouse) [31–33]. The remaining groups received an equal volume of PBS. For miRNA intervention studies, mice were divided into Sham, LPS, LPS + miR-143-3p mimic (5 nmol/mouse in 100 µL PBS), and LPS + miR-143-3p inhibitor (10 nmol/mouse) groups; injections were administered immediately following LPS challenge. For TLR4 knockdown experiments, lentiviral particles (1 × 10⁷ TU/mouse, sh-NC or sh-TLR4) were delivered via tail-vein injection 72 h prior to LPS administration. The primary endpoint was set at 72 h post-LPS injection (48 h after BMSC-Exo or miRNA mimic/inhibitor administration), a time point at which cardiac dysfunction and inflammatory responses are known to peak. At this endpoint, echocardiography was performed under isoflurane anesthesia, after which mice were euthanized and blood and cardiac tissues were collected for histological examination, immunofluorescence staining, flow cytometric analysis, serum biomarker measurement, and cytokine profiling. Survival was monitored over a 7-day period.

Echocardiography

Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane (4% for induction) and positioned supine on a 37 °C heated platform. Chest hair was removed, and pre-warmed ultrasound gel was applied. Transthoracic echocardiography was performed using a Vevo 3100 system (VisualSonics, Canada) equipped with an MX550D transducer (40 MHz). Left ventricular (LV) M-mode images were acquired at the level of the papillary muscles. LV end-diastolic diameter (LVIDd) and end-systolic diameter (LVIDs) were measured using Vevo LAB software, and ejection fraction (EF) and fractional shortening (FS) were calculated. Measurements from three consecutive cardiac cycles were averaged. All analyses were conducted by an investigator blinded to experimental group allocation.

Histology and Immunofluorescence

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) Staining

Heart tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4–5 μm. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, differentiated with 1% acid alcohol, blued with 0.6% ammonia, stained with eosin for 2 min, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. Images were acquired under a light microscope (Olympus BX53). Interstitial edema was assessed on H&E-stained sections according to established criteria, including widened intercellular spaces, reduced eosinophilia of the extracellular matrix, and perivascular connective tissue separation. All sections were evaluated by two independent observers blinded to group allocation.

Masson’s Trichrome Staining

Paraffin sections were stained using a Masson’s trichrome staining kit (Zhongshan Goldenbridge, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Collagen fibers appeared blue, nuclei blue-black, and cardiomyocytes red. The percentage of collagen-positive area relative to the total myocardial area was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA).

Immunofluorescence

After deparaffinization and antigen retrieval (citrate buffer, pH 6.0, boiling for 15 min), sections were blocked with 5% BSA for 30 min and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against iNOS (1:200; Abcam, UK) or Arg1 (1:200; Cell Signaling Technology, USA). After washing, sections were incubated with Cy3-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:400; Jackson ImmunoResearch) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by DAPI (1 µg/mL) for 5 min. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX73) and merged with ImageJ.

Flow Cytometry

Fresh heart tissue was minced and digested with collagenase IV (2 mg/mL) and DNase I (0.1 mg/mL) in HBSS for 30 min at 37 °C with intermittent shaking. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 70-µm cell strainer, centrifuged, and subjected to red blood cell lysis. Cells were stained with APC-conjugated anti-CD11b (BioLegend) for surface labeling. For intracellular staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized using a Fixation/Permeabilization kit (eBioscience, USA), then stained with PE-conjugated anti-iNOS (M1) and FITC-conjugated anti-CD206 or PE-Cy7-conjugated anti-Arg1 (M2) antibodies (all from BioLegend). Data were acquired on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter) and analyzed with FlowJo V10 software. Macrophages were gated as CD11b⁺ cells, and the proportions of iNOS⁺ (M1) and CD206⁺/Arg1⁺ (M2) subsets were determined.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Serum levels of TNF-α, IL-6, IL-10, cardiac troponin T (cTnT), creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB), and myoglobin (MYO) were measured using commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ protocols (Elabscience, China for cytokines; Abcam, USA for myocardial injury markers). Absorbance was read at 450 nm using a BioTek Epoch microplate reader, and sample concentrations were extrapolated from standard curves.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells or tissues using RNAiso Plus (Takara, Japan). For miRNA detection, RNA was enriched using RNAiso for Small RNA (Takara). RNA concentration and purity were determined with a NanoDrop One (Thermo Fisher). Reverse transcription for mRNA was performed using PrimeScript RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara), and for miRNA using stem-loop primers and the Mir-X miRNA First-Strand Synthesis Kit (Takara). qPCR was carried out on a QuantStudio 6 Flex system (Applied Biosystems) using SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (Takara). GAPDH and U6 were used as internal controls for mRNA and miRNA, respectively. Primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (China). Relative expression was calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method.

Western Blotting for Signaling Pathway Proteins

Total protein was extracted from cells or heart tissues using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche, Switzerland). Protein concentration was measured with a BCA kit (Beyotime). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against TLR4 (1:1000), MyD88 (1:1000), p-NF-κB p65 (Ser536, 1:1000), total NF-κB p65 (1:1000), and GAPDH (1:5000) (all from Cell Signaling Technology). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, Jackson ImmunoResearch) for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were detected using ECL substrate (Millipore) and imaged with a ChemiDoc MP system. Band intensities were quantified using Image Lab software (Bio-Rad).

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All in vitro experiments were independently repeated at least three times. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. Multiple group comparisons were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. Survival data were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method with the Log-rank test. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance levels are indicated as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001; ns means non-significant where P > 0.05.

Results

Successful Isolation and Characterization of BMSCs and BMSC-Exos

Flow cytometric analysis confirmed that the isolated cells robustly expressed the BMSC-positive surface markers CD29, CD44, CD73, CD90, and CD105 (> 90% positive), while minimally expressing the hematopoietic lineage markers CD34, CD45, and CD11b (< 2% positive) (Fig. 1A). Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) revealed that BMSC-Exos exhibited a mean diameter of approximately 86.3 nm, with a predominant size distribution ranging from 70 to 120 nm (Fig. 1B). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) visualized typical cup-shaped vesicles measuring 30–100 nm in diameter (Fig. 1C). Western blotting further substantiated the enrichment of exosomal marker proteins—including CD9, CD63, CD81, ALIX, TSG101, and HSP70—within the isolated vesicle population (Fig. 1D). Collectively, these data verify the successful isolation of high-purity BMSC-Exos.

BMSC-Exos Promote M2 Polarization of LPS-Stimulated Macrophages In Vitro

Immunofluorescence imaging demonstrated that PKH67-labeled BMSC-Exos (green) were readily internalized by RAW 264.7 macrophages (stained with CD86, red), as evidenced by pronounced co-localization (Fig. 2A). Stimulation with LPS (1 µg/mL) for 24 h potently induced M1 polarization, reflected by elevated mRNA and protein levels of iNOS, TNF-α, and CXCL1, concomitant with diminished expression of the M2 markers Arg1 and Chi3l3 (Fig. 2B, C, E). Flow cytometry corroborated these findings, revealing an augmented proportion of iNOS⁺ (M1) cells and a reciprocal reduction in Arg1⁺ (M2) cells following LPS exposure (Fig. 2B, C). The original flow cytometry data files are available in Supplementary Data 1. Notably, co-incubation with BMSC-Exos (final concentration 50 µg/mL, added 30 min post-LPS) effectively reversed the LPS-elicited phenotypic switch: M1-associated transcripts/proteins were downregulated, M2-associated markers were upregulated, and the percentage of M2-polarized macrophages was restored (Fig. 2B, C, E). ELISA of culture supernatants further demonstrated that BMSC-Exos markedly diminished the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines IFN-β, TNF-α, and IL-6, while concurrently elevating the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (Fig. 2D). Taken together, these in vitro findings establish that BMSC-Exos suppress LPS-driven M1 polarization and actively promote an M2 phenotypic shift.

BMSC-Exos Attenuate LPS-Induced Sepsis-Related Myocardial Injury and Foster Cardiac M2 Macrophage Polarization In Vivo

Intraperitoneal administration of LPS (5 mg/kg) reliably induced a septic state. Twenty-four hours post-LPS challenge, mice in the treatment cohort received a single tail-vein injection of BMSC-Exos (100 µg exosomal protein per mouse). Compared with the Sham group, LPS-challenged mice exhibited reduced survival; BMSC-Exo treatment conferred survival benefit, improving 7-day survival from 41.7% to 83.3% (Fig. 3A). Immunofluorescence analysis of cardiac tissue revealed that LPS challenge led to heightened infiltration of iNOS⁺ M1 macrophages and a concomitant decrease in Arg1⁺ M2 macrophages (Fig. 3B). Flow cytometric quantification confirmed that BMSC-Exo administration robustly reduced the iNOS⁺/TNF-α⁺/CXCL1⁺ (M1) population while expanding the Arg1⁺/Chi3l3⁺ (M2) compartment within CD11b⁺ cells (Fig. 3C and D). Histological examination via H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining disclosed disordered myocardial fiber architecture, extensive inflammatory cell infiltrates, and increased collagen deposition in the LPS group, all of which were markedly ameliorated by BMSC-Exo treatment (Fig. 3E and F). Interstitial edema, characterized by widened intercellular spaces and pale eosinophilic staining of the extracellular matrix, was evident in the LPS group and was substantially attenuated following BMSC-Exo administration (Fig. 3E). Quantitative analysis of Masson’s trichrome-stained sections confirmed that the collagen-positive area was significantly increased in the LPS group compared with sham controls and was substantially reduced by BMSC-Exo treatment (P < 0.01; Fig. 3F), indicating that BMSC-Exos attenuate sepsis-induced myocardial fibrosis. Serological profiling indicated that BMSC-Exos significantly lowered circulating myocardial injury biomarkers cTnT, CK-MB, and myoglobin (MYO) (Fig. 3G), and reduced serum levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines IFN-β, TNF-α, and IL-6, while augmenting IL-10 concentrations (Fig. 3H). Echocardiographic assessment at 72 h post-LPS demonstrated pronounced cardiac dysfunction in LPS-treated mice (reductions in LVEF and LVFS), which was substantially rescued by BMSC-Exo intervention (Fig. 3I). These in vivo results confirm that BMSC-Exos improve cardiac performance, mitigate myocardial injury and inflammation, and promote local M2 macrophage polarization in septic mice.

MiR-143-3p is a Key MiRNA in BMSC-Exos Associated with Inflammatory Pathways

To identify the molecular cargo responsible for the immunomodulatory properties of BMSC-Exos, we conducted miRNA sequencing on BMSCs and their derived exosomes. Pearson correlation analysis attested to high inter-replicate reproducibility (R² > 0.95) (Fig. 4A), and density plot illustrated the overall miRNA expression distribution (Fig. 4B). A volcano plot unveiled a repertoire of differentially expressed miRNAs, among which miR-143-3p, miR-155-5p and miR-1246 ranked as some of the most significantly enriched in BMSC-Exos (log₂FC > 2, adjusted P < 0.05) (Fig. 4C). Hierarchical clustering heatmap further delineated the distinct miRNA expression profiles between BMSCs and BMSC-Exos (Fig. 4D). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of predicted target genes highlighted prominent terms including “inflammatory response,” “regulation of immune response,” and “cellular response to lipopolysaccharide” across Biological Process, Cellular Component, and Molecular Function categories (Fig. 4E). KEGG pathway classification and enrichment scatter plot implicated signaling cascades such as the MAPK and PI3K–Akt pathways, both of which operate downstream of TLR4/NF-κB signaling (Fig. 4F, G). Importantly, qRT-PCR validation confirmed that miR-143-3p, but not miR-155-5p and miR-1246, was robustly reduced in BMSC-Exos under LPS stimulation (Fig. 4H). Collectively, these results identify miR-143-3p as a candidate effector miRNA enriched in BMSC-Exos and closely associated with TLR4-related inflammatory pathways.

MiR-143-3p Regulates Macrophage Polarization and Inflammatory Cytokine Secretion In Vitro

To functionally validate miR-143-3p, we performed gain- and loss-of-function experiments in RAW 264.7 cells. qRT-PCR confirmed efficient overexpression and inhibition of miR-143-3p following transfection (Fig. 5A). Immunofluorescence staining for CD86 (M1 marker) revealed that miR-143-3p overexpression attenuated LPS-induced CD86 signal intensity, whereas miR-143-3p inhibition enhanced it (Fig. 5B). Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that transfection with a miR-143-3p mimic (50 nM) substantially decreased the proportion of M1 macrophages (CXCL1⁺, iNOS⁺, TNF-α⁺) and reciprocally increased the M2 population (Arg1⁺, Chi3l3⁺) following LPS stimulation; conversely, a miR-143-3p inhibitor (50 nM) exerted opposite effects (Fig. 5C). qRT-PCR analysis confirmed that the mimic upregulated M2-associated transcripts (Arg1, Chi3l3) and downregulated M1-associated markers (CXCL1, iNOS, TNF-α), while the inhibitor partially reversed the pro-M2 transcriptional program conferred by BMSC-Exos (Fig. 5D). ELISA of culture supernatants further demonstrated that the miR-143-3p mimic suppressed the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines IFN-β, TNF-α, and IL-6, and enhanced IL-10 production; the inhibitor abrogated the anti-inflammatory effects of BMSC-Exos (Fig. 5E). These data establish that miR-143-3p is both necessary and sufficient to drive macrophage M2 polarization and attenuate inflammatory cytokine secretion in vitro.

In Vivo Modulation of MiR-143-3p Affects Myocardial Injury and Cardiac Macrophage Polarization

To interrogate the in vivo function of miR-143-3p, we administered a miR-143-3p mimic (5 nmol/mouse) or inhibitor (10 nmol/mouse) via tail-vein injection immediately following LPS challenge. qRT-PCR confirmed robust myocardial miR-143-3p overexpression in mimic-treated mice (Fig. 6A). The miR-143-3p mimic conferred a survival advantage compared with the LPS-alone group, whereas the inhibitor did not improve survival (Fig. 6B). Echocardiographic evaluation revealed that the miR-143-3p mimic significantly restored LVEF and LVFS relative to the LPS group, whereas the inhibitor exacerbated cardiac dysfunction (Fig. 6C). Immunofluorescence staining of cardiac tissue showed that miR-143-3p mimic reduced iNOS⁺ M1 macrophage infiltration and increased Arg1⁺ M2 macrophage abundance; the inhibitor produced opposite effects (Fig. 6D). Flow cytometric quantification confirmed that the mimic decreased M1 markers (CXCL1, iNOS, TNF-α) and increased M2 markers (Arg1, Chi3l3) in cardiac macrophages, whereas the inhibitor reversed this pattern (Fig. 6E). Histopathological analysis via H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining demonstrated that the miR-143-3p mimic attenuated LPS-induced myocardial inflammation and collagen deposition, while the inhibitor aggravated these pathological changes (Fig. 6F and G). Serological profiling indicated that the mimic lowered serum myocardial injury biomarkers cTnT, CK-MB, and myoglobin (Fig. 6H), and reduced circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines IFN-β, TNF-α, and IL-6, while elevating IL-10; the inhibitor exerted opposing effects (Fig. 6I). These in vivo findings corroborate that miR-143-3p is a critical protective factor in SRMI, capable of modulating cardiac macrophage polarization and mitigating myocardial injury.

MiR-143-3p Directly Targets TLR4, and TLR4 Knockdown Promotes M2 Polarization In Vitro

To identify the downstream effectors mediating the anti-inflammatory actions of miR-143-3p, we performed bioinformatic target prediction using TargetScan 8.0 and miRDB databases. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the predicted targets revealed significant overrepresentation of genes associated with the MAPK, PI3K–Akt, and Ras signaling pathways (see also Fig. 4F, G). Notably, these cascades converge downstream of Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), a master pattern-recognition receptor that recognizes LPS and drives pro-inflammatory cytokine expression via the MyD88/NF-κB axis. Given the central role of TLR4 in sepsis-related inflammation and its predicted interaction with miR-143-3p, we prioritized TLR4 as a candidate target for experimental validation.

Dual-luciferase reporter assays confirmed that the miR-143-3p mimic significantly repressed the luciferase activity of a reporter construct harboring the wild-type TLR4 3′-UTR, while leaving the activity of a mutant version unaffected, thereby validating direct binding (Fig. 7A). Western blot analysis demonstrated that miR-143-3p negatively regulates TLR4 protein expression both in RAW 264.7 macrophages (Fig. 7B) and in C57BL/6 mouse myocardial tissue (Fig. 7C). Immunofluorescence staining for CD86 revealed that stable knockdown of TLR4 using lentiviral shRNA (sh-TLR4) attenuated LPS-induced M1 polarization (Fig. 7D). Flow cytometric analysis confirmed that TLR4 knockdown reduced M1 marker expression (CXCL1, iNOS, TNF-α) and increased M2 marker expression (Arg1, Chi3l3) compared with LPS and LPS + sh-NC controls (Fig. 7E). qRT-PCR analysis showed that TLR4 knockdown partially reversed the LPS-induced upregulation of M1-associated transcripts (CXCL1, iNOS, TNF-α) and downregulation of M2-associated transcripts (Arg1, Chi3l3) (Fig. 7F). ELISA of culture supernatants demonstrated that TLR4 knockdown decreased pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion (IFN-β, TNF-α, IL-6) and increased IL-10 production (Fig. 7G). Western blotting validated efficient TLR4 knockdown and further revealed that sh-TLR4 suppressed LPS-induced activation of the MyD88/NF-κB pathway, as evidenced by reduced MyD88 and phospho-p65 (p-p65) protein levels (Fig. 7H). These results demonstrate that TLR4 constitutes a key downstream effector through which miR-143-3p operates, and that targeting TLR4 is sufficient to drive macrophage polarization toward an M2 phenotype.

TLR4 Knockdown In Vivo Induces M2 Polarization and Alleviates Sepsis-Related Myocardial Injury

To substantiate the pivotal role of TLR4 in vivo, we achieved systemic TLR4 knockdown by tail-vein delivery of lentivirus encoding sh-TLR4 (1 × 10⁷ TU/mouse) 72 h prior to LPS challenge. TLR4 depletion (LPS + sh-TLR4 group) significantly improved mouse survival compared with the LPS + sh-NC cohort (Fig. 8A). Echocardiography demonstrated that TLR4 knockdown substantially restored LVEF and LVFS relative to LPS controls (Fig. 8B). Immunofluorescence analysis of cardiac tissue revealed that TLR4 knockdown reduced iNOS⁺ M1 macrophage infiltration and increased Arg1⁺ M2 macrophage abundance (Fig. 8C). Flow cytometric quantification confirmed that TLR4 depletion decreased M1 markers (CXCL1, iNOS, TNF-α) and increased M2 markers (Arg1, Chi3l3) in cardiac macrophages (Fig. 8D). Serological profiling indicated that TLR4 knockdown lowered serum levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines IFN-β, TNF-α, and IL-6, while elevating IL-10 (Fig. 8E). Histopathological examination via H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining showed that TLR4 depletion mitigated LPS-induced myocardial inflammation and fibrosis (Fig. 8F and G). Serum myocardial injury biomarkers cTnT, CK-MB, and myoglobin were also significantly reduced in the sh-TLR4 group (Fig. 8H). Western blot analysis of cardiac tissue confirmed that TLR4 knockdown suppressed LPS-induced activation of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling axis, as indicated by decreased MyD88 and phospho-p65 levels (Fig. 8I). A schematic summarizing the BMSC-Exo/miR-143-3p/TLR4/NF-κB axis is presented in Fig. 8G. This in vivo rescue experiment definitively establishes that targeted reduction of TLR4 expression faithfully recapitulates the protective effects afforded by miR-143-3p overexpression or BMSC-Exo treatment—namely, promoting M2 polarization, dampening inflammation, and enhancing cardiac function—thereby positioning TLR4 as an indispensable node within this therapeutic axis.

Discussion

Sepsis-related myocardial injury (SRMI), also referred to as septic cardiomyopathy, is a prevalent and life-threatening complication of sepsis, with uncontrolled inflammation and immune dysregulation constituting core pathogenic drivers [5]. In the present study, we delineated the therapeutic efficacy of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (BMSC-Exos) in SRMI and uncovered a previously uncharacterized mechanistic axis: BMSC-Exos convey miR-143-3p to macrophages, wherein this miRNA directly targets TLR4, suppresses the downstream MyD88/NF-κB cascade, and consequently fosters M2 polarization while attenuating myocardial injury.

BMSC-Exos promote M2 polarization and confer cardioprotection. Our in vitro experiments demonstrated that BMSC-Exos are efficiently internalized by macrophages and effectively redirect LPS-stimulated cells from a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype toward an anti-inflammatory, reparative M2 state, as reflected by coordinated shifts in surface marker expression, cytokine secretion profiles, and gene transcription. These observations align with prior reports indicating that MSC-derived exosomes modulate macrophage polarization in models of myocardial infarction and ischemia-reperfusion injury [34, 35]. In the LPS-induced septic mouse model, tail-vein administration of BMSC-Exos significantly improved survival, preserved cardiac contractile function, lowered circulating myocardial injury biomarkers, and mitigated histological evidence of cardiac damage. Notably, BMSC-Exo treatment augmented the proportion of M2-polarized macrophages within the myocardium while concurrently diminishing M1 infiltration, underscoring that immunomodulation of the local cardiac microenvironment is integral to the observed protection. The present study did not distinguish between embryonic derived resident (CCR2⁻) and monocyte derived recruited (CCR2⁺) macrophages. In septic hearts, the CCL7/CCR2 axis drives a massive infiltration of CCR2⁺ monocytes that rapidly become the numerically dominant macrophage population, while resident subsets—including reparative MHC IIʰⁱCCR2⁻ and homeostatic MHC IIˡᵒCCR2⁻ populations—are proportionally reduced [36, 37]. Because activated phagocytes exhibit enhanced exosome uptake, the recruited monocyte derived macrophages likely represent the principal targets of BMSC Exos in our model. Lineage tracing and single cell transcriptomic studies will be required to definitively resolve the contribution of each macrophage subset to the observed therapeutic effects.

MiR-143-3p constitutes a critical effector cargo of BMSC-Exos. Through miRNA sequencing coupled with bioinformatic screening, we identified miR-143-3p as one of the most highly enriched miRNAs in BMSC-Exos. Intriguingly, its abundance was markedly diminished in circulating exosomes isolated from septic mice relative to healthy controls, suggestive of a pathophysiological depletion of this protective signal during sepsis. Gain- and loss-of-function experiments established that miR-143-3p is both necessary and sufficient for the M2-polarizing and anti-inflammatory effects of BMSC-Exos in vitro. Furthermore, systemic delivery of a miR-143-3p mimic phenocopied the cardioprotective effects of BMSC-Exos in vivo, whereas administration of a specific miR-143-3p inhibitor aggravated cardiac dysfunction and exacerbated inflammatory injury. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to identify miR-143-3p as a functional exosomal payload mediating BMSC-Exo efficacy in SRMI.

The role of miR-143-3p in cardiac pathophysiology appears to be highly context-dependent. In myocardial infarction, miR-143-3p was shown to promote cardiac fibrosis by targeting sprouty3 and activating the p38, ERK, and JNK pathways in cardiac fibroblasts [38]. In ischemia-reperfusion injury, miR-143-3p exacerbated cardiomyocyte apoptosis through direct suppression of Bcl-2 and subsequent activation of the mitochondrial apoptotic cascade [39]. In both sterile injury models, endogenous miR-143-3p exerted detrimental effects. By contrast, in infection-driven inflammatory conditions, miR-143-3p appears to play a protective role. Circulating miR-143-3p is downregulated in septic patients and correlates with disease severity [27], and its anti-inflammatory activity has been demonstrated in acute lung injury [28]. Our findings extend this protective paradigm by showing that exogenously delivered miR-143-3p, via BMSC-derived exosomes, targets TLR4 in macrophages to suppress MyD88/NF-κB signaling and promote M2 polarization, thereby attenuating sepsis-induced myocardial inflammation. This functional divergence likely reflects the distinct pathological drivers (sterile injury and fibrosis versus systemic hyperinflammation), the cellular source and delivery mode of the miRNA (endogenous expression versus exosomal transfer), and the engagement of different downstream targets (SPRY3 and Bcl-2 versus TLR4) in a cell-type-specific manner. Future studies employing cell-type-selective delivery strategies will be essential to fully dissect the context-dependent functions of miR-143-3p in cardiac disease.

Although miR-143-3p mimic treatment significantly reduced serum cytokine and cardiac injury marker levels, these parameters remained modestly elevated compared with sham controls, yet echocardiographic indices of systolic function were preserved at sham-comparable levels. The incomplete normalization of circulating markers likely reflects the multifactorial nature of sepsis-driven inflammation, in which several parallel pathways—including the NLRP3 inflammasome, oxidative stress, complement cascades, and metabolic reprogramming—operate alongside TLR4/NF-κB signaling and cannot be fully antagonized by TLR4 inhibition alone [12, 40]. The restoration of cardiac function despite this residual inflammatory state may be explained by the non-linear relationship between inflammatory burden and myocardial contractility, whereby even a partial cytokine reduction relieves sepsis-induced myocardial depression given the heart’s functional reserve [5]. Additionally, exosomal miRNAs have been shown to exert direct cardiomyocyte-protective effects independent of immune modulation. For example, miR-29b-3p from macrophage-derived exosomes targets GSK-3β to promote autophagy in cardiomyocytes during septic injury [33]. It is therefore plausible that BMSC-Exo-derived miR-143-3p may similarly confer direct protection on cardiomyocytes, a possibility that warrants further investigation.

Mechanistically, miR-143-3p directly engages TLR4 and restrains NF-κB signaling. Dual-luciferase reporter assays validated direct binding of miR-143-3p to the 3′-UTR of TLR4 mRNA. Western blot analyses revealed that either miR-143-3p overexpression or BMSC-Exo treatment substantially reduced TLR4 protein abundance both in cultured macrophages and in cardiac tissue, whereas miR-143-3p inhibition exerted the opposite effect. TLR4 silencing via shRNA closely recapitulated the phenotypic consequences of miR-143-3p overexpression—namely, enhanced M2 polarization and attenuated inflammatory responses—and, importantly, partially rescued the detrimental effects elicited by miR-143-3p inhibition. Concomitantly, both miR-143-3p upregulation and TLR4 knockdown diminished MyD88 expression and attenuated NF-κB p65 phosphorylation, confirming that the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB axis represents the principal downstream pathway engaged. These findings are entirely congruent with the established role of TLR4 as a master regulator governing M1 macrophage polarization [11, 41, 42] and demonstrate that miR-143-3p executes its immunomodulatory function via post-transcriptional suppression of this signaling cascade.

Comparison with existing literature. Several investigations have documented that MSC-derived exosomes convey alternative miRNAs—including miR-1-3p and miR-19b-3p—to modulate macrophage behavior in cardiovascular pathologies [43, 44]. Our work, however, furnishes the first evidence specifically implicating miR-143-3p as an essential exosomal cargo in the setting of SRMI. Moreover, the integrative strategy combining a “source enrichment–disease depletion” screening paradigm with rigorous epistatic rescue experiments (TLR4 knockdown) establishes a compelling causal nexus linking the miRNA, its molecular target, and the resultant protective phenotype. Such mechanistic granularity is often absent from analogous studies. We recognize that BMSC-Exos, M2 polarization, and TLR4/NF-κB signaling have each been investigated in other disease settings. The contribution of the present work is therefore the demonstration that BMSC-Exo-delivered miR-143-3p can engage the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB axis to reprogram macrophages in SRMI. Although this finding represents an incremental step, it provides a basis for further exploration of exosome-mediated immunomodulation, including its potential intersection with macrophage metabolic reprogramming in sepsis.

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, the in vivo dose of BMSC Exos (100 µg/mouse) and the in vitro concentration (50 µg/mL) were selected based on preliminary testing and are consistent with effective doses of MSC derived exosomes reported in recent studies (100 µg/mouse and 50 µg/mL, respectively, in myocardial infarction models; 100 µg/mouse in sepsis induced lung injury) [31–33]. Nonetheless, a formal dose response evaluation was not performed, and the optimal therapeutic regimen remains to be defined. Second, while the LPS-induced endotoxemia model is highly reproducible and well-suited for mechanistic dissection, it does not fully recapitulate the polymicrobial complexity inherent to clinical sepsis; the cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) model is generally regarded as more clinically representative [45]. Third, cardiac function was evaluated at a single 72 h post-LPS time point, a window during which inflammatory responses and contractile dysfunction are known to peak [46]; longitudinal assessments at additional time points would afford a more comprehensive portrayal of functional recovery dynamics. Fourth, although BMSC-Exo treatment exhibited a clear trend toward improved 7-day survival (41.7% versus 83.3%), this difference did not attain statistical significance, likely attributable to the modest group size (n = 6 per cohort). Comparable non-significant survival trends have been observed in other mesenchymal stem cell investigations, and larger sample sizes would be anticipated to confer statistical power [47]. Fifth, only female mice were used. This choice was based on the well-documented higher susceptibility of male mice to LPS-induced mortality [29, 30] and the reduced aggression- and stress-related variability in female animals; the generalizability of these findings to male subjects remains to be determined. Sixth, although dual-luciferase reporter, expression regulation, and independent TLR4 knockdown experiments collectively support TLR4 as a direct target of miR-143-3p, a co-transfection rescue experiment was not performed; such an experiment would further clarify whether the effects of miR-143-3p are exclusively mediated through TLR4 suppression. Additional caveats include the fact that exosomes harbor a plethora of bioactive constituents beyond miR-143-3p, which may act synergistically, and that our investigation predominantly centered on macrophages; direct effects upon cardiomyocytes or other cardiac-resident cell populations cannot be excluded. Finally, clinical translation will necessitate overcoming challenges pertaining to large-scale exosome manufacturing, batch-to-batch standardization, and long-term safety assessment.

Conclusions

In summary, the present study establishes that BMSC-Exos mitigate SRMI through the intercellular transfer of miR-143-3p to macrophages. Within recipient cells, miR-143-3p directly targets TLR4 and abrogates downstream MyD88/NF-κB signaling, thereby driving an M2 phenotypic shift and curtailing cardiac inflammation and injury. This work unveils a previously unrecognized intercellular communication axis and furnishes a robust experimental foundation for the development of exosome-based or miRNA-directed precision therapeutics targeting SRMI.
