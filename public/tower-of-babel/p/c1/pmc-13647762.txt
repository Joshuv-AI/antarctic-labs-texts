Transnational strategies to promote female circumcision practice: voices from ghanaian families with relatives living in Europe and North America
Authors: Alhassan Abdullah, Felix Mensah, Aisha Adam Mahama, Ebenezer Cudjoe, Peter Dwumah, Clifton R Emery
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe results from qualitative studies have revealed that some African migrants subject their young girls to female genital cutting (FGC) before or after migrating to high-income countries, such as the USA and the UK. This raises important questions as to why some African migrants continue to adhere to FGC after migration, given that the practice is socially rejected in destination countries and, in some cases, already criminalised in countries of origin. Little has been documented on the motivational factors for African migrants to continue promoting the practices even after migrating to Europe and North America. Consequently, we investigated the strategies used by Ghanaian families in motivating commitment to FGC norms and practices among their relatives in Europe and North America.MethodsUsing a narrative qualitative research approach, we interviewed 13 family members in rural Ghana who strongly supported FGC and have family members who have migrated to Europe and North America. The interviews were analysed in NVivo using the narrative thematic analysis strategy.FindingsThe interviews revealed both targeted and non-deliberate strategies, such as pressure from family members, building confidant relationships, and encouraging participation in African community associations. Family members became confidants and a source of connection for their relatives who felt isolated or experienced racism. However, they capitalised on the bonds and trusting relationships to influence their relatives to support FGC through daily conversations.ConclusionThe findings give important insights into how norms are promoted across borders, highlighting the need to strengthen existing transnational collaborative efforts to address FGC, including cross-border legislation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12905-026-04690-x.Keywords: FGC, Female genital cutting, Transnational, Female circumcision, High-income countries

Introduction

After decades of research into the negative consequences of female genital cutting (FGC), the practice remains a global public health, child protection, and human rights concern [1–5]. Current estimates suggest that over 230 million girls and women worldwide are living with the consequences of FGC, with more than 144 million of them residing in Africa [6]. Evidence from a meta-analysis involving 406,068 women across 30 countries and 296,267 girls across 25 countries revealed that over 36% of women aged 15–49 years have had part(s) of their vulva cut [7]. More than half of these cases (53%) occur in Sub-Saharan Africa, including Ghana [8].

FGC is considered a traditional practice in some communities in about 30 African countries (including Ghana) and is sustained by socio-economic, political and cultural normative factors [9–11]. However, growing research evidence has revealed that several African migrants in high-income countries (such as the USA, the UK, and Australia) are living with FGC, though the practice is non-normative and criminalised in these developed countries [12–14]. The European Commission reported over 600,000 women living with FGC across its 13 member states [15]. Nationally, the number of women and girls living with FGC is estimated at 70,000 in Germany, 87,600 in Italy, and 53,000 in Australia [10, 15]. The number of women and girls affected or at risk of FGC is estimated at over 500,000 in the United States, and 180,000 girls are at risk within the European Union [15, 16]. Some qualitative studies in Australia and the USA reported findings from African migrants who subject their young girls to FGC or transport them to Africa to undergo FGC because of their commitment to the cultural norms underpinning FGC [10–13, 17, 18]. It is perplexing, however, to conceive why an African immigrant would support the practice of FGC in a country such as the USA, the UK, or Australia, where the practice is non-normative and outlawed. This research seeks to answer this question by exploring the strategies and measures Ghanaian families use to influence their relatives in Europe and North America to support the norms and practice of FGC. Specifically, the research seeks to achieve the following objective:

To explore the strategies Ghanaian families use to motivate and promote FGC among their relatives living in Europe and North America.

To explore the strategies Ghanaian families use to motivate and promote FGC among their relatives living in Europe and North America.

FGC and cultural normative underpinnings: theory and empirical evidence

Female genital cutting (FGC) refers to a procedure that involves the removal of part(s) of the vulva or other injury to the genital organs of a female child/young girl (mostly aged 0–15) for religious, social, or cultural reasons [10]. The practice has historical and cultural roots in several communities in Africa and Asia. Notably, FGC is motivated by the cultural and normative beliefs that cutting or removing the genital organs of a young girl will minimise their sexual drive, thereby enabling them to remain chaste until marriage [19, 20]. Thus, FGC is linked to the norm of marriageability, because girls who are cut are deemed to have stable marriages due to their low risk of promiscuity [21]. In some communities, FGC is sustained by traditional norms that regard the practice as a symbol of transition for girls from childhood to adulthood [19]. Elbendary et al. [22] reported that nearly one-third of their study participants intended to circumcise their daughters. Their decisions were driven by beliefs about purity, marriageability, and conformity to cultural and religious expectations. These normative beliefs provide a logical justification and rationale for people to commit to FGC.

The continuation of FGC norms and their practice is also influenced by social sanctions that incentivise FGC in communities where it is normative. For example, research evidence suggests that communal recognition, honour, and respect given to families and women who undergo FGC are among the social incentives that sustain FGC norms and practices in Africa [19]. Stigma for failing to undergo FGC, for failing to find a suitable husband, and for being ostracised from the community are notable punitive sanctions used to promote adherence to FGC norms in some African communities [21–23]. Studies have revealed that failure to undergo FGC in African communities, where FGC is culturally normative, can evoke shameful feelings among young girls, their parents, and the extended family [23]. Others may feel lonely or isolated or like they do not belong and may be perceived as having sinned against their tribe and God (see 21,23). It is evident that these social sanctions, including punitive measures, are structured within the African communities where the practice is considered normative and supported by some community members. Hence, it is perplexing to understand why and how African migrants in Europe and North American countries may feel obliged to support FGC, since the sanction systems and practices are absent in those contexts.

Migration and FGC: a theoretical analysis

The impact of migration on FGC is theorised to be positive, particularly for African migrants moving to Europe and North American countries. Theoretically, the social convention theory posits that a common convention or normative practice, such as FGC, could be abandoned by migrants because they believe that people in the new environment no longer practise it or do not support it [24]. This theoretical postulation shows that African migrants in Europe and North American countries are more likely to abandon the practice due to the absence of community structures that motivate commitment to the practice. Indeed, empirical findings revealed positive attitudinal changes towards abandoning FGC among African migrants from Somalia, Sudan, Ghana, Egypt, Eritrea, Togo, and Guinea living in Spain, the Netherlands, Sweden, and other Western countries [17, 25–28]. Participants in Pastor-Bravo et al.’s [26] study revealed that the education they received on FGC in Spain, the new laws on FGC, and the new social norms in Spain influenced their shift in attitude. These factors underscore the positive influence of the migration context in changing commitment to FGC norms and practices. In contrast, the positive effects of migration also suggest that those who continue to practise FGC post-migration may be influenced by external factors, such as pressures from family members in Africa (cf.12, [29]). This research will explore some of these external factors by engaging with families living in Africa.

Promoting commitment to FGC Norms: a focus on high-income countries

Although we did not locate any empirical research evidence exploring how family members in Ghana influence their relatives in Europe and North American countries to support FGC, limited findings from some African countries provide evidence showing how commitments are motivated in a transnational context. In a qualitative study among Somali migrants in Norway, Johansen [30] demonstrated that individuals often navigate contradictory norms. African migrants reported strong disapproval of cutting yet hesitated to confront relatives back home who endorsed the practice. Some of these migrants argued they could not challenge their family members due to insecurities about challenging family authority (ibid.). A similar study among Somali and Sudanese migrants in Norway revealed instances in which pressure from extended families, including in-laws, motivated them to support FGC [31]. Families feared rejection or dishonour if their daughters were uncircumcised, and women reported that potential in-laws often questioned their suitability for marriage on this basis. Gutiérrez-García et al. [32] reported that silence around FGC was ingrained from childhood, where girls were denied open information and were socialised to accept the practice as necessary. This silence enables family members to influence others to support FGC, regardless of where they are living.

Alhassan et al. [33] revealed that religion, marriageability, decency, and sexuality remain central reasons for sustaining the practice in Europe, just as in the origin countries. Also, Gele et al. [34] provided quantitative evidence from Norway, indicating a strong association between perceived religious obligation and support for FGC practices (OR 47.3, CI: 17.6–126.7). In the same study, respondents who regarded FGC as a prerequisite for a trustworthy marriage (OR 2.02, CI: 1.10–3.71) or who valued the Sunna form of FGC (OR 14.03, CI: 6.72–29.29) were more likely to support the continuation of FGC in high-income countries (ibid.). Kawous et al. [35], in their study among African migrants in the Netherlands, found that some families fear relatives will cut their daughters without consent, while others deliberately travel back to their countries of origin during holidays to perform the procedure, where prosecution is less likely. Some African migrants reported how a lack of knowledge, family pressure, and even aesthetic preferences reinforce the commitment to FGC among Africans in Spain [36].

Study context: Ghana

FGC in Ghana presents a paradox of legal prohibition alongside cultural normative expectations. Ghana criminalised FGC in 1994, imposing a minimum three-year prison sentence on circumcisers and others who facilitate the act (Criminal Code, Section 69 A). Despite this clear punitive framework and progress in reducing the prevalence of FGC in Ghana, the practice remains a significant concern in the country’s northern and rural communities [37, 38]. Historically, FGC served as a definitive rite of passage into womanhood among certain ethnic groups, where traditional circumcisers held significant social status as the custodians of this transition [37, 38]. According to the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (GDHS) [39], the national prevalence of FGC among women aged 15–49 is 2.4%. However, there is significant regional variation, with prevalence rates as high as 32.5% in the Upper West Region and 13% in the Upper East Region [37–40]. Studies in Ghana underscore the role of cultural and social norms in influencing FGC practices (see 37,42). For example, Sakeah et al.’s [38] study in the Bawku Municipality and Pusiga District found that older women and peers actively reinforced FGC through pressure and sanctions. These key promoters often frame the practice not as a violation of law, but as a preservation of ethnic purity and moral standing.

In the context of transnational processes sustaining FGC among Africans, Ghana is a crucial setting given the growing migration of Ghanaians to Europe and North America. In 2020, about one million Ghanaian-born citizens lived abroad, representing 3.2% of the national population [41]. Nearly half of these one million people resided in five countries: the United States (~ 201,000), the United Kingdom (~ 131,000), Italy (~ 55,000), Germany (~ 33,000), and Canada (~ 25,000) [41]. This sizeable diaspora population increases the likelihood that cultural practices, including FGC, are negotiated across borders, making Ghana central to understanding how origin-country norms and practices, such as FGC, manifest in high-income countries.

Methods

Study design

The narrative qualitative approach guided this investigation and has been implemented in line with the interpretivist qualitative paradigm [42]. This paradigm enables qualitative researchers to adopt specific approaches, such as narrative, to understand how people make sense of an experience or event and interpret it [42]. Narrative researchers have a key focus on how stories are narrated, the nature of the stories, and the meanings people attribute to the stories [43, 44]. According to Riessman [44], the narrative qualitative approach is appropriate for research that seeks to elicit stories about lived experiences, events, or social phenomena. The narrative qualitative approach was therefore considered appropriate for exploring stories on how family members in Ghana promote commitment to FGC among their relatives who have migrated to Europe and North America. The use of the narrative approach enabled the researchers to ask questions that prompted participants to share stories about specific interactions with family members in the selected countries related to FGC.

Participants and recruitment

The participants were 13 women who were purposively selected from a national survey on kinship care and parenting practices in Ghana, based on specific eligibility criteria. The survey was conducted from November 2024 to April 2025 (see anonymised, 2025). It included 835 kinship caregivers from 18 communities across four regions in Ghana, who were randomly sampled using the probability-proportional-to-size sampling technique. The survey included items measuring kinship caregivers’ support for the practice of FGC, including for girls under their care, their FGC history, and beliefs and attitudes towards FGC. Examples of the FGC questions and statements included: (1) “I support the practice of female genital cutting for girls (including the children under my care) in my community”; (2) “I support the practice of female genital cutting for girls (including my own daughter) in my community”; and (3) “I believe female genital cutting should continue.” Caregivers indicated their endorsement based on a four-point Likert scale: 1 = Strongly Disagree; 2 = Disagree; 3 = Agree; 4 = Strongly Agree. Eighty-eight kinship caregivers from the survey responded “Agree” or “Strongly Agree” to the FGC-related questions. We contacted these 88 women via the mobile numbers they provided in the initial survey to confirm whether they had any relatives who had migrated to countries outside Ghana and if they would be interested in participating in a follow-up qualitative interview. Only 17 of the 88 caregivers confirmed they had relatives who had migrated to countries outside Ghana. We defined relatives as nuclear family members or extended family members with whom they have close relationships. Thirteen out of the 17 eligible women agreed to be interviewed. Four declined to participate without providing a reason.

A second set of consent documents, including a participant information sheet, was provided to participants for approval after the content was explained verbally by a member of the research team. The information sheet detailed the sensitive nature of the topic, the potential traumatic distress from sharing stories about FGC, and support services available to participants. It also stressed the participant’s right to withdraw from the interview at any time and to decline to answer specific questions. Because the practice of FGC is criminalised in Ghana under Section 69 A of Ghana’s Criminal Code, 1960 (Act 29), the information sheet highlighted the researchers’ obligation to report the participants’ involvement in or perpetration of FGC under the mandatory reporting obligation in the Children’s Act of Ghana (Act 560). The researchers also explained that they had no reporting obligations in cases where the participants had experienced FGC during their childhood. The consent form and information sheet were also read aloud to participants before starting interviews. The research protocols and procedures obtained approval from the Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology, Ghana.

In-depth narrative interviews

In-depth interviews were conducted with the caregivers using a semi-structured interview guide. Silverman [42] argued that semi-structured interviews allow researchers to explore issues in depth and to use probing and paraphrasing techniques for detailed insights. Although the study focused on the participants’ stories about how they motivate their relatives who have migrated to countries outside Ghana to support FGC norms and practices, the initial questions focused on traditional cultural practices in Ghana, such as festivals and naming ceremonies, before moving to questions on FGC. This strategy was implemented to minimise the risk of social desirability bias. Examples of questions on the interview guide included: (1) You indicated your belief in the continuation of FGC as a cultural practice; can you share with me how you are supporting your relatives who are living outside Ghana to also support the practice? (2) What are the things you and the family have been doing to make sure they do not abandon the practice? (3) Can you share examples of the conversations you have been having with those relatives about maintaining Ghanaian cultural practices, including FGC? The full interview guide for the study is provided in the supplementary materials section.

Interviews with the 13 women were conducted at their residences or private locations chosen by the participants. Two researchers (a female and a male), who were involved in the survey, conducted the interviews. The female researcher led the interviews, while the male researcher took notes and memos. During the consent process, the research participants were asked whether they preferred to be interviewed by a male or female researcher and whether they would like a second interviewer to be present during the interviews. All confirmed the female researcher as their preference and indicated their approval for the male participant to be present during the interviews. All interviews were conducted in the local Twi language, the common lingua franca in the study areas, and were recorded on a mobile phone for transcription purposes. The interviews averaged 63 min and were held between May and June 2025. A 40 cedi (~ 2.00 USD at the time) gift (cash) was given to each participant to compensate for their time.

Data coding and analysis

All interviews were translated into English and transcribed concurrently by the two researchers who conducted the interviews. Before beginning the analysis, we read and vetted the transcripts to familiarise ourselves with the content and ensure its accuracy. The transcripts were imported into NVivo 14 for analysis. We then followed Fraser’s [45] narrative thematic analysis strategy to generate themes from the interview data, focusing on stories, responses, and narratives about FGC. In doing so, two researchers independently created initial labels for segmented stories based on a line-by-line reading of the transcripts using the code function in NVivo. The researchers also developed analytic memos in NVivo documenting the reflections on the coded quotes (narratives). Examples of labels for the segmented stories included: “threatened them”, “African community helps”, and “bond and trust”. The segmented stories were reviewed and compared at project meetings. An 88% similarity (inter-rater reliability) was recorded after comparing the labels generated by the two researchers. The labels and associated segmented stories were then refined at project meetings, involving three researchers, to create holistic stories (known as themes). The themes were created by merging segmented stories that shared similar ideas or meanings using the merge and “drag and drop” functions in NVivo. The final themes and stories are presented in the findings section.

Key themes

Themes from the narratives on FGC motivation strategies were: (1) Protect cultural identity; (2) Pressure from family heads; (3) Confidant relationship; and (4) Participation in community events. The themes represented the strategies family members used to promote FGC among their relatives who had migrated to Europe and North America. Detailed discussions of the themes with direct quotes from participants are presented below.

Protect cultural identity

All 13 participants confirmed they had indirectly promoted commitment to the norms of FGC through their daily and weekly discussions with their relatives living abroad. According to them, they constantly encouraged their family members to maintain their cultural identity and not to abandon their cultural roots. By doing so, they reinforced narratives that encouraged their relatives to uphold and support the beliefs and rationale underpinning Ghanaian cultural practices, including FGC. One participant narrated how she may have promoted her sister’s belief in FGC through her justification of the norm of marriageability:

“…so, one day my sister, who is living in London, told me how hard it is to find a responsible man or husband in the Ghanaian community due to gossips about some Ghanaian women and girls who sleep around with men for a visa. Through the discussion, I reiterated the need for her to abstain from having relationships with men in order to maintain a positive image about herself. I then explained why our elders’ promoted practices, such as FGC, because in such context, someone who maintained her virginity will have a positive image and be sought by most of the responsible men in the Ghanaian community.” (P4, Rural).

Other participants commented on the reasons why it is necessary to encourage their relatives abroad to support Ghanaian cultural practices. The following narratives summarise these assertions:

“My sister’s daughter, who moved to Canada four years ago, called me last month to share her experience of giving birth in Canada. She said the nurses there strongly discouraged her not to co-sleep with the baby. But she got offended because co-sleeping with the child offers spiritual protection, etc. So, she intends to still co-sleep with the child. According to her, she feels it is part of the Western strategies of colonisation. I told her direct that she is right and highlighted that it is the main reason why she should keep on with our practice, including FGC. And that she shouldn’t pay heed to those pieces of advice because they are meant to colonise her mind and change her African identity.” (P7, Rural).

“Since she travelled, she kept telling me about how she feels the white people don’t understand us and our way of life. On one day, she told me how her work colleagues were amazed and appreciated our traditional naming ceremony process. So, I advised her to also educate them on FGC and other practices they label as bad. But she said she won’t do that because she thinks FGC is bad; I became ashamed for using that example.” (P1, Rural).

The above narratives underscore how casual interactions with relatives in high-income countries often prompt commentary intended to encourage commitment to FGC norms. It also shows that some relatives resist or challenge attempts by their family members to convince them to accept FGC.

Deliberate pressure from family heads

Six of the 13 participants shared narratives of deliberate, direct pressure from family members, especially family heads, to ensure that their relatives do not abandon FGC practices. According to them, people who travel outside Ghana are more likely to abandon the practice due to the negative commentaries about FGC in the diaspora. As a result, they sometimes use aggressive measures, including threats of abandonment, to ensure their relatives remain committed to the traditional cultural practice of FGC, regardless of where they live.

“It is not just about those who travel to other countries, even our relatives who move to the city, like Accra and Kumasi, often return to criticise FGC, trokosi and other traditional practices that they once supported. So, the risk is there. As a result, when we notice through our interactions that the person has started condemning the practice, we consult the family heads to call them and advise them.” (P9, Rural).

Another participant from the same community shared a story about how their family head threatened one of their relatives not to abandon FGC.

“Let me tell you this sad story. My brother, Kofi, lives in the USA with his wife, Aba (pseudonym). Last year, the wife got pregnant, and the scan showed it will be a girl. So, in one of our discussions, we started talking about a potential name and plans for her naming ceremony, then I suggested they bring the baby to Ghana when she is around five for proper traditional practices, such as traditional bathing and to do FGC. My brother said he won’t allow any of his children to undergo FGC because it is outmoded and risky. We called the wife to convince him, but she sided with the husband. So, we informed our family head, who then called to threaten them that they will be abandoned from the family if they don’t do it. Unfortunately, she experienced a miscarriage. But it has become a big issue for us now, because he is accusing we the family members of having something to do with the miscarriage his wife experienced. He thinks we did some spiritual works to cause that.” (P11, Rural).

It appears threats by family leaders are generally effective because of cultural expectations of respect for elders and the consequences of losing family connections. Comments by two participants confirmed this:

“…They cannot say no to the family head or the elders of the community because it is a sign of great disrespect. We don’t do that in our community. So, they have no option when the family head calls them to tell them to do it.” (P11, Rural).

“Even though they are not here physically…they are connected to us emotionally and spiritually. So, they wouldn’t like to lose that connection if they are abandoned by the family.” (P8, Rural).

The authority of the family heads and how they strengthen FGC norms was echoed by a participant whose relatives live in Sweden:

“My brother’s friend circumcised his baby in one of the countries I mentioned, but I know he did that because of the pressure he got from their family head. He may be disowned if he decided not to do it.” (P6, Rural).

Confidant relationship

Some participants who expressed strong support for the continuation of FGC discussed how they established trusting relationships and connections with relatives living abroad to ensure they continued support for the practice. According to them, their relatives often complain about loneliness and the individualistic nature of developed countries, making it difficult even to find someone to talk to. As a result, they build connections with them, becoming their confidants with the added intention of providing emotional support. However, it appears this confidant relationship creates an environment in which they can influence their relatives’ position on FGC. One participant narrated how her relationship with her daughter has supported her thoughts about FGC:

“Ever since she went…abroad, she always calls to complain that the place is lonely and that you won’t even get someone to talk to. I think it became worse in her case since she can’t speak the native language of the people. I have therefore become her close friend. Although we were cool before she left, but I think I have become her closest friend now. We talk almost every day for at least one hour. I think that connection has helped me to change her beliefs about FGC, and all our traditional practices. I am pleased that she is committing to our ways of doing things.” (P5, Rural).

Comments by another participant highlighted alternative ways that confidant relationships influence the sustenance of FGC norms in a transnational context:

“I am her friend and mother. She discusses almost everything with me. Before she makes any decision, she will consult me first. That privilege position has enabled me to change her thinking about so many things. She now celebrates our traditional festival there even though she is the only one from our community in the suburb. She now has belief in FGC as well.” (P3, Rural).

These narratives highlight how family members in Ghana use strategic measures, including a close relationship with their loved one, as avenues to sustain FGC norms. They also suggest that these trusting relationships are established or strengthened due to the challenges their relatives face in high-income countries. Neena discussed how she became her ex-husband’s close confidant due to the racism he experienced.

“Although we divorced after he travelled, we have maintained close contact due to our children. But it was not that strong until he narrated a racist incident he experienced at the workplace. He cried when narrating the incident to me. Since then, we have become closer even more than we used to be when we got married. I have become his trusted friend, we share a lot, and I influence his decision on a lot of things. So, it is more than possible that I can change his mind on things like FGC due to the bond I share with him.” (P2, Urban).

Participation in community events

The research participants highlighted encouraging participation in Ghanaian community activities and events as one of the actions they take to ensure their relatives maintain their commitment to the norms of FGC and other cultural practices. They argued that their relatives were more likely to maintain their Ghanaian cultural practices and identity and to support FGC if they socialised with Ghanaians and actively participated in Ghanaian events: “I strongly encourage her to look out for Ghanaians and to participate in whatever they do” (P7, Rural).

Participation in Ghanaian community events appears to enable their relatives to secure cultural and social support. One participant spoke of her daughter:

“…She is part of a Ghanaian community in [the] Bronx, which I strongly encouraged her to join when she travelled. She has found it extremely helpful. She tells me that it has enabled her to strengthen her Ghanaian identity, enjoy Ghanaian foods, have Ghanaian conversations and feel belonged. Last two weeks she lost her job, but she got a new job last week through a recommendation by someone from the Ghanaian community. So, even though my motive for encouraging her to participate in such events was to ensure they continue with all our cultural practices, not just FGC, but I feel she has got more benefit through that.” (P9, Rural).

Two participants explained how active participation in Ghanaian community events in the diaspora could promote FGC:

“You know what, I believe that she will abandon the practice if she sees that she is the only one who support the practice there. So, getting closer to the Ghanaians will enable her meet other Ghanaians who support the practice and all of our cultural practices, which will support her.” (P5, Rural).

“I encouraged her to attend the Sunday events, such as marriage ceremonies, funerals and naming ceremonies, organised by the Ghanaian community so that she will get other people to talk to. I think she will accept advice from the Ghanaians who have lived there for years and knows the context better than me. She is more likely to accept it when such people tell her about the benefits of keeping FGC more than me.” (P10, Rural).

It appears that community and social groups established by Ghanaian migrants in high-income countries may be effective in promoting FGC, as the perceived rationale for FGC could be accepted by white people who are indigenes of those countries. One participant described how an experience shared by her daughter, who lives in the Bronx, New York, prompted her comment on the need to strengthen FGC.

“She tells me that they have a little women association group, formed by Ghanaian women in one of the suburbs in the Bronx, USA. She said the association perform marriage ceremonies, naming ceremonies and funerals in the same Ghanaian way. She told me the white people were amazed with some of the cultural practices, and some of the white people sometimes join them during funerals to learn more about the practice. I explained to her that they should be proud of what they are doing because it helps promote our African identity and culture. Because a lot of the things they, the white people, criticises about our culture, such as FGC, are made because of lack of understanding. If they knew the benefit of FGC in ensuring that young girls get good marriage, I am sure they will not discourage it.” (P13, Rural).

Discussion

This study is the first qualitative study in Ghana that provides empirical evidence and insights into how Ghanaian families influence commitment to FGC norms and practices among their relatives living in Europe and North America. Broadly, the study extends knowledge on how FGC practices are promoted among African migrants in Europe and North American countries through their engagement and social interactions with their relatives living in Africa. These findings are useful for informing preventive strategies and normative change interventions for newly-arrived African migrants.

The four core themes reported in this study can be classified, analytically, into deliberate and non-deliberate strategies. Deliberate strategies are defined as targeted, purposeful actions or interactions primarily intended to motivate relatives to support FGC practices. Examples of deliberate strategies include direct pressure by family members, including threats, and deliberately establishing confidant relationships to promote support for FGC. Non-deliberate strategies, on the other hand, are non-targeted and planned actions/interactions that indirectly encourage relatives to support FGC practices. For example, family members’ intentions to encourage their relatives to maintain their African culture and identity included narratives that indirectly promoted FGC practices. Though the goal of such interactions and actions was to ensure that the relatives uphold their African identity, they indirectly promoted FGC by mentioning it as one of the broader acceptable cultural practices in Ghana.

The findings showed that appreciation of accepted Ghanaian cultural practices (such as naming and funeral ceremonies) by Europeans made some Ghanaians living in Europe believe that Europeans could support FGC if they were made to understand the cultural rationale for practising FGC. It is highly likely that a white person, with no understanding of how Ghanaian funerals are celebrated, will be fascinated after witnessing a traditional funeral ceremony for the first time. However, that does not suggest that they are oblivious to the apparent adverse effects of cultural practices, such as FGC, on children, nor does it suggest that they affirm the normative explanation that removing part of the genitals of a young girl will enable them to secure a husband [19, 20]. Both qualitative and quantitative findings from studies involving Africans in high-income countries have shown that some of these migrants uphold the normative belief that FGC enables young girls to remain chaste and eventually find responsible husbands [19, 20, 34]. Thus, it is safe to argue that the motivational strategies employed by the family members living in Africa, whether deliberate or not, play a role in strengthening the practice transnationally. That said, it appears the family members in this study promoted FGC in those conversations because they failed to untangle the difference between negative and positive cultural practices. Whereas naming ceremonies and funerals are accepted cultural practices in Ghana [46] and proudly celebrated by Ghanaians in the diaspora [47], FGC and co-sleeping practices remain illegal and highly contested cultural practices, respectively.

The study found that migrants sharing about loneliness and racist acts they experienced created avenues for their relatives in Ghana to become their confidants and to capitalise on that confidant relationship to promote narratives that encouraged FGC. There is a plethora of research evidence on the nature of isolation and loneliness experienced by Africans who migrate to high-income countries, such as the United States [46–49]. Some of this research evidence highlights older Africans as among those immigrants most affected by loneliness and social isolation [49]. Similarly, racism experienced by black African migrants is discussed [50–53]. Our findings have shown that these experiences predispose African migrants to influence from their relatives living in Ghana. This is because in their quest to overcome loneliness, some migrants seek connection and belonging from their relatives in Africa, thereby allowing the relatives to influence their views on cultural practices, such as FGC. Isolation and loneliness are social sanctions/punishments that are employed in some African communities to force people to practice FGC (see 23,56). However, our findings have shown that when African migrants experience isolation, loneliness, and racism in Europe and North America, it could also lead to them supporting FGC through the influence of the relatives they seek belongingness from in Africa.

The quest for these Ghanaian migrants to seek belongingness through the Ghanaian communities in Europe and North American countries also emerged as other avenues where their beliefs in FGC could be strengthened. The family members in this study reported that they encouraged their relatives to actively participate in activities organised by the Ghanaian communities. While the primary motive for such encouragement was to ensure their relatives felt they belonged, some participants noted that such community participation could enable them to strengthen their belief in FGC and other Ghanaian cultural practices. They feared that these relatives would abandon FGC if they did not interact with people from the same culture who support FGC. Indeed, postulations following the social convention theory suggest that a practice regarded as normative in one context, such as FGC, could be abandoned when people migrate to contexts where it is not practised or they cease to practice it. Empirical evidence has also confirmed that several African migrants in high-income countries, including those from Ghana, Somalia, Togo, Sudan, and Eritrea, have abandoned FGC post-migration [17, 25–28]. Hence, it is not surprising that the family members have identified community participation as one of the measures they use to strengthen FGC beliefs among their relatives in Europe and North American countries.

The findings also revealed pressure from family members to ensure their relatives continue their commitment to FGC norms after migrating to Europe and North American countries. Pressure, including threats to exclude them from the family, was used by family members and family heads to ensure their relatives did not abandon FGC. Although African migrants widely reported pressure from family members in Africa as the key practice that affects their commitment to FGC [12, 29–31, 36], our findings have shown the rationale and how those who apply the pressure (family members back home) perceive them to work. The findings have shown that although these African migrants are physically separated from their family members in Africa, they are connected spiritually and emotionally. This connection aligns with social convention theory, where FGC serves as a collective requirement for community belonging rather than a purely individual or domestic choice. Traditional norms that operate in Ghanaian communities, including respecting elders [54] and the authority of family heads still prevail [55–57]. As such, the community acts as an extended family that regulates behaviour through implicit social pressure and the threat of symbolic violence [32, 58], where the loss of cultural identity is felt as a form of exclusion. When these family heads threaten to ostracise them, they are likely to comply with their directives out of fear of losing connection with the family. Indeed, the initial finding on how African migrants use their family members in Africa to address loneliness and isolation lends some credence to why they are likely to subject themselves to the influence and threats of these family members. This is because maintaining the practice becomes a tool for preserving their symbolic capital and ensuring they remain integrated into the social provisions of their community of origin.

Implications of normative change interventions and FGC abandonment

This study has implications for interventions against FGC in both Ghana and high-income countries. It reinforces the argument that family influence and community ties remain critical in sustaining or weakening harmful cultural practices across borders. Victims of FGC are at increased risk of experiencing severe chronic physical pain, obstetric fistulas, and birth complications, and long-term psychological trauma, including anxiety and post-traumatic stress.

In Ghana, preventive programs should prioritise community education that clearly separates positive cultural practices, such as naming ceremonies and funerals, from harmful practices like FGC. Family heads should be empowered to understand that maintaining cultural identity should not include harmful practices. Current community education programs on FGC in Ghana should also highlight the risk of families pressuring relatives abroad to practise FGC. Current legislation should be expanded to include punishment for those who influence or pressure their family members in the diaspora to practice FGC.

In Europe and North America, African migrant networks, country associations and community development organisations should be targeted to provide supportive spaces where African migrants can resist family pressures and counter harmful narratives from home. African migrant community leaders and associations should be empowered to act as change agents and supported to address threats, racism, and loneliness experienced by migrants. The findings also highlight the need for measures to be taken to address loneliness and racism among Black Africans in Europe and North American countries. Further, since migrants often comply with host country laws, efforts should be made to re-engage African migrant communities to reaffirm legal prohibitions on FGC and encourage reporting of individuals who promote harmful practices within and outside the country. Cross-border collaboration between Ghanaian child protection and gender-based violence agencies and African migrant-support organisations in Europe and North American countries is essential to dismantling the normative authority sustaining FGC.

Limitation

Some limitations apply to the study findings. First, the data was collected only from family members in Ghana. The perspectives of Ghanaian migrants themselves were not included, which limits the balance of the findings. Second, most participants were from rural areas, where commitment to traditional cultural practices is often stronger. Families in urban areas, as well as highly educated individuals, may hold different views about FGC. Thus, future studies should use a “family dyad” sample involving migrants and their relatives in Ghana to strengthen the findings. Research that involves the purposive selection of Ghanaians in urban areas will also help enrich the study findings. Although we did not purposively target relatives of Ghanaians living in six countries in Europe and North America, it is possible that different findings or nuances could emerge from research involving relatives of Ghanaians who have migrated to other developing or less developed countries.

Further, the study questions were framed broadly to cover many cultural practices, such as naming ceremonies and funerals, to reduce bias around the sensitive nature of FGC. While this encouraged open discussion, it may have negatively influenced participants’ responses in merging positive cultural practices with harmful ones, such as FGC. We recommend that future studies use innovative techniques, such as vignettes, to focus solely on FGC. Finally, the presence of a male researcher during interviews may have increased the risk of social desirability bias, due to the sensitive nature of the topic.

Conclusion

This study clarifies the complex transnational mechanisms that sustain FGC norms among Ghanaian migrants in high-income countries. In particular, it highlights the strategies Ghanaian families use to motivate and promote support for FGC among their relatives who have migrated to Europe and North America. The findings reveal a paradox of persistence, where the combination of legal prohibition and physical distance is circumvented through complex social and emotional strategies. Family members in rural Ghana capitalise on the vulnerabilities of the diaspora to reposition FGC as a marker of cultural belonging and identity. By using traditional authority and the threat of social ostracisation, family members ensure that FGC remains a collective social convention despite individual geographic mobility. As a result, efforts to dismantle FGC in a transnational context require a dual approach: addressing the structural issues faced by Ghanaian migrants in Europe and North America, such as racism and loneliness, while simultaneously empowering community leaders in Ghana to decouple cultural heritage from harmful physical practices.
