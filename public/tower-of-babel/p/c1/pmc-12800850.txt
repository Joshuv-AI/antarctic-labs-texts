Impact of Transcatheter Tricuspid Valve Repair on Pre- and Post-Procedural Hospitalization Rates
Authors: Allen A. Razavi, Aminah Sallam, Betemariam Sharew, Claire Perez, Mamoo Nakamura, Andrew Czarnecki, Michael E. Bowdish, Natalia N. Egorova, Joanna Chikwe, Derrick Y. Tam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The effectiveness of tricuspid transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) in reducing heart failure (HF) hospitalizations is unclear. Using Medicare and Medicaid data (2015-2019), 487 patients undergoing isolated tricuspid TEER (n = 258) or concomitant tricuspid and mitral TEER (n = 229) were identified. HF hospitalization rates were compared across 4 time periods (in days: 1-30, 31-90, 91-182, 183-365) using Poisson regression. Patients (median age: 80 years) had fewer HF hospitalizations after tricuspid TEER (adjusted rate ratio 0.55, 95% confidence interval: 0.43-0.70, P < 0.001) and when separated by concomitant tricuspid and mitral TEER (adjusted rate ratio 0.57, 95% confidence interval: 0.42-0.79, P < 0.001). In real-world analysis, tricuspid-TEER is associated with a reduced HF hospitalization rate.

Severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR) contributes significantly to adverse clinical outcomes, including heart failure (HF) hospitalization and reduced quality of life.1 In clinical practice, TR often has been managed conservatively with diuretic therapy, which may alleviate congestion but does not improve survival.2 Surgical repair or replacement of the tricuspid valve is the only definitive treatment; however, historically, this treatment has been underutilized due to its high level of operative risk. Early studies reported double-digit operative mortality rates (∼10%-12%) for isolated tricuspid valve surgery, leading many patients to be deemed poor surgical candidates.2 Although contemporary surgical series demonstrate improved outcomes—with recent registry data showing an operative mortality of approximately 5%-6%—a large proportion of patients with severe TR still do not undergo surgery due to advanced age, comorbidities, and perceived risk.3

Transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) of the tricuspid valve offers a less invasive option for patients at prohibitive surgical risk. The Trial to Evaluate Cardiovascular Outcomes in Patients Treated with the Tricuspid Valve Repair System Pivotal—a prospective randomized controlled trial comparing tricuspid TEER with medical management—demonstrated that tricuspid TEER reduced regurgitation severity and improved quality of life. However, the trial did not show a significant difference in survival or HF hospitalization rates at 1 year for the TEER vs the medical therapy groups.4,5 This absence of a short-term clinical outcome benefit, beyond symptomatic improvement, underscores the need to assess the impact of tricuspid TEER on hard clinical endpoints in real-world practice.6 In this study, we examined a national administrative claims dataset to evaluate the effect of transcatheter tricuspid valve TEER on pre- vs post-procedural HF hospitalization rates in routine clinical practice.

Methods

Study overview

The US Medicare and Medicaid administrative claims database was queried to identify patients undergoing first-time tricuspid intervention with tricuspid TEER from 2015-2019 using the International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision (ICD-10). The ICD-9 or ICD-10 diagnosis codes documented in all inpatient admissions were used to identify baseline comorbidities. Patients were excluded if they had a history of prior valve surgery, underwent emergent tricuspid TEER, experienced in-hospital mortality, or switched to Medicare Advantage plans. The study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for observational research, and institutional review board approval was obtained from Cedars-Sinai Medical Center (STUDY00001188, approved February 19, 2021), with a waiver of informed consent.

Study endpoints

The primary endpoint was the rate of HF-related hospitalizations before vs after the tricuspid TEER. We defined a HF hospitalization as any inpatient hospital admission with a principal diagnosis of HF (based on ICD-10 diagnostic codes for HF). For each patient, we examined hospitalizations in the 1-year period before the TEER and in the 1-year period after the TEER. To characterize temporal trends, each 1-year period was subdivided into 4 prespecified intervals: 1-30 days, 31-90 days, 91-182 days, and 183-365 days pre-procedure and the corresponding 1-30 days, 31-90 days, 91-182 days, and 183-365 days postdischarge. These intervals were chosen a priori to assess short-term vs longer-term trends in hospitalization rates relative to the intervention and inspired by the article by Czarnecki and colleagues.7 Patients were censored at the time of death or at the end of the 1-year postdischarge period, whichever came first, meaning each patient could contribute up to 365 days of follow-up after TEER. The secondary endpoint was the rate of death or HF hospitalization. As a sensitivity analysis, we separately evaluated HF hospitalization rates before and after isolated tricuspid TEER and concomitant tricuspid/mitral TEER.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics and early mortality for the overall cohort are presented in Table 1; comparisons between those who received isolated tricuspid TEER and concomitant tricuspid and mitral TEER are presented in Supplemental Table S1. A Student t test was used for normally distributed continuous variables; the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for non-normally distributed continuous variables; and the χ2 test was used for categorical variables. For the primary endpoint of rate of HF-related hospitalizations, a Poisson regression model was used to compare incident rates of HF hospitalizations across the intervals, adjusting for age, sex, and TEER type with a logarithmic offset for follow-up time within 1 year. We obtained adjusted rate ratios (aRRs) with 95% confidence intervals for HF hospitalization, comparing the postdischarge period to the pre-procedural period. To account for within-subject correlation in this before-and-after design, generalized estimating equations with a log link Poisson function were used, with offset for follow-up time, and were clustered by participant identifier, to account for both dependence of observations and overdispersion. In a sensitivity analysis, we applied the same Poisson regression separately to patients undergoing isolated tricuspid TEER and those receiving concomitant mitral TEER. For the secondary endpoint of rate of death or HF hospitalization, we counted the occurrence of either a HF hospitalization or a death in each interval and used a similar Poisson modelling approach to compare the combined event rates before vs after TEER. Statistical significance was assumed to be P < 0.05. Results

Baseline characteristics

Among 487 patients undergoing tricuspid TEER, 258 (52%) underwent isolated tricuspid TEER, and 229 (48%) underwent concomitant tricuspid and mitral TEER. The median age at intervention was 80 years (interquartile range: 75-85 years). A total of 61% of patients were women; 66.7% had chronic kidney disease; 46.6% had peripheral vascular disease; and 31.4% had a prior myocardial infarction (Table 1). Chronic lung disease (in 42.7%) and diabetes (in 40.7%) were common. Patients undergoing isolated tricuspid TEER were younger (median age, 79 years [range: 73-84] vs 81 years [range: 76-87], P = 0.002) with a higher prevalence of liver disease (53.5% vs 36.7%, P < 0.001) than that in those with concomitant tricuspid and mitral TEER. The remaining comorbidities between the cohorts were similar (Supplemental Table S1).

Early outcomes

The 30-day mortality rate was 3.8% (n = 18), and the 1-year mortality rate was 22.0% (n = 107). No statistically significant difference occurred in 30-day (3.9% vs 3.5%, P = 0.87) and 1-year mortality (23.2% vs 20.5%, P = 0.50) between isolated and concomitant TEER groups, respectively (Supplemental Table S1).

HF hospitalization rates

In the year before tricuspid TEER, 37.3% of patients had at least one HF hospitalization; in the year following tricuspid TEER, 23.0% experienced at least one HF hospitalization. We found a progressive increase in the frequency of HF hospitalizations approaching the time of intervention, followed by a stepwise decrease thereafter (Fig. 1). The adjusted rate ratio (aRR) for HF hospitalization in the year after TEER, as compared to that in the year prior to TEER, was 0.55 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.43-0.70; P < 0.001; Fig. 1). The rate of death or HF hospitalization also decreased after TEER (aRR: 0.79, 95% CI: 0.62-0.99, P = 0.031; Fig. 2). Similar findings were observed when the analysis was separated by patients undergoing isolated tricuspid TEER (aRR: 0.54, 95% CI: 0.39-0.74) and concomitant tricuspid and mitral TEER (aRR: 0.57; 95% CI: 0.42-0.79; Supplemental Table S2).Figure 1Heart failure (HF) hospitalization rates before and after tricuspid transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER). Discussion

Our study evaluated HF hospitalization rates before and after TEER in a real-world Medicare and Medicaid cohort. The principal finding was a significant reduction in HF hospitalizations post-TEER, with an aRR of 0.55, reflecting a 45% decrease relative to the pre-procedural period. Similar benefits were observed after incorporating death into the composite outcome, and in the sensitivity analysis separating isolated tricuspid TEER from concomitant tricuspid and mitral TEER.

Here, we demonstrate the safety and efficacy of tricuspid TEER in a real-world cohort of elderly patients in the US. Compared to the Trial to Evaluate Cardiovascular Outcomes in Patients Treated with the Tricuspid Valve Repair System Pivotal, our cohort was older, had a greater number of comorbidities, and experienced an escalating rate of HF hospitalizations prior to intervention. Despite these differences, we observed a significant reduction in HF readmissions following tricuspid TEER, underscoring the value of this therapy in routine clinical practice. This finding is of particular importance, as a reduction in hospital readmissions may contribute to an improvement in quality of life for patients with TR.

Prior studies have shown that procedural success—often defined as residual TR ≤ 2+—is associated with improved outcomes, including lower rates of HF hospitalization and mortality.8,9 However, findings from the Transcatheter Tricuspid Valve Therapies (TriValve) registry raise important questions about futility in select patients: among those with a TRI-SCORE ≥ 8, even technically successful TEER did not confer a reduction in mortality or HF admissions, suggesting that TEER has limited prognostic benefit in this subgroup.10 Although we were unable to calculate TRI-SCOREs, due to inherent limitations of our dataset, the observed 21% reduction in the composite of death or HF hospitalization (aRR 0.79, 95% CI: 0.62-0.99) in our high-comorbidity Medicare population suggests that TEER still may offer meaningful clinical benefit in real-world practice. Taken together, these data highlight the potential of tricuspid TEER to reduce morbidity, even among patients traditionally considered high-risk, and support its broader adoption in contemporary practice.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. This is a retrospective analysis of administrative claims without a contemporaneous control group, as subjects served as their own controls. In addition, postprocedural changes in medical therapy—including adjustment of guideline-directed medical therapy or diuretic regimens—may have contributed to the observed reduction in HF hospitalizations. Because this information is not available in the claims dataset, we cannot isolate the independent effect of TEER from concurrent changes in medical management. The Medicare claims database also does not include detailed echocardiographic parameters, such as residual TR grading at discharge, precluding an assessment of their potential impact on clinical outcomes. We cannot account for improved medical management, device technology refinement, operator expertise, or patient selection trends evolving over time. Off-label device use during this period may limit conclusions made about intervention efficacy. The benefits of mitral repair may differ from those of isolated tricuspid intervention. Despite the fact that our sensitivity analysis shows similar outcomes in patients undergoing isolated tricuspid TEER and those with concomitant mitral and tricuspid TEER, these findings should be interpreted with this limitation in mind. Although our study focused on HF hospitalizations—a clinically relevant outcome in this patient population—this approach may not fully capture the overall burden of post-TEER hospitalizations. Future studies using more granular clinical data should evaluate all-cause hospitalizations to provide a more comprehensive assessment of postprocedural outcomes. The exclusion of patients with any prior valve surgery—rather than those with only previous tricuspid interventions—may limit the generalizability of our findings. However, given the retrospective nature of our study, we chose a more homogeneous cohort, to enhance internal validity. Future prospective studies with more detailed surgical histories could help address this limitation The exclusion of patients who died during hospitalization—likely representing the most severe cases—may introduce bias; however, this exclusion was necessary to accurately assess postdischarge HF hospitalizations, our primary outcome of interest.

Conclusion

Our analysis of contemporary US practice suggests that tricuspid TEER may alleviate the HF hospitalization burden among patients with TR. Additional prospective studies are needed to validate these findings, optimize patient selection, and refine therapeutic strategies for severe TR.
