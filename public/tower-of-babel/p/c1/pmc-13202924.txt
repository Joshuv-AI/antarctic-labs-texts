Interventions to improve mental health in caregivers of adult intensive care patients: a systematic review
Authors: Sandra Lange, Wioletta Mędrzycka-Dąbrowska, Paola Arcadi, Sawa Kwiatkowska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe stay of a close person at the ICU entails strong stress accompanied by anxiety, the sense of lack of control, and helplessness. Consequently, this may result in the development of a set of disorders which are described in the literature as post-intensive care syndrome–family (PICS-F). That is why interventions to improve mental health and prevent PICS-F are necessary and become more and more important.MethodsA systematic review was guided by the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Tools for use in JBI Systematic Reviews and was conducted following the recommendations of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses statement. Four databases were searched. Studies published in English between 2014 and 2025 were included. The final database searches were performed in February 2026.ResultsA total of 46 studies met the inclusion criteria. Most of them involved supportive/educational interventions, which took various forms (direct, by phone or via mobile devices). The results assessed in caregivers were diversified, similarly to tools used to measure those results. The majority of studies indicates solely a partial improvement in the caregiver outcome. In turn, if the studies reveal a positive effect, the researchers rarely specify the size of that effect.ConclusionThe available literature describes many interventions addressed to families of ICU patients. Some of the studies indicate that there is a potential in improving mental health status. Given large methodological differences in the studies, it is difficult to state unambiguously which of interventions are most effective and bring about the most permanent effect. It is necessary to continue a top quality RCT in this field in order to identify effective interventions which may be incorporated into everyday ICU practices.Implications for clinical practiceEffective communication with ICU patients’ family members and the provision of information can be crucial in clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04625-9.

Introduction

Families of critically ill intensive care unit (ICU) patients are exposed to the risk of unfavorable psychological consequences [1]. In the literature, they are defined as post-intensive care syndrome–family (PICS-F). PICS-F refers to a wide spectrum of sequelae that extend beyond psychological symptoms, encompassing physical, social, and even economic dysfunctions that may emerge after the ICU experience. From a clinical and nursing perspective, this phenomenon has profound implications for the continuity and quality of family-centered care, as it affects caregivers’ ability to support the patient’s recovery and their own health-related quality of life. Thus, PICS-F comprises mental burdens (depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress symptoms (PTSS), post‐traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), complicated grief), as well as physical and economic burdens, which start when the patient is admitted to the ICU [2, 3]. That is why it is important to understand the impact of a critical illness on the caregivers’ family and psychological outcomes. The sources of negative impact may be connected with the patient, the family, and protective factors [1]. For example, Kotfis et al. identified that the patient’s delirium is a source of the family’s psychological disorders [4]. In their study, Shirasaki et al. proved that one third of families of COVID-19 patients admitted to ICUs had PICS-F symptoms [5]. Smith et al. proved that negative mental symptoms are common in substitute decision-makers and are connected with role incongruence [6]. Across these studies, family distress was consistently associated with the patient’s clinical instability, uncertainty, and perceived loss of control, highlighting the cumulative burden experienced by relatives of ICU patients [7]. Family members of patients that survived a critical illness also experience sleep disorders and low sleep quality for even up to two months after ICU discharge [8]. In addition, the caregivers of patients that survived at the ICU with unfavorable physical/mental outcomes experience the greatest burden, which, in turn, results in their worse mental outcomes and health-related quality of life [9]. These findings highlight the need for early and structured support for ICU families, aimed at reducing the psychological distress and long-term sequelae associated with critical illness.

The relatives play a key role in the recovery of patients both at the critical illness stage, as well as during rehabilitation and after discharge at home [10]. The Society of Critical Care Medicine (SCCM) underlines that it is important to strengthen the family’s empowerment and engagement in its ABCDEF care package at ICUs [11]. Strategies improving well-being and preventing PICS-F development should be implemented already during the patient’s ICU stay [12].

Overall, these findings indicate that PICS-F is a common, multifactorial phenomenon that depends on patient-, family-, and care-related factors. This highlights the need for early and structured interventions targeting caregivers during and after the intensive care unit stay.

Although PICS-F covers mental, physical, social, and economic domains, this review deliberately focuses on well-being (mental health and interpersonal relationships, such as anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress, satisfaction, and communication). These outcomes are most commonly targeted by interventions in the intensive care unit, are highly sensitive to modifiable aspects of care (e.g., communication, family involvement), and directly impact caregivers’ ability to participate in decision-making and support the patient’s recovery.

Background

Several reviews that have been published to date present various strategies for caregivers of ICU patients. Zante et al. indicate that the provision of information and proactive communication seem to play a key role for PICS-F treatment. They also state that certain interventions may worsen the post-ICU status of families and further top quality studies in this field are necessary [13]. Their findings suggest that information alone may not be sufficient, and that a multidimensional approach combining education, communication, and emotional support might be required to address the complex psychological needs of family members. Cherak et al. performed the meta-analysis of 56 studies, where they analyzed how mental health-related interventions influence negative and positive psychological symptoms (such as: anxiety, depression, PTSD, distress, burden, courage, humanity, justice, transcendence, temperance, and wisdom and knowledge) in informal caregivers of critically ill patients (ICU, PICU, neonatal ICU). The review and meta-analysis indicated that interventions improved short-term symptoms (anxiety, depression, humanity and transcendence) in caregivers of critically ill patients, but increased the burden at the same time. In turn, no impact on long-term psychological outcomes was observed [14].Kiwanuka et al. reviewed qualitative and quantitative studies (mixed method review) in order to find evidence for nurse-led family interventions and the impact of such interventions on family outcomes in adult critical care settings. The evidence suggests that interdisciplinary nurse-led family interventions may improve family outcomes. Interventions that brought about a slight or medium effect included: a bundled approach, educational interventions with digital storytelling, nurse-driven emotional support, and informational nursing interventions [15]. In turn, Bohart et al. assessed the evidence in terms of the feasibility and effectiveness of patient and family centered interventions at ICUs. They compared single- and multi-component interventions with ordinary care in order to reduce delirium, depression, anxiety, and PTSD in patients and their family members. The study indicated moderate/low certainty evidence that patient and family centered care does not have an impact on the outcomes of patients and psychological outcomes (anxiety, depression and PTSD) of family members. They stated that it is still not certain whether patient and family centered care may have a positive impact on the outcomes of critically ill patients and their families [16]. Nevertheless, this lack of definitive evidence may be partially attributed to methodological heterogeneity, small sample sizes, and insufficiently tailored interventions rather than to the inefficacy of the family-centered care concept itself. Future research should aim to isolate the active components of such interventions to clarify which elements truly drive improvements in caregiver well-being.

Despite of the increasing number of studies on the well-being and mental health of ICU patients’ families, there remains a lack of clear evidence about how different types of interventions influence caregivers’ psychological outcomes. Previous reviews have mainly focused on partial aspects of family support—such as communication or education—without systematically integrating emerging digital and telehealth-based modalities and have rarely compared intervention characteristics, delivery modes, providers, and outcome domains within a single synthesis. This review analyses and synthesizes such knowledge. It adopts a comprehensive perspective that includes both traditional and technology-mediated interventions, aiming to map their design, delivery modalities (including face-to-face, digital, and hybrid formats), and measurable impact on caregivers’ well-being. Through the analysis of patterns of effectiveness and reported adverse events across different studies, this review may provide insight into how and under what conditions caregiver interventions may be beneficial. In addition, the above review may help improve the quality of care at ICUs by identifying key components that can be realistically incorporated into everyday clinical practice and interprofessional routines.

Aim

The purpose of this study is to identify which interventions aimed at improving the well-being (at ICUs and after ICU discharge) of caregivers of ICU patients have been developed, as well as the clinical effectiveness of those interventions which could be incorporated into everyday clinical practices.

Method

Study design

This systematic review of the literature was guided by the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Tools for use in JBI Systematic Reviews [17], and was conducted following the recommendations of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [18].

Search methods

The following databases were searched: PubMed, CINAHL, Web of Science, and Scopus. Keywords were identified based on the PICO framework. The following keywords were used: ‘Intensive Care Units’, ‘Critical Care’, ‘ICU’, ‘Intensive Care’, Family’, ‘Families’, ‘Spouse’, ‘Caregiver’, ’Caregivers’, ‘Nursing Care’, ‘Nursing Intervention’, ‘Nursing Interventions’, ‘Nonpharmacological intervention’, ’Non-pharmacological intervention’, ‘Nonpharmacological interventions’, ‘Non-pharmacological interventions’, ‘ICU diary’, ‘ICU diaries’, Music Therapy’, ‘Music’, ‘Communication’, ‘Follow-Up’, ‘Support’, ‘Post-Intensive Care Syndrome’, ’Post intensive care syndrome’, ‘PICS’,’ Post-intensive care syndrome family’, ’Post intensive care syndrome family’, ‘PICS-F’, ’Quality of Life’, ‘Anxiety’, ‘Depression’, ‘Psychological outcome’, ’Psychological outcomes’, ‘Behavioral outcome’, ‘Behavioural outcome’, ‘Behavioral outcomes’, ‘Behavioural outcomes’, ‘Burden’. Keywords were entered along with their combinations using AND or OR. In PubMed, Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) were applied alongside free-text terms to increase sensitivity and specificity. In CINAHL, CINAHL Headings were used in combination with text words. The number of articles found during each search test was limited to studies conducted between 2015 and 2025. The final search was carried out on February 24, 2026. The literature search was done by SL. All publications were analyzed by title and abstract to exclude duplicates and irrelevant entries. Full-text articles were then read and critically evaluated according to the eligibility criteria. Two reviewers (SL and SK) independently screened and selected the studies, with disagreements resolved by consensus or consultation with a third reviewer (WM-D), thereby minimizing selection bias. The reference lists of studies identified during the initial search were also screened to ensure that no relevant research was overlooked. Operational definition of the main concepts used in this review

ICU Patient caregiver- a person who has a close relationship with a patient hospitalized in the ICU and plays an important supportive and/or decision-making role during and after hospitalization. The key criterion is that they perform a caregiving role (and do not receive payment for it) rather than their formal degree of relationship.

Well-being - a multidimensional construct encompassing mental health (anxiety, depression, stress, PTSS, PTSD, complicated grief, burnout, automatic thoughts), life satisfaction and quality of life, social and family functioning, personal resources including hope, spirituality, and resilience.

Non-pharmacological intervention-any structured, non pharmacological activity primarily aimed at improving caregivers’ psychological or relational outcomes, delivered during ICU stay and/or post ICU.

Search outcomes

A total of 1643 records were initially retrieved from the four databases. After removing duplicates (312) and screening titles and abstracts(1286), 1598 records were excluded. 45 full-text studies were assessed for eligibility. The primary reasons for exclusion were: study design, wrong population and pharmacological intervention. An additional 110 records were identified by checking references. Of these, 20 reports were assessed for eligibility(1 was excluded). Ultimately, the final analysis included a total of 46 articles (Fig. 1).

Data extraction

To enhance consistency, a standardized data extraction form was developed based on JBI recommendations, capturing descriptive, methodological, and outcome-related variables. Each included study was reviewed independently by two reviewers (SL and SK), and discrepancies were discussed and resolved by consensus. Data extracted included: (a) study characteristics (author, year, country, and design), (b) participant and caregiver demographics, (c) intervention characteristics (type, duration, provider, and delivery mode), (d) outcome measures, and (e) main results and conclusions. Although the template was not piloted, all reviewers used a shared coding manual to ensure reliability.

Quality assessment

Three researchers independently assessed the quality of the included studies (SL, SK, WM-D). Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. The critical appraisal tools used to assess quality were JBI (checklist for randomized controlled trials [19] and quasi-experimental studies [20]). The score was divided into three categories: high (if ≥ 80% of the assessment tool items received a point), moderate (if ≥ 65% of the assessment tool items received a point) and low (if ≤ 55% of the assessment tool items received a point) (Table 2). A point was only awarded if the answer to a question from the tool was ‘Yes’ (Supplementary materials).

Data synthesis

In this review, the outcomes of studies were analyzed by the use of a narrative synthesis [21] because of the large heterogeneity of the identified studies, which applied various effectiveness assessment methods and different outcome measures. At first, an approach applied in each intervention and target group (caregivers of ICU patients or ICU patients and their caregivers) were identified. Then, the results of the studies included in the review were subject to an initial synthesis. Relationships in and between the studies were examined and the soundness of the synthesis was assessed. The effectiveness of the studies was described based on improvement in the outcomes of caregivers (whether significant) and the clinical significance of those outcomes (the size of the effect).

A narrative synthesis approach was deemed appropriate given the methodological diversity and variability in outcomes across studies. Quantitative pooling (meta-analysis) was not feasible due to the heterogeneity in intervention types, outcome measures, and follow-up periods. Following Popay et al.’s (2006) framework, the synthesis process consisted of four stages: (1) development of a preliminary synthesis, (2) exploration of relationships within and between studies, (3) assessment of the robustness of the synthesis, and (4) contextual interpretation of findings. Studies were grouped according to intervention type (e.g., communication, family education/support, flexible visitation, telehealth) and delivery mode (face-to-face, telephone, digital). The narrative integration focused on identifying converging evidence patterns and highlighting gaps in effectiveness and measurement consistency [21].

The interventions were divided into the following thematic groups: Support/educational Support, Flexible visitation, Communication, Diaries, Engagement in care. The categories were developed inductively during the data extraction process. After an initial review and analysis of the characteristics of the interventions included in the studies, repetitive types of activities were identified and then grouped into coherent, conceptually distinct thematic areas. This approach made it possible to capture the actual diversity of interventions and their dominant components.

Ethical approval

Since the study was based on a systematic review, no ethical approval was needed.

A total of 46 studies were included in this systematic review, comprising 35 randomized controlled trials (RCTs, including 8 pilot studies) and 11 quasi-experimental studies (7 pre–post intervention and 4 non-randomized designs). The studies were published between 2015 and 2025 and conducted across multiple countries, including the United States, Canada, Brazil, Japan, China, Korea, several European nations, and the Middle East, reflecting a broad international scope.

Sample sizes ranged from 5 (pilot study) to 1,106 participants, with 20 studies including fewer than 100 participants per group. The studies recruited either patient–caregiver dyads, caregivers alone, or caregivers together with healthcare professionals. Interventions were tested across diverse intensive care settings, including cardiothoracic, surgical, medical, neurological, trauma, and mixed ICUs, as well as cardiology and transplant ICUs.

Most of the included studies tested interventions designed to improve psychological or well-being outcomes among caregivers or family members of critically ill patients. The most common categories of interventions were: family support or support plus education (n = 23), communication-focused interventions (n = 16), flexible visitation models (n = 3), ICU diary-based approaches (n = 2), and interventions enhancing family engagement in patient care (n = 2) (Supplementary materials).

Overall, 33 studies tested a single-component intervention, while 13 evaluated multicomponent or bundled strategies combining education, communication, and emotional support. In most RCTs, the control group received standard or usual ICU care.

The interventions were mainly delivered by interdisciplinary teams (n = 21) or nursing professionals (n = 15), while other providers included chaplains, psychologists, and trained facilitators (n = 10). The duration and intensity of interventions varied widely, ranging from single 2-hour educational sessions to prolonged programs extending for several months after ICU discharge.

In summary, the included studies were heterogeneous in design, duration, providers, and outcome measures, which justified the use of a narrative synthesis rather than a meta-analysis.

Risk of bias

All studies hereunder met quality criteria (low-high quality) (Table 2). Out of 34 randomized studies, 14 were assessed as high quality, 16 as moderate quality, and 5 as low quality studies. Out of 11 quasi-experimental studies, 4 were assessed as high quality, and 7 as moderate quality studies. The detailed assessment is attached in the form of supplementary materials.

Methodological limitations and the risk of error were usually based on the inability to blind the study due to the character of interventions. The participants and intervening persons knew which group is an intervention one, which could influence, for example, the relatives’ subjective reports. Another risk of bias in the studies resulted from potential gaps in follow-up/loss to follow-up, which were not described in detail in some studies. Thus, the participants’ loss and differences in loss between groups are not clear, which is likely to influence the final outcome. Furthermore, some studies did not report detailed information on randomization and allocation (e.g. whether allocation was hidden). This is particularly important in bias selection minimization.

In the case of quasi-experimental studies, the risk of bias resulted from the lack of a control group in some studies and potential gaps in follow up/loss to follow up, which were not described in detail.

The certainty of evidence for the main outcomes was rated as low, mainly due to inconsistent effect directions across studies and heterogeneity in interventions. Detailed assessment and justification are provided in the supplementary materials.

Targeted outcomes

The studies differed in terms of outcome measures. Most studies focused on more than one outcome. Most frequently measured outcomes included: anxiety (n = 37), depression (n = 33), satisfaction (n = 28), and stress/PTSS/PTSD (n = 27). Satisfaction was measured, among others, on the basis of: overall satisfaction, family needs, communication, decision making, transition conditions, quality of interventions, clinician–surrogate concordance, goal-concordant care, quality of dying and death, patient- and family-centeredness, spiritual care. 12 studies measured other outcomes (a.o. perception of control uncertainty, complicated grief, symptom burned, general functioning, family sense of coherence, herth hope, medical outcome, mental health, automatic thoughts, spiritual well-being, resilience), and 10 studies assessed the quality of life of families of critically ill patients (Supplementary materials).

Effectiveness of interventions

Out of the 46 analyzed studies, 27 (59%) reported a positive impact of the intervention on at least one caregiver outcome compared to pre-intervention or control conditions. Among these, 18 studies demonstrated partial improvement—for instance, significant benefits in depression but not anxiety, or short-term improvements that did not persist at follow-up. Six of these were pilot studies (five RCTs and one quasi-experimental).

The partially effective interventions were primarily categorized as Family Support/Education (n = 9), Diaries (n = 1), Engagement in care (n = 1), Communication (n = 5), and Flexible visitation (n = 1). These interventions were delivered through face-to-face, telephone, or digital formats and implemented by interdisciplinary teams, advanced practice nurses (APNs), ICU nurses, or caregivers under professional guidance.

Nine studies (20%) showed consistent improvement across all measured outcomes, including four communication interventions, two flexible visitation programs, two family support/education interventions, and one engagement-in-care intervention. These programs typically combined educational content with emotional or relational support and were delivered by interdisciplinary or nursing-led teams using multimodal approaches (e.g., printed materials, videos, or structured meetings).

Effect sizes were reported in only four studies and ranged from medium to large according to Cohen’s (1988) interpretation, highlighting the paucity of quantitative effect reporting across this body of evidence.

Nineteen studies (41%) did not show statistically significant differences between intervention and control groups, while three studies suggested possible adverse effects—such as increased distress, exacerbation of post-traumatic symptoms, or reduced satisfaction—likely due to emotional burden during participation or contextual stressors.

Overall, the findings indicate that while several interventions can improve psychological and relational outcomes among ICU caregivers, the evidence base remains heterogeneous in methodological rigor, measurement consistency, and long-term follow-up. Further high-quality randomized trials with standardized outcome reporting are warranted to confirm and sustain these effects.

Discussion

This review demonstrated that several interventions aimed at improving the mental health of family members of ICU patients appear to be effective. However, given the marked methodological heterogeneity across studies, it remains difficult to identify which specific interventions yield the most robust and sustained benefits for families of critically ill patients. Similar conclusions were drawn by Sak-Dankosky et al., who conducted the systematic review of interventions improving the well-being of oncological patients’ families [67], and Sevillano-Garayoa et al., who studied interventions for caregivers of close persons with advanced illness at home [68].

The ICU stay of a loved one represents an intensely stressful experience, commonly associated with anxiety, loss of control, and helplessness. These reactions can evolve into post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depressive symptoms, or decreased quality of life among relatives [68, 69]. That is why interventions focused on family support and education are necessary and gain on importance. As our review revealed, the literature describes a number of interventions which have been examined to date. However, they only report some partial improvement in the psychological outcomes of families. For example, Chiang et al. assessed the impact of a single 2-hour psychological education program based on a cognitive and behavioral approach on stress and anxiety reduction in main family caregivers of ICU patients and analyzed whether the program contributes to the satisfaction of needs related, among others, to information. They found out that satisfaction with information among the families improved [25]. This suggests that the program is effective, however not in all dimensions. Similarly, the review by Sevillano-Garayoa et al. pointed out that although many interventions aimed at improving the caregiver’s outcomes, like depression, anxiety, uncertainty, quality of life, burden and self-care or competence, the general effectiveness of those outcomes was limited and frequently non-durable [68]. Problems of caregivers of ICU patients are multidimensional. Apart from psychological aspects, there are also physical and social ones. Single-component interventions, i.e. psychoeducation or coping-skills training, are likely not to include the whole spectrum of such needs. That is why they will show improvement solely in certain outcomes. Nevertheless, for example, a multi-component intervention studied by White et al., which included both communication training for partner nurses, support path for families and support in the implementation of the intervention, was proven to be effective solely in some of caregivers’ outcomes [32]. This may result from the fact that interventions are usually standard and caregivers have various needs, mental resources, family situations, support level. What may influence the effectiveness of those interventions? Thus, we come to a conclusion that the effectiveness of caregiver support interventions may be limited if, for example, caregivers had prior mental disorders, like depression, or suffer of chronic stress. In those cases, standard interventions focused on the improvement of mental well-being are not likely to bring out the expected outcomes. That his why it may be important to evaluate the caregiver’s mental status prior to the implementation of the support program.

Effective communication with families of ICU patients is one of determinants of high quality care at ICUs [69, 70]. The studies indicate that proactive communication and the provision of information may have a key impact on the treatment of PICS-F [13]. In our review, interventions implemented during the stay at ICU and after hospitalization or death included, among others: a condolence letter, text messages, video talks, storytelling, the optimization of communication between ICU personnel, a communication facilitator at ICUs. The pilot study by Barnato et al. (storytelling at the early mourning stage) indicated a potential in PICS-F prevention [23]. Structured storytelling may help integrate memories and difficult emotions. On the other hand, sending a condolence letter did not contribute to improvement in depression, PTSD or mourning and increased the risk of prolonged grief disorder [28]. People in mourning may need a dialogue and not only written words as well as express emotions and establishing a relationship emotions and establishing a relationship. Secondly, a condolence letter may evoke memories from the ICU and disturb the process of adaptation to the changed situation. The provision of information was proven to have a positive impact in the study conducted during the Covid-19 pandemic. The daily provision of audio and/or video information concerning the patient’s health (diagnostic tests, treatment, care, changes in consciousness) contributed to statistically significantly lower levels of anxiety and depression among families of ICU patients [48]. That is why the regular and safe provision of information could bring about positive outcomes to families of ICU patients. An additional person acting as a communication facilitator at the ICU may effectively reduce long-lasting symptoms of depression in family members, but did not prove to have significant effects in terms of anxiety and PTSD [40]. This may suggest that more intensive interventions focused on anxiety and PTSD are necessary. In accordance with the review conducted by Zante et al., which emphasized the key role of communication and information sharing in alleviating PICS-F symptoms, we found that communication-focused interventions often improved short-term satisfaction and, in some cases, symptoms of depression. However, as with the review authors’ conclusions, the effects were often partial and did not persist over time [13]. Similarly, a meta-analysis by Cherak et al. showed small short-term improvements in anxiety and depression among informal caregivers of critically ill patients, but found no clear long-term benefits and even a potential increase in caregiver burden. Our findings are consistent with this pattern: improvement was typically observed in single domains (e.g., depression but not anxiety) and only rarely across the entire spectrum of psychological outcomes [14].

The policy of flexible visitation combined with family education proved to have positive effects on the well-being of the family, including: anxiety, depression, satisfaction [42]. The analysis also covered the extent to which the policy of flexible visitation had on long-lasting symptoms (after a year): anxiety, depression and PTSD, among family members [41]. This may be caused by the fact that families have a greater sense of control and causativeness if they are able to be close to the patient. The family is able to understand the clinical situation better and contact healthcare personnel. The effect may be long-lasting because by being close to the patient during hospitalization, caregivers could adapt to the changed situation, prepare themselves to potential death without sense of absence with their close person. Similarly to interventions based on the engagement of caregivers in care, which proved to improve the mental well-being of caregivers by reducing their anxiety [31, 60] and increasing their satisfaction with care [60].

Interventions may be provided in person, as well as by use of digital devices, like phones, applications, websites. This observation is consistent with the review conducted by Sak-Dankosky et al., who analyzed the impact of interventions on the improvement of well-being among families of oncological patients [67]. Although new technologies give wide opportunities for contact with the patient’s family or access to necessary knowledge and support, and interventions using such technologies are feasible, it is necessary to analyze what may be done to make them more effective. First of all, new technologies should not be used to substitute contact with healthcare personnel. The tools should be enable families to establish a contact with healthcare personnel if necessary. A key issue is to identify the family’s needs concerning information and contact with healthcare personnel in advance, as well as take into account the emotional state of the family who, in the face of the changed and stressful situation, will need clear and simple communications.

Although in the clinical practice, it is recommended that interdisciplinary teams should cooperate, the studies often prove that interventions are provided by nurses because they have the closest contact with patients [71]. Similar observations were made by Sevillano-Garayoa et al., in the review of interventions for caregivers caring for persons with advanced illness at home [68]. In our review, interventions were most frequently provided by nurses, as well. One study analyzed the impact of the implementation of an APN nurse at the ICU on well-being, satisfaction, depression, anxiety, PTSD symptoms after the ICU hospitalization. For families, it was important that the intervention should be implemented immediately after the ICU admittance of their close person [46]. Taking into account barriers frequently reported by ICU nurses, like the lack of time or the pressure of duties, the implementation of an APN nurse for families of patients may be an effective solutions. Firstly, the intervention could be implemented at an early stage when the family needs the greatest support. Secondly, the interventions could be better adjusted to needs of each family based on a prior assessment. In addition, the APN nurse may play a function of a “navigator” for families, who will make their communication with ICU personnel easier and help them understand how the specific ICU environment operates. Therefore, it would be necessary to implement APNs for caregivers in intensive care units and to continue research in this area. Compared to the mixed-methods review by Kiwanuka et al., which suggested that nurse-led and combined interventions may have small to moderate effects, our analysis presents a more nuanced view. While several multicomponent interventions involving education, emotional support, and structured communication showed consistent benefits, others—despite robust designs and large samples—did not result in significant psychological improvement. This suggests that the simple presence of multiple components does not guarantee effectiveness; rather, timing, individualization, and intensity of the intervention may be the determining factors [15].

The existing literature describes and studies many interventions addressed to families of ICU patients. Nevertheless, many of those interventions are only partially effective, e.g. they only reduce symptoms of depression, but do not influence anxiety, or are effective, but not permanent. Surprisingly, some of those interventions even worsened the families’ outcomes. In addition, the consequences of an ICU stay on caregivers can be long-lasting and may even intensify after discharge or death. In turn, the programs are usually short-term interventions and that is why may be efficient solely at specific points in time and are not permanent. In turn, the studies indicate that resilience may be an important protective factor for the mental well-being of family members of ICU patients [72]. Thus, it is necessary to examine whether the effectiveness and durability of interventions for families of ICU patients may be dependent on characteristics of families as such (e.g. the level of resilience) and whether it is not necessary to start with actions focused on the strengthening of such characteristics. This may, in turn, increase the effectiveness and durability of other interventions addressed to families of ICU patients.

Limitation

This review has several limitations that should be considered. Four databases were used to prepare this review. Although the selected databases are widely used sources of scientific research, the limited number of databases may have affected the scope of the literature searched and resulted in the omission of publications available in other sources (e.g., works published in local journals). In addition, limiting the review to studies published in English may have led to the exclusion of relevant research published in other languages. Furthermore, the protocol for this review was not published or registered a priori, which we also consider to be a limitation. The limitation concerning the ability to generalize the outcomes may be also caused by a small study group in some studies (20 studies recruited less than 100 participants). In addition, please note that the pilot studies typical for preliminary intervention studies were mainly conducted to assess the feasibility and acceptability of interventions prior to the full RCT and not to assess the size of effects. What is important, the studies rarely reported the size of effects, which forms a limitation to the assessment of the clinical significance of interventions.

Conclusion

The available literature describes many interventions addressed to families of ICU patients. Overall, the findings indicate that while several interventions can improve psychological and relational outcomes among ICU caregivers, the evidence base remains heterogeneous in methodological rigor, measurement consistency, and long-term follow-up. Further high-quality randomized trials with standardized outcome reporting are warranted to confirm and sustain these effects.

Implications for clinical practice

Caregivers of critically ill patients experience numerous psychological, physical, and social burdens, which is why a structured approach to providing support for caregivers should be implemented in clinical practice. Within the first 48–72 h of a patient’s admission to the ICU, an initial assessment of the caregiver’s mental health should be conducted, covering mental health symptoms (e.g., HADS), stress levels (e.g., PSS), and family needs (e.g., CCFNI), and the results should be documented and used to plan further interventions. Based on this assessment and its results, caregivers should be classified into a level of support. For example: Caregivers with low scores (in the low risk group for mental health deterioration) should receive standard education and access to informational materials. Caregivers with moderate scores should additionally receive scheduled supportive conversations, psychoeducation, and symptom monitoring. For caregivers in the high-risk group or those already showing clinical symptoms, intensive individual support (including a psychologist) should be implemented (a multicomponent intervention). Due to the workload and staff shortages in the ICU, which are often reported by staff, it is reasonable to create the position of an advanced practice nurse (APN) dedicated to caregivers, whose tasks would include conducting needs assessments, coordinating psychological support, conducting informational interviews, educating families, and monitoring stress symptoms, which would also relieve the nursing staff. However, the financial constraints faced by some hospitals may be a barrier to hiring a APN. Therefore, a more feasible solution may be to invest in targeted training for ICU nurses and to designate a leader responsible for implementing and carrying out interventions that support the families of ICU patients. This approach can improve the quality of care without significantly increasing costs. Interventions should be implemented as early as possible, when stress levels are at their highest, in order to limit the development of long-term psychological consequences. In addition, effective communication is extremely important in clinical practice. Communication should be standardized by using clear, non-technical language, adapting the amount and form of information to the perceptual abilities of caregivers, and checking for understanding. It should be emphasized that new technologies supporting communication can be used, but should not replace direct contact with medical staff. Flexible visiting policies and involving caregivers in simple caregiving tasks should also be considered, as this can have a positive impact on both the short-term and long-term mental functioning of caregivers. This approach, based on early identification of needs, risk detection, clearly defined interventions, and the designating of dedicated APN staff, could increase the feasibility of the recommendations and enable their practical implementation in ICU settings.
