Social Support and Family Contact Among Young People With a History of Homelessness in Canada
Authors: Tyler J Frederick, Nick Kerman, Stephen Gaetz, William O'Grady, Sean Kidd
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe aim of the study was to improve our understanding of social support‐ and family‐related dynamics and outcomes in a large sample of young people with a history of homelessness in Canada. The analysis used survey data from 934 people aged 16–24. Linear and logistic regression were used. The majority of the young people felt supported by friends and stable adults. The majority also sought improved relationships with family, with many having already started that work. There was a positive association between contact with family and wellbeing. However, a proportion of young people indicated a lack of support, and 30% reported having no interest in improving relationships with family. The findings provide support for initiatives aimed at cultivating the support networks of young people, but the findings also underscore the complexity of family and social support dynamics for young people with a history of homelessness.Keywords: family, homelessness, housing, quality of life, social determinants of health, social support, youth

Introduction

Youth and young adults (usually defined as individuals aged 13–25 years) make up approximately 20% of the homeless population in Canada (Gaetz et al. 2013; Employment and Social Development Canada 2018). Compared to adults experiencing homelessness, young people face a range of unique challenges related to their age and social status, including ageism, limited work history, less experience living independently, developmental social needs, and varied levels of maturity (Gaetz 2014; Karabanow 2004). In this context, social support has been shown to have a key role in how young people navigate the challenges of homelessness. For example, social support has been shown to affect resource acquisition (Stablein 2011), help seeking (Oliver and Cheff 2014), physical protection (McCarthy et al. 2002), risk behaviors (Wenzel et al. 2012), and transitions away from homelessness (Mayock et al. 2011; Mallett et al. 2010). Further, research shows increased wellbeing and reduced risk among young people who have higher levels of social support in general (Fulginiti et al. 2022; Barczyk et al. 2014; Wenzel et al. 2012; and Rice et al. 2007).

In this context, one particular area of interest is support from family members. Compared to their housed peers, youth with a history of homelessness often have less access to the resources, support, and social capital of their families and caregivers (Barker 2012). As a result, young people with a history of homelessness often rely on alternative forms of support, including a greater reliance on street‐based peers and service providers (Smith 2008; Stablein 2011).

In research from the past 15 years, contact with family members has been linked to a range of positive outcomes, including: reduced suicide risk (Fulginiti et al. 2022; Cleverley and Kidd 2011), fewer HIV risk behaviors (Rice et al. 2007), and a greater likelihood of exiting homelessness (Mallett et al. 2010). In light of this growing research, the paradigm of seeing the family as part of the ‘problem’ has been shifting in Canada and efforts have been made to develop and expand interventions aimed at strengthening family and ‘natural’ supports (existing non‐kin supportive adults). These kinds of supports are argued to be more sustainable and durable than professional or homelessness‐connected peer networks (Borato et al. 2020; Winland et al. 2011).

However, despite these positive associations with family support, the dynamics around family relationships and reconnection are complex. Rates of familial abuse and conflict are high among young people with a history of homelessness and so reconnection is not always wanted or desirable (Gaetz et al. 2016). Experiences of conflict and rejection can be particular pronounced among some groups, including 2SLGBTQ, young people and family denial and rejection can persist as young people attempt the transition away from homelessness (Abramovich and Kimura 2021; Abramovich et al. 2022). Similarly, research with wider populations finds that reconnecting with family members can be a source of stress and hardship (Frederick et al. 2022; Mayock et al. 2011).

The aim of the present study was to improve our understanding of this complexity by examining social support‐ and family‐related outcomes in a large sample of young people with a history of homelessness in Canada. We begin this analysis with a review of the relevant research on social capital and social support in the lives of young people experiencing homelessness.

Literature Review: Social Capital and Social Support

Social capital is a key concept for understanding how relationships impact the ways young people navigate homelessness (Barker 2012; Smith 2008; Stablein 2011). There are varied definitions of social capital, but they share an emphasis on how social networks provide resources and support, and a recognition that these social connections are important to pursuing goals and navigating social spaces (Brown et al. 2020; Bourdieu 1986; Coleman 1988; Oliver and Cheff 2014; Putnam 1995).

Young people with a history of homelessness are commonly regarded as lacking access to particular kinds of social capital because estrangement within the family home reduces their access to a key source of social capital that housed young people rely on—the social support and networks of their caregivers (Barker 2012; Smith 2008). Young people experiencing homelessness are found to have reduced access to practical forms of capital including financial support, guidance, and help accessing and navigating school and work settings. For example, de la Haye et al. (2012) explored social support at a dyadic level by asking youth to identify network members and the type of support they provide. They found that 56% of the members did not provide any support, 8% provided only tangible support, 13.8% only emotional support, and 22% provided both. Similarly, Falci et al. (2011) found that networks of emotional support are larger than those providing practical help.

Other research on social capital and social estrangement emphasizes the collective and trust‐based aspects of social capital (Oliver and Cheff 2014; Putnam 1995). Smith (2008), for example, asserted that it is not enough for young people experiencing homelessness to have individuals in their network, they must also trust and relate to those individuals (also see Stewart and Townley 2020). Thus, even if young people retain a relationship with their parents or other family members, those individuals may not serve as a meaningful source of support because of gaps in trust and reciprocity, as well as because the caregivers may have their own social and economic struggles (Barker 2012). In this context, the supportive role of peers and “street families” is emphasized for a range of support, such as emotional support, protection, information, and resource sharing (McCarthy et al. 2002; Stablein 2011; Smith 2008). However, young people also describe problems of trust in street‐based peer networks and conflict with peers is reported (Barker 2014; Gaetz et al. 2016). A recent analysis from Brown et al. (2020), used latent class analysis to identify different profiles of social support among 1046 young people experiencing homelessness. In their study, 68% of their sample belonged to the class reporting low/no support, whereas only 17% belonged to classes reporting varied types of support from home‐based networks. Absolute homelessness, depression, heroin use, and sexual minority status were associated with youth having low/no support. Young people with a history of foster care were more likely to be in the service provider support group.

Providing a longitudinal perspective, Falci et al. (2011) examined changes in social support over three years and found, that as young people experienced longer episodes of homelessness, the size of their emotional and instrumental networks declined. Young people with histories of abuse tended to lose home supports at a faster rate than others, but individuals who used substances had a slower decline of instrumental home support for an unknown reason. Encouragingly, the odds of reporting a family member in their network tended to increase over time for young people, suggesting the possibility of reconciliation. In other research with a focus on families, Milburn et al. (2005) find that young people with fewer ‘problem behaviors’ (contact with criminal justice system, substance use, and unprotected sex) were more likely to report family support one year later (also see Rice et al. 2007). However, in contrast to other research, they found that being female and having histories of abuse were not linked to the likelihood of reporting family support after one year.

Overall, the research on patterns in social support find that young people with a history of homelessness have better access to emotional supports than practical supports. Further, being male, having problematic substance use, and having histories of caregiver abuse, early life problems, and risk behaviors are associated with poorer social support and reduced likelihood of networks involving family members, though the findings are not unanimous. 2SLGBTQ+ young people also appear at risk of low social support, particularly as it relates to family support (Johnson et al. 2005).

Despite the valuable insights from this past research, important questions remain about what factors shape levels of social support and the presence of family members in a young person's network. One key limitation of the past research is the small sample sizes involved, which may be a factor in the variability of findings across samples. Moreover, few studies have looked specifically at the desire for family reconnection among young people with a history of homelessness. As most of the research was conducted a decade or longer ago, there is a need for further investigation into dynamics of social support and family contact given that the approach to support and social services has shifted over the past few decades. In particular, support policies are placing more emphasis on early intervention and prevention, including services aimed at building family and natural supports (FNS). Further, key pathways into youth homelessness, such as drug use, mental illness, immigration, and 2SLGBTQ+ identity have been the focus of societal shifts that may shape youths' relationships and support networks.

The 2019 Without a Home‐National Youth Homelessness Survey was the second pan‐Canadian, cross‐sectional study of young people experiencing homelessness (Kidd et al. 2021). Using data from the national survey, this study examined the nature of social support and family reconnection among this national sample. Accordingly, three research questions were addressed: [1] What factors predict levels of support from friends and family? [2] What factors predict contact with family members? and [3] What factors influence the desire for reconnection with family?

Materials and Methods

Participants and Recruitment

Recruitment began with the identification of community agencies across Canada that served youth experiencing homelessness. These agencies were identified by the Canadian Observatory on Homelessness, A Way Home Canada, and associated networks. A convenience sample of youth experiencing homelessness was then recruited from 98 community agencies in 49 communities across Canada. Youth were eligible to participate in the survey if they were receiving services at one or more of the 98 youth‐serving agencies. Staff engaged youth by informing them about the study purpose and activities and provided those interested with the consent form and survey materials. Participants received CAD $10 honorarium for completing the study. Written consent was obtained from all participants and the study was approved by the research ethics board at the lead institution.

No specific age criteria were used during data collection beyond participants being eligible for services at an agency that serves young people with a history of homelessness. For the present analysis, however, we selected for young people aged 16–24 years. The minimum age of 16 reflects when young people in Canada are typically regarded as being able to legally consent for themselves, and 24 is the upper limit reflected in the widely endorsed Canadian definition of youth homelessness and is a time when young people tend to ‘age out’ of youth supports and into reliance on the adult social support system (Canadian Observatory on Homelessness 2016). A total of 934 participants were included in the analysis based on having non‐missing responses for age, gender, sexuality, and racialization.

Measures

The survey was comprised of brief, self‐report instruments. Several standardized scales were shortened to prevent survey fatigue among participants, which is a common research concern with youth experiencing homelessness (Kidd et al. 2017). The survey was made available in paper copy and online formats. Data were collected over 14 weeks between January and April 2019.

For this analysis, relationships and social support was measured using a modified version of the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS; Zimet et al. 1988). Six MSPSS items used a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree” to measure social support from friends (α = 0.92). The component questions focus on themes of trust, emotional support, comfort, and help if things go wrong. Four additional items using the same 5‐point scale were used to assess receipt of emotional and instrumental support from a stably housed adult (α = 0.79). The component questions of this scale focus on emotional support, help in an emergency, and someone who will check‐in on them. Single items were also used to collect information on regular contact with family (yes/no) and desire to improve relationships with family members (dichotomized into yes/no).

Several family‐related background factors were used as predictor variables in the analysis. Five yes/no questions from the Adverse Childhood Experiences‐Questionnaire (ACE‐Q; Felitti et al. 1998; Liu et al. 2021) were summed to measure experiences of abuse (verbal, physical, sexual) and neglect. Respondents were also asked to identify present or past involvement in child protective services (yes/no). Three additional questions from the ACE‐Q were summed to gauge exposure to other adverse childhood experiences (problem drinking/use of street drugs, mental illness, or prison sentence among a household or family member). The analysis also included a single‐item (yes/no) that asked, “Did anyone try to help you when you became homeless the first time?”; and a single item variable assessing past contact with child protective services.

Other covariates in the analysis also included a number of foreground health and wellbeing measures. The Global Appraisal of Individual Needs–Short Screener (GAIN‐SS) was used to assess substance use problems in the past year (Dennis et al. 2006). The five items of the GAIN‐SS measured frequency of problems caused by substance use; responses of problems within the past year were summed to compute a total score (higher scores reflective of greater substance use problems) (α = 0.88). The GAIN‐SS has been used with adolescent and homeless populations in past research (Goering et al. 2011; Kidd et al. 2019). The survey also asked respondents about markers of resilience using 13 questions from the CD‐RISC (Connor and Davidson 2003). The items used a 5‐point, Likert‐type scale to assess feelings of self‐worth, self‐efficacy, and coping. The select items showed excellent internal consistency (α = 0.95). A single item with a 5‐point, Likert‐type scale ranging from “very poor” to “very good” was also used to assess quality of life. Respondents were also asked to identify their current housing status; homelessness was defined as staying in a shelter or temporary setting.

Single items were used to collect sociodemographic information on gender, age, racialized identity, Indigenous identity, and sexual orientation. Racialized and Indigenous identity were amalgamated to address some challenges related to the phrasing of the survey questions on racialized identity.

Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to describe the sample. The study's research questions were assessed using ordinary least squares linear regression models for the continuous dependent variables (social support from friends and social support from stable adults) and logistic regression models for the dichotomous variables (family contact and desire for reconnection). There were some modest correlations (rs ≤ 0.43) between predictor variables, but no evidence of violation to the assumptions of linear and logistic regression models. Variables were entered in a single step for all four models.

Given that all survey items were voluntary and many youth completed the study autonomously, there was a sizeable amount of missing data (range: 0%–42%). Multiple imputation by fully conditional specification (predictive mean matching) was used to produce 40 imputations, each with a maximum of 40 iterations, to address missing data, which were suspected to be not missing at random. All variables were included in the imputation model, but the missing values for the demographic, family contact and desire for reconnection variables were not imputed. The questions used to construct the friend support and adult support scales were imputed, but participants were removed from the regression analysis if data were missing on two or more of the items for adult support, or more than three of the six items for friend support. Multiple imputation produces less biased estimates than other methods of handling missing data that are not missing at random (van Ginkel et al. 2020; Woods et al. 2021). Findings are presented as the pooled estimates for the multiply imputed data. All analyses were conducted in SPSS 28.

The mean age was 20.1 years and 46.0% of the sample reported being currently housed. Further, over half the participants reported a history of child welfare involvement. Overall, this demographic profile of the sample is consistent with other Canadian research of young people with a history of homelessness, including the first iteration of the survey ([anonymized for review]).

Similarly, for adult support, 60% of the sample was over 12 (equivalent to agree or strongly agree if averaged) with a mean of 11.4 and an SD of 3.7. Two ordinary least squares linear regression models were performed to predict support from friends and a stably housed adult. Friend support was higher among those with higher quality of life (b = 0.731), resilience (b = 0.108), and among female identified young people (b = 1.003). In contrast, support from friends was lower among racialized young people (b = −1.646) and those reporting substance use problems (b = −0.303). Adult social support was higher among those reporting higher quality of life (b = 0.802), resilience (b = 0.024), and those in housing (b = 0.953). Support from an adult was lower among youth reporting more childhood abuse and neglect (b = −0.143), and for racialized young people (b = −0.606). All significant predictors of social support had small effect sizes, with the exception of quality of life for adult support, which had a moderate effect size.

Family Contact and Desire for Reconnection

Most participants reported monthly or more frequent contact with members of their family (70%). The most common sources of family contact were mothers (65%), fathers (40%), and siblings (38%); however, this information should be interpreted with caution due to the high number of respondents (37%) who were unsure or chose not to answer.

The results of the logistic regression showed that family contact was higher among those who received help when first homeless (OR = 1.55), whereas childhood abuse and neglect had a negative association (OR = 0.814) (see Table 4). Specific to early help, it was reported by two‐thirds of the sample and was most commonly provided by friends and social service workers. Less than one‐third (18%) of participants indicated that a parent provided them support when they first became homeless. This information should also be interpreted cautiously though, as almost half the sample were unsure or chose not to identify the source of the early help. Family contact was also higher among female (OR = 1.345) and transgender identified youth (OR = 1.591), as well as those with housing (OR = 1.436), yet lower among individuals that identify as LGBQ (OR = 0.656). Quality of life was positively correlated with family contact. Of note, family contact and adult support were moderately correlated (r = 0.312; p < 0.001), suggesting that the sources of adult support extend beyond the family.

Discussion

This study aimed to understand the factors associated with social support, family contact, and desire for family reconnection among a large sample of Canadian young people with a history of homelessness. The findings indicated that many young people feel supported by the friends and stably housed adults in their life despite their unstable housing history. The elements of supportive friendships considered in this analysis included trust, comfort, emotional support, and help when there is a problem. The adult support we measured included emotional support, help in an emergency, and someone checking in on them. Despite these encouraging findings, it is important to note that a substantial segment of young people reported a lack of support, with male and racialized youth, and those with problematic substance use and histories of abuse being at particular risk of insufficient support. These findings are consistent with past research on homeless young people (Brown et al. 2020; Tyler et al. 2019); however, the social isolation of racialized young people remains an under‐researched issue and future research is needed on the contributory factors of low support among racialized young people.

Overall, the study findings align with a growing evidence base that affirms the promise and the complexity of family relationships among young people with a history of homelessness (Mayock et al. 2011; Falci et al. 2011; Frederick et al. 2022). The research demonstrates that many young people with a history of homelessness can retain relationships with family members even in the context of homelessness and that family members can be a source of support (Brown et al. 2020; Mallett et al. 2010). Further, most participants in this study had a desire for improving relationships with their family members, which is consistent with work elsewhere on the desire for family contact (Mayock et al. 2011; Winland et al. 2011).

For participants in contact with family members, similar to other research in this area, we find that family contact is associated with wellbeing and greater stability (higher quality of life, resilience, and housing) (Barczyk et al. 2014). These findings suggest that family contact might have positive benefits on the lives and stability of young people with a history of homelessness although the causal direction of the relationship is not entirely clear. As it relates to housing specifically, the association could indicate that supportive adults and family members are helping the individuals in the sample to find housing. Housing could also provide a degree of stability wherein it is easier for adults to find youth, or the housing provides a more stable base for contact and communications between young people and their support networks.

The positive association between family contact and markers of wellbeing and stability validates emerging support models such as FNS that help young people with a history of homelessness to connect with supportive adults in their existing network (Borato et al. 2020; Change Collective 2019). FNS work can function broadly as a service philosophy that more actively considers and incorporates family members and natural supports across a range of practices, or as a more specific intervention (Borato et al. 2020). As an intervention, FNS work primarily involves counseling or skills building for young people and their family members/supports (as defined by the young person) but can also include referral work and the provision of practical and financial supports for the young person and their support network. The goals of FNS depend on individual circumstances, but they can include helping youth to return home by addressing conflict in the home, promoting recontact and gradual relationship building with family members (without a return to home), helping young people to identify sources of durable support outside their family networks, and helping young people to emotionally process family conflict and rejection (Borato et al. 2020). There is a growing knowledge base about FNS that demonstrates positive impacts (Harper et al. 2015; Main and Ledene 2019; Winland et al. 2011) and there have been growing calls to promote its use across youth serving agencies as the current availability of the model is limited (Somefun et al. 2023).

FNS work has a potential role to play in preventing homelessness by helping to stabilize young people or rehouse them with family members or other supportive adults in their lives. However, at present, FNS supports are typically accessed through emergency shelter providers which can limit their preventive impact. In this regard, policy efforts should focus on ways to enhance access to FNS‐type supports before homelessness occurs. There are promising support models that are located further upstream that can support family relationships including school‐based programs (MacKenzie et al. 2024), shelter diversion programs (Main and Ledene 2019), and integrated service hubs for youth (Turpin et al. 2024). These services can help connect young people and their families with counseling and support prior to incidents of homelessness.

Despite the findings in this research that show positive associates with family contact, the findings also demonstrate that family dynamics remain complex and that more research is needed to support the development of FNS and other intervention models. One such complexity is the desire for family reconnection, which this research shows is variable and hard to predict. The results show that 30% of the youth were not interested in family reconnection and a further 26% were interested but yet to start the process. This suggests that over half the sample are experiencing significant barriers or hesitancies to re‐establishing contact with family. Additionally, though not examined in this analysis, there is qualitative research that underscores that, even when a desire exists, establishing FNS can be a complex and slow process defined by small gains rather than wholesale transformation (Mayock et al. 2011). These findings are important because they highlight the need for targeted programming that addresses the long‐term community and relationship needs of young people with limited or unviable family connections. It also underlines, for FNS and other relationship supports, the importance of incorporating non‐kin supports such as “chosen families” or other natural supports into the model.

Another complexity related to this research concerns the possibility that the connections between family contact and wellbeing may not be causal and may be impacted by unmeasured characteristics of a young person and their experiences. For example, the association between greater resilience, quality of life, and family contact, in some instances, could be an indicator of young people who have the capacity to establish and maintain family connections in the face of complexity, rather than a causal outcome of those relationships. This possibility needs additional research, but this idea is supported by research showing that ‘resilient’ young people can be at‐risk of assuming demanding caregiver roles for parents and siblings with mental, cognitive, and physical health challenges (Frederick et al. 2022). It should be noted that FNS work can be helpful in these situations for supporting young people to set boundaries and maintain healthy relationships in the face of demanding relationships (Borato et al. 2020).

Another complexity to consider is that there is a subgroup of youth with characteristics and experiences that make accessing supports more difficult for them across the life course. For example, there may be young people whose mental health or developmental needs make it more difficult for them to access supports, including FNS. To this point, several studies, including this one, have demonstrated negative impacts of childhood abuse, early problem behaviors, and conduct disorder on future levels of social support (Falci et al. 2011; Milburn et al. 2005). Moffitt's (1997) developmental perspective is useful here for highlighting the ways in which developmental traits can interact with complex home environments to set young people on high‐risk trajectories. The results of the present analysis lend support to this possibility by showing that receiving help when first experiencing homelessness was positively associated with family contact and a desire for reconnection, suggesting the impact of early differences among youth. Similarly, as it relates to the positive association between family contact and housing, it is plausible that some of this association is explained by inequities in housing pathways that could be inadvertently selecting particular sub‐groups of young people, such as those who are more networked and less marginalized. These ideas are given support by research suggesting that individuals with ‘complex needs’ can be labeled as difficult clients within the emergency support system and can be de‐prioritized for services (Quirouette 2016). Intervention with these young people requires that the relevant service sectors intervene sooner to support the underlying causes of social isolation, including untreated trauma and mental illness, and developmental disorders. It also points to the necessity of evaluation and review processes aimed at rooting out potential biases and inequities in service provision.

Conclusion

This study had several limitations. First, questionnaires were independently completed with youth and not checked for completion by service agency staff, resulting in higher rates of missing data. Multiple imputation was used to mitigate the impacts of this limitation. Secondly, the stability of youth who had housing was not assessed. As these youth continued to use services aimed at homeless youth, their housing may have been precarious, unsafe, or temporary, making these youth potentially less distinct from participants without housing. Third, Indigenous identity was subsumed under racialized identity in the dataset, which obscures the unique experiences of Indigenous young people and the dichotomization of the variable obscures important differences between racialized groups. Fourth, the stability of youth's desire for family reconnection over time was unknown. As desire may fluctuate based on dynamics in a young person's circumstances and relationships this question warrants further research and may have implications for family reconnection interventions. Fifth and finally, the original dataset does not record if young people are living with family members at the time of the interview and so this could be impacting the findings, including exaggerating the connection between family support and housing. Despite these limitations, this study provides important insights into social support and family reconnections dynamics among young people experiencing homelessness using a large, national sample from Canada.
