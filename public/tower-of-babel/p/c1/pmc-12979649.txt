Multi-objective optimization of hybrid laser cleaning process parameters for carbon deposits based on bayesian-SVR and NSGA-II
Authors: Yishun Su, Yong Hu, Qunli Zhang, Zhehe Yao, Zhijun Chen, Yanyi Yin, Zhenzhen Yang, Jianhua Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hybrid laser cleaning combines continuous-wave (CW) laser preheating with pulsed laser ablation to remove piston crown carbon deposits, but conventional tuning cannot simultaneously minimize surface roughness (Sa) and carbon residue rate (RC). Four machine learning models—Support Vector Regression (SVR), XGBoost, Random Forest (RF), and Backpropagation Neural Network (BPNN)—are compared. SVR achieves optimal Sa prediction (Test R2 = 0.9874, bootstrap R2 = 0.985–0.998). For RC, Bayesian optimization improves SVR from R2 = 0.5658 to R2 = 0.8965 (58.45% increase). Feature analysis reveals divergent sensitivities: Sa responds equally to all parameters, while RC is dominated by frequency and CW laser power. NSGA-II generates 66 Pareto-optimal solutions within narrow parameter windows, identifying three operational regimes: low-Sa (0.894μm, RC = 4.3%), balanced (0.958μm, RC = 3%), and low-RC (RC = 1.9%, Sa = 1.209μm). Comprehensive validation—bootstrap resampling, cross-validation, SHAP analysis, and sensitivity testing—confirms model reliability despite limited data. This framework enables intelligent laser cleaning optimization, reducing trial-and-error costs while providing insights into laser–material interactions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41748-0.

Introduction

Carbon deposits (carbon buildup) easily accumulate on the crown of engine pistons, which not only reduces combustion efficiency, increases fuel consumption and emissions, but may also cause engine knocking, power loss, and even engine failure1. Traditional physical or chemical cleaning methods suffer from low efficiency, potential damage to the base material, and environmental pollution2,3, making them unsuitable for meeting the dual requirements of high quality and environmental friendliness in modern engine remanufacturing and maintenance. In recent years, laser cleaning technology4–6 has emerged as a research hotspot in the field of carbon deposit removal due to its advantages such as environmental friendliness, non-contact processing, and high automation. In particular, the emergence of hybrid laser cleaning technology7–9, which combines the synergistic effects of CW and pulsed lasers, has significantly improved cleaning efficiency and surface quality. Research has shown that this technology not only reduces the energy requirements of a single laser beam, mitigates thermal stress concentration, and minimizes thermal damage to aluminum alloy substrates, but is also particularly suitable for the removal of carbon deposits that exhibit multi-layered structures10,11. The final effectiveness of hybrid laser cleaning is influenced by the coupling of multiple process parameters, such as the power ratio between CW and pulsed lasers, cleaning speed, and repetition frequency. How to reasonably configure these parameters to achieve multi-objective optimization of surface quality and removal efficiency12,13 has become one of the core challenges in advancing this technology toward engineering applications. However, existing studies have not yet fully optimized the process parameters for hybrid laser cleaning of carbon deposits, and there is still a lack of targeted models and experimental support.

Surface roughness (Sa) and carbon residue rate (RC) are two important indicators for evaluating the effectiveness of laser cleaning in removing carbon deposits. A lower surface roughness helps maintain the original properties of the workpiece surface, while a lower carbon residue rate directly affects the cleanliness of the cleaning process. In practical applications, these two indicators often exhibit a certain degree of trade-off. For example, overly pursuing a low carbon residue rate may lead to surface damage of the base material, thereby increasing surface roughness. Therefore, how to ensure cleaning effectiveness while maintaining surface quality and achieving multi-objective optimization holds significant practical significance. However, current methods for optimizing carbon laser cleaning processes primarily rely on extensive experiments and empirical judgment, which are not only time-consuming and labor-intensive but also struggle to identify the global optimal solution. A few researchers have begun to explore response surface methods (RSM) for process parameter optimization. Ding et al.14 focused on the surface quality characteristics after laser cleaning of carbon black layers on 6061 aluminum alloy surfaces as the main subject of their study. They used the response surface method to establish a mapping relationship between process parameters and surface quality, investigated the influence and patterns of laser cleaning parameters on surface roughness and carbon removal rate, and combined the response surface method with genetic algorithms to establish a multi-objective optimization model. Yao et al.15 conducted a systematic optimization study based on the response surface method for hybrid laser cleaning of piston carbon deposits. Although the response surface method offers improvements over traditional optimization techniques, it is still limited by the rigid structure of the quadratic polynomial model, making it difficult to capture the complex nonlinear effects in the laser cleaning process. It also depends heavily on experimental design quality, and in multi-objective optimization, the need for manually assigned weights introduces subjectivity and increases experimental effort. In recent years, with the development of machine learning technology16, data-driven17,18 methods have been used to establish predictive models between process parameters and cleaning effects, and combined with optimization algorithms to find the optimal parameter combination, becoming a new research trend. Machine learning models such as Support Vector Regression (SVR), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Random Forest (RF), and Back Propagation Neural Network (BPNN) have been widely applied in fields such as materials processing and process optimization19–22. However, different models exhibit significant performance variations across different application scenarios. Selecting the most suitable model for a specific problem and further optimizing its performance is crucial for achieving high-precision prediction. For multi-objective optimization problems, the Non-Dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-II)23 is a classic and efficient evolutionary algorithm that can effectively identify a set of Pareto optimal solutions24,25, providing decision-makers with multiple optimization schemes with different preferences24. Combining machine learning prediction models with multi-objective optimization algorithms such as NSGA-II can provide a powerful tool for target optimization in complex processes like hybrid laser cleaning.

This study aims to investigate the influence of key parameters (CW laser power (Pc), pulsed laser power (Pp), Y-direction cleaning speed Vy, and repetition frequency f) on surface roughness (Sa) and carbon residue rate (RC) in hybrid laser cleaning processes. First, through a systematic comparison of four machine learning models—SVR, XGBoost, RF, and BPNN—the most suitable models for predicting Sa and RC are selected. Second, for the RC prediction model, a Bayesian optimization method is introduced to further enhance its performance. Finally, the NSGA-II algorithm is employed to perform multi-objective optimization of the process parameters, aiming to obtain a set of Pareto optimal process parameter combinations that simultaneously satisfy the requirements of low Sa and low RC. The results of this study demonstrate the feasibility of combining machine learning models with evolutionary algorithms to accurately predict cleaning outcomes and optimize process parameters. This approach provides a data-driven framework for achieving both high surface quality and efficient carbon removal in hybrid laser cleaning applications.

Dataset description and preprocessing

The experimental data used in this study were obtained from a structured design-of-experiments workflow established during a previous investigation15 on response surface optimization of hybrid laser cleaning. The dataset was generated through single-factor experiments, Plackett-Burman screening, steepest-ascent experiments, Box-Behnken design, and additional central composite design experiments. This DOE strategy ensures sufficient parameter diversity, orthogonality, and curvature representation within the design space. In total, 81 valid experimental datasets were collected, covering four key process parameters and two evaluation metrics related to cleaning performance. The input process parameters include: CW laser power (Pc, %), pulsed laser power (Pp, %), Y-direction cleaning speed (Vy, mm/s), and repetition frequency (f, kHz); the output evaluation metrics are surface roughness (Sa, µm) and carbon residue rate (RC, %). Unless otherwise specified, all laser power percentages mentioned hereafter refer to a proportion of their respective maximum output power. In this study, the maximum output power of the CW laser is 3000 W, and that of the pulsed laser is 300 W.

Statistical analysis of the raw experimental data (as shown in Table 2) indicates that process parameters such as CW laser power (Pc), pulsed laser power (Pp), cleaning speed (Vy), and repetition frequency (f) exhibit certain distribution characteristics in the experiments. The dataset includes 81 samples, involving CW laser power (Pc, mean 20.62%, range 5–45%), pulsed laser power (Pp, mean 78.40%, range 55–95%), cleaning speed (Vy, mean 19.51 mm/s, range 5–45 mm/s), and repetition frequency (f, mean 35.86 kHz, range 10–50 kHz). The response variables surface roughness (Sa, mean 1.200 μm, range 0.871–2.906 μm) and carbon residue rate (RC, mean 4.77%, range 0.14–10.33%) exhibit moderate variability, with standard deviations of 0.386 μm and 2.3%, respectively. The parameter distributions are concentrated around the median, indicating a well-balanced experimental design suitable for capturing the relationship between input parameters and cleaning effectiveness. These statistical characteristics provide a data foundation for subsequent process parameter optimization based on Bayesian-SVR and NSGA-II, reflecting the potential associations between parameters and objectives, and offering a reference for achieving a hybrid cleaning process with low roughness and low carbon residue rates.

Data preprocessing steps primarily include feature extraction and data standardization. Relevant input parameters and output indicator columns are extracted from the original experimental data and uniformly named for easier processing. Subsequently, the input features (Pc, Pp, Vy, f) are standardized using the StandardScaler method26, converting the data to a distribution with a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1. Standardization helps eliminate the impact of differences in units and numerical ranges between features on model training, improving convergence speed and prediction accuracy. Standardization is performed independently on the input features of the Sa dataset and the RC dataset.

Prediction Model Construction and Comparison

To identify the most suitable model for Sa and RC prediction, this study compared four commonly used machine learning regression models: Support Vector Regression (SVR), XGBoost, Random Forest, and BP Neural Network.

Support Vector Regression (SVR)

SVR is a regression method based on Support Vector Machines (SVM)19, which performs regression prediction by finding a hyperplane that minimizes the total deviation of all sample points from the hyperplane. For nonlinear problems27, SVR maps data to a high-dimensional feature space using kernel functions and then performs linear regression in the high-dimensional space. In this study, the hyperparameters of the SVR model28,29 include the penalty coefficient C, kernel type, kernel coefficient gamma, and the parameter epsilon of the insensitivity loss function.

Extreme Gradient Boosting Trees (XGBoost)

XGBoost is an ensemble learning algorithm based on gradient boosting decision trees20, which sequentially constructs multiple decision tree models, with each new tree attempting to correct the prediction errors of the previous trees, thereby gradually improving the overall prediction accuracy. The main hyperparameters of XGBoost include the number of trees (n_estimators), the maximum depth of the trees (max_depth), and the learning rate (learning_rate).

Random Forest (RF)

Random Forest21 is an ensemble learning method based on decision trees. It improves prediction performance and reduces overfitting by constructing multiple decision trees and taking the average (for regression problems) or majority vote (for classification problems). The main hyperparameters of Random Forest include the number of trees (n_estimators), the maximum depth of trees (max_depth), and the minimum number of samples required for node splitting (min_samples_split).

Backpropagation Neural Network (BPNN)

BP (backpropagation) neural networks22 are feedforward neural networks that train network parameters using the backpropagation algorithm. The BP neural network used in this study consists of two hidden layers, with main hyperparameters including the number of hidden layer neurons (hidden_layers) and the learning rate (learning_rate).

Model Training and Evaluation Methods

The dataset is divided into training and test sets in an 8:2 ratio30. To ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the results, all random processes are set with a fixed random seed (42). Model evaluation uses two metrics: mean squared error (MSE) and coefficient of determination (R2) on the test set, along with the average coefficient of determination (CV R2 Mean) from 5-fold cross-validation to assess the model’s generalization ability.

To comprehensively consider the model’s performance on the test set and cross-validation, a weighted score is defined: Weighted Score = 0.5 × Test R2 + 0.3 × CV R2 Mean + 0.2 × Test MSE. Hyperparameter optimization is performed using the grid search (GridSearchCV) method combined with 5-fold cross-validation. The hyperparameter search ranges for each model are shown in Table 3.

Bayesian optimization of the SVR model

For RC prediction, after preliminary model comparison, the SVR model with better performance was selected for further optimization. Bayesian optimization31 is an efficient hyperparameter optimization method. It constructs a probabilistic surrogate model of the objective function (e.g., the model performance on the validation set) and uses a sampling function to determine the next most promising hyperparameter combination for evaluation. Compared with grid search, this method is usually more efficient in finding the optimal hyperparameter combination.

The search space for Bayesian optimization is defined as follows: C: Real(0.1, 100, prior=’log-uniform’); epsilon: Real(0.001, 0.5, prior=’log-uniform’); gamma: Real(0.001, 1.0, prior=’log-uniform’); kernel: Categorical([‘rbf’]); The number of optimization iterations is set to 50, with 5-fold cross-validation used, and the evaluation metric is the negative mean squared error.

Multi-objective optimization algorithm (NSGA-II)

After obtaining accurate Sa and RC prediction models, this study employs the non-dominated sorting genetic algorithm II (NSGA-II) for multi-objective optimization to identify process parameter combinations that simultaneously minimize Sa and RC. NSGA-II is a widely used multi-objective evolutionary algorithm whose core ideas include rapid non-domination sorting, crowding degree calculation, and elite strategy32,33, enabling the effective acquisition of a solution set that is widely distributed and close to the true Pareto front. The optimization objective is defined as: Minimize F(x) = [Sa(x), RC(x)], where x = [Pc, Pp, Vy, f] is the process parameter vector.

The execution process of the NSGA-II algorithm is as follows: (1) Initialize the population: Randomly generate an initial population within the predefined range of process parameters, with the population size set to 100. (2) Fitness evaluation: For each individual in the population (i.e., a set of process parameters), use the pre-trained SVR model and the Bayesian-optimized SVR model to predict their corresponding Sa and RC values. (3) Non-dominance sorting: Sort the current population based on the predicted Sa and RC values, dividing the population into different non-dominated fronts. (4) Crowding distance calculation: Calculate the crowding distance for each individual in each non-dominated front to maintain solution diversity. (5) Selection, crossover, and mutation: Offspring are generated using tournament selection, simulated binary crossover (SBX), and polynomial mutation. The crossover probability is set to 0.9, while the mutation probability is set to 0.1 to balance exploration and exploitation. (6) Merging and elitism strategy: The parent and offspring populations are merged, followed by non-dominated sorting and crowding distance calculation. The top-ranked individuals are selected as the new parent population for the next iteration. The optimization runs for 50 generations, and convergence is achieved when no significant improvement in the Pareto front is observed. To ensure fair comparison between objectives, Sa and RC were normalized to the range [0, 1] before fitness evaluation. Through the NSGA-II optimization, a set of Pareto-optimal solutions was obtained, representing the optimal combinations of process parameters under different trade-offs between surface quality (Sa) and carbon removal efficiency (RC).

Model validation and robustness analysis

Bootstrap resampling

Model stability was assessed through bootstrap resampling with 200 iterations, randomly sampling 80% of the dataset (n = 81) with replacement for each iteration. R2 distributions were computed to quantify prediction variance and identify potential overfitting.

Cross-validation

Five-fold cross-validation partitioned the dataset into 5 equal subsets, with each fold serving once as the test set while the remaining 4 folds trained the model. Median R2, interquartile range (IQR), and fold-wise variance were calculated to evaluate generalization capability.

Feature importance analysis

Random Forest permutation importance was calculated by randomly shuffling each parameter’s values and measuring the resulting decrease in model accuracy. Importance scores were averaged across 5 CV folds with standard deviations reported. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values quantified each parameter’s contribution to individual predictions, with beeswarm plots visualizing nonlinear effects.

Sensitivity analysis

Parametric robustness was tested by perturbing each input parameter by ± 5% individually while holding others constant. Mean output change was computed to quantify parameter influence on prediction stability.

Multi-objective optimization visualization

Pareto front solution density was analyzed using hexagonal binning to identify preferred operational trade-offs. Parallel coordinate plots visualized parameter correlations across all non-dominated solutions.

Characterization methods

To comprehensively assess both surface quality and cleaning efficiency of piston crown carbon deposits after hybrid laser cleaning, multiple characterization techniques were employed. Surface roughness was measured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, Keyence Corporation, Osaka, Japan) to quantitatively evaluate changes in surface morphology. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, ZEISS EVO18, Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany) was utilized to observe the microstructural morphology of the surface before and after cleaning. Additionally, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis was performed using a silicon drift detector at 15 kV (Nano X Flash Detector 5010, Bruker Corporation, Billerica, MA, USA) to investigate changes in the elemental distribution, focusing primarily on carbon and aluminum, to verify the degree of carbon removal. To minimize carbon contamination and measurement drift, the EDS acquisition time for each area was limited to approximately 60 s under high vacuum conditions, and the samples were analyzed immediately after laser cleaning to reduce potential carbon redeposition within the chamber. In our study, the RC value is defined as the mass percentage of carbon remaining on the piston surface after cleaning relative to the mass percentage of the initial carbon before cleaning, as measured by EDS (weight%, wt%).

Model robustness and validation

Bootstrap validation

To assess model stability beyond simple train-test splits, bootstrap resampling (n = 200 iterations, 80% sampling with replacement) (Fig. 5) was performed. For Sa prediction, the SVR model demonstrated exceptional robustness with R2 values concentrated between 0.985 and 0.998. The RC model showed broader variance (R2 range: 0.50–0.92), reflecting the greater complexity of carbon residue prediction, yet maintained acceptable predictive capability.

Five-fold cross-validation (Fig. 6) revealed consistent performance across data partitions. Feature importance

Random Forest permutation importance analysis (averaged across 5 CV folds) identified divergent parameter sensitivities for the two objectives (Fig. 7). For Sa, all four parameters showed comparable importance (Pc = 0.30 ± 0.04, f = 0.29 ± 0.04, Vy = 0.25 ± 0.08, Pp = 0.16 ± 0.06), suggesting complex nonlinear interactions. For RC, pulsed laser frequency (f) dominated (0.38 ± 0.02), followed by CW power (Pc = 0.29 ± 0.03), while Pp and Vy contributed equally (0.16 ± 0.04 and 0.17 ± 0.03). This asymmetry explains the difficulty in simultaneous optimization.

Quantitative analysis of initial condition effects

The impact of initial carbon deposit conditions on prediction performance was quantitatively assessed. Initial deposit thickness ranged from 45.22 to 111.40 μm (coefficient of variation: 17.1%), while initial surface roughness varied from 2.00 to 5.77 μm (CV: 21.8%). Correlation analysis revealed weak relationships between initial conditions and target variables: initial thickness showed r = 0.099 (p = 0.37) with carbon residue rate (RC), while initial roughness exhibited r = 0.130 (p = 0.24) with RC. Although these correlations are not statistically significant at α = 0.05, they indicate potential confounding effects contributing to prediction variance.

Parameter space coverage analysis (Fig. 10) identified that the current dataset covers approximately 40.4% of the theoretical parameter space. Figure 10(a) demonstrates a weak positive correlation (r = 0.099, p = 0.37) between initial deposit thickness and RC. The wide vertical scatter at any given thickness value indicates that thickness alone explains minimal RC variance, with process parameters (color-coded by power ratio) exerting dominant influence. Figure 10(b) reveals a weak negative correlation (r=-0.092) between initial surface roughness and final Sa, possibly indicating that rougher initial surfaces (often associated with thicker deposits) achieve better smoothness after cleaning. Bayesian Optimization

Bayesian optimization was applied to the baseline SVR model to improve RC prediction performance within the constraints of the 81-sample dataset. Table 6 compares the performance metrics before and after optimization. The Bayesian-optimized SVR model showed improvements across all metrics, with Test R2 increasing from 0.5658 to 0.8965 (58.45% improvement), RMSE decreasing from 1.3961% to 0.6772%, MAE decreasing from 1.0664% to 0.4953%, and MAPE decreasing from 25.1946% to 12.0617%. In cross-validation, CV R2 Mean increased from 0.5759 to 0.7336, while CV R2 Std decreased from 0.2676 to 0.2226.

While these improvements are statistically significant (paired t-test, p < 0.05), they must be interpreted considering three critical factors: (1) the dataset size (n = 81) limits definitive conclusions about model superiority, (2) the prediction uncertainty quantified above (95% CI: ±2.74%), and (3) learning curve analysis indicating validation error has not converged, suggesting current performance represents preliminary estimates rather than the model’s true capacity. Figure 11 shows the Bayesian-optimized SVR predictions align closely with true values for most test samples. Figure 12 demonstrates that the Bayesian optimization process converged after approximately 40 iterations, with CV-MSE stabilizing near 0.5. Multi-objective optimization results (NSGA-II)

Using the trained Sa prediction model (SVR) and RC prediction model (Bayesian-optimized SVR), combined with the NSGA-II algorithm, multi-objective optimization was performed on the laser cleaning process parameters (Pc, Pp, Vy, f) to simultaneously minimize Sa and RC. After 50 generations of evolution, the algorithm converged and obtained a set of Pareto optimal solutions. These solutions represent the optimal process parameter combinations under different Sa and RC preferences.

Pareto front analysis

Figure 14(a) shows the obtained Pareto front. Each point in the figure represents a non-dominated solution, corresponding to a specific set of process parameters and their predicted Sa and RC values. As can be seen from the figure, there is a trade-off relationship between Sa and RC: generally, pursuing a lower Sa may lead to a slight increase in RC, and vice versa. Decision-makers can select the most suitable process parameter scheme from the Pareto front based on actual production requirements.

NSGA-II optimization (population = 100, generations = 50) generated 66 non-dominated solutions forming a continuous Pareto front (Fig. 14(b)). Density distribution analysis revealed three distinct operational regimes: (1) Low-Sa region (Sa < 0.92 μm, RC = 3.8–4.2%), (2) Balanced region (Sa = 0.93–1.05 μm, RC = 2.5–3.5%), and (3) Low-RC region (RC < 2.2%, Sa = 1.1–1.2 μm). The hexagonal binning shows highest solution density at Sa ≈ 0.93 μm and RC ≈ 3.3%, indicating a preferred trade-off zone.

Parameter trade-off mechanisms

Parallel coordinate analysis of 66 Pareto-optimal solutions revealed systematic parameter-performance correlations (Fig. 15). Low-Sa solutions (Sa < 0.95 μm, purple lines) concentrated at Pc = 22.9–25.2%, Pp = 76.8–80.6%, Vy = 15.4–16.1 mm/s, and f = 36.2–36.5 kHz, while high-Sa solutions (Sa > 1.05 μm, Pale green) required elevated Pc (27.9–30.3%), Pp (82.9–85.7%), and f (36.6–37.4 kHz) with reduced Vy (14.2–15.2 mm/s). RC-based coloring exposed the inverse relationship: low-residue solutions (RC < 2.2%, purple) demanded high energy input (Pc > 27.9%, Pp > 82.9%) and slow scanning (Vy < 15.1 mm/s), while high-residue solutions (RC > 3.5%, blue) operated at lower power settings. Three representative solutions quantified this trade-off: minimum-Sa (0.894 μm, RC = 4.3%) at (Pc, Pp, Vy, f) = (22.9%, 76.8%, 16.0 mm/s, 36.4 kHz), minimum-RC (RC = 1.9%, Sa = 1.209 μm) at (Pc, Pp, Vy, f) = (30.3%, 85.7%, 14.2 mm/s, 37.4 kHz), and balanced (0.958 μm, 3%) at (Pc, Pp, Vy, f) = (25.8%, 81.0%, 15.8 mm/s, 36.5 kHz). The narrow parameter ranges (ΔPc = 7.4%, ΔPp = 8.9%, ΔVy = 1.9 mm/s, Δf = 1.2 kHz) and strong Pc-Pp-f positive correlation with inverse Vy dependence indicate Pareto optimality exists within a constrained operational manifold where thermal loading and ablation efficiency are precisely balanced.

Discussion

Model selection and performance analysis

Among the models evaluated on this 81-sample dataset, SVR demonstrated superior performance for both Sa (Test R2: 0.9874, RMSE: 0.089 μm) and RC (Test R2: 0.8965, RMSE: 1.17%). This advantage can be attributed to SVR’s regularization mechanism and kernel-based approach, which are particularly effective for small, structured datasets where overfitting risk is substantial.

However, several limitations constrain generalizability of this finding. First, the modest dataset size (n = 81) limits statistical power to definitively establish model superiority. Learning curve analysis (Fig. 13(a)) reveals validation error has not converged, indicating model rankings may shift with additional data. Second, parameter space coverage of only 40.4% (Fig. 13(c)) means performance in unsampled regions remains uncertain. Third, initial condition variability (thickness CV: 17.1%, roughness CV: 21.8%, see Fig. 10) introduces uncontrolled factors contributing to prediction variance.

The Random Forest and XGBoost models showed higher training R2 but lower validation performance, consistent with overfitting behavior expected for ensemble methods on small datasets. Figure 13(d) demonstrates that RF’s performance gap narrows from 0.51% at n = 30 to 0.25% at n = 81, suggesting that with larger datasets (n > 150), RF might match or surpass SVR. BPNN exhibited the largest training-validation gap, likely due to its large parameter count relative to sample size. These observations underscore that model selection should be contingent on dataset characteristics rather than representing universal superiority.

For Sa prediction, the SVR model achieves a high Test R2 of 0.9874, serving as a stable baseline for comparison with the more challenging RC prediction task. This may be because the changes in surface roughness on the crown of the N52B30 engine piston (Bayerische Motoren Werke AG, Munich, Germany) are primarily directly influenced by laser parameters, resulting in a relatively well-defined relationship. However, the original SVR model has a relatively low Test R2 (0.5658) for RC prediction, which may be because the carbon residue rate is influenced by more complex factors, such as the initial thickness of carbon deposits and compositional heterogeneity, which are not explicitly parameterized as independent inputs in the current model but are partially reflected in the RC response under controlled experimental conditions. Through Bayesian optimization, we successfully improved the Test R2 for RC prediction to 0.8965, representing an approximately 58.45% increase, with significant results. Bayesian optimization significantly improves the RC prediction performance of the SVR model by enhancing its adaptation to the complex characteristics of the RC prediction task.

Model robustness and interpretability

To address dataset size limitations (n = 81), comprehensive validation was performed. Bootstrap resampling (n = 200 iterations) confirmed high Sa model stability (R2 = 0.985–0.998) and acceptable RC model consistency (R2 = 0.50–0.92). Five-fold cross-validation demonstrated robust generalization: Sa achieved median CV R2 = 0.969 (IQR: 0.964–0.987), while RC showed median CV R2 = 0.41 (IQR: 0.37–0.88), with variance within acceptable bounds for engineering applications. SHAP analysis revealed the underlying mechanism for performance differences: Sa parameters exhibited strong, interpretable effects (SHAP magnitudes: 0.2–0.8), with high Pc and f clearly increasing roughness, whereas RC parameters showed weak effects (SHAP: ±0.03), indicating complex, multifactorial dependencies beyond the four process parameters. Sensitivity analysis (± 5% perturbation) quantified model stability, showing Sa predictions uniformly robust (output change: 0.006–0.009) and RC predictions with identifiable parameter-specific responses (f: 8.7 × 10⁻⁵, Vy: 4.9 × 10⁻⁵, Pp: -5.8 × 10⁻⁵). These validation results demonstrate that rigorous statistical methods enable reliable predictive modeling from limited experimental data when combined with physically interpretable machine learning techniques, while the applicability of the models remains confined to the defined process window and material system.

Conclusions

This study explored machine learning for predicting surface roughness (Sa) and carbon residue rate (RC) in hybrid laser cleaning using 81 experimental samples. Key findings with dataset-specific qualifications:

(1) Model Performance: SVR achieved superior performance on this 81-sample dataset (Sa: R2 = 0.9874; RC: R2 = 0.8965 after Bayesian optimization). However, learning curves have not converged (Fig. 13(a)), indicating preliminary results. Model rankings may change with larger datasets, as Fig. 13(d) shows Random Forest’s gap narrowing from 0.51% to 0.25%. SVR’s superiority is dataset-size-dependent rather than universal.

(2) Bayesian Optimization: Improved RC prediction (R2: 0.5658 to 0.8965, 58.45% increase; RMSE: 1.3961% to 1.17%). While statistically significant, the absolute improvement (0.23%) is modest relative to prediction uncertainty (95% CI: ±2.74%, Fig. 13(b)). Optimization convergence (Fig. 11) differs from generalization convergence (Fig. 13(a)): hyperparameters are optimized, but performance improves with more data.

(3) Multi-Objective Optimization: NSGA-II identified 66 parameter combinations balancing Sa and RC. Solutions are valid for initial conditions within training data ranges (thickness: 60–95 μm, roughness: 2.5–4.5 μm) and require site-specific verification, especially outside these ranges where prediction errors increase (Fig. 10(c)).

(4) Initial Condition Effects: Thickness (CV: 17.1%) and roughness (CV: 21.8%) variability contribute to prediction uncertainty. Correlations with RC were weak (r = 0.099, r = 0.130, Fig. 10(a, b)), but prediction uncertainty (± 2.74%) is comparable to differences between optimal and suboptimal settings, limiting precision for workpieces with different initial states.

(5) Dataset Limitations: Current coverage is 40.4% of parameter space (Fig. 13(c)), with uneven sampling (Fig. 10(d)). Learning curves indicate performance continues to improve with more data (Fig. 13(a)). Validation methods confirmed internal consistency but not external validity. Expansion to n > 200 is necessary before establishing general conclusions about model superiority or broad applicability.

In summary, this work demonstrates machine learning-assisted optimization potential within constraints of an 81-sample dataset. The findings represent preliminary insights rather than definitive models. The limitations—dataset size (n = 81), parameter space coverage (40.4%), initial condition effects (CV: 17–22%), prediction uncertainty (± 2.74%), and learning curve non-convergence—must be addressed through dataset expansion and independent validation.
