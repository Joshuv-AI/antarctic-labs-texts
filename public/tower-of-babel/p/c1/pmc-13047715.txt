Teaching empathy in Balint groups
Authors: Mirjana Stojanović-Tasić, Milan Latas, Emilija Novaković, Marija Milić, Mirjana Virijević, Kristina Rakić, Nenad Milošević, Jovana Milošević, Jelena Aritonović Pribaković, Suzana Adžić, Romana Petrović, Milica Popović Radovanović, Mary Vukša, Danijela Ilić, Milica Bogdanović, Biljana Trajković, Andrija Milović
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of this study was to examine whether participation in Balint groups is associated with higher levels of empathy among physicians. This study was carried out with a sample of 210 physicians working in primary health centers in Belgrade. Among them, 70 doctors had completed Balint training lasting at least one year, while the remaining 140 doctors had never participated in such training (the non-Balint group). Empathy levels among doctors were assessed using the Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI), an evaluation scale developed by Davis that consists of four subscales: perspective taking, fantasy, empathic concern, and personal distress. We found that physicians who completed Balint training had significantly higher scores on the perspective taking, fantasy, and empathic concern subscales compared to those who did not complete the training (p < 0.001). Regarding the personal distress subscale, there was no statistically significant difference between the two groups of physicians (p= 0.530). In the multivariate logistic regression models, participation in the Balint group emerged as the only significant predictor for the perspective taking and empathic concern subscales (p < 0.001), while for the fantasy subscale, both participation in the Balint group and being a medical specialist were identified as significant predictors. Participation in Balint groups is significantly associated with higher levels of empathy among physicians.

Introduction

The term empathy originates from the word Einfühlung, which was first used by Robert Vischer in 1883 in his discussion of aesthetics and the formation of perception [1]. Moreover, the term is most commonly associated with Theodor Lipps, a German philosopher and psychologist who is believed to have introduced the concept of Einfühlung (translated as ‘empathy’ or ‘feeling into’) in the early 20th century to describe the emotional evaluation of other people's feelings [2]. In 1910, Edward Titchener translated the term Einfühlung into English as empathy, deriving it from the Greek word empatheia, which signifies sensitivity, passion or suffering [3].

It is acknowledged that empathy plays a critical role in the physician‒patient relationship and has a positive impact on health outcomes [4,5]. Empathy in clinical practice refers to the physician's capacity to understand a patient's experiences, suffering, and perspectives as if standing in the patient's place, while maintaining appropriate professional distance, and to communicate this understanding through thoughtful attention to the patient's emotional state [6].

Empathy is considered a multidimensional construct consisting of two primary components: affective empathy and cognitive empathy [7–10].

Affective empathy refers to the ability to emotionally respond to the mental states of others and is typically divided into two components: empathic concern and personal distress. In contrast, cognitive empathy involves the capacity to understand others' perspectives or mental states, and is commonly categorized into perspective taking and fantasy [11,12].

Researchers agree that empathy can be developed through a variety of approaches [8,13–15], and one of the most effective methods is through Balint groups, which provide a structured setting for medical professionals to analyze patient interactions and explore emotional responses [16,17]. Participation in Balint groups provides physicians with a structured forum to reflect on challenging patient encounters, explore emotional reactions, and develop both cognitive and affective components of empathy, which may enhance clinical decision-making and doctor–patient rapport [18].

Balint groups were established in the 1950s in London under the guidance of psychoanalyst Michael Balint, who emphasized the therapeutic and diagnostic significance of the doctor–patient relationship. Through collaborative work with general practitioners, he developed a structured educational model—later termed the Balint group method—designed to enhance physicians' reflective capacities and interpersonal understanding in clinical practice [19].

Typically, Balint groups consist of six to twelve members and are facilitated by one or two certified Balint leaders. They meet at regular intervals—weekly, every two weeks or monthly—over a period of several years [20–22].

In Serbia, there are several studies on empathy, but they have been conducted on populations such as teachers [23], psychology students [24], future teachers [25], and medical students [26]. To the best of our knowledge, only one study has been conducted on a physician population [27]; however, it employed a different methodological approach and did not explore the relationship between Balint training and empathy. Moreover, these studies did not use Davis's scale but rather other instruments for measuring empathy.

Internationally, there is also a substantial body of research on empathy among medical students, who bear significantly less responsibility and engagement in working with patients compared to physicians [11,28–30]. Although Balint groups have been shown internationally to enhance physician empathy and communication [17], evidence from Serbia remains limited. Empathy is particularly important in primary care because it is associated with better patient satisfaction, improved adherence to treatment, and stronger physician–patient relationships, making its cultivation through reflective practices like Balint groups relevant for medical education and professional development in our context [31]. The significance of our study lies in investigating empathy among primary care physicians, who play a crucial role in the healthcare system and encounter complex patient interactions requiring both cognitive and affective empathy. Given the lack of studies examining Balint group participation among practicing physicians, the aim of this study was to examine whether regular participation in Balint groups is associated with higher levels of empathy among primary care physicians, addressing a gap in research on practicing clinicians as opposed to medical students.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted on a sample of 210 physicians employed at primary healthcare centers in Belgrade. Of the total sample, 70 physicians had completed Balint group participation lasting at least one year (regular attendance as group members, rather than formal leadership training), while the remaining 140 had no prior experience with such training and comprised the Non-Balint group. The training sessions were held every two weeks, with participants completing a minimum of 36 h of Balint group work annually. The physicians who participated in this study, which was conducted between October and December 2016, were selected using a computer-generated random sampling method from six primary healthcare centers. The inclusion criteria required participants to be either general practitioners (non-specialists) or primary healthcare specialists, to have a minimum of five years of work experience, to have participated in Balint groups for at least one year, and to be employed at one of the selected health centers.

Participants were selected based on a list provided by the Balint Society of Serbia. Eligible respondents included physicians who had completed Balint training and were present at their workplace on the day of data collection. Before the study commenced, the directors of the participating primary healthcare centers were formally informed about the research. The researcher personally administered the questionnaires to all the participants.

For the non-Balint group, the inclusion criteria consisted of holding a position as a general practitioner (non-specialist) or primary healthcare specialist, having a minimum of five years of professional experience, and being employed at one of the selected healthcare centers.

Exclusion criteria for both groups included physicians who had been on sick leave or vacation for at least one month prior to the data collection period, those who had not been actively working for more than one year (due to extended study abroad, prolonged illness or frequent job changes over the past five years), as well as individuals who had recently experienced significant mental or physical stress unrelated to their professional environment (e.g. serious illness or death in the family, divorce, etc.).

Self-administered anonymous questionnaires were distributed to all the participants. Each physician who had completed Balint training was matched with two randomly selected non-Balint physicians from the same healthcare center, with equivalent levels of education and specialization. All prospective participants received written information explaining that their involvement in the study was voluntary and that all data would be treated with strict confidentiality. Informed written consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.

The study design and consent procedure were approved by the ethics committees of the six participating healthcare centers (Approval Nos: 2370; 2047;1116/2-1;360-3 and 1237-2;971/3), as well as the Faculty of Medicine, University of Belgrade, Serbia (Approval No: 29/VI-12).

Instruments

Our study utilized the Empathy Assessment Scale, specifically Davis's Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI), which demonstrated good internal consistency in this study, with Cronbach's alpha values of 0.662 for perspective taking, 0.749 for fantasy, 0.686 for empathic concern, and 0.625 for personal distress [32]. These values were reported in previous studies [33,34]; in the current study, no full confirmatory factor analysis was conducted, which is acknowledged as a limitation. It distinguishes between the affective and cognitive components of empathy and is designed for use in the general population [35].

The original English version of the interpersonal reactivity index (IRI) was translated into Serbian in accordance with internationally accepted methodological guidelines for the translation and cultural adaptation of questionnaires. Following the translation, a pilot study was conducted with 30 primary care physicians, who completed the questionnaire and then repeated it two weeks later. However, a confirmatory factor structure and broader psychometric validation for this population were not performed, which should be considered when interpreting the findings.

The IRI consists of 28 items, divided into four subscales, each comprising seven distinct items that assess different dimensions of empathy. These subscales are: perspective taking, fantasy, empathic concern, and personal distress [36]. The perspective taking subscale measures the tendency to adopt the point of view of others in everyday situations. An example item from this subscale is: ‘I sometimes try to understand my friends better by imagining how things look from their perspective’. The fantasy subscale measures the tendency to imaginatively transpose oneself into the feelings and actions of characters in movies, novels, plays, and other fictional situations. An example item from this subscale is: ‘I really get involved with the feelings of the characters in a novel’. The empathic concern subscale measures the tendency to experience feelings of warmth, compassion, and concern for other people. A typical item from this subscale is: ‘I am often quite touched by things that I see happen’. The personal distress subscale also assesses typical emotional responses, specifically detecting feelings of personal discomfort and unease in reaction to the emotions of others. An example item from this subscale is: ‘Being in a tense emotional situation scares me’ [37]. The scale offers five response options, ranging from A to E. Option A states: ‘Does not describe me well,’ while option E states: ‘Describes me very well.’ Responses are scored on a scale from 0 to 4 [35].

Participants also filled out a brief questionnaire collecting information on their basic sociodemographic characteristics (such as sex, age, marital status, and parental status) as well as work-related details, including length of medical service, specialization, subspecialization, vocational training, and academic accomplishments.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis of the collected data included methods of descriptive statistics, hypothesis testing, correlation analysis, and regression modeling. Among the descriptive methods, measures of central tendency (arithmetic mean and median), measures of variability (standard deviation, range), and structural indicators expressed in percentages were used. To test hypotheses about differences in arithmetic means, Student's t-test was applied, while Pearson's chi-square test was used to assess differences in frequencies for categorical variables. For the correlation analysis, Spearman's rank correlation coefficient was chosen for correlational analyses because the IRI subscale scores are ordinal and do not strictly meet the assumption of parametric correlation. To model the relationships between the IRI subscales and potential predictors, logistic regression methods were applied. In the process of hypothesis testing, a significance level of 0.05 was chosen.

Results

Descriptive characteristics of participants

All 210 physicians—comprising 70 Balint-trained and 140 non-Balint participants—completed the questionnaires. Nine non-Balint participants who initially declined participation were randomly replaced with nine other eligible respondents. The sample was representative of the sex distribution within the Serbian primary health care system. The majority of primary care physicians were female (82.9%), while males accounted for 17.1%. The average age of the participants was 48.3 years (±9.6), with ages ranging from 30 to 65 years. Discussion

Although the data were collected in 2016, the fundamental structure of primary healthcare in Serbia and the use of Balint groups have remained largely stable. Physician workloads, patient complexity, and the need for enhanced empathic engagement persist, suggesting that the findings are still applicable. Moreover, these results provide a benchmark for future studies evaluating longitudinal changes in empathy and the impact of Balint participation in the local context. According to the results of this study, physicians who had completed Balint training scored higher on three subscales—perspective taking, fantasy, and empathic concern—compared to those without such training. The only subscale for which there was no statistically significant difference between the two groups of physicians was personal distress; specifically, 42.9% of the physicians with Balint training and 41.4% of those without training scored high on this subscale. In medicine, two subscales are of particular importance—perspective taking and empathic concern—because both are oriented toward others, whereas the other two subscales assess feelings of anxiety and personal discomfort, which are self-oriented [38]. In our study, participation in a Balint group emerged as the sole predictor for both of these subscales. The results further indicated that physicians who attended this training were 2.8 times more likely to achieve high scores on the perspective taking subscale and 6.7 times more likely to achieve high scores on the empathic concern subscale. Consistent with our findings, which show that physicians without Balint training score lower on these subscales, other studies have also demonstrated that scores on these two subscales—considered the most important in the medical field—tend to decline during various stages of medical education. Lisa Bellini and Judy Shea [38], in their study conducted on a population of internal medicine residents in the United States using the Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI), demonstrated that physicians scored high on these subscales at the beginning of their residency. However, over time, there was a decline in both empathic concern and perspective taking. Bellini and colleagues [39] also measured empathy among medical interns over the course of one year, ultimately arriving at the same conclusion—namely, a decline in empathy levels over time. Furthermore, a study conducted on a population of American college students measured empathy levels over the period from 1979 to 2009. During this time, the most significant decline was observed in scores on the empathic concern subscale, followed by a decrease in perspective taking—particularly after the year 2000 [40].

According to Davis [41], the capacity for fantasy and personal distress are important factors that influence the quality of the empathic response. In our study, participation in a Balint group and medical specialization emerged as significant predictors for the fantasy subscale. More specifically, the findings showed that physicians with Balint group experience were five times more likely to achieve high scores on this subscale, while medical specialists were 2.4 times more likely to do so compared to physicians without specialization.

Items on the fantasy subscale measure the tendency to identify with characters in movies, novels, plays, and other fictional situations, which is precisely what Balint group work encourages. Michael Balint [42] believed that the fantasies of group participants could help clarify the case shared by the physician-presenter.

During the discussion, images, fairy tales, and symbols emerge. He believed that nothing expressed was wrong. ‘Think clearly, think freely. Have the courage to embrace your own foolishness,’ Balint said [43]. In this way, he encouraged the group members, believing that every idea could provide a clue to the unconscious content of the story. ‘I viewed the case presented by the physician-presenter as if it were a dream, and the presenter's thoughts, along with the comments and ideas of the group members, as free associations [42].’

The findings of our study suggest that non-specialist physicians report significantly higher personal distress compared to their specialist counterparts. Our explanation for this finding is that specialists, who typically have longer medical experience than non-specialist physicians, have undergone a passive reduction in ‘personal distress’—what Hoffman refers to as ‘overintense empathy’—through a process of habituation. When an individual is exposed to others' distress over an extended period of time—as is, in our view, the case in the medical profession—the cumulative effect of such exposure may lead to a reduction in empathic distress [44]. We also believe that specialists feel more secure in their work environment, are better positioned than non-specialist physicians, and have greater decision-making authority and professional autonomy—all of which may contribute to a reduction in personal distress. Similarly, a study conducted in the United States on a population of internal medicine residents found that levels of personal discomfort increased midway through residency but returned to baseline by the end of the training period [38].

In our study, we also examined the relationship between the length of Balint training and scores on the IRI subscales. This was of particular importance to us due to the potential for positive selection bias, given that Balint training in our country is conducted on a voluntary basis. We took into account the fact that physicians with higher levels of empathy, who were motivated to improve their psychological skills in working with patients, may have been more likely to enroll in this training. This possibility was anticipated by Michael Balint himself in his study, The Doctor Study [45]. For this reason, it was important for us to examine whether empathy scores were associated with the duration of training. Our results indicated a significant moderate positive correlation between the length of Balint training and the score on the perspective taking subscale. It is well known that Michael Balint cautioned group participants not to expect any changes in their psychological insight or psychotherapeutic skills during the first or second year of training. He asserted that the true benefits of their work would typically become evident in the third year of training [46]. Consistent with our findings, the results of a Swedish study demonstrated a significant change associated with the duration of training, thereby providing evidence of a measurable effect [47]. Our explanation for the positive association between the duration of this training and the scores on this subscale lies in the dual-phase nature of empathy. It is well established that empathy has a two-phase structure [48], consisting of empathic understanding and empathic response. Empathic understanding of the thoughts and feelings transferred from the patient to the physician usually occurs naturally—that is, spontaneously—provided that the physician has the capacity for it. This capacity is not a learned behavior but must be an inherent trait. However, an effective empathic response can be developed through training. For this reason, we believe that the increase in scores with the length of Balint group experience occurred only on the cognitive subscale perspective taking. Davis [49] also concluded that individuals with high scores on this subscale are more likely to help others when prompted but not spontaneously. Bratek et al. [50] argue that higher scores on this subscale contribute to a better understanding of patients' complaints and improve doctor–patient relationships.

As previously noted, in our study, physicians who had undergone Balint training did not differ from those without such training on the personal distress subscale, indicating that Balint training had no effect on this particular subscale. The personal distress subscale measures self-oriented feelings of discomfort and distress resulting from observing another individual's negative experience [32], and is considered self-focused [39]. Hoffman [44] argues that when the victim's distress is too intense, the observer's empathic distress may become so overwhelming that it transforms into personal distress, which he refers to as ‘overintense empathy’. Although little is known about the psychological processes involved in ‘overintense empathy’ and the mechanisms underlying the shift from empathic to personal distress, our explanation for why Balint training had no effect on this subscale aligns with Hoffman's assertion that this is an unconscious process. According to Hoffman, this shift occurs when the observer's empathic distress becomes so intense and unbearable that it transforms into a feeling of personal distress [44]. We also believe that the heightened levels of personal distress observed in our physicians, resulting from witnessing others' negative experiences, may have been influenced by decades of political, economic, and social turmoil in the country and surrounding region.

In a study among Danish primary care physicians [51], no association was found between length of work experience or specialty training and higher empathy levels. In contrast, our study showed that physicians with more work experience and specialists scored higher on the perspective taking subscale. We believe that the higher cognitive empathy observed in our physicians with longer work experience and specialization is a result of their clinical experience, through which they have developed the skill to more easily put themselves in their patients' ‘shoes’, that is, to better understand their complaints, compared to less experienced physicians. Consistent with our study, Bratek et al. [50], who conducted research on a population of physicians in Poland using the IRI, found similar results. In their study, participants' age was positively correlated with the perspective taking subscale and negatively correlated with the fantasy and personal distress subscales, paralleling our findings where older physicians scored higher only on the perspective taking subscale. Based on the results of our study, where age was not significant on the fantasy subscale, as well as findings from the Polish study, we can hypothesize that the ability to imagine hypothetical situations declines with age. The absence of an increase in personal distress scores with age, as observed both in their study and ours, could be interpreted as physicians feeling more comfortable with patients' suffering over time, which may be beneficial in preventing emotional exhaustion and burnout syndrome [50].

The Polish study [50] found no correlation between empathy measured by the IRI and two sociodemographic factors: marital and parental status. In our study, physicians who were married and those who were parents scored higher on the perspective taking subscale. Since this subscale reflects cognitive empathy, we believe that being married or having children—that is, living in a relationship requiring compromises and more mature patterns of functioning—has taught these physicians to more easily see things from another's perspective and to accept others' points of view. In our study, no association was found between participants' sex and empathy, which aligns with a Danish study [51]. However, our findings contrast with those of American [52], Korean [53], Polish [50], and Italian [54] studies, where female physicians demonstrated higher empathy scores.

Emerging evidence suggests that higher levels of clinical empathy may play a protective role against physician burnout, even when burnout is not directly measured. Empathy has been associated with improved physician satisfaction and reduced emotional exhaustion, which are key components of burnout [55,56]. Specifically, physicians with greater empathic engagement report better coping with work‑related stress and stronger resilience, factors that may mitigate the risk of burnout in high‑demand clinical settings. Conversely, lower empathy has been linked to higher emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced professional accomplishment—central domains of burnout [57,58]. Although our study did not directly assess burnout, the observed higher empathy scores among Balint group participants may, therefore, reflect a profile that is less prone to burnout and more capable of sustaining empathic patient care over time.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Owing to its cross-sectional design, each variable was assessed at a single point in time, which does not provide insight into the ongoing educational process within Balint groups or the development of empathy over time. Additionally, the reliance on self-reported questionnaire data introduces the possibility of response bias. Furthermore, a full confirmatory factor analysis was not conducted in the present study, which should be considered a methodological limitation. Future longitudinal research is needed to more thoroughly explore the dynamics of Balint group education and its relationship with empathy.

Conclusions

Overall, our study provides evidence that Balint groups are effective in fostering empathy among primary care physicians. These findings also offer strong support for incorporating Balint group training into medical education curricula. However, further research is required to confirm the relationship between Balint training and the development of empathy.
