Multifaceted roles of TaGW2 in wheat growth–defense trade-offs
Authors: Shumin Li, Peiyin Zhang, Xuemin Wang, Zhen Qin, Zhensheng Kang, Hude Mao
Published: 2025

Main text

Climate variability has profoundly influenced global crop yields and food security by altering rainfall patterns, temperature regimes, and expanding the geographic distribution of crop diseases (Mao et al., 2023). With global mean temperatures projected to rise by 1.5°C–2.0°C by 2050, food systems face unprecedented challenges, potentially reducing crop production by 30%–40% worldwide (Gebrechorkos et al., 2025). This challenge is particularly critical for wheat, where climate-induced extreme weather events jeopardize efforts to increase annual production from the current 763 million tons to the 858 million tons required to feed a projected global population of 9.7 billion by 2050 (Mao et al., 2023). To address this challenge, global wheat breeding programs have devoted considerable time and resources to developing stress-tolerant germplasm that combines high yield with environmental adaptability, thereby supporting sustainable agricultural production. However, despite steady improvements in yield potential, modern wheat varieties have become increasingly sensitive to environmental stress. The molecular mechanisms underlying the intrinsic trade-offs between yield and stress resilience remain poorly understood. Recently, the RING-type E3 ubiquitin ligase TaGW2 has emerged as a pivotal regulator balancing these traits.

Ubiquitination plays an essential role in both plant development and stress adaptation. In Arabidopsis, the E3 ligase DA2 directly interacts with DA1 to activate its protease activity through ubiquitination. The activated DA1 subsequently cleaves substrates such as the deubiquitinase UBP15 and transcription factors TCP15 and TCP22, thereby restricting cell proliferation and organ growth (Figure 1A). More recently, K48-linked polyubiquitination has been shown to perform nonproteolytic functions: DA2 auto-ubiquitination, together with the deubiquitinases UBP12 and UBP13, fine-tunes DA1 activity to regulate organ size (Chen et al., 2024). Furthermore, the histone deubiquitinase OTU1 negatively influences seed size by deubiquitinating chromatin and repressing DA1 and DA2 transcription (Keren et al., 2020). In rice, GRAIN WIDTH 2 (GW2), a homolog of DA2, cooperates with OsDA1 to regulate OsUBP15 stability, thereby restricting cell proliferation through the ubiquitination and degradation of substrates such as WG1, GW6a, and EXPLA1—ultimately reducing grain size and weight (Hao et al., 2021). Interestingly, GW9 is also ubiquitinated by GW2 but functions as a negative regulator of grain size and weight (Wen et al., 2024). GW2 homologs are widely conserved across crop species, including wheat. Knockdown or knockout of TaGW2 markedly increases grain weight and yield, confirming its conserved role as a negative regulator of grain size (Zhang et al., 2018). Recent studies have further elucidated the molecular mechanisms by which TaGW2 regulates grain yield, identifying several key substrates such as TaDA1, TaAGPS, and TaSPL14 (Jian et al., 2024). Beyond these characterized substrates, Li et al. (2024) identified 808 lysine ubiquitination (Kub) sites across 562 proteins with altered ubiquitination through ubiquitylome profiling of tagw2 knockout mutants and the wild-type wheat cv. Kenong199. Among these, 218 Kub sites (on 159 proteins) exhibited increased ubiquitination in the mutant, with enriched biological processes including “vesicle-mediated transport,” “protein folding,” “protein degradation,” “cellular oxidant detoxification,” and “jasmonic acid biosynthesis.” Conversely, 590 Kub sites (on 403 proteins) showed decreased ubiquitination and were enriched in pathways associated with stress adaptation, including “stress response,” “response to water deprivation,” “vesicle-mediated transport,” “protein degradation,” “lignin biosynthesis,” and “ubiquitin-dependent protein catabolism.” These findings demonstrate that TaGW2 interacts with a broad range of proteins and that ubiquitination of its substrates is closely linked to multiple traits, particularly stress response.

Building on these findings, significant progress has been made in elucidating TaGW2’s roles in mediating abiotic and biotic stress resistance (Figure 1C). In addition to its negative regulation of grain size and weight, TaGW2 suppresses wheat resistance to leaf and stripe rust by promoting the ubiquitination of TaSGT1, TaVPS24, and TaSnRK1γ. Notably, CRISPR–Cas9-mediated editing of TaGW2 simultaneously enhanced yield and rust resistance, providing a promising strategy to overcome yield–defense trade-offs in wheat (Liu et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025a). TaGW2 also positively regulates drought resistance by facilitating the ubiquitination and degradation of TaARR12, the wheat homolog of the Arabidopsis B-type ARR transcription factor ARR12. TaARR12 binds directly to the promoters of group-A bZIP genes, repressing their transcription and thereby suppressing ABA signaling and reducing drought tolerance (Li et al., 2024). In contrast, TaGW2 negatively regulates salt tolerance by mediating the ubiquitination and degradation of VASCULAR PLANT ONE-ZINC FINGER 1 (TaVOZ1), which binds to the 5′-CTTCTT-3′/5′-AAGAAG-3′ motifs in the promoters of class 1 high-affinity K+ transporter (HKT1) genes, activating their transcription to reduce shoot Na+ accumulation (Li et al., 2025b). Collectively, the characterization of these TaGW2–substrate regulatory modules reveals the molecular basis of stress adaptation in wheat and offers promising targets for molecular breeding aimed at improving both yield and stress resilience.

Furthermore, Li et al. (2025b) demonstrated that TaGW2 localizes to both the nucleus and cytoplasm and contains a conserved C-terminal transactivation domain. Functional analyses uncovered an unexpected “moonlighting” role of TaGW2 as a transcriptional repressor; it antagonizes TaVOZ1-mediated activation of HKT1 genes by binding to the same DNA recognition motifs (5′-CTTCTT-3′/5′-AAGAAG-3′). Notably, neither Arabidopsis DA2 nor rice GW2 can bind these motifs, suggesting that the transcriptional repressor activity of TaGW2 represents a wheat-specific functional innovation. To our knowledge, this is the first report of an E3 ubiquitin ligase functioning as a transcriptional regulator to modulate downstream biological processes. To further elucidate the TaGW2 regulatory network, Li et al. (2025b) combined DNA affinity purification sequencing and RNA sequencing analyses, identifying 1419 candidate target genes potentially regulated by TaGW2. These targets are enriched in diverse biological processes, including cell cycle progression, cell division, embryo and root development, photosynthesis, stomatal movement, and responses to water deprivation, salt and osmotic stress, abscisic acid, and brassinosteroids. This broad regulatory spectrum underscores the multifaceted role of TaGW2 in coordinating developmental programs with stress adaptation.

In summary, recent advances establish TaGW2 as a multifunctional regulator that integrates developmental and stress response pathways, offering promising opportunities for improving wheat productivity under sustainable agriculture frameworks. Nevertheless, several key questions remain. At the ubiquitination level, how does TaGW2 selectively recognize distinct substrates to simultaneously regulate yield and multiple stress responses? Do these substrates participate in cross-pathway interactions that mediate physiological outcomes? At the transcriptional level, although TaGW2 exhibits moonlighting activity as a transcription factor, the structural domains mediating its DNA-binding activity remain unidentified. Moreover, the molecular mechanisms governing its functional transition between ubiquitin ligase activity and transcriptional repression are still unclear, as is the molecular framework through which it coordinates wheat growth–defense trade-offs. Finally, given its central role in determining grain size and weight, TaGW2 may also participate in additional developmental processes—including cell cycle regulation, embryo formation, root architecture, leaf morphogenesis, and hormone signaling—that remain to be elucidated.
