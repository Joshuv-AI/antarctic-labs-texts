Assessing the association between the domains of the Atlas Context Assessment and implementation outcomes of the TeamBirth project: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Christopher M Westgard, Lauren Spigel, Vanessa L Neergheen, Ki-Do Eum, Sam Woodbury, Ami Karlage, Stuart Lipsitz, Francine L Maloney, Trisha Short, Amber Weiseth, William Berry, Meagan Elam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe know context matters for successful implementation, but few studies have shown which contextual areas are most influential to implementation outcomes. The Atlas Context Assessment was designed to collect context data and use it to inform the design of appropriate implementation strategies.MethodsThis cross-sectional study examines the association between organizational context and implementation of a birthing hospital intervention across 23 hospitals in Oklahoma. The study assessed organizational context with the Atlas Context Assessment prior to implementation of the TeamBirth intervention. The analysis estimates the association between specific contextual factors at baseline and the odds of a patient participating in a TeamBirth huddle (during labor and delivery or postpartum). The association was tested by conducting a logistic regression analysis with hospital-level Atlas scores that predict the odds of a patient participating in a huddle, while controlling for multiple covariates.ResultsThe study identified significant associations between four out of five of the organizational context domains and the odds of participating in a TeamBirth huddle during labor and delivery. Specifically, the ability to implement (aOR = 2.26, 95% CI: 1.04–4.94), the strength of the implementation team (aOR = 2.89, 95% CI: 1.37–6.10), commitment and motivation (aOR = 6.38, 95% CI: 2.38–14.38), and the internal culture (aOR = 4.00, 95% CI: 1.22–13.07) domains were all significantly associated with adoption of the TeamBirth huddles during labor and delivery. Participating in a TeamBirth huddle in the postpartum setting was significantly associated with three out of five Atlas domains (Implementation Team, Commitment and Motivation, and Internal Culture).ConclusionsThese findings underscore the critical role of organizational-level contextual factors in determining the implementation success of the TeamBirth project. The application of the Atlas Context Assessment provided valuable insights into which domains, specific barriers and facilitators most influence implementation outcomes. By enabling sites to strategically target these contextual elements, the tool supports the efforts to create a more conducive environment for effective implementation. This study establishes a foundation for future research aimed at optimizing contextual determinants to enhance the adoption and success of healthcare interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14319-2.Keywords: Health services research, Implementation science, Readiness, Organizational context, TeamBirth, Maternal health, Context assessment

Background

Context matters to the successful uptake and sustainment of quality-improvement initiatives in healthcare [1]. However, implementers rarely assess their institutional context and apply the results to their implementation strategies [2, 3]. On a broad level, it remains unclear how to define context [4], which aspects of context (if any) matter more than others, and how best to measure context [5, 6]. Giving implementers with limited time and resources the ability to measure and address context will require a targeted, easy-to-use, practical context assessment tool based in implementation theory and validated under many conditions.

Ariadne Labs developed the Atlas Context Assessment Toolkit (Atlas) through a landscape review and expert consultation to ensure face validity [1]. Atlas is an open-access, intervention-agnostic package of surveys and reports designed to quickly deliver site-level context data to implementers to inform implementation strategies. It reflects key principles of implementation science, emphasizing that context significantly influences the success of clinical practice change [7–9]. Informed by evidence from health care and business fields, Atlas draws on established frameworks such as CFIR [7], MUSIQ [10], R = MC² [11], and i-PARIHS [12], which highlight the importance of site-specific factors like leadership, organizational culture, readiness, and logistical characteristics. By integrating insights from the literature and expert opinion, Atlas systematically captures contextual and moderating factors, supporting robust analysis through the Atlas Context Data Repository. Atlas is administered to hospital leaders and staff prior to implementing a new quality improvement program. It includes 45 items organized into 5 domains: Ability to Implement, Implementation Team, Clinical Team Functionality, Commitment and Motivation, and Internal Culture [13]. The full list of questions can be seen in Appendix 1. Results from Atlas are then presented to clinical teams during a coaching session to strengthen areas that need improvement. Previous iterations of the Atlas were validated and shown to be feasible, useful, and possible to scale [1].

In this study, we assessed the association of Atlas results with the adoption of the clinical intervention, TeamBirth. The TeamBirth intervention aims to improve communication, safety, and shared decision-making during childbirth and in-patient postpartum care through a shared planning board and structured conversations called “huddles.” [14] A TeamBirth huddle includes the patient, their support person(s), and the clinical care team, which typically consists of the nurse, midwife or obstetrician, and sometimes an anesthesiologist or other specialists. The objective of the huddle is to establish a shared understanding of the care plan by reviewing the patient’s preferences and goals, discussing the current clinical situation and progress, and aligning on the next steps in care while addressing any questions or concerns. This structured conversation fosters collaboration, and transparency, ensuring the patient remains at the center of the decision-making process. Recent studies found that experiencing a huddle was significantly associated with an increase in patient trust and autonomy scores as measured through the Mother’s Autonomy in Decision-Making (MADM) scale and Health Care Relationship Trust Scale-Revised (HCRTS-R) scale, which suggests its potential to improve equity and patient outcomes in childbirth experiences [15].

Ariadne Labs partnered with the Oklahoma Perinatal Quality Improvement Collaborative (OPQIC) to bring the TeamBirth program to all birthing hospitals in the state of Oklahoma. As part of the standard implementation process, TeamBirth used Atlas with all participating hospitals to better understand facility context before program launch. Atlas measures the contextual factors that have previously been shown to be important to the implementation of the TeamBirth program, but no study has tested the association of contextual factors on implementation outcomes of TeamBirth. We aimed to estimate the association between hospital performance on each of the five Atlas domains at baseline and the odds of a patient participating in a TeamBirth huddle during labor and delivery or in-patient postpartum care. Participation in a TeamBirth huddle reflected effective adoption of the TeamBirth intervention.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study examines the association between organizational context and effective implementation of TeamBirth. The study assessed organizational context with Atlas prior to implementation of the TeamBirth intervention. The analysis estimates the association between specific contextual factors on each of the five Atlas domains at baseline (pre-implementation) and the odds of a patient participating in a TeamBirth huddle during (1) labor and delivery or (2) in-patient postpartum care prior to discharge.

Study setting and population

This study took place in 23 of the 32 Oklahoma birthing hospitals affiliated with OPQIC that agreed to participate in research as part of their implementation of TeamBirth. Participating hospitals launched the TeamBirth program in their labor and delivery and postpartum units between March 2022 and March 2023, which allowed outcomes to be collected for at least 12 months after program launch, and the data to be analyzed before the grant ended in September 2024. Those that were launched after this period would not complete their 12-month outcome data collection period in time for it to be included in this study and were excluded. Hospitals were located across the state of Oklahoma in both urban and rural areas. The study population included patients who delivered at participating hospitals and hospital staff (e.g., leaders, clinicians, and implementers) working in the labor and delivery or postpartum units in participating hospitals. Atlas was administered to hospital staff prior to launching TeamBirth, between February 2022 and March 2023. Data were collected from patients during their inpatient postpartum stay, after TeamBirth launched at their respective birthing hospitals, between May 2022 and March 2024.

Eligibility criteria

Hospitals were eligible for this study if they were located in Oklahoma, implemented TeamBirth in their labor and delivery or postpartum units, and were able to collect patient survey data. Hospital staff were eligible for the study if they provided care in a labor and delivery or postpartum unit at an eligible hospital. Patients were eligible for the study if they were 15 years of age or older (18 in the Cherokee National health center), experienced a live birth, and completed a TeamBirth patient survey after TeamBirth launch. Any hospital staff who did not complete Atlas (defined as hitting the submit button at the end of the survey) were excluded from this study. We considered use of the “submit” button an acknowledgment that the survey responses were final, minimizing likelihood of duplicate responses, and signifying consent to have these data included in the report. The hospital staff could submit their survey with incomplete information as none of the questions were required to be answered. Patients with partially completed surveys were excluded from the study.

Measurement tools

The Atlas surveys were hosted on the Harvard University Qualtrics platform. Each hospital received a unique link to access the survey. The patient experience survey, also hosted on the Harvard University Qualtrics platform, was administered by hospital staff via tablet, QR code and completed on the patient’s device, or on paper during in-patient postpartum care prior to discharge. The patient experience survey collected information regarding the patient’s participation in TeamBirth informational huddles and sociodemographic information. The survey was piloted and further described in Neergheen et al., 2024 [16]. Both the Atlas survey and the patient experience survey collected anonymous data. The survey participants’ names were not collected. All responses from the paper-based surveys were manually entered into Qualtrics by members of the sites’ implementation teams. Sites chose how to administer the patient surveys.

Key variables

Predictor: The predictor variable of interest was the state of the organizational context pre-implementation of the intervention. Atlas quantified the context and provided hospital performance scores on each of the five domains included in the assessment. The five domains included: (1) Ability To Implement; (2) Implementation Team; (3) Clinical Team Functionality; (4) Commitment and Motivation; and (5) Internal Culture. Each domain consists of a range of individual survey items, ranging from 5 to 21 items. Each item was scored on a 4-point Likert scale (e.g., agree = 4, somewhat agree = 3, somewhat disagree = 2, and disagree = 1). “Don’t know” and “not applicable” options were coded as missing. Mean scores were calculated for each survey item at the hospital level. Hospital-level domain scores were then generated by calculating the mean of the survey items corresponding to each domain, with domain scores ranging from 1 to 4.

Outcomes: The primary implementation outcomes of this study was adoption of TeamBirth at each hospital’s labor and delivery unit or postpartum unit. Adoption was assessed via two measures: 1) patient-reported participation in a TeamBirth huddle during labor and delivery and 2) patient-reported participation in a TeamBirth huddle during in-patient postpartum care. Response options for the question were “Yes,” “No,” “Not sure,” and “Prefer not to answer.”

Covariates: Covariates for hospital characteristics (e.g., location, resources), patient characteristics (e.g., age, socioeconomic status), and birth characteristics (e.g., induction of labor) were included in the analysis. The final model included the following covariates: urban or rural hospital designation; number of attending or private obstetricians and midwives; existence of obstetric residency program; model of care (Labor, Delivery, and Recovery (LDR) or Labor, Delivery, Recovery, and Postpartum (LDRP)); patient’s age, education, and health insurance status; medical risk and birth complications. The final model for postpartum huddle included the following covariates: urban or rural hospital designation; number of attending or private family practice doctors and pediatricians; existence of a family practice residency program; patient’s age; induction of labor.

Urban or rural hospital designation was determined through rural-urban commuting area (RUCA) codes based on hospital addresses obtained through program records. All other hospital characteristic data (e.g., number of attending or private providers, existence of residency programs, and models of care) were reported by hospital leaders. The patients’ insurance status was coded into the following categories: Medicaid only, Medicaid and other insurance, Indian Health Service and other government insurance, No insurance or prefer not to answer, and private insurance only. Patients’ age was categorical to maintain anonymity on patient surveys. Medical risk and complications was a broad question that asked patients if they had a high-risk pregnancy or a medical complication. Responses were coded to include high risk pregnancy only, medical complication only, high risk pregnancy and medical complication, and neither.

Analyses

The analysis was conducted with a multiple logistic regression to estimate the odds ratio (OR) and 95% Wald confidence intervals (CIs) of participating in a TeamBirth huddle during 1) labor and delivery or 2) postpartum care, given hospital-level mean scores on each of the five Atlas domains at baseline. OR with 95% CIs were calculated to estimate the strength and direction of associations. The OR and CIs were reported from unadjusted and adjusted models The CIs and p-values accounted for clustering of outcomes from patients within the same hospital using a generalized estimating equations (GEE) approach for large cluster (hospital) sizes [17, 18].

To account for confounding bias, the model was adjusted for covariates related to hospital, patient, and labor characteristics. A logistic regression was used to test the relationships between each proposed covariate and 1) huddle during labor and delivery and 2) huddle during postpartum. Covariates that had a statistically significant relationship with each outcome of interest were considered for inclusion in our final model. We also created a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) diagram (Appendix 2) based on theory and literature to identify potential covariates; the DAG illustrated the relationships between variables related to our exposure and outcome and suggested a potential adjustment set [19]. The research team discussed any discrepancies between the statistical analyses and the DAG to determine the final model. While we found a significant statistical relationship between induction of labor and labor and delivery huddle, we concluded a priori via DAG analysis that induction of labor was unrelated to the Atlas domains, and thus not a confounder. We conducted a sensitivity analysis and found that the results did not change after removing induction of labor from our model. Furthermore, our DAG indicated that selection of hospital should have been included in the adjustment set for our postpartum huddle model, however, this variable was unmeasured.

Data analysis was performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). The authors wrote this manuscript in accordance with the STROBE Checklist.

Terminology

Not all pregnant and birthing people identify as women. We therefore use gender-neutral terms, such as “birthing person” and “patient” to describe our study population. We feel that this inclusive language more accurately reflects our sample [20].

Results

Participants

A total of 23 hospitals participated in the study, with 1,098 leaders and staff and 5,725 patients included in the analysis. This corresponded to a response rate of 60.3% for the Atlas survey. A response rate for the patient survey was not able to be calculated because hospitals were not able to provide an accurate denominator of patients eligible to be surveyed. The authors screened 28 hospitals for eligibility, and 5 were excluded because they were: missing data (n = 3); did not administer the patient surveys (n = 1); or only implemented the huddles in the NICU (n = 1). Across all participating hospitals, 327 clinicians were excluded from the analysis because they did not submit a survey, and 798 patients were excluded because they only partially completed the survey, completed a patient survey before TeamBirth launch date, or did not respond to the implementation outcome questions.

Of the 5,725 patients surveyed, 78.6% (n = 4499) and 70.8% (n = 4052) experienced a labor and delivery huddle and postpartum huddle, respectively. Maternal age, education, insurance status, and risk factors varied across the overall sample and by huddle status. In the overall sample, the rate of missingness (where no response was given to the survey question) ranged from 0% (maternal risk and complications) to 5.5% (miles traveled to hospital) (Table 1).

Discussion

The goal of this study was to assess the relationship between performance on the Atlas Context Assessment– a quantitative measure of context across five domains– and the successful adoption of an intervention, specifically within the framework of the TeamBirth initiative in Oklahoma Hospitals. By assessing the associations between hospital context across the five Atlas domains and the likelihood of patients participating in a TeamBirth huddle during labor and delivery or postpartum care, we demonstrated the ability of Atlas to measure areas of context that affect the uptake of an improvement initiative. The analyses supported our hypothesis that many of the contextual factors measured by Atlas are significantly associated with the adoption of the intervention. Differences in results between the labor and delivery and postpartum units are likely due, in part, to the fact that labor and delivery was prioritized for TeamBirth implementation. Sites interested in implementing the initiative in both units began with labor and delivery, expanding to postpartum units at later stages. As a result, the period of implementation outcome follow-up was shorter in the postpartum units, and the degree of focus and resources allocated to implementation differed between the two settings. Notably, the Commitment and Motivation, Internal Culture, and Implementation Team domains showed the strongest associations with successful implementation in both labor and delivery and postpartum settings, underscoring their importance in creating a favorable environment for improving the quality of patient care. Conversely, the analysis did not find a significant relationship between elements related to the Clinical Team Functionality domain and intervention adoption. We know clinical team functionality is important for hospital-based interventions to be implemented successfully [21]. Staff skills, ability, and confidence were cited in 30 distinct studies as an important barrier (if weak) or facilitator (if strong) for implementing quality improvement interventions [22]. The lack of a significant association between the Clinical Team Functionality domain and adoption in this study may have been influenced by the small variance in the domain score, having the smallest standard deviation (SD = 0.11) of all of the domains.

The influence of healthcare organizational context on the successful implementation of new practices is increasingly recognized, yet the mechanisms underlying this influence remain poorly understood [23]. Previous research has also demonstrated the importance of readiness in predicting early implementation outcomes, but showed that the relationship is highly dependent on context, particularly in terms of appropriateness and fit of the intervention [24]. The literature is sparse in identifying which specific elements of readiness and context are most influential to implementation success [24]. Similar studies have highlighted the relevance of contextual dimensions like leadership, clinician motivation and engagement, resource availability, and organizational culture in the implementation of quality improvement initiatives [25, 26]. The significant associations seen in the Atlas Commitment and Motivation, Internal Culture, and Implementation Team domains appear to confirm the importance of these elements. However, most studies focused on context are qualitative, which limits the ability to assess which contextual dimensions have the largest degree of influence on implementation outcomes. Research on this topic has advanced through the development and use of The Alberta Context Tool (ACT), a survey tool used to measure organizational context that was developed for researchers to quantify and assess dimensions of context that are facilitators or barriers to knowledge translation, specifically [27]. Atlas further advances the field by providing a practice-focused context assessment tool for health care settings, that is intervention agnostic, theory based, and designed to be used prospectively to help customize implementation strategies to align with site specific needs [28]. A recent study utilizing the ACT with a group of physicians, nurses, and allied health professionals (e.g., occupational health, social workers) in acute care hospitals across Australia found that higher scores on the culture and social capital dimensions were significantly associated with a higher likelihood of delivering guideline concordant stroke care. These two dimensions were the only dimensions demonstrating a significant association [29]. The strong association seen between the Atlas Internal Culture domain and adoption of TeamBirth is consistent with the significance identified in the ACT study. However, our study found that the Atlas Clinical and Team Functionality domain, which has similar questions to those in the ACT social capital domain, was not significantly associated with TeamBirth adoption. As mentioned above, this is an interesting difference from other findings in the literature and merits further investigation to see if this relationship stays consistent with other interventions.

This study offers significant implications for research and practice by identifying specific aspects of organizational context that are most closely aligned with measures of implementation success. The significant association between Atlas domains and successful adoption of TeamBirth huddles suggests that pre-implementation assessment of context can help in preparing to implement an improvement initiative. Targeting efforts to improve the weaker domains identified by Atlas, especially commitment and motivation to taking on an improvement initiative and creating a safety- and improvement-oriented culture, may meaningfully contribute to better implementation outcomes. The results of the survey can be used to design implementation strategies to address specific identified weaknesses and overcome potential barriers within organizational contexts. This provides a compelling case for the use of the Atlas toolkit during exploration and preparation phases of implementation.

These insights can guide health organizations in optimizing their implementation processes, ultimately leading to more successful and sustainable interventions. Future research is needed to better understand the mechanisms by which Atlas results can help improve effective implementation, such as adjusting implementation strategies and plans. Organizations may also benefit by promoting the integration of context assessment tools like Atlas into standard practice, ensuring that context is prepared and supported on an on-going basis before new implementation efforts begin. This study also provides the groundwork for future research aimed at further validating Atlas across different settings, interventions, and implementation outcomes. Adding data from repeating Atlas surveys will also allow us to study how changes in context over time relate to implementation outcomes.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design limits our ability to establish causality between the contextual factors assessed by Atlas and the implementation outcomes of the TeamBirth initiative. Furthermore, our survey data could not account for all potential confounders, and thus our results may be biased due to unmeasured confounding.

Additionally, all participating hospitals were offered the same level of support and coaching from the TeamBirth team throughout the implementation process, including one coaching session on the results of the Atlas surveys. Some hospitals may have adjusted their implementation strategies in response to the Atlas results, which may have affected the outcomes. These limitations suggest that further research, ideally with longitudinal designs, a control group, and more rigorous sampling methods, is needed to confirm and expand upon our findings.

Conclusion

In this study, the authors identified significant quantitative associations between specific elements of organizational context, as measured by the Atlas Context Assessment, and the implementation outcomes of the TeamBirth program across 23 hospitals in Oklahoma. Specifically, the strength of the Implementation Team, Commitment and Motivation, and the Internal Culture domains were all significantly associated with the implementation outcome of receiving a TeamBirth huddle both during labor and delivery and receiving a TeamBirth huddle during postpartum care. These findings highlight the critical role that these contextual elements play in the success of implementation efforts. By addressing potential issues in these domains, organizations can create a more supportive environment for change, leading to better implementation outcomes and ultimately improved patient care. This study lays the groundwork for further research into how specific contextual factors can be optimized to enhance the success of healthcare interventions.
