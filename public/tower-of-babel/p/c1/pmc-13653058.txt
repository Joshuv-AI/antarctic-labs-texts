Expectations before periacetabular osteotomy and relation to post-operative outcomes and satisfaction
Authors: Samantha Ferraro, Luke C Mendola, Patricia Miller, Young-Jo Kim, Michael B Millis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA periacetabular osteotomy (PAO) seeks to improve native hip function in patients with pre-arthritic dysplasia. Fulfilment of expectations has been shown to influence post-operative satisfaction in spine surgery and knee arthroplasty, but has not been investigated for PAOs. This study analyses relationships between preoperative patient expectations, patient-reported outcomes, and patient satisfaction in young adults undergoing PAO. Patients undergoing PAO in 2012–2014 at a tertiary care centre were asked to complete a preoperative survey indicating their expectations on a Likert scale (not improved—greatly improved) in six domains: pain, stability, stiffness, walking, locking/catching, and athletics. At 7–9 years after PAO, patients reported outcomes on the same Likert scales. Patients were also asked about satisfaction (very dissatisfied—very satisfied) in each domain and overall helpfulness of the PAO (extremely unhelpful—extremely helpful). Change between preoperative expectation and long-term outcome was assessed using the Wilcoxon signed rank test for paired ordinal data. The association between expectations/outcomes alignment with satisfaction and perceived helpfulness of PAO was assessed with Spearman rank correlation. Alignment between patient expectations and long-term outcomes correlated with long-term satisfaction for all domains, with the strongest correlation in the pain domain. Alignment between patient expectations and long-term outcomes also correlated with perceived helpfulness of the PAO for all domains except for walking ability and stiffness. Regardless of preoperative expectation level, patients whose expectations align with their outcomes are generally more satisfied and perceive their procedure as more helpful compared with those with misaligned expectations and outcomes.Keywords: patient expectations, periacetabular osteotomy, patient satisfaction

Introduction

Acetabular dysplasia, a frequent cause of late morbidity in many countries around the world, is associated with inadequate coverage of the femoral head by the acetabulum. One of the most common surgical treatments for symptomatic skeletally mature patients with pre-arthritic dysplasia is the Bernese periacetabular osteotomy (PAO). This procedure reorients the acetabulum to provide better coverage of the femoral head, thus increasing stability of the hip and improving symptoms in properly selected patients [1]. The PAO requires months of limited activity and extensive physiotherapy before patients can return to their normal daily lives.

This study is an extension of previous work by Boye et al. [2], which demonstrated discrepancies between preoperative patient and surgeon expectations before PAO. In the current work, which evaluated the same patient cohort, we have analysed how patient expectations relate to post-operative patient-reported outcomes after PAO. We suspect that this information on the association between expectations and outcomes may lead to improved patient-reported outcome measures.

As medicine shifts towards a focus on patient-centred care, patient perspectives are an increasingly valued measure of success following therapeutic treatment. Numerous studies have examined patient perspectives on treatment outcomes, contributing to the goal of accurately portraying treatment effectiveness based on information reported by the patient, rather than their physician [3–6]. Many researchers have shown that one factor influencing patient satisfaction is fulfilment of expectations after treatment [7–10]. Noble et al. [7] found that satisfaction after total knee arthroplasty correlated significantly with fulfilment of expectations. Likewise, Rampersaud et al. [8] demonstrated that satisfaction after spine surgery is higher when expectations are reported as fulfilled.

The notion that fulfilment of expectations is associated with greater patient satisfaction is consistent and unsurprising. It is unclear, however, whether higher or lower preoperative patient expectations are more closely associated with patient-reported satisfaction and outcomes. De Groot et al. [11] found that patients undergoing lumber surgery who did not expect post-operative pain experienced less disappointment than those who did expect pain. Similarly, Yee et al. [12] showed that patients with high expectations for spinal surgery reported greater functional outcome scores after the surgery. On the contrary, Mancuso et al. [13] found that patients with higher expectations for their activity level after total hip arthroplasty were more dissatisfied with the surgery. Along the same lines, Eisler et al. [14] suggest that unrealistically high expectations lead to lower post-operative satisfaction. The current study seeks to further investigate how preoperative expectation levels relate to patient-reported outcomes and satisfaction.

One of the patient-reported measures used in this study is Global Treatment Outcome (GTO), which asks patients to indicate whether their procedure was ‘extremely unhelpful,’ ‘unhelpful,’ ‘neither nor unhelpful,’ ‘helpful,’ or ‘extremely helpful.’ This measure provides valuable information on the patient’s overall perception of the procedure. GTO following treatment of hip conditions has been analysed by other research groups, such as Goronzy et al.’s [15] study on PAO outcomes and various femoroacetabular impingement studies [16, 17].

A recent meta-analysis by Ahmad et al. [18] gave valuable information on the expected survival of the dysplastic hip after PAO with the end point of arthroplasty. It did not explore the domains of patient expectations or functional outcomes, which is the focus of our report.

The current work further investigates the patient cohort studied by Boye et al. [2] by observing how short-term follow-up data (6 month–1 year after PAO) and long-term follow-up data (7–8 years after PAO) were related to preoperative expectations. We sought to address the following questions:

What is the association between preoperative patient expectations and follow-up patient-reported outcomes? And how does alignment between preoperative patient expectations and long-term outcomes relate to long-term satisfaction and GTO?

Methods

Study design

Data were collected both retrospectively and prospectively for this study. Following approval by the Institutional Review Board, demographic characteristics, preoperative questionnaire responses, and short-term follow-up questionnaire responses were compiled for the patient cohort previously analysed by Boye et al. [2]. This cohort consisted of 115 individuals who underwent a PAO between October 2012 and April 2014 and completed a preoperative expectations questionnaire. They were all skeletally mature at time of surgery. All had preoperative hip symptoms for at least 3 months, and had radiographs demonstrating acetabular dysplasia without severe arthritis (Tonnis grade 0–1) [19]. Two patients died between time of their PAO and this follow-up analysis. All included patients had undergone PAO between October 2012 and April 2014, were skeletally mature at the time of PAO, had experienced hip symptoms for at least 3 months, had radiographic evidence of acetabular dysplasia, and Tonnis Grade 0 or 1 osteoarthritis. Excluded from our study were patients who did not complete a preoperative expectations questionnaire. Patients who had bilateral PAOs during the study timeframe were only enrolled for their first PAO (Fig. 1).

Cohort selection flow diagram.

A total of 113 patients met the inclusion criteria and were asked to complete a long-term follow-up questionnaire about their hip improvement since their PAO and satisfaction with the surgery. Patients were contacted via mail, email, and telephone.

Questionnaires

The preoperative expectations questionnaire employed in our previous study asked about anticipated improvement in the following six domains: hip pain, stability, athletic ability, walking ability, locking/catching, and stiffness. A Likert scale was used: not improved at all, slightly improved, moderately improved, and greatly improved. Patients were also asked to indicate the importance of each domain in their decision to undergo a PAO, using the following scale: not important at all, slightly important, moderately important, very important, and extremely important.

At short-term follow-up (6 month–1 year after PAO), patients were asked about their satisfaction in the six domains. The following Likert scale was used: very dissatisfied, somewhat dissatisfied, just as I suspected, and beyond expectation.

Patients answered one additional question about their GTO, indicating how helpful or unhelpful they found the surgery to be overall. The GTO scale responses were the following: extremely unhelpful, unhelpful, neither helpful nor unhelpful, helpful, and extremely helpful.

At long-term follow-up (7–8 years after PAO), patients were asked to indicate their improvement in the six domains, using the following scale: not improved at all, slightly improved, moderately improved, and greatly improved. Additionally, patients answered the same questions asked in the short-term questionnaire: level of satisfaction in the six domains and GTO.

Patients did not have access to their preoperative questionnaire responses when completing follow-up questionnaires.

Statistics

Change between preoperative expectation and long-term outcome was assessed using the Wilcoxon signed rank test for paired ordinal data for each domain. Patient-reported satisfaction and fulfilment at short-term follow-up was compared with long-term follow-up patient-reported satisfaction and fulfilment using the Wilcoxon signed rank test for each domain and outcome. The association between the difference in patient preoperative expectation and long-term outcome versus patient-reported long-term satisfaction and the GTO was assessed by estimating the Spearman rank correlation coefficients for each domain for each long-term outcome. Lastly, Spearman rank correlation analysis was used to assess the relationship between radiographic severity of dysplasia, as measured by preoperative lateral centre edge angle (LCEA), anterior centre edge angle (ACEA), alpha angle, and Tonnis angle, and patient preoperative expectations. Correlation coefficients (r) were interpreted as weak (<0.3), moderate (0.3–0.5), or strong (>0.5) associations. All P-values were adjusted to control the false discovery rate using the method of Benjamini and Hochberg [20].

Results

A total of 113 patients underwent PAO surgery at an average age of 26.5 years (SD, 9.5; range, 12.7–47.3 years) and completed preoperative questionnaires on the expected improvement in six domains (Table 1). A total of 66 patients responded to short-term follow-up questionnaires at a median 8 months (IQR, 6.5 to 12.2 months) and 72 patients responded to long-term follow-up questionnaires at a median 8.0 years after surgery (IQR, 7.5 to 8.4 years).

Global treatment outcome and long-term satisfaction (research question 2)

At short-term follow-up, 86% of patients (57/66) reported that they found their PAO to be helpful (22/66, 33%) or extremely helpful (35/66, 53%). Only four patients (6%) reported that the procedure was unhelpful. At long-term follow-up, 94% of patients (68/72) reported that they found their PAO to be helpful (18/72, 25%) or extremely helpful (50/72, 69%). One patient found the procedure neither helpful nor unhelpful, two patients found the procedure unhelpful, and one patient reported the procedure as extremely unhelpful.

Alignment between patient expectations and long-term outcomes was found to correlate with long-term satisfaction for all domains (all P < .05), with the strongest correlation in the pain domain (r = 0.46; 95% CI = 0.24–0.63; P < .001) (Table 4). Alignment between patient expectations and long-term outcomes was also found to correlate with GTO for all domains (P < .05) with the exception of walking ability and stiffness (Table 4). This suggests that when patients’ outcomes match their expectations, they are generally more satisfied at follow-up and report that their surgery was more helpful than if outcomes did not match expectations.

Discussion

Our results found that, in general, patient expectations do not differ significantly from patient-reported outcomes. Interestingly, we found no significant association between radiographic severity and preoperative expectations, suggesting that patients’ outlooks may not be directly influenced by the objective severity of their dysplasia. These findings may be attributable to psychological factors such as the self-fulfilling prophecy phenomenon, which has been observed across the field of medicine [21, 22]. For patients with a subarachnoid haemorrhage, Kramer et al. [23] suggested that pessimistic expectations may lead to a greater chance of death following withdrawal of life-sustaining measures. Similar findings have been shown for other neurological conditions [24, 25], but not for orthopaedic conditions, to the best of our knowledge. Further investigation is needed to understand the possible role of the self-fulfilling prophecy phenomenon in patient-reported outcomes following PAO or other orthopaedic procedures.

Alignment between patient expectations and outcomes correlated strongly with satisfaction in all domains and for GTO in all except stiffness and locking. This suggests that when their expectations are met, patients tend to be more satisfied and view their surgery as helpful. There may be an opportunity to improve satisfaction and GTO by working with patients preoperatively and post-operatively to manage expectations and guide patients through the PAO procedure and recovery.

Our study found that greater alignment between patient expectations and outcomes correlated with higher satisfaction and GTO. However, patient expectations alone did not correlate significantly with satisfaction or GTO for any domain. This differs from studies by De Groot et al. and Yee et al. showing that high preoperative expectations correlate with better post-operative satisfaction, and studies by Mancuso et al. and Eisler et al. showing that high preoperative expectations correlate with worse post-operative satisfaction. Our findings suggest that, rather than being associated with expectation level, satisfaction is associated with the relationship between expectations and outcomes. Therefore, an effective way to improve patient satisfaction may be to place more emphasis on working with patients to set realistic preoperative expectations that are as predictive of outcomes as possible.

While not one of our primary research questions, we did investigate the relationship between patient fulfilment and satisfaction at short-term follow-up compared with longer term follow-up. Fulfilment and satisfaction with the procedure did not change significantly across these timepoints for any domain, with the exception of walking ability. This suggests that patient fulfilment/satisfaction at 6 months—1 year tends to be a strong indicator of their fulfilment/satisfaction at 7–8 years following PAO. Therefore, the 1-year post-operative window is a crucial period that dictates how fulfilled and satisfied patients will likely feel in the long term.

There are several noteworthy patients that warrant further investigation with qualitative research.

For example, there are several patients across each of the six domains who reported an outcome two to three Likert scale levels below their preoperative expectation, yet reported their surgery as helpful or extremely helpful at long-term follow-up. Similarly, there are several patients across each domain who reported an outcome two to three Likert scale levels below their preoperative expectation, yet were somewhat or very satisfied at long-term follow-up. In contrast, at least one patient in both the walking ability and stiffness domains reported feeling very dissatisfied after a two to three level improvement in outcome compared with expectation. This suggests the presence of additional factors, besides preoperative expectations being met or exceeded, that have significant influence over patients’ satisfaction and view on the helpfulness of their procedure. Qualitative research would be valuable to better understand the positive factors leading to satisfaction and perceived helpfulness of the PAO in patients with outcomes much worse than expected, as well as the negative factors leading to dissatisfaction and perceived unhelpfulness of the PAO in patients with outcomes much better than expected.

While this study provides valuable insights, there are a few limitations to consider. First, up to 36% of patients did not complete long-term follow-up surveys, which may introduce response bias if those with complications or less favourable outcomes were less likely to participate—though this possibility is difficult to verify. Second, data on post-operative complications or reoperations (such as conversion to total hip replacement or hip arthroscopy) were not systematically collected in the original cohort, limiting our ability to assess how these events may have influenced long-term expectations or satisfaction. Third, patient-reported expectations and outcomes are inherently subjective and may be influenced by recall bias, social desirability, or shifting perspectives over time. Fourth, although we examined radiographic severity and adjusted for key patient factors, unmeasured confounding may still be present. Finally, because this study was conducted at a single tertiary care centre, findings may not be generalizable to other institutions or patient populations. Despite these limitations, our findings offer important insights into patient expectations and long-term outcomes following PAO and highlight the need for further research to optimize preoperative counselling and patient-centred care.
