MGF100 but not MGF300 family is a potential multigene-deleted target for ASFV attenuation and live attenuated vaccine development
Authors: Wen Dang, Fan Xu, Yu Du, Tao Li, Huanan Liu, Zhengwang Shi, Hong Tian, Jijun He, Haixue Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAfrican swine fever (ASF) is a highly contagious and lethal disease, but few therapeutic options are available for its treatment. Therefore, there is an urgent need for the development of safe and effective vaccines. In this regard, we described the differential effect of deletion of whole MGF100 and MGF300 families from genotype II highly virulent strains on African swine fever virus (ASFV) replication, virulence, and induction of protection. The resulting ASFV-Δ100 and ASFV-Δ300 mutants demonstrated reduced growth kinetics in vitro, with the former displaying aberrant virus morphogenesis. ASFV-Δ100 was efficiently attenuated, whereas ASFV-Δ300 retained its virulence. In the homologous lethal challenge, the two mutants achieved the same protection rate, with the former providing more protection against pathology in organs. Mechanistically, we found that ASFV-Δ100 was capable of inducing a robust innate immune response in vitro and a consistent P30 antibody response in vivo. In conclusion, the MGF100 family is a potential multigene-deleted target for ASFV live-attenuated vaccine development.

Introduction

African swine fever (ASF) is a highly infectious and severe hemorrhagic viral disease in swine with a high mortality rate. The disease is circulating on African, European, and Asian continents, as well as recently in the Dominican Republic and Haiti [1]. As the only member of the Asfarviridae family, genus Asfivirus, the etiological agent of ASF virus (ASFV), is a double-stranded DNA virus wrapped in a multi-layer icosahedral structure with a diameter of approximately 250 nm, and is the only DNA virus (arbovirus) that can be transmitted through arthropods [2]. The spread of the virus continues, exacerbated by the fact that there are currently no effective therapeutic options against ASFV infections [3]. Vaccination is seemingly a promising tool, of which live attenuated vaccines have made the most progress in terms of efficacy and protection, although some concerns have been raised regarding safety issues.

The multigene families (MGFs) are mainly distributed at both ends of the viral genome and play important roles in affecting viral virulence and regulating interferon and inflammatory responses. MGFs are divided into five major categories: MGF100, MGF110, MGF300, MGF360 and MGF505 [4]. MGFs have always been good options for deletion as a method of virus attenuation, in which combinational deletions of the MGF360 and MGF505 genes are well characterized. The virulent Genotype II Georgia 2007/1 isolate harboring combinational deletions of MGF360 and MGF505 genes is highly attenuated in swine and can provide protection against homologous challenge [5]. In parallel, the same deletions exerted the same effects on the Genotype I virulent strain, as deletions of MGF360 genes (MGF360-10 L, 11 L, 12 L, 13 L, and 14 L) and MGF505 genes (MGF505-1 R, 2 R, and 3 R) and interrupting genes (MGF360-9 L and MGF505-4 R) in the genome of Benin 97/1 reduced its virulence in domestic pigs and induced a protective response [6]. Apart from that, several non-MGF genes including EP402R, A238L (an NF-κB inhibitor), A179L and A224L (apoptosis inhibitors), and K169R (thymidine kinase TK) were capable of conferring strain-dependent attenuation following deletion [7].

These data imply that multiple-gene deletions, especially deletions of MGFs, are a promising way to produce attenuation when there is no clear identification of the function and role of individual target genes (Table S12). Besides, Antivirals represent a promising tool for the control of ASF. For example, cyclic cidofovir (cHPMPC) is hypothesized to inhibit ASFV replication by targeting viral DNA polymerase X (PolX) or the RNA polymerase in late-stage, whereas brincidofovir demonstrates potent, dose-dependent suppression of ASFV replication in porcine alveolar macrophages (PAMs), markedly reducing infectious viral titers. While several compounds have shown anti-ASFV activity in vitro, vaccination remains the cornerstone intervention for sustainable disease control and eventual eradication [8–10].

Compared with the MGF110, MGF360, and MGF505 families, the MGF100 and MGF300 families have been relatively less studied. MGF100-1 R is a non-essential gene of African swine fever virus (ASFV) and is not classified as a virulence gene [11]. Nevertheless, previous studies have reported that the natural co-deletion of MGF100-1 R along with other genes is associated with a significant reduction in pathogenicity in pigs [12].

Research findings demonstrate that both MGF300-2 R and MGF300-4 L significantly inhibit the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby suppressing the innate immune response. Furthermore, both genes have been identified as novel virulence factors of African swine fever virus (ASFV), and their deletion results in moderate attenuation of viral pathogenicity in pigs [13,14]. It has been empirically suggested that, compared to single-gene deletions, multiple-gene deletions are more capable of attenuating ASFV and developing live attenuated candidates.

In this study, two novel live-attenuated ASFV candidates were constructed based on the highly virulent genotype II ASFV CN/GS 2018 strain via deletion of the MGF100 and the whole MGF300 gene families, designated ASFV-Δ100 and ASFV-Δ300, respectively. To screen feasible gene deletion targets and promising vaccine candidates for ASFV vaccine development, we systematically compared the pathogenicity, immunogenicity and protective efficacy of the two mutants. Collectively, the MGF100 family, rather than the MGF300 family, serves as a superior target for ASFV attenuation. The ASFV-Δ100 strain exhibits great potential as a safe and effective live attenuated vaccine candidate.

Materials and methods

Cells, viruses and reagents

Porcine bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were isolated from femurs and tibias of 4-week-old crossbred piglets through aseptic dissection [16]. BMDMs cell lines were cultured with RPMI 1640 medium (Invitrogen, catalog no. C11875500BT), supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (Excell, catalog no. FSP500 × 5), 1% antibiotics (Solarbio, catalog no. P1410) and 10 ng/mL recombinant porcine granulocyte macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF, ThermoFisher Scientific, catalog no. 14–7331-81), HEK293T cell lines were cultured with the same conditions except GM-CSF. All the cells were maintained in cell culture plate (Corning® 6/12/96-well plate, catalog no. 3516/3513/3599) in an incubator with 5% CO2 at 37°C.

The ASFV CN/GS 2018 strain (GCA_004135325.1) was used to construct the gene-deletion mutants and for the experiments involving in the parental wild-type ASFV (ASFV-WT).

The VP72, VP30 and VP54 antibodies used in this experiment were produced by our laboratory and stored at −80°C. The DYKDDDDK tag (D6W5B) rabbit mAb (Binds to same epitope as Sigma’s Anti-FLAG® M2 Antibody) (catalog no. 14793S), HA-tag (6E2) mouse mAb (catalog no. 2367S), Anti-mouse IgG(H+L), F(ab’)2 fragment (Alexa Fluor® 488 Conjugate) (catalog no. 4408S), Anti-rabbit IgG (H+L), F(ab’) 2 Fragment (Alexa Fluor® 594 Conjugate) (catalog no. 8889S) and DAPI (catalog no. 4083S) were purchased from CST. Anti-β-Actin mouse monoclonal antibody (1C7) was purchased from Abbkine (catalog no. A01010). HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (catalog no. BF03008) and goat anti-mouse IgG (catalog no. BF03001) were purchased from BIODRAGON.

Construction of MGF-deleted ASFV mutants

Schematic diagram depicting the deletion of MGF100 and MGF300 families was shown in Figure 1, respectively. In the case of MGF100 family, as MGF100-1 R and MGF100-2 L/3 L are distributed at different genomic regions, ASFV-Δ100 was constructed by replacing MGF100-1 R with the reporter gene EGFP under the control of ASFV VP72 promoter as well as replacing MGF100-2 L/3 L with VP72-mCherry reporter cassette. For MGF300 family, as all the members cluster in the same region, ASFV-Δ300 was constructed by the deletion of MGF300 family and insertion of the VP72-mCherry reporter cassette. The resulting virus from each homologous recombination event was purified by first isolating single cells expressing the expected fluorescent proteins via fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) and later seeding them into fresh BMDMs. All the recombinant viruses underwent at least 10 rounds of single cell sorting combined with limiting dilutions. The virus obtained from the last round of purification procedure was proliferated in BMDMs to generate virus stocks. To ensure the absence of parental ASFV CN/GS 2018 and the desired complete deletions of targeted genes in each recombinant mutant genome, virus DNA was extracted and subject to PCR as well as whole genome sequencing for further purity confirmation (Table S10). The protocol was described previously with minor modifications [5,17–19]. Next-generation sequencing of ASFV genomes

BMDMs were seeded in 96-well plates and infected with ASFV-Δ100 and ASFV-Δ300, respectively (MOI = 1). At 48-h intervals, 10 μL of supernatant was harvested and transferred to fresh BMDMs cultures, repeated for a total of 20 serial passages. Then, ASFV genome of the 20th passage were extracted from the cell cultures following the protocol by E.Z.N.A.® Tissue DNA Kit (OMEGA, catalog no. D3396-02). Full-length whole genome sequencing was performed using the MGI SEQ 2000 platform and annotated based on NCBI NT (de redundant nucleotide Library) and NR (de redundant amino acid Library) databases. Sequencing depth and genome coverage were systematically analyzed using the MGI SEQ 2000 platform. The results were defined as reliable when target regions achieved a mean depth of 30000X, as shown in Figure S3, and the whole-genome sequence of two mutants were upload on Mendeley Data (ASFV-Δ100 DOI: 10.17632/ppjh9ds5cw.1, ASFV-Δ300 DOI: 10.17632/j9fj838bbs.1).

Virus titration

The wild-type ASFV CN/GS 2018 and each mutant virus was quantified by using the hemadsorption (HAD) assay as described previously with minor modifications [20]. BMDMs were seeded in 96-well plates. The virus suspensions were 10 times serially diluted and seeded to the plates. The HAD results were recorded on day 5 post-inoculation (p.i.), and 50% HAD doses (HAD50) were calculated by using the method of Reed and Muench [21].

Growth kinetics of ASFV in BMDMs

BMDMs were grown in 96-well cell culture plates and infected with either parental strains (ASFV CN/GS 2018) or gene-deleted mutant strains (ASFV-Δ100 and ASFV-Δ300) at an MOI of 0.1. At 2, 12, 24, 36, 48, 72 and 96 h postinfection (hpi), cells and supernatants were harvested together and subjected to three consecutive freeze-thaw cycles; the resulting lysates – including both cellular and extracellular viral components – were then pooled and titrated by HAD assay. The results were depicted as multi-step growth curve.

Biosafety statement and facility

All experiments with live ASFVs were carried out within the biosafety level 3 (BSL-3) facilities in Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute (LVRI), CAAS approved by the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs and China National Accreditation Service for Conformity Assessment.

Animal study

Fourteen ASFV-free large white outbred pigs typically weighing about 30 kg were obtained from a licensed livestock farm and were randomly divided into two groups housed in two separated rooms in Biosafety Level 3 laboratory (BSL-3) in Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute (LVRI), CAAS. One group (ear tag No. 5400, 5401, 5410, 5415, 5418, 5422 and 5437) were inoculated intramuscularly (IM) with ASFV-Δ100 (106.0 HAD50/pig) referring to as ASFV-Δ100 group, whereas the other group (ear tag No. 5412, 5414, 5426, 5427, 5432, 5433 and 5386) were administered with the same dose of ASFV-Δ300 referring to as ASFV-Δ300 group. Following 21 d of vaccination, 2 out of 7 pigs were humanely euthanized in ASFV-Δ300 group, while all the 7 pigs survived in ASFV-Δ100 group. In the lethal challenge experiment, 5 pigs in control group (ear tag No. 5541, 5542, 5543, 5544, 5545) and all 5 residual pigs in ASFV-Δ300 group were challenged IM with a lethal dose (102.0 HAD50) of highly virulent ASFV CN/GS 2018 strain. In order to compare the protective rate in two groups, 5 out of 7 pigs from ASFV-Δ100 group were randomly selected for lethal challenge experiment. Daily monitoring was conducted for each pig, which included recording rectal temperatures and observing the clinical signs of each pig, such as lethargy, anorexia, depression, vomiting, fever, skin hemorrhages, bloody diarrhea, and joint swelling. In addition, serum was collected every 2 d [22], as were oral swabs, nasal swabs, and fecal swab samples. From each necropsied pig, tissues and organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, submandibular lymph nodes, hepatogastric lymph nodes and mesenteric lymph nodes) were collected for viral load detection using qPCR and immuno-histochemistry examination. This animal experiment, along with previous studies [23], has already been approved by the Committee on Animal Research and Ethics of Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute (LVRI) (SYXK(甘)2020–0010).

The owner (Mr Zhang) of the farm (Kangle County Hengrong Livestock Breeding Co., Ltd., Gansu, China) animals provided verbal informed consent for their animals to participate in this study, and this consent was documented prior to experimental procedures.

Anesthesia and euthanasia procedure

At the scientific or moderate severity humane endpoint, as determined according to a unified standard (including clinical indicators such as persistent high fever, extreme depression of spirit, anorexia, significant emaciation, breathing difficulties, and inability to stand), or at the end of animal experimentation, pigs were properly restrained using a snatch. Intramuscular administration of an overdose of Zoletil®50 (Virbac, catalog no. 50) was performed at 16 mg per kilogram of body weight. Monitoring of anesthesia depth is performed every 5–10 min. Once the animals demonstrated muscle relaxation, absence of movements, absence of palpebral and loss of consciousness [24], they are subjected to euthanasia. All anesthesia and euthanasia procedures in this study were in compliance with animal welfare requirements and were strictly conducted in accordance with the “Standard Operating Procedures for Anesthesia and Euthanasia of Laboratory Animals” (Document No.: LVRI/HL/SY024-03–03), issued by the Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences.

Laser confocal microscopy

For the analysis of subcellular localization of MGF100 and MGF300 proteins, HEK293T cells seeded on glass bottom cell culture dishes (NEST, catalog no. 801,001) were transfected with overexpression plasmids or backbone vector for 30 h. Then, the cells were incubated with the relevant antibodies and examined with a Zeiss LSM 880 laser-scanning confocal microscope as described previously [25].

Western blotting

Cultured cells were lysed in loading buffer containing 1 × SDS, heated at 100°C for 10 min, then loaded into SDS-PAGE gel with protein ladder (ThermoFisher Scientific, catalog no. 26,616). Then proteins were further electrophoretically transferred onto the polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Merck-Millipore, catalog no. ISEQ00010) for 2 h with an electric current of 250 mA. Subsequently, the membrane was blocked with a mixture of 5 ml TBS blocking buffer (5% skimmed milk) for 1 h, followed by overnight incubation with primary antibodies at 4°C. The membrane was washed three times and then incubated with an appropriate secondary antibody for 1 h. After washing three times, proteins were detected with Enhanced Chemiluminescent (NCE biotech, catalog no. P10300) [26].

Regarding the appearance of the WT of western blots in both Figure 2 and Figure S4: All WB samples were prepared concurrently – using the same BMDM cell passage, MOI and uniform harvest time – to ensure experimental consistency and minimize technical variability. The WT and gene-deletion mutant samples were processed in parallel under identical conditions. The inclusion of the WT band in both figures serves a deliberate comparative purpose: it enables direct visual assessment of protein expression across different strains while maintaining figure clarity and logical flow for readers. This presentation does not reflect data duplication but rather strategic reuse of a shared internal control to enhance interpretability. Transmission electron microscopy

For transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis of virus morphogenesis, BMDM cells were infected with different mutant strains for 30 h and fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde (Solarbio, catalog no. P1126) in 0.1 M phosphate buffer for 1 h at RT. Cells were then post-fixed with 2% osmium tetroxide and embedded in epoxy according to standard procedures. After polymerization, about 80 nm-thick sections were obtained and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate as previously described [27]. Samples were observed under the JEM-1400 transmission electron microscope operated at 80 kV. In this study, mature, immature, and aberrant viral particles were counted and analyzed morphologically. Viral particle morphology was assessed in three BMDM cells infected with each strain. A total of 197 viral particles were counted for the WT strain, 128 for ASFV-Δ100, 147 for ASFV-Δ300, 116 for ASFV-ΔMGF100-1 R, 91 for strain ASFV-ΔMGF100-2 L, and 93 for ASFV-ΔMGF100-3 L. Statistical analysis was performed with t-test and corrected p values are presented in the figures (**p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001).

qPCR assay

ASFV DNA was extracted from BMDMs or organ tissues by using E.Z.N.A.® Tissue DNA Kit (OMEGA, catalog no. D3396-02) according to the manufacture’s protocols. The qPCR was carried out on a QuantStudio system (Applied Biosystems, USA) by using Pro taq HS premix probe qPCR kit (ACCURATE BIOLOGY AG, catalog no. AG11704) (Table S11).

RNA-seq analysis

BMDM cells were mock infected or infected with WT ASFV, ASFV-Δ100 and ASFV-Δ300 mutants at 0.1 MOI for indicated time periods (18 and 30 hpi). Cells were harvested for RNA extraction using SteadyPure Universal RNA Extraction Kit (ACCURATE BIOLOGY AG, catalog no. AG21017). The transcriptome sequencing and analysis were conducted by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The original results were analyzed on the cloud platform of Oebiotech (https://cloud.oebiotech.com/#/home). The p values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. A corrected p value of <0.05 and absolute fold change (|log2FC|) of >2 were set as the thresholds for significantly differential gene expression.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as Mean ± SEM. Comparisons between groups were performed with t-test using GraphPad Prism 5.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and all corrected p values are presented in the figures and tables (*, p < 0.05; **, p < 0.01; ***, p < 0.001).

Results

Sequence conservation, temporal expression profile, and subcellular localization of MGF100 and MGF300 families

The open reading frames (ORFs) of the MGF100 family were located at positions 11,094–11468 (MGF100-1 R), 179,519-179944 (MGF100-2 L) and 180,309-180617 (MGF100-3 L) of the full-length genome sequence of ASFV CN/GS 2018. Likewise, four members of the MGF300 family are located at positions 19,732–20538 (MGF300-1 L), 21,365–21847 (MGF300-2 R), 21,937–22251 (MGF300-3 L) and 21,937–22929 (MGF300-4 L). Because MGF300-3 L is a C-terminally truncated version of MGF300-4 L, it was not further characterized in this study. A comparative study of conservation was performed to evaluate the conservation of MGF100 and MGF300 members across variants of ASFV genotypes I, II, IV, and V (data resource: African Swine Fever Virus Database).

The amino acid identity of MGF100-1 R was high across genotype I/II strains, ranging from 94% to 100% (Fig. S1A, Table S1). Consistent with the conservation results for MGF100-1 R, MGF100-2 L demonstrated the same trends. Notably, two genotype II variants with a truncated version of MGF100-2 L were observed, with a size of 48 amino acids in Ukraine_Kyiv_2014_QED21767.1, and 75 amino acids in Ukraine_Kyiv_2014_QED21766.1. In addition, relatively high conservation was observed, ranging from 95% to 97% in genotype I strains and from 82% to 90% in other genotypes (Fig. S1B, Table S2). The situation in MGF100-3 L is relatively different. Across most genotype II strains, a length of 102 amino acids is predominantly observed with high identity. However, a longer length of 146 amino acids was observed in the South_Africa_KNP_Pretorisuskop_1996strains. Surprisingly, genotype I strains all carry a 146 amino-acid MGF100-3 L protein with an identity of 84%. The amino acid identity in Genotype IV, VII, IX and XI strains is divergent, ranging from 75% to 97% (Fig. S1C, Table S3).

In the context of MGF300-1 L, the identity was high, ranging from 95% for genotype I to 100% for genotype II (Fig. S2A, Table S4), which was also the case for MGF300-2 R (Fig. S2B, Table S5). Of note, there are two dominant versions of MGF300-4 L, one 254 amino acids in length and the other 330 amino acids in length. Irrespective of the length, MGF300-4 L was highly conserved in Genotype I/II strains, but diverse in other genotype strains (Fig. S2C, Table S6).

A previous study demonstrated the transcriptome profile of ASFV genes, highlighting the distinctive patterns of individual gene expression. Three members of the MGF100 strain demonstrated the same transcriptional trend during ASFV infection. Similar to VP30, the expression level of MGF100 members peaked at the very early of 3 h post-infection (hpi), plateaued for the next 6 h, and began to decline starting at 9 hpi (Figure 2(A)). However, MGF300 members exhibit divergent transcriptional patterns. MGF300-2 R and MGF300-4 L demonstrated the same trends as MGF100. In contrast, MGF300-1 L was shown to be a late gene as its expression continuously increased over time (Figure 2(C)).

The spatial localization of MGF100 and MGF300 in transfected cells was further characterized. By employing IFA in 293T cells, we found that proteins encoded by MGF100 and MGF300 were predominantly localized in the cytoplasm, except for MGF300-1 L, which demonstrated a dominant distribution in the cytoplasm and a discrete distribution in the nucleus (Figure 2(B,D)).

Construction and characterization of ASFV-Δ100 and ASFV-Δ300 mutants

To understand how the MGF100 family is involved in ASFV replication capacity in vitro and virulence in vivo, a three-gene-deleted ASFV-Δ100 mutant was constructed and further characterized (Figure 1(A)). The precision of MGF100 family deletion was further validated by PCR (Figure 1(A)) and whole-genome sequencing (Fig. S3B). As anticipated, the expected deletion is achieved. Likewise, the ASFV-Δ300 mutant was developed using the same strategy and demonstrated a high efficiency of expected genomic modifications, as analyzed by PCR (Figure 1(B)) and whole-genome sequencing (Fig. S3C). However, apart from the expected deletions, during the process of homologous recombination, some unexpected genomic modifications were observed (Table S7 and S8). Notably, the amino acid substitutions E199L, O174L, and M1249L – identified in both ASFV-Δ100 and ASFV-Δ300—are not de novo mutations arising during viral propagation. Rather, they may represented preexisting background polymorphisms in the parental strain. Critically, this parental strain served as the unmodified control virus in all subsequent in vivo and in vitro experiments, and retained its original virulence phenotype throughout the study. Thus, these variants were unlikely to contribute to phenotypic alterations in viral pathogenicity.

In depicting the multistep virus growth curves of the two mutants, we found that ASFV-Δ100 and ASFV-Δ300 demonstrated the same trends in replication capacity during the first burst period, starting from 2 to 12 hpi. However, ASFV-Δ100 and ASFV-Δ300 viral loads were significantly reduced by approximately 0.5 log compared with ASFV-WT, depending on the time points considered (Figure 1(C,E)), indicating that the MGF100 and MGF300 families are determinants of virus growth in vitro. Consistent results were observed at the protein level (Figure 1(D,F)).

MGF100-2 L is potentially involved in the assembly of ASFV particles

The MGF family has been shown to be multifunctional in roles ranging from suppression of the type I interferon response to virulence in pigs. However, the role of MGFs in ASFV morphogenesis remains poorly understood. Cells infected with ASFV-WT or MGF300-deleted ASFV clearly showed whole virions in either the mature (Figure 3(A), black arrow) or immature (Figure 3(A), white arrow) stages surrounding the membrane assembly intermediates. In particular, electron-dense protein/DNA accumulation was observed in the center of the factory. Partly formed icosahedral structures are commonly observed, with the capsid protein layer accumulating on the outer face and inner core shell proteins assembled on the inner face. However, in the cells infected with MGF100-deleted ASFV, we found that the membrane assembly intermediates displayed a subset of forms, including curved, linear, and semilunar membranes, as well as membrane fragments devoid of dense capsid or core shell protein layers (Figure 3(A), red arrow). Furthermore, quantitative morphometric analysis of virus particles – categorized as mature, immature, or aberrant – within viral factories demonstrated that the parental ASFV-WT and ASFV-Δ300 each yielded about 50% mature particles; in contrast, ASFV-Δ100 exhibited a marked shift toward aberrant morphology, with >60% of particles classified as aberrant (Figure 3(C)). To elucidate which member of the MGF100 family is involved in ASFV morphogenesis, individual genes of the MGF100 family were deleted from ASFV-WT (Fig. S3 and Fig. S4). Our data suggest that the deletion of MGF100-2 L itself results in changes in ASFV morphogenesis (Figure 3(B), red arrow), in contrast to MGF100-1 R- and MGF100-3 L-deleted viruses, ASFV-Δ100-2 L generated approximately 40% aberrant particles (Figure 3(D)). Our results implying that MGF100-2 L is involved in the assembly of ASFV particles, although the exact mechanism is unclear. Evaluation of ASFV mutants virulence and protective efficacy in pigs

To evaluate the potential of the MGF100 and MGF300 families as virulence factors whose mutants can be used as live attenuated vaccines, in vivo evaluation of safety, immunogenicity, and protective efficacy was performed. In detail, domestic pigs weighing about 30 kg were intramuscularly (IM) vaccinated with ASFV-Δ100 (n = 7) or ASFV-Δ300 (n = 7) at a dose of 106.0 HAD50 which was lethal for pigs in the case of ASFV-WT. During a period of 21 d observation, a series of clinical symptoms compatible with ASFV infection was monitored (Figure 4(A)). All animals in the ASFV-Δ100 group survived and remained healthy, and only two of them (No. 5400 and No. 5410) displayed recurrent transient fever from 6 dpv to 12 dpv (Figure 4(B,D)). However, two animals (No. 5386 and No. 5433) in the ASFV-Δ300 group started to develop fever at 4 d post-vaccination (dpv) and were humanely euthanized at 13 and 16 dpv (Figure 4(B,F)). The remaining pigs were symptomatic; however, they had a relatively poor health status, as evidenced by less food intake and depression. Apparently, ASFV-Δ300 was partially attenuated and retained some level of virulence in pigs compared with ASFV-Δ100. MGF100 and MGF300 were associated with the virulence of ASFV.

In the evaluation of immunogenicity and protective efficacy, vaccinated pigs were challenged with a lethal dose (102.0 HAD50) of ASFV-WT via the IM route. To facilitate the comparison between the two mutants, five out of seven surviving pigs in the ASFV-Δ100 group were selected for the challenge experiment. Five naïve animals challenged with ASFV-WT virus started to develop fever at 4 d post-challenge (dpc), peaked at 6 dpc, and subsequently were humanely euthanized within 9 dpc (Figure 4(C,E)). In contrast, four out of five pigs in the ASFV-Δ100 and ASFV-Δ300 groups survived the homologous lethal challenge, with one pig in the former group dying at 9 dpc and one pig in the latter group dying at 13 dpc (Figure 4(C,E,G); Table S9).

To further observe the clinical presentations of the animals during vaccination and challenge, virus shedding and viremia were quantitatively assessed. Pigs in the ASFV-Δ100 group demonstrated fluctuating, but relatively high levels of viremia at the beginning of vaccination. Viremia tended to decrease to a low level at the end of vaccination (18–20 dpv) (Figure 5(A)). In the ASFV-Δ300 group, two dead pigs displayed high levels of viremia, which was consistent with other clinical symptoms. The surviving pigs tended to have comparable levels of viremia to the ASFV-Δ100 group (Figure 5(C)). Virus shedding in fecal and oral-nasal swabs was the same for both mutants (Fig. S5). During the challenge period, surviving pigs in both groups developed transient fever between 2 and 6 dpc, but quickly regained their healthy status. Consistently, surviving pigs from both groups displayed a low or undetectable level of viremia and virus shedding, suggesting good protection (Figure 5(B,D); Fig. S5). These data primarily imply that ASFV-Δ300 is attenuated but is more pathogenic than ASFV-Δ100. ASFV-Δ100 demonstrated higher immunogenicity against homologous challenge

The underlying protective mechanism of live attenuated vaccines against ASFV infection is not fully understood; however, the evidence suggests that the antibody response can play a key role [28,29]. In the vaccination phase, the ASFV-Δ100 group reached the VP30 antibody peak at 12 dpv at a fast and uniform pace (Figure 5(E)). In the ASFV-Δ300 group, the VP30 response was slightly delayed and inconsistent among pigs, although it plateaued at the end of animal study (Figure 5(G,H)). During the challenge experiment, the VP30 response remained at the same plateau, except for one dead animal in the ASFV-Δ100 group, which demonstrated a slight decline in VP30 (Figure 5(F)). In contrast, all naive pigs were humanely euthanized before VP30 response was fully activated. These data imply that the VP30 response is a potential marker for measuring the protective response exerted by live-attenuated vaccines.

ASFV-Δ100 is capable of providing more protection against pathology on organs following lethal challenge

As the protection rate was the same for ASFV-Δ100 and ASFV-Δ300 in the animal challenge experiment, we further compared the protective efficacy of both mutants at the pathological level. When detecting the viral loads in organ samples, it was observed that viral loads in the ASFV-Δ100 group were significantly lower when compared to the ASFV-Δ300 group and ASFV-WT group (Figure 6(A)). These findings indicate that ASFV-Δ100, but not ASFV-Δ300, provides greater protection against homologous challenge. In search of more evidence to support our hypothesis, postmortem data demonstrated pulmonary congestion, foaming of the trachea and bronchus, and severe alveolar and interstitial pulmonary edema in dead pigs from the control group (red arrow) [30]. Specifically, the spleen is characterized by enlargement and friability, accompanied by dark red or black round edges (red arrow). Petechiation of the kidney capsule is clearly and frequently observed with severe ecchymoses (cortices and pelves) (red arrow). In addition, hepatic congestion, gallbladder bleeding, lymph node enlargement, and severe hemorrhage were also common in organs from the dead pigs (Figure 6(B), red arrow). In contrast, fewer ASFV-compatible pathological lesions were observed in ASFV-Δ100 and ASFV-Δ300 pigs. It seems that the severity of pathological lesions in both groups was greatly, but equally, relieved. To gain more evidence, histopathological observations of the organ injury were performed. Consistent with pathological findings, notably, the most obvious immunohistochemical changes associated with ASFV infection are multifocal interstitial hemorrhages, apoptosis/necrosis of cells, and the presence of moderate amounts of positively labeled cells, consistent with histiocytes [31]. Particularly in pathology, organs infected with the ASFV-WT virus showed large-scale interstitial neutrophilic infiltration (black arrow) and hemorrhage (green arrow), which in the two immunized groups showed no bleeding. The lymph nodes in the WT group showed tissue coagulative necrosis (yellow arrow), the cell morphology disappeared, and the germinal center tissue structure was severely damaged (red arrow), but the other two groups only showed infiltration of inflammatory cells (black arrow) and porosity and disordered cell arrangement (white arrow) (Figure 7(A)). ASFV-Δ100 can induce more significant innate immune responses in vitro

The mechanism underlying the ability of ASFV-Δ100 to confer more protection against homologous challenge than ASFV-Δ300 was further explored. In the present study, ASFV-Δ100 was capable of triggering more differentially expressed genes (DEGs) at 18 hpi and 30 hpi when compared with the ASFV-WT virus and ASFV-Δ300, irrespective of upregulated DEGs and downregulated DEGs (Figure 8(A)). The hierarchical clustering of DEGs showed that ASFV-Δ100 was able to trigger a higher level of interferon regulatory and inflammatory genes, as exemplified by CXCL10, PLAC8, DDX60, ISG20, IFIT family, and TMEM156, when compared to ASFV-WT virus (Figure 8(B)). However, ASFV-Δ300 infection weakened its ability to induce an antiviral response as compared to the ASFV-WT virus, as evidenced by the fact that the levels of some innate antiviral genes were reduced. The results were also confirmed by GO-KEGG analysis, which showed that ASFV-Δ100 enriched more significant DEGs in antiviral and innate immune pathways, including defense response to the virus, negative regulation of viral genome replication, type I interferon signaling pathway, and response to interferon-γ (Figure 8(C)). ASFV-Δ100 induced a more pronounced innate immune response in vitro.

Since the bulk RNA-seq analysis was performed on the total cell population, the transcriptional profiles inevitably include signals from both directly infected cells and bystander cells that respond to paracrine factors secreted by infected cells. Therefore, the observed transcriptomic changes reflect a combined response of infected cells and bystander cells, rather than only the intrinsic variation of virus-infected cells. How these interferon regulatory and inflammatory genes are involved in the induction of protection by ASFV-Δ100 is not clear, but we could primarily conclude that the induction of innate antiviral response and interferon signaling within the host innate immune system is a key factor that contributes to the potential of ASFV-Δ100 as a promising, safe, and effective live attenuated ASFV vaccine candidate.

Discussion

The African swine fever virus is notorious for causing great economic loss and is a threat to the pig industry in every country. Traditionally, in the African continent since 1921, it first leapted into Eastern Europe in 2007, when the disease was confirmed in the Caucasus region of Georgia [32]. From there, ASF sporadically occurred in the European Union (EU), affecting wild boars in particular. In 2018, the virus spread to China, which marked the first occurrence of ASF in Asia [33,34]. Later, the ASF spread to Oceania in 2019/2020 and reappeared in the Americas in 2021 [35,36]. Recently, from 2020 to 2024, ASF has been spreading further in Africa, Asia, and Europe. The worldwide spread of ASF explains the emergent need for vaccines.

In search of references, we are informed that the ASFV live attenuated vaccine is making more progress when compared to other types of vaccines. In particular, ASFV-G-ΔI177L, a modified live vaccine formulated as a commercially available vaccine, is gaining increasing attention. In 2020, it was reported that the development of a highly effective live-attenuated ASFV vaccine by deletion of the I177L gene led to sterile immunity against the current highly virulent epidemic Eurasia strain [37]. Mechanistically, I177L induces host inflammatory responses by facilitating the TRAF6-TAK1 axis and NLRP3 inflammasome assembly [38]. Later, a subset of live attenuated vaccines was developed based on the I177L gene-based deletion backbone, which proved to be effective in pigs. Meanwhile, ASFV-G-ΔI177L reached similar and comparable safety and efficacy in challenge studies either by the oronasal (ON) route or by intramuscular (IM) administration [39]. ASFV-G-ΔI177L was first licensed and used in Vietnam with good feedback from pig farms, although some concerns were raised with the deficit of less protection against the emerging recombinant genotype I/II strain and genetic instability in vitro and in vivo [40,41]. Although live attenuated vaccines are under debate, ASFV-G-ΔI177L is a promising option for constraining ASF.

Another live attenuated vaccine, ASFV-G-ΔMGF, has also been licensed in Vietnam for commercial use, although follow-up research on this vaccine is rare. However, this proves that multiple gene deletion of MGFs is a promising tool for ASFV attenuation and live attenuated vaccine development. However, the role of individual MGF genes is different and not fully characterized, let alone the combinational effects of deleting members of ASF MGFs on virus attenuation, especially those from different families.

In this study, we pioneered the complete deletion of MGF100 and MGF300 families from the Genotype II Eurasian virulent strain for virus replication, virulence, and induction of protection. At the same time, we showed that compared to MGF300, MGF100 acts as a more crucial virulence factor and a much stronger suppressor of the host immune response. As a result, ASFV-Δ100 was more attenuated, as evidenced by the lack of death and relatively rare clinical signs in the vaccination study, and more protection in the challenge study, as exemplified by 80% protection and relatively light macroscopic findings. These data imply that MGF100 is a promising target for the development of live-attenuated vaccines against ASFV.

Comparative evaluation of ASFV-Δ100 and the domestically developed seven-gene – deleted strain HLJ/18-7GD demonstrated that both candidates are fully attenuated in pigs, whereas HLJ/18-7GD confers complete (100%) protection against homologous viral challenge. Notably, ASFV-Δ100 was administered at a higher immunization dose while still exhibiting a favorable safety profile. In contrast, HLJ/18-7GD has undergone extensive preclinical evaluation, including long-term assessments of maternal – fetal transmission, neonatal tolerance, and heterologous cross-protection against diverse ASFV genotypes, thereby enabling a more rigorous and multifaceted validation of its safety and efficacy. Such comprehensive evaluation remains lacking for ASFV-Δ100 and ASFV-Δ300 [42–44]. Conversely, the two mutants generated in this study are the first ASFV strains reported to date with complete and targeted deletion of all members of either the MGF100 or MGF300 gene families. This strategy may provide a higher level of biological safety while simultaneously restoring the host antiviral immune response capacity.

MGFs are not known to participate directly in viral assembly or to constitute structural components of viral particles. However, in our study, deletion of MGF100-2 L resulted in the appearance of atypical structures that are rarely observed in ASFV-WT – infected cells, and these abnormalities were closely associated with the attenuated phenotype and altered immunogenicity of the mutant strain. Disruption of normal viral assembly and the formation of aberrant viral particles caused by MGF100 deletion are likely to impair viral replication efficiency and virion maturation, thereby collectively contributing to attenuation of the mutant strain in vivo. Furthermore, morphologically defective viral particles and incomplete assembly intermediates may act as potent immune stimulators. These abnormal structures are more readily recognized and internalized by host antigen-presenting cells, potentially triggering enhanced innate immune activation and adaptive immune responses. Such effects may further contribute to the unique immunogenic characteristics of the ASFV-Δ100 vaccine candidate.

Our study has some limitations. The protective efficacy of this live attenuated vaccine candidate has been assessed exclusively through homologous lethal challenge – a design suitable for initial immunogenicity and proof-of-concept evaluation, yet inherently limited in scope. To address this gap, forthcoming studies will implement a tiered heterologous challenge strategy, incorporating multiple epidemiologically relevant genotype isolates, thereby enabling a rigorous, systematic assessment of cross-protective breadth and real-world applicability.

A key limitation of the current study is the relatively narrow scope of protective immune assessment. Specifically, only innate immune parameters and VP30-specific humoral responses were evaluated, whereas critical correlates of cell-mediated immunity and functional immune profiling were not included. Supporting this concern, Jia et al. and Dong et al. independently demonstrated that effective protection conferred by gene-deleted ASFV vaccines is associated not only with robust antibody responses against VP30 and VP72, but also with significantly elevated IFN-γ levels, both of which positively correlate with clinical protection [45,46]. Furthermore, accumulating evidence suggests that vaccine-induced T-cell immunity may play a non-redundant role in controlling ASFV infection and mitigating disease pathology [47]. To address this knowledge gap, our future studies will implement an integrated immunological evaluation strategy incorporating rigorously standardized cellular assays and high-dimensional immune profiling. This approach will enable a more comprehensive mechanistic dissection of how T-cell – mediated immunity contributes to clinical protection, viral control, and tissue pathology following ASFV challenge.
