Excessive subgaleal suction from a variable vacuum drainage system used for managing chronic subdural hematomas: a cautionary note. Illustrative case
Authors: Barry Ting Sheen Kweh, Damien Bainbridge, Hari Haran Ramakonar, Gabriel Yin Foo Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDNegative-pressure drainage is commonly used to manage chronic subdural hematomas. A patient underwent burr hole drainage of a chronic subdural hematoma with subgaleal drainage set at the medium-pressure setting using a variable drainage system. Postoperative CT imaging documented extensive subarachnoid and parenchymal hemorrhage. Chronic subdural hematoma formation is a complex self-­propagating inflammatory process that results in the accumulation of blood and degradation products between the arachnoid and dural layers.1 The estimated incidence of this pathology is 1.7–20.6 per 100,000 persons annually and continues to rise in the setting of aging populations.2 The underlying pathophysiology is theorized to be secondary to torn bridging veins usually due to trauma, but also occasionally due to intracranial hypotension or intra-axial lesions such as arachnoid cysts or tumors.3 The subdural compartment of the brain is particularly susceptible to accumulating blood given the vein walls are thinnest at the dural cell border layer with weak junctions.1 Consequently, there is release of angiogenic products that lead to membranes and granulation.3

The resultant mass effect from accumulation of these blood products can lead to neurological sequelae requiring surgical drainage. Contemporary literature suggests that a postoperative drain, whether subdural or subgaleal in location, reduces the rate of recurrence.4 It remains controversial as to whether active or passive drainage is preferable in reducing subdural hematoma recurrence. The current sentinel case warns clinicians that vigilance is required if utilizing active suction from a subgaleal drain in an attempt to minimize recurrence given the potential for significant multicompartmental diffuse hemorrhage as well as bradycardia.5–8

Illustrative Case

A previously well and healthy 68-year-old man presented with headaches following a fall 3 weeks earlier. He was not on any antiplatelet or anticoagulant medications. Examination revealed mild left hemiparesis. Noncontrast CT of the brain demonstrated a large left 26-mm convexity chronic subdural hematoma with 9 mm of midline shift (Fig. 1). He underwent uneventful left burr hole evacuation of the subdural hematoma with placement of a VariVac subgaleal drain on medium suction.

A:Preoperative axial noncontrast CT image of the brain demon-strating a large 26-mm left chronic subdural hematoma causing local sulcal effacement. B: Preoperative coronal CT image confirming effacement of the ipsilateral left frontal horn of the lateral ventricle with 9 mm of contralateral midline shift. C:Postoperative axial CT image obtained 4 hours postsurgery, demonstrating multicompartmental hemorrhage with right sylvian fissure subarachnoid hemorrhage and left sulcal temporal subarachnoid hemorrhage (arrow). D:Postoperative coronal CT image demonstrating brain herniation through the burr hole (arrow) with acute adjacent blood as a consequence of the subgaleal drain suction force.

The patient awoke neurologically intact but reported a significant headache, which progressed to intractable nausea and vomiting 4 hours postoperatively. The drain output was 200 mL of blood-stained fluid. This was turned to passive drainage while an urgent CT study of the brain was conducted. Clinically, his headaches significantly improved with the suction ceased. Repeat imaging demonstrated herniation of the brain through the burr holes with multicompartmental hemorrhage including in the contralateral right sylvian fissure and left temporal intraparenchymal region (Fig. 1). CT angiography was unremarkable for any vascular lesion. The patient remained neurologically intact and the drain was removed after repeat imaging on postoperative day 2 demonstrated reduction in the herniation but a new contralateral subdural hygroma as a consequence of the suction effect (Fig. 2). The patient was discharged home well, and it is likely that a degree of preexisting cerebral atrophy increased his tolerance for the multicompartmental intracranial hemorrhage. This unusual case raised suspicion that the suction effect had caused brain herniation through the burr holes and the drain was malfunctioning.

A: Axial CT image of the brain obtained on postoperative day 2, demonstrating improvement in the left-sided chronic subdural mass effect but a new right-sided chronic subdural hygroma as a consequence of the left to right suction force exerted by the subgaleal drain. B: Coronal CT image of the brain obtained after the drain was adjusted to passive drainage only, demonstrating improvement in the brain herniation pattern through the burr hole with slow resolution of the acute blood products.

Study Description

Independent laboratory testing of the VariVac drains was performed (Fig. 1) at the Department of Biomedical Engineering, Sir Charles Gairdner Hospital. A Rigel VenTest 810 calibrated gas flow analyzer (Rigel Medical) was utilized to test unused sterile VariVac drainage systems with manufacturer-stated suction pressures between 0 and 15 kPa (0–113 mm Hg). The system consists of a prevacuumed ­500-mL canister, fluid nonreturn valve, adjustable vacuum regulator with a 10-Fr polyvinyl chloride drain, and trocar. There are four settings on these drains: position 0 (gravity or passive drainage), position 1 (low suction, 5.0 kPa), position 2 (medium suction, 10.0 kPa), and position 3 (high suction, 15.0 kPa).

There was significant variability across the three tested VariVac drains. The manufacturers of the VariVac drain have a published range of 0–15 kPa (0–113 mm Hg). The testing outcomes showed that the upper limit of this range was being exceeded on position 2 (medium) in two of three canisters studied (Fig. 3). An excessively high ­suction force of up to 25 kPa was attained on position 3 (Table 1) in all three canisters. One of the canisters did provide suction pressures more closely aligned to the quoted manufacturer settings, but only on the low and medium settings. During the investigation, it was also incidentally found that a relatively minor and inadvertent physical interference of the actual valve (Fig. 4) during adjustment of the setting dials resulted in transient spikes of extreme negative pressures up to 48.8 kPa (366 mm Hg).

Discussion

Surgical intervention is generally indicated for chronic subdural hematomas greater than 10 mm in depth or causing more than 5 mm of midline shift.9 Relief of mass effect mitigates the development of neurological deficit. Santarius et al., in their landmark randomized controlled trial of 269 patients, demonstrated the benefits of a drain following burr hole drainage of chronic subdural hematomas.4 Indeed, use of a postoperative drain led to reduced recurrence (9.3% vs 24%, p < 0.01) and less mortality (8.6% vs 18.1%, p = 0.04).4 The trial was terminated early given the superiority of the intervention despite some authors, such as Wen et al., previously warning that drains are not benign.10,11

The contentious debate between subdural versus subgaleal drains has yet to be resolved.7 On one hand, Hwang et al. contend that subgaleal drains are just as effective as subdural drains in minimizing recurrence (13.2% vs 8.5%, p = 0.35) but carry significantly less risk of inadvertent parenchymal injury.12 Oral et al. concurred with this sentiment and emphasized that the subgaleal drain is technically safer, especially when passing the subdural drain beyond margins of the burr hole.13 Subdural drains carry risk both on insertion and removal. Häni et al. confirmed a higher rate of intraparenchymal injury during insertion, while Karamani et al. also reported a case of a death from a hematoma after subdural drain removal, presumably because of rupture of fragile vessels due to friction.14,15 This risk is entirely obviated by use of a subgaleal drain that does not contact the brain.

Observations

Given the equipoise between subdural and subgaleal drains, one purported benefit of subgaleal drains is the ability to apply suction given the lack of direct contact with the brain parenchymal surface. It has been standard practice at our institution for subgaleal drains to be placed on suction, but this represented a sentinel event. We speculate that the multicompartmental hemorrhage was attributable to excessive pressure or traction forces on the brain, leading to global rupturing of fragile sulcal pial vessels—a concern some authors have previously raised. In other words, the negative suction force on the local subdural space was transmitted across the entire global intracranial compartment, leading to remote multicompartmental hemorrhage. Habib et al. even proposed a subgaleal pocket more commonly performed in pediatric patients as an alternative to drainage.16 For this reason, Yadav et al. compared passive versus active drainage in subgaleal drains alone in their prospective study of 260 patients, with no difference in mortality but reduced recurrence when active rather than passive drainage was used.6 Ozgen et al. conducted a similar study and found that active drainage was a safe means of reducing recurrence compared to passive drainage groups.5 Finally, Grubor et al. supported active drainage after their study of 452 patients demonstrated reduced recurrence with active rather than passive drainage (12.7% vs 20.1%, p = 0.02).17 An additional benefit of active suction is a supposed reduced rate of infections (5% vs 1.7%, p < 0.05).18

These studies are countered by Junior et al., who conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of 1949 patients and found no difference in passive and active drainage (16.4% vs 13.6%; OR 0.87, 95% CI 0.58–1.31) when measured with respect to recurrence rates.19 However, Sjåvik et al. looked exclusively at recurrence requiring surgery and found that active suction reduced this rate compared to passive drainage (10.8% vs 20.0%, p < 0.01).20 Ebel et al. conducted a post hoc analysis of two randomized controlled studies (cSDH-Drain-Trial and TOSCAN Trial) of 581 patients.18 The reoperation rate following recurrence was actually higher in the active suction group than in passive cohort (23.1% vs 14.1%, p < 0.01), although this appears anomalous in the context of the broader literature.18

Given the conflicting results of several substantial trials in the literature, it seems reasonable to consider that the role of active or passive suction may not necessarily be a binary outcome.21 Instead, drainage should be considered along a continuous spectrum in which the optimal drainage pressure is yet to be elucidated. For passive drainage, some authors used a Jackson-Pratt drain with optional application of negative pressure by compressing the drain bulb. This drain can generate between −50 and −100 mm Hg, but very few trials specifically quantified the degree of suction applied, and therefore there is likely to be heterogeneity between different surgeons. Moreover, reapplication of the suction postoperatively was not controlled for in these trials.18,22

Chew et al. specifically used a suction pressure between 50 and 100 mm Hg (6–13 kPa) and found there were reduced rates of recurrence.22 However, in the broader literature (inclusive of the larger randomized trials), the actual suction pressures utilized are underdocumented. While the optimal suction pressure remains enigmatic, various authors have reported significant complications. Demaerel et al. described a fatal case of brain herniation through a burr hole.23 These authors did not quantify the suction pressures applied by the Jackson-Pratt drain used.23 Woo et al. further documented a precipitous plunge of supratentorial compartment pressure, which results in the potential for venous infarction.24 Based on our experience and investigative findings, we speculate that at least some of such complications may be attributed to excessively high drainage suction pressures, which were not recognized. Additional means of reducing recurrence are also evolving, such as adjunctive middle meningeal artery embolization, which may mitigate the impetus for suction with its possible benefits.25

Lessons

This paper presents a cautionary tale with ramifications that will resonate with fellow neurosurgeons who provide care for patients presenting with chronic subdural hematomas. First, excessive suction on the brain can result in global shearing forces and multifocal multicompartmental hemorrhage. Clinicians should be vigilant when utilizing drains on suction for this and turn off the suction immediately before obtaining imaging if there is any concern. Second, we describe the case of a commercially produced suction canister that did not perform to manufacturer specifications. This should prompt clinicians to test local equipment appropriately to avoid inadvertent harm to patients, whenever doubt arises. We have modified our own institutional practice to utilize only gravity drains following evacuation of chronic subdural hematomas given this warning lesson.

The ongoing controversies regarding type and pressures of drainage are likely to persist for the immediate foreseeable future. However, it is clear that chronic subdural hematoma is a common but complex pathology that requires meticulous attention to every phase of patient care. In particular, irrespective of postoperative drainage modality used, the possibility of excessive suction forces leading to adverse outcomes should be considered.8,26 The recognition of this surgical complication and subsequent pursuit of contributing factors may have represented an early warning, at least at our institution. Despite the catastrophic radiological appearances, there were no permanent neurological sequelae. This led to an immediate change of practice to use drains on passive drainage or low suction pressures, while other options of drainage systems are being explored. An inspection of previously reported cases leads us to speculate that unexpectedly low negative pressures may have been a significant contributing factor.27

Subgaleal drainage with variable-pressure systems following evacuation of chronic subdural hematomas is a common neurosurgical practice. It is imperative that caution be exercised when utilizing VariVac drains as they may deliver negative pressures that are higher than anticipated, possibly resulting in unintended clinical complications.
