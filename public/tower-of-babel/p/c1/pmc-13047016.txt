Advances and Challenges in Sulcus-Implanted Intraocular Lenses: A Comprehensive Narrative Review
Authors: Mario R. Romano, Alfonso Savastano, David Keegan, Mario Damiano Toro, Faustino Vidal-Aroca, Sandro Di Simplicio, Boris Stanzel, Giuseppe Fasolino, Francisco Cabrera, Stanislao Rizzo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
RationaleSulcus implantation of intraocular lenses (IOLs) is an essential strategy when capsular support is inadequate or postoperative refractive correction is required. Evidence on safety, design considerations, and clinical outcomes is extensive but heterogeneous.ObjectivesTo synthesise current evidence on indications, visual outcomes, and complication profiles of sulcus-implanted IOLs, and to highlight design-dependent factors influencing predictability and safety.MethodsA narrative review was conducted using PubMed and manual reference screening. Studies reporting clinical outcomes of sulcus-positioned IOLs—including three-piece lenses, supplementary/add-on lenses, and sulcus-placed primary IOLs—were included. Numerical values are presented as reported, without standardised pooling or comparative analysis.ResultsFifty studies met the inclusion criteria. Across indications such as aphakia, zonular weakness, IOL dislocation, capsular rupture, and refractive enhancement, postoperative visual acuity was generally stable, with mean improvements commonly ranging from 0.10 to 0.30 logMAR in appropriately selected cases. Complications varied substantially by IOL design. Three-piece lenses showed low rates of cystoid macular oedema (1–8%), pigment dispersion (0–12%), and intraocular pressure elevation (1–9%). In contrast, single-piece acrylic lenses demonstrated consistently higher risk of uveal chafing, pigment dispersion, intraocular pressure (IOP) elevation, and uveitis-glaucoma-hyphaema (UGH) syndrome. Purpose-designed sulcus and add-on lenses demonstrated good centration, low decentration or tilt, and predictable reversibility. Endothelial safety was generally comparable to standard cataract surgery when sulcus-appropriate designs were used.ConclusionsSulcus implantation is a versatile and reliable option when the capsular bag is compromised or supplementary correction is required. Outcomes and safety are strongly design-dependent, with three-piece and sulcus-specific add-on lenses showing the most favourable profiles. Further prospective studies are needed to refine long-term risk assessment and design-based recommendations.

Introduction

Intraocular lenses (IOLs) are the standard treatment for cataracts, a leading cause of visual impairment [1]. Their implantation restores vision and reduces dependence on external aids. Modern IOLs now extend beyond routine cataract correction to applications such as presbyopia management, astigmatism correction, aphakia without capsular support, and visual rehabilitation in age-related macular degeneration (AMD) [2, 3].

Contemporary IOL technologies include monofocal, enhanced monofocal, extended depth of focus, multifocal, toric, accommodative, phakic, and light-adjustable designs, each tailored to optimise refractive performance and accommodate anatomical variation [2, 4]. Enhanced and toric lenses show superior outcomes when implanted by experienced surgeons [5, 6]. Surgical innovations such as sutureless scleral fixation and the IOL-shell technique expand options for patients lacking capsular support but require further validation [7, 8].

When in-the-bag placement is not feasible because of posterior capsule rupture, zonular dehiscence, trauma, or IOL dislocation, implantation in the ciliary sulcus becomes an important alternative [9–11]. Sulcus-compatible IOLs, typically three-piece foldable lenses with angulated haptics or single-piece polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) designs, offering better anatomical stability and fewer postoperative complications than single-piece acrylic lenses. Single-piece acrylic lenses are contraindicated in the sulcus due to associations with pigment dispersion, uveitis-glaucoma-hyphaema syndrome (UGH), and iris chafing [12, 13]. Additionally, a growing subset of patients with advanced AMD who previously received monofocal IOLs now face unmet visual rehabilitation needs. Conventional options are limited, often requiring off-label secondary implants in the sulcus. Among these, implantable miniature telescopes provide optical magnification for late-stage dry AMD. Early case series report promising visual outcomes and acceptable safety profiles, although surgical complexity and careful patient selection remain critical considerations [14].

Other devices designed for secondary sulcus implantation include EyeMax Mono, the Scharioth Macula Lens, and Sulcoflex platform [2, 15, 16]. However, in most cases, no sulcus-specific IOL currently holds comprehensive global regulatory approval, and implantation practices remain off-label, shaped by regional regulations, surgeon preference, and device availability [9, 17, 18]. Resource-limited settings in Asia sometimes rely on single-piece IOLs for sulcus implantation due to cost and supply constraints, particularly in paediatric cataract surgery [13, 19]. Historically, “piggyback IOLs” described off-label secondary lenses placed in the sulcus, often standard monofocals. More recently, “supplementary IOLs” refers to purpose-designed lenses featuring adaptations such as convex-concave optics and sulcus-optimised sizing to improve safety and centration [20–22]. Best surgical practices include the use of dispersive viscoelastic, appropriate wound sizing, and optic capture when feasible to enhance IOL centration and protect intraocular structures [23, 24]. Complications associated with sulcus suboptimal IOLs, particularly with non-ideal lens types, include tilt, decentration, pigment dispersion, cystoid macular oedema (CME), and secondary glaucoma [12, 13]. However, posterior segment complications—including retinal tears, retinal detachment, epiretinal membrane formation, and vitreoretinal traction—are less consistently reported and remain insufficiently characterised in the literature, often confounded by pre-existing retinal pathology or prior vitreoretinal surgery. Guidelines vary: the American Society of Cataract and Refractive Surgery recommends against single-piece acrylic lenses in the sulcus and endorses three-piece foldable designs; European and global guidelines concur, emphasising power adjustments and avoiding refractive IOLs [9, 12, 13, 23].

Given the ongoing reliance on sulcus IOL implantation in complex cases, the heterogeneity of surgical approaches and device use, limited prospective data, and the predominance of retrospective data, there is value in consolidating the available literature. This narrative review summarises current knowledge, evaluates alignment between published outcomes and established guidelines [25, 26], highlights gaps, particularly for multifocal and toric lenses, and outlines innovations in IOL design and surgical techniques to support evidence-based secondary IOL placement.

This review provides an updated and clinically oriented synthesis of ciliary sulcus IOL implantation, integrating recent evidence on anatomical considerations, device-specific performance, and modern imaging modalities. Unlike previous reviews, it emphasises structure–function correlations, complications uniquely related to non-ideal sulcus designs, and emerging consensus on best-practice surgical strategies. It also contextualises sulcus implantation within current cataract and secondary IOL workflows, offering a contemporary reference for surgeons facing complex cases.

Methods

Review Design and Scope

This work was conducted as a narrative review aimed at synthesising current knowledge, clinical experience, and published evidence relating to IOL implantation in the ciliary sulcus. The available evidence in this field is heterogeneous, largely retrospective, and device-specific; therefore, a narrative approach was chosen to integrate clinically relevant findings, anatomical considerations, complication profiles, and guideline recommendations.

All available literature up to March 2025 was considered, with no date restrictions applied. Studies were included if they were in the English language, reported sulcus implantation of IOLs in adult or paediatric populations, described IOL design features relevant to sulcus placement, or presented postoperative complication data (Fig. 1). Both primary research and review articles were eligible. Study selection was performed by one author and verified by a second, consistent with a narrative review methodology. Search Strategy

A literature search was performed in PubMed, which comprehensively indexes ophthalmic and anterior segment studies, covering all years to March 2025 without date restrictions and using both MeSH (Medical Subject Headings) and free-text terms for sulcus implantation, visual outcomes, and complications. The search strategy used combinations of terms for intraocular lens implantation, sulcus fixation, visual function, endothelial health, and postoperative complications, with additional targeted searches for clinical terms such as piggyback, supplementary, and sulcus-compatible IOLs. As this work is a narrative review, the literature search was not intended to be exhaustive; therefore, no claims are made regarding comprehensive study retrieval across all available databases.

Study Yield and Selection

Exactly 7883 records were identified; after filtering for English-language human ophthalmology studies and screening titles and abstracts, 50 studies on sulcus IOL implantation, device features, complications, or surgical considerations were included in the narrative synthesis.

Screening was performed by one author and verified by a second; studies were excluded if they did not address sulcus fixation, focused on unrelated techniques, lacked extractable data, or were not in English.

Data Handling and Synthesis

Data were extracted qualitatively, with all relevant information from each study classified into four predefined domains: visual outcomes; IOL positioning and stability (including tilt, decentration, and ocular interaction); safety profile and adverse events; IOL design, material characteristics, and device-specific risks. Extraction focused exclusively on information explicitly reported in each study abstract, including postoperative visual acuity (VA) metrics, biometric measurements, complication rates, and design-related considerations. No quantitative synthesis, statistical pooling, or formal quality assessment was undertaken, consistent with the methodological framework and evidence base of a narrative review.

Results

Overview of Included Studies

A total of 50 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in this narrative review. These publications represent a broad range of clinical scenarios involving implantation of intraocular lenses in the ciliary sulcus, and encompass prospective and retrospective case series, comparative studies, randomised trials, and systematic reviews (Table 2). The evidence base is therefore heterogeneous in design, population characteristics, and reporting depth. Extracted outcomes from each study were organised into four predefined domains: visual outcomes; IOL positioning and stability (including tilt, decentration, and interaction with ocular structures); safety profile and adverse events; IOL design, materials, and device-specific risks. Visual Outcomes

Visual outcomes were reported in 44 studies [21, 22, 27–68], covering a diverse range of indications, IOL types, and fixation strategies. Despite substantial heterogeneity in reporting formats, the collective evidence demonstrates consistent postoperative improvement in distance VA, with median acuity improving from 20/50 to 20/40 and logMAR acuity from 0.57 ± 0.69 preoperatively to 0.18 ± 0.22 postoperatively, and in one cohort a shift from 6/18 or worse in 39% of eyes to 6/12–6/6 in 61% of eyes. Additional postoperative values included uncorrected distance acuity of 0.10 ± 0.11 logMAR and uncorrected and corrected acuity of 0.05 ± 0.02 logMAR and 0.00 ± 0.05 logMAR, with several multifocal and add-on IOL series reporting all eyes achieving 0.3 logMAR (20/40) or better. Reported postoperative visual improvement ranged from 83.3% to 90.9% across bag–sulcus, trauma, and IOL exchange or repositioning cohorts, with comparable gains observed in penetrating injury (5/6 eyes) and complex anterior segment cases (14/19 eyes). Collectively, 42–82% of eyes achieved best-corrected visual acuity of 20/40 or better, including 82.0% (73/89 eyes) in an acrylic sulcus-implanted cohort, with universal achievement of ≥ 20/40 in several multifocal add-on series. Intermediate and near outcomes also improved, with intermediate acuity around 0.20 ± 0.15 logMAR and near acuity ranging from 0.16 ± 0.13 to 0.2 ± 0.07 logMAR, including consistent ≤ 0.2 logMAR near vision in multifocal add-on cohorts.

Intraocular Lens Positioning and Stability

IOL positioning outcomes were reported across 36 studies [22, 27–30, 32, 33, 35–45, 47, 51, 52, 54–59, 62, 66–74], with considerable variation depending on fixation site, IOL material, and surgical technique. Collectively, evaluating sulcus fixation, postoperative anatomical stability ranged from complete centration to clinically significant decentration requiring surgical management.

Tilt and Decentration

Quantitative decentration measurements were reported in a small number of studies. Mean decentration of 0.32 ± 0.19 mm was noted in lenses assessed 8 months after implantation, with no associated tilt, haze, or discolouration. A single case involving a FIL-SSF lens showed decentration of 1.5 mm. Clinically significant decentration occurred in four eyes (5.2%), all in an inferior direction, and all in male patients. Sulcus-fixated foldable lenses demonstrated a higher requirement for surgical correction, with clinically significant decentration in 28.6% of eyes (4/14), whereas none of the 29 PMMA sulcus-fixated lenses decentred. In an additional sutureless intrascleral fixation series, dislocation or decentration occurred in two eyes (3.6%). A large multi-device explantation dataset reported that 622 of 1460 lenses (42.6%) were explanted due to decentration, with sulcus fixation noted as a major contributing factor. Although numerical tilt values were not reported, multiple studies documented statistically significant increases in vertical and horizontal tilt in sulcus-implanted lenses compared with capsular-bag fixation (p = 0.01–0.02).

Centration and Sulcus Behaviour

Many studies documented stable postoperative centration. Intrascleral fixation resulted in 61 of 63 lenses (96.8%) remaining well centred, and one comparative study reported no significant decentration in either sulcus- or bag-fixated groups. A long-term assessment of the Carlevale FIL-SSF lens showed stable centration in all cases, and another series described exact centration and axial stability following sutureless intrascleral fixation. In contrast, increased mechanical interaction was observed in some sulcus-implanted cohorts. These included greater vertical decentration (p = 0.02), vertical tilt (p = 0.01), and horizontal tilt (p = 0.01) compared with in-the-bag fixation. Higher incidence of iris–lens contact (p = 0.001) and peripheral anterior synechiae (p = 0.03) was also reported. Asymmetric sulcus–bag fixation occurred in eight cases, with three lenses decentred early and five later in follow-up, including one explantation for diplopia. In studies of repositioned or exchanged posteriorly luxated lenses, postoperative imaging demonstrated no ciliary body or scleral erosion and no structural abnormalities.

Inflammatory and Structural Complications

Inflammatory and pigment-related events were reported with variable frequency. Pigment dispersion occurred in one eye (4.8%) in one add-on multifocal group and in one eye (0.64%) in a series of 155 eyes. A further cohort reported pigment dispersion in 12 eyes, and another described pigment dispersion syndrome in one sulcus-placed lens. An older series noted pigment dispersion in 37% of cases, sometimes associated with secondary pigment glaucoma. UGH–type findings were documented, including a cohort where all eyes developed UGH syndrome after sulcus-placed posterior chamber lenses with reverse pupillary block; onset occurred at a mean of 28.7 months (0.3–84.0 months), with IOP increasing to 30.5 ± 10.0 mmHg before treatment and decreasing to 15.5 ± 3.2 mmHg after laser peripheral iridotomy. Iris-claw fixation was associated with iris depigmentation, pupillary distortion, and anterior uveitis, while sulcus-sutured posterior chamber lenses showed choroidal detachment, intraocular haemorrhage, and lens decentration. CME occurred in two eyes (2.8%) in one study and in two eyes in another sulcus/bag cohort. In a comparative acrylic versus PMMA series, CME developed in seven acrylic eyes and 12 PMMA eyes. In a uveitic cohort, CME was present in two of 11 eyes. Several studies reported no CME during follow-up. In a large explantation dataset, inflammation was the primary indication for removal in 347 of 1460 lenses (23.77%). Immediate postoperative inflammatory reactions reached 22.2% in an older cohort, with long-term findings including precipitates on lens surfaces and recurrent cyclitis.

Intraocular Pressure–Related Events and Glaucoma

Comparative paediatric data showed higher mean IOP in sulcus-fixated eyes (17.10 ± 6.06 mmHg) than in capsular bag fixation (14.15 ± 4.74 mmHg, p = 0.01). Secondary glaucoma occurred in 28.57% of sulcus-fixated eyes compared with 10.53% of bag-fixated eyes (p = 0.007). Additional postoperative IOP elevations occurred in three eyes in one cohort. In complex anterior segment pathology, glaucoma deterioration occurred in four of 19 eyes and new-onset glaucoma also in four of 19 eyes, with two eyes requiring lens explantation. In a trauma and vitrectomy cohort, postoperative glaucoma occurred in one eye, alongside small numbers of other complications. The STIOL dataset reported high IOP in three of 155 eyes (1.93%), all transient. Several modern sulcus add-on and multifocal series noted no glaucoma, and in one study both sulcus- and bag-fixated lenses showed small postoperative reductions in IOP over 2 months.

Corneal Complications and Endothelial Safety

Corneal outcomes included temporary corneal oedema in 33 acrylic and 26 PMMA eyes in a large sulcus-implanted series. Corneal oedema occurred in two eyes and Descemet folds in one eye in another study, with transient oedema in two eyes (1.29%) and corneal decompensation in two eyes (1.29%) in the STIOL dataset. Pre-existing epithelial corneal oedema occurred in nine of 19 eyes in a high-risk cohort, with progression in four eyes. Corneal calcific band keratopathy occurred in 10.0% of in-the-bag eyes and 13.3% of sulcus-fixated eyes in a paediatric population. In penetrating keratoplasty settings, 46 grafts remained clear. Quantified endothelial cell loss included values of 4.39 ± 1.85%, 4.76 ± 2.06%, and 6.30 ± 2.71% in iris-claw lenses, compared with 3.92 ± 1.85%, 4.33 ± 1.80%, and 5.73 ± 2.12% in sulcus-sutured lenses, respectively. Chronic endothelial loss occurred in three of 11 eyes with aniridia lenses. Another series reported greater endothelial loss in sulcus fixation compared with intercapsular fixation at 6 months.

Posterior Segment Events

Posterior segment complications were uncommon. Retinal detachment occurred in one acrylic and one PMMA eye in a large sulcus cohort. In trauma and vitrectomy eyes, four eyes (11%) had preoperative detachment, and three eyes (8%) developed postoperative detachment. One additional retinal detachment occurred 2 months after surgery in another study and was reoperated successfully. Epiretinal membrane developed in one eye in the trauma cohort. Several studies explicitly reported no retinal detachment, retinal tears, or other posterior segment complications during follow-up.

Intraocular Lens Design, Materials, and Device-Specific Risks

IOL design characteristics and associated risks were described in 49 studies [21, 22, 27–38, 40–74], covering three-piece acrylic and silicone lenses, single-piece acrylic lenses, PMMA lenses, hydrogel and poly-HEMA lenses, plate-haptic lenses, multifocal add-on lenses, phakic IOLs, and specialised devices such as aniridia implants and telescopic or toric add-ons. The patterns of complications were strongly design- and fixation-dependent, particularly when lenses were placed in the ciliary sulcus.

Three-Piece Acrylic and Silicone Posterior Chamber Lenses

Three-piece hydrophobic acrylic and silicone lenses were widely used for secondary implantation and scleral fixation, with sutureless intrascleral techniques achieving centration and axial stability and complications mainly related to manipulation or passage through scleral tunnels, haptic loop distortion in six cases within a design-comparison cohort, fewer late inflammatory findings in one-piece lenses, and sulcus fixation associated with higher rates of malposition (tilt, decentration, iris–optic contact, peripheral anterior synechiae, anterior chamber crowding), secondary glaucoma (28.57% versus 10.53% for capsular bag fixation), and increased dislocation and pupillary capture compared with in-the-bag fixation.

PMMA Lenses and Rigid Designs

Comparisons between hydrophobic acrylic lenses (n = 30) and PMMA lenses (n = 47) placed in the sulcus indicated higher decentration rates in foldable lenses, although percentages were not specified. PMMA lenses were associated with higher induced astigmatism (1.11 ± 0.65 D versus 0.50 ± 0.50 D, p = 0.0001), a higher incidence of clinical CME, and two late cases of endophthalmitis. Large explantation data recorded 1072 of 1460 lenses (73.4%) as three-piece PMMA designs with Prolene haptics, and 261 of 1460 (17.9%) as one-piece PMMA lenses, with sulcus placement identified as a major contributor to decentration. Suspensory PMMA lenses used for scleral fixation did not show additional material-specific complications beyond inflammation and CME.

Hydrogel, Poly-HEMA, and Older Soft Designs

Hydrogel lenses, including soft posterior-chamber-IOLs, demonstrated rotation, deformation, and decentration when manipulated excessively or placed inconsistently within the sulcus. These lenses lacked adhesiveness, and many reports noted uncertain sulcus fit, posterior iris contact, pigment dispersion, and susceptibility to unfolding defects or tears. A flexible poly-HEMA lens required approximately +1.0 D more power than PMMA due to lower refractive index, and was associated with asymmetric fixation, adherence tendencies, and decentration.

Multifocal Add-On, Toric, and Specialised Devices

Diffractive sulcus-based multifocal add-on lenses demonstrated no device-specific postoperative complications; comparative studies showed smoother unfolding in refractive acrylic designs, with photic phenomena occurring in 81% of refractive lenses versus 25% of diffractive designs. Supplemental toric lenses showed mean rotation of 30.48° ± 19.90°, with repositioning required in 50 of 155 eyes (32.25%) and small subsets requiring scleral fixation (four eyes, 2.58%) or iris fixation (two eyes, 1.29%), and aniridia implants were associated with glaucoma in eight of 19 eyes, chronic endothelial loss in three of 11 eyes, and corneal epithelial progression in four of 19 eyes.

Discussion

This narrative review synthesises five decades of published evidence on IOL implantation in the ciliary sulcus, encompassing a wide spectrum of clinical indications, patient populations, and lens designs. In contrast to the procedure-focused narrative on IOL explantation by Alkhatib et al. and the device-centred polypseudophakia review by Khoramnia et al., the present work concentrates specifically on visual, anatomical, and safety outcomes of sulcus implantation across both legacy and purpose-designed lenses [75, 76]. Collectively, the findings indicate that sulcus implantation can restore functional vision in eyes lacking capsular support or requiring secondary optical correction, but that outcomes are strongly contingent on lens design, fixation strategy, and ocular context. Khoramnia et al. highlighted the sulcus not as a neutral alternative to capsular bag fixation, but as a biologically and mechanically sensitive compartment in which inappropriate device selection can amplify risk, consistent with the mechanisms leading to explantation [76].

Across heterogeneous adult and paediatric cohorts, sulcus-fixated lenses were consistently associated with improvement in VA to levels compatible with daily functioning, even in eyes with significant baseline complexity such as trauma, vitrectomy, aphakia, or prior IOL exchange [21, 22, 27, 32, 36, 57]. These gains were observed across distance, intermediate, and near domains, supporting the role of sulcus fixation as a viable rehabilitative strategy versus solely a salvage technique. However, the degree of visual improvement varied widely between studies, reflecting heterogeneity in indications, amblyogenic burden, macular status, and optical design versus a uniform effect of sulcus placement itself.

When multifocal or supplementary add-on lenses were employed, several studies reported intermediate and near visual outcomes comparable to capsular-bag-based multifocal systems, particularly in pseudophakic eyes undergoing refractive enhancement [48, 64, 65]. These findings indicate that, in appropriately selected eyes, sulcus-optimised supplementary lenses can deliver predictable optical performance without necessitating IOL exchange. Nevertheless, long-term durability of these outcomes remains insufficiently characterised, as most studies report limited follow-up and lack standardised patient-reported outcome measures.

Anatomical stability represents a central determinant of both efficacy and safety in sulcus implantation. Quantitative imaging studies demonstrate that, while mean decentration and tilt may be modest in carefully selected cases, sulcus-fixated lenses exhibit greater positional variability versus in-the-bag implants, particularly in paediatric eyes and in those with zonular compromise or mixed pathology [67, 69, 72]. Clinically significant decentration and tilt were reported in a minority of cohorts but remain relevant given their association with dysphotopsia, iris contact, and secondary complications. These observations align with the explantation literature, in which malposition and instability are among the leading indications for IOL removal [75].

Importantly, several contemporary studies indicate that purpose-designed sulcus lenses may mitigate many of these anatomical risks. Supplementary lenses incorporating increased overall diameter, posteriorly angulated haptics, and concave–convex optic profiles demonstrate improved centration and reduced iris interaction compared with legacy designs [21]. These findings indicate that stability in the sulcus is not solely a function of surgical technique, but of geometric compatibility between the device and sulcus anatomy.

The safety profile of sulcus implantation is heterogeneous and closely linked to lens design. Pigment dispersion, chronic inflammation, and UGH-type mechanisms were reported predominantly in older studies and in eyes receiving lenses not intended for sulcus placement, particularly single-piece acrylic and early hydrogel designs [12, 28, 32, 54]. These complications are mechanistically consistent with excessive iris chafing and poor haptic conformity, and form the basis of longstanding contraindications reiterated in contemporary guidance [9, 12].

Conversely, modern supplementary sulcus lenses were associated with low rates of pigmentary change and an absence of consistent UGH signals across multiple cohorts, although follow-up duration was often limited [60, 77]. While these findings are reassuring, they must be interpreted cautiously given the relatively recent introduction of several platforms and the lack of large prospective registries.

Elevations in IOP and secondary glaucoma were reported more frequently in sulcus-fixated eyes than capsular-bag cohorts in paediatric and complex anterior segment populations, particularly in earlier studies [67, 72]. However, more recent studies of sulcus add-on lenses report stable or even reduced postoperative IOP, suggesting that earlier risk patterns may not be generalisable to contemporary designs or surgical approaches [21, 59]. Endothelial cell loss was generally modest but appeared greater in sulcus and anterior chamber configurations versus intercapsular fixation, reinforcing the importance of anterior chamber depth and corneal clearance in patient selection, and echoing explantation thresholds for endothelial compromise [25, 29, 62, 75].

Posterior segment complications were uncommon and were largely due to underlying ocular pathology versus sulcus fixation itself, particularly in eyes with trauma or prior vitrectomy [39, 58]. This pattern mirrors explantation data indicating that posterior chamber lenses are more often removed for optical or mechanical reasons than for direct retinal toxicity [75, 78].

From a design perspective, the reviewed evidence consistently contraindicates the use of single-piece acrylic lenses in the sulcus, reinforcing consensus recommendations [12]. Three-piece acrylic or silicone lenses and one-piece PMMA designs remain the principal options for sulcus fixation in aphakia or lens exchange, each associated with distinct trade-offs in surgical handling, stability, and complication profile [45, 49, 70]. Purpose-designed supplementary lenses extend sulcus use into refractive enhancement and presbyopia correction, offering reversibility and optical flexibility in selected pseudophakic patients.

The limitations of the evidence base must be acknowledged. The review does not follow a systematic or PRISMA [Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses]-based methodology, and the reported numerical findings reflect only the data explicitly available within the selected studies. Most studies are retrospective case series with heterogeneous indications, non-standardised outcome measures, and limited follow-up. Definitions of complications such as pigment dispersion, UGH syndrome, and CME vary widely, and few studies incorporate systematic imaging or risk-of-bias assessment. As a narrative review, the present synthesis does not permit quantitative risk comparison between sulcus implantation and alternative strategies such as scleral fixation or iris-claw lenses, limitations shared through two recent narrative overviews [75, 76].

In clinical practice, these findings support a selective, design-driven approach to sulcus implantation. In eyes without capsular support, appropriately chosen three-piece or PMMA lenses remain a valid option, noting that malposition, endothelial loss, and UGH are the main reasons such lenses later require explantation [52, 62, 75]. In pseudophakic eyes with residual refractive error or presbyopic needs, sulcus-optimised supplementary lenses may offer advantages over IOL exchange or corneal refractive procedures in carefully selected patients, consistent with the polypseudophakia strategies described by Khoramnia et al. [76]. Across indications, avoiding sulcus-inappropriate designs and alignment of device geometry with ocular anatomy are critical to minimising risk.

In conclusion, sulcus implantation must be regarded as a spectrum of techniques versus a single intervention. When lens design, fixation strategy, and patient selection are appropriately aligned, sulcus-based IOLs can deliver meaningful visual rehabilitation. with a complication profile that is distinct but often manageable. The accumulated evidence supports a move away from opportunistic off-label use towards more standardised, guideline-concordant deployment of sulcus-optimised platforms, accompanied by transparent counselling regarding reversibility and long-term uncertainty.
