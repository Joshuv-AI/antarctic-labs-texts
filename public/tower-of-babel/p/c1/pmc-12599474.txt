Treatment strategies for cisplatin-ineligible metastatic bladder cancer: Emerging therapies and future perspectives
Authors: Kyung Hwan Kim, Do Thanh Truc Phan, Hong Koo Ha, Eui Hyun Jung, Geehyun Song, Ho Kyung Seo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecently, the combination of enfortumab vedotin and pembrolizumab has been recommended as the preferred first-line therapy for metastatic or locally advanced urothelial carcinoma owing to its substantial survival benefits. However, its high-cost limits accessibility, leading to many patients receiving cisplatin-based chemotherapy as a more practical alternative. Therefore, cisplatin-based combination chemotherapy remains a widely used first-line treatment for metastatic bladder cancer (MBC). Nonetheless, nearly half of the patients are ineligible for cisplatin owing to factors such as renal impairment, poor performance status, or other comorbidities. The definition of cisplatin ineligibility, which is often based on the Galsky criteria, lacks robust scientific validation, and limited pharmacokinetic data on the effects of cisplatin in patients with renal impairment are available. This review explored alternative treatment strategies for cisplatin-ineligible patients, including immune checkpoint inhibitors, carboplatin-based regimens, antibody-drug conjugates, and novel combination therapies. Notably, recent advancements, such as the combination of enfortumab vedotin and pembrolizumab, have shown promising survival benefits in this patient population. Additionally, emerging targeted therapies, such as fibroblast growth factor receptor inhibitors, are reshaping the treatment landscape of cisplatin-ineligible patients with MBC, emphasizing the need for personalized approaches that balance efficacy and safety.Keywords: Cisplatin; Drug therapy, combination; Immune checkpoint inhibitors; Immunoconjugates; Urinary bladder neoplasms

INTRODUCTION

Bladder cancer (BC) is the ninth most commonly diagnosed malignancy worldwide, with approximately 613,000 new cases and 220,000 cancer-related deaths reported in 2024 globally [1]. Metastatic bladder cancer (MBC) is a particularly aggressive disease with a poor prognosis and limited long-term survival outcomes. Without treatment, the 5-year overall survival (OS) for MBC is less than 5%, highlighting the critical need for effective systemic therapy [2].

BC is sensitive to chemotherapy, especially cisplatin-based regimens [3]. For many years, cisplatin-based chemotherapy has been the cornerstone of treatment for MBC, offering significant survival benefits [3]. However, nearly 50% of patients are considered ineligible for cisplatin therapy because of various factors such as renal dysfunction, poor performance status, or other comorbidities [3]. Cisplatin ineligibility is often defined by Galsky’s criteria, which include factors like creatinine clearance and Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status. However, this definition remains inconclusive and complicates the treatment decisions. The lack of clear criteria highlights the need for precise guidelines [4].

Therefore, alternative therapeutic strategies have emerged to address these challenges. These include immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), and targeted therapies such as fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) inhibitors [5]. These treatments provide hope for cisplatin-ineligible patients, as novel combination regimens show promise for improving survival outcomes.

This review explored optimal treatment strategies for cisplatin-ineligible patients with MBC, examining ICIs, ADCs, targeted therapies, and emerging combinations to address cisplatin ineligibility limitations.

DISCUSSION

1. Cisplatin eligibility criteria and definition of ineligibility

Cisplatin-based chemotherapy is a key treatment for urothelial carcinoma (UC), particularly in patients with metastatic or muscle-invasive BCs. This platinum-containing agent works by cross-linking DNA, preventing its replication and transcription, and making it highly effective in destroying rapidly dividing cancer cells and improving survival rates [6]. However, the efficacy of cisplatin is limited by its toxicity, particularly its nephrotoxicity. Accumulation in renal proximal tubules leads to oxidative stress and cell death, potentially causing acute kidney injury or chronic kidney disease, particularly in patients with preexisting renal impairment [7]. Other serious side effects include ototoxicity, which can result in irreversible hearing loss, and peripheral neuropathy, which impairs quality of life due to numbness and pain [8]. Because of these toxicities, it is crucial to evaluate patients to determine their eligibility for cisplatin therapy carefully. Patients with impaired renal function, poor performance status, or other comorbidities are often considered to be ineligible for cisplatin therapy.

All patients who are candidates for systemic therapy (chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and/or targeted therapy) should be evaluated for their ability to tolerate the therapy. Several clinical guidelines have standardized the criteria for defining cisplatin ineligibility, with the Galsky criteria being the most widely used [4]. These criteria were developed to ensure consistency in the treatment of patients at risk for severe toxicity from cisplatin-based chemotherapy. Before the criteria were established, patient selection was subjective, leading to variability in clinical practice [4,7]. The Galsky criteria provide a framework based on factors such as renal function, performance status, and comorbidities to standardize treatment decisions. The criteria were developed by a group of genitourinary medical oncologists, who reviewed the published data and gathered inputs from 120 international medical oncologists. Sixty-five responses were obtained and analyzed to create a consensus definition of “cisplatinineligible” [4]. The panel’s recommended criteria include: (1) ECOG performance status of 2, and/or (2) creatinine clearance <60 mL/min, and/or (3) Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) grade ≥2 hearing impairment, and/or (4) CTCAE grade ≥2 peripheral neuropathy [3,4].

Key factors include renal function and performance status. The most critical criterion is renal function, where creatinine clearance (CrCl) <60 mL/min indicates a high risk of nephrotoxicity, rendering the patient ineligible for cisplatin. Although this threshold is widely accepted, there is debate regarding whether patients with slightly better renal function can tolerate reduced doses of cisplatin [4,7]. A previous study assessed the renal safety and efficacy of cisplatin-based chemotherapy in 60 patients with solitary kidneys after nephroureterectomy for upper urinary tract UC. A significant decrease in the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was observed after treatment, but nephrotoxicity requiring temporary hemodialysis was observed in only two patients [9]. This retrospective study reviewed patients who received gemcitabine plus cisplatin (GC) as first-line chemotherapy, including neoadjuvant and adjuvant treatments. Patients who received GC after nephroureterectomy were in the solitary kidney group (16 patients), whereas those with both kidneys (BK) were in the BK group (31 patients). The incidences of grade 3/4 hematological toxicity and acute kidney injury did not differ significantly between the groups. The mean changes in serum creatinine and eGFR from baseline to post-chemotherapy cycles were similar in both groups [10]. Therefore, patients with upper urinary tract carcinoma and a solitary kidney with normal renal function need not necessarily be classified as cisplatin-ineligible.

Age is another factor complicating treatment decisions. Although age alone should not be a criterion for excluding patients from cisplatin therapy, older adult patients are at a higher risk of cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity and acute kidney injury [11]. Studies have shown that cisplatin-induced renal toxicity is significantly higher in older adults than in younger individuals [12]. Consequently, clinicians often take precautions such as dose adjustments, hydration, and the use of protective agents, such as magnesium, when treating older adult patients with cisplatin. Performance status, typically assessed using the ECOG scale, is another critical factor. Patients with an ECOG score of 2 or higher are usually ineligible, but some argue that this threshold can be relaxed in specific cases [13]. Other comorbidities, such as preexisting hearing loss and neuropathy, are also important considerations, as cisplatin can exacerbate these conditions, leading to permanent damage [8].

Although the Galsky criteria are widely used, their rigidity has been questioned. Some experts advocate for a more individualized approach by exploring alternative dosing strategies or biomarkers to better assess patient suitability [3].

2. Pharmacokinetics of cisplatin in decreased renal function

Understanding the pharmacokinetics of cisplatin in patients with decreased renal function is crucial to managing the efficacy and safety of this chemotherapeutic agent. Cisplatin is primarily excreted through the kidneys, and renal impairment delays its elimination, resulting in higher concentrations and prolonged exposure, which increase the risk of adverse effects, such as nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity [14].

Administered intravenously, cisplatin binds extensively to plasma proteins and is widely distributed in the body. Approximately 90% of cisplatin binds to plasma proteins within a few hours after administration, while the remaining 10%, which is plasma-free, is responsible for both its antitumor effects and toxicity. This unbound fraction has a half-life of 20–30 minutes following bolus administration in patients with healthy renal function [15,16]. However, in patients with renal dysfunction, cisplatin clearance is significantly reduced, leading to drug accumulation and worsening of its toxicity. This emphasizes the need for careful monitoring of renal function and dose adjustment to minimize the risk of toxicity [17].

The drug accumulates in the renal cortex, particularly damaging the S3 segment of the proximal tubule, causing a significant decrease in GFR [18,19,20]. Mechanisms of damage include the formation of reactive species that disrupt DNA and proteins, vasoconstriction that reduces renal blood flow and causes ischemic injury, and the release of proinflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-6, which exacerbate kidney inflammation and injury [21,22,23,24].

3. Reduced or split dose cisplatin-based chemotherapy

Reduced- or split-dose cisplatin regimens are used to balance efficacy and safety in patients who are unable to tolerate standard high-dose cisplatin because of factors such as impaired renal function, advanced age, or poor performance status [25,26]. Cisplatin is associated with significant toxicity, particularly nephrotoxicity, ototoxicity, and neurotoxicity, which can be exacerbated in high-risk patients [12]. To mitigate these risks, clinicians often use dose-reduction strategies or split-dose regimens that allow for better tolerability while maintaining clinical effectiveness [26].

Several studies have demonstrated that reduced-dose cisplatin regimens yield therapeutic outcomes comparable to those of standard-dose regimens. For instance, a meta-analysis of patients with head and neck cancer found that weekly low-dose cisplatin, compared to high-dose regimens every 3 weeks, showed no significant differences in OS but had lower severe hematological and renal toxicity, favoring high-risk patients [27]. Additionally, Murakami et al. [28], following Kintzel’s guidelines for cisplatin dose reduction in renal impairment, reported that dose reductions did not compromise clinical outcomes or increase adverse events (AEs), suggesting its viability for patients with metastatic UC (mUC) and CrCl between 30 and 60 mL/min [28]. In contrast, Sugimoto et al. [29] observed no significant oncological or toxicological differences between full-dose GC, dose-reduced GC, and gemcitabine-carboplatin in real-world practice, indicating that reduced doses may suffice without increasing toxicity.

Split-dose cisplatin reduces peak plasma concentration, thereby decreasing the risk of nephrotoxicity and other dose-limiting toxicities. This dosing schedule enhances patient compliance by being more tolerable, thereby avoiding the need for dose reduction or alternative treatments. Small studies in patients with mUC with impaired renal function (ClCr, 40–60 mL/min) demonstrated the feasibility of administering cisplatin on days 1 and 8 of a 21-day cycle at 35 mg/m2 using varied split-dose schedules. However, no prospective randomized trial has compared this method with conventional dosing. Izumi et al. [30] suggested that a split dose may be preferable for cisplatin-ineligible patients, although further large-scale studies are required for confirmation.

4. Carboplatin-based chemotherapy strategies with/without avelumab maintenance

Carboplatin-based chemotherapy is widely used in cisplatin-ineligible patients with mUC because of renal impairment or poor performance status. Carboplatin or cis-diamine (1,1-cyclobutanecarboxylato) platinum (II) is an analog of cisplatin. Compared to cisplatin, carboplatin is less potent but associated with fewer side effects, particularly nephrotoxicity [31]. Carboplatin combined with gemcitabine has demonstrated efficacy in patients with mUC who are cisplatinineligible. Several studies have reported an overall response rate (ORR) of 24% to 41.2%, with a median progression-free survival (PFS) of 4–5.8 months and an OS of 7.2–9.3 months. Toxicities were manageable, making carboplatin a viable and well-tolerated alternative for patients with impaired renal function, balancing efficacy and safety (Table 1) [32,33,34,35]. These findings reinforce the role of carboplatin in the treatment of mUC.

Avelumab has emerged as a crucial component of mUC treatment, especially in patients ineligible for cisplatin. The JAVELIN Bladder 100 trial demonstrated that avelumab maintenance significantly improved OS in patients who achieved response or stable disease after first-line platinum-based chemotherapy [36]. For patients treated with carboplatin-based chemotherapy, the trial revealed that those receiving avelumab maintenance had a median OS of 19.9 months compared to 12.9 months in those who did not receive avelumab (hazard ratio [HR] for OS, 0.66). In terms of PFS, the avelumab group had a median PFS of 3.0 months, compared to 1.9 months for patients who did not receive avelumab (HR for PFS, 0.59) [37]. These results underscore the significant survival benefit of avelumab maintenance therapy, making it a key therapeutic option for patients with mUC, including those treated with carboplatin-based chemotherapies.

In real-world settings, patients receiving carboplatin and gemcitabine, followed by avelumab maintenance therapy, experienced significant improvements in OS and PFS. Moreover, real-world data from AVENANCE, READY, and PATRIOT-II studies support the effectiveness of this treatment approach in patients with mUC [38,39,40]. Specifically, patients treated with carboplatin-based chemotherapy followed by avelumab maintenance therapy had a median OS of 18.9 months from the start of first-line avelumab maintenance treatment [39]. This strategy is currently recommended for patients ineligible for cisplatin treatment. Furthermore, maintenance immunotherapy functions by sustaining the antitumor immune response after chemotherapy, reducing tumor burden, and improving outcomes in a subgroup of patients who would otherwise have limited therapeutic options [39]. This treatment regimen has become the standard of care for cisplatin-ineligible patients with mUC, offering a balanced approach for efficacy and manageable safety.

5. Enfortumab vedotin plus pembrolizumab combination

Enfortumab vedotin (EV) is a novel ADC that has emerged as a significant therapeutic option for patients with mUC who have previously undergone treatment with platinum-based chemotherapy and ICIs [41,42]. EV specifically targets nectin-4, a protein overexpressed in UC cells, and directly delivers the potent microtubule-disrupting agent monomethyl auristatin E to cancer cells [42,43]. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) granted accelerated approval for EV based on promising results from the EV-201 trial, which demonstrated an objective response rate of 44% in patients with mUC [41,43]. EV efficacy has been further supported by the results of the EV-301 trial, which compared EV to standard chemotherapy options, such as docetaxel and paclitaxel, in patients who had previously received platinum-based therapy and programmed death protein 1 (PD-1)/programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitors [44,45]. After a median follow-up of 23.75 months, the median OS was 12.91 months in the EV group compared to 8.94 months in the chemotherapy group, with a difference of 3.97 months (HR 0.704, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.581–0.852; one-sided p=0.00015). This trial highlighted EV superiority, showing a significant improvement in OS compared with traditional chemotherapy regimens [45,46].

Besides EV, pembrolizumab, a PD-1 receptor inhibitor, is effective in treating mUC, especially in cisplatin-ineligible patients. This humanized monoclonal antibody, proven in MBC management post-platinum chemotherapy, showed in the KEYNOTE-045 phase III trial that it significantly enhanced OS over traditional chemotherapy, such as docetaxel, making it a standard for second-line advanced UC treatment [47,48]. The trial reported an ORR of 21% for pembrolizumab, with a median OS of 10.3 months, compared to 7.4 months for the chemotherapy group [47]. Furthermore, pembrolizumab has become a critical therapeutic option for patients with MBC who are ineligible for cisplatin-based chemotherapy and are often burdened by comorbidities, renal impairment, or poor performance status, which precludes the use of standard cisplatin regimens [49,50]. The KEYNOTE-052 trial, a pivotal phase II study, evaluated pembrolizumab efficacy as a first-line treatment for cisplatin-ineligible patients with locally advanced or mUC. The study enrolled 370 treatmentnaïve patients and revealed an ORR of 29%, with 6% achieving complete response (CR) [50]. These results highlight the potential of pembrolizumab to provide meaningful clinical benefits in challenging patient populations.

The combination of EV and pembrolizumab leverages EV’s cytotoxicity to alter the tumor microenvironment and enhance the efficacy of pembrolizumab immunotherapy. By destroying cancer cells, EVs expose tumor antigens and increase tumor susceptibility to pembrolizumab-mediated immune attacks. In cisplatin-ineligible patients with advanced or metastatic UC, early-phase trials, including phase Ib/II EV-103/KEYNOTE 869 Cohort A, have shown high ORRs (≥65%) [51]. The clinical benefit of this combination was further confirmed in randomized phase II and III trials [52,53]. Specifically, in a randomized phase II trial (EV-103/KEYNOTE-869 Cohort K), 149 patients with previously untreated, locally advanced, or metastatic UC ineligible for cisplatin-based regimens were randomly assigned to receive a combination of pembrolizumab plus EV (76 patients) or EV alone (73 patients) [53]. Based on these outcomes, the FDA granted accelerated approval in April 2023 for the combination of EV and pembrolizumab as first-line treatment for cisplatin-ineligible patients with locally advanced/mUC.

In a phase III trial of EV-302/KEYNOTE-A39, the efficacy of EV plus pembrolizumab was compared with that of GC or carboplatin in a first-line treatment setting [52]. This trial included a subgroup of 408 cisplatin-ineligible patients and provided critical insights into the benefits of this combination in this population [18]. In this subgroup, EV plus pembrolizumab improved OS (median not reached vs. 13 months, HR 0.43, 95% CI 0.31–0.59) and PFS (median 11 months vs. 7 months, HR 0.43, 95% CI 0.33–0.55) compared with chemotherapy alone. With limited treatment options available for mUC, the results of the EV-302/KEYNOTE-A39 trial have significantly shifted the treatment paradigms. Based on these findings, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network now recommends EV plus pembrolizumab combination as a Category 1 preferred treatment option, highlighting its efficacy regardless of cisplatin eligibility [54].

6. Emerging combination therapies with FGFR inhibitors

The FGFR pathway is critical in many cancers, including UC. FGFR1–4 mutations or alterations promote tumor growth by driving cell proliferation. Approximately 15% to 20% of patients with UC harbor FGFR mutations, making them an important therapeutic target. Given the prevalence of these alterations, FGFR inhibitors have become essential for the treatment of advanced UC [55,56,57].

Erdafitinib, the first FDA-approved FGFR inhibitor for mUC with FGFR mutations, achieved an ORR of 40% and PFS of 5.5 months in the BLC2001 trial [58]. Other FGFR inhibitors, such as pemigatinib and infigratinib, are currently being studied [59,60]. Although FGFR inhibitors have shown promising results as monotherapies, their resistance and limited response rates highlight the need for combination therapies. Therefore, the combination of FGFR with other therapeutic modalities, such as ICIs and chemotherapy, is being explored to enhance treatment efficacy and overcome drug resistance [59,61]. Nonetheless, there are conflicting data regarding FGFR alterations and responses to ICIs. In particular, some studies have suggested a reduced response of FGFR alterations to ICIs [62,63]. FGFR alterations are associated with T cell exhaustion in the UC immune microenvironment, potentially resulting in reduced T cell-mediated antitumor immune responses [64]. However, other studies have indicated no significant difference in the ORR between FGFR wild-type and mutated subgroups, and FGFR knockdown has been associated with the upregulation of interferon-gamma response genes [65]. Therefore, combining FGFR and PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors could increase efficacy, reverse resistance, address tumor heterogeneity, and target the immune microenvironment [65].

Several trials have explored the use of FGFR inhibitors as first-line therapies for cisplatin-ineligible patients with mUC (Table 2). These include trials comparing FGFR inhibitor monotherapy with FGFR inhibitor-ICI combinations, such as NORSE (NCT03473743), FIDES-02 (NCT04045613), and FIGHT-205 (NCT04003610) [66,67,68]. In another trial, FORT-2 (NCT03473756) compared ICI monotherapy with FGFR-ICI combinations, while THOR (NCT03390504) compared FGFR inhibitors, chemotherapy, and pembrolizumab monotherapy [69,70].

The phase I/II NORSE trial assessed the efficacy of erdafitinib alone and in combination with cetrelimab (an IgG4 anti-PD-1 inhibitor) in cisplatin-ineligible patients with FGFR alterations [66]. These patients were enrolled in the first-line therapy. The combination of erdafitinib and cetrelimab resulted in an ORR of 54.5%, with six CRs and a 12-month OS of 68%. Erdafitinib alone had an ORR of 44.2%, with one CR and a 12-month OS of 56% [71]. Ten patients experienced grade 3 AEs. The most frequent treatment-emergent AEs (of any grade) in the erdafitinib plus cetrelimab group and erdafitinib alone group were hyperphosphatemia (68.9% vs. 83.7%), stomatitis (59.1% vs. 72.1%), and diarrhea (45.5% vs. 48.8%). Grade ≥3 treatment-related AEs occurred in 45.5% (erdafitinib plus cetrelimab) and 46.5% (erdafitinib alone) of patients. One cetreliofftarget mab-related death secondary to pulmonary failure occurred in the erdafitinib plus cetrelimab group (NCT03473743).

The phase Ib/II FORT-2 trial (NCT03473756) investigated the safety, tolerability, and efficacy of a combination of rogaratinib and atezolizumab as the first-line treatment in cisplatin-ineligible patients with locally advanced/mUC [69]. These patients exhibited FGFR1/3 mRNA overexpression, as detected by RNA in situ hybridization of archival tissues using RNAscope. Preliminary results presented at the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) 2020 Congress revealed an ORR of 54% and a disease control rate (DCR) of 83% among 26 patients treated [72]. These findings suggest that the combination of rogaratinib and atezolizumab may overcome some limitations of the FORT-1 trial [73].

Another interesting phase II trial, FIGHT-205 (NCT04003610), compared pemigatinib alone, pemigatinib plus pembrolizumab, and the standard of care for patients with BC ineligible for cisplatin with centrally confirmed FGFR3 mutation/rearrangement [68]. Unfortunately, the study was terminated after enrolling seven patients. Additionally, a phase II combination trial of futibatinib and pembrolizumab (NCT04601857) is currently enrolling patients and has shown promising preliminary safety and tolerability data [74].

The combination of lenvatinib and pembrolizumab was investigated as a front-line treatment in the phase III LEAP-011 trial (NCT03898180), which evaluated the combination in cisplatin-ineligible patients with PD-L1 combined positive score ≥10 or those deemed ineligible for any platinum-based regimen regardless of PD-L1 expression [75].

7. Future perspectives on the treatment of cisplatin-ineligible patients

The treatment landscape for cisplatin-ineligible patients with advanced UC is evolving, driven by emerging ADCs, targeted therapies, and immunotherapies. Significant advances include ADCs, such as sacituzumab govitecan targeting Trop-2 and HER-2 in advanced UC, offering targeted cytotoxic delivery that enhances efficacy and minimizes off-target effects, which are critical in ongoing trials. FGFR inhibitors, such as erdafitinib, show promise, with future research likely focusing on boosting efficacy through combinations, especially with ICIs [66,67,68,69,70]. Early trials of FGFR-ICI combinations have improved outcomes, and further research is needed to refine patient selection and sequencing strategies. Additional pathways, such as HER2 and PI3K, also offer new potential therapies [76,77,78]. Overcoming drug resistance remains critical for the treatment of cisplatin-ineligible patients. Specifically, understanding resistance mechanisms and adopting adaptive treatment strategies are vital. Biomarker-guided personalized medicine plays a central role in customizing therapies for individuals, ensuring precise and effective treatment [79,80,81]. Importantly, it is essential to balance the treatment efficacy and minimize toxicity, especially given the frequent comorbidities in these patients. Finally, novel agents and combinations, including PARP inhibitors and other targeted therapies, are being investigated to enhance their efficacy [82].

CONCLUSIONS

Treating MBC in cisplatin-ineligible patients remains challenging because of the unclear definition of ineligibility, limitations of existing therapies, and complex patient profiles. Nearly 50% of patients cannot receive cisplatin-based chemotherapy, the standard treatment for MBC, because of renal dysfunction, poor performance status, and other comorbidities. Consequently, alternative treatments, such as ICIs, ADCs, and FGFR inhibitors, have been developed. Notably, recent combinations of EV and pembrolizumab have shown promising survival benefits, offering new hope for these patients. Ongoing research into novel agents and combinations, including ICIs with ADCs, FGFR inhibitors, and alternatives such as gemcitabine/carboplatin, underscores the dynamic progression of MBC treatment.
