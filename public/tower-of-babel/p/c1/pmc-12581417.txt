Long-term waist circumference and diabetes risk in premenopausal women: role of shared risk factors
Authors: Hye Ah Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe menopausal transition, a critical period for midlife women’s health, is marked by visceral fat redistribution. Although obesity and diabetes share common risk factors, their independent contributions to health risk remain unclear. This study investigated the association between 10-year mean waist circumference (WC) and incident type 2 diabetes in premenopausal women, and evaluated the impact of shared risk factors on both measures.MethodsUsing data from a community-based cohort with 16 years of follow-up, 1,482 premenopausal women at baseline were analyzed. The mean WC over 10 years was assessed, and its impact on incident type 2 diabetes over the subsequent six years was evaluated using Cox proportional models and visualized via mixed graphical model. Numerous demographics, lifestyle, genetic, and metabolic factors were considered shared risk factors.ResultsOver 10 years, WC increased by 0.22 cm annually (mean WC 78.3 cm). In the subsequent six years, 88 new cases of type 2 diabetes were identified. The mean WC varied with age, study area, education level, parental diabetes, alcohol consumption, and metabolic factors. Additionally, each 1 cm increase in the mean WC raised type 2 diabetes risk by 8% (95% CI: 1.05–1.11). Genetic factors; current smoking; and baseline metabolic factors were also independently associated with type 2 diabetes risk. This association persisted in women who transitioned to menopause during follow-up.ConclusionsThe association between higher WC during the menopausal transition and type 2 diabetes risk was observed. Comprehensive interventions aimed at lowering abdominal obesity could help reduce the risk of type 2 diabetes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-025-04086-3.Keywords: Abdominal obesity, Diabetes, Menopausal transition, Risk factor, Waist circumference

Background

The global diabetes burden increased by 129.7% from 1990 to 2017 and is expected to increase further [1]. According to the latest International Diabetes Federation (IDF) Diabetes Atlas reports, the global prevalence of diabetes was estimated at 589 million in 2024 and is projected to reach 853 million by 2050, primarily driven by type 2 diabetes [2]. Therefore, preventing diabetes is a major public health challenge.

Various factors influence the development of diabetes. Among them, anthropometric measurements such as body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) are cited as the leading risk factors [1, 3, 4]. These measures are interrelated, and consequently, the ranking of predictive factors for diabetes varies across studies. Nevertheless, there is a broad consensus that they play a significant role in the development of type 2 diabetes [5, 6]. Furthermore, these obesity-related factors are closely correlated with cardiometabolic risk factors, such as high fasting blood glucose (FBG), high blood pressure (BP), high triglycerides (TG), and low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), which may contribute to the development of type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease [7, 8]. Because obesity and type 2 diabetes share common upstream risk factors, such as unhealthy lifestyle behaviors and sociodemographic characteristics, a comprehensive approach is required to better understand their association [9, 10]. However, numerous risk factors are considered only as covariates or only the effect of a specific risk factor is reported, making it difficult to disentangle their independent effects. Therefore, a stepwise approach may be needed to accurately assess the individual contributions of each risk factor for type 2 diabetes.

Biological sex is a major determinant of differences in health risk, and several studies have found sex differences in the impact of anthropometric measurements on the development of type 2 diabetes [4, 11, 12]. In particular, women undergo unique physiological changes during midlife, with the menopausal transition, a critical period of physiologic change leading up to the final menstrual period, being a major health milestone in the second half of life [13, 14]. Menopause is defined retrospectively as 12 months after the permanent cessation of menstruation [13, 14]. This transition is characterized by a significant decline in estrogen levels and redistribution of subcutaneous fat to visceral fat [14, 15], which may contribute to an unfavorable cardiometabolic profile and endothelial dysfunction [16]. Given these physiological changes, WC and WHR have been suggested as better predictors of type 2 diabetes in middle-aged women [4, 5]. Additionally, studies indicate that the impact of obesity on type 2 diabetes in middle-aged women may differ depending on the pre- and postmenopausal stage [11, 17]. However, most studies have focused on predicting type 2 diabetes using a single measurement rather than long-term changes [18]. The transition to menopause typically occurs in women in their mid-to late 40 s; however, in approximately 5% of women, early menopause occurs between the ages of 40 and 45 [17]. Moreover, the physiological changes leading up to the final menstrual period can last 4 to 10 years [14]. Given that women are at increased risk of type 2 diabetes during the menopausal transition [13–15], further research is needed to better understand this period, particularly focusing on longitudinal changes in WC, a common indicator of visceral fat, and the subsequent risk of type 2 diabetes.

Therefore, considering the menopausal transition, this study aimed to evaluate long-term changes in WC over ten years and to investigate whether long-term WC levels are associated with the risk of developing type 2 diabetes, focusing on women who were premenopausal at baseline. Additionally, the relationship between baseline characteristics and both long-term WC levels and the risk of type 2 diabetes was examined.

Methods

Data source and study subjects

This study was conducted using data from a community-based cohort of the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study (KoGES), a population-based cohort that underwent long-term follow-up. It was designed to target middle-aged Koreans (40–69 years old) in two areas of Ansan (a medium-sized city) and Anseong (a rural area), Gyeonggi Province, to identify the risk factors for chronic diseases. A total of 10,030 people (n = 5,012 in Ansan and n = 5,018 in Anseong) participated in the study, and repeated biannual surveys were conducted since the baseline survey in 2001–2002. Data were collected using questionnaires, physical examinations, and clinical tests. A detailed description of this study can be found in the literature [19]. This study included data up to the eighth follow-up (conducted from 2017 to 2018; follow-up rate = 61.4%). No significant differences in characteristics between participants and non-participants were reported in a previously published study [19]. Of the study cohort, 52.6% were women (n = 5,272). In this study, study subjects were selected based on the following criteria:

Women without a history of cancer, cardiovascular disease, or diabetes (n = 4,084).

Women without a history of cancer, cardiovascular disease, or diabetes (n = 4,084).

Premenopausal women aged 40–55 years at baseline (n = 1,636).

Individuals who had their WC measured at least twice during the ten-year follow-up period (n = 1,560).

Ultimately, data from 1,482 premenopausal women aged 40–55 years at baseline were included in the analysis.

Measurements

WC (in cm) was measured at the midpoint between the last rib and the iliac crest with the tape kept horizontal while the participants stood upright. Measurements were taken at the end of a gentle exhalation, with the participant breathing calmly, and care was taken not to compress the skin with the tape. This study followed women who were premenopausal at baseline longitudinally and assessed changes in their WC over a 10-year follow-up period (i.e., exposure period). Among the study subjects, 57.8% had their WC measured at every time point during the exposure period, including at baseline. A prior study based on the same dataset examined the 6-year trajectory of WC and reported generally flat and stable patterns over time [9]. In the current study population, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for repeated measures of WC was 0.80, indicating high within-person consistency. In light of these findings, the average of all repeated WC measurements was calculated and referred to as ‘mean WC’.

Outcome ascertainment

Incident type 2 diabetes during the six years following the exposure period (the 10-year trajectory of WC) was defined as individuals who were diagnosed with type 2 diabetes by a physician, were taking diabetes medication, or had a fasting blood glucose (FBG) ≥ 126 mg/dL or hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%. Follow-up began at the fifth follow-up survey and ended on the date of physician-diagnosed diabetes, detection of FBG ≥ 126 mg/dL or HbA1c ≥ 6.5% during follow-up, or the last follow-up (date of the eighth follow-up, conducted in 2017–2018) (whichever was earlier).

Covariates

The following risk factors were included as covariates: age; study region; education level; parental diabetes; current smoking; alcohol intake; physical activity; sleep duration; total energy intake; dietary diversity score (DDS); dietary inflammatory index (DII); unweighted genetic risk score for diabetes; and FBG, HbA1c, HDL-C, mean arterial pressure (MAP), and TG levels.

Anthropometric measurements vary depending on the study region [20, 21], so it was included. Lifestyle factors, current smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, and sleep duration were also considered. Based on a previous study [22], alcohol consumption was categorized as non-drinkers, ≤ 14 g/day, and >14 g/day in women. Physical activity was defined based on responses to a questionnaire asking about the average daily time spent in moderate- or vigorous-intensity activities. ‘Meets guidelines’ for physical activity was defined as moderate activity lasting more than 150 min/week or vigorous activity lasting more than 75 min/week. Participants who did not meet these criteria were classified as the ‘insufficiently active’ group, and those who reported no physical activity were classified as the ‘inactive’ group. Average sleep duration was categorized as ≥ 7 hours or not based on previous study [23].

With regard to dietary factors, total energy intake, DDS, and food-based DII were considered. In the baseline survey, dietary data were collected using a dish-based semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) (consisting of 103 food items), and individual DSS and DII were calculated based on the FFQ data. The DDS proposed by Kim et al. [24], which was calculated based on five food groups (grains, meat/fish/eggs/legumes, vegetables, fruits, and milk), was modified and used in this study. It was evaluated based on the twelve food groups (grain, chicken, processed meat, processed red meat (non-ruminant), processed red meat (ruminant), eggs, fish and seafood, legumes, nuts and seeds, vegetables, fruit, and milk), based on a previous study [25]. A score of 1 was assigned for each food group consumed at least once per week, and the total DDS was calculated by summing these scores across the 12 food groups (range: 0–12). A high number indicates that, on average, a variety of food groups are consumed. Na et al. developed a food-based DII using Korean population data based on the FFQ [26]. It comprises seven anti-inflammatory (green vegetables, eggs, citrus fruits, legumes, red fruits, bread wheat flour, nuts and seeds) and three pro-inflammatory (mixed coffee sweetened drinks, white rice, and beef) food groups. The individual’s DII was calculated by multiplying the average daily intake (g) of each food group by the corresponding effect size of that food group on high-sensitivity C-reactive protein [26]. Detailed definitions of the food groups and the effect sizes are provided in the study by Na et al. [26]. Higher values indicate greater intake of foods that promote inflammation.

An unweighted genetic risk score (GRS) was calculated based on the number of risk alleles for each of the 25 diabetes-associated variants using genotype data from the Illumina HumanExome BeadChip v1.1 (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). These variants have previously been identified in genome-wide association studies in Asia [27]. Detailed information on genetic variants is provided elsewhere [27]. Higher values indicate a greater genetic predisposition to diabetes. Only 1,220 study participants had genetic data available. Therefore, subjects with missing data were categorized as the missing group. Additionally, baseline FBG, HbA1c, HDL-C, MAP, and TG levels were considered metabolic features [8].

Statistical analysis

Summary statistics are expressed as means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges for numeric variables, and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Given that the average WC showed significant differences across the study areas, the differences in characteristics between the areas were estimated. Group differences were analyzed using the t-test and Mann-Whitney U test for numerical variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. To observe the changes in WC over ten years, the average changes at different follow-up time points were tracked. This was assessed using a linear mixed model with a random intercept, including covariates and time as fixed effects. The covariance matrix is determined based on the lowest Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) values. Residual diagnostics confirmed that the assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity were adequately met.

To explore the risk factors associated with the mean WC and repeated measures of WC, a linear regression analysis and a linear mixed model, both adjusted for covariates, were performed. The two-way interaction effect among the covariates was also examined.

The effect of the mean WC over ten years on the incidence of type 2 diabetes was assessed using the Cox proportional hazards model, and hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated. The covariates were considered simultaneously in a multivariate model. The proportional hazard assumption was assessed using the Schoenfeld residuals test and log-minus-log plot and was found to be satisfactory. The interaction effects of the mean WC and covariates were assessed by adding a product term. To assess collinearity, the variance inflation factor was calculated in the multivariate model and showed a value of less than 2.0. To strengthen the validity of the results, sensitivity analyses were performed using the following criteria: (1) women whose WC was measured at least three times during the exposure period (n = 1,312) and (2) women who had experienced menopause during the same period as the WC change observation (n = 829). Additionally, the effect of using baseline WC or abdominal obesity (≥ 80 cm) [28] used instead of mean WC was calculated. To address the bias arising from missing data, pooled estimates were generated from 10 datasets created using the multiple imputation (MI) method, following Rubin’s rule. MI was conducted using the ‘mice’ package in R, under the assumption that data were missing completely at random. For MI, all covariates, along with type 2 diabetes incidence and mean WC, were included in the imputation model. The proportion of missing values for covariates was generally less than 3%, with the most common being total energy (missing in 54 participants, 3.6%). Default imputation methods were applied according to variable type. Additionally, the linearity of the effect of mean and baseline WC on incident type 2 diabetes was assessed using restricted cubic splines, with knots selected based on the lowest BIC.

To capture the complex interrelationships among covariates, mean WC, and incident type 2 diabetes, a mixed graphical model (MGM) was used for visualization. It was done using the ‘mgm’ R package [29]. In MGM analysis, conditional independence between pairs of variables in multivariate data is represented by partial correlation coefficients. To identify only meaningful associations, ℓ1-penalized Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression with an appropriately chosen regularization parameter (λ) was performed. The selection of k = 2 corresponds to a pairwise MGM that includes, at the most, two-way interactions. The optimal regularization parameter (λ) was determined using 10-fold cross-validation. In the resulting graphical model, the absence of an edge between two nodes (variables) indicates conditional independence, given all other variables. Edges can be classified as positive (green), negative (red), or gray, where gray represents connections involving categorical variables without a defined sign. The strength of the association is represented by the thickness of the edges. To quantify the predictability of each variable based on its connected neighbors, the explained variance (R2) for continuous variables and correct classification (CC) for categorical variables were computed. These measures reflect how well a given node can be predicted by other nodes to which it is connected. The layout of the network was determined using the Fruchterman–Reingold algorithm, ensuring that strongly associated nodes were positioned closer to one another for improved visualization. Additionally, edge selection probabilities in the network were evaluated using 100 bootstrap samples, with each sample generated by resampling with replacement.

Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and R version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 in a two-tailed test.

Results

Among the 1,482 premenopausal women at baseline, 36.3% lived in rural areas and the average age was 43.9 years. Of these, 3.1% reported drinking more than 14 g per day, and 2.7% reported currently smoking. At baseline, the mean FBG and HbA1c were 80.3 mg/dL and 5.4%, respectively. During the follow-up period, the mean WC of women over 10 years was 78.3 cm, and 88 new cases of type 2 diabetes were identified over the subsequent six years (4.4 per 1,000 person-years [95% CI 3.6–5.4]) (Table 1). Depending on the study region, there were differences in the average age, education level, and lifestyle factors such as physical activity, sleep time, and food intake. Compared with rural women, women in non-rural areas had a higher proportion of high education levels but a higher proportion of physical inactivity and insufficient sleep. They also consumed lower daily calories but a wider variety of foods. The mean WC of rural women was 80.4 cm, which was higher than that of non-rural women, and the incidence of type 2 diabetes was also higher in rural areas than in non-rural areas (5.6 vs. 3.7 per 1000 person-years, P = 0.039) (Supplemental Table 1).

Figure 1 shows the results of tracking the WC of the women during the ten-year follow-up period. The results showed a tendency for the average WC to increase as the follow-up period extended beyond the baseline (+ 0.22 cm/year, P < 0.001). On average, an increase of 2.3 cm in WC was observed over ten years, based on multiple measurements taken during this period. In women who had experienced menopause, the mean WC was 0.71 cm greater than that of all women. The average change in WC according to study region was significant (+ 4.0 cm vs. + 1.4 cm), with individuals in the rural region showing a higher mean WC compared to those in the non-rural region at all observation time points (Supplemental Fig. 1).

Tracking of waist circumference in women over ten years. FU: follow-up, WC: waist circumference. Adjusted mean waist circumference (circles) and 95% confidence intervals (bars) during the follow-up period are presented. These values were obtained from a linear mixed model with a random intercept, using an unstructured covariance matrix (Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) = 40,942.6 for the total population and BIC = 25,848.5 for women who had experienced menopause). The model was adjusted for age, study region, education level, parental diabetes, current smoking, alcohol intake, physical activity, sleep time, total energy intake, dietary diversity score, dietary inflammatory index, unweighted genetic risk score, fasting blood glucose, hemoglobin A1c, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, mean arterial pressure, triglycerides, and time as fixed effects. The mean waist circumference (dashed line) represents the summarized value of the waist circumference measured multiple times for each individual during the follow-up period.

Even after controlling for covariates, the mean WC differed across the study areas, with a higher average in the rural areas (+ 6.1 cm). In addition, age, education level, parental diabetes, alcohol consumption, and metabolic factors were independently related to mean WC. These tendencies were also observed in repeated WC measurements using the linear mixed model (Table 2). No two-way interaction effects between the covariates and study region on the mean WC were observed in either statistical model (data not shown). For repeated measures of WC, even when the analysis was restricted to subjects who had experienced menopause, age, study region, education level, alcohol consumption, and metabolic factors remained significant (Supplemental Table 2).

Table 3 presents the effect of the mean WC over ten years, including various risk factors, on incident type 2 diabetes. In rural women, the risk of type 2 diabetes was approximately 1.5 times higher, the risk was not statistically significant. The FBG, HbA1c, and TG levels at baseline also showed independent association with type 2 diabetes. In addition to genetic factors related to type 2 diabetes, current smoking has also been linked to the development of type 2 diabetes. Furthermore, for every 1 cm increase in the mean WC, the risk of type 2 diabetes increased by 8% (95% CI 1.05–1.11). This effect remained when the pooled estimates were derived from the MI datasets. The effect of the mean WC on incident type 2 diabetes did not differ across the study areas (P interaction = 0.432). No other sublevel factors induced any risk differences (Supplemental Fig. 2). The effect of mean WC on the incident type 2 diabetes showed linearity (P nonlinear = 0.054), whereas baseline WC did not (Supplemental Fig. 3).

Furthermore, the effect of mean WC on incident type 2 diabetes persisted even when the analysis was restricted to subjects with at least three WC measurements (HR = 1.08, 95% CI 1.05–1.12) or those who had experienced menopause (HR = 1.07, 95% CI 1.02–1.11). However, when the baseline WC was applied instead of the mean WC, the effect was attenuated (HR = 1.04, 95% CI 0.99–1.08) in women who had experienced menopause. Even when the mean WC exceeded 80 cm over ten years, it was associated with an increased risk of type 2 diabetes (HR = 3.16, 95% CI 1.81–5.50) (Supplemental Table 3).

Discussion

This study assessed the influence of shared risk factors on long-term WC levels, represented by mean WC, and the development of type 2 diabetes in a stepwise manner, focusing on premenopausal women at baseline. Over ten years, WC increased by an average of 0.22 cm per year, with rural women having a WC 6.1 cm higher than their counterparts. Additionally, lower educational levels and higher alcohol consumption were associated with greater WC. Poor metabolic health at baseline was also associated with increased WC. Subsequently, mean WC over ten years was associated with an increased risk of type 2 diabetes, regardless of the study region. Current smoking was not associated with mean WC, but was linked to the development of type 2 diabetes. Furthermore, the effect of mean WC on incident type 2 diabetes persisted in women who had experienced menopause during the observation period.

Differences in neighborhood environments may be associated with health risks. Consistent with this study, several studies have observed differences in lifestyle- and adiposity-related features between urban and rural areas [21]. Women may be more vulnerable to environmental influences on obesity than men, and neighborhood-level characteristics may constrain the formation of healthy lifestyle habits [20]. In this study, rural women tended to consume more calories on average, but they also had lower dietary diversity and higher DII values, indicating greater intake of foods that promote inflammation. Meanwhile, the level of physical activity of rural women was higher than that of non-rural women; however, it was difficult to determine this in detail because leisure-time physical activity was not separated from physical activity. This study found that mean WC may influence type 2 diabetes incidence regardless of region, whereas regional differences appeared to affect type 2 diabetes incidence indirectly through their influence on mean WC, rather than directly. However, as this study included only two communities, it is difficult to draw universal conclusions. Further research involving more diverse regions is needed to better address environmental contributors to health inequalities.

In addition to differences attributed to regional environments, education level and alcohol consumption were independently associated with the mean WC in women. Risk factors, such as education level and alcohol consumption, may be associated with a wide range of health spectra. A meta-analysis based on 11 studies conducted in women found that a low education level was associated with a 1.7-fold higher risk of abdominal obesity as assessed by WC [30]. It has been argued that higher educational attainment is linked to increased health literacy and greater material and financial resources, which in turn lead to healthier behaviors and reduced health risks [30]. The relationship between alcohol consumption and obesity shows conflicting results depending on sex, age, and research methodology; however, some studies have shown a positive relationship in women [31]. In this context, alcohol consumption may affect the mechanisms related to appetite [32], liver fat accumulation, inflammatory responses, or hormone interference [33]. Furthermore, the correlations between alcohol consumption and various metabolic indicators were examined (Fig. 2). The association between alcohol consumption and type 2 diabetes is controversial [34], and the null findings may have been influenced by mediating factors. Interestingly, smoking, which strongly correlated with alcohol consumption, showed no association with WC and seemed to have a direct effect on the development of type 2 diabetes. Thus, to reduce health risks, it would be beneficial to address the cluster of unhealthy behaviors.

In general, sexual dimorphism in body fat composition is known, with women having more subcutaneous fat than visceral fat; visceral fat accumulation accelerates after menopause [35]. Accordingly, the increase in WC with age was more prominent in women, which may be explained by a decline in the effects of estrogen due to aging [13, 15, 36]. Furthermore, circulating estrogens play a protective role in glucose homeostasis by influencing both central and peripheral regulation, thereby contributing to the prevention of type 2 diabetes [13]. Indeed, the Study of Women’s Health Across the Nation reported that pre-menopausal estradiol levels were inversely associated with the risk of type 2 diabetes during the menopausal transition [37].

A dose-response meta-analysis of 38 studies found that a 1-cm increase in WC increased the risk of developing type 2 diabetes by 5% in women [18], supporting the findings of this study. That estimate was mostly based on baseline measurements, but increased risks were observed when repeated measurements were taken into account, which is consistent with this study. Some studies identified trajectory patterns in WC that are monotonous over the observation period [9, 38]. Aligning with previous studies, this study showed a high intra-individual reliability of multiple WC measurements, whereas a single WC measurement failed to maintain its association with type 2 diabetes development, especially in women who had experienced menopause. In fact, the Cox model using mean WC (BIC = 1071.0) showed better fit than the model using baseline WC (BIC = 1085.6) (Supplemental Table 3). Furthermore, the linearity of mean WC with type 2 diabetes risk observed in this study supports interpretation in a context consistent with clinical risk thresholds (≥ 80 cm). Long-term averages may outperform single measurements in association studies, as they are less affected by measurement error and regression dilution. This suggests that the prediction of type 2 diabetes based on a single measurement during sensitive life-course periods, when body shape and preferential gain of abdominal fat may change, may not fully capture long-term risk.

According to the Global Burden of Diseases (GBD) study, in 2021, high fasting plasma glucose levels, high BMI, and dietary risk factors were assessed as major risk factors contributing to the burden of type 2 diabetes in women of childbearing age (15–49 years). In particular, the global type 2 diabetes burden attributable to high BMI has more than doubled between 1990 and 2021 [39]. In obesity epidemiology, as the increasing trend continues, the burden of diabetes due to high BMI is expected to continue to increase. Several previous studies suggest that abdominal adiposity measurements may be a superior indicator of type 2 diabetes risk prediction in women compared to general adiposity measurements [4, 5, 11], but not all [6, 40]. Some studies show that ratio indices such as WHR and waist-to-height ratio have a good predictive ability for type 2 diabetes [4, 5, 11], whereas others argue that these ratios are difficult to interpret biologically and are less sensitive to weight gain [3, 11]. Therefore, a definitive conclusion has not yet been reached. However, it is clear that reducing obesity is important for reducing type 2 diabetes risk, and abdominal and general obesity needs to be managed in women [4].

In interpreting the results of this study, the following points should be considered. These results were obtained from two specific communities, so there are limitations in generalizing the results. Due to the absence of directly measured data on fat distribution and sex hormone levels, a detailed interpretation is limited. Although measurement errors may bias the results, this is likely mitigated by using the mean WC from repeated measurements. However, residual bias may still exist due to the incomplete capture of menopausal transition timing in relation to WC accumulation. This study evaluated the associations across a broad range of covariates and identified shared risk factors for both high WC and type 2 diabetes, but only considered baseline data. Unmeasured covariates may have affected the results. However, the consistency of the findings was strengthened by various sensitivity analyses. Moreover, because the study design excluded patients who developed type 2 diabetes during the WC observation period, the possibility of an inverse association can be ruled out. Nevertheless, potential selection bias remains possible due to differences between included and excluded subjects, which could have affected the results. The limited number of events in this study may have reduced statistical power and precision, particularly for lifestyle covariates. Accordingly, future studies applying penalized Cox or shrinkage methods could help identify key risk factors and ensure more robust estimates.

Conclusions

This study focused on the menopausal transition period, tracked changes in WC over 10 years, and assessed the relationship between mean WC and subsequent type 2 diabetes risk. WC tended to increase with age, and on average, a higher WC was associated with a higher risk of type 2 diabetes, a pattern that remained consistent even in women who had experienced menopause. Additionally, the study found that unhealthy lifestyle habits, such as alcohol consumption and smoking, are associated with higher WC and type 2 diabetes risk. Given that women undergoing menopause may face a higher risk of developing metabolic and cardiovascular diseases, strategies aimed at reducing WC and promoting healthy lifestyle habits could be beneficial.
