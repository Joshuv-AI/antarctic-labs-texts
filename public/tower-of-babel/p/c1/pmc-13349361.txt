Revolutionizing Drug Delivery: The Power of Ultrasound in Ameliorative Effects of Functionalized Single-Walled Carbon Nanotube Encapsulated Dacarbazine in a Murine B16F10 Melanoma Model
Authors: Moghadaseh Mahdinejad, Mohammad Bagher Tavakoli, Azade Taheri, Fateme Heidari, Seyed Hossein Hejazi, Tahereh Komeili Movahed, Hadi Nasrollahi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The field of drug delivery has witnessed remarkable advancements aimed at optimizing therapeutic outcomes and reducing adverse effects. In this study, we examined the ameliorative effect of ultrasound waves in combination with Functionalized Carbon nanotubes -encapsulated Dacarbazine (fSWCNT-DTIC) in a murine B16F10 melanoma model.Materials and Methods:The Preparation of single-walled carbon nanotube encapsulated Dacarbazine (fSWCNT-DTIC) accrued at several steps. The melanoma tumor model was induced by injection of B16F10 cells subcutaneously into the right shoulder of c57bl/6 mice. After the tumor reached a volume of 100mm3each group was treated locally with 60 μL of either vehicle (normal saline), Dacarbazine, Carbon nanotube, Ultrasound, or a combination of them. Tumor Growth inhibition, systemic toxicity, and also liver and lung histopathological changes were assayed in different groups. In all analyses, a significance level of 0.05 was deemed appropriate.Result:The highest Tumor Growth inhibition (%88.21) was seen in the group that received single-walled carbon nanotubes encapsulated Dacarbazine and also exposed to ultrasound radiation. In various groups, there was no noticeable variance in weight loss at the experimental period. Combination therapy improved the chaotic condition, inhibited the spread of cancer cells in the lungs, reduced severe inflammation in the lung lobes and reversed abnormal patterns in the liver.Conclusion:The combination of ultrasound and SWCNT-encapsulated Dacarbazine, could make a significant difference in the treatment process and improve the treatment outcomes for cancer patients.

INTRODUCTION

In recent years, the field of drug delivery has witnessed remarkable advancements aimed at optimizing therapeutic outcomes and reducing adverse effects.[1] Among these innovative approaches, ultrasound-assisted drug delivery has emerged as a promising technique with significant potential.[2] Recently the application of ultrasound in drug delivery, specifically focusing on its ability to enhance the efficacy of drug delivery has attracted more attention.[34]

Melanoma, a malignancy primarily affecting melanocytes, is a highly aggressive form of skin cancer with limited treatment options.[5] Conventional chemotherapy drugs often face challenges related to poor solubility, rapid clearance from the body, and systemic toxicity.[6] To overcome these limitations, researchers have turned their attention to nanotechnology-based drug delivery systems, such as carbon nanotubes (CNTs).[78] CNTs possess unique properties that make them ideal candidates for drug delivery applications. Their high surface area, tunable physicochemical properties, and ability to penetrate biological barriers enable efficient encapsulation and targeted release of anticancer agents.[910] Furthermore, their excellent biocompatibility and low immunogenicity make CNTs a safe and viable option for clinical translation.[11]

In recent years carbon nanotube coating has gained attention owing to their ability to encapsulate, protect them from degradation, and efficiently deliver them to the tumor site.[812] However, the challenge lies in achieving optimal drug release kinetics and effective penetration through the tumor microenvironment.[13] This is where ultrasound comes into play.

Ultrasound, a non-invasive imaging technique widely used in clinical practice, has shown great promise as a complementary tool in drug delivery.[14] Its ability to generate mechanical waves that induce controlled cavitation and acoustic streaming can significantly enhance drug penetration through biological barriers, including solid tumors.[15] Furthermore, ultrasound can be precisely tuned to trigger drug release from Nano-carriers, promoting localized drug deposition while minimizing off-target effects.[16]

Due to the many benefits of ultrasound ineffective treatment, this article aims to explore the synergistic effect of ultrasound and carbon nanotube-encapsulated Dacarbazine in the treatment of mouse B16F10 Melanoma model. It will delve into highlighting how it enhances drug penetration and maximizes therapeutic efficacy.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials, chemicals, and reagents

Dacarbazine MEDAC (DACARBAZINE CITRATE-DTIC) was purchased from MEDAC GMBH (Germany). SWCNT (single-wall carbon nanotube purity >90%) was purchased from US research nanomaterials, B16F10 melanoma cell line was obtained from the Pasteur Institute (Iran), DMEM, Fetal Bovine Serum, Phosphate Buffered Saline, Penicillin/Streptomycin, and Trypsin-EDTA solutions were provided by Gibco Company (United States).

Synthesis of single-walled carbon nanotube encapsulated Dacarbazine

The Preparation of single-walled carbon nanotube encapsulated Dacarbazine (fSWCNT-DTIC) accrued at several steps including evaluation of Dacarbazine concentration (using UV-visible spectrophotometry method), evaluation of the encapsulation efficiency following different formulations of fSWCNT-DTIC nanoparticles, characterization of fSWCNT- DTIC (using Fourier transform infrared spectra, UV-visible, Zeta potential assay and Transmission electron microscopy assays) and evaluation of Dacarbazine release from fSWCNT (Data is being published).

Cell culture

Murine melanoma cells (B16F10 - ATCC: CRL-6475; mouse melanoma cell), were purchased from the Pasteur Institute (Iran) and maintained by sub-culturing in RPMI 1640 medium (R0883, Sigma Aldrich, USA) supplemented with 2 mM L-Glutamine (G8540, Sigma-Aldrich), 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS, Sigma–Aldrich) and Penicillin/Streptomycin (1 U/ml and 1 μg/ml), at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere incubator (Eppendorf, Germany).

Cell viability test

B16F10 melanoma cells were detached from a culture dish when they reached 70-80% coverage, then they were washed and finally suspended in a culture medium. Cell count and viability were determined by trypan blue staining followed by a hemocytometer. 1 × 106 cells were then suspended in 0.1 mL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) pH-7.4 and used for induction of the melanoma tumor model.

Animals

A study was conducted using 48 female C57/BL6 mice, with a weight range of 22-25 g. The mice were purchased from the Pasteur Institute in Tehran, Iran, and the experiment commenced after receiving approval from the experimental animal ethics committee (Ethics code: IR.LUI.REC.1396.3.896). The mice had access to fresh drinking water ad libitum and were kept in a room with controlled temperature (23 ± 1 °C), humidity (55 ± 10%), and light/dark cycle (12/12 h).[17] They were also given standard laboratory chow (Pars Animal Feed Co, Tehran, Iran) with free access. Before the experiment, the mice were acclimatized to the laboratory conditions for a week.

Induction of tumor by B16F10 melanoma cells

The melanoma tumor model was induced by injection of 1 × 106 B16F10 cells in 100 μl of PBS subcutaneously into the right shoulder of C57BL/6 mice.[18]

In short, after anesthesia with ketamine (45–55 mg/kg) and xylazine (6.5–8.5 mg/kg), with povidone–iodine, the right shoulder of mice was shaved and cleaned. Then 1 × 106 B16F10 melanoma cells were injected with 100 μl of PBS. The tumor-bearing model mice were utilized for experimentation once the tumor volume reached 100 mm3 (7 days after tumor cell injection)

Experimental design and treatment protocol

C57BL/6 mice have been divided into 8 groups (n = 6) [Table 1].

Treatment protocol in different experimental groups

Each group received a local treatment of 60 μL, consisting of either the vehicle (0.9% normal saline), including Dacarbazine, Carbon nanotube, Ultrasound, or a combination thereof [Table 1]. The animals were housed separately for the next assay.

The intensity range typically employed for inducing cellular changes is less than 3 W/cm2, while the frequency range of 0.8-3 MHz has been established as effective for cancer treatment.[19] Consequently, an ultrasound frequency of 1 MHz and an intensity of 2 W/cm2 were employed in this study.

In vivo anti-tumor efficacy assay

The tumor size was measured using a caliper every 2 days until 15 days [Figure 1]. Tumor volume was calculated by the modified ellipsoidal formula: V = ½ (Length × Width2)[20] in which the Length was determined as the greatest longitudinal diameter and the width was determined as the greatest transverse diameter. Mice were euthanized when tumors reached 1000 mm3 or skin ulceration appeared to be imminent.

Measurement of tumor growth using caliper

Tumor Growth inhibition (TGI) in different groups was calculated as: TGI = 100×(1-Vt/Vc)

Where Vc refers to the size of the tumor in the control group and Vc refers to the size of the tumor in the treated group.[21]

Tissue preparation and histopathology examination

For histopathological analysis, liver and lung samples were collected and samples from liver and lungs were promptly immersed in a 10% formalin solution upon collection. Subsequently, they were cleared in a xylene series, embedded in paraffin wax, cut into 5 μm sections, mounted on glass slides, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin dye (H&E stain) following Bancroft and Layton’s protocol.[22] Photomicrographs and histology examinations were taken using a light microscope (Leica DM750, Leica Microsystems, India). The light microscope was used to capture photomicrographs and conduct histology examinations. An expert pathologist, who was unaware of the experiment, analyzed these images for abnormalities using the Image J software (Image J, National Institute of Health, Bethesda, MD).[23]

In vivo evaluation of systemic toxicity

To assess the systemic toxicity of the treatment we compared the weight loss and conducted histological analysis of the liver and lungs with those of the control group in an in vivo setting.

Statistical analysis

The data are shown as mean ± SEM. statistical analysis involved one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s test for multiple comparisons using the statistical software package SPSS version 18. In all analyses, the significance level of 0.05 was used for all analyses.

RESULTS

Assessment of in-vivo anti-tumor efficacy

The therapeutic effect on tumor-bearing mice was investigated. As seen in Figure 2, the control, ultrasound (US), carbon nanotube (fSWCNT), and carbon nanotube with ultrasound radiation (US + fSWCNT) groups showed significant tumor growth. In the group that received single-walled carbon nanotubes containing Dacarbazine (fSWCNT + DTIC), the tumor volume showed a notable decrease in comparison to the Dacarbazine (DTIC) group. The tumor volume in the group that received single-walled carbon nanotubes encapsulated Dacarbazine and also exposed to ultrasound radiation had the smallest amount of size over time (P < 0.05). TGI in different groups is shown in Figure 3. This was the highest (%88.21) for the group with combination therapy using single-walled carbon nanotubes encapsulated Dacarbazine combined with ultrasound radiation.

DISCUSSION

This study was conducted on a murine B16F10 melanoma model, where mice were treated with functionalized SWCNT-encapsulated DTIC and subjected to ultrasound exposure. The combination of SWCNT encapsulation and ultrasound stimulation resulted in improved drug uptake by tumor cells leading to a significant reduction in tumor growth. By utilizing SWCNTs as drug carriers and ultrasound as a trigger for drug release, researchers were able to enhance drug delivery and improve therapeutic outcomes.

Melanoma is a highly aggressive malignancy with high metastatic potential that resists most conventional therapy. In the last 30 years incidence and mortality rates of melanoma have increased worldwide.[24] Malignant melanoma can spread rapidly from the primary site of origin through the lymphatic and circulatory system and can metastasize to multiple organs of the body (i.e., the lung, liver, lymph nodes, bone, and brain).[25]

It is well-documented that the lung is a frequent site for metastatic disease, with approximately 18% of melanoma patients developing lung metastases during their follow-up period.[26] Unfortunately, the prognosis for patients with metastatic disease in the lungs is very grim, with a 5-year survival rate of less than 10%.[2728] The results of this study indicated the presence of multiple metastatic foci in the lungs of the control, fSWCNT, DTIC, and US groups. The alveolar sacs in the lung showed disorganization with a significant increase in pleomorphic tumor cell growth. (arrows in Figure 4) in contrast, the group that received the combination treatment exhibited minimal disorganization.

Liver metastases are also frequently identified in various types of cancers such as lung, colorectal, pancreatic, breast, and melanoma cancer. Multiple immune cells have been found to play a role in promoting and maintaining the spread of cancer cells to the liver.[2930] In the present study the number of immune cells increased in the cancer control groups. As noted in Figure 5, There was a noticeable presence of severe lobular inflammation and disorganization in the hepatic acinar pattern observed in the cancer control group in comparison to the DTIC combination therapy groups.

Combination therapy has indeed shown great promise in enhancing the effectiveness of cancer treatments, especially when it comes to drug delivery and efficacy.[31]

There are several mechanisms by which this combination therapy could work. Ultrasound can induce mechanical stress and cavitation effects that can rupture the cell membrane, allowing for improved uptake of some drugs into the cancer cells.[32] Additionally, ultrasound can also increase the permeability of blood vessels and enhance the circulation of drugs throughout the tumor tissue.[33] The study findings further validated the beneficial impact of ultrasound within the respective groups.

Ultrasound is a non-invasive and widely used medical imaging technique that has been explored for its potential therapeutic applications.[34] One promising area of research involves using ultrasound to enhance the delivery of anti-cancer drugs to tumors, thereby increasing their efficacy while minimizing systemic toxicity.[35]

Our results demonstrated that the combination of ultrasound and SWCNT-encapsulated Dacarbazine has the potential to significantly improve the efficacy of cancer treatments, providing a novel and promising avenue for future cancer research. SWCNTs are known to be selectively taken up by cancer cells, making them an attractive vehicle for targeted drug delivery.[8] Based on previous studies, SWCNTs can be easily functionalized with a variety of molecules and can also penetrate the cell membrane, making them ideal for delivering drugs directly to cancer cells.[81236]

The possibility of using ultrasound to enhance the delivery of anti-cancer drugs to tumors has been extensively explored in research studies.[3537] Researchers have investigated various delivery methods, such as liposomes, exosomes, and nanoparticles to improve drug efficacy and minimize systemic toxicity.[3839] However, one promising approach involves using SWCNTs encapsulated with a chemotherapy drug (Dacarbazine) in combination with ultrasound. So; in this study, we developed a novel SWCNT-encapsulated Dacarbazine formulation that demonstrated enhanced anti-cancer activity when combined with ultrasound. We found that the SWCNT-Dacarbazine complex was selectively taken up by cancer cells and, when exposed to low-frequency ultrasound, exhibited significantly higher cytotoxicity compared to the drug alone. Additionally, we observed that ultrasound exposure increased the penetration of the drug into the tumor tissue, leading to improved treatment outcomes. These findings are consistent with previous research on ultrasound-enhanced drug delivery, which has shown that ultrasound can improve the uptake of drugs by cancer cells and increase their local concentration, leading to improved therapeutic outcomes.[1632] Furthermore, the use of SWCNTs as a drug carrier is effective in enhancing drug delivery to tumors due to their high surface area and ability to cross cell membranes.[83640]

In conclusion, the combination of ultrasound and SWCNT-encapsulated Dacarbazine represents a promising new avenue for improving cancer treatment, offering a potential solution to overcome the limitations of conventional chemotherapy and enhance therapeutic efficacy. By enhancing drug delivery to tumors and minimizing systemic toxicity, the potential impact on cancer therapy and patient outcomes is truly exciting! The next crucial step would indeed be executing rigorous clinical trials to ensure both the safety and efficacy of this innovative approach.
