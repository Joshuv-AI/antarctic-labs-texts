‘Never let your enemy see you broken’: cultural trauma script elements in the Republic of Georgia – an international consortium study
Authors: Ana Papava, Jana D Javakhishvili, Nino Makhashvili, Keti Mamuchashvili, David J Eberle, Rahel Bachem, Celestin Mutuyimana, Andreas Maercker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Cultural trauma scripts are a new concept describing a sequential pattern of trauma reactions within a specific cultural context. The current study is a part of an international Network Project and explores cultural trauma scripts in Georgia, a country heavily affected by collective traumatic experiences stemming from a colonial past and armed conflicts.Objective: We aimed to identify elements of Georgian cultural trauma scripts, examine their associations within a temporal network, determine the cultural values linked to trauma reactions, and develop a substantially revised Georgian version of the Cultural Scripts of Trauma Inventory (CSTI).Method: Six focus groups were conducted: two with trauma experts— clinicians (N = 7) and four with survivors (N = 11) referred by clinicians. Data were analysed using qualitative content analysis.Results: Analysis revealed interconnected elements that create nodes within Georgian trauma scripts. One node follows a progressive sequence: self-deprecation, perceived non-acceptance, shame/guilt, pain concealment, isolation, with additional connections to maintaining façades of normality, deteriorating physical health, and caring for others. Another node centres on hypervigilance, mistrust, and avoidance. Georgian cultural values emphasize togetherness (‘the strength is in togetherness’), protection of dignity and reputation (‘never let your enemy see you broken’), and prioritizing others’ needs over one's own. These findings informed the development of the 68-item Georgian CSTI.Conclusion: Georgian trauma survivors follow culturally specific scripts where pain concealment, driven by shame and the feeling of non-acceptance, creates paradoxical isolation despite collectivistic values. Clinically, silence about suffering reflects adaptive cultural values rather than symptom absence. The Georgian CSTI provides a culturally grounded tool.KEYWORDS: Trauma, cultural trauma scripts, value orientations, pTSD, C-PTSD, Georgian culture

Introduction

Georgia is a former Soviet country with a history of Russian colonisation since the eighteenth Century and living under totalitarian rule for 70 years during the Soviet period, when 10% of the country's population was severely repressed (sent to exile in Siberia and/or killed). Research indicates that these experiences have led to transgenerational trauma-related responses at individual and collective levels (Javakhishvili, 2014, 2018; Skhirtladze et al., 2016). In recent years, Georgia has experienced several armed conflicts. Currently, 20% of the country’s territory is under Russian occupation, and 8% of the population has been forcibly displaced (UNHCR, 2024). The research conducted among internally displaced persons (IDPs) in Georgia shows that 23.3% meet criteria for Post-Traumatic Stress Disorders (PTSD), 14.0% for depressive disorders, and 10.4% for anxiety disorders (Makhashvili et al., 2014). The recent study comparing the mental health of internally displaced persons (IDPs) with that of the local Georgian population (Kovess-Masfety & Makhashvili, 2025) found that IDPs bear a substantially greater mental health burden. They experience higher levels of psychological distress and anxiety-related disorders and remain at significantly elevated risk, compared to non-IDPs, for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) with functional impairment (OR = 12.12), anxiety disorders (OR = 3.38), and panic-related symptoms (OR = 1.72). However, the study also reported comparable prevalence rates of depressive disorders and substance use disorders across the two populations, alongside a notably lower prevalence of suicidal ideation among IDPs relative to non-IDPs.

In Georgian mental healthcare, diagnoses, including stress-related disorders, such as PTSD and Complex PTSD (C-PTSD), are typically based on either the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) by the World Health Organisation or the Diagnostic (WHO, 2019) and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) by the American Psychiatric Association (APA, 2013). Both nosological systems are primarily rooted in Western cultural frameworks. Yet research shows that cultural factors influence both the prevalence and expression of PTSD and C-PTSD (Burri & Maercker, 2014; Perilla et al., 2002; Mearcker et al., 2019). As a result, individuals may exhibit posttraumatic symptoms that are not described in contemporary classifications, which could lead to misdiagnosis and inappropriate interventions.

Different cultures exhibit distinct modes of expressing distress, and cultural variations in trauma expression are receiving increasing attention within the global traumatic stress community, including initiatives such as the Global Collaboration on Traumatic Stress project Matrix. In some cultural contexts, distress may be primarily communicated through somatic symptoms, whereas in others, emotional or cognitive manifestations are more prominently emphasized. For example, intrusive thoughts and memories are common across cultures, but how they are interpreted and emotionally processed can vary substantially depending on cultural norms, values, and explanatory frameworks (Vera et al., 2019). In certain communities, distressing dreams or specific avoidant behaviours may occur more frequently or carry different meanings (Bovey et al., 2025). Cognitive appraisals also differ between cultures (Jobson et al., 2024), and idioms and metaphors used to describe trauma vary across cultures (Bovey et al., 2025; Meili & Maercker, 2019; Rechsteiner et al., 2019, 2020). A set of clinical symptoms may be considered typical or normative within a specific society or culture, even though they are included in formal diagnostic criteria (Chentsova-Dutton et al., 2014). Certain psychological reactions are interpreted differently depending on a group's historical background and cultural characteristics (Bachem et al., 2024; Mutuyimana & Maercker, 2024).

To advance understanding of trauma within specific cultural contexts, the concept of Cultural Trauma Scripts has been introduced (Chentsova-Dutton & Maercker, 2019). This framework conceptualizes trauma responses as culturally shaped patterns. It encompasses characteristic sequences of reactions, such as behaviours, cognitions, memories, attentional processes, bodily sensations, sleep disturbances, emotional experiences, motivation, coping strategies, and ritualized practices – all of which are relevant to post-traumatic adaptation (Maercker et al., 2024).

Values, shaped by cultural norms and historical experiences, play a central role in guiding how individuals interpret events, express emotions, and respond to psychological distress within their specific sociocultural environments. For instance, cultural values, such as collectivism versus individualism, profoundly influence how people perceive and experience mental health issues, and guide whether and how they seek help (Tse & Ng, 2014; Heim et al., 2019; Schwartz et al., 2012).

Despite this, there is still a lack of research that specifically identifies the cultural factors shaping the development and expression of post-traumatic stress. Gaining such insights would enhance our understanding of culturally specific mental health responses and support the integration of these considerations into the therapeutic approaches for stress-related disorders.

Building on this background, the research aims to identify elements of cultural trauma scripts in Georgia, to examine associations between these elements, to determine cultural values linked to trauma reactions, and to contribute to the development of a substantially revised Georgian version of the Cultural Scripts of Trauma Inventory (CSTI), which systematically records culture-specific trauma reactions.

Methods

This qualitative study was conducted within a larger Network Project coordinated by the University of Zurich (Eberle et al., 2024). The study design involved focus groups (FGs) with trauma survivors and trauma experts, using a semi-structured interview guide containing open-ended questions addressing emotional responses, changes in worldview, motivation, cognitive processes, behavioural patterns, interpersonal consequences, bodily reactions, and post-traumatic growth. It also included items exploring cultural value orientations and whether these changes could be linked to Georgian cultural values (see the interview guides as S4 and S5 in supplementary materials). Participants were invited to describe the traumatic reaction sequence, including how emotions, thoughts, behaviours, and experiences were interrelated and unfolded over time.

The interview guide was based on the preliminary Cultural Scripts of Trauma Inventory (Pre-CSTI), developed and validated in earlier Network Project work with Swiss 101 individuals (Eberle et al., 2024). The team at the University of Zurich developed the Pre-CSTI, based on theoretical and empirical work on cultural trauma scripts. The Pre-CSTI contains 63 items, and it was validated on Swiss participants with traumatic experience (n = 101). Findings showed a high internal consistency for the total score (α = .95) as well as for the subscales (worldviews (α = .84)); body-related phenomena (α = .87); cognitions and affects (α = .87); interpersonal consequences (α = .84); embitterment (α = .85); and growth (α = .85). These preliminary findings indicate adequate internal consistency of the Pre-CSTI, supporting its use as a starting point for cultural adaptation in the present study. Both the Pre-CSTI and the interview guide were translated into Georgian and underwent linguistic and cultural adaptation by the Georgian study team. Each FG session lasted approximately 1.5–2 h, allowing for in-depth discussion.

Data collection/procedure

Data were collected between May 2023 and May 2024. Six focus groups were implemented: two with trauma experts and four with trauma survivors. All sessions were conducted face-to-face, except for one held online via Zoom. The FG topic is sensitive or potentially retraumatizing; therefore, to establish a trustworthy and psychologically safe environment, focus groups were conducted in small groups of 2–3 participants within a private and comfortable setting at the university. The sessions were facilitated by the first author (AP), a clinical psychologist experienced in working with trauma. She actively monitored participants’ emotional states and was prepared to provide immediate support and stabilisation in case of distress.

After conducting 6 focus groups, no new themes or codes emerged in subsequent sessions, and participants’ responses became increasingly repetitive. This consistency across groups indicated that saturation had been achieved, and further data collection was unlikely to provide additional insights.

Reflexivity and positionality

The research team's professional background warrants acknowledgement, as it may have influenced both data collection and interpretation. All team members have clinical and research expertise in psychotraumatology, which informed study design and facilitated rapport with participants, though this shared professional lens may also have introduced interpretive bias. Regarding positionality, the Georgian team members occupied a cultural insider position, sharing linguistic, historical, and sociocultural context with participants. This facilitated interpretive depth but carried the risk of normalizing culturally embedded assumptions. The Swiss team, as cultural outsiders, coordinated research implementation and conducted a thorough critical review of the manuscript. To manage potential bias, reflexivity strategies were employed throughout analysis, including regular reflective discussions within the Georgian team to critically examine emerging interpretations and peer debriefing to minimize confirmatory bias.

Sample

Overall, 18 adults participated in focus groups. They were recruited through psychosocial and mental health services in Georgia, including Club Synergy (a trauma-informed, transdiagnostic service for youth), the Georgian Centre for Psychosocial and Medical Rehabilitation of Torture Victims (GCRT) (a trauma-focused service for torture survivors and their family members), and Solatium (a trauma-focused service for IDPs). The sampling approach was convenient.

Survivor groups included 11 Georgian adults (8 women, 3 men) with varied trauma exposure: war-related (n = 5), domestic violence, accidents, or traumatic loss. Expert groups included 7 Georgian clinicians. Further details are presented in Table 1.

Data analysis

Two independent coders (AP and KM) analysed verbatim transcripts using qualitative content analysis (Kuckartz & Rädiker, 2023) in MAXQDA 2024 under the supervision of two senior researchers (JJ and NM), following a hierarchical coding framework.

First, coders independently applied initial codes, then compared and merged them into consolidated categories. Second, thematic codes were developed to capture key components of Georgian Cultural Trauma Scripts, identifying co-occurring codes, sequential relationships, and structural frameworks. Third, cultural values associated with trauma were coded and integrated into the thematic structure, and a temporal network was constructed illustrating how script elements clustered and sequenced over time. Finally, the Pre-CSTI was refined by removing uncoded items and incorporating emergent items (mentioned by ≥2 participants). The first author compiled all items to produce the Georgian CSTI.

Ethical consideration

The Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Arts and Sciences at Ilia State University approved the study. The participant signed a consent form prior to data collection.

Categories of elements

Affects and cognitions

Affects and cognitions, along with interpersonal relationships, were the most prevalent categories in Georgian trauma scripts.

Self-deprecation: Survivors described a diminished sense of self, which often initiated the causal-temporal scripts of trauma. Self-deprecation included feelings of being unlikable or unattractive due to suffering (item 1). Many felt they were not good enough, lost confidence in their abilities (item 2), viewed themselves as unsuccessful (item 3), and sometimes expressed self-disgust (item 4 [P]).

Feeling Lost: Many respondents described identity crisis-related confusion and a deep uncertainty about one’s identity and life meaning (items 12 [P], 13, 14). Participants linked this to feelings of being frozen, hiding pain, and/or dysfunctional relationships.

Helplessness: Helplessness was described as a perceived lack of ability or power to influence one’s circumstances or take meaningful action in daily life (item 21).

Grief: Grief was reported following the loss of someone or something significant, such as the destruction of one’s home during armed conflict and was frequently linked to embitterment. Georgian IDPs described losing a household or land as nearly as devastating as losing a loved one (item 20).

Embitterment: Embitterment was expressed that everything is unfair and that nothing could compensate for the endured pain. Survivors described feeling oppressed, mistreated, and experiencing intense pain and anger about injustice (item 41 [P], 42).

Anger: Anger emerged as a recurring emotional response, often linked to experiences of violated dignity, injustice, or prolonged suffering (item 10).

Feeling of Worthlessness: Feelings of worthlessness were primarily noted by experts, who explained that traumatized individuals experiencing profound shame or injustice often perceive their dignity as compromised, contributing to a diminished self-worth (item 11).

Shame: Shame was described by some survivors in relation to the traumatic event itself, while others reported shame associated with having mental health difficulties (item 6 [P]).

Guilt: Guilt emerged when survivors appraised the traumatic event as being their fault, resulting in persistent self-blame. Participants also discussed stigma – both external and internal – as closely connected to experiences of shame and guilt (item 7 [P]).

Decreased Mood and Motivation: Survivors frequently described decreased mood and a profound loss of motivation (item 19), which were closely intertwined, with many reporting extended periods lacking energy or desire for daily activities (item 18).

Fear: Fear or a sense of threat was a central element, interconnected with other elements forming one of the main network nodes. Participants described various fears: death (item 9), being alone, rejection (item 36), and the future (item 5). Anxiety was also mentioned (item 8).

Worldviews

The World is Dangerous: Participants reported worldview shifts from perceiving the world as safe and benevolent to viewing it as unsafe, dangerous (item 22 [P]).

The World is Unfair: Participants frequently described their experiences as fundamentally unfair, emphasizing the moral wrongness of what had happened and its enduring impact (item 23 [P]).

Behaviour

Maintaining the Façade of Normal Life: Survivors actively presented themselves as functioning ‘normally’ despite significant internal distress, maintaining routines and appearing unaffected (item 50). They pushed themselves to work and remain productive (item 51), exerting greater effort to complete tasks that once felt effortless despite substantially decreased functional capacity (item 52). Some participants also reported that they maintain relationships, even though they felt uncomfortable, in order to preserve a facade of normalcy (item 34). This intentional self-regulation prevented distress from becoming visible to others, upholding normality while feeling internally overwhelmed.

Avoidance: Avoidance emerged frequently and was most often linked to trauma reminders. Participants reported avoiding thoughts, emotions, places, people, or situations that triggered distress (item 49). Some described that avoidance is related to substance use or addiction (item 56 [P]).

Interpersonal relationships

The feeling of Nonacceptance from Others: Not being accepted by others emerged as a significant issue. Although Georgian participants did not explicitly use the term rejection, they described situations that closely resembled it. Many explained that, after the traumatic event and the subsequent changes in their emotional and behavioural patterns, friends and loved ones seemed unable to accept their new reality, contributing to feeling like an outcast from society and making social acceptance difficult (item 25).

Concealing Pain: Concealing emotional distress was described as a central Georgian script reaction. Survivors frequently withheld emotions and thoughts even from those closest family and friends (items 28, 29 [P], 30). This tendency was closely linked with shame, feelings of nonacceptance from others, and the wish to maintain a façade of normality. In Georgian, the term describing this experience translates as ‘to be locked in’ or ‘shut down’ – an expression participants repeatedly used.

Isolation: The sustained effort to hide emotional pain often resulted in increased isolation (item 27 [P]). Participants described becoming more cautious in relationships, approaching social interactions with heightened sensitivity. For some, this manifested as withdrawal from close relationships (items 32, 33) and a preference for being alone (item 31). Others responded oppositely – remaining in relationships at any cost due to a fear of being alone, even when relationships became dysfunctional (item 35). Some participants reported that their relationships in the family were disrupted and became more chaotic (item 25 [P]).

Mistrust: Mistrust emerged as a prominent element associated with isolation and heightened threat perception, triggered when situations or people reminded survivors of past harm (item 24 [P]). This made them increasingly cautious and suspicious in interpersonal relationships.

Caring for Others: Caring for others was described as a key reason for concealing emotional pain (item 37). Survivors believed withholding suffering protected loved ones – particularly family members – from additional worry or distress (item 40). Beyond this protective intention, some noted that caring for others also helped them regain control or purpose after trauma, increasing motivation. In some cases, caring for others evolved into self-sacrifice, with survivors taking on increasing responsibilities (item 38) and focusing on others’ needs while neglecting their own (item 39).

Body-Related changes – worsening of physical health

Stress-Related Reactions: Participants reported experiencing stress-related reactions, including dizziness, palpitations, shortness of breath (item 53 [P]), and pervasive bodily tension (item 63).

Pain and Discomfort: They also reported a range of pains and discomfort, e.g. heartache, headaches, back and stomach pain, limb discomfort (items 54, 57, 58, 59 [P], 61), with stomachache being the most frequent and commonly associated with perceived threat, tension, or hypervigilance. A few mentioned a ‘lump in the throat’ sensation that hindered swallowing and speaking (item 60), and an unexplained burning sensation across the skin (item 64). Numbness, particularly in the face and hands, was frequently mentioned, as were episodes of bodily ‘freezing’ sometimes accompanied by dissociative experiences (item 62, 66).

Exhaustion: Several participants described marked exhaustion and physical weakness (item 55).

Sleep Disturbances: Sleep disturbances were widespread, including difficulties initiating or maintaining sleep and recurrent nightmares (item 65).

Worsening of physical health: Participants also linked persistent distress to physical health deterioration, including chronic conditions such as cardiovascular and endocrine disorders. Concealing physical pain was described as parallel to suppressing emotions, contributing to delayed help-seeking and worsening health. Clinicians noted that Georgian trauma survivors tend not to attend to bodily sensations and typically seek medical care only when symptoms become intolerable or significantly impair functioning. In most other cases, physical sensations were ignored or not fully identified (items 67, 68). While some accounts suggested experiences that may resemble dissociative processes, such as emotional detachment from bodily sensations, these were not consistently represented across the data and are therefore not interpreted here as a central mechanism linking psychological distress to physical health deterioration.

Post-Traumatic growth

Empowerment: Empowerment emerged as a process of discovering inner strength and acquiring new skills following trauma. This newfound strength, often stemming from the traumatic event itself, helps confront and manage subsequent symptoms. Some participants linked this self-discovery and skill acquisition to the period immediately after isolation (items 44, 46, 47 [P], 48).

Appreciation and Meaning of Life: They also developed a greater appreciation for their own life (item 45 [P]). For some, the new meaning or life that they found following a traumatic experience served as a source of motivation (item 48). This meaning in life motivated them to continue living and functioning.

Becoming more Empathetic: After overcoming traumatic symptoms, survivors reported a better understanding and acceptance of themselves. This newfound self-awareness has made them more empathetic toward others and more focused on human connections (item 43).

Georgian cultural scripts of trauma inventory (GCSTI)

The present study represents a cultural adaptation and substantial revision of the Preliminary Cultural Scripts of Trauma Inventory (Pre-CSTI). Based on findings of the focus group discussions in Georgia, some items were excluded from Pre-CSTI that were not culturally relevant (not mentioned or acknowledged in the FG discussions by participants), and some new items were peer-reviewed, approved by the supervisors’ team and added to the questionnaire. The resulting 68 items are organized by category in Supplementary Table 1. Of 68 items, 45 were newly generated, while 23 derived from Pre-CSTI, reflecting substantial cultural specificity. This qualitative stage thus produced the first Georgian version of CSTI. Importantly, this study represents an initial, exploratory research aimed at comprehensive item generation. Future research will focus on validation and allow psychometric reduction to yield a shorter, validated scale.

Sequelae of Georgian trauma script

The associative network of Georgian trauma scripts was created by linking elements that participants mentioned together. The frequency with which these elements were co-mentioned determines the central components of the script. These salient components were prioritized in the formulation of CSTI items (see Table S3 – GCSTI Items and Network Elements in the supplementary materials). The nodes represent interconnected elements with a temporal or causal sequence. Figure 1 illustrates the connection counting methodology. Two nodes are described in the text.

The conceal pain node

The first is a script that begins with self-deprecation and diminishing thoughts, such as I am not likeable, which lead to a feeling of non-acceptance from others. This then results in shame and guilt, which progress into hiding pain and becoming isolated. Thus, individuals tend not to share their emotions and thoughts, ultimately leading to isolation. People often create distance from close friends or others, sometimes even ending relationships.

Additional elements interconnect with pain concealment. First, survivors maintain facades of normal life, striving to appear unaffected and continue normal activities without drawing attention to suffering. Second, pain concealment relates to deteriorating physical health. Third, it connects to caring for others, where survivors redirect focus outward to mask their internal distress.

The sense of threat node

Fear or a sense of threat forms this node's core element, directly connecting to pain concealment – when experiencing fear, individuals conceal this emotion. Self-deprecation and fear are bidirectionally linked within the network. The threat response connects to panic, hypervigilance, mistrust and notably, avoidance behaviours. Avoidance manifests in multiple forms: evading trauma reminders, withdrawing from people, and, in some cases, using substances (particularly alcohol) as an avoidance strategy.

Value orientation

This section presents cultural values articulated by participants in relation to their post-traumatic reactions. Participants frequently employed proverbs to illustrate their value orientations – a characteristic feature of their narratives.

‘Never let your enemy see you broken’ – this proverb was mentioned multiple times in the FG-s as related to trauma reactions. Respondents explained that they must be strong and not show their weakness, not only to the enemy but also to others. Participants identified themselves with fighters, and because of that identity, they urged themselves to be strong and fight till the end.

‘Strength is in togetherness’ – this proverb prompted participants to highlight the significance of having people around and not being alone.

To care for others and to live their life – Participants mentioned that this is a very Georgian trait to care for others, but sometimes it means constantly meeting others’ needs and neglecting yourself instead.

Support and benevolence in the face of a real and immediate threat – Participants mentioned that they felt supported by one another during the current danger, such as during the war, when they had to defend themselves. They were highly attentive and caring towards each other.

Respondents mentioned that they change their values after a traumatic event. Mostly, participants described changes in values related to interpersonal relationships; for example, some survivors mentioned that they became more self-focused.

Discussion

This qualitative study, based on focus groups with trauma survivors and expert clinicians, enabled the distinction of Georgian trauma reactions and corresponding cultural script categories. In total, seven categories are distinguished, with the most prominent being Affects and Cognitions and Interpersonal Consequences. Additionally, categories include Behaviour, Body-related issues, and Growth. Although all these trauma reactions or elements are situated within the causal-temporal network, Figure 1 illustrates which reactions seem to be central to Georgian culture and how they are interconnected. Two nodes emerged as central. The larger node centres around concealing pain, which is closely linked to other script elements, such as feelings of non-acceptance, shame, maintaining a facade of normal life, deteriorating physical health, caring for others, and isolation. The second significant node of reactions centres around the core reaction – fear or a sense of threat, associated with self-deprecation, isolation, hypervigilance, panic, and avoidance. These findings allowed us to produce a preliminary Georgian version of CSTI.

Cross-Cultural positioning

The Georgian cultural scripts of trauma study form part of the broader Trauma Scripts Network, which spans multiple cultural contexts including Switzerland, East Africa, and Georgia. Findings across these sites suggest that while certain trauma-related elements appear across cultures, their relative importance, cultural meaning, and interconnections differ substantially, revealing culture-specific patterns that cannot be captured by universal diagnostic frameworks alone (Maercker & Mutuyimana, 2026), that is, revealing distinct patterns shaped by underlying cultural values. Swiss scripts, for instance, reflect individualistic values centred on functioning and performance, with key elements including self-deprecation, the urge to overcompensate, and the silent endurance of suffering (Bachem et al., 2024). East African scripts, by contrast, are anchored in collective values such as community reputation, with bitterness, mistrust, pain, and a need for care emerging as central features (Mutuyimana & Maercker, 2023). Georgian trauma scripts similarly reflect a collectivistic cultural logic, yet with a distinctive tension: rather than seeking communal support, individuals appear to turn inward – concealing pain, maintaining a façade of normality, and withdrawing into isolation – suggesting that the imperative to protect others and preserve social harmony may paradoxically maintain or overcompensate private suffering. Additional Georgian elements include feelings of non-acceptance, shame, mistrust, and deteriorating physical health, together pointing to a pattern in which distress is managed through concealment rather than expression.

Certain elements appear across all three studies, but their centrality and associations differ. For example, concealment of suffering in Georgian culture links to fears of social rejection and shame, or protecting loved ones from worry. In Swiss culture, it relates to maintaining functionality and overcompensation (Bachem et al., 2024). In East African cultures, hiding sadness connects to preserving community reputation and limited family harmony (Mutuyimana & Maercker, 2023). Notably, research on third-generation descendants of repressed Georgian families shows high mistrust and avoidance of unfamiliar people (Javakhishvili, 2018), aligning with present findings.

The broader patterns of similarity and difference across these cultural scripts will be explored more fully in the next stage of the multisite study.

Pain concealment node and collectivistic values

Georgian culture is primarily collectivistic (Hofstede, 1980; Schwarz, 1990), with self-identity strongly linked to community and family through close kinship ties and nuclear family structures (Nijaradze, 2001; Surmanidze, 2000, 2001; Tsuladze, 2003). Consistent with this collectivistic orientation, the largest node identified in the study – concealment of pain – belongs to the social relationships domain. This cultural script reflects values captured in Georgian proverbs: ‘Never let your enemy see you broken’ (მტერს დაცემული არ უნდა ეჩვენო; mters dacemuli ar unda echveno) and ‘You must be well to upset your enemy’ (მტრის ჯინაზე კარგად უნდა იყო; mtris jinaze kargad unda iko), where pain is viewed as weakness to be hidden. This script likely developed from Georgia's history of recurring attacks from neighbouring countries, where maintaining dignity by concealing vulnerabilities became essential for preserving national and individual reputation (Surmanidze & Tsuladze, 2008). The posttraumatic response of concealing pain thus aligns with deeply embedded cultural values about protecting one's image from adversaries, paralleling the concept of ‘face’ in Chinese culture, where individuals avoid humiliation to protect personal and social reputation (Zane & Yeh, 2002).

Three interconnected cultural values reinforce pain concealment within this node.

First, shame plays a central role in Georgian social relationships, closely linked to pride and self-dignity (Surmanidze & Tsuladze, 2008). The Georgian concept of dignity, ‘tavmokvareoba’ (თავმოყვარეობა), literally means ‘self-love’ or ‘self-respect’ though it refers not to self-care or arrogance, but as a multidimensional construct encompassing self-appreciation, pride, and the demonstration of strength. Displaying shameful behaviour indicates one lacks ‘tavmokvareoba’; such a person is ‘tavmochrili’ (თავმოჭრილი) – ‘one's head is cut off.’ Concealing pain thus preserves self-dignity and allows continued ‘self-love.’ Second, fear of rejection motivates hiding pain to maintain social connections. In collectivistic culture, togetherness signifies strength: ‘the strength is in togetherness’ (dzala ertbashia; ძალა ერთობაშია), while being alone represents weakness and poverty: ‘The lonely man is poor even in eating’ (marto kacia tchamashiac codvaa; მარტო კაცი ჭამაშიაც ცოდვაა). Fearing abandonment, individuals avoid expressing emotions that others might stigmatize***. Third, caring for close ones motivates concealment, consistent with previous Georgian studies (Makhashvili et al., 2021; Shekriladze et al., 2021). This caring can become overcompensation, where survivors manage others’ lives while becoming self-alienated.

These pain-concealment strategies create paradoxical outcomes: survivors may isolate themselves, ultimately experiencing the feared aloneness that is stigmatized in Georgian culture, or remain in dysfunctional relationships, disregarding their own feelings to preserve connections. Interestingly, some participants reported growth in solitude, developing self-understanding and adopting more individualistic values.

Fear and threat node

The second prominent node centres on fear and threat, interconnected with self-deprecation, isolation, hypervigilance, panic, and avoidance. This cluster aligns with core (C)PTSD symptoms (ICD-11): persistent threat, anxiety, lack of safety, and difficulties trusting their environment. Mistrust emerged as a key element, manifesting as caution, suspicion or social isolation. This response may reflect Georgia's history of wars and repressions. Research demonstrates that greater traumatic exposure correlates with lower trust levels (Bell et al., 2019), civil war negatively impacts social trust (Kijewski & Freitag, 2018), and individuals tend to mistrust those connected to their trauma (al Ajlan, 2023). Thus, mistrust in the Georgian cultural trauma script likely reflects both individual trauma exposure and collective historical trauma, where chronic vigilance became adaptive for survival across generations (Javakhishvili, 2018).

This study represents the first qualitative exploration of cultural trauma scripts in Georgia, with a methodologically strong sample providing dual perspectives from trauma survivors and expert clinicians.

Future research directions

The qualitative study was the first phase of developing and validating the Georgian CSTI. The next phase involves quantitative validation and examining how cultural trauma scripts relate to PTSD, C-PTSD, depression and anxiety symptoms.

Based on the cultural presentations of suffering, it is valuable to, e.g. examine the broader topic of mistrust, considering the scarcity of research in this field, and understanding the concept of the ‘enemy’ in Georgian culture.

Conclusion

The study identified key elements of Georgian trauma scripts, which seem to form at least two distinct nodes in a qualitative network analysis: pain concealment rooted in collectivistic values of shame, social acceptance, and care for others; and fear/threat connected to mistrust, hypervigilance, and avoidance. These cultural scripts reflect Georgia's unique intersection of collectivistic orientation and historical trauma exposure.

The Georgian trauma reactions do not fully align with (C)PTSD diagnostic criteria (Hyland et al., 2021). However, the core symptoms of the Georgian trauma scripts network are difficulties in interpersonal relationships, which are also symptoms of C-PTSD. These findings have direct clinical implications: therapeutic interventions for Georgian trauma survivors should address internalized stigma, culturally shaped cognitions about pain concealment, and the tension between collectivistic values and trauma-induced isolation. Recognizing that silence about suffering reflects adaptive cultural values rather than symptom absence may improve therapeutic alliance and treatment outcomes.

More broadly, this study demonstrates that culturally adapted trauma assessment and intervention strategies may be more effective than universally applied approaches, supporting the continued development of culturally specific instruments like the Georgian CSTI.
