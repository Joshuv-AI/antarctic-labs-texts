Clinical and Radiographic Outcomes Following Complex Spine Reconstruction in Neurofibromatosis with Severe Spinal Deformity
Authors: Kwadwo Poku Yankey, Derrick Nyantakyi Owusu, Arthur Sackeyfio, Francis Addai, Irene Wulff, Liudmila Gross-Fenandez, Oheneba Boachie-Adjei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction:Neurofibromatosis (NF) associated spine deformity is uncommon and can be debilitating to patients. Surgery for deformity correction is associated with improved neurological status and quality of life, however, the surgical correction of the spinal deformity can be challenging can be and associated with high complication rates. We present a series of NF patients with curves >100° or treated with a vertebral column resections (VCR).Hypothesis:Spine deformity correction surgery in NF patients with curves >100° or indicated for VCR have higher rates of postsurgical complications.Materials and Methods:Twelve consecutive NF patients from a multi-centre study group who underwent complex spine reconstruction as a result of NF associated deformity (>100° or treated by VCR) were enrolled in the study. Analysis included demographic, clinical, pr-operative, intraoperative, and postoperative complications. scoliosis research society (SRS)-22 scores were used to compare pre-and 2-years postoperative functional outcomes.Results:Mean age and body mass index were 14 ± 2.9 and 19.8 ± 4.7 kg/m², respectively. Of the twelve patients enrolled, six patients had severe kyphoscoliosis, two patients presented with kyphosis, and four with scoliosis. Halo-gravity traction was used in 75% (9/12) of patients. Mean preoperative coronal and sagittal Cobb angles were 99.7 ± 22.5 and 99.1 ± 35.4, respectively. Seven patients had VCR and five had posterior column osteotomies. At 2-years postoperatively, mean coronal and sagittal Cobb angles were 44.9 ± 17.5 and 62.7 ± 21.7, respectively. Two patients with preoperative motor deficits improved at 1 year follow-up and 1 (8.3%) patient had new neurologic deficit postoperatively but recovered within a year follow-up. About 16.70% (2/12) had post-op pulmonary complications (atelectasis and pleural effusion) and 16.7% (2/12) patients required a revision surgery for implant related complications. There were no pseudarthrosis, visual complications (blindness) or deaths in the study cohort. health related quality of life measures, including SRS-22 scores demonstrated significant improvement at 2-years follow-up.Conclusion:Complex reconstruction resulted in significant curve improvement in both coronal and sagittal planes in NF-associated spine deformity. There were low of postsurgical complications. SRS outcomes improved in all patients after 2 year f/u.

Introduction

Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF-1) is an autosomal dominant genetic disorder. The worldwide incidence is approximately 1:2500–3000. Scoliosis is the most common musculoskeletal disorder in NF-1 with an incidence of about 10%.[1] Spine deformity in NF-1 may be classified into dystrophic or non-dystrophic based on the presence of dystrophic features on radiographic imaging. Non-dystrophic NF-1 scoliosis is managed similarly to idiopathic scoliosis.[2,3] The mainstay of treatment for dystrophic deformity curves is surgery,[2,4] because these curves tend to progress rapidly. Surgery in these patients with multiple bony and soft tissue abnormalities as well as poor bone mineral density[5,6] is associated with high complications.

Patients with severe deformities over 100° or those requiring 3-column osteotomies (3CO), regardless of spine curve magnitudes, present a challenge to the treating surgeon. These patients may have preoperative nutritional deficiency, pulmonary compromise or neuro-deficits. Surgery in these patients is known to be associated with a high rate of complications.[7,8,9] In this cohort of patients with NF-1 and large curves exceeding 100° or treated with 3CO, the risk of surgery is significant.

To this end, our study sought to investigate the clinical and radiological outcomes in this group of high-risk patients following spinal reconstruction.

Materials and Methods

We reviewed 12 complex spine deformity patients with NF-1 who were enrolled in the FOX paediatric spine study database from 17 national and international sites. Inclusion criteria were patients aged 10 years + 0 months–20 years + 11 months at time of surgery and meeting at least one of these two under listed criteria: (1) had Cobb angle of ≥ 100° in either the coronal and/or sagittal plane and planned for a definitive instrumentation and fusion OR (2) scheduled for a vertebral column resections (VCR) irrespective of Cobb angle. We queried the data for gender, aetiology, curve type, comorbidities, neurological abnormalities, and depression.

Intraoperative parameters, such as duration of surgery, estimated blood loss (EBL), upper and lower instrumented vertebrae, number of vertebrae fused, and intraoperative complications were assessed. Furthermore, intraoperative spinal cord monitoring with somatosensory evoked potentials, transcranial motor evoked potential, or descending neurogenic evoked potentials was performed on all patients and intraoperative alerts recorded. Intraoperative neurologic monitoring change was not considered a complication if there was no postoperative neurological deficit. Follow-up neurological data were obtained at first postoperative visit (from discharge up to 9 months postoperative), 1-year visit (10–21 months), and 2-year visit (>22 months).

Postoperative clinical and radiographic outcomes such as postoperative Cobb angles and complications were measured. Scoliosis research society (SRS)-22 questionnaire was used to evaluate the patient reported quality of life outcomes pre- and post-op. All patients included in the study had at least a 2-year follow-up.

The primary outcome was to investigate the rates of postoperative complications in patients with neurofibromatosis undergoing surgery for correction of severe spinal deformity. Data were initially processed in Microsoft excel but final analysis was done in STATA 16. Analysis involved mainly descriptive statistics with frequency tables and percentages. Paired t test was used to determine if there was statistically significant difference between preoperative and postoperative parameters. Statistical significance was pegged at 0.05 alpha level.

Results

Baseline, intraoperative, and postoperative parameters

A total of 12 spine deformity patients with NF-1 with Cobb angle greater than 100 degrees in either sagittal or coronal planes or indicated for VCR, with 2 years follow-up after spine arthrodesis were included in the study. The mean age was 14 ± 2.9 years with range of 11–20 years. Mean body mass index was 19.8 ± 4.7 kg/m². Deformity types realised in this study were kyphoscoliosis,[6] kyphosis,[2] and scoliosis.[4] Before surgery, nine patients underwent halo-gravity traction (HGT) for a mean duration of 52 days. For intraoperative parameters, the mean operation room time and EBL were 469 ± 176.5 min and 1291.6 ± 422.6 mL, respectively. Seven patients had VCR. The mean fusion level was 13 ± 1.30. Intraoperatively, the study realised one intraoperative neuro-deficits characterised by an incomplete bilateral lower extremity motor deficit with a sensory level at T6. Intraoperative neuromonitoring was utilised to detect neurophysiological signal changes. Postoperative complications were neuro-deficits (1), implant related complications (2), and pulmonary (2). Comparison of pre-op and post-op radiographic and quality of life outcomes

Overall, preoperative major coronal Cobb angle (96.9 ± 21.3) corrected to 44.9 ± 17.5 (≈54.4% correction) at 2-year follow-up (P < 0.001). Furthermore, preoperative sagittal Cobb angle also improved from 104.4 ± 32.0 to 62.7 ± 21.7 (≈37.2% correction) at 2-year follow-up (P < 0.001). The quality of life of patients significantly improved in all SRS-22 domains at 2-years: function (P < 0.0001), pain (P = 0.007), self-image (P = 0.012), mental health (P < 0.0001), satisfaction (P < 0.0001), and overall SRS-22 score (P < 0.0001). Discussion

Spine reconstruction in patients with NF-1 complex spine deformities is challenging. The risk of complications in surgery for patients with curves over 100 degrees or who undergo 3CO is high.[9,10] In our study of 12 patients with NF-1 spinal deformities, there were five reported complications representing a rate of 41.6%. Two patients had implant related complications, two patients had pulmonary complications, and one patient developed new neurologic deficit (NND). There were no reported cases of pseudoarthrosis and no deaths. In a review of 147 consecutive series of VCR for severe paediatric spinal deformity Lenke et al.[9] reported an overall complication rate of 59%.

Implant related complications are among the most common complications following surgical treatment of NF-1 spinal deformities.[1,11,12] Risk factors for implant related complications include age less than 9 years, kyphosis more than 50° and application of growing rods.[11]

The two implant related complications we had occurred in patients with dystrophic NF-1 kyphoscoliosis. These two patients had kyphosis angles of 76° and 80°. The abnormal anatomy in dystrophic NF-1 deformity makes screw placement and other anchors challenging. Li et al.,[13] in the review of 39 consecutive patients treated surgically for dystrophic lumbar scoliosis reported a screw malposition rate of 30.5% compared to 12.1% screw malposition rate in adolescent idiopathic scoliosis patients.[14]

Dystrophic features include scalloping of vertebrae, widening of spinal canal, hypoplastic or absent pedicles, spinning of transverse processes, laminae defect or hypoplasia.[2] Vertebral anomalies combined with osteopaenia render spinal instrumentation relatively weak.[2,15]

Some authors recommend the use of orthotics postoperatively, until fusion has occurred.[2,15]

The rate of pseudoarthrosis is reported to be as high as 40% in spine surgery for NF-1.[2] It is, therefore, important to adequately prepare fusion bed for bone grafting.[2,16] Augmentation of fusion should be considered at 6 months if there is a possibility of fusion failure.[2,17] None of our patients developed pseudoarthrosis. This is most likely due to optimal preparation of the fusion bed with adequate bone grafting.

Respiratory complications including pneumonia, atelectasis and pleural effusion were the most common early postoperative complications in review of 147 consecutive VCRs for severe paediatric spinal deformity by Lenke et al.[9] Respiratory complications made up 1% of major complications in review of 427 consecutive patients who underwent surgery for complex spinal deformity.[7]

In our study, 2/12 patients had pulmonary complications: one of them with pleural effusion requiring the insertion of a chest tube. 1/12 patients developed new neurologic deficit in our review. This was in a patient with significant thoracic kyphosis of 155° and who needed a VCR. Both of these factors have been reported as significant risk factors for the development of NND.[7,10,18] Preoperative HGT has shown promise in reducing surgical complications, particularly neurological issues, according to existing research.[10,18]. This study, however, did not specifically examine how HGT affected postsurgical outcomes. Although nine patients received preoperative HGT, which may have played a role in the observed Cobb angle improvements, the study design lacked the necessary measures to definitively determine HGT’s impact.

In a large series of 286 consecutive paediatric patients of mixed aetiology with curves over 100° or treated with VCR, NND occurred in 27 patients (9.4%). Twenty-one of the patients had obtained full neurologic recovery during a 2 year follow-up period.[10]

In our series, the one patient who developed NND together with the two patients who had neuro-deficits preoperatively regained normal neurological function at the 1-year postoperative review period. In the event of NND, Boachie-Adjei et al.[10] advocate maintaining haemoglobin above 10 g/dL; administration of methylprednisolone intraoperatively and postoperatively using the trauma protocol 35 mL/kg bolus followed by 5 mL/kg/h over 24 h. Neuro imaging with computed tomograpgy and/or magnetic resonance imaging scans to rule out impingement of neural elements or canal stenosis is necessary. In the absence of neural compromise, supportive treatment and waiting are advised.[10]

There was significant correction of deformity curves in both the sagittal and coronal planes at 2 years follow-up. Overall SRS-22 total scores and scores in all domains significantly improved at the 2-year follow-up period. There were no deaths in the study. This should encourage surgeons that in carefully planned surgeries for NF-1 with Curves greater than 100° and/or need VCR, the outcomes are good.

The limitation of the study includes the relatively small sample size. These patients with severe deformities are rare and this series represent the most severe patients. The follow-up of this study is 2 years. It is possible that the non-union may occur later.

In conclusion, NF-1 patients with severe spinal deformity can be safely treated with surgical deformity correction without significant complications.
