Association between renal replacement therapy and in-hospital mortality in patients with severe acute kidney injury: a retrospective cohort study based on MIMIC-IV database
Authors: Guanyuan Tian, Zhibin Lin, Zhanmao Chen, Zhanyi Qiu, Zheyan Zheng, Yuan Lin, Tietao Chen, Zhonghan Xu, Yubin Cai, Kefei Wu, Xiaoli Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSevere acute kidney injury (AKI) occurs frequently in intensive care unit (ICU) settings and correlates strongly with adverse patient outcomes. Whether renal replacement therapy (RRT) can effectively improve mortality outcomes in these patients remains controversial, primarily due to interference from confounding by indication. Within nephrology and critical-care practice, identifying which severe-AKI subgroups derive a survival benefit from RRT is a question of direct bedside relevance, and observational analyses with rigorous bias control are particularly informative when balanced randomised data remain limited.MethodsThis retrospective cohort analysis utilized the MIMIC-IV database (version 2.2). The study population comprised adult patients (age ≥ 18 years) diagnosed with KDIGO Stage 3 AKI during their first ICU admission. Vascular causes of AKI (renal-artery stenosis, renal infarction, abdominal-aortic-aneurysm associated ischaemia) and clearly post-obstructive AKI were excluded a priori, as these phenotypes carry distinct trajectories that may not respond to RRT in a comparable manner. Patients were stratified into treatment and control groups based on RRT receipt. The clinical indication for RRT was operationalised through a composite set of pre-specified criteria (refractory hyperkalaemia, severe metabolic acidosis, refractory volume overload, uraemic complications, and oligo-anuria) that were entered into the propensity-score model in addition to severity scores and laboratory derangement, in order to reduce residual operator-dependent variation in the initiation decision. To address baseline characteristic differences, propensity score matching (PSM) was conducted using 1:1 nearest neighbor matching with a caliper of 0.02. The primary endpoint was in-hospital all-cause mortality; secondary endpoints encompassed ICU mortality, 28-day mortality, ICU length of stay, total hospital length of stay, RRT-related complications (catheter-associated bloodstream infection, mechanical line complications, bleeding events, and intradialytic hypotension), and renal recovery. Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Pre-specified subgroup analyses also stratified by chronic-kidney-disease status, RRT modality, and the qualifying KDIGO Stage-3 criterion (creatinine, urine output, or both).ResultsA total of 5,847 patients with KDIGO Stage 3 AKI were enrolled, of whom 2,156 (37%) underwent RRT. Pre-matching analysis revealed substantially greater disease severity in the RRT group (SOFA score: 12.4 ± 4.2 vs. 8.6 ± 3.8, P < 0.001). Following 1:1 PSM, 1,842 matched pairs were established, with all covariate standardized mean differences (SMDs) below 0.1, indicating satisfactory matching. Within the matched cohort, in-hospital mortality was lower in the RRT group than in the controls (35% vs. 42%, P = 0.002), and the corresponding mortality risk was reduced (HR = 0.78, 95%CI: 0.68–0.89, P < 0.001). The direction and magnitude of the association were consistent across CKD versus non-CKD strata, across the qualifying KDIGO Stage-3 criterion, and across RRT modality, and RRT-related complications were observed but did not exceed rates previously reported in the literature. Subgroup analyses confirmed consistent findings of RRT regardless of sepsis status, mechanical ventilation use, age, or disease severity.ConclusionAfter the largest confounders for indication were controlled through propensity score matching, RRT was, at a minimum, non-inferior to non-RRT management with respect to in-hospital mortality, and the matched-cohort estimates were consistent with an association with reduced mortality, among patients with severe AKI. These findings should not be read as proof of a causal protective effect, but they support the position that, in appropriately selected critically ill patients with KDIGO Stage 3 AKI – particularly those with refractory hyperkalaemia, severe acidosis, oligo-anuria, or refractory volume overload – RRT should not be withheld on the basis of perceived futility.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) represents a relatively common yet serious complication among critically ill patients, affecting approximately 20–57% of ICU admissions globally [1–3]. The KDIGO guidelines published in 2012 established a standardized diagnostic framework for AKI, stratifying severity into three stages based on the magnitude of serum creatinine elevation and degree of urine output reduction [4, 5]. Stage 3 AKI denotes the most severe form of renal impairment, with associated mortality rates reaching 40–60% in critically ill populations [6, 7].

Stage 3 is itself a heterogeneous category, defined by any one of several criteria – a three-fold rise in serum creatinine, a creatinine ≥ 4.0 mg/dL with an acute increment, prolonged oliguria, anuria, or initiation of RRT – and the biological trajectory of patients meeting one criterion is not necessarily identical to that of patients meeting another, a heterogeneity that must be acknowledged when interpreting any treatment-effect estimate at this stage [8].

For patients with severe AKI, RRT continues to serve as a cornerstone therapeutic intervention, effectively correcting metabolic derangements, alleviating volume overload, and managing uremic complications [9, 10]. Nevertheless, considerable controversy persists regarding optimal timing for RRT initiation and patient selection criteria. Several landmark randomized controlled trials (RCTs), including the ELAIN study [11], AKIKI trial [12], IDEAL-ICU study [13], and the multinational STARRT-AKI trial [14], have yielded inconsistent and sometimes contradictory conclusions when investigating the mortality impact of early versus delayed RRT initiation strategies. More recent updates from the AKIKI-2 trial and from individual-patient-data meta-analyses have further suggested that aggressive early initiation does not confer a generalisable mortality benefit, and have moved the question from “when to start” toward “which patient profile benefits” [15, 16].

The central challenge in evaluating RRT efficacy using observational data lies in confounding by indication – patients receiving RRT typically exhibit more critical illness, higher SOFA scores, and more pronounced physiological derangement [17, 18]. A further, related challenge is that the decision to initiate RRT is partly operator-dependent: physician thresholds for starting dialysis vary across centres and even across providers within the same unit, so a binary RRT/non-RRT exposure flag is, on its own, an imperfect summary of the clinical reasoning behind that decision [19]. In the present analysis we therefore enriched the propensity-score model with the specific clinical indications that drove (or did not drive) RRT initiation, rather than relying solely on severity scores. This inherent selection bias gives rise to a paradoxical phenomenon: unadjusted crude analyses frequently demonstrate elevated mortality among RRT recipients, potentially obscuring the true therapeutic signal.

The MIMIC-IV database contains comprehensive clinical records from over 50,000 ICU admissions at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center spanning 2008 to 2019, representing one of the largest publicly accessible electronic health record datasets currently available [20, 21]. This data resource provides robust support for implementing sophisticated statistical methods to address confounding bias.

Propensity score matching (PSM) serves as an important tool for causal inference in observational research, constructing balanced comparison groups that simulate randomized trial conditions [22, 23]. By pairing RRT and non-RRT groups according to patients’ probability of receiving treatment, PSM reduces – although it does not eliminate – confounding factor influences, thereby yielding more reliable treatment effect estimates.

The present study aimed to investigate the relationship between RRT and in-hospital mortality among critically ill patients with KDIGO Stage 3 AKI using the MIMIC-IV database. Although nephrology is the natural disciplinary home of this question, severe AKI is a cross-specialty problem that intersects with urological practice through obstructive uropathy, post-operative urological AKI, and bedside vascular access for haemodialysis; framing the question in a way that is accessible to the urological readership is therefore appropriate, and the present paper is offered with this cross-disciplinary readership in mind. We hypothesized that following adequate adjustment for disease severity and baseline characteristics via PSM, RRT would demonstrate an association with non-inferior, and potentially improved, survival outcomes.

Materials and methods

Data source

Data for this retrospective cohort study were extracted from the MIMIC-IV database (version 2.2) [20]. This freely accessible database encompasses de-identified health information from patients admitted to ICUs at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) in Boston, Massachusetts, between 2008 and 2019. The dataset includes demographic data, vital sign monitoring, laboratory results, medication records, procedures, diagnostic codes, clinical notes, and outcome information. Researchers obtained database access through the PhysioNet platform following completion of required CITI training certification [24]. Given that the database contains only de-identified information, ethical review exemption was granted per the PhysioNet data use agreement.

Study population

Inclusion criteria specified adult patients (age ≥ 18 years) who developed KDIGO Stage 3 AKI during their first ICU admission. According to the 2012 KDIGO Clinical Practice Guideline [4], KDIGO Stage 3 AKI was diagnosed when any of the following criteria were met: (1) serum creatinine elevation to ≥ 3.0 times baseline; (2) serum creatinine ≥ 4.0 mg/dL (≥ 354 µmol/L) with acute increase ≥ 0.5 mg/dL (≥ 44 µmol/L); (3) urine output < 0.3 mL/kg/h for ≥ 24 h; (4) anuria persisting ≥ 12 h; or (5) initiation of renal replacement therapy. Baseline serum creatinine was defined as the lowest documented value within 365 days preceding admission. For patients lacking prior creatinine data, baseline creatinine was back-calculated using the MDRD formula assuming a baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 75 mL/min/1.73m2 [25].

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded if any of the following applied: (1) pre-existing diagnosis of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) or chronic kidney disease (CKD) Stage 5 on maintenance dialysis; (2) prior kidney transplantation; (3) hospital stay less than 24 h; (4) age exceeding 89 years due to data de-identification requirements; (5) baseline variable missing rate ≥ 20%; (6) pregnancy; (7) admission primarily for palliative care or comfort measures; (8) a primary or contributory AKI aetiology that was vascular in nature (renal-artery stenosis with acute decompensation, renal infarction, abdominal-aortic-aneurysm-associated renal ischaemia, or aortic-dissection-related renal hypoperfusion); or (9) clearly post-obstructive AKI (bilateral ureteric obstruction, complete bladder outlet obstruction, or other unrelieved urological obstruction). These last two categories were excluded a priori because they represent non-nephron-centred injuries whose primary management is mechanical (revascularisation or de-obstruction), and the clinical question of whether RRT confers a mortality benefit is not directly addressed in these patients. A total of 184 patients meeting criterion (8) and 226 patients meeting criterion (9) were therefore not enrolled in the analytic cohort.

Exposure definition

The primary exposure was defined as RRT receipt during the index ICU admission. RRT utilization was identified through procedure codes and treatment records in the MIMIC-IV database, encompassing continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT, including continuous venovenous hemofiltration [CVVH], continuous venovenous hemodialysis [CVVHD], and continuous venovenous hemodiafiltration [CVVHDF]), intermittent hemodialysis (IHD), and sustained low-efficiency dialysis (SLED). Patients receiving any RRT modality were assigned to the RRT group, while those not receiving RRT comprised the non-RRT control group. Both modality (CRRT versus IHD/SLED) and the recorded clinical indication driving RRT initiation were captured. The pre-specified clinical indications were: (i) refractory hyperkalaemia (serum potassium > 6.0 mEq/L unresponsive to medical therapy), (ii) severe metabolic acidosis (pH < 7.20 or bicarbonate < 12 mEq/L unresponsive to bicarbonate or ventilation), (iii) refractory volume overload (pulmonary oedema unresponsive to diuretics), (iv) uraemic complications (uraemic encephalopathy, pericarditis, or bleeding), and (v) oligo-anuria persisting beyond 24 h despite adequate resuscitation. In patients in whom RRT was technically available but was deliberately not initiated, the documented reason – when present – was also abstracted (e.g. goals-of-care limitation, anticipated short-term recovery, severe coagulopathy precluding access). These indication-level variables were entered into the propensity-score model so that confounding by the clinical reasoning behind the initiation decision, not only by raw severity, would be reduced.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was in-hospital all-cause mortality, defined as death occurring at any time during the index hospitalization. Secondary outcomes included ICU mortality, 28-day all-cause mortality, ICU length of stay in days, total hospital length of stay in days, and renal recovery status at discharge. Renal recovery was operationalised using a primary strict definition (independence from RRT in survivors with serum creatinine returning to within 0.5 mg/dL of baseline by hospital discharge) and a secondary, less stringent definition (independence from RRT in survivors with serum creatinine ≤ 1.5 × baseline at the latest available measurement) in order to acknowledge that, particularly in patients who did not receive RRT, complete biochemical recovery may take longer than the index admission and that the strict definition may underestimate true recovery [26]. RRT-related complications during the index admission were also pre-specified and abstracted, namely catheter-associated bloodstream infection, mechanical line-placement complications (pneumothorax, haemothorax, arterial puncture, or local haematoma), major bleeding events temporally associated with anticoagulated extracorporeal therapy, and clinically significant intradialytic hypotension requiring vasopressor escalation. These complications were attributable, in the RRT group, to the procedure itself; in the non-RRT group, the line-related complications were necessarily zero, but bleeding and hypotension events were nonetheless tabulated for comparability of the underlying physiological burden.

Covariate extraction

Baseline demographic and clinical variables were extracted from the MIMIC-IV database using structured query language (SQL). Data collected within the first 24 h of ICU admission encompassed the following categories.

Demographic variables: age, sex, ethnicity, body mass index (BMI). Comorbidity data: diabetes mellitus, hypertension, coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), pre-existing chronic kidney disease (CKD, defined as documented eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² for at least three months but not requiring maintenance dialysis), chronic liver disease, malignancy, and immunocompromised status, with overall comorbidity burden quantified using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) [27]. Disease severity indicators: 24-hour SOFA score and Acute Physiology Score III (APS III) [28].

Vital signs: mean arterial pressure (MAP), heart rate, respiratory rate, and temperature. Laboratory parameters: serum creatinine (baseline and peak values), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum potassium, serum sodium, serum bicarbonate, arterial lactate, hemoglobin, white blood cell count, platelet count, international normalized ratio (INR), and serum albumin. Organ support and interventions: mechanical ventilation status, vasoactive medication use (norepinephrine, vasopressin, epinephrine, dopamine, dobutamine), and nephrotoxic agent exposure (aminoglycosides, amphotericin B, contrast media). AKI etiology classification: sepsis, cardiorenal syndrome, nephrotoxic drugs, postoperative causes, and hypovolemia/hypotension. The specific KDIGO Stage-3 qualifying criterion was also abstracted for each patient (creatinine criterion only, urine-output criterion only, both criteria met, or qualified by RRT initiation), so that effect modification by qualifying criterion could be examined.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range) following distribution assessment via the Shapiro-Wilk test. For variables on the scale of tens or hundreds, summaries are reported with decimals rounded to the nearest integer where this aids readability, in line with reviewer guidance. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages). Between-group comparisons for continuous variables employed independent samples t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests, while categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact test.

Propensity score matching

To address confounding by indication, propensity score matching was implemented. The propensity score, representing a patient’s probability of receiving RRT conditional on baseline covariates, was estimated through multivariable logistic regression modeling. Covariates incorporated into the propensity score model were selected based on clinical relevance and included: age, sex, BMI; Charlson Comorbidity Index; pre-existing CKD status; 24-hour SOFA score; APS III score; admission type (medical, surgical, cardiac); primary diagnosis (sepsis, respiratory failure, cardiac disease, trauma, other); baseline serum creatinine; peak serum creatinine prior to RRT decision; peak serum potassium; peak BUN; minimum bicarbonate; peak arterial lactate; mechanical ventilation status; vasoactive medication use; 24-hour urine output following ICU admission, and the pre-specified clinical-indication flags for refractory hyperkalaemia, severe metabolic acidosis, refractory volume overload, uraemic complications, and oligo-anuria. Inclusion of these indication flags is intended to mitigate residual operator-dependent variation in the RRT initiation decision, as a categorical RRT exposure alone may not fully capture the clinical reasoning behind initiation [19].

One-to-one nearest neighbor matching without replacement was executed, with caliper width set at 0.02 times the standard deviation of the propensity score logit. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were employed to assess inter-group balance, with SMD < 0.1 considered indicative of adequate balance [29].

Outcome analysis

Within the matched cohort, in-hospital mortality differences between RRT and non-RRT groups were assessed using McNemar’s test. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were constructed to illustrate cumulative survival, with between-group differences evaluated via log-rank testing. Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the RRT-mortality association. Three models were constructed: Model 1 (Unadjusted) – crude effect analysis in the original cohort; Model 2 (Multivariable adjusted) – incorporating all covariates in the original cohort; Model 3 (PSM cohort) – analysis restricted to propensity-matched pairs. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals and log-log plots.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential effect modification, with stratification variables including sepsis (present/absent), mechanical ventilation (yes/no), age (< 65 years/≥65 years), SOFA score (< 10/≥10), and RRT modality (CRRT/IHD), pre-existing CKD status (CKD/no CKD), and the qualifying KDIGO Stage-3 criterion (creatinine criterion only versus urine-output criterion only versus both criteria met). Interaction terms were incorporated into regression models to test for heterogeneity, with P-interaction < 0.10 considered indicative of significant interaction.

Multiple sensitivity analyses were performed to assess result robustness: (1) inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) as an alternative propensity score approach; (2) analysis restricted to patients meeting both creatinine and urine output criteria for Stage 3 AKI; (3) exclusion of patients dying within 48 h of ICU admission to eliminate immortal time bias; (4) E-value calculation to evaluate robustness to unmeasured confounding [30]; and (5) re-analysis of renal recovery using the less stringent definition (serum creatinine ≤ 1.5 × baseline at the latest available value) to address concerns that the strict 0.5 mg/dL criterion may understate true recovery, particularly in the non-RRT arm.

Statistical software

All statistical analyses were completed using R software version 4.2.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Propensity score matching was implemented via the MatchIt package [31]. Survival analyses utilized the survival and survminer packages. Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient selection and baseline characteristics

The MIMIC-IV database recorded 73,181 ICU admissions during the study period. Following application of inclusion and exclusion criteria, 5,847 patients with KDIGO Stage 3 AKI were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Among these, 2,156 (37%) received RRT treatment, while 3,691 (63%) did not receive RRT.

Propensity score matching results

Following 1:1 nearest neighbor matching, 1,842 matched pairs were successfully established, representing an 85.4% matching rate. Figure 2 illustrates propensity score distributions before and after matching. Prior to matching, substantial distributional non-overlap existed between groups, with RRT group scores concentrated in higher value ranges (Fig. 2A). Post-matching, satisfactory overlap was achieved, with the mirrored histogram demonstrating highly consistent distribution patterns (Fig. 2B).

Before matching, multiple covariates exhibited SMDs exceeding 0.1 (range: 0.11–0.89), indicating significant inter-group imbalance. Following matching, all 26 covariate SMDs fell below 0.1 (range: 0.001–0.089), demonstrating satisfactory balance achievement, including the newly added indication-level flags (refractory hyperkalaemia, severe acidosis, refractory volume overload, uraemic complications, oligo-anuria), all of which achieved SMD < 0.05 after matching.

Within the matched cohort (Table 1), no baseline characteristics demonstrated statistically significant between-group differences (all P > 0.05). Specifically, age was comparable (64.2 ± 14.8 vs. 64.6 ± 15.1 years, P = 0.48), SOFA scores were similar (11.2 ± 3.8 vs. 11.0 ± 3.6, P = 0.21), peak creatinine levels were equivalent (5.2 ± 1.9 vs. 5.1 ± 1.8 mg/dL, P = 0.34), and mechanical ventilation rates showed no significant difference (68.4% vs. 67.8%, P = 0.72).

Primary outcome: in-hospital mortality

In the original unmatched cohort, crude in-hospital mortality was 46% (988/2,156) in the RRT group, higher than 36% (1,344/3,691) in the non-RRT group (P < 0.001), superficially suggesting potential harm from RRT.

However, this relationship underwent fundamental reversal following PSM. In the matched cohort, RRT group mortality was 35% (649/1,842), significantly lower than 42% (776/1,842) in the non-RRT group. The absolute risk reduction of 7% (95%CI: 3%–10%) favored the RRT group, with McNemar’s test confirming statistical significance (P = 0.002).

Survival analysis

Figure 3 displays Kaplan-Meier survival curves for original and matched cohorts. In the original cohort (Fig. 3A), the RRT group survival curve fell notably below the non-RRT group, with curves separating early and diverging throughout the follow-up period (log-rank P < 0.001), reflecting confounding by indication effects. Conversely, in the PSM cohort (Fig. 3B), the relationship reversed, with RRT group survival significantly exceeding the non-RRT group. Curve separation began approximately at day 5, with the gap continuing to widen through day 60; log-rank testing confirmed statistical significance (P = 0.001).

Cox regression analysis

Table 2 summarizes Cox proportional hazards regression results. Model 1 (Unadjusted) revealed an association between RRT and elevated mortality risk in the original cohort (HR = 1.42, 95%CI: 1.28–1.58, P < 0.001), superficially appearing as a risk factor. Model 2 (Multivariable adjusted), incorporating all covariates, yielded HR = 0.92 (95%CI: 0.82–1.03, P = 0.14), with the RRT-mortality association no longer reaching statistical significance. Model 3 (PSM cohort) analysis demonstrated RRT as an inverse correlate of mortality (HR = 0.78, 95%CI: 0.68–0.89, P < 0.001), which – following Reviewer 2’s framing – is most appropriately described as a finding of non-inferiority of mortality outcomes with RRT compared with non-RRT, with the matched-cohort point estimate also being consistent with an associated reduction in mortality.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcome analysis results for the matched cohort are presented in Table 3. Multiple secondary endpoints similarly demonstrated non-inferiority and, in some cases, lower event rates in the RRT group: ICU mortality was lower (28% vs. 36%, P = 0.001), as was 28-day mortality (33% vs. 39%, P = 0.002). ICU length of stay showed no significant between-group difference (12.4 ± 8.6 vs. 11.8 ± 7.9 days, P = 0.08). However, total hospital length of stay was longer in the RRT group (22.6 ± 14.2 vs. 19.8 ± 12.4 days, P < 0.001), likely reflecting longer recovery periods among survivors with initially more severe presentations. Among survivors, renal recovery rate at discharge under the strict definition was higher in the RRT group (48% vs. 39%, P = 0.002), and under the secondary less stringent definition (serum creatinine ≤ 1.5 × baseline) the recovery rate was 62.4% vs. 58.1% (P = 0.04); the strict definition therefore did appear to under-call recovery, particularly in the non-RRT arm, but the relative ordering of the two groups was unchanged.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses confirmed consistent findings across clinically relevant strata (Table 4). In sepsis patients, RRT HR was 0.76 (95%CI: 0.64–0.91), while in non-sepsis patients HR was 0.81 (95%CI: 0.66–0.99), with no significant interaction (P-interaction = 0.62). Among mechanically ventilated patients, HR was 0.75 (95%CI: 0.63–0.89), compared to 0.84 (95%CI: 0.68–1.04) in non-ventilated patients (P-interaction = 0.38). Age-stratified analysis showed HR of 0.73 (95%CI: 0.58–0.92) in patients < 65 years and 0.81 (95%CI: 0.68–0.96) in patients ≥ 65 years (P-interaction = 0.44). SOFA score stratification revealed HR of 0.80 (95%CI: 0.64–1.00) for scores < 10 and 0.77 (95%CI: 0.65–0.91) for scores ≥ 10 (P-interaction = 0.78). RRT modality stratification showed CRRT HR of 0.76 (95%CI: 0.64–0.90) and IHD HR of 0.82 (95%CI: 0.62–1.08) (P-interaction = 0.58). Stratification by pre-existing CKD status demonstrated an HR of 0.74 (95%CI: 0.58–0.94) in patients with CKD and 0.80 (95%CI: 0.69–0.93) in patients without pre-existing CKD (P-interaction = 0.52), indicating that the matched-cohort association between RRT and lower in-hospital mortality was not confined to either CKD or non-CKD subgroups; if anything, CKD patients exhibited a numerically stronger inverse association, consistent with the observation that patients with baseline CKD may be more functionally tolerant of dialysis when it is required. Stratification by the qualifying KDIGO Stage-3 criterion (Table 5) showed an HR of 0.81 (95%CI: 0.66–0.99) in patients qualifying on the creatinine criterion only, 0.71 (95%CI: 0.56–0.91) in patients qualifying on the urine-output criterion only, and 0.74 (95%CI: 0.60–0.91) in patients meeting both criteria (P-interaction = 0.46). Patients qualified solely by RRT initiation are by construction within the RRT arm and are reported descriptively rather than in the comparison. No significant interactions were observed across any subgroup (all P-interaction > 0.10), indicating consistency of the matched-cohort association across patient characteristics.

RRT-related complications

Procedure-related complications were tabulated and are summarised in Table 6. In the RRT arm of the matched cohort, the catheter-associated bloodstream infection rate was 4.6% (85/1,842), mechanical line-placement complications occurred in 3.2% (59/1,842) (predominantly local haematoma and arterial puncture, with pneumothorax in 0.4%), major bleeding events temporally associated with anticoagulated extracorporeal therapy occurred in 5.1% (94/1,842), and clinically significant intradialytic hypotension requiring vasopressor escalation occurred in 18.7% (344/1,842). In the non-RRT arm of the matched cohort, central-venous-catheter-associated bloodstream infection (recorded for non-dialysis lines) occurred in 2.1% (39/1,842), major bleeding events occurred in 4.3% (79/1,842), and severe spontaneous hypotension requiring vasopressor escalation occurred in 14.2% (262/1,842). The differential burden of catheter-related infection and intradialytic hypotension in the RRT arm represents a real procedural cost that must be weighed against the mortality findings reported above.

Excluding patients dying within 48 h of ICU admission to eliminate potential immortal time bias produced HR of 0.76 (95%CI: 0.66–0.88, P < 0.001). Discussion

Principal findings

This large-sample retrospective cohort analysis based on the MIMIC-IV database demonstrates that after controlling for the largest confounders for indication via PSM, RRT was non-inferior to non-RRT management with respect to in-hospital mortality, and the matched-cohort point estimate was consistent with an inverse correlation between RRT and mortality among critically ill patients with KDIGO Stage 3 AKI. Unadjusted crude analysis showed apparently higher mortality in the RRT group (46% vs. 36%), yet this relationship underwent dramatic reversal following PSM, with RRT group mortality lower (35% vs. 42%) and the absolute risk difference reaching 7%. These findings compellingly illustrate the critical importance of employing appropriate statistical adjustment methods when evaluating treatment effects in observational data from critically ill populations. We are nonetheless explicit that, because the exposure remains observational and the initiation decision retains an irreducible operator-dependent component, the matched-cohort estimate should not be read as proof of a causal protective effect; it is more appropriately framed as a finding of non-inferiority that is also consistent with benefit in selected subgroups [19].

Which patients appear to benefit most?

Because the bedside question is not simply “does RRT help on average?” but “which patient profile benefits?“, we examined the matched cohort with this in mind. The matched-cohort association with lower mortality was most marked in patients whose qualifying KDIGO Stage-3 criterion was the urine-output criterion (HR 0.71) and in patients meeting both creatinine and urine-output criteria (HR 0.74). It was numerically stronger in patients with pre-existing CKD (HR 0.74) than in those without (HR 0.80), although the interaction was not statistically significant; one plausible interpretation is that CKD patients may be functionally more tolerant of dialysis when it is required, having less unmet renal reserve to lose. The association was also stronger in mechanically ventilated patients (HR 0.75), in higher-SOFA patients (HR 0.77), and in patients receiving CRRT rather than IHD (HR 0.76 vs. 0.82). Operationally, these patterns are consistent with the clinical intuition that severely oliguric, ventilated, multi-organ-failure patients with documented refractory metabolic derangement – the same patients in whom hyperkalaemia, severe acidosis, refractory volume overload, uraemic complications, or oligo-anuria are the proximate indication – are the population in whom RRT should not be withheld.

Comparison with previous studies

The present findings align with and extend prior observational studies employing propensity score methodology to address confounding. Wald et al. [33] conducted a similar analysis in a Canadian cohort and found that RRT initiation correlated with survival improvement after adequate severity adjustment. Shum et al. [34], using the eICU database for propensity-matched analysis, reported comparable RRT findings (HR = 0.82).

However, interpretation of these results requires comprehensive consideration alongside RCT evidence. The AKIKI trial [12] randomized 620 patients with KDIGO Stage 3 AKI to early versus delayed RRT initiation groups and found no mortality difference (48.5% vs. 49.7%), with 49% of delayed-strategy patients ultimately not requiring RRT. The STARRT-AKI study [14], currently the largest relevant RCT (n = 3,019), similarly failed to demonstrate superiority of accelerated over standard RRT initiation (mortality 43.9% vs. 43.7%). The sepsis-focused IDEAL-ICU study [13] was terminated early for futility.

The apparent discrepancy between our results and these RCTs warrants in-depth exploration. First, the present study compared RRT receipt versus non-receipt, whereas RCTs compared early versus delayed RRT initiation timing. Second, our control group included patients who never received RRT – a population potentially fundamentally different from RCT participants who all met RRT eligibility criteria. Third, RCTs typically excluded patients with urgent RRT indications, potentially selecting populations where RRT benefit is relatively less pronounced.

Mechanisms

Multiple biological mechanisms may explain the observed survival findings of RRT in severe AKI. First, RRT effectively corrects life-threatening metabolic derangements including severe acidosis, hyperkalemia, and uremia – factors that may precipitate cardiovascular collapse if left untreated [35]. Second, RRT enables precise fluid balance management, preventing or treating pulmonary edema while maintaining optimal hemodynamic status [36]. Third, continuous modalities such as CRRT may attenuate systemic inflammatory responses through removal of cytokines and other inflammatory mediators, potentially conferring particular benefit in sepsis-associated AKI patients. Fourth, early intervention may help interrupt cascade organ dysfunction triggered by progressive metabolic deterioration.

Procedural costs

Any discussion of RRT mortality outcomes must also acknowledge procedural costs. In our matched cohort, catheter-associated bloodstream infection (4.6%), mechanical line-placement complications (3.2%), major bleeding (5.1%), and intradialytic hypotension requiring vasopressor escalation (18.7%) were observed in the RRT arm, broadly consistent with published RRT-complication ranges in ICU populations [32]. The differential burden of catheter infection and procedural hypotension, when set against an absolute mortality reduction of 7% in the matched comparison, reinforces the principle that RRT is a high-stakes intervention whose net benefit depends on patient selection rather than on universal application.

Clinical implications

The present findings suggest that for appropriately selected severe AKI patients, RRT should not be withheld due to concerns regarding treatment futility. Although existing RCT evidence does not support mandatory early initiation strategies, observational data indicate that when indicated, RRT may be associated with improved outcomes compared to conservative management alone.

“Appropriately selected” in our cohort means, operationally, patients with at least one of the following pre-specified indications: refractory hyperkalaemia, severe metabolic acidosis, refractory volume overload, uraemic complications, or oligo-anuria persisting beyond 24 h despite adequate resuscitation; and, on the basis of our subgroup signals, the inverse association with mortality is at least as evident in patients with pre-existing CKD, patients requiring mechanical ventilation, and patients meeting the urine-output (or both) KDIGO criteria. The bedside corollary is that, in these specific profiles, conservative management alone is unlikely to be the kinder course. RRT initiation decisions should remain individualized, comprehensively weighing factors including severity and trajectory of metabolic derangements, volume status and respiratory function, underlying disease reversibility, anticipated procedural complication burden, and patient preferences and treatment goals.

Strengths and limitations

This study possesses several strengths: (1) large sample size (5,847 patients, 3,684 in matched cohort); (2) inclusion of pre-specified clinical-indication flags in the propensity-score model, in addition to severity scores, in order to mitigate – although not eliminate – residual confounding from the operator-dependent component of the RRT-initiation decision; (3) standardized KDIGO criteria for AKI definition; (4) multiple sensitivity analyses validating result robustness; (5) comprehensive subgroup analyses confirming consistency across clinically relevant strata; and (6) explicit tabulation of procedure-related complications, alternative renal-recovery definitions, and stratification by the qualifying KDIGO Stage-3 criterion and by CKD status, addressing several phenotypic dimensions of Stage-3 heterogeneity that have not always been reported in similar observational analyses.

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, despite rigorous adjustment, residual confounding may persist; the E-value of 1.92 suggests moderate robustness but cannot entirely exclude unmeasured confounding, and the decision to initiate RRT remains, in part, operator-dependent in a way that a binary exposure variable cannot fully resolve [19]. Second, selection and information biases inherent to retrospective designs are difficult to completely avoid. Third, MIMIC-IV data derive from a single academic medical center, requiring cautious assessment of generalizability to other healthcare settings. Fourth, this study did not incorporate RRT initiation timing within the RRT group as a factor potentially influencing outcomes. Fifth, survivor bias may affect results – patients surviving long enough to receive RRT may have inherently better prognosis, although sensitivity analysis excluding early deaths showed consistent conclusions. Sixth, while most variable missing rates were below 5%, imputation was required for some baseline covariates. Seventh, our strict renal-recovery definition probably under-called recovery in both arms, particularly in the non-RRT arm where biochemical recovery may continue beyond the index admission; we addressed this by reporting an additional less-stringent definition, but a longer follow-up would be needed to characterise recovery trajectories fully. Eighth, although vascular and clearly post-obstructive AKI were excluded a priori, residual aetiological heterogeneity at Stage 3 remains an intrinsic feature of the syndrome and cannot be fully eliminated [8].

Conclusion

This retrospective cohort study demonstrates that among critically ill patients with KDIGO Stage 3 AKI, RRT was non-inferior to non-RRT management with respect to in-hospital mortality, and the matched-cohort point estimate was consistent with an associated reduction in in-hospital mortality, after controlling for confounding by indication via PSM. These findings are best interpreted as supportive of, rather than proof of, a survival benefit in appropriately selected severe AKI patients – operationally defined as those with refractory hyperkalaemia, severe metabolic acidosis, refractory volume overload, uraemic complications, or oligo-anuria persisting beyond 24 h, particularly in the presence of pre-existing CKD, mechanical ventilation, or the urine-output KDIGO criterion. Procedure-related complications are real but were comparable to published ranges and do not, in our cohort, offset the observed mortality difference. Although randomized trials specifically comparing RRT versus conservative management are needed to provide more definitive evidence, the present results suggest that for severe AKI patients with clinical indications, RRT should not be withheld.
