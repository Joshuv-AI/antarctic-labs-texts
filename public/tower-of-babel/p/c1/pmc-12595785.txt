Predictive analysis of influencing factors on HIV testing behavior among college students in Shanxi, China
Authors: Danhe Zhao, Xinyang Zhang, Xiaoting Liang, Yanqing Zhao, Baobei Niu, Li Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite high level of acceptance, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) testing rate among Chinese college students remained low. Few studies examined the extent of the relationship between HIV testing behavior and its influencing factors among college students. This study aimed to investigate HIV testing rates among college students and examine key factors associated with HIV testing behavior.MethodsThe convenience sampling method was used for college students from four colleges in Shanxi from October to December 2023. A network questionnaire survey was designed to collect information on general demographics, sexual encounters, and HIV testing behavior. Logistic regression and the decision tree model were combined to identify key factors influencing HIV testing behavior among college students.ResultsAmong 3,115 college students, the rate of HIV testing was 2.5% (n = 77) in the past year. The binary logistic regression analysis indicated that students who had received school sex education (aOR = 2.78, 95%CI:1.51–5.09), homosexual orientation (aOR = 3.12, 95%CI:1.09–8.93), and had engaged in a sexual encounter in the past year (aOR = 5.13, 95%CI:2.50-10.53) were positively associated with HIV testing behavior. In addition, compared with freshmen, only sophomores were more likely to undergo HIV testing (aOR = 1.87, 95%CI:1.01–3.43). Further, the decision tree model revealed that the most significant factor influencing HIV testing behavior was having had a sexual encounter in the past year, followed by being in love and having received school sex education. The accuracy rate of the decision tree model was 97.53%, the precision rate was 97.53%, and the F1 value was 0.99.ConclusionHIV testing rate among the college students was relatively low. Sexual experience, being in love and school sex education were significant predictors of HIV testing behavior. Being offered HIV self-testing services and implemented sex education programs at an early age may promote HIV testing behavior among college students.

Background

Human immunodeficiency virus(HIV) infection remains a significant public health challenge among adolescents globally [1–4]. In 2023, approximately 1.3 million new HIV infections were reported worldwide, with individuals aged 15–24 accounting for 16% of these cases [5]. In China, from 2010 to 2019, a total of 23,307 newly diagnosed HIV/Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome (AIDS) cases involved students aged 15–24 [6]. It is concerning that the number of newly diagnosed college students with HIV has seen an annual growth rate ranging from 30% to 50% over the past few years in China [7]. Young individuals, particularly college students, were considered a high-risk group for HIV transmission [8]. Driven by their sensation seeking [9], college students were susceptible to engaging in high-risk sexual behaviors, such as unprotected sex or having multiple sexual partners, which were associated with an increased risk of HIV infection [10, 11]. Given these findings, it is crucial to strengthen HIV testing initiatives [12]. Such initiatives enable the early identification of infected individuals and the timely initiation of antiretroviral therapy (ART), which serves as a core strategy for mitigating the HIV epidemic [13].

Despite that Chinese college students have shown relatively high acceptance of HIV testing [14], the testing rate remained low [15–17]. Current research has identified several factors associated with HIV testing behavior among college students [15, 18]. The low uptake of testing may be partly attributed to students’ perceived shame if diagnosed with HIV [18]. In addition, misinformation, limited service availability, and the high cost of HIV self-testing kits may hinder HIV testing behavior [19]. Therefore, promoting HIV testing through effective interventions is essential to reduce the risk of HIV transmission among college students.

Many traditional statistical methods have been applied to analyze HIV testing behavior and its influencing factors among college students [15, 20]. However, their effectiveness is often limited by the complexity of data, which involve nonlinear relationships and high dimensions [21]. For example, there was a negative nonlinear association between the frequency of unprotected sexual behavior and the likelihood of HIV testing [22]. In addition, Li et al. found various factors influencing HIV testing behavior, such as gender, sexual orientation, dating experience, and sexual behavior [17]. These factors collectively result in a high-dimensional dataset due to their complex interactions. Moreover, there were few studies on the extent of the relationship between HIV testing behavior and its influencing factors among college students. To bridging these gaps, decision tree models can effectively handle the complexity of the data [23] and reveal the relative importance of different predictors [24]. Nevertheless, given their susceptibility to overfitting and limitations in feature selection, decision tree models were often combined with logistic regression to leverage the complementary strengths of both methods [25].

This study aims to investigate HIV testing rate among college students in Shanxi, China, and examine factors associated with HIV testing behavior. Logistic regression and the decision tree model were combined to analyze factors associated with HIV testing behavior, and variable importance was assessed by using the decision tree model. These findings may inform the development of evidence-based intervention strategies.

Methods

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional study was conducted by using the convenience sampling method. Four undergraduate colleges in Shanxi, including comprehensive, medical, and liberal arts majors, were selected. From October to December 2023, students from different grades and majors were surveyed. Within each college, 1–3 classes were chosen based on accessibility, and all students selected were invited to participate.The inclusion criteria include college students: (1) aged 18–25; (2) who can read the informed consent online, and agree to complete the questionnaire; (3) who can read in Chinese and complete the questionnaire independently.

Sociodemographic characteristics

The sociodemographic variables in this study included age, gender, major (medical or non-medical), grade (freshman, sophomore, junior, senior), place of registration (Shanxi or outside Shanxi), nation (Han or other), and all forms of sexual education received at school (yes or no). Sexual orientation was assessed through the question “Which sexual orientation do you consider yourself to be?” with response options homosexual, heterosexual, bisexual, or uncertain.

Sexual encounters

Sexual encounters referred to any form of intimate physical contact involving direct genital, oral, or anal mucosal exposure between individuals. Sexual encounters contain the following: in love (yes or no), attitude toward having sexual encounters during college (acceptable or unacceptable), acceptance of partner’s previous sexual experience (yes or no), having experience a sexual encounter ever (yes or no), age of first sexual encounter, having had a sexual encounter in the past year (yes or no), condom use during sexual encounters (never, sometimes, consistently), and had a recent sexual encounter with a boyfriend/girlfriend (yes or no).

HIV testing behavior

HIV testing behavior was assessed through the question “Have you done any HIV testing in the past year?” with response options Yes or No.

Quality control

This study was based on a set of mature and widely recognized questionnaires [26]. After conducting a pre-survey, the questionnaire was revised and improved.

Prior to the survey, the questionnaire quick response (QR) code was distributed to class groups, and students were informed of the precautions regarding questionnaire completion by school counselors or recruited volunteers. Participants scanned the QR code on their mobile devices to complete and submit the questionnaire anonymously online. All investigators received standardized training to avoid influencing responses. After submission, the investigators reviewed the questionnaires to ensure quality. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of the questionnaire used in this study was 0.83.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed via SPSS 22.0 and RStudio 4.4.1 software. We described categorical variables by using frequency and percentage, and used the Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test to compare group differences in HIV testing behavior among college students. Univariable logistic regression analysis was performed, and variables with p-value less than 0.1 were entered into a binary logistic regression model fitted by using the forward stepwise likelihood ratio (LR) method to identify associated factors. The p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test was utilized to assess the goodness-of-fit of binary logistic regression model.

Further, we employed the decision tree model to examine the primary factors influencing HIV testing behavior among college students. This model offers an intuitive representation of the relative importance of different factors, displayed along each path from the root to the leaf node, and ranks them based on their impact [24]. HIV testing behavior was treated as the dependent variable, and variables found significant in the univariable analyses were included as independent variables.

The parameters of the decision tree model were set as follows: The significance level for splitting nodes and merging categories was set as 0.05, and the maximum depth of the decision tree was restricted to three levels. Each parent node was required to contain a minimum of 100 samples, while each resulting child node had to contain at least 50 samples. If a node’s sample size failed to meet these minimum requirements, it was designated as a leaf node and no further splits were conducted [27]. For sensitivity analysis of different problem orders, we recorded the structure of decision trees obtained under different orders and calculated evaluation indicators such as accuracy rate (number of samples correctly classified), precision rate (ratio of the number of positive samples to all predicted positive samples), and F1 value (harmonic mean of precision and sensitivity) to highlight the robustness of the results.

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fenyang College of Shanxi Medical University (No.2024012). All respondents agreed to provide informed consent online before this survey.

Characteristics of sexual encounters

Among the 3,115 students surveyed, 21.9% (n=682) reported being in love, 27.4% (n=853) students' attitudes were acceptable toward having sexual encounters during college, 26.2% (n=816) accepted partner's previous sexual experience, and 4.0% (n=124) have experienced a sexual encounter ever, with the median age at first sex being 19 years old (ranging from 12 to 25 years). Further, it was found that 3.2% (n=100) college students had engaged in a sexual encounter in the past year, with 63% (n=63) consistently using condoms and 7% (n=7) never using condoms. Among the 100 students, 90% had a recent sexual encounter with a boyfriend/girlfriend (Table 1).

HIV testing reporting rates among various groups

Among 3,115 college students surveyed, we found that 2.5% (77/3115, 95%CI 1.9%-3.1%) underwent HIV testing in the past year, and of these, 85.7% (66/77) were aware of their test results. Among 100 students who reported sexual activity in the past year, only 11.0% (11/100) underwent HIV testing. Furthermore, we analyzed the HIV testing reporting rates among various subgroups of college students.

In the univariable analysis, significant differences were observed in variables, including grade, receiving school sex education, sexual orientation, being in love, the attitude to having sexual encounters during college, having experienced a sexual encounter ever, and having had a sexual encounter in the past year(P<0.05) (Figure 1).

Association between the influencing factors and HIV testing behavior

Binary logistic regression analysis revealed that students who had received school sex education (aOR 2.78, 95%CI 1.51-5.09), homosexual orientation (aOR 3.12, 95%CI 1.09-8.93), and had engaged in a sexual encounter in the past year (aOR 5.13, 95%CI 2.50-10.53) were positively associated with HIV testing behavior. In addition, compared with freshmen, only sophomores were more likely to undergo HIV testing (aOR 1.87, 95%CI 1.01-3.43). (Figure 2). The Hosmer-Lemeshow test indicated that the logistic regression model adequately fit the data (χ2= 3.47, P= 0.838).

Decision tree analysis of factors influencing HIV testing behavior

We explored the importance of the influencing factors by using the decision tree model. The results revealed that having had a sexual encounter in the past year, being in love, and having received school sex education were influencing factors in HIV testing behavior. The first level of classification was based on whether individuals had engaged in a sexual encounter in the past year, indicating that having a sexual encounter was the primary factor influencing HIV testing behavior. The proportion of students who had engaged in a sexual encounter in the past year (10.5%) was higher than that of students who had not engaged in a sexual encounter (2.3%). The second level of screening was whether or not they were in love. The proportion of students who had not engaged in a sexual encounter in the past year and were in love (4.3%) was higher than that of students who were not (1.8%). The factor identified at the third level was whether they received school sex education. The proportion of students who had not engaged in a sexual encounter in the past year, were in love, and had received sex education (5.9%) was higher than that of students who had not receive such education (0.7%) (Figure 3). The prediction results indicated that the accuracy rate was 97.53%, the precision rate was 97.53%, and the F1 value was 0.99. These indicators indicated that the decision tree model performed well.

Discussion

This study primarily evaluated HIV testing behavior among college students and identified the key factors influencing it. The HIV testing rate among college students was found to be relatively low. Similar findings have been reported in other studies conducted in China[17]. This may reflect inadequate self-protective behaviors and limited health monitoring during sexual activity. Therefore, identifying the determinants of HIV testing is crucial for designing targeted interventions.

The decision tree analysis indicated that a sexual encounter in the past year was the most significant factor influencing HIV testing behavior. Additionally, students who were in love were more likely to be tested than those who were not. Previous studies have found an association between students' sexual experience and HIV testing behavior[16, 17]. Specifically, sexually active college students, particularly those without fixed partners, were prone to engaging in risky sexual behaviors[11]. Given that students often face financial burdens due to tuition and daily expenses, providing free HIV testing is essential[28]. Therefore, schools should provide free HIV self-testing kits and condoms in dormitories or campus clinics for students, which can significantly increase testing rates, especially among high-risk groups[29], Furthermore, college students in love should be encouraged to seek HIV testing together.

Logistic regression analysis revealed that homosexual students were more likely to undergo HIV testing than students of other sexual orientations, which was consistent with results observed in China[15]. Previous research has indicated that homosexual students were more likely to engage in unprotected sexual behaviors[30]. Additionally, they tend to have a higher perception of HIV risk, which, in turn, may further motivate them to seek HIV testing[31]. However, studies have shown that concerns about privacy may discourage students from getting tested[32]. Therefore, schools are advised to provide anonymous HIV self-testing kits to enhance the privacy of testing[33], which would encourage students, particularly homosexual students, to participate in HIV testing.

In addition, school-based sex education was associated with HIV testing behavior. This study indicated that students who had received school sex education were more likely to undergo HIV testing. Research has shown that students' satisfaction with the school sex education curriculum can significantly influence their HIV testing behavior[16]. These findings underscore the critical role of school-based sex education in promoting HIV testing among college students.

However, sex education in schools remained inadequate in China[34]. Specifically, less than 10% of schools in China provide formal sex education programs, with most focusing exclusively on HIV prevention[35]. Research indicated that only 55.6% of college students in China have received sexual education in school[36]. Moreover, there was a significant shortage of well-trained educators and sex education materials in China[37]. At present, the internet has become the primary source of sexual knowledge for college students[38]. While easy access to pornographic content may pose risks[39], the internet can also be leveraged to promote HIV prevention services through platforms such as dating apps and video sites[40]. Evidence suggested that Internet-based sex education packages can improve the accessibility of comprehensive sex education[37, 41]. Therefore, it is suggested that schools strengthen their sex education programs by implementing Internet-based sex education packages to promote HIV testing.

Finally, sophomores were more likely to be tested than freshmen. Among sophomores, higher levels of HIV-related knowledge[42] and greater exposure to HIV testing campaigns were positively associated with engaging in HIV testing[31]. Thus, schools should promote HIV-related education at an early age and strengthen HIV testing promotion through freshmen orientation activities, such as military training and health lectures. By being informed about the current state of the HIV epidemic, students' awareness of infection risks can be improved, which can encourage them to seek HIV testing.

Limitations

There were some limitations in this study. Firstly, as the participants completed the questionnaires online through convenience sampling, the survey sample was unevenly distributed across gender, grade, major, and other demographic characteristics. Secondly, the questionnaire was self-administered and required participants to recall past sexual encounters, which involved sensitive personal information, making responses susceptible to recall bias and reporting bias. In addition, the sensitivity of sexual encounters may have contributed to underreporting, and information regarding the number of sexual partners were not collected. In the future, a stratified random sampling method should be employed to improve the representativeness of the sample, with particular attention given to sexually active college students, especially those with multiple sexual partners.

Despite these limitations, this study extended previous evidence regarding HIV testing behavior among college students. Specifically, it explored in depth the importance of factors influencing HIV testing behavior. These findings may provide critical insights for informing public health policy.

Conclusion

In summary, this study found that HIV testing rate among college students was relatively low. The most significant factor associated with HIV testing behavior was sexual experience, followed by being in love and having received school sex education. Thus, these factors should be prioritized in public health policy and schools are encouraged to provide free HIV self-testing services to ensure timely access to HIV testing. In addition, disseminating HIV-related knowledge at an early age and implementing internet-based sex education programs may help increase the HIV testing behavior among college students.
