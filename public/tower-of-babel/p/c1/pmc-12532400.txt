Kaempferol regulates Ewing sarcoma progression via miR-26b-5p-mediated expression of the family with sequence similarity 98 member A
Authors: Yiquan Ji, Tian Gao, Zehua Xu, Bao Chen, Kai Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ewing sarcoma (ES) is a highly aggressive pediatric bone cancer with limited treatment options for metastatic or recurrent cases. This study investigated the antitumor effects of kaempferol, a natural flavonoid, on ES and its underlying molecular mechanisms. In vitro experiments demonstrated that kaempferol significantly inhibited the proliferation, migration, and invasion of SK-ES-1 cells in a dose- and time-dependent manner. Mechanistically, kaempferol upregulated miR-26b-5p, which directly targeted and suppressed FAM98A, a pro-oncogenic protein. This regulation led to the inhibition of the EGFR/PI3K/AKT/NF-κB signaling pathway, reducing tumor growth and metastasis. In vivo studies further confirmed that kaempferol suppressed ES tumor growth in a xenograft mouse model, while miR-26b-5p knockdown partially reversed this effect. Our findings suggest that kaempferol exerts its antitumor activity in ES by modulating the miR-26b-5p/FAM98A axis and downstream signaling pathways, highlighting its potential as a novel therapeutic agent for ES treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40360-025-01008-9.

Introduction

Originating primarily in the bone, Ewing sarcoma (ES) is a common bone and soft tissue malignancy, second only to osteosarcoma in incidence in children and adolescents. Because its incidence in children is higher than that in adults [1], it is also referred to as “pediatric bone cancer.” ES has a high degree of malignancy, poor prognosis, is prone to recurrence, and seriously affects the health of children [2]. Especially for ES patients with recurrence and metastasis, the survival rate is very low (20–30%) [3]. Unfortunately, studies have shown that approximately 20–25% of patients first diagnosed with ES have developed distant metastases [4]. Currently, no effective treatment plan is available for recurrent and metastatic ES. Therefore, exploring the role and molecular mechanisms of more traditional Chinese medicines or plant-derived components in the adjuvant therapy of ES is crucial for developing new treatments.

Kaempferol is a naturally occurring flavonoid widely distributed in vegetables, fruits, and traditional Chinese medicinal herbs (such as ginkgo biloba and tea leaves). It exhibits significant antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and cardiovascular protective effects [5]. In terms of antitumor activity, studies have reported that kaempferol markedly inhibits the viability of melanoma cells, inducing cell cycle arrest and apoptosis [6]. Additionally, kaempferol can trigger ROS-dependent apoptosis in pancreatic cancer cells through Akt/mTOR signaling [7]. Furthermore, research has demonstrated that kaempferol induces apoptosis in human osteosarcoma cells, exerting inhibitory effects on these cells both in vivo and in vitro [8]. However, the impact of kaempferol on ES has not yet been reported.

The regulation of ES occurrence and development via microRNA (miRNA) binding to target genes has been widely reported [9–11], revealing a correlation between ES and miRNA. Moreover, studies have demonstrated that kaempferol exerts antitumor effects in different cancers, a mechanism potentially associated with its regulation of miRNA expression [12, 13]. MiR-26b is abnormally expressed in various tumors, where it plays a role as an oncogenic or tumor suppressor gene, and has been reported to be regulated by kaempferol [14]. Most studies report that miR-26b is weakly expressed in tumors; It possesses the capability to hinder the growth of diverse tumor types, encompassing thyroid cancer [15], thereby exhibiting antitumor potential. However, the relationship between miR-26b and ES remains unclear.

Family with sequence similarity 98 member A (FAM98A) is a calcium-regulating protein whose abnormality can promote the progression of various cancers, such as non-small cell lung cancer [16] and endometrial cancer [17]. The carcinogenic effect of FAM98A on non-small cell lung cancer is related to the EGFR/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [16]. The role of the PI3K/AKT pathway in cancer pathogenesis and development has been well documented, and activated AKT has been shown to regulate downstream NF-κB activity [18], which plays an important role in various cancers [19]. Multiple studies have shown that kaempferol has a regulatory effect on the PI3K/AKT pathway [20–22]. Hence, the present investigation endeavors to illuminate the functional roles of kaempferol and miR-26b-5p on ES and determine whether kaempferol suppresses ES progression by regulating miR-26b-5p/FAM98A axis and further regulating the EGFR/PI3K/Akt/NF-κB signaling pathway.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and materials

Human ES (SK-ES-1) and osteoblast (hFOB) cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA). Cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 with supplements in a standard incubator. Genetic tools (miR-26b-5p mimic, inhibitor, and miR-NC) were purchased from GenePharma (Shanghai, China), and the lentiviral vector plasmids constructed by GENECHEM Biotech (Shanghai, China) were used to transfect SK-ES-1 cells with Flag-tagged FAM98A-overexpressing vectors and control vectors to establish stable FAM98A-overexpressing cell lines. Transfection were performed using Lipofectamine 2000 (#11668019, Invitrogen). Kaempferol (molecular formula: C₁₅H₁₀O₆; molecular weight: 286.23 g/mol; purity: 99.86%) was purchased from Selleck Chemicals LLC (Houston, Texas, USA).

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from SK-ES-1 cell line using a specific TRIzol reagent (ELK Biotechnology, catalog #EP013), following the manufacturer’s guidelines. To quantitatively analyze gene expression, the QuantStudio 6 Flex platform (Life Technologies) was employed to conduct RT-qPCR assays. Each qPCR reaction was performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. Data were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method. GAPDH was used as the internal control for normalization. The primer sequences used for RT-qPCR are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Cell viability assay

SK-ES-1 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5,000 cells per well. Cell survival rates were evaluated through a Cell Counting Kit-8 assay (# C0038; Beyotime). Subsequently, CCK-8 solution (10 µL) was added to each well of the SK-ES-1 cultures following the specified incubation period. Cells were then incubated at 37 °C for a duration ranging from 1 to 4 h. For quantitative analysis, absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (#DR-200Bs; Diatek).

Cell migration and invasion assay

The migration and invasion capabilities of SK-ES-1 cells were evaluated using Transwell chambers (#3422; Corning) with an 8 μm pore size. For invasion assessments, chamber membranes were pre-coated with 100 µg/mL Matrigel (#356234; BD). Following this, 1 × 105 cells were introduced into the upper chamber, and various treatments were applied. The lower compartments were filled with complete culture medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and incubated for 24 h. Afterward, the migrated cells were gathered, fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde, and visualized via 0.1% crystal violet staining. Images (IX51; OLYMPUS; 100×) of five randomly selected regions per chamber were taken to determine the number of migrating cells.

Luciferase reporter assay

FAM98A wild-type and variant luciferase reporter constructs were designed to interact with the miR-26b-5p binding site. These constructs were introduced alongside miR-26b-5p mimics into cells using Lipofectamine 2000. Luciferase activity was determined after 24 h using a dual-reporter system (Promega); Renilla luciferase activity was used for normalization.

Western blot analysis

To detect protein expression levels, FAM98A (#ab204083, 1:500, Abcam), p-EGFR (#AF3048, 1:1000, AFFINITY), EGFR (#AF6043, 1:1000, AFFINITY), p-PI3K (#AF3241, 1:1000, AFFINITY), PI3K (#AF6241, 1:1000, AFFINITY), p-AKT (#AF0016, 1:1000, AFFINITY), AKT (#AF6261, 1:1000, AFFINITY), P65 (#AF5006, 1:1000, AFFINITY), and GAPDH (#AF7021, 1:5000, AFFINITY) antibodies were used. Afterward, the membranes were washed with Tris-buffered saline containing Tween, followed by incubation with a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody (Beyotime, #A0216, 1:1000). Signals were visualized using an ECL-based chemiluminescence system (ASPEN, #AS1059).

In vivo tumorigenesis assay

Male BALB/c nude mice (4–5 weeks old) were sourced from Sipeifu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). To obtain SK-ES-1 cell lines exhibiting miR-26b-5p downregulation, we transfected these cells with AntagomiR-26b-5p (GenePharma, China). To establish a nude mouse subcutaneous tumor model, we used the following groups: normal SK-ES-1 cells (Control), kaempferol (20 mg/kg), kaempferol and miR-26b-5p-knockdown (kaempferol + AntagomiR-26b-5p). Briefly, SK-ES-1 cells (5 × 10⁶ cells in 200 µL PBS suspension) from each group were subcutaneously inoculated into the right axilla of mice. Intervention began when the tumor volume reached 100 ± 10 mm³: the control group received olive oil by gavage, while the treatment groups were administered the corresponding drug (20 mg/kg kaempferol olive oil solution) once daily. Tumor dimensions were recorded using a digital caliper biweekly for four weeks. After the treatments, the mice were humanely euthanized via cervical dislocation, and the tumors were excised for subsequent weighing. The tumor volume was derived using the formula V = (length × width2) / 2. The ethical oversight of the animal experiments conducted in this research was granted by the Jiaxing Second Hospital’s Research Ethics Committee (reference number 2022110023 S).

Bioinformatics analysis

We used miRanda (http://www.microrna.org/), miRDB (https://mirdb.org/), and TargetScan (http://www.targetscan.org/vert_71/) to determine the probable downstream effectors of miR-26b-5p.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean values and the standard deviations. Statistical evaluations were performed using SPSS software version 22.0 (IBM, NY, USA). To assess the statistical significance of the differences across experimental setups, one-way ANOVA was applied, incorporating the Newman–Keuls approach. Each experiment was performed at least thrice independently. Statistical significance was denoted as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

Results

Kaempferol inhibits SK‑ES‑1 cell growth, migration, and invasion

The chemical structure of kaempferol is shown in Fig. 1A. To investigate the effect of kaempferol on cell activity, SK-ES-1 cells were exposed to varying concentrations of kaempferol for 24, 48, and 72 h. The CCK-8 results demonstrated that kaempferol inhibited the viability of SK-ES-1 cells in a time- and dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1B). The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of kaempferol treatment on SK-ES-1 cells for 24 h was 76.48 µM. Subsequently, in the following experiments, SK-ES-1 cells were treated with 40 and 80 µM kaempferol for 24 h. Furthermore, we assessed the influence of kaempferol on SK-ES-1 cell migration and invasion through Transwell chamber assays. Our observations revealed that treatment of SK-ES-1 cells with 40 and 80 µM kaempferol resulted in a reduction in the number of migrating and invading cells (Fig. 1C, D). These results demonstrate that kaempferol can inhibit the proliferation, migration, and invasion of SK-ES-1 cells.

MiR-26b-5p is lowly expressed while FAM98A is highly expressed, and miR-26b-5p exerts a regulatory effect on FAM98A in SK-ES-1 cells

Previous studies have shown that kaempferol can regulate miR-26b-5p and upregulate its expression [14]. Our results indicate that compared to hFOB cells, the expression of miR-26b-5p is reduced in SK-ES-1 cells (Fig. 2A). After treatment with kaempferol, the expression of miR-26b-5p in SK-ES-1 cells increased (Fig. 2B). To further decrease and increase miR-26b-5p levels, we transfected SK-ES-1 cells with miR-26b-5p mimic, inhibitor, and miR-NC. Our findings demonstrate that the miR-26b-5p mimic and inhibitor effectively upregulated and downregulated miR-26b-5p expression in SK-ES-1 cells, respectively (Fig. 2C).

Using bioinformatics tools, we investigated the downstream targets of miR-26b-5p. We screened potential miR-26b-5p targets in the miRanda, miRDB, and TargetScan databases to identify relevant candidate targets (Fig. 2D). Comprehensive analysis led us to hypothesize that the 3′-UTR of FAM98A contains sequences complementary to the seed region of miR-26b, suggesting a possible interaction (Fig. 2E). To ascertain the association between miR-26b-5p and FAM98A, we constructed dual-luciferase reporters incorporating both wild-type (FAM98A-WT) and mutated (FAM98A-MT) miR-26b-5p binding sequences (Fig. 2F). Luciferase assays confirmed that miR-26b-5p mimics notably decreased the relative luciferase activity in SK-ES-1 cells transfected with FAM98A-WT, whereas it had no effect on cells transfected with FAM98A-MT (Fig. 2G).

Using RT-qPCR, we quantified the expression of FAM98A in hFOB and SK-ES-1 cells. Our results showed that FAM98A was significantly upregulated in SK-ES-1 cells compared to hFOB cells (Fig. 2H). Furthermore, enhanced miR-26b-5p expression in SK-ES-1 cells led to a reduction in FAM98A expression (Fig. 2I). These findings suggest that miR-26b-5p can regulate the expression of its downstream target, FAM98A, in SK-ES-1 cells.

In vitro, kaempferol exerted inhibitory effects on SK-ES-1 cells by regulating miR-26b-5p, with its mechanism associated with FAM98A and the downstream EGFR/PI3K/Akt/NF-κB signaling pathway

To investigate the potential of kaempferol in inhibiting SK-ES-1 cells via miR-26b-5p, we conducted rescue experiments to analyze the effects of kaempferol on SK-ES-1 cell proliferation, migration, and invasion after miR-26b-5p downregulation. Our findings revealed that kaempferol suppressed SK-ES-1 proliferation, however, this inhibitory effect was partially mitigated by miR-26b-5p downregulation (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, compared to the control group, kaempferol significantly suppressed SK-ES-1 cell migration, but this effect was reversed by the downregulation of miR-26b-5p (Fig. 3B and C). Similarly, kaempferol inhibited SK-ES-1 cell invasion, but this inhibitory effect was partially reversed after miR-26b-5p downregulation (Fig. 3D and E). These results suggest that kaempferol can induce inhibition of SK-ES-1 cell proliferation, migration, and invasion by modulating miR-26b-5p.

Subsequently, we explored kaempferol’s ability to regulate FAM98A and the downstream EGFR/PI3K/Akt/NF-κB pathway in SK-ES-1 cells via miR-26b-5p mediation through Western blot analysis. Treatment of SK-ES-1 cells with kaempferol led to a decrease in FAM98A protein expression, which was reversed upon miR-26b-5p downregulation. Additionally, after kaempferol treatment, the phosphorylation levels of EGFR, PI3K, and AKT (p-EGFR, p-PI3K, and p-AKT, respectively) in SK-ES-1 cells were significantly reduced, while their total protein levels (EGFR, PI3K, AKT) remained unchanged. Notably, miR-26b-5p downregulation corrected the aberrant protein expression in the EGFR/PI3K/AKT pathway induced by kaempferol in SK-ES-1 cells. Furthermore, nuclear protein extraction from SK-ES-1 cells revealed that kaempferol reduced nuclear NF-κB p65 levels, and this effect was attenuated by miR-26b-5p downregulation (Fig. 3F and G). These findings further validate that kaempferol’s regulation of miR-26b-5p plays a pivotal role in ES cell progression, and this effect is linked to FAM98A and the downstream EGFR/PI3K/Akt/NF-κB signaling pathway.

FAM98A overexpression partially reverses kaempferol-mediated inhibition of SK-ES-1 cell proliferation, migration, and invasion via the EGFR/PI3K/Akt/NF-κB pathway

To further elucidate the role of FAM98A in kaempferol-mediated inhibition of SK-ES-1 cells via miR-26b-5p regulation, we conducted gain-of-function experiments by transiently transfecting SK-ES-1 cells with either a FAM98A recombinant expression vector (FAM98A group) or an empty vector control (EV group), followed by kaempferol treatment. qRT-PCR confirmed the transfection efficiency (Fig. 4A). CCK-8 assays demonstrated that kaempferol significantly suppressed SK-ES-1 cell proliferation, but this inhibitory effect was partially counteracted upon FAM98A overexpression (Fig. 4B). Moreover, transwell migration assays revealed that kaempferol treatment markedly reduced SK-ES-1 cell migration compared to the control group, whereas FAM98A upregulation reversed this suppression (Fig. 4C and D). Similarly, kaempferol inhibited SK-ES-1 cell invasion, but this effect was partially attenuated following FAM98A overexpression (Fig. 4E and F). These results indicate that kaempferol exerts its inhibitory effects on SK-ES-1 cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, at least in part, through FAM98A regulation.

Western blot analysis further demonstrated that kaempferol treatment significantly decreased the phosphorylation levels of EGFR, PI3K, and Akt (p-EGFR, p-PI3K, and p-Akt, respectively) in SK-ES-1 cells without altering their total protein expression. Notably, FAM98A overexpression restored the aberrant activation of the EGFR/PI3K/Akt pathway induced by kaempferol (Fig. 4G and H). Additionally, nuclear protein extraction assays showed that kaempferol reduced nuclear NF-κB p65 levels, and this effect was mitigated by FAM98A upregulation (Fig. 4G and H). Collectively, these findings provide compelling evidence that FAM98A plays a critical role in kaempferol-mediated suppression of ES progression. Our study further clarifies the mechanistic basis of kaempferol’s antitumor effects, highlighting its regulation of the miR-26b-5p/FAM98A/EGFR/PI3K/Akt/NF-κB signaling axis in ES cells.

Kaempferol suppresses ES tumor growth in vivo by regulating miR-26b-5p

To further investigate the effects of kaempferol and miR-26b-5p on ES tumor growth in vivo, we established a xenograft mouse model by subcutaneously injecting SK-ES-1 cells (Figs. 5A). The control group received no treatment, the kaempferol group was administered kaempferol alone (20 mg/kg), and the kaempferol + AntagomiR-26b-5p group received both kaempferol (20 mg/kg) and AntagomiR-26b-5p. The in vivo experimental results showed that, compared with the control group, the tumor weight and size in the kaempferol group were significantly reduced. However, in the kaempferol + AntagomiR-26b-5p group, although tumor weight and size were smaller than those in the control group, they were significantly increased compared with the kaempferol group (Fig. 5B and C). In summary, kaempferol inhibited subcutaneous tumor growth, while downregulation of miR-26b-5p promoted their growth. These findings demonstrate that the inhibitory effect of kaempferol on ES tumor growth in vivo is associated with miR-26b-5p.

Discussion

In recent years, although the 5-year survival rate of localized ES patients has significantly improved due to advances in multimodal therapy, the prognosis for metastatic or recurrent ES remains poor, necessitating the development of novel adjuvant therapies with high efficacy and low toxicity. This study discovered that the natural flavonoid kaempferol significantly inhibits proliferation, migration, and invasion of SK-ES-1 cells while attenuating tumor growth in vivo, demonstrating therapeutic potential against ES. Mechanistic investigations revealed that kaempferol’s anti-ES effects are closely associated with its upregulation of miR-26b-5p expression: compared with normal osteoblasts (hFOB), SK-ES-1 cells exhibited reduced miR-26b-5p levels, which were markedly elevated upon kaempferol treatment. Rescue experiments confirmed that miR-26b-5p knockdown partially reversed kaempferol’s inhibitory effects, indicating its pivotal role in this process. Notably, miR-26b exhibits dual regulatory characteristics in cancers, demonstrating tumor-suppressive functions (e.g., in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma [23] and multiple myeloma [24]) while potentially promoting progression (e.g., in pituitary adenomas [25]). This study reveals miR-26b-5p’s likely tumor-suppressive role in ES, yet its incomplete reversal of kaempferol’s effects suggests the compound’s multi-target mechanism - consistent with previous reports that kaempferol modulates various miRNAs (including miR-195/497 [12], miR-26a-5p [26], and miR-155 [27]) to influence diverse disease processes.

Further studies revealed that FAM98A may function as a downstream target of miR-26b-5p, which is aberrantly upregulated in ES cells and associated with the EGFR/PI3K/AKT/NF-κB signaling pathway. Experimental validation demonstrated that kaempferol treatment reduced the levels of p-EGFR, p-PI3K, p-AKT, and nuclear NF-κB p65, while inhibition of miR-26b-5p or upregulation of FAM98A reversed this effect, indicating that kaempferol suppresses ES progression via the miR-26b-5p/FAM98A/EGFR/PI3K/AKT/NF-κB axis. Notably, even in miR-26b-5p-knockdown cells, kaempferol retained its direct inhibitory effect on the EGFR/PI3K/AKT/NF-κB cascade, suggesting a dual mechanism of action involving both miR-26b-5p-dependent and -independent pathways. This multi-target property may contribute to synergistic therapeutic efficacy. Although the PI3K/AKT/NF-κB axis is a known target of kaempferol in other cancers [28], this study is the first to elucidate its specific regulatory mechanism in ES through the miR-26b-5p/FAM98A node. The aberrant overexpression of FAM98A and its functional relevance distinguish this finding from studies in colorectal cancer [29] or lung cancer [30], providing novel translational insights for developing low-toxicity adjuvant therapies against metastatic ES. Future studies should further dissect these parallel pathways to optimize combination treatment strategies.

Kaempferol exhibits significant selective cytotoxicity toward tumor cells. Its IC50 value against SK-ES-1 cells (76.48 µM) was lower than that for osteosarcoma U-2OS cells (148.36 µM), while the IC50 for normal osteoblasts (hFOB) exceeded 200 µM [8], indicating its stronger cytotoxic effect on tumor cells. Moreover, previous studies support the safety of kaempferol, demonstrating good biocompatibility in various in vitro and in vivo models [31, 32], providing a safety basis for its clinical application. However, although kaempferol has shown anti-ES activity in vitro and in animal models, its clinical translation requires more comprehensive evaluations, including pharmacokinetics, long-term toxicity, and maximum tolerated dose studies. Compared with existing standard ES treatment regimens (e.g., vincristine/doxorubicin/cyclophosphamide combination therapy), kaempferol may present lower risks of cardiotoxicity and myelosuppression, but its efficacy still needs to be directly compared with chemotherapeutic agents in ES models. Additionally, other natural compounds (e.g., Toosendanin [33], magnolol [34]) also exert anti-ES effects through different pathways, yet kaempferol’s unique miR-26b-5p/FAM98A regulatory mechanism confers potential advantages. Future studies should systematically compare the efficacy, toxicity, and mechanisms of these natural agents to optimize combination therapy strategies.

Although kaempferol exhibits anti-ES potential, its clinical application still faces multiple challenges. Firstly, its poor aqueous solubility, rapid metabolism, and low oral bioavailability (approximately 2%) limit therapeutic efficacy [35]. To improve delivery efficiency, researchers have developed various strategies. For instance, phospholipid complexes (KP-PC) enhance solubility and absorption, increasing peak plasma concentration (Cmax) and area under the curve (AUC) by approximately 3-fold [36]. Nanoformulations (e.g., liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles) can improve stability and tumor targeting [37]. Additionally, kaempferol exhibits a hormesis effect, demonstrating optimal activity at 10–50 mg/kg, while higher doses may induce toxicity, necessitating precise dosing [38]. Another critical issue is potential drug-drug interactions. Kaempferol inhibits CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein, which may affect the metabolism of co-administered drugs (e.g., nifedipine), requiring dose adjustments to avoid adverse effects [39]. Furthermore, although miRNA-based therapies offer multi-target regulatory potential, they still face challenges such as off-target effects, limitations in delivery systems (e.g., immunogenicity, poor tumor penetration), and TLR-mediated immunotoxicity [40]. Future research should focus on optimizing delivery systems (e.g., targeted nanocarriers) and exploring combination strategies (e.g., kaempferol with miRNA modulators) to enhance therapeutic efficacy while minimizing side effects.

Although this study revealed the potential mechanism by which kaempferol inhibits ES through regulating the miR-26b-5p/FAM98A/EGFR/PI3K/AKT/NF-κB signaling axis, several limitations remain. First, the experiments were conducted only in the SK-ES-1 cell line, and future studies should validate these findings in different ES cell models and primary cells. Second, the tumor tissues from in vivo experiments were not subjected to Western blot or immunohistochemical analysis, which limits further mechanistic validation. Additionally, due to the lack of human ES tissues and paired adjacent non-tumor samples, we analyzed the expression of FAM98A and miR-26b-5p in public databases (e.g., TCGA, GEO) but found no significant differences. This may be attributed to insufficient sample size, tumor heterogeneity, or technical variations, necessitating further validation in larger, well-annotated clinical cohorts to assess their clinical relevance. Although the data support that kaempferol upregulates miR-26b-5p, the precise regulatory mechanisms (e.g., transcriptional activation or epigenetic modifications) still require further elucidation through ChIP-seq or methylation sequencing. Moreover, NF-κB nuclear translocation was assessed only by nuclear fractionation; future studies should employ immunofluorescence co-staining for more accurate subcellular localization analysis. Finally, this study did not validate the regulatory role of this signaling axis in metastasis models, which will be a key focus of subsequent research. These limitations provide clear directions for future studies to comprehensively elucidate the anti-metastatic mechanism of kaempferol in ES.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings demonstrate that kaempferol’s regulation of miR-26b-5p plays a pivotal role in ES progression, which is associated with FAM98A and the downstream EGFR/PI3K/Akt/NF-κB signaling pathway. Kaempferol may serve as a novel candidate therapeutic agent for ES.
