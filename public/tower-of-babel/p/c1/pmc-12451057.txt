Temporal Bone Carcinoma Masquerading as Complicated Chronic Otitis Media
Authors: Sushil Raj Shrestha, Manu Malhotra, Abhishek Bhardwaj, P S Suji, Sajjad Ahmed Khan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis case highlights the importance of early recognition of chronic ear discharge, facial nerve palsy, and swelling as potential indicators of aggressive conditions like squamous cell carcinoma. Future research should focus on improving early diagnostic techniques and ensuring continuity of care in advanced malignancies.Keywords: chronic ear discharge, facial nerve palsy, palliative treatment, squamous cell carcinoma, temporal bone erosion

Introduction

Temporal bone carcinoma (TBC) is a rare type of cancer that occurs in the head and neck, accounting for less than 0.2% of cases. The annual estimate of this pathology is 1.3 cases per million population [1]. The most common type is squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), with the pinna being the most common subsite. It is more common in people in their 70s, with a slightly higher incidence in males. The most prevalent types of cancer in these regions are SCC and basal cell carcinoma [2, 3].

Long‐standing COM, previous radiation therapy (RT), and exposure to sunlight are the main causes of this cancer. These factors lead to chronic inflammation of the middle ear mucosa, which can lead to metaplasia [4]. The clinical presentation of TBC is non‐specific, such as otorrhea and hearing loss. However, certain red flag signs like bloody ear discharge, ear pain, and facial weakness should raise suspicion of cancer. This can be challenging because these symptoms can also occur in complicated COM, leading to a delay in diagnosis [5]. A delayed diagnosis can worsen the prognosis and survival rates by allowing the cancer to progress. The stage of the tumor is an important factor in determining the course of the disease.

The main treatment is extensive surgical removal of the tumor since it tends to spread locally rather than to nearby lymph nodes or distant sites. However, even after surgery, there is a high rate of local recurrence, and salvage therapies have limited success [1]. 5‐year survival rates range from 10% for advanced disease to 83% for early disease [6]. There are various staging systems, and the Arriga et al. classification for the University of Pittsburgh is one of the accepted ones [7]. The presence of spread to lymph nodes or distant organs significantly worsens the prognosis and automatically upstages the tumor to a more advanced stage IV.

Case Report

Case History/Examination

A 15‐year‐old male presented to the Otorhinolaryngology outpatient department with a complex history of right ear‐related symptoms over the past decade. The primary complaint was a 10‐year history of ear discharge, initially profuse and copious, but which had gradually become scanty and foul‐smelling over the past year. Blood‐stained discharge was noted for the past 6 months. Alongside this, the patient developed a progressively enlarging swelling behind the right ear over the past 3 months, which was associated with severe otalgia (ear pain) for the last 2 months. The patient also reported a gradual loss of hearing in the right ear over the past 8 years. These symptoms were accompanied by intermittent high‐grade fever and headaches that had recently worsened over the last 2 weeks. Notably, the patient developed a left‐sided facial deviation, which raised concern about possible cranial nerve involvement. He had not seen any physician within the years due to negligence.

The patient's medical history was unremarkable, and there was no significant family history of similar complaints or known hereditary conditions. Upon clinical examination, the patient's general condition was stable, and his mental status was intact. He was afebrile but appeared mildly cachectic (weight loss) as a result of chronic illness. There was no alteration in his sensorium. A focused physical examination revealed right‐sided grade‐V lower motor neuron facial nerve palsy, as per the House‐Brackmann classification, indicating significant facial nerve dysfunction. The examination of the ear revealed a reddish, firm mass occupying the entire right external auditory canal (EAC), extending towards the meatus (Figure 1). The mass was tender to touch and had a tendency to bleed upon palpation. The post‐aural swelling was also firm and tender with a local rise in temperature, suggesting an inflammatory component.

Reddish, firm mass occupying the right external auditory canal.

On audiological assessment, pure tone audiometry showed moderate to severe hearing loss in the right ear, but bone conduction testing could not be performed due to tenderness associated with the post‐aural swelling. Visual inspection of the tympanic membrane was not possible because of the mass obstructing the view of the ear canal and middle ear structures. There were no other significant findings on local or systemic examination.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment)

Given the complex and progressive nature of the patient's symptoms, several differential diagnoses were considered, including chronic suppurative otitis media (CSOM), cholesteatoma, benign or malignant external auditory canal tumors, and malignant otitis externa. The patient's longstanding ear discharge, the new development of facial nerve palsy, and the significant mass effect raised concern for an aggressive, locally invasive pathology such as squamous cell carcinoma (SCC).

To evaluate the extent and nature of the mass, high‐resolution computed tomography (HRCT) of the temporal bone was performed. The HRCT revealed a large soft tissue mass measuring 6.7 × 5.4 × 7.2 cm within the right external auditory canal, with extensive erosion of the surrounding bony structures, including the mastoid, squamous, and petrous temporal bones. The mass also involved the external, middle, and internal ear structures. The presence of bony fragments within the lesion further suggested the invasive and destructive nature of the pathology.

A subsequent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan of the brain and temporal bone was performed to assess the intracranial extension of the lesion. MRI confirmed that the mass had extended beyond the temporal bone, invading the middle ear cavity, mastoid antrum, and mastoid air cells (Figure 2). The lesion also extended intracranially, reaching the floor of the right middle cranial fossa. This intracranial extension caused mild compression and displacement of the right temporal lobe, with enhancement of the overlying dura, indicating tumor involvement. These imaging findings were consistent with an aggressive, locally advanced malignancy.

A large right‐sided soft tissue mass (6.7 × 5.4 × 7.2 cm) with erosion of mastoid, squamous, and petrous temporal bone, involving external, middle, and inner ear structures (A and B). Right EAC heterogeneous soft tissue mass with middle ear and mastoid invasion, intracranial extension to middle cranial fossa, mild temporal lobe compression, and dural enhancement (C and D).

To confirm the diagnosis, a biopsy was taken from the right EAC mass. Histopathological examination (HPE) of the biopsy sample revealed a diagnosis of squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). The findings included hyperplastic squamous epithelium with marked dysplasia, angulated tumor cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm, significant pleomorphism, hyperchromasia, and the presence of atypical mitotic figures, all consistent with a diagnosis of SCC.

Given the extensive nature of the disease and the involvement of both temporal bone structures and intracranial extension, the patient was referred to the radiation and medical oncology departments for palliative treatment. A multidisciplinary approach involving surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy was discussed, but due to the advanced stage of the disease and the patient's poor prognosis, the focus was on palliative care rather than curative treatment.

Results and Conclusions (Outcomes and Follow‐Up)

The diagnosis of squamous cell carcinoma of the external auditory canal with extensive local and intracranial invasion was confirmed through histopathology and imaging. The HRCT and MRI scans demonstrated the aggressive nature of the tumor, with significant bone erosion and intracranial extension, which were consistent with an advanced‐stage malignancy. The patient's hearing loss, facial nerve palsy, and post‐aural swelling were all consequences of the tumor's growth and invasion of surrounding structures.

Given the advanced nature of the disease, a palliative treatment plan was recommended, which included radiation therapy to control symptoms and reduce tumor burden. The patient was referred to the radiation and oncology departments for further management. However, despite thorough counseling and the availability of palliative options, the patient was unfortunately lost to follow‐up after this initial referral. Attempts to contact the patient for further follow‐up were unsuccessful, and no additional treatment or supportive care was provided.

This case underscores the importance of early diagnosis and timely intervention in cases of chronic ear discharge, facial nerve palsy, and swelling, which can be indicative of more serious, aggressive pathologies like squamous cell carcinoma. The patient's presentation, including chronic otorrhea, hearing loss, and facial nerve involvement, should prompt suspicion of a malignant process. Early intervention could have potentially improved the patient's prognosis and quality of life, particularly in such advanced cases.

The lack of follow‐up care highlights a significant challenge in managing complex cases, as patients often face barriers to consistent care, whether due to socio‐economic reasons, psychological factors, or logistical challenges. This case serves as a reminder of the critical need for a comprehensive and coordinated approach to managing advanced malignancies and the importance of ensuring continuity of care for optimal patient outcomes.

Discussion

SCC of the temporal bone and middle ear is a rare malignancy but is associated with considerable morbidity and mortality, accounting for 60%–80% of all temporal bone malignancies, followed by basal cell carcinoma [8]. There is no universally accepted staging system for TBC. The Pittsburgh staging is based on radiographic findings and has been successfully correlated with both clinical outcomes and the HPE of the temporal bones involved [6].

Due to their rarity, the etiology of these tumors is poorly understood, which hinders their performance in risk assessment studies. COM is a risk factor for TBC, especially SCC [9]. Similar to our case, early symptoms include purulent, foul‐smelling otorrhea, severe otalgia, bleeding, and itching. Differentiating between a chronically draining ear associated with inflammation and malignancy is usually tricky due to its rare incidence. Hearing loss, headache, tinnitus, vertigo, and aural fullness are other malignancy‐related symptoms that can present. There could be cranial nerve palsies. Trismus can ensue from spreading into the glenoid fossa, and dural involvement can cause excruciating pain and headache [8].

Of particular note is the patient's age—15 years—which is strikingly rare for a squamous cell carcinoma of the temporal bone. The literature overwhelmingly reports SCC‐TB in older adults, typically in the fifth to seventh decades of life. This makes our case exceptional. The manuscript previously stated no identifiable risk factors were found; however, in such a young patient, the possibility of underlying predisposing conditions such as genetic syndromes (e.g., Fanconi anemia, xeroderma pigmentosum), immunosuppression, or significant environmental exposures (e.g., chronic radiation or industrial carcinogen exposure) should be discussed. The absence of such factors should be explicitly evaluated and documented. Simply stating that no risk factors were identified underrepresents the significance of this age‐related anomaly.

Additionally, although the case references the Pittsburgh classification system for temporal bone carcinomas, it does not assign a specific stage to the tumor. Given the availability of detailed imaging and anatomical findings—including facial nerve involvement and extensive bony erosion—applying the Pittsburgh staging would have provided valuable clinical context and prognostic insight. Omitting this weakens the clinical framing and makes it difficult to assess the disease severity in standardized terms.

While the histopathological diagnosis of SCC is adequately described, the biopsy technique used to obtain the diagnostic tissue is not mentioned. Given the deep and delicate location of the tumor, the method of biopsy—whether via transcanal, postauricular, endoscopic, or open approach—should be specified to provide a complete clinical picture and to inform future similar cases.

Moreover, the patient was lost to follow‐up and did not receive treatment, thereby restricting this report to the diagnostic phase. This highlights the critical need for early suspicion and diagnosis in similar presentations. Over the 10‐year history of symptoms—namely chronic otorrhea and progressive hearing loss—certain red flags may have been overlooked. These include persistent otalgia, facial nerve dysfunction, and failure to respond to multiple courses of antibiotics. An earlier high‐resolution CT or MRI could have revealed destructive changes earlier, allowing timely intervention. Greater awareness among primary care physicians and otolaryngologists is essential in cases of refractory chronic ear infections with atypical features, especially in younger patients.

Bloody or serosanguinous discharge, persistent ear infections refractory to appropriate antibiotic treatment, and other symptoms should be examined with a suspicion of malignancy. Facial nerve palsy, other cranial nerve palsies, or headaches should be treated, considering the possibility of malignancy in such a background. HRCT scans show the bony anatomy of the temporal bone and demonstrate the extent of bony erosion [7]. As previously stated, the facial nerve in this case was grade‐V at the time of presentation.

Classic surgeries include sleeve resection, lateral temporal bone resection, subtotal temporal bone resection, and total temporal bone resection. In contrast, others include total en bloc removal of the temporal bone surrounding the tumor and piecemeal removal of the gross tumor while preserving vital neurovascular structures, followed by RT [10], showing similar survival of 50%, 48.6%, and 50% in a retrospective review of 144 patients [11]. He was referred to the oncology team for palliative chemo‐radiotherapy. He did not, however, seek medical attention.

The disease's prognosis is often poor and worsens when the facial nerve is paralyzed, the tumor margins are positive, the dura is involved, and the regional lymph nodes are involved [8]. However, in our case, lymph node involvement was absent.

Conclusion

TBC are rare tumors in the head and neck region, with SCC being the most common type and the pinna being the frequently affected subsite. The challenge lies in differentiating these malignancies from complicated COM, as they share similar symptoms like blood‐stained discharge, ear pain, and facial palsy. It is important for healthcare professionals to have a high level of suspicion for malignancy in such cases, as early detection and intervention can potentially improve survival outcomes.
