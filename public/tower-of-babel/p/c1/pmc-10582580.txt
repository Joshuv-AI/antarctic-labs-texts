Study on acoustic properties of hydrate-bearing sediments with reconstructed CO2 hydrate in different layers during CH4 hydrate mining
Authors: Yi-Jian Zhu, Xing Huang, Hao Li, Yu-jie Zhu, Xiao-Hui Wang, Yi-Fei Sun, Peng Xiao, Chang-Yu Sun, Guang-Jin Chen
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: CH4 / CO2 hydrate, Reservoir reconstruct, P-wave velocity, Elastic modulus, Subsidence

Introduction

Natural gas hydrates, non-stoichiometric envelope compounds, are mainly formed by CH4 and water molecules under suitable low temperature and high pressure conditions, which can be found in permafrost regions and deep marine sediments [1]. As a clean energy source, NGHs have high energy density and large reserves. Moreover, 160–180 m3 of CH4 can be stored in the 1 m3 of NGHs, and there are around 3 × 1015 m3 CH4 (STP) overall in NGHs which contain twice as much organic carbon as known fossil fuel (coal, oil, and natural gas) reserves [2]. It is vital to achieve commercial hydrates exploitation and carbon dioxide sequestration due to the rising worldwide need for renewable energy and the increasing greenhouse effect.

Nowadays, relatively mature hydrate mining techniques were widely studied in the laboratory including depressurization [3], [4], [5], heat stimulation [6], [7], inhibitor injection [8], [9], [10], and CO2 replacement [11], [12], [13]. Due to its ease of use and low energy consumption, depressurization was frequently employed in hydrate mining. However, many issues had been revealed in the laboratory and on-site NGHs mining, such as sand production, reservoir instability, and subsidence, which could result in serious geological disasters and lower gas production efficiency [14]. The gas production efficiency was further decreased by the reformation of hydrates from water production that clogged wellbores and channels. When developing enhanced hydrate recovery, concerns that could result in geologic risks, like reservoir stability and subsidence during hydrate mining should be strengthened. The famous Storgga landslide, Amazon landslide, Gisborne Coast, as well as the Agadir Basin landslide, and Cape Blanc landslides were closely related to NGHs decomposition [15], [16]. In addition, the reservoir collapse phenomenon, subsidence law, reservoir stiffness weakening, and other phenomena were also discovered and investigated in the researchers' simulation calculations and experiments [17], [18], [19], [20]. Large volumes of hydrate cementing and supporting influence on the sediment was known to increase the reservoir stability [21], [22], [23]. Therefore, it is difficult to ensure the stability of the reservoir without the intervention of other methods, and it is unknown whether the existing efficient exploitation methods can guarantee the smooth exploitation of the whole gas production process. CO2-CH4 replacement was recognized by researchers as a safe mining method, but the barrier to its commercial application was its extremely low mining efficiency [24]. To enhance the replacement process, N2/H2 was mixed with CO2 to encourage the decomposition of CH4 hydrate by reducing its partial pressure [12]. Research results showed that the mixture gas replacement rate can be as high as 85 %, and up to 90 % of CH4 could be recovered [11], [25]. Production safety depended on the ability to maintain formation stability during the replacement process, yet there was a dearth of pertinent research in this area.

As a novel method, the artificial impermeable CO2 hydrate cap was built to prevent overlying water incursion and to considerably enhance the gas-to-water ratio [26]. By injecting CO2 emulsion into the overburden in a one-dimensional tubular reactor before CH4 hydrate mining, the feasibility of this method was effectively proven. The results showed that the gas–water ratio and CH4 recovery ratio of hydrate mining with CO2 hydrate cap were much higher than that without CO2 hydrate cap. The CO2 hydrate cap in the experiment could withstand a maximum pressure difference of 4 MPa before finally returning to equilibrium. The method might not succeed in a large-scale mining environment since a tiny tubular reactor could easily construct a closed mining environment. The fabrication of a CO2 hydrate cap in a larger 3-D cylindrical reactor with liquid CO2 served to further confirm the viability of this method [27]. However, during CH4 hydrate mining, it was unknown if the CO2 hydrate cap was mechanically stable and whether the reservoir as a whole was stable. NGHs were often deposited in submarine sediments about 900–1500 m of water depth, and the sediments were subjected to significant amounts of stress [28], [29], [30]. There will be significant challenges to the mechanical stability of CO2 hydrate cap and pressure-bearing capabilities.

For effective reservoir protection, it is crucial to have real-time knowledge of how reservoir stability changes while CH4 hydrates are mined. In the form of strain or stress data, triaxial shear or direct shear tests revealed changes in reservoir mechanical properties during hydrate decomposition, but the apparatus itself prevents them from being employed in the field area [31], [32], [33]. As a mature technology, ultrasound has been widely used in the chemical industry to promote chemical reactions such as emulsification, polymerization, etc. [34], [35], [36], [37], [38], [39], [40]. Acoustic cavitation phenomena is produced by low frequency ultrasound, and the energy and heat generated at the moment of bubble collapse can promote various physical and chemical processes [41]. P-wave as a type of ultrasound waves are widely used in the field of gas hydrate research. The application of ultrasound waves in gas hydrates was listed in Table 1, which contributed to the achievements of SDGs. The P-wave instrumentation is cost-effective, user-friendly, and possesses the ability to provide approximate seismic exploration and development estimations, as well as precise solution density calculations for further refinement [42], [43]. The constant production of hydrate that was cemented to the sediment improves its overall strength, and the P-wave velocity rose as hydrate saturation did [44]. Consequently, rather than using complicated CT or triaxial testing, P-wave velocity can be utilized as an indirect way to simple reflect the mechanical strength of hydrate deposits. Wang et al. estimated the elastic modulus of reservoir using the P-wave velocity from the CO2-CH4 displacement experiment and discovered that the P-wave velocity was still decreasing [45]. Ren et al. exploited hydrate below the freezing point in order to ensure reservoir stability during hydrate exploitation [46]. The experimental results showed that the P-wave velocity was relatively constant during hydrate exploitation, the elastic modulus of the reservoir was stable, indicating that the stiffness of the reservoir was still stable, but the axial pressure was small, making it challenging to simulate the actual stress environment. In our earlier study, CH4 hydrate was mined utilizing CO2/N2 below the freezing point under 10 MPa stress conditions, which significantly reduced reservoir subsidence and increased the stiffness of the reservoir, while also enhancing the recovery of hydrate [47]. There were some restrictions on the setting where this technology may be used. Ensuring reservoir stability while efficiently exploiting hydrates is a top priority for future hydrate development. In this work, the P-wave velocity, elastic modulus, and reservoir subsidence of different layers are measured in situ in order to reflect the reservoir stability during CH4 hydrate exploitation. A safe and efficient hydrate exploitation scheme using reservoir reconstruction in different layers and various hydrate exploitation methods were investigated, which could act as guidelines for future commercial hydrate exploitation.

Experiments

Apparatus and materials

The schematic diagram of the experimental apparatus is shown in Fig. 1a. It mainly consists of six parts: gas injection system, reaction system, temperature control system, axial load system, gas recovery system, and data collection system. The main body of reactor was constructed of 316 stainless steel, which had a 3.25 L capacity and a maximum working pressure of 20 MPa. The temperature, pressure, and ultrasonic probes were placed longitudinally on both sides of the reactor, and the locations of the probes and the interior design of the reactor are shown in Fig. 1b. The accuracy of temperature probes, pressure probes, and ultrasonic probes was ±0.1 K, ±0.01 MPa, and 5 m/s, respectively. The frequency of the ultrasonic probe was 100 KHz. Both a gas cylinder and a buffer tank made up the gas injection system. The low-temperature water bath regulated the overall temperature of the system with an accuracy of 0.01 K. The hand pump was selected as the principal component of the axial pressure loading system to load overlying stresses on the sediment. The gas recovery system consisted of a backpressure valve, a sand filter, a gas–liquid separator, and a gas recovery cylinder. The temperature, pressure data, and displacement data were gathered by the computer from the experimental process, and the ultrasonic data was analyzed in real time by a specialized device.

CH4, N2, CO2 with a purity of 99.99 mol%, and the CO2/N2 gas mixture were supplied by Beijing Haipu Gas Company. Quartz sands used as the HBS in the experiments were supplied by Yifeng Quartz sands Co. The sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) was purchased from Energy Chemical. The deionized water was prepared in our laboratory.

Experimental procedures

In order to relieve reservoir subsidence and enhance reservoir stability during CH4 hydrate mining, CO2 hydrate reservoirs were constructed above or below the CH4 hydrate, respectively, before CH4 hydrate mining. The CO2/N2 injection (50:50 mol%, 80:20 mol%) and depressurization methods of hydrate mining were used to compare the features of reservoir subsidence and changes in reservoir stability, respectively. The schematic diagram of the experimental process was shown in Fig. 2.

Schematic diagram of the whole experiment.

The main specific experimental procedures were as follows:

CH4 hydrate formation stage （A）: The pre-cooled quartz sand was separated into two quantitative parts, one of which was fully mixed with a quantitative amount of ice water to prepare CH4 hydrate-bearing sediments. The dry sand part would become a reconstructed reservoir. Then the dry sand and wet sand were put into the reactor in a certain order. After the leakage test, CH4 was injected into the reactor at room temperature to produce a pressure of about 8.3 MPa for rapid hydrate formation, while the equilibrium pressure of CH4 hydrate at 275.15 K was 3.111 MPa [52]. When the temperature and pressure in the reactor were stabilized, the CH4 hydrate-bearing sediments were regarded as finished. Reservoir pressure will be reduced to about 0.5 MPa above the CH4 hydrate phase equilibrium pressure.

Reservoir reconstruction (B1) and axial pressure loading stage (B2) (B): In order to construct a high quality and highly saturated CO2 hydrate reservoir, 1000 ppm of pre-cooled SDS solution was preferred and injected into the dry sand reservoir at the corresponding location [53]. Following the injection of SDS, gaseous CO2 was slowly injected into the reactor to quickly sweep the reconstruction reservoir and allowed the CH4 gas to be blown away, preventing the excessive formation of CO2-CH4 hydrate. The gaseous CO2 was repeatedly injected into reactor until the rate of CO2 hydrate formation was really low under this condition. The slow reduction in reservoir pressure indicated the end of stage B1. Then, a 10 MPa overlying stress was applied to the entire hydrate reservoir, and the steady axial pressure indicated the end of stage B2.

CH4 hydrate mining stage (C) & (SR): In this stage, depressurization (C) and CO2/N2 (50:50 mol%, 80:20 mol%) injection (SR) had been used to mine CH4 hydrate, respectively. For depressurization mining (C), the pressure of the backpressure valve was adjusted to 2.3 MPa which was lower than the CH4 hydrate phase equilibrium pressure (3.111 MPa) at 275.15 K and higher than the CO2 hydrate phase equilibrium pressure (1.573 MPa). When the temperature and pressure in the reactor were stabilized and the pressure in the gas recovery cylinder no longer increased, the experiment of depressurization mining was completed. For CO2/N2 injection mining (SR), the backpressure valve was adjusted to 2.3 MPa. After sufficient CO2/N2 sweeping, the mining well valve was closed. The gas in the reactor and gas production were analyzed by gas chromatography (GC, Agilent 7890B). Based on the requirement that the CH4 content in the reactor no longer raised, the next round sweeping would be conducted. The end of the experiment was marked when the gas content in the reactor no longer changed after the CO2/N2 injection sweeping. The difference between the two different compositions of CO2/N2 was that the partial pressure of CO2/N2 (50:50 mol%) was lower than the phase equilibrium pressure of CO2 hydrate, while CO2/N2 (80:20 mol%) was higher than the phase equilibrium pressure of CO2 hydrate after sufficient sweeping.

Results and discussion

In this work, a series of experiments were carried out to investigate the hydrate reservoir stability of different layers during CH4 hydrate depressurization or CO2/N2 injection mining after CO2 hydrate reservoir reformation at different layers. The specific experimental conditions were listed in Table 2. The experiments used the P-wave velocity measurement device to measure the P-wave velocity of different layers in situ, and the stability of the entire reservoir was assessed using the P-wave velocity and the subsidence characteristics of the entire reservoir. The mechanical properties of the reservoir changed more obviously when the elastic modulus was calculated from the P-wave velocity. The below-reservoir was reconstructed in Run 1 and Run 2, the above-reservoir was reconstructed in Run 3, and the above and below reservoirs were reconstructed in Run 4 to investigate the change of acoustic properties, mechanical strength of each reservoir during CH4 hydrate depressurization mining. In addition, the role of the reconstructed reservoirs at different layers on the overall reservoir stability and reservoir subsidence was also investigated. For Run 5 and Run 6, different compositions of CO2/N2 injection combined with above-reservoir reconstruction were used to mine CH4 hydrate, and the advantages and disadvantages of gas injection for reservoir stability and subsidence were investigated during CH4 hydrate mining.

Below-reservoir reconstruction combined with depressurization

Below-reservoir was reconstructed in Run 1 and Run 2, and reservoir stability was compared in these two Runs by immediate depressurization mining (Run 1) or depressurization mining after stabilization for a period of time (Run 2). The whole experimental process can be divided into four stages: CH4 hydrate formation stage (A), reservoir reconstruction (B1) and axial pressure loading stage (B2) (B), hydrate mining stage (C), and CO2 sequestration stage (D). In stage A, CH4 gas was injected into the reactor at room temperature to provide a pressure about ∼8.3 MPa, and the low-temperature water bath was raised to the experimental temperature. In the meantime, due to the significant exothermic of hydrate formation and the sluggish rise in low-temperature water bath, the temperature of the reactor gradually stabilized at the temperature needed for the experiment. With the continuous formation of hydrates during stage A, the hydrate sediment continuously cemented and consolidated by hydrates, the stiffness of the hydrate sediment rose, and the P-wave velocity rose as the hydrate saturation in the sediment rose. Fig. 3b showed that the P-wave velocity of the middle reservoir with a relatively high hydrate saturation reached 2778 m/s at the end of stage A, while the P-wave velocity of the below-reservoir with a relatively low CH4 hydrate saturation reached 2128 m/s in Run 1. The gravitational effect of water, which resulted in higher water saturation in the middle reservoir than in the top layer, was the primary cause of the difference in P-wave velocity of the CH4 hydrate layer. Additionally, more water and CH4 gas generated more hydrate, leading to higher hydrate saturation in the middle reservoir than in the above-reservoir. It should be noted that there was no water in the bottom layer and therefore no CH4 hydrate formation at this stage. 1000 ppm SDS solution was used in stage B1 to generate CO2 hydrate faster and more efficiently to reconstruct the below-reservoir. As shown in Fig. 3a, the below-reservoir temperature T1 rapidly increased from 274.65 K to 276.55 K when gaseous CO2 was injected into the below-reservoir. Accordingly, as CO2 hydrates continually formed, the P-wave velocity (VP1) rapidly raised from 1087 m/s to 3704 m/s. In stage B2, to replicate the overburden stress that the actual hydrate reservoir was subjected to, the entire reservoir was constantly compressed until the overburden pressure reached 10 MPa and remained stable. Due to the decrease in acoustic energy attenuation following axial pressure loading when the sediment and hydrate terminate more closely, the value of VP2 increased from 2778 m/s to 3448 m/s and the value of VP3 increased from 2083 m/s to 2273 m/s. In stage C, the pressure in the kettle quickly lowered to 2.3 MPa (at this point, the CH4 hydrate's phase equilibrium pressure was about 3.1 MPa). Meanwhile, the middle reservoir temperature T2 decreased from 274.95 K to 274.45 K and the top reservoir temperature T3 decreased from 275.25 K to 274.45 K due to the rapid decomposition of CH4 hydrate. The phase equilibrium pressure of CO2 hydrate at 275.15 K was about 1.573 MPa, so the bottom CO2 hydrate reservoir would not decompose at 2.3 MPa during the whole mining process. CH4 hydrate gradually lost its cementing and supporting properties due to continuing decomposition, and the water produced from this process further eroded and destabilized the reservoir's skeleton. After the end of stage C, the VP2 decreased by 2395 m/s from 3448 m/s to 1053 m/s, the VP3 decreased by 1068 m/s from 2273 m/s to 1205 m/s. The P-wave velocity of the bottom CO2 reservoir remained constant, and the reservoir stiffness remained stable during stage C, however, the CH4 hydrate reservoir became very unstable. In stage D, the greenhouse gas CO2 was injected into the reservoir to sequestrate and reconstruct the reservoir. Because of the “memory effect” caused by the presence of CH4 hydrate decomposition water[54], CO2 hydrate formed quickly, and the P-wave velocity and stratum stiffness of the reservoir quickly recover to their pre-methane hydrate mining states. It should be noted Run 2 was different from Run 1 in that the below-reservoir was adjusted to stabilize the entire system for a period of time rather than immediately going to depressurization and stage D was not conducted after the completion of hydrate mining. Comparing Fig. 3b and Fig. 3c, it could be found that the overall trends were similar, and the small differences in VP2 and VP3 in both experiments were due to the non-homogeneity of the hydrate. The VP1 in Run 1 was much higher than that in Run 2 after reservoir reconstruction at similar hydrate saturation, which might be due to the “wall climbing effect” of hydrate that made the hydrate contact with the probe better in Run 1 so that the acoustic energy attenuation was less. The hydrate melting at the acoustic probe in Run 1 after the injection of gaseous CO2 in the reservoir at room temperature restored the P-wave velocity to the normal value and confirmed the previous speculation. The P-wave velocity of the bottom CO2 hydrate reservoir remained stable during CH4 hydrate mining, and the higher reservoir stiffness could successfully prevent the collapse of the entire reservoir and served as a “foundation”.

Above-reservoir reconstruction combined with depressurization

The above-reservoir was reconstructed in Run 3 to investigate the mechanical stability of the CO2 hydrate cap and its important role in relieving reservoir subsidence. Methane hydrate was mined by depressurization under constant overlying stress of 10 MPa after reservoir reconstruction. Fig. 4a illustrates the variation of temperature and pressure for the above-reservoir reconstruction in Run 3. In addition to the different locations of the reconstructed reservoir, the trends of temperature and pressure in Fig. 4a are consistent with that in Fig. 3a. Although the CH4 hydrate saturation was roughly the same in each group of experiments, the amount of water migration during the formation process varied, and the various levels of water saturation in the various layers eventually resulted in some variations in the P-wave velocities of the various layers at the end of hydrate formation. At the end of stage A, the values of VP1 and VP2 were 2857 m/s, 2381 m/s, respectively. The aging of the hydrate at the probe was the cause of the various decreases in VP1 and VP2 in stage B1, which was consistent with the phenomenon described in the literature [21]. At the end of stage B2, the P-wave velocity values of the reservoir from top to bottom were 2703 m/s, 3030 m/s, and 2857 m/s respectively. The top reconstructed CO2 hydrate reservoir, in contrast to the bottom reconstructed reservoir, exhibited almost the same P-wave velocity fall pattern as the CH4 hydrate reservoir during Stage C. The VP3 value of the reconstructed reservoir decreased by 481 m/s from 2703 m/s to 2222 m/s in 73 min. This was primarily caused by an increase in shear stress on the above-reservoir following the decrease in pore pressure, which damaged the reconstructed reservoir by shear and caused a sharp decrease in its P-wave velocity. The decomposed CH4 gas penetrated the top CO2 cap through the fissure caused by the shear damage. The phase equilibrium of CO2 hydrate was broken which caused decomposition of CO2 hydrate, as a result, the VP3 decreased continuously.

Variation of temperature and pressure for the above-reservoir reconstruction (a), P-wave velocity (b) for Run 3 with time in the whole experimental process.

Above & below reservoir reconstruction combined with depressurization

Reservoir reconstructions were conducted on both the above and below CH4 hydrate reservoirs to contrast their respective reservoir stability during CH4 hydrate mining. In Run 4, CH4 hydrate reservoirs were only present in the middle reservoir. In order to prolong the hydrate mining procedure and assessed the stability of the reformed above and below reservoirs, higher saturated hydrate samples were prepared. Thus, the VP2 was 3333 m/s higher than that of other Runs before CH4 hydrate mining. Stages B1 and B1′, respectively, involved reservoirs reconstructed in the bottom and top layers in conjunction with Fig. 5a and Fig. 5b. Similar to Runs 1–3, only the relevant reservoir temperature and P-wave velocity responded to the reservoir reconstruction with a correspondingly enhanced response, the CH4 reservoir was unaffected. Due to multiple gas injections during the above-reservoir reconstruction, more water was forced to migrate downward. As a result, the VP1 was higher than the VP3 which was caused by the higher CO2 hydrate saturation. As shown in Fig. 5b, the VP3 decreased by 621 m/s in 212 min from 2439 m/s to1818 m/s during stage C, while the VP1 only decreased by 208 m/s from 3333 m/s to 3125 m/s. That is, the above-reservoir was subject to strong shear damage from overlying stresses while the below-reservoir was almost unaffected. In contrast to the above-reservoir, the VP1 gradually decreased from 3125 m/s to 1887 m/s as a result of the prolonged disturbance caused by decomposing CH4 gas. Compared to the top CO2 hydrate reservoir with complete loss of stiffness strength, the bottom CO2 hydrate reservoir still has some strength. The top reconstructed reservoir prevented the direct force from acting as a pressure-bearing role on the CH4 hydrate reservoir, which could, to a certain extent, reduced the large deformation of the CH4 hydrate reservoir to protect the mining equipment inside the reservoir and ensured smooth production. After the CH4 hydrate decomposition, the relatively steady strength of reconstructed below-reservoir could prevent reservoir collapse. The reservoir reconstructions at different layers contributed differently to the overall reservoir stability, but all played a pivotal role in reservoir stability during CH4 hydrate mining.

Above-reservoir reconstruction combined with depressurization & CO2/N2 injection

The results of above experiments showed that the reconstructed CO2 cap was essential to maintaining reservoir stability during CH4 hydrate mining. It successfully carried the enormous stress that the higher seawater caused and lowered the possibility of the CH4 hydrate reservoir deforming. However, the rigidity of the cap was severely compromised, as a result, the decomposition of CO2 hydrate in the cap caused by shear damage sustained, endangering the safety of the production process. It was reported that the gas (CO2/H2) injection method could effectively achieve water blocking and pressure reduction to enhance CH4 recovery while sequestering CO2 in a water-rich environment [17]. The mechanical stability of the reservoir during mining in the absence of significant overburden stress was unknown, similar to other recovery enhancement methods, which did not guarantee the successful implementation of the method in actual production.

To ensure the mechanical stability of the cap and the overall stability of the reservoir during CH4 hydrate mining, intermittent CO2/N2 (50:50 mol% in Run 5, 80:20 mol% in Run 6) injection was used. The stage A-B for Run 5 and Run 6 were identical to those for Run 3. During the sweeping reservoir (SR) stage, CO2/N2 was injected into the reservoir, and CH4 hydrate decomposition and CO2 hydrate formation might occur within the reservoir. The next sweeping reservoir would be performed when the CH4 gas content in the reservoir was not increasing or was increasing slowly. As shown in Fig. 6b and Fig. 6d, during the whole SR stage, the VP3 increased from 3704 m/s to 3846 m/s in Run 5, while the VP3 decreased from 3704 m/s to 3448 m/s in Run 6. This demonstrated that both CO2/N2 components could successfully prevent the top CO2 cap from shear damage to maintain its high stiffness strength in Run 5 and Run 6. Additionally, intermittent reservoir sweeping could remove CH4 gas in time to maintain CO2 concentration and prevent CO2 hydrate decomposition. The formation pressure of CO2 hydrate at 275.15 K was roughly 1.573 MPa. This meant that, in the CH4 hydrate layer, the two gas injection components had different functions during SR stage. As seen in Fig. 6b, because the partial pressure of CO2 in the reactor was about 1.15 MPa lower than the phase equilibrium partial pressure of CO2 hydrate, CO2/N2 (50:50 mol%) worked to lower the phase equilibrium partial pressure of CH4 hydrate and purged out the CH4 gas to encourage CH4 hydrate decomposition. Therefore, with the decomposition of CH4 hydrate, the VP2 decreased from 2857 m/s to 1667 m/s, and the VP1 decreased from 1887 m/s to 855 m/s in Run 5, respectively. Since the partial pressure of CO2 in Run 6 was about 1.84 MPa, which was greater than the phase equilibrium partial pressure of CO2 hydrate of 1.573 MPa, CO2/N2 played a role in reducing the partial pressure and replacing CH4 hydrate in the CH4 hydrate reservoir. During CO2-CH4 replacement and CH4 hydrate decomposition, the P-wave velocity did not vary much as a result of the formation of CO2 hydrate, and the stiffness strength of the reservoir was consistently steady.

Characteristics of elastic modulus of different layers during CH4 hydrate mining

Testing for elastic properties (an indirect method) and testing for mechanical strength (a direct method) were the two main techniques used to evaluate the mechanical characteristics of hydrate reservoirs [44]. Since the elasticity testing method was most likely used in actual hydrate mining, we conducted elasticity tests on hydrate reservoirs at different layers to assess changes in reservoir stability. The elastic modulus was calculated by M=ρmixVP2. The specifics of the calculation of the formula was described in detail in our reported work [47].

The variation of elastic modulus of different layers with time for Run 2, Run 3, Run 4 and Run 6 during CH4 hydrate mining are shown in Fig. 7. The trends of elastic modulus and P-wave velocity were roughly similar. As shown in Fig. 7a, the elastic modulus of bottom reconstructed CO2 hydrate reservoir was slowly rising from 12.15 GPa to 13.54 GPa during CH4 hydrate mining, while the elastic modulus of middle reservoir decreased from 15.52 GPa to 3.97 GPa and the elastic modulus of above-reservoir decreased from 9.27 GPa to 2.33 GPa. This was because the below-reservoir was less impacted by the shear stress above it, and the bottom CO2 hydrate reservoir was more compacted due to continuing compaction of the hydrate decomposition sediments. In contrast, the CH4 hydrate reservoir was negatively impacted by hydrate decomposition and was subject to stronger shear, which resulted in a significant loss of reservoir stiffness. As shown in Fig. 7b, the elastic modulus of above-reservoir slowly decreased from 10.51 GPa to 8.86 GPa caused by the significant shear impact carried on by the reduced pore pressure, and the decomposition of CO2 hydrate in the reservoir caused it to accelerate from 8.86 to 1.92 GPa. During CH4 hydrate depressurization mining, the elastic modulus of the CH4 hydrate reservoir in different Runs did not vary considerably. As shown in Fig. 7c, the top reconstructed CO2 hydrate reservoir had similar elastic modulus variation as Run 3, while the bottom reconstructed CO2 hydrate reservoir was different from Run 2. This might be caused by partial CH4 gas entering the bottom CO2 hydrate reservoir and causing the CO2 hydrate decomposition. As shown in Fig. 7d, the overall elastic modulus of different layers did not change considerably, while the CO2 hydrate cap had an increase in elastic modulus from 8.7 GPa to 9.12 GPa. The CO2 hydrate cap was more stable and benefits from shear-resistant and reservoir stiffness stability during hydrate mining. The CO2/N2 (80:20 mol%) in Run 6 could form CO2 hydrate at 2.3 MPa in the reactor to repair the reservoir in time and maintain the stiffness of the reservoir, in contrast to Run 5. The reconstructed CO2 hydrate reservoir could be made stable to increase its shear resistance by injecting CO2/N2 at a specific CO2 concentration. Meanwhile, the decomposed water of CH4 hydrate could quickly form CO2 hydrate to repair the reservoir in time.

The evolution of elastic modulus of different layers with time for Run 2, Run 3, Run 4 and Run 6 during CH4 hydrate mining.

Characteristics of reservoir subsidence during CH4 hydrate mining

Reservoir subsidence was a common occurrence in hydrate mining, which put reservoir stability in danger and increased the risk of landslides, reservoir collapse, and other unfavorable conditions that led to geological disasters [18], [55]. Fig. 8a and Fig. 8b showed the variations of subsidence and sedimentation rate with time during CH4 hydrate mining under 10 MPa overlying stress. As shown in Fig. 8a, the overall reservoir subsidence for Run 1 and Run 2 with concurrent below-reservoir reconstruction was 14.36 mm and 13.01 mm after hydrate mining, respectively. Fig. 8b showed the reservoir reached the maximum sedimentation rate of 0.208 mm/min within 11 min in Run 1, while in Run 2, the sedimentation rate only reached 0.055 mm/min within 40 min. That was, compared to the immediate depressurization after reconstruction in Run 1, the hydrate was better cemented to the sediment, more integral, and the overall reservoir strength was enhanced in Run 2. Therefore, the stability of the reconstructed reservoir after a certain period of time was more conducive. For Run 3, although the stiffness of the above-reservoir was greatly reduced due to shear failure and partial CO2 hydrate decomposition, the pressure-bearing effect of the reservoir was still strong, and the maximum sedimentation rate was only 0.028 mm/min, and the final subsidence was only 5.35 mm. Compared with the bottom reconstructed reservoir, the above-reconstructed reservoir had a much stronger effect on relieving reservoir subsidence. For Run 4, the stiffness strength of the above reservoir was relatively weak because the saturation of the CO2 hydrate reservoir was lower than that in Run 3. In addition, due to the decomposition of partial CO2 hydrate in the above & and below-reservoir, the final subsidence of the overall reservoir was 8.73 mm.

Variations of subsidence (a) and sedimentation rate (b) with time during CH4 hydrate mining.

After the initial CO2/N2 (50:50 mol%) sweeping, the sedimentation rate of overall reservoir reached 0.1 mm/min at the peak of CH4 hydrate decomposition rate, and the overall reservoir finally subsided by 14.23 mm in Run 5. This implied that, while maintaining the stiffness strength of the cap, intermittent CO2/N2 sweep might encourage the flow of fluid or sand in the CH4 hydrate reservoir and original skeleton structure of the reservoir was harmed, making the reservoir softer. Therefore, the key to reducing reservoir subsidence was stable cap and completed reservoir structure. For Run 6, the maximum sedimentation rate of the reservoir was only 0.025 mm/min, and the final subsidence was 4.56 mm. This indicated that the formation of CO2 hydrate could prevent the flow of free water, sand, and stabilize the reservoir to prevent skeletal damage under the condition of 2.3 MPa intermittent CO2/N2 (80:20 mol%) sweeping.

As shown in Fig. 8a and Fig. 8b, reconstructing reservoir of different layers could relieve reservoir subsidence to varying degrees, and the sedimentation rate of overall reservoir decreased obviously as compared to an unreconstructed reservoir. In particular, the top reconstructed CO2 hydrate reservoir had a pressure-bearing effect and was crucial in relieving reservoir subsidence. However, the CO2/N2 injection could preserve the hard stiffness of the cap, in addition, the skeleton structure of the reservoir was weakened by intermittent CO2/N2 sweeping, and the reservoir became softer unless CO2 hydrates form to stabilize the reservoir. Inspired by this work, Fig. 9 shows schematically how a mature gas injection method for hydrate mining paired with reservoir pre-reconstruction could provide safe and effective hydrate exploitation. In addition to preventing the top and bottom reconstructed CO2 hydrate reservoirs from being destroyed and the overall reservoir from subsiding and collapsing, a reasonable CO2/N2 injection scheme also improved CH4 recovery through gas injection and sweeping while stabilizing the reservoir with the formation of CO2 hydrate.

Diagrammatic representation of reservoir reconstruction combined with gas injection for hydrate exploitation to guarantee reservoir stability.

CH4 recovery ratio

Fig. 10 illustrates the CH4 recovery ratio under reconstruction reservoir of different layers combined with various mining methods for Runs 2 to 6. It could be seen that although there existed varied degrees of damage to the reconstructed reservoir during CH4 hydrate mining by depressurization, there was no significant difference in the final CH4 recovery for Runs 2–4. For Run 5, the intermittent CO2/N2 (50:50 mol%) sweeping accelerated the decomposition of CH4 hydrates, but since no CO2 hydrate formed, its recovery was almost consistent with Run 2. The P-wave velocity and reservoir subsidence analysis indicated that Run 6 exhibited superior performance in preserving the general stability of the reservoir during hydrate mining. However, Fig. 10 demonstrated that its CH4 recovery was comparatively lower. This was because partially pore channels were obstructed by CO2 hydrate and the surface of CH4 hydrate was covered by CO2 hydrate. In the middle and late stages of replacement, the lower content of N2 in CO2/N2 (80:20 mol%) was affected by mass transfer resistance greatly reducing the efficiency of N2 replacement of CH4 in the small 512 cage[56], [57]. To improve CH4 hydrate decomposition ratio and CH4 recovery ratio, the composition of the gas injection and the mechanism of gas injection need to be further adjusted. Further research was required to determine how reservoir stability and recovery ratio were impacted by the use of gas injection techniques in combination with other exploitation techniques (such as heat injection, inhibitor injection, etc.).

Conclusions

To improve the stability of the overall reservoir during CH4 hydrate mining, reservoirs with different layers were reconstructed by formed CO2 hydrate. The experiments used P-wave velocity measurement device to monitor in situ the change of reservoir stiffness strength in different layer, and the elastic modulus estimated by P-wave velocity clearly represented the change of mechanical properties in various layers. The subsidence of the reservoir with a 10 MPa overburden stress demonstrated how the reconstructed reservoir affected the subsidence of the reservoir at different locations. The experimental conclusions were summarized as follows:

(1)A strong indication that the mechanical strength of the bottom reconstructed reservoir was stable enough and its P-wave velocity remained constant during CH4 hydrate depressurization mining under 10 MPa overlying stresses. However, the above-reconstructed reservoir was heavily fractured and damaged by overlying stress shear, and the P-wave velocity decreased continuously. The simultaneous reconstruction of the above & below reservoirs demonstrated that, so long as the reservoir was not continuously eroded by decomposing CH4 gas, the mechanical strength will stay stable. During CH4 hydrate depressurization combined with CO2/N2 (50:50 mol%, 80:20 mol%) injection mining, the stable P-wave velocity demonstrated that the above-reservoir had sufficient ability to resist the shear of overlying stress. Furthermore, each reservoir P-wave velocity could be stabilized at the proper rate of CO2 hydrate formation, ensuring the stability of the entire reservoir.

In this work, the role of reconstructed reservoirs in different layers was clarified during CH4 hydrate depressurization/intermittent CO2/N2 injection mining. The research not only offered a novel idea for the future safe and effective mining of NGHs but also provided simple and efficient guidance for assessing the stability of hydrate reservoirs, turning the appropriate utilization of the greenhouse gas CO2 and realizing the geological storage of CO2.
