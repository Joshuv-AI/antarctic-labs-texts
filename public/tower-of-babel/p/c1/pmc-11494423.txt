Visual and Auditory Sensory Impairments Differentially Relate with Alzheimer’s Pathology
Authors: Gihwan Byeon, Min Soo Byun, Dahyun Yi, Joon Hyung Jung, Nayeong Kong, Yoonyoung Chang, MUSUNG KEUM, Gijung Jung, Hyejin Ahn, Jun-Young Lee, Yu Kyeong Kim, Koung Mi Kang, Chul-Ho Sohn, Dong Young Lee, for the KBASE Research Group
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWe intended to investigate the relationships between visual sensory impairment (VSI) or auditory sensory impairment (ASI) and brain pathological changes associated with cognitive decline in older adults.MethodsWe primarily tried to examine whether each sensory impairment is related to Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pathology, specifically beta-amyloid (Aβ) deposition, through both cross-sectional and longitudinal approaches in cognitively unimpaired older adults. Self-report questionnaires on vision and hearing status were administered at the baseline. Neuroimaging scans including brain [11C] Pittsburgh Compound B PET and MRI, as well as clinical assessments, were performed at baseline and 2-year follow-up.ResultsCross-sectional analyses showed that the VSI-positive group had significantly higher Aβ deposition than the VSI-negative group, whereas there was no significant association between ASI positivity and Aβ deposition. Longitudinal analyses revealed that VSI positivity at baseline was significantly associated with increased Aβ deposition over 2 years (β = 0.153, p = 0.025), although ASI positivity was not (β = 0.045, p = 0.518). VSI positivity at baseline was also significantly associated with greater atrophic changes in AD-related brain regions over the 2-year follow-up period (β = −0.207, p = 0.005), whereas ASI positivity was not (β = 0.024, p = 0.753). Neither VSI nor ASI positivity was related to cerebrovascular injury, as measured based on the white matter hyperintensity volume.ConclusionThe findings suggest that VSI is probably related to AD-specific pathological changes, which possibly mediate the reported relationship between VSI and cognitive decline. In contrast, ASI appears not associated with AD pathologies but may contribute to cognitive decline via other mechanisms.Keywords: Dementia, Sensation disorders, Alzheimer disease, Amyloid

INTRODUCTION

The prevalence of visual sensory impairment (VSI) and auditory sensory impairment (ASI) is increasing in the aging population [1,2]. Both VSI [3,4] and ASI [5,6] are also associated with an increased occurrence of dementia and cognitive decline in older adults. Moreover, both impairments are more likely to cause cognitive impairment when they occur simultaneously than when occurring alone [7,8].

Despite growing evidence for the relationship between sensory impairments and cognitive decline in epidemiological studies, the underlying neuropathological mechanisms whereby VSI and ASI are related to cognitive impairment have not yet been clearly elucidated. Several studies have attempted to link the VSI or ASI measure with brain changes related to cognitive decline in older adults, such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pathology, brain atrophy, and cerebrovascular injury. A cross-sectional study showed that decreased visual contrast sensitivity, an ability to differentiate between an object and its background, was related to brain beta-amyloid (Aβ) deposition [9]. Furthermore, retinal changes have been repeatedly associated with brain Aβ pathology in individuals with AD dementia or preclinical AD [10-12], as well as with AD pathologies in postmortem brains [13]. However, limited information is currently available regarding the relationship of VSI with longitudinal brain changes.

In terms of the ASI, a longitudinal study demonstrated that ASI was related to a faster rate of whole brain atrophy [14]. However, a couple of cross-sectional studies showed that hearing loss or the pure tone audiometry threshold, an objective measure of hearing levels, was not associated with brain Aβ deposition [14,15], indicating that the pathway linking hearing and cognition is independent of AD-specific brain changes. Although limited information is available on the longitudinal relationship between VSI or ASI and the progression of brain pathologies, these findings suggest that VSI and ASI may be related to cognitive decline via different neuropathological links, i.e., AD pathology-dependent vs. independent links.

The present study aimed to investigate the relationships of VSI and ASI with pathological brain changes associated with cognitive decline in cognitively unimpaired older adults. We targeted cognitively unimpaired individuals to minimize potential errors in the self-reported assessment of sensory impairment. Our primary objective was to examine whether each sensory impairment is related to AD pathology, specifically Aβ deposition, through both cross-sectional and longitudinal approaches. Furthermore, we explored whether well-established risk factors for AD [16-20], such as age, sex, and apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 allele (APOE4) positivity, moderate the relationship between each sensory impairment and Aβ deposition. For exploratory purposes, we also examined the relationships among VSI, ASI, and other brain changes, including brain atrophy and cerebrovascular injury. Lastly, we investigated the relationships between sensory impairments and both subjective and objective measures of cognitive decline.

METHODS

Participants

This study was part of the Korean Brain Aging Study for Early Diagnosis and Prediction of Alzheimer’s Disease (KBASE), an ongoing prospective cohort study that began in 2014. Participants were recruited from four sites (i.e., two public centers dedicated to dementia prevention and management and two memory clinics at two university hospitals) in Seoul, South Korea. Individuals potentially meeting the criteria were informed about the study. Those who expressed interest were then invited to undergo an eligibility assessment. Additionally, community volunteers were gathered through various means, including online advertisements, posters, brochures distributed at primary recruitment sites, and recommendations from other participants, family members, friends, or acquaintances.

As of June 2022, 297 cognitively normal (CN) participants aged 55−90 years who had completed baseline positron emission tomography (PET) scans for brain Aβ depositions were enrolled in the study. Among the participants, 226 completed the follow-up PET scans after 2 years (Fig. 1). The participants met the following inclusion criteria: (1) 55 to 90 years of age; (2) Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) score of 0; and (3) no diagnosis of mild cognitive impairment or dementia. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) any serious medical, psychiatric, or neurologic disorder that could affect mental function; (2) any severe communication problem that could hinder clinical examination or brain scanning; (3) contraindications to magnetic resonance imaging, such as a pacemaker or claustrophobia; (4) absence of a reliable informant; (5) illiteracy, defined as the inability to read; and (6) participation in another clinical trial or treatment with an investigational product. More detailed information on the participants has been provided in a previous study [21].

Clinical Evaluation and Objective Cognitive Assessments

Participants underwent standardized clinical assessments at both baseline and 2-year follow-up by trained board-certified psychiatrists. The assessments were based on the Korean Brain Aging Study for the KBASE clinical assessment protocol, which incorporates the Korean version of the Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer’s Disease (CERAD-K) clinical assessment [22]. The global and each domain of the CDR score were rated for all participants to evaluate the severity of cognitive impairment. The CDR-Sum-of-Box was calculated as the sum of the CDR scores of all domains [23]. All participants also underwent comprehensive neuropsy-chological assessment, administered according to a standardized protocol incorporating the CERAD-K neuropsychological battery, at both baseline and 2-year follow-up by trained psychometrists. The protocol included eight tests in the CERAD-K neuropsychological battery: the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), Verbal Fluency (VF), 15-item Boston Naming Test (BNT), Word List Memory (WLM), Word List Recall (WLR), Word List Recognition (WLRc), Constructional Praxis (CP), and Constructional Recall [24]. The CERAD total score (TS) was calculated by summing the scores of six tests included in the CERAD-K neuropsychological battery (VF, BNT, WLM, WLR, WLRc, and CP) [25,26]. The z-score of the TS was used as a measure of global cognition. The z-score was calculated based on a previously reported age, education, and sex-stratified norm for the CERAD-K TS [27].

Assessment of Subjective Memory Complaint

Subjective memory complaints were assessed using the Subjective Memory Complaint Questionnaire (SMCQ) [28] at both the baseline and 2-year follow-up. The SMCQ consisted of 14 items. Among them, four items were for global memory function, and the remaining 10 items were for everyday memory function. The participants’ responses to each question were restricted to either ‘yes’ or ‘no.’ The highest possible total score on the SMCQ was 14 points (SMCQ-T). Higher SMCQ scores indicate more severe subjective memory complaints. The study of SMCQ development demonstrated that it had good reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.864; intra-class correlation coefficient for test-retest reliability = 0.828) [28].

Assessment of Visual and Auditory Sensory Impairment

Both the VSI and ASI were assessed at baseline through interviews with trained nurses. The participants were asked to self-evaluate their visual or auditory functions. In terms of VSI, scores of 0 to 4 were assigned as follows: 0, ‘normal’; 1, ‘reduced, but able to view newspaper or television without wearing glasses or lenses’; 2, ‘reduced, so that I can view newspaper or television only with glasses or lenses’; 3, ‘reduced, as a result, I cannot watch newspapers or TV even if I wear glasses or lenses’; and 4, ‘cannot see at all.’ In the case of ASI, scores of 0 to 4 were assigned as follows: 0, ‘normal’; 1, ‘reduced but able to communicate without using a hearing aid’; 2, ‘reduced so that communication is possible only using hearing aids’; 3, ‘difficulty communicating even when hearing aid is used’; and 4, ‘I can’t hear at all.’ As shown in Table 1, most participants corresponded to 0 (24.2%), 1 (35.6%), or 2 (40.4%) for visual function, and most of them corresponded to 0 (70.2%) or 1 (28.5%) for hearing. Hence, for visual function, 0 and 1 were defined as ‘VSI negative (VSI–)’ and 2, 3, and 4 as ‘VSI positive (VSI+),’ and for hearing function, 0 was defined as ‘ASI negative (ASI–)’ and 1, 2, 3, and 4 as ‘ASI positive (ASI+).’

Measurement for Potential Confounders

Annual incomes were assessed and categorized into three groups: below the minimum cost of living (MCL), more than MCL but below two MCLs, and two or more MCLs (http://www.law.go.kr). The MCL was determined according to the administrative rules published by the Ministry of Health and Welfare, Republic of Korea, in November 2012. The Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) was used to measure the severity of depressive symptoms [29]. The presence of six vascular risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, transient ischemic attack, stroke, and coronary artery disease, was systematically evaluated, and the vascular risk score (VRS) was calculated as the total number of vascular risk factors [30]. Genomic DNA was extracted from whole blood, and APOE genotyping was performed. APOE ε4 (APOE4) positivity was defined as the presence of at least one ε4 allele.

Measurement of Brain Changes with Neuroimaging

All participants underwent simultaneous three-dimensional (3D) [11C] Pittsburgh compound B (PiB)-positron emission tomography (PET), 3D T1-weighted MRI scans, and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery MRI scans using a 3.0T Biograph mMR (PET-MR) scanner (Siemens) at the baseline and 2-year follow-up visit.

For PiB-PET, after intravenous administration of 555 MBq of 11C-PiB (range, 450−610 MBq), a 30-minute emission scan was obtained 40 minutes after injection. Acquisition of PiB-PET images and preprocessing details were described in a previous report [31]. With regard to cerebral Aβ deposition measurement, an automatic anatomic labeling algorithm [32] and a region-combining method [33] were applied to determine regions of interest (ROIs) to characterize the PiB retention level in the frontal, lateral parietal, posterior cingulate-precuneus, and lateral temporal regions. The global Aβ retention value was the mean standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) for all voxels of the four ROIs, calculated by dividing the mean uptake value of a reference region. For the cross-sectional analysis of baseline data, the inferior cerebellar gray matter in the Spatially Unbiased Infratentorial Template for the cerebellum (SUIT) atlas [34] was used as the reference region for intensity normalization. Amyloid positivity was defined based on an SUVR greater than 1.4 in at least one of the four ROIs, including the frontal, lateral parietal, posterior cingulate-precuneus, and lateral temporal regions [33,35]. The cutoff of 1.4 was applied to all four ROIs after the whole brain uptake was normalized (divided) by the uptake level in the cerebellar gray matter. The positivity of participants was assigned based on the SUVR obtained using inferior cerebellar gray matter as the reference region. To examine the characteristics of the SUVRs serially as a continuous variable, a larger composite of the reference regions was incorporated, which included the inferior cerebellar gray matter in the SUIT, cerebellar white matter (thresholded at 50%) [36], pons, and cerebrum white matter (thresholded at 95% and eroded by three voxels) [37,38]. For longitudinal analyses on delta-amyloid, SUVRs acquired using the larger composite reference ROI were used. The value of change in amyloid was obtained by subtracting the baseline SUVR, which also used a composite reference, from the follow-up SUVR, which also used a composite reference.

For the assessment of brain atrophy, T1-weighted MR images were acquired in the sagittal orientation and the parameters were as follows: repetition time = 1,670 ms; echo time = 1.89 ms; field of view = 250 mm; and 256 × 256 matrix with 1.0 mm slice thickness. All the obtained 3D T1-weighted images were automatically segmented using FreeSurfer version 6.0 (http://surfer.mnr.mgh.harvard.edu/), and manual corrections for minor segmentation errors were performed for all scans by trained image analysts. Two ROIs derived from the FreeSurfer output in the native space were used to represent atrophic patterns based on previous studies: the spatial pattern of abnormality for recognition of early Alzheimer’s disease (SPARE-AD) volume and the spatial pattern of abnormality for recognition of brain aging (SPARE-BA) volume. The SPARE-AD volume used in the study was the sum of the volumes of the regions identified to be most sensitive to AD-related atrophy adjusted by the total intracranial volume (TIV), which included the hippocampus, inferior temporal gyrus, parahippocampal gyrus, posterior cingulate, precuneus, entorhinal cortex, and middle temporal gyrus [39]. Similarly, the SPARE-BA volume was the TIV-adjusted sum of the volumes of the regions identified to be most associated with age-related degeneration of the brain, which included the insular cortex, thalamus proper, anterior, and middle cingulate cortices, frontal cortex, inferior parietal cortex, and superior temporal gyrus [39]. Lower values indicated greater atrophy in these regions [40].

Fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) images were acquired in sagittal plane, with the following parameter settings: TR, 5,000 ms; TE, 173 ms; echo spacing, 3.46 ms; FOV, 250 mm; matrix size, 256 × 256; slice thickness, 1.0 mm. To measure white matter hyperintensities (WMHI) volume as a measure of cerebrovascular injury, we followed the validated automatic procedure reported previously with some minor modification [41,42]. Briefly, the procedure consisted of 11 steps: spatial co-registration of T1 and FLAIR images, fusion of T1 and FLAIR images, T1 segmentation, attainment of transfor-mation parameters, deformation and acquisition of the white matter mask, acquisition of FLAIR within the white matter mask, intensity normalization of the masked FLAIR, nomination of candidate WMHI with a designated threshold, creation of a junction map, and elimination of the junction. Using the final WMHI candidate image, the WMHI volume was extracted from the native space for each participant.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics on demographic, clinical, and neuroimaging characteristics of baseline subjects and those who completed the follow-up evaluation were first obtained. Subsequently, the chi-square test was used for categorical variables, and the independent t test was conducted for continuous variables to assess differences between the two groups. When normality assumptions were not met, non-parametric analyses were employed. Next, the normality of outcome variables was assessed by checking skewness (< 3) and kurtosis (< 8), and visual interpretation through Q-Q plot. Multiple linear regression models with baseline positivity of VSI or ASI as independent variables and neuroimaging markers (i.e., SPRAE-BA, SPARE-AD, or WMHI volume) as dependent variables were analyzed, controlling for sex, age, annual income, education, GDS, VRS, and APOE4 positivity as covariates. Because the baseline Aβ retention did not show normality in the data distribution, multiple logistic regression analysis was performed with dichotomized amyloid positivity as a dependent variable and the same independent and covariates. A similar regression model with baseline positivity of VSI or ASI as the independent variable and 2-year change in each neuroimaging marker (i.e., global Aβ retention, SPRAE-BA, SPARE-AD, or WMHI volume) as the dependent variable was also analyzed, controlling for the same covariates. The amount of change (i.e., Δ values) in each neuroimaging marker for 2 years was calculated by subtracting the baseline value from the value at the 2-year follow-up assessment. In conducting the aforementioned multiple regression analysis and multiple logistic regression analysis, model fits were confirmed by examining the R2 value and the p value of the F statistics in regression analysis, and the Nagelkerke R2 and Hosmer Lemeshow test in logistic regression, respectively. Additionally, we explored the moderation effects of conventional biologic risk factors for AD and Aβ pathology such as age, sex, and APOE4 positivity for AD and Aβ pathology on the relationship between VSI (or ASI) and baseline Aβ positivity (or Δ Aβ retention). We included two-way interaction term between VSI (or ASI) and any one of the factors, as well as from VSI (or ASI) itself, as an independent variable in the regression models. Afterwards, the effect of VSI or ASI on each cognitive outcome measure (or 2-year change in each measure) was analyzed using a similar regression model with the same covariate as described above. All statistical analyses were performed using the IBM SPSS Statistics 24 software (IBM Corp.). A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Association of Visual and Auditory Sensory Impairment with Brain Beta-amyloid Deposition: Primary Results

Cross-sectional multiple logistic regression analyzes of the baseline data suggested a marginal increase in Aβ positivity in the VSI+ group compared to the VSI– group, but this difference did not achieve statistical significance (OR = 1.916, p = 0.087). There was no significant association between ASI positivity and Aβ positivity (OR = 1.578, p = 0.242) (Table 3). Longitudinal analyses revealed that the VSI+ group exhibited a greater increase in global Aβ retention (β = 0.153, p = 0.025) over a 2-year period compared to the VSI– group. However, there was no significant difference in global Aβ retention change between the ASI+ and ASI– groups (β = 0.045, p = 0.518) (Table 4, Supplementary Table 2 [available online], and Fig. 2). Additional exploratory analyses for the moderation effect of age, sex, and APOE4 positivity on the association between sensory impairment and baseline Aβ positivity (or Δ Aβ retention) did not show a significant interaction between VSI (or ASI) and any of the three factors (Tables 5, 6).

Associations of Visual and Auditory Sensory Impairment with Other Neuroimaging Markers

Additional exploratory analyses showed that there was no significant association between VSI positivity and SPARE-BA, SPARE-AD, or WMHI volume. However, VSI positivity at baseline was associated with decreased SPARE-AD volume (β = −0.207, p = 0.005) over 2-years, although it was not related to changes in SPARE-BA and WMHI volume (Table 3). With regard to ASI, there was neither a cross-sectional nor a longitudinal association between ASI positivity and any other neuroimaging marker (Table 7).

DISCUSSION

In the current study of cognitively unimpaired older adults, the VSI+ group had a greater increase in Aβ deposition over the 2-year follow-up period compared to the VSI– group. Although a recent study similarly showed that visual contrast sensitivity was cross-sectionally associated with cortical amyloid deposition [9], no previous study has demonstrated that VSI is associated with accelerated Aβ pathology over time. Our additional exploratory analysis also demonstrated that VSI was associated with accelerated AD-specific regional brain atrophy (i.e., reduction of SPARE-AD volume), but not with accelerated age-related brain atrophy (i.e., SPARE-BA volume) or cerebrovascular injury (i.e., WMHI volume).

Several possible mechanisms may explain the association between VSI and increased AD pathology. First, a common pathological process associated with AD may impair the eyes, retina, and brain. A preclinical study showed that tau deposition in the retina causes local inflammation and dysfunction of retinal ganglion cells in an AD animal model [43]. A clinical study demonstrated that retinal Aβ plaques were significantly increased in individuals with AD dementia compared to normal controls. Our previous report also showed that macular thickness and retinal nerve fiber layer thickness were lower in CN individuals with brain amyloid deposition than in those without amyloid deposition [12]. Taken together, AD pathology may commonly impair not only the brain but also the retina, resulting in VSI even in cognitively unimpaired older adults. Second, changes in circadian rhythm caused by VSI may contribute to more severe AD pathology. VSI can induce changes in melatonin secretion and subsequent alterations in the circadian rhythm [44]. Circadian rhythm alterations may impair the clearance of cerebral Aβ or tau protein through the glymphatic system [45-47].

In contrast to VSI, we did not find a significant relationship between ASI and any neuroimaging marker for AD brain pathology, particularly Aβ deposition or its changes. Supporting our finding, a previous clinical study showed that ASI was not associated with Aβ deposition in the brain [15]. Another study also revealed that Aβ deposition on PET was not related to aging-related hearing loss, as measured by pure tone audiometry, over a five-year period [48]. Similarly, another postmortem brain study reported no significant difference in AD pathology between individuals with and without subjective hearing loss [49]. Although additional exploratory analyses did not show a significant relationship between ASI and any regional brain atrophy, a recent MRI study demonstrated that cortical thinning of the bilateral frontal, right temporal, and bilateral occipital areas in the brain was more prominent in individuals with auditory impairment than in those without such impairment [50]. Hearing impairment was also significantly related to lower volumes of the temporal cortex, hippocampus, inferior parietal lobe, and precuneus in a previous large cohort study [51]. Given reports supporting the association between ASI and regional brain atrophy, the null association between ASI and atrophy of the cortical regions (i.e., SPARE-AD and SPARE-BA) in the current study may be related to the proportion of individuals with ASI and the severity of ASI. Only 29.8% (n = 88) of the participants in the current study had ASI, and most ASI+ individuals had a mild degree of impairment, as shown in Table 1. As described in the exclusion criteria in the Methods section, individuals with severe hearing difficulties that would make clinical examinations difficult were excluded when participants of the KBASE cohort were recruited. If more individuals with severe ASI are included, different results may be obtained.

Additional exploratory analyses did not show any interaction between age, sex, or APOE4 positivity and VSI (or ASI) on baseline Aβ positivity and 2-year change of Aβ retention. The results indicate that while age, sex, and APOE4 positivity are well-known biological risk factors for both clinical AD dementia and Aβ pathology, the association between sensory impairment and Aβ pathology is not moderated by the presence of these risk factors.

In contrast to the AD neuroimaging markers, no objective cognitive measures were related to VSI. This null finding contrasts with previous epidemiological findings that VSI is related to an increased risk for cognitive impairment [3,4]. This discrepancy may be related to the participant characteristics. Our study targeted only CN older adults, whereas previous epidemiological studies included individuals with diverse cognitive spectra from CN to dementia. It would be more difficult to detect subtle cognitive decline associated with VSI in cognitively unimpaired individuals than in populations that include both CN and cognitively impaired individuals. In contrast, AD brain pathology, particularly Aβ deposition, precedes cognitive decline by several years or even a decade [52].

Similarly to the null association between ASI positivity and neuroimaging markers, ASI positivity was not related to the performance of objective cognitive tests (i.e., MMSE and CERAD-TS) either cross-sectionally or prospectively. The lack of association between ASI and objective cognitive performance, as well as between VSI and objective cognitive performance, could potentially be attributed to the characteristics of our study population, which consists of CN individuals. However, ASI showed a significant association with an increase in SMCQ, a measure of subjective cognitive complaints, over 2 years. In line with our finding of SMCQ change, previous studies have reported that individuals with ASI complained about memory decline more frequently [53]. The reason why ASI is associated with increased subjective cognitive complaints may be that deprivation of sound stimuli can increase the cognitive load and attentional cost related to effortful listening [54,55]. Alternatively, hearing difficulties are known to induce depression, which may increase cognitive complaints [56,57]. However, even after controlling for depressive symptoms, the association between ASI and SMCQ score increase was still significant, which does not support the mediation of depressive mood.

In contrast to ASI, VSI was not associated with SMCQ. All VSI positive participants in the present study had only mildly reduced visual sense and were able to view newspapers or television with glasses. Such mild VSI in CN older adults may not be sufficient to cause increased cognitive load or depressive mood.

Our study revealed that VSI and ASI were differently associated with brain AD pathology, particularly Aβ deposition, through longitudinal approaches. Our finding of the association between VSI and a longitudinal increase in Aβ in cognitively unimpaired individuals indicates that VSI could be an early predictor or risk factor for future AD-related cognitive impairment. In contrast, our result does not support that ASI is associated with cognitive decline via AD pathological changes. Despite these novel implications, this study had some limitations. First, although all participants were cognitively unimpaired and expected to accurately report any discomfort in their visual or auditory function, the accuracy of the VSI and ASI assessments may have been reduced due to their reliance on subjective reports. Additionally, different criteria used to define VSI and ASI could have influenced the findings. Therefore, further studies using more objective tests for VSI (e.g., visual acuity test and visual contrast sensitivity test) and ASI (e.g., pure tone audiometry) need to be performed to confirm these findings. Secondly, as previously mentioned, most participants in this study had relatively mild sensory impairments. This could have resulted in relatively small effect sizes for the significant results or made it difficult to detect significant findings. Additionally, the relatively short follow-up period might have contributed to the small effect sizes or the negative results observed in the longitudinal analyses. Further studies including individuals with more severe sensory impairments and longer follow-up periods are necessary.

In conclusion, the findings of the current study suggest that VSI and ASI are differently associated with cerebral AD pathology. VSI is likely related to AD-specific pathological changes that may mediate the relationship between VSI and cognitive decline. In contrast, ASI appears not to be associated with AD pathologies but may contribute to cognitive decline via other mechanisms.
