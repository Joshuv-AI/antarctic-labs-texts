Nanomaterials targeting ferroptosis for osteoarthritis treatment: a systematic review of preclinical evidence
Authors: Siyang Cao, Deren Lan, Yihao Wei, Hui Zeng, Hua Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteoarthritis (OA) lacks effective disease-modifying therapies. Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of programmed cell death, has emerged as a novel pathogenic mechanism in OA. Nanomaterials (NMs), with tunable physicochemical properties and targeting capacity, offer promising opportunities for ferroptosis modulation. This systematic review synthesizes preclinical evidence on NM-based strategies in OA.MethodsThis review followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines and was prospectively registered on INPLASY (INPLASY202570110). Comprehensive searches of seven databases (PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, ProQuest, Ovid, Cochrane Library) were performed up to July 27, 2025. Eligible studies included in vitro and/or in vivo investigations of NM-based approaches targeting ferroptosis in OA models. Independent screening, data extraction, and risk of bias assessment were conducted. Owing to substantial heterogeneity in nanomaterial platforms, experimental models, and outcome measures, a qualitative evidence synthesis was performed and meta-analysis was not considered appropriate.ResultsTwenty-two eligible studies were identified. Investigated nanoplatforms included polymeric nanoparticles, metal-based carriers, nanozymes, hydrogels, and biomimetic systems. Across in vitro and in vivo models, NMs were reported to modulate ferroptosis through regulation of iron metabolism, scavenging of reactive oxygen species, reinforcement of antioxidant defenses, and modulation of ferroptosis-related signaling. Reported preclinical outcomes included attenuation of lipid peroxidation, preservation of mitochondrial integrity, reduction of chondrocyte death, and improvement in cartilage structure and joint function.ConclusionPreclinical evidence supports NM-based ferroptosis modulation as a promising avenue for disease-modifying OA therapy. Translation will require standardized protocols, independent replication across diverse settings, adoption of advanced human-relevant models, and integration with multi-target strategies beyond ferroptosis.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04500-4.Keywords: Nanomedicine, Osteoarthritis, Ferroptosis, Preclinical evidence, Systematic review

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is one of the most prevalent degenerative joint disorders worldwide, characterized by progressive cartilage erosion, subchondral bone remodeling, osteophyte formation, and persistent low-grade inflammation [1]. By 2050, the global population affected by OA is projected to be almost 1 billion, largely driven by aging, obesity, and population growth [2]. Clinically, OA represents a leading cause of chronic pain, disability, and loss of independence in older adults, with a profound impact on quality of life. Beyond the personal burden, OA contributes substantially to healthcare expenditures and indirect societal costs through reduced productivity and premature retirement [3]. Despite decades of research, current therapeutic strategies remain largely symptomatic [4]. Early interventions such as exercise, weight management, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs may relieve pain but do not modify disease course [5]. Intra-articular injections of corticosteroids or hyaluronic acid offer temporary symptomatic relief without preventing structural deterioration. At advanced stages, joint replacement surgery becomes inevitable, but prosthetic implants are costly, have limited durability, and carry risks of revision and complications [6]. Collectively, these limitations highlight the urgent need for disease-modifying therapies that directly address the molecular mechanisms driving OA progression.

Recent advances in cell death biology have identified ferroptosis, an iron-dependent and lipid peroxidation–driven form of programmed cell death (PCD), as a central pathogenic mechanism in OA [7, 8]. Ferroptosis is characterized as a unique modality of PCD, markedly distinct from classical pathways such as apoptosis, necrosis, and autophagy [9]. Its initiation depends largely on the excessive buildup of intracellular iron coupled with uncontrolled lipid peroxidation. The execution of this death program is governed by an intricate interplay of metabolic signaling cascades. Among these, pathways linked to iron metabolism and redox reactions, antioxidant defenses, and lipid peroxidative processes play central roles in orchestrating ferroptotic outcomes (Fig. 1). In the OA context, ferroptosis has been implicated in chondrocyte loss, extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation, and synovial inflammation, thereby accelerating structural and functional deterioration of the joint [10]. Evidence from preclinical models demonstrates that iron overload exacerbates cartilage degeneration and oxidative stress, whereas pharmacological inhibitors of ferroptosis protect cartilage integrity and improve joint function [11, 12]. These findings not only establish ferroptosis as a mechanistic driver of OA but also underscore its translational promise as a therapeutic target.

Molecular mechanisms of ferroptosis. The principal metabolic pathways implicated in iron-induced ferroptosis include ironmetabolism, amino acid metabolism, and lipid peroxidation. In iron metabolism, processes such as ferritinophagy and iron import/exportdisturb intracellular iron balance. Excess free iron can drive lipid peroxidation and trigger ferroptosis through the Fenton or Fenton-likereactions. Mitochondria also play a central role, as their metabolic activity contributes to iron handling and ROS generation during ferroptosis. Inamino acid metabolism, two major protective axes are involved: the cyst(e)ine/GSH/GPx4 system and the NAD(P)H/FSP1/CoQ₁₀ pathway. Glutamine catabolism, cysteine depletion, and elevated extracellular glutamate reduce glutathione synthesis, weakening the antioxidant defenseand promoting ferroptosis. Within the NAD(P)H/FSP1/CoQ₁₀ system, FSP1 prevents ferroptosis by acting as an oxidoreductase. It reducesCoQ₁₀ to its active antioxidant form, CoQ₁₀H₂, using NAD(P)H as a cofactor, thereby suppressing lipid peroxidation. In lipid metabolism, freepolyunsaturated fatty acids, such as arachidonic acid or adrenic acid, are activated by ACSL4 (and ACSL1) and incorporated into phospholipidsby LPCAT3. These phospholipids are subsequently oxidized by PEBP1-associated proteins, cytochrome POR, and lipoxygenases, ultimatelydriving lipid peroxidation and ferroptotic cell death. In recent years, notable advances have been made in designing nanoplatforms that address the limitations of conventional OA therapies, giving rise to the emerging concept of “OA nanomedicine” [13, 14]. Nanomaterials (NMs) are particularly attractive because their structure—defined by size, morphology, and porosity—can be precisely controlled and readily functionalized [15, 16]. Specifically, surface modification with targeting ligands and the incorporation of stimuli-responsive motifs allow nanocarriers to release therapeutic agents selectively at lesion sites while minimizing drug accumulation in healthy tissues, thereby reducing adverse effects [17]. Beyond acting as carriers, many NMs exhibit intrinsic bioactivities, such as scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) or modulating local redox balance, which are directly relevant to OA pathology [18]. This convergence of customizable delivery and inherent therapeutic functionality defines the emerging field of OA nanomedicine (Fig. 2).

Importantly, the translational potential of nanomedicine should be viewed within a realistic clinical context [19]. Nanomedicine-based therapies have already achieved regulatory approval in several non-OA indications, particularly in oncology, infectious diseases, and genetic medicine, exemplified by liposomal doxorubicin, albumin-bound paclitaxel, liposomal amphotericin B, and the lipid nanoparticle siRNA patisiran [20–22]. These examples indicate that nanomedicine is not merely a conceptual platform, but a clinically achievable therapeutic modality when material design, disease biology, and manufacturing requirements are appropriately aligned. At the same time, they also caution against unrealistic hype, as successful translation remains indication-specific and depends on clear therapeutic advantage, scalable production, and acceptable safety profiles.

However, despite encouraging results, the existing literature on ferroptosis-targeted nanomedicine in OA remains fragmented. Studies employ diverse NM formulations, cell and animal models, intervention protocols, and outcome measures, often yielding heterogeneous and difficult-to-compare results. Moreover, the field has expanded rapidly but without systematic synthesis, leaving knowledge gaps in methodological quality, translational relevance, and therapeutic generalizability. These challenges hinder the ability of clinicians, policymakers, and researchers to evaluate the true potential of this emerging therapeutic paradigm.

To address this gap, the present systematic review (SR) provides the first comprehensive synthesis of preclinical evidence on NMs targeting ferroptosis in OA. Following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines and a prospectively registered protocol, we critically appraise the methodological rigor of available studies, summarize therapeutic strategies and outcomes, and identify limitations and future research priorities. By integrating current findings, this SR aims to support the rational design of ferroptosis-targeted nanomedicines and accelerate their translation into clinically relevant interventions for OA.

Data & methods

Registration and protocol

This SR was prospectively registered on the International Platform of Registered Systematic Review and Meta-analysis Protocols (INPLASY) under the code INPLASY202570110. The full protocol is accessible at: https://inplasy.com/inplasy-2025-7-0110/. The SR was conducted in strict adherence to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [23].

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed across Embase, Ovid, ProQuest, PubMed, Scopus, the Cochrane Library, and Web of Science, covering all records from database inception to July 27, 2025. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords organized into three clusters: (1) ferroptosis-related terms (e.g., “ferroptosis” and “iron death”), (2) OA-related terms (e.g., “osteoarthritis”, “degenerative arthritis”, “osteoarthrosis”, and “arthrosis”), and (3) NM-related terms (e.g., “nano*”). These clusters were integrated using Boolean logic (#1 AND #2 AND #3) to capture literature addressing NM-based approaches for ferroptosis-targeted OA therapy. To improve precision and minimize irrelevant retrievals, we iteratively refined the strategy through manual pre-screening of preliminary results and consultation with three subject-matter experts. The full PubMed search string is provided in Supplementary Table S1 as a representative example to ensure reproducibility.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

(1) Original studies investigating nanodrug delivery systems (NDDSs) or NMs with intrinsic ferroptosis-modulating properties for the prevention or treatment of OA. (2) Studies involving in vitro and/or in vivo experimental models. (3) Publications available in English.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Non-original articles, including reviews, dissertations, editorials, commentaries, conference abstracts, meta-analyses, case reports, corrigenda, and bibliometric analyses. (2) Duplicate publications across different journals or titles. (3) Studies without accessible full text.

Study selection

All retrieved records were imported into NoteExpress version 4.2 (Aegean Software, Beijing, China) for organization and automatic duplicate removal. Titles and abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers (SYC and DRL) according to the eligibility criteria. Full texts of potentially relevant studies were assessed in detail. Disagreements were resolved by consensus or arbitration with a third reviewer (YHW).

Risk of bias assessment

The in vivo studies were independently evaluated for risk of bias by two reviewers (SYC and YHW) using Review Manager 5.4 (Cochrane Collaboration, Oxford, UK), following the Systematic Review Centre for Laboratory Animal Experimentation’s (SYRCLE) risk of bias tool [24]. For in vitro studies, an adapted bias assessment framework from prior work (Table 1) [7, 25] was applied, addressing domains such as randomization of treatment allocation, blinding of outcome assessment, reproducibility of experimental conditions, and completeness of reporting. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and adjudication by a third reviewer (DRL).

Data collection and extraction

A standardized extraction form was used to collect study-level and experimental data. Extracted variables included: study details (author, year, country), cell type and source, animal characteristics (species, age, weight, sex), sample size, experimental grouping, drug delivery route, intervention duration, and key findings. Data extraction was performed by one reviewer (SYC) and independently verified by another (YHW). Any inconsistencies were resolved by team consensus. These extracted variables were subsequently used to support structured qualitative synthesis across mechanistic categories and experimental settings.

Rationale for qualitative, narrative-integrated evidence synthesis

Given the substantial heterogeneity across the included studies in nanomaterial design, physicochemical properties, cargo composition, experimental models, ferroptosis-related targets, intervention protocols, and outcome measures, a formal meta-analysis was not considered appropriate for this review. In addition, many outcomes were reported using non-uniform experimental indicators and time points, which further limited direct comparability and meaningful statistical pooling across studies. Therefore, while maintaining SR methodology in study identification, screening, extraction, and risk-of-bias assessment, we adopted a narrative-integrated evidence synthesis approach in Sect. 3 (Results & Discussion). Specifically, study characteristics, mechanistic categories, and preclinical outcome patterns were summarized together with concise contextual interpretation to enhance thematic continuity and facilitate cross-study comparison. Broader translational implications, methodological caveats, and future perspectives were further synthesized in the later part of the section.

Results & discussion

Owing to the marked heterogeneity of the included preclinical studies, this section presents the evidence using a narrative-integrated synthesis, in which descriptive findings are summarized together with focused contextual interpretation to improve thematic coherence across nanomaterial categories and ferroptosis-related mechanisms.

Study selection

The initial database search retrieved 89 records: PubMed (n = 6), Embase (n = 19), Web of Science (n = 29), Scopus (n = 32), and ProQuest (n = 3), with no additional studies identified from Ovid or the Cochrane Library. After removing 49 duplicates, 40 unique records remained. Following title and abstract screening, 7 studies were excluded for irrelevance to OA, ferroptosis, or NM interventions. The full texts of 33 articles were assessed in detail, of which 11 were excluded—primarily due to being review articles, lacking ferroptosis-related outcomes, or not involving NM-based interventions. Finally, 22 studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in this SR. Study characteristics

All included studies were published during 2024 ~ 2025, indicating the recent emergence of this research area (Fig. 4). The evidence base was entirely preclinical and originated from a single national context. Most studies (17/22, 77.3%) incorporated both in vitro and in vivo experiments, whereas two (9.1%) were limited to cell-based studies and three (13.6%) to animal studies. Among the 19 studies involving in vitro experiments, more than half (10/19, 52.6%) used primary cells. Among the 20 animal studies, mouse models predominated (14/20, 70.0%). Risk of bias in studies

In vitro studies

The methodological quality of the included in vitro studies was assessed across seven domains: cell selection, confounding variables, exposure measurement, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and funding bias. Most studies were rated as low risk in the majority of domains; however, exposure measurement and blinding were the most frequently identified sources of concern (Fig. 5A and B). None of the 19 studies specified whether semi-quantitative or qualitative analyses were performed by independent observers, nor did they report details of blinded outcome assessment, resulting in a uniformly high risk of bias in this domain. In addition, protocols for NM exposure—such as concentration–time curves, replicate design, or cross-assay validation—were often insufficiently described, which may have limited comparability across studies. These methodological limitations should be considered when interpreting the reported therapeutic effects and translational relevance of the included studies. In particular, incomplete reporting of blinding procedures and exposure protocols indicates areas where methodological transparency could be improved in future studies.

Risk of bias assessments presented as traffic-light plots and weighted bar charts: (A ~ B) in vitrostudies; (C ~ D) in vivo studies

In vivo investigations

The results of SYRCLE’s risk of bias assessment are summarized in Fig. 5C and D. Only one study (5.0%) employed a random-digit method and was judged as low risk in sequence generation [26]. By contrast, all 20 studies (100%) were rated as unclear for baseline characteristics and random allocation of animals to outcome assessment, indicating insufficient reporting in these domains. Allocation concealment was not reported in any study, and random housing was rarely specified (3/20, 15.0%) [27–29]. Moreover, none of the studies adequately described blinding of caregivers or investigators, and only four studies reported blinding of outcome assessors [26, 30–32]. For incomplete outcome data, all studies were rated as unclear risk, whereas selective reporting was consistently rated as low risk. Two studies (10.0%) presented additional concerns captured in other domains [29, 33].

Taken together, the in vivo studies showed limited reporting in several key methodological domains, particularly randomization, allocation concealment, and blinding. These features should be taken into account when interpreting the robustness and translational applicability of the reported therapeutic effects. Overall, the SYRCLE assessment suggests that methodological reporting remains incomplete in the current in vivo literature.

NM-based modulation of ferroptosis in OA

Owing to the heterogeneity of nanomaterial designs, mechanistic targets, and preclinical outcome measures, the following subsections summarize the included evidence using a narrative-integrated approach, combining structured description of representative studies with focused mechanistic synthesis.

Regulation of iron metabolism and homeostasis

Iron overload-induced ferroptosis has been increasingly implicated in OA pathogenesis. Excess free iron (Fe²⁺) promotes Fenton reactions, generating ROS and initiating lipid peroxidation [34]. In the included studies, NMs designed to regulate iron homeostasis mainly acted through three mechanisms: iron chelation, competitive inhibition of iron uptake, and suppression of iron release. By reducing the labile iron pool, these approaches were reported to mitigate ferroptosis initiation.

ApoFn nanocages: inflammation-targeted iron chelation

ApoFn nanocages: inflammation-targeted iron chelation

Apoferritin (ApoFn) nanocages, derived from ferritin through iron removal, serve as a natural platform for both iron chelation and inflammation-targeted therapy [35]. Their hollow protein structure (≈ 12 nm) provides excellent biocompatibility and stability [36]. Importantly, their intrinsic ability to sequester labile iron reduces oxidative stress and ferroptosis in chondrocytes. This is particularly relevant in OA, where iron overload accelerates ROS generation, lipid peroxidation, and ECM degradation [37]. A distinctive feature of ApoFn is its affinity for transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1), which is upregulated in inflamed chondrocytes under interleukin-1β (IL-1β) stimulation [38–40]. This enables receptor-mediated uptake and selective accumulation in OA lesions without further modification. Preclinical studies show that SM04690-loaded ApoFn (SM@ApoFn) lowers intracellular Fe²⁺ and ROS, restores glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPx4)/acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) balance, and preserves mitochondrial integrity, thereby suppressing ferroptosis (Fig. 6A) [41]. In parallel, it promotes ECM homeostasis by increasing aggrecan and collagen II while reducing catabolic enzymes. In vivo, SM@ApoFn demonstrates prolonged joint retention and effective targeting of inflamed cartilage, achieving superior therapeutic efficacy compared with free SM04690 or ApoFn alone. Collectively, the available preclinical evidence suggests that ApoFn nanocages may serve as a multifunctional platform integrating iron homeostasis regulation with inflammation-guided drug delivery in OA.

(2)Catechol-containing copolymer nanoparticles: targeted iron chelation

Catechol-containing copolymer nanoparticles: targeted iron chelation

Recent advances identify catechol-containing copolymer nanoparticles (NPs) as a promising nanoplatform for regulating ferroptosis in OA. In one design, an amphiphilic block copolymer, PMEMA-b-P, was synthesized with thioester groups for ROS scavenging and catechol moieties for iron chelation (Fig. 6B) [42]. When self-assembled with curcumin (Cur), the resulting nanoparticles (Cur-NPs) displayed favorable physicochemical stability and biocompatibility. Mechanistically, Cur-NPs lowered intracellular Fe²⁺, ROS, and lipid peroxidation in IL-1β-stimulated chondrocytes. They also restored mitochondrial membrane potential, inhibited ACSL4 expression, and ultimately prevented ferroptosis. Notably, even blank NPs without Cur showed partial anti-ferroptotic activity, underscoring the intrinsic therapeutic roles of catechol and thioester groups. Beyond ferroptosis suppression, Cur-NPs improved ECM homeostasis by downregulating the catabolic enzyme matrix metallopeptidase 13 (MMP13) and upregulating collagen II, thereby supporting cartilage preservation. Collectively, the included study suggests that catechol-containing polymeric NPs can function both as drug carriers and as active regulators of iron metabolism and oxidative stress. Their combined actions—integrating targeted iron chelation, ROS scavenging, and Cur delivery—support their potential relevance as a multifunctional preclinical nanoplatform for OA intervention.

(3)GaSeₓ NPs: element supplementation with iron competition

GaSeₓ NPs: element supplementation with iron competition

Elemental imbalance in the OA microenvironment is a key driver of cartilage degradation [43, 44]. Clinical analyses of cartilage consistently reveal selenium (Se) and gallium (Ga) deficiencies alongside iron overload, providing the rationale for developing GaSeₓ NPs. These ultrasmall particles, synthesized by ultrasound-assisted exfoliation, exhibited robust antioxidant activity. This effect arose from Se-mediated selenoprotein synthesis and Ga³⁺ competition with Fe³⁺ during cellular uptake (Fig. 6C) [27]. Mechanistically, GaSeₓ NPs stabilized GPx4 by enhancing its translation and preventing ubiquitin-mediated degradation. This preserved mitochondrial function and reduced lipid peroxidation. At the same time, Ga³⁺ antagonized excess iron, lowering intracellular Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ levels without disrupting normal iron homeostasis. In addition, activation of the NFE2 like bZIP transcription factor 2 (Nrf2)–heme oxygenase 1 (HO-1) pathway further strengthened cellular antioxidant defenses. In vitro, GaSeₓ treatment reversed IL-1β-induced chondrocyte senescence. It also downregulated the catabolic enzymes matrix metallopeptidase 3 and MMP13 while increasing collagen II and SRY-box transcription factor 9 expression, thereby supporting ECM integrity. In vivo, intra-articular injection of GaSeₓ in a destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) mouse model alleviated cartilage erosion, reduced osteophyte formation, and improved both gait and pain sensitivity. Overall, the available preclinical findings suggest that GaSeₓ NPs may provide a multifaceted strategy combining element supplementation, iron competition, and GPx4-related regulation to suppress ferroptosis and support cartilage homeostasis in OA.

(4)β-diketone functionalized microspheres: iron sequestration and antioxidant defense

β-diketone functionalized microspheres: iron sequestration and antioxidant defense

Iron overload–induced mitochondrial dysfunction is a major driver of chondrocyte ferroptosis in OA [45, 46]. To address this, a composite system (Gel@P-PTPC) was developed by embedding Cur-derived β-diketone functionalized micelles into GelMA hydrogel microspheres (Fig. 6D) [47]. Upon ROS-responsive disassembly, the exposed β-diketone groups chelated intracellular Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ through metal coordination, thereby selectively sequestering reactive iron. Gel@P-PTPC also reduced iron influx by downregulating TfR1 and increased CDGSH iron-sulfur domain 1 to restrict mitochondrial iron uptake, preserving mitochondrial integrity. In parallel, the antioxidant β-diketone scaffold of Cur attenuated lipid peroxidation via the Nrf2–solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11)–GPx4 pathway, restoring redox balance. Through this dual regulation of iron homeostasis and oxidative stress, Gel@P-PTPC suppressed ferroptosis and maintained ECM stability. It enhanced type II collagen expression while reducing MMP13 levels. In vivo, intra-articular injection of Gel@P-PTPC prolonged therapeutic retention, alleviated cartilage erosion, and reduced osteophyte formation. It also improved histological Osteoarthritis Research Society International scores. Taken together, the included preclinical findings suggest that β-diketone-functionalized microspheres may offer a minimally invasive approach coupling metal coordination–based iron sequestration with antioxidant defense to mitigate ferroptosis-associated cartilage degeneration in OA.

Disrupted iron metabolism has been increasingly recognized as an important contributor to ferroptosis in OA, prompting the development of NMs aimed at restoring iron homeostasis [48]. The included studies illustrate several complementary approaches. ApoFn nanocages combine iron chelation with transferrin receptor–mediated targeting in inflamed chondrocytes. Catechol-containing copolymer NPs integrate iron-binding catechol groups with ROS-scavenging components while enabling Cur delivery. GaSeₓ NPs address elemental imbalance through Ga-mediated competition with iron and Se-associated antioxidant support. β-Diketone-functionalized microspheres couple reactive iron sequestration with redox modulation and prolonged intra-articular retention. Collectively, the available preclinical evidence suggests that regulation of iron homeostasis may represent an important mechanistic axis in ferroptosis-targeted OA nanomedicine. Across different nanoplatforms, reported benefits included attenuation of ferroptotic injury, preservation of ECM integrity, and improvement of joint pathology in preclinical models.

ROS scavenging and oxidative stress modulation

Excess ROS, such as H₂O₂ and O₂⁻·, contribute to ferroptosis by accelerating lipid peroxidation and impairing mitochondrial function [49]. In the included studies, NMs countered this process mainly through two strategies. Some mimicked natural antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx), to directly eliminate ROS. Others employed ROS-responsive drug release to inhibit ROS-producing enzymes such as NADPH oxidase 4 (NOX4), thereby alleviating oxidative stress in a targeted manner.

ROS-responsive microspheres: targeted NOX4 inhibition and redox modulation

ROS-responsive microspheres: targeted NOX4 inhibition and redox modulation

NOX4 is a key driver of oxidative stress and ferroptosis through its regulation of intracellular ROS production [50–52]. To counter this, researchers developed a ROS-responsive microsphere system composed of poly (vinyl acetate–methyl methacrylate) (PVA-MMA) hydrogel shells encapsulating disulfide-linked mPEG-TK NPs loaded with GLX351322 (GLX), a selective NOX4 inhibitor (Fig. 7A) [53]. The dual-layer design, termed mPEG-TK-GLX@PVA-MMA, provides mechanical stability while enabling ROS-triggered sustained drug release. In vitro, the microspheres exhibited excellent biocompatibility at therapeutic concentrations and effectively suppressed tert-butyl hydroperoxide (TBHP)–induced macrophage activation. They downregulated pro-inflammatory cytokines, reduced ROS and superoxide accumulation, and alleviated iron overload. Lipid peroxidation assays and mitochondrial membrane potential analyses further confirmed robust protection against ferroptosis. By combining selective NOX4 inhibition with ROS-responsive release, these microspheres were reported to interrupt the feed-forward loop linking oxidative stress, inflammation, and ferroptosis in macrophages. This study suggests the potential utility of ROS-adaptive delivery systems for prolonging intra-articular drug retention and achieving more precise redox modulation in OA models.

(2)Bio-heterojunction nanoplatforms: multi-enzyme mimicry for ROS scavenging

Bio-heterojunction nanoplatforms: multi-enzyme mimicry for ROS scavenging

To counter excessive oxidative stress and ferroptosis in OA, bioengineered heterojunction nanoplatforms have emerged as versatile redox modulators. A representative system, MBM-HW, combines 2D Mo₄/₃B₂₋X MBene nanosheets with a cerium (Ce)–gallic acid metal–polyphenol network, coated with a hyaluronic acid–WYRGRL (HW) shell for cartilage targeting (Fig. 7B) [54]. This design exploits the abundant surface vacancies of MBenes and the redox-active states of molybdenum (Mo) and Ce, enabling synergistic electron transfer and multi-enzyme mimetic activity, including SOD, CAT, and GPx functions. MBM-HW exhibits potent ROS scavenging, responsive release of active agents in acidic or ROS-enriched microenvironments, and enhanced chondrocyte uptake via hyaluronic acid receptors and collagen II–specific peptide interactions. In vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated that MBM-HW alleviates mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular senescence, and inflammation while preserving ECM homeostasis. It upregulates GPx4, suppresses lipid peroxidation and iron overload, and effectively blocks chondrocyte ferroptosis. Multi-omics analyses further revealed that these therapeutic effects are mediated through suppression of the protein kinase R-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase/eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2α integrated stress response, promoting chondrocyte survival under stress conditions. Overall, the available preclinical evidence suggests that multi-enzyme mimetic bio-heterojunctions may function as redox-adaptive nanoplatforms capable of modulating the interconnected processes of oxidative stress, inflammation, and ferroptosis in OA.

(3)Pt-based nanozyme hydrogels: sustained ROS scavenging and ferroptosis protection

Pt-based nanozyme hydrogels: sustained ROS scavenging and ferroptosis protection

Platinum (Pt)-based nanozymes have emerged as promising agents for redox modulation in inflammation due to their robust catalytic stability and multi-enzyme mimetic activities [55]. In a recent study, Pt NPs were encapsulated within silk fibroin/oxidized pullulan (SF/oxPL) hydrogels via Schiff-base crosslinking, forming an injectable, self-healing intra-articular platform (Fig. 7C) [33]. This composite hydrogel enabled pH-responsive, sustained release of Pt nanozymes over several weeks, providing stable ROS scavenging within the inflammatory joint microenvironment. Functionally, the Pt@SF/oxPL system exhibited CAT- and SOD–like activities, efficiently eliminating H₂O₂, •OH, and •O₂⁻ radicals. In vitro, ROS clearance prevented lipid peroxidation, preserved mitochondrial integrity, reduced malondialdehyde levels, and restored glutathione (GSH), collectively attenuating ferroptotic chondrocyte death. Pt@SF/oxPL also upregulated ferroptosis-protective proteins and decreased pro-inflammatory cytokines. In vivo, intra-articular injection of Pt@SF/oxPL in OA rats mitigated cartilage degradation, restored ECM proteins, and reduced matrix catabolic enzymes. It also alleviated synovitis, improved subchondral bone microarchitecture, and preserved cartilage, as confirmed by histological and immunohistochemical analyses. These findings suggest that nanozyme-integrated hydrogels may provide a sustained approach for ROS regulation and ferroptosis suppression, with potential relevance for future OA intervention studies.

(4)Antioxidant polymeric NPs: ROS scavenging and GPx4 preservation

Antioxidant polymeric NPs: ROS scavenging and GPx4 preservation

Antioxidant polymer NPs have emerged as promising platforms to counteract ROS-driven ferroptosis in OA [56]. Poly(p-coumaric acid) nanoparticles (PCA NPs), derived from the phytophenolic monomer p-coumaric acid, exhibit potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects in temporomandibular joint OA (Fig. 7D) [57]. PCA NPs scavenge ROS, suppress matrix metalloproteinase activation, and restore mitochondrial function, thereby protecting chondrocytes from ferroptotic death and promoting ECM repair. In vivo, intra-articular delivery of PCA NPs significantly alleviated cartilage degeneration and subchondral bone erosion, outperforming hyaluronic acid in long-term efficacy.

Complementing this approach, supramolecular nanodrugs constructed from epigallocatechin-3-gallate and selenomethionine via Mannich condensation (ES NDs) provide both ROS scavenging and Se supplementation (Fig. 7E) [58]. ES NDs preserve GPx4 activity, reduce lipid peroxidation, and correct Fe²⁺ overload, thereby maintaining chondrocyte metabolism. In murine OA models, intra-articular ES NDs achieved prolonged joint retention and effectively mitigated cartilage destruction. Taken together, the included studies suggest that antioxidant polymer NPs may offer a versatile approach for ROS clearance, ferroptosis suppression, and chondroprotection in preclinical OA models.

(5)ROS-responsive nanocarriers: targeted drug release and redox regulation

ROS-responsive nanocarriers: targeted drug release and redox regulation

Stimuli-responsive nanoplatforms have emerged as advanced strategies for OA therapy by enabling controlled drug release within the oxidative microenvironment of diseased joints [17, 59]. One example is ROS-sensitive fenofibrate-loaded dextran nanoparticles (FN-CNPs) (Fig. 7F) [60]. These amphiphilic carriers, modified with type II collagen-targeting peptides, selectively release fenofibrate under elevated ROS conditions, reducing lipid peroxidation and restoring mitochondrial homeostasis. FN-CNPs upregulate ferroptosis-protective proteins, protect chondrocytes from ferroptotic death, and concurrently downregulate inflammatory mediators and catabolic enzymes. In murine OA models, intra-articular FN-CNPs markedly alleviated cartilage degeneration and preserved ECM integrity.

Complementing this approach, a hybrid hollow Prussian blue@lipoic acid@polydopamine (HPB@LA@PDA) nanoplatform combines hollow Prussian blue nanozymes with lipoic acid, encapsulated within a polydopamine shell (Fig. 7G) [61]. This construct provides sustained, pH-sensitive release of antioxidant payloads and exploits the multi-enzyme-like activity of the Prussian blue core to neutralize ROS. HPB@LA@PDA synergistically reduces oxidative stress, modulates macrophage polarization, and protects chondrocytes from apoptosis and ferroptosis. In vivo, it significantly attenuated cartilage destruction and remodeled the inflammatory joint microenvironment. Together, these studies suggest that ROS-responsive drug-delivery systems may provide versatile approaches for targeted drug release, redox modulation, and ferroptosis suppression in preclinical OA settings.

Beyond dysregulated iron metabolism, excessive ROS and oxidative stress are also important drivers of chondrocyte ferroptosis and cartilage degeneration in OA [62]. The included NM-based strategies were developed to restore redox balance through targeted ROS scavenging and adaptive stress modulation [63]. ROS-responsive microspheres encapsulating NOX4 inhibitors targeted an important enzymatic source of oxidative stress. Multi-enzyme mimetic bio-heterojunctions, composed of Mo- and Ce-based components, emulated endogenous antioxidant activities to preserve mitochondrial function and maintain ECM stability. Pt nanozyme-loaded hydrogels provided sustained ROS clearance within the joint cavity. Complementary strategies included antioxidant polymer NPs delivering phytophenolic or Se-based antioxidants, as well as ROS-responsive drug-delivery systems enabling stimuli-triggered cargo release in oxidative microenvironments. Collectively, current preclinical evidence suggests that oxidative stress regulation represents an important mechanistic component of ferroptosis-targeted OA nanomedicine. Across studies, reported effects included ROS reduction, attenuation of lipid peroxidation, preservation of mitochondrial function, and improvement of cartilage-related outcomes.

Signal pathway-mediated ferroptosis regulation

While iron homeostasis and oxidative stress are widely implicated in ferroptosis in OA, growing evidence also suggests that intracellular signaling pathways influence chondrocyte fate and inflammatory responses [6, 64]. In the included studies, NMs were further engineered to modulate signaling cascades integrating oxidative, metabolic, and inflammatory cues, beyond direct ROS scavenging or labile iron chelation. By acting at upstream regulatory levels, these strategies were reported to interfere with pathways linking ferroptosis to matrix degradation and synovial inflammation.

TRPV1–CaMKII Axis: magnetothermal and photothermal activation

TRPV1–CaMKII Axis: magnetothermal and photothermal activation

Transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) plays a key role in OA pathology by modulating macrophage-driven inflammation and chondrocyte ferroptosis [65–67]. Conventional pharmacological agonists, such as capsaicin, are limited by systemic toxicity and poor spatiotemporal control. To overcome these drawbacks, nanotechnology-enabled magnetothermal and photothermal platforms have been developed for precise TRPV1 activation. Lv et al. engineered magnetic NPs conjugated with TRPV1 monoclonal antibodies (Fig. 8A) [68]. Under alternating magnetic field stimulation, these NPs generated localized heat sufficient to open TRPV1 channels. This magnetothermal activation enhanced calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII) phosphorylation, suppressed macrophage cytokine production, inhibited chondrocyte ferroptosis, and alleviated synovitis, cartilage degeneration, and pain in DMM models.

Similarly, Li et al. developed citrate-stabilized gold nanorods conjugated with anti-TRPV1 antibodies, which respond to near-infrared (NIR) irradiation to activate TRPV1 in a controlled manner (Fig. 8B) [69]. This photothermal stimulation restored GPx4 expression, reduced lipid peroxidation, suppressed ferroptosis, and mitigated cartilage and subchondral bone damage in OA mice. Together, these studies suggest that nanoscale magnetothermal and photothermal platforms can selectively engage the TRPV1–CaMKII axis and may provide a minimally invasive, pathogenesis-oriented approach for suppressing both inflammation and ferroptosis in preclinical OA models.

(2)Regulation of inflammatory–metabolic crosstalk

Regulation of inflammatory–metabolic crosstalk

Inflammatory signaling and metabolic reprogramming interact closely to drive ferroptosis in OA [70]. Li et al. reported that synovial macrophages in OA exhibit downregulated interleukin-10 (IL-10) receptor α and enhanced glycolysis mediated by hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) [28]. This metabolic shift amplifies inflammatory cytokine release and indirectly promotes chondrocyte ferroptosis, establishing a pathogenic link between synovial inflammation and cartilage degeneration. To counteract this, a biomimetic NP system (MI@UN) was engineered, comprising NO-prednisolone encapsulated in UIO-66-NH₂, surface-modified with IL-10, and coated with macrophage membranes (Fig. 8C). MI@UN effectively suppressed synovial glycolysis, alleviated inflammation, and protected chondrocytes from ferroptotic damage, thereby slowing OA progression in vivo.

Complementing this approach, Chen et al. identified ethyl gallate as a potent antioxidant and ferroptosis inhibitor and incorporated it into a pH-responsive zinc (Zn)-based metal–organic framework (EG@PZA) (Fig. 8D) [30]. This system enabled lysosomal escape, acid-triggered release, and bioactive Zn²⁺ supplementation. EG@PZA attenuated oxidative stress, ECM degradation, ferroptosis, and chondrocyte senescence, resulting in significant structural and functional improvements in murine OA models. Taken together, the available preclinical studies suggest that nanoplatforms targeting inflammatory–metabolic crosstalk may help normalize macrophage metabolism, suppress chondrocyte ferroptosis, and modulate the joint microenvironment in OA.

(3)HIF-1α signaling: glycolytic reprogramming and ferroptosis resistance

HIF-1α signaling: glycolytic reprogramming and ferroptosis resistance

Aberrant glycolytic metabolism is increasingly recognized as a driver of chondrocyte ferroptosis in OA [71, 72]. α-Solanine, a glycoalkaloid extracted from potatoes, has been investigated for its ability to modulate this metabolic imbalance. In lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced OA models, α-solanine suppressed ferroptotic hallmarks—including GPx4 downregulation, lipid ROS accumulation, and mitochondrial damage—while restoring antioxidant defenses such as GSH. Mechanistically, α-solanine attenuated excessive glycolysis by downregulating key enzymes, reducing adenosine triphosphate (ATP) overproduction and metabolic stress. RNA-seq analysis and subsequent validation identified HIF-1α as the central mediator of this effect. The compound accelerated HIF-1α protein decay and inhibited HIF-1α-dependent glycolytic gene expression, linking metabolic reprogramming directly to ferroptosis resistance. To enhance delivery efficiency, α-solanine was incorporated into PEI-modified UIO-66-NH₂ metal–organic framework nanoparticles (USP) (Fig. 8E) [29]. This nanocarrier exhibited high drug-loading capacity, cartilage-targeting ability, and sustained release, markedly amplifying α-solanine’s therapeutic efficacy in OA rats. USP treatment restored cartilage integrity, reduced subchondral bone sclerosis, and more effectively downregulated ferroptotic and glycolytic markers compared with free drug. Collectively, these findings suggest that HIF-1α may represent an important link between glycolytic dysregulation and ferroptosis, and they support the potential relevance of α-solanine-loaded nanoplatforms for metabolism-oriented intervention in OA models.

(4)Mitochondrial quality control: nanoplatforms restoring homeostasis and mitophagy

Mitochondrial quality control: nanoplatforms restoring homeostasis and mitophagy

Mitochondrial dysfunction is increasingly recognized as a central contributor to chondrocyte ferroptosis and OA progression [73, 74]. NM-based strategies have recently been explored to restore mitochondrial homeostasis by enhancing mitophagy and suppressing ROS [75]. Yi et al. developed pH-responsive lipid NPs (LNPs@UA) encapsulating Urolithin A, designed to exploit the acidic joint microenvironment and target mitochondria via triphenylphosphine modification (Fig. 8F) [76]. These NPs efficiently localized to mitochondria, reduced ROS levels, and promoted PTEN-mediated mitophagy, alleviating structural mitochondrial damage. LNPs@UA also upregulated antioxidant and ferroptosis-protective markers—including GPx4, SLC7A11, HO-1, Nrf2—while suppressing ACSL4 expression and intracellular Fe²⁺ accumulation, collectively preventing ferroptosis. In vivo, intra-articular administration of LNPs@UA ameliorated synovial inflammation, reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, and preserved cartilage matrix integrity. Transcriptomic analyses further confirmed suppression of NF-κB signaling and activation of reparative pathways, highlighting the dual role of these NPs in mitochondrial protection and inflammation control. These findings suggest that restoration of mitochondrial homeostasis—through targeted mitophagy activation and ferroptosis inhibition—may represent a relevant nanotherapeutic direction in preclinical OA research.

Beyond direct iron chelation and ROS scavenging, targeting intracellular signaling pathways provides an additional layer for ferroptosis regulation in OA. Magnetothermal and photothermal nanoplatforms engaged the TRPV1–CaMKII axis and were associated with suppression of macrophage inflammation and chondrocyte ferroptosis. Interventions targeting inflammatory–metabolic crosstalk emphasized the role of synovial macrophage glycolysis in sustaining cytokine release and ferroptotic stress, and biomimetic or metal–organic framework-based systems were reported to modulate this pathogenic loop. At the chondrocyte level, α-solanine nanodelivery inhibited HIF-1α-related glycolytic reprogramming, while mitochondria-targeted lipid NPs carrying Urolithin A promoted mitophagy and attenuated ROS-driven ferroptotic cascades. Collectively, the available preclinical evidence suggests that signaling pathways function as important upstream modulators of ferroptosis in OA. These studies also indicate that nanotechnology may influence ion channel activity, metabolic flux, and mitochondrial quality control in ways relevant to cartilage protection in preclinical models.

Targeting central ferroptosis regulators

Recent preclinical studies suggest that directly targeting central ferroptosis-related regulators may represent an effective strategy for OA intervention. Wang et al. developed ROS-responsive cerium@deferasirox@pterostilbene (Ce@D&P) NPs, integrating deferasirox (iron chelator), pterostilbene (antioxidant), and Ce ions (multi-enzyme mimetic) to achieve multi-target modulation of ferroptosis (Fig. 9A) [32]. These NPs chelated intracellular iron, scavenged ROS, and reinforced GPx4 activity while downregulating ACSL4, thereby restoring redox balance and attenuating lipid peroxidation. Beyond ferroptosis suppression, Ce@D&P promoted ECM synthesis and inhibited catabolic enzymes, highlighting dual chondroprotective functions. Transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses further revealed modulation of lipid metabolism, phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B, and mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling pathways, demonstrating broad ferroptosis-related regulatory effects. in vivo, intra-articular administration alleviated cartilage degradation and preserved joint integrity in DMM models, confirming therapeutic potential.

Complementing this approach, Wang et al. identified DNA damage-inducible transcript 3 (DDIT3) as a key driver of ferroptosis in OA cartilage, linked to GPx4 suppression (Fig. 9B) [31]. To counteract DDIT3, starlike gold nanoparticles (Au NPs) were engineered for efficient siDDIT3 delivery combined with NIR-mediated photothermal therapy. The thorn-like morphology enhanced small interfering RNA (siRNA) transfection, promoted cellular uptake, and enabled surface-enhanced Raman scattering for real-time tracking. Functionally, siDDIT3-loaded Au NPs reduced ROS accumulation, limited Fe²⁺ deposition, and restored GPx4 levels, effectively suppressing ferroptosis. In murine OA models, this dual strategy preserved ECM composition, reduced subchondral bone hyperplasia, and improved overall joint pathology. Taken together, the available preclinical findings suggest that nanodrugs directly targeting key ferroptosis-related mediators—including iron metabolism, GPx4, ACSL4, and DDIT3—may exert coordinated anti-ferroptotic and chondroprotective effects. By integrating iron chelation, redox modulation, and gene silencing within advanced nanoplatforms, these approaches provide a useful framework for future OA nanomedicine research.

Cell membrane-camouflaged nanoplatforms for ferroptosis regulation in OA

Cell membrane-camouflaged nanoplatforms have recently emerged as a notable strategy in preclinical OA research, offering immune evasion, targeted delivery, and ferroptosis modulation [77, 78]. By harnessing the intrinsic biological properties of cellular membranes, these systems enhance joint retention and achieve synergistic therapeutic effects. One approach employs chondrocyte membrane-coated, anti-ferroptotic drug-loaded liposomes (CM@Lipo), which inherit homotypic recognition and cartilage-targeting capabilities (Fig. 10A) [26]. These NPs prolong drug retention in the joint cavity and selectively deliver anti-ferroptotic agents to diseased cartilage. In preclinical models, CM@Lipo effectively reduced lipid peroxidation, limited Fe²⁺ accumulation, and preserved ECM integrity, thereby protecting cartilage from ferroptotic injury. A complementary strategy uses Cur-loaded biomimetic nanosponges (CM@Cur-NPs) coated with macrophage membranes (Fig. 10B) [79]. The macrophage-derived shell endowed these nanosponges with cytokine-scavenging activity, neutralizing tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 while enhancing Cur bioavailability. This dual action suppressed oxidative stress, downregulated ferroptosis drivers, and upregulated protective markers such as SLC7A11 and GPx4. In vivo, CM@Cur-NPs attenuated cartilage degeneration and promoted ECM synthesis, highlighting their ability to concurrently suppress inflammation and ferroptosis. Expanding the therapeutic scope, M2 macrophage membrane-coated liposomal systems carrying kartogenin (CM@Lipos-KGN) were designed to both mitigate cartilage degeneration and stimulate regeneration (Fig. 10C) [80]. The M2 membrane conferred anti-inflammatory activity and improved NPs uptake under inflammatory conditions, while kartogenin promoted chondrogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells. Notably, CM@Lipos-KGN stabilized metabolic homeostasis, suppressed chondrocyte ferroptosis, and activated regenerative pathways, resulting in dual protection and repair of cartilage in OA models. Collectively, the available preclinical studies suggest that these biomimetic nanoplatforms may broaden the design space of ferroptosis-targeted OA nanomedicine. By integrating homotypic targeting, immune modulation, ferroptosis suppression, and, in some cases, regenerative stimulation, cell membrane-coated systems may help address several limitations of conventional NPs in joint retention and biological specificity. Reported benefits included attenuation of cartilage degeneration, preservation of ECM integrity, and improvement of joint-related outcomes in preclinical models.

Integrative synthesis of ferroptosis-targeted nanomaterial strategies in OA

Taken together, the available preclinical evidence suggests that ferroptosis-targeted nanomaterial strategies in OA converge on several interconnected mechanistic axes, including restoration of iron homeostasis, suppression of oxidative stress, modulation of upstream signaling pathways, direct targeting of central ferroptosis-related mediators, and biomimetic enhancement of retention and biological specificity. Although these nanoplatforms differ substantially in composition, structural design, cargo loading, and delivery behavior, their reported therapeutic effects show notable convergence at the outcome level. Across in vitro and in vivo models, these interventions were associated with attenuation of lipid peroxidation, preservation of mitochondrial integrity, reduction of ferroptosis, improvement of ECM homeostasis, and alleviation of cartilage degeneration.

From a translational perspective, the included studies suggest that the potential value of OA nanomedicine lies not only in drug delivery, but also in the capacity of nanoplatforms to integrate targeting, microenvironment-responsive release, and intrinsic bioactivity within a single system. At the same time, the current evidence remains limited to heterogeneous preclinical models, and differences in experimental design, mechanistic readouts, and methodological quality should be considered when interpreting the consistency and generalizability of the reported findings. Overall, the current literature supports ferroptosis modulation as a mechanistically relevant and therapeutically promising direction in OA nanomedicine, while also highlighting the need for stronger standardization, independent validation, and more human-relevant translational models.

Translational challenges and emerging frontiers

Despite the encouraging preclinical advances summarized above, a considerable gap remains between proof-of-concept and clinical translation. Most available evidence is derived from standardized rodent models and simplified cellular systems, which only partially recapitulate the chronic, heterogeneous, and mechanically complex nature of human OA. Consequently, these findings—though promising—must be interpreted with caution, as critical gaps persist in mechanistic clarity, standardized evaluation methodologies, and translational preparedness. Furthermore, disease heterogeneity, the intricate regulation of ferroptotic pathways, and the stringent requirements for clinical-grade nanomedicine collectively pose formidable barriers to clinical application. Limitations of animal and cellular models

Limitations of animal and cellular models

Preclinical research commonly relies on in vitro systems and animal models as transitional platforms toward clinical application. Yet, animal models face persistent drawbacks, including interspecies variability, ethical concerns, and substantial financial costs. Notably, despite encouraging results in animal testing, over 90% of drug candidates fail to advance through clinical trials [81]. At present, most preclinical studies rely on rodent models. Commonly used approaches include surgically induced methods such as anterior cruciate ligament transection or DMM, as well as chemically induced models using sodium iodoacetate (Table 3). In cell-based experiments, oxidative or inflammatory stress is typically triggered by agents such as IL-1β, H₂O₂, erastin, TNF-α, or LPS (Table 2). While these systems are useful for probing ferroptosis-related mechanisms, they largely mimic acute injury or transient inflammation and fail to capture chronic drivers such as aging, obesity, and menopause that are central to human OA [82–84]. Two-dimensional (2D) culture systems are further limited by oversimplified physiology, rendering them unreliable for predicting pharmacokinetic properties such as absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion [85]. Rodents, such as rats and mice, also differ substantially from humans in cartilage thickness, metabolic rate, immune responses, and iron-handling pathways, leading to discrepancies in NM penetration, retention, and ferroptotic regulation [86–89]. Large-animal models—including sheep, dogs, and pigs—better approximate human joint size, cartilage structure, and load-bearing mechanics. Nevertheless, their application is constrained by high costs, ethical concerns, limited standardization, and a lack of long-term or ferroptosis-focused studies. Together, these limitations reduce the predictive value of current preclinical research. Structural and biomechanical differences between animal and human cartilage, compounded by the absence of models faithfully reproducing chronic OA phenotypes, underscore the urgent need for improved translational platforms [90, 91]. Importantly, the penetration and retention of NMs vary considerably across species, and findings derived from commonly used animals may overestimate clinical drug delivery efficiency. These shortcomings must be carefully acknowledged, and future efforts should prioritize the development of human-relevant, chronic, and integrative models to more accurately evaluate ferroptosis-targeted nanomedicines for OA.

(2)Incomplete mechanistic understanding of ferroptosis in OA

Incomplete mechanistic understanding of ferroptosis in OA

The role of ferroptosis in OA pathogenesis remains incompletely defined. Joint-resident cells—including chondrocytes, synovial fibroblasts, and immune populations—exhibit differential susceptibilities to ferroptosis, yet their coordinated contributions to disease progression are still unresolved [70]. Ferroptosis is also tightly intertwined with other PCD pathways, such as apoptosis, pyroptosis, and autophagy [92], but the lack of an integrated regulatory network hampers efforts to establish causal hierarchies or identify therapeutic windows. Microenvironmental cues—mechanical loading, hypoxia, iron overload, and chronic inflammation—further shape ferroptotic activity by modulating canonical regulators including GPx4, System Xc⁻, and ACSL4-mediated lipid peroxidation [11, 93, 94]. However, it is not yet clear whether ferroptosis functions as a primary driver of cartilage degeneration or merely acts as a downstream amplifier, and this role may shift across different stages of OA. Methodological limitations compound this uncertainty: current findings are largely descriptive, and markers such as lipid peroxidation or GPx4 loss overlap with other PCD pathways [7]. A particularly unresolved question is the identity of the terminal executor that mediates cell demise following lipid peroxidation. Clarifying this mechanism could help define unique hallmarks that discriminate ferroptosis from other cell death forms [95, 96]. Addressing these gaps will be essential for establishing reliable biomarkers of ferroptosis in OA and for guiding rational therapeutic design.

(3)Neglect of disease heterogeneity and personalized strategies

Neglect of disease heterogeneity and personalized strategies

Over the past decades, heterogeneity in OA phenotypes and endotypes has been a key factor underlying the failure of many clinical trials testing disease-modifying OA drugs [1]. Variations in clinical presentation, imaging features, and biomarker profiles hinder consistent efficacy, as distinct phenotypes often display variable sensitivity to the same therapeutic regimen. To address this complexity, several classification systems have been proposed. For example, Mobasheri et al. (2017) identified phenotypes characterized by synovitis, metabolic dysregulation, aging, cartilage damage, traumatic injury, and subchondral bone alterations [97], while the Categorization of Osteoarthritis CHecklist project distinguished load-driven, structure-driven, inflammatory-driven, metabolic-driven, systemic factor-driven, and mixed types [98]. Importantly, these categories are not mutually exclusive, and multiple phenotypes may coexist within a single patient, underscoring the necessity of precision therapy. Personalizing treatment for defined OA subgroups—such as inflammatory or metabolic OA—has the potential to enhance therapeutic efficacy [99]. However, research at the intersection of ferroptosis, OA, and nanomedicine continues to treat OA as a uniform condition, neglecting subgroup-specific differences in ferroptotic susceptibility and therapeutic responsiveness. The lack of phenotype-tailored NM strategies and the absence of companion diagnostic tools limit translational relevance and may partly explain the heterogeneous outcomes reported in preclinical studies.

(4)Insufficient multi-target and integrative therapeutic strategies

Insufficient multi-target and integrative therapeutic strategies

OA is a whole-joint disease characterized by cartilage degradation, synovitis, subchondral bone remodeling, muscle atrophy, and alterations in gut microbiota [100]. In a recent study (June 2025), Wei Xiang et al. reported that macrophages progressively acquire senescent phenotypes during OA progression. These senescent macrophages promote skeletal muscle atrophy through the paracrine induction of ferroptosis [101]. Importantly, the cardiac drug coenzyme Q₁₀ (CoQ₁₀) was found to counteract this process by inhibiting ferroptosis, thereby mitigating muscle wasting. Collectively, this work delineated a verifiable “OA microenvironment–senescent macrophages–ferroptosis–muscle atrophy” axis and highlighted three nanomedicine intervention points at the cellular, subcellular, and metabolic levels.

Despite recent progress, most ferroptosis-targeted nanomedicine studies still concentrate on isolated processes, such as chondrocyte death or inflammation. This narrow focus overlooks the multifactorial and systemic nature of OA. Integration with complementary approaches—including mesenchymal stem cell therapy and modulation of the gut–joint axis [102]—remains scarce, leading to incomplete therapeutic strategies. Future research should embed NM-based interventions within multi-target and multimodal treatment frameworks to support personalized OA management, reduce side effects, and improve long-term outcomes [103, 104]. These strategies must also move beyond cartilage to address systemic modulators, thereby reflecting the true complexity of OA.

(5)Barriers to clinical translation

Barriers to clinical translation

Although preclinical outcomes are encouraging, no NM formulation has yet received clinical approval for OA treatment, underscoring a persistent translational gap. This translational gap is not unique to OA, nor is it exclusive to nanomedicine. Across the broader medical field, discrepancies between promising preclinical outcomes and eventual clinical approval are common, particularly for complex therapeutic platforms [105]. As noted earlier, nanomedicine-based therapies have already achieved regulatory approval in several non-OA indications, indicating that clinical translation is feasible under appropriate conditions. For OA, the key challenge is therefore not whether NM-based therapy can be translated in principle, but how to align formulation design, disease context, manufacturability, and evaluation strategies to enable successful translation [103]. This underscores the importance of simplified yet robust formulation design, disease-relevant patient stratification, clinically meaningful outcome selection, and early consideration of quality requirements [106, 107]. Accordingly, successful translation in OA will require not only continued preclinical innovation, but also greater attention to clinical feasibility, cost-effectiveness, and biosafety [108].

Substantial scientific and preclinical evaluation challenges also remain. Owing to limited experimental windows, safety assessments typically emphasize acute toxicity, whereas long-term biostability, immunogenicity, and potential disruption of iron homeostasis have not been systematically assessed [103, 109]. Moreover, standardized methodologies for characterizing biodistribution, pharmacokinetics, and critical quality attributes—such as particle size distribution, surface charge, coating stability, and iron release kinetics—are lacking, which restricts reproducibility and comparability across laboratories [99, 110].

Even if upstream scientific issues are addressed, challenges in manufacturing and quality control continue to impede nanomedicine translation. The shift from laboratory-scale synthesis to industrial production frequently alters NP morphology, surface characteristics, or drug loading, which can reduce therapeutic efficacy. Many nanodrugs are intentionally designed as multicomponent systems to confer unique physicochemical properties and enhanced therapeutic benefits in OA. However, the dependence on expensive raw materials, complex fabrication steps, and demanding purification protocols often results in low yields and inconsistent batches, thereby restricting industrial scalability [109]. To enable widespread clinical use, large-scale manufacturing requires strict batch-to-batch uniformity under Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) regulations. Yet the physicochemical features of NPs remain highly sensitive to even minor fluctuations during production. Sterility control presents a further challenge: conventional terminal sterilization is unsuitable for formulations that contain heat-labile biomolecules or structurally complex assemblies, as such methods may compromise integrity and cause functional degradation [111]. As a result, aseptic processing is frequently employed, but this approach necessitates highly stringent facilities, tightly controlled environments, and labor-intensive procedures, all of which substantially increase manufacturing costs. Additional stability-related issues—including lyophilization procedures, storage conditions, and long-term preservation—further complicate the pathway to clinical translation. Thus, compliance with GMP standards is indispensable to guarantee reproducibility, batch uniformity, and rigorous quality control, which are prerequisites for regulatory approval and clinical adoption [112]. According to the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA), nanomedicine-based products must also adhere to Title 21 of the Code of Federal Regulations, which requires the definition of critical quality attributes and validation of in-process controls to ensure consistency, potency, and purity across production batches.

Finally, the regulatory landscape for “OA-ferroptosis-nanomedicine” remains ambiguous, as these products may fall under drug, device, or combination classifications. Divergent evaluation frameworks across agencies further complicate global translation. Overcoming these barriers will require interdisciplinary collaboration to refine design principles, strengthen standardization and independent validation, and better align international regulatory expectations, thereby narrowing the divide between NM innovation and clinical application in OA management.

Future prospects

Whole-joint targeting strategies in ferroptosis-oriented nanomedicine for OA

Whole-joint targeting strategies in ferroptosis-oriented nanomedicine for OA

Current investigations in the field of “OA-ferroptosis-nanomedicine” have predominantly concentrated on chondrocytes, which play a central role in the pathophysiology of OA (Table 2) [113]. However, OA is now recognized as a whole-joint disease, with pathological changes affecting not only articular cartilage but also the subchondral bone, synovial membrane, ligaments, and periarticular tissues [4]. Emerging evidence suggests that ferroptosis may contribute to inflammatory activation in synoviocytes and to aberrant iron metabolism in osteoblasts and subchondral bone, yet these compartments remain underexplored. A major obstacle is the limited ability of NMs to penetrate deeper joint regions and maintain sufficient local retention [114]. Beyond physical barriers, tissue-specific microenvironments—such as vascularization in the synovium or hypoxia in the subchondral niche—further influence NP distribution and ferroptosis regulation. Rational modulation of NP attributes, including size, morphology, and surface chemistry, may enhance tissue penetration and improve targeting of these non-cartilaginous structures. Such design principles are critical for developing next-generation nanomedicines that integrate therapeutic and diagnostic functions across the entire joint. Addressing OA as a complex organ-level disorder, rather than a cartilage-centric disease, will be pivotal for advancing comprehensive and clinically relevant theranostic strategies [112].

(2)Hybrid membrane and organelle-inspired biomimetic nanomedicine

Hybrid membrane and organelle-inspired biomimetic nanomedicine

Recently, cell-membrane-coated NDDSs have emerged as a promising therapeutic platform with the potential to overcome several obstacles in OA treatment [77]. Unlike conventional NPs modified by chemical or physical surface engineering, these biomimetic systems are fabricated by fusing synthetic cores with natural cell membranes. Such coatings preserve the inherent complexity of membrane-bound receptors, proteins, and bioactive molecules, thereby conferring advantages including immune evasion, tissue-specific targeting, and surface functionality [78]. Compared with uncoated NPs, cell-membrane-coated constructs exhibit prolonged intra-articular retention, reduced off-target toxicity, and enhanced therapeutic efficacy [115, 116]. For example, chondrocyte membranes retain chondrocyte-specific membrane proteins. These include chondrocyte integrins, CD44, cartilage oligomeric matrix protein, ATP-binding cassette transporter proteins (ABCB1, P-glycoprotein), calcium channels, and glycosaminoglycan receptors [117]. These membrane proteins enable chondrocyte membrane vesicles to bind specifically to cartilage tissue. They can thus target damaged cartilage and directly participate in the cartilage repair process, providing targeted therapeutic properties [118]. Similarly, CD47 on erythrocyte or platelet membranes provides a “don’t eat me” signal that reduces immune clearance [119, 120]. To date, macrophage, red blood cell, platelet, neutrophil, mesenchymal stem cell, and chondrocyte membranes have all been employed as biomimetic coatings in preclinical nanomedicine [77, 78].

Most current research, however, relies on monotypic cell membranes, whose functions are inherently limited relative to the complexity of the joint microenvironment [121]. To address this, hybrid systems that integrate two or more types of cell or organelle membranes are attracting increasing attention. Such strategies could enable multi-functional modification, simultaneously harnessing complementary biological properties and better adapting to in vivo heterogeneity. In oncology, organelle- and hybrid-membrane nanoplatforms have already demonstrated considerable promise [122, 123]. For instance, Gong et al. developed outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM)-coated NPs that attenuated drug-induced apoptosis and thrombocytopenia, and OMM-functionalized sensors that selectively detected B-cell lymphoma protein 2 modulators [124]. Zou et al. engineered glioblastoma–mitochondrial hybrid membrane NPs, achieving enhanced blood–brain barrier penetration and superior tumor suppression with Gboxin delivery [125]. Likewise, a ternary nanovaccine composed of cancer, bacterial, and dendritic cell membranes elicited robust anti-tumor immunity in multiple preclinical models [126].

Translating these advances to OA, hybrid systems tailored to cartilage pathology are especially compelling. For example, fusing chondrocyte membranes with mitochondrial membranes could confer dual targeting: cell-level uptake through cartilage-specific markers and sub-organelle localization via mitochondrial proteins. Such platforms might directly address mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and ferroptotic susceptibility in chondrocytes [127]. Similarly, combining chondrocyte membranes with macrophage or neutrophil membranes may couple cartilage tropism with inflammation-homing and cytokine-scavenging capacities, while integration of erythrocyte- or platelet-derived components could extend residence time through immune modulation.

Despite this promise, significant challenges remain. Technical hurdles include reproducible sourcing and fusion of diverse membrane types, preservation of protein orientation and functionality, potential immunogenicity, and scalable manufacturing under clinical-grade conditions. Rigorous proteomic and lipidomic characterization will be essential to ensure reproducibility and safety. Moreover, robust in vivo validation in relevant OA models, alongside quantitative biodistribution and pharmacodynamic readouts, will be required before clinical translation. Overall, hybrid cell- and organelle-mimetic biomimicry represents a forward-looking strategy for ferroptosis-targeted nanomedicine in OA. By enabling multifunctional, disease-contextualized targeting, such platforms hold the potential to overcome key translational barriers—but their success will depend on careful design, standardized production, and comprehensive preclinical evaluation.

(3)Green and bio-inspired NMs: plant-derived exosome nanovesicles and herbal self-assembled platforms in OA–ferroptosis research

Green and bio-inspired NMs: plant-derived exosome nanovesicles and herbal self-assembled platforms in OA–ferroptosis research

Green nanotechnology represents a sustainable and bio-inspired strategy for the design and application of NMs, aiming to minimize environmental impact while harnessing renewable, non-toxic, and naturally derived resources [128]. By employing synthesis methods that reduce energy consumption and avoid harmful by-products, green nanotechnology not only advances eco-friendly manufacturing but also provides biocompatible and clinically relevant platforms for drug delivery [129–131]. Within the emerging “OA–ferroptosis–nanotechnology” framework, bio-inspired green NMs such as plant-derived exosome nanovesicles (PDENs) and self-assembled herbal bioactive materials (SAHBMs) are attracting increasing attention. These systems combine ecological sustainability with inherent therapeutic potential, offering unique opportunities to overcome the limitations of conventional synthetic nanocarriers and to directly address ferroptosis-related mechanisms in OA.

PDENs, a representative class of green NMs, are NPs secreted by plant cells, typically less than 200 nm in diameter [132]. Advances in nanotechnology, biochemistry, and molecular biology have significantly improved methods for isolating, purifying, and identifying PDENs [133]. These developments have deepened the understanding of their biological effects and broadened their prospects for biomedical applications [134]. Growing evidence highlights the therapeutic potential of PDENs, which have demonstrated efficacy in treating multiple diseases [135–137]. Beyond direct therapeutic effects, they function as naturally derived drug delivery systems with notable advantages over conventional approaches [138]. Specifically, PDENs offer superior biocompatibility, stability, biodistribution, circulation time, and cellular uptake compared with synthetic or mammalian-derived nanovesicles [135, 139]. A previous bibliometric study indicated that current PDEN-related research is concentrated on gastrointestinal and circulatory system disorders, as well as malignant tumors [140]. However, significant gaps remain regarding their cross-species regulation potential in degenerative musculoskeletal diseases. Addressing this gap underscores the need to explore PDENs within the emerging “OA–ferroptosis–nanotechnology” research framework [133].

Apart from PDENs, SAHBMs have emerged as another promising class of green NMs with growing therapeutic potential. They are increasingly regarded as valuable candidates in drug discovery pipelines [141]. Self-assembly is a process where disordered molecular units spontaneously organize into ordered architectures through noncovalent interactions such as hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking, van der Waals forces, and electrostatic attractions [142, 143]. Remarkably, many natural small molecules can undergo this process without chemical modification or auxiliary carriers, forming supramolecular constructs including NPs [144], micellar systems [145], and hydrogels [146]. In biological microenvironments, these assemblies disassemble through stimuli-responsive bond dissociation, enabling controlled release and bioactivity of the phytochemical payload [147].

Compared with their monomeric counterparts, phytochemical-derived assemblies exhibit markedly improved pharmaceutical performance. These include: (1) greater aqueous solubility and chemical stability [148], (2) enhanced translocation across biological barriers [149–151], (3) controlled drug release kinetics [152], (4) responsiveness to external stimuli for precise delivery [153], (5) synergistic therapeutic effects [154], and reduced cytotoxicity [155]. Beyond these pharmacological advantages, plant-based molecules provide new opportunities for biomaterials (BMs) design. Their assemblies offer distinctive benefits: they originate from renewable sources, ensuring ecological sustainability, and exhibit excellent biocompatibility and degradability [156, 157]. Their fabrication usually involves simple, eco-friendly processes aligned with green chemistry principles. Furthermore, owing to their botanical origin, they retain inherent therapeutic activities capable of modulating disease-related molecular pathways [158], while enabling self-delivery and complete drug-loading efficiency.

In contrast to conventional non-bioactive NMs, phytochemical-based supramolecular assemblies represent a new class of intelligent, multifunctional systems [159]. They overcome the pharmacokinetic limitations of individual phytochemicals while embodying transformative concepts in material innovation. Self-assembly strategies have been extensively investigated across diverse biomedical fields, including drug delivery, bioactive membranes, cancer diagnostics, bone tissue regeneration, and the treatment of various pathological conditions [160]. Collectively, these advances highlight SAHBMs as a rapidly progressing frontier in “OA–ferroptosis–nanotechnology” research with strong potential for clinical translation. Moreover, their intrinsic bioactivity and eco-friendly design offer promising solutions to longstanding challenges in pharmacotherapy, particularly in improving patient adherence and overall healthcare outcomes [161].

(4)Data-driven nanomedicine design: artificial intelligence and machine learning approaches

Data-driven nanomedicine design: artificial intelligence and machine learning approaches

NDDSs have demonstrated significant promise in improving therapeutic efficacy, enhancing targeting ability, and offering favorable biocompatibility in diverse biomedical contexts (Tables 2 and 3). However, their rational design remains highly challenging due to the multivariate and nonlinear relationships between physicochemical properties, formulation parameters, and biological outcomes [162]. The composition and preparation process of nanomedicines directly influence their pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic behavior, yet identifying optimal combinations of materials, ratios, and process conditions is a complex, iterative endeavor. Traditional experimental approaches often depend on empirical knowledge and labor-intensive trial-and-error optimization. Moreover, researchers still lack systematic and quantitative understanding of structure–activity relationships in nanomedicine. These limitations hinder efficient decision-making at the preclinical stage and ultimately restrict clinical translation [163].

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML), as transformative paradigms of the twenty-first century, offer powerful strategies to overcome longstanding bottlenecks in biomedical research [164–167]. These technologies have also reshaped the broader scientific landscape, with nanomedicine emerging as a particularly prominent field of application. Within this domain, the integration of AI and ML is redefining research practices and opening new opportunities for therapeutic innovation. Computational methods are increasingly used to identify biomarkers, predict toxicities, and model nanoparticle interactions with biological systems [168]. ML models can discover nonlinear relationships within multidimensional datasets, learn predictive rules from experimental and simulated data, and prioritize design strategies that maximize therapeutic performance. By analyzing large datasets that include physicochemical descriptors (e.g., particle size, surface charge, aspect ratio, coating proteins), formulation parameters, and biological outputs (e.g., drug release kinetics, intra-articular retention, chondrocyte uptake, ferroptosis inhibition), ML can identify critical determinants of NM efficacy [169–171]. This data-driven approach enables rational prioritization of candidate formulations and significantly reduces reliance on trial-and-error experiments. In turn, it enhances development efficiency, lowers costs, and accelerates clinical translation. The impact of AI, however, extends beyond design optimization. In manufacturing, it contributes to improved process refinement, higher production efficiency, and more rigorous quality assurance. In the clinical arena, AI-powered analytical tools interrogate large-scale datasets to generate diagnostic insights and support the advancement of precision medicine [172]. Taken together, these applications underscore the transformative potential of AI across the full spectrum of nanomedicine, from design and production to clinical implementation [173].

In the context of ferroptosis-targeted nanomedicine for OA, ML-guided models hold particular value. OA pathophysiology is heterogeneous, involving diverse cell types and microenvironments, while ferroptosis itself intersects with multiple PCD pathways [1, 174, 175]. ML may assist in mapping NM features to ferroptosis-relevant outcomes, such as lipid peroxidation inhibition, GPx4 rescue, or iron chelation efficiency. For example, supervised learning algorithms may predict intra-articular retention time or cartilage penetration efficiency based on NP size, surface chemistry, or membrane camouflage properties. Deep learning approaches, including graph neural networks, can represent complex nanostructures and extract design principles not easily captured by conventional models. Active learning frameworks may further accelerate formulation optimization by iteratively selecting the most informative experiments, thus maximizing insight from relatively small datasets that are common in OA research.

Although the convergence of AI, ML, and nanomedicine is still in its early stages, it presents both formidable challenges and exceptional opportunities for advancing research. Current datasets in OA nanomedicine are often limited in size, fragmented across laboratories, and generated under heterogeneous experimental conditions, which restrict the generalizability of ML models. This lack of consistency and quality constrains the generalizability of ML models and hampers their ability to construct reliable predictive frameworks, making the modeling of complex multivariate relationships computationally prohibitive [173]. Addressing these issues requires a comprehensive strategy that not only expands data resources but also improves their efficient utilization. Such an approach typically rests on three complementary pillars: systematic mining of historical knowledge, large-scale computational simulations, and automated acquisition of experimental data. Interpretability poses an additional obstacle. Predictions produced by black-box models frequently lack mechanistic clarity, reducing their acceptance in translational contexts. This limited transparency further diminishes transferability across different nanoparticle platforms or biological environments, thereby restricting broader applicability [173, 176, 177]. To overcome these barriers, closer integration of ML with domain-specific biological knowledge—such as ferroptosis signaling, oxidative stress pathways, and cartilage microenvironment dynamics—will be essential. Embedding biological context ensures that models are not only predictive but also mechanistically meaningful, strengthening their utility in nanomedicine research and clinical translation.

Looking forward, the convergence of ML with multi-omics (e.g., transcriptomics, metabolomics, and proteomics from OA tissues), imaging data, and pharmacological readouts could generate holistic datasets suitable for robust predictive modeling [178]. Closed-loop systems that couple ML predictions with automated NP synthesis and high-throughput screening may establish self-optimizing pipelines for ferroptosis-targeted OA therapies. In parallel, the development of transparent and mechanistically informed ML models will be critical for regulatory acceptance and eventual clinical translation [179].

In summary, AI- and ML-guided NM design represents a powerful frontier for accelerating the discovery and optimization of ferroptosis-targeted nanomedicine in OA. By tackling the intrinsic challenges of formulation design and linking material properties with biological responses, these strategies can shift conventional empirical methodologies toward systematic, data-driven frameworks. Such an approach not only improves therapeutic efficacy and translational feasibility but also expedites the progression from conceptual design to clinical implementation, ultimately facilitating the development of highly personalized and effective treatments [180, 181].

(5)Organoid and joint-on-chip models as next-generation platforms in OA research

Organoid and joint-on-chip models as next-generation platforms in OA research

Traditional preclinical evaluation of nanomedicines has relied on 2D cell cultures and animal models. While these approaches have been indispensable, their limitations are well recognized. Primary cells in monolayer culture often lose viability and phenotype over extended culture periods, restricting their utility for evaluating long-term, multi-dosed regimens [182, 183]. Animal models, despite being central to safety assessment, are costly, low-throughput, and often fail to capture the complexity of human joint pathophysiology [184, 185]. Inter-species differences in pharmacokinetics, immune responses, and transporter expression profiles contribute to translational discrepancies, leading to false negatives and false positives in toxicology studies [186–188]. Policy developments are accelerating the transition toward human-relevant platforms. The FDA Modernization Act 2.0 and the agency’s 2025 roadmap on animal-testing alternatives highlight the urgency of moving beyond traditional models [189]. A 2025 feature further described “animal methods bias”, the tendency of journals and funders to favor animal models as the default. Addressing this bias underscores the need for policies that not only legitimize but also expand the adoption of alternative, human-relevant methodologies [190].

Against this backdrop, organoids and organ-on-a-chip (OoC) platforms have emerged as promising solutions, combining biological fidelity with experimental flexibility [191]. Organoids are three-dimensional (3D) structures derived from stem cells or primary tissues that self-organize into organ-like architectures, recapitulating aspects of human physiology and pathology [192]. Similarly, OoC systems use microfluidic engineering and BMs to replicate tissue–tissue interfaces, biomechanical forces, and microenvironmental cues in a controllable manner [193–195]. More broadly, recent organoid reviews suggest that integration of organoids with microfluidics and AI may strengthen their utility as human-relevant platforms for disease modeling, drug screening, and translational evaluation [196]. These technologies mitigate the interspecies discrepancies inherent in animal models and more accurately reflect human-specific genetic and environmental variables [197–200]. Importantly, they allow for longitudinal culture, repeated dosing, and dynamic monitoring, aligning more closely with the requirements of chronic diseases such as OA. Moreover, miniaturized organoid platforms cultivated in vitro often reproduce human-specific physiological and pharmacological responses that rodent systems fail to capture [201]. Recent work also indicates that AI-assisted organoid assessment may improve the standardization and scalability of organoid-based evaluation by integrating multidimensional readouts from omics, histology, morphology, function, and drug screening [202].

Encouragingly, the United States National Institutes of Health has recently taken a pivotal step in organoid research by establishing the nation’s first federally funded center—the Standardized Organoid Modeling (SOM) Center (https://www.nih.gov/som). This three-year initiative, supported by $87 million, is designed to generate standardized, reproducible, and widely accessible organoid models. These models are expected to accelerate drug discovery and translational science, provide more accurate tools for disease modeling, and strengthen public health protection while reducing reliance on animal experimentation. The launch of the SOM Center marks a decisive milestone toward more precise and ethically responsible biomedical research.

For OA research, the relevance of these platforms is particularly pronounced. Cartilage and synovium-derived organoids have been developed to model tissue degeneration, inflammatory signaling, and ECM remodeling, offering new avenues to investigate ferroptosis and other PCD pathways under human-relevant conditions [203–206]. Microfluidic “joint-on-chip” systems integrate multiple joint compartments—cartilage, synovium, subchondral bone, and immune cells—while enabling the application of mechanical loading and shear stress to mimic physiological conditions [207]. Such models could provide unique opportunities to study the pharmacodynamics of ferroptosis-targeted nanomedicines, including intra-articular distribution, cartilage penetration, subcellular localization (e.g., mitochondria), and redox modulation. Moreover, combining patient-derived cells with these platforms paves the way for personalized disease modeling and precision testing of nanotherapeutics [208, 209].

The advantages of organoids and OoCs extend beyond physiological relevance. These systems support high-throughput screening and multi-parameter analysis, generating rich datasets that can be integrated with transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic profiling for systems-level insights [197–200]. Furthermore, they are compatible with advanced technologies such as CRISPR-based gene editing, 3D bioprinting, engineered BMs, and AI, expanding their potential applications in nanomedicine development [197–200, 210]. More conceptually, AI virtual organoids have recently been proposed as organoid-scale digital twins that may extend organoid-based research toward multimodal, longitudinal, and computationally executable human-relevant models [211]. Compared to traditional in vivo methods, they offer superior reproducibility, scalability, and opportunities for inter-laboratory standardization [212].

Despite recent advances, significant obstacles remain. Organoids frequently lack vascularization, immune components, and stromal elements, limiting their ability to replicate systemic drug responses and native tissue complexity [213, 214]. OoC platforms, while highly sophisticated, require standardization of fabrication protocols and readouts to ensure reproducibility across laboratories [215]. Maintaining structural integrity in complex 3D tissues, along with batch-to-batch variability in stem cell–derived models, further complicates their reliability. These shortcomings highlight the urgent need for robust quality management frameworks and the implementation of standardized control measures [216]. Incorporating mechanical cues that reflect joint biomechanics remains technically demanding, yet essential for modeling OA pathogenesis and treatment [217]. Addressing these limitations will be crucial to fully realize the translational potential of these technologies [218].

The momentum remains strong, and these technologies are advancing rapidly. Organoids and joint-on-chip platforms constitute a transformative step in preclinical nanomedicine evaluation. By bridging the gap between reductionist in vitro models and imperfect animal studies, they hold the potential to accelerate the development and validation of ferroptosis-targeted NMs for OA therapy [219, 220]. With continued refinement, standardization, and integration into regulatory frameworks, these human-relevant models are poised to reshape the translational pipeline, ultimately improving therapeutic outcomes and public health [221].

(6)Beyond ferroptosis: targeting the network of PCD pathways in OA

Beyond ferroptosis: targeting the network of PCD pathways in OA

Because the disease microenvironment is highly complex, therapies that target a single factor often fail to deliver substantial clinical benefits. As a result, multimodal strategies that combine therapeutic mechanisms in a synergistic manner are increasingly recognized as more effective than conventional monotherapies [81]. Ferroptosis has emerged as a compelling pathogenic axis in OA, yet the disease itself is inherently multicellular and multiscale, with multiple PCD modalities acting in parallel or sequentially within cartilage, synovium, and subchondral bone [92, 222]. An exclusive focus on ferroptosis risks overlooking actionable crosstalk among death pathways and the possibility of compensatory switching when a single axis is inhibited. A more forward-looking strategy is to design nanoplatforms capable of sensing and modulating the PCD network as a whole, enabling context-specific, combinatorial, or sequential interventions.

Beyond ferroptosis, at least two PCD modalities warrant attention in OA biology and therapy design. Pyroptosis—an inflammatory, gasdermin-mediated lytic death typically downstream of caspase-1/4/5/11 and inflammasome activation (e.g., NOD-like receptor thermal protein domain associated protein 3)—has been implicated in chondrocytes and synovial fibroblasts, where it amplifies IL-1β/interleukin-18 signaling and matrix-degrading cascades [223]. Necroptosis, governed by RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL, yields membrane rupture and damage associated molecular patterns release and has been associated with cartilage degeneration and synovial inflammation in preclinical settings [175]. Nanocarriers encapsulating RIPK1/3-targeted siRNAs or small-molecule inhibitors could provide a precision approach to suppress necroptotic signaling while minimizing systemic toxicity.

Beyond these better-characterized pathways, two emerging PCD modalities have introduced novel hypotheses. Cuproptosis, a copper-dependent mitochondrial death program driven by copper binding to lipoylated tricarboxylic acid cycle enzymes, has recently been implicated in OA [224, 225]. Jiang et al. demonstrated that elevated oxygen tension can induce cuproptosis in chondrocytes, and that copper chelation alleviates OA progression in preclinical models [226]. Similarly, Li et al. reported that cuprorivaite microspheres suppressed cuproptosis and oxidative stress, thereby protecting cartilage in vitro and in vivo [227]. While these findings suggest therapeutic tractability, rigorous validation across diverse models is required before cuproptosis can be considered an established OA mechanism. Disulfidptosis, a recently described cytoskeleton-collapsing death driven by aberrant disulfide crosslinking of actin filaments under cystine/NADPH imbalance, has not been fully explored in OA [228, 229]. Its biochemical logic—rooted in redox and cytoskeletal stress—provides a rationale for systematic investigation, particularly since both pathways are dysregulated in joint tissues.

Taken together, these insights highlight that ferroptosis is but one node in a broader cell-death landscape. Next-generation nanotherapeutic designs should adopt a network-based perspective—integrating multi-modal sensing, redox and metal homeostasis regulation, and selective inflammasome or necroptosis blockade. Incorporating design principles such as organelle-level targeting (e.g., mitochondria, cytoskeleton), stimuli-responsive release (ROS, Cu²⁺, NADPH imbalance), and combination payloads (antioxidants, siRNA, chelators) could not only enhance efficacy but also mitigate compensatory resistance. Such strategies may ultimately yield multifunctional, precision-engineered nanoplatforms capable of reshaping the therapeutic paradigm for OA.

(7)Nano-omics in OA: from ferroptosis profiling to nanodrug development

Nano-omics in OA: from ferroptosis profiling to nanodrug development

The integration of nanotechnology and omics has led to the creation of a new field of research, known as nano-omics, which involves using nanotechnology to isolate and analyze substances from biological fluids for subsequent omics research and analysis [230]. Nano-omics has brought transformative advancements in the study of diseases, enabling tailored therapeutic approaches where treatments are customized according to individual patient profiles. This transition from a one-size-fits-all model to precision medicine represents a fundamental change in healthcare, with the potential to deliver more effective and less invasive interventions [231]. In the evolving landscape of medical research, the fusion of nanotechnology and omics sciences thus represents a paradigm shift in personalized laboratory medicine [232, 233].

Nano-omics integrates nanoscale materials with genomics, proteomics, metabolomics, and transcriptomics, offering unprecedented opportunities to refine diagnostics and therapeutics [231]. Within the context of OA, such integration is particularly relevant: nanotechnology-enabled omics profiling can sensitively capture ferroptosis-related molecular events—such as lipid peroxidation products, GSH metabolism, or GPx4 activity—in synovial fluid, cartilage, or subchondral bone. These disease- and pathway-specific signatures may serve as biomarkers to guide the rational design and evaluation of nanodrugs. Moreover, coupling nano-omics with patient-derived samples could stratify OA patients according to their ferroptosis susceptibility, thereby supporting more precise therapeutic targeting. Such insights could also inform optimization of nanocarrier properties—such as particle size, surface chemistry, and drug payload—to enhance pharmacodynamics and ferroptosis modulation in vivo.

Advancements in personalized laboratory medicine are propelled by nano-omics, offering not only high-resolution insights into cellular processes but also novel applications such as enhanced diagnostic accuracy, targeted drug delivery, personalized treatment plans, real-time monitoring of treatment efficacy, and improved prognostics and predictive analysis [231, 234]. At the same time, several challenges must be acknowledged. The complexity and heterogeneity of multi-omics datasets demand robust bioinformatic pipelines, standardized protocols, and advanced computational models to ensure reproducibility across laboratories [235–237]. High-dimensionality and data variability complicate clustering and integration, underscoring the need for AI and ML tools to enable efficient modeling, fusion, and visualization [238]. Equally important are concerns regarding data privacy and security, which necessitate the development of secure storage and sharing platforms. From a translational perspective, omics-derived biomarkers and therapeutic strategies require validation in large-scale clinical trials and alignment with regulatory frameworks. Finally, the high costs of omics technologies and data processing remain a barrier to accessibility, particularly in low-resource settings, highlighting the need for cost-reduction strategies and global collaboration [239–242].

Overall, nano-omics represents a promising bridge between NM engineering and systems-level biology. By enabling sensitive detection of ferroptosis-related processes, guiding biomarker discovery, and facilitating individualized nanodrug development, it has the potential to accelerate the translation of ferroptosis-targeted nanomedicine for OA.

Strengths & limitations

Strengths

This SR is, to our knowledge, the first to synthesize evidence on NM-based ferroptosis modulation for OA. Unlike previous narrative reviews, it reduces subjective selection and reporting bias. Adherence to PRISMA 2020 guidelines and prospective protocol registration ensured transparency and reproducibility. A comprehensive multi-database search minimized selection bias and improved retrieval accuracy. Independent screening, risk of bias assessment, and data extraction strengthened methodological rigor and provided a more reliable evidence base for future translational and clinical research.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the evidence base was restricted to preclinical in vitro and in vivo studies, which limits direct extrapolation to clinical practice. Second, substantial heterogeneity in experimental models, NM formulations, intervention protocols, and outcome measures limited direct comparability across studies, thereby precluding meaningful statistical pooling and meta-analysis and necessitating narrative synthesis. Third, incomplete methodological reporting in many studies hindered accurate risk of bias assessment and may have introduced biased estimates [7, 243, 244]. Fourth, despite extensive multi-database searching, relevant studies may still have been missed, and restricting the review to English-language publications may have contributed to selection and publication bias [140].

A further limitation is the strong geographical concentration of the available evidence, as all included studies originated from a single national context. This concentration may limit the external validity and cross-population generalizability of the current findings, because OA varies across populations in genetic background, comorbidities, and clinical settings worldwide. In addition, the lack of independent replication across different geographic regions reduces confidence in the reproducibility of the reported preclinical effects. This concentration also raises the possibility of publication bias, which may contribute to overestimation of therapeutic benefit.

Ultimately, despite rigorous methodology, the robustness of this SR depends on the quality and completeness of the primary studies. Addressing these limitations will require larger, standardized, and geographically diverse investigations, alongside stronger international collaboration and harmonized reporting standards.

Conclusion

In summary, current evidence indicates that research at the intersection of OA, NMs, and ferroptosis has expanded rapidly in recent years, with the available literature currently concentrated in China. Collectively, these studies support ferroptosis as both a mechanistic driver of OA pathology and a promising therapeutic target, while also establishing preclinical experimental platforms that may serve as a basis for future translational development. Nevertheless, most findings remain confined to preclinical models, and significant challenges persist in ensuring reproducibility, methodological rigor, and cross-regional generalizability. Achieving reliable clinical translation will require independent replication across different geographic regions, harmonized standards, and the inclusion of diverse patient populations. Experience from approved nanomedicine-based therapies in other disease areas further suggests that successful translation will also depend on clear clinical positioning, scalable manufacturing, and rigorous quality control.

Looking ahead, advancing this interdisciplinary field will require continued efforts to refine mechanistic insights, engineer next-generation nanoplatforms, and integrate precision medicine approaches. Importantly, expanding therapeutic paradigms beyond ferroptosis to encompass other PCD modalities may open additional opportunities for disease modification. With sustained global cooperation and rigorous translational strategies, nanomedicine targeting ferroptosis and related pathways may contribute to the future development of more effective therapeutic options for OA.
