Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) and acute delirium are common clinical complications of critical illness. AKI is a vastly heterogeneous syndrome that has long been shown to be associated with worse clinical outcomes.1 Even mild and transient AKI appears to herald a greater risk for both short- and long-term adverse events compared with patients who do not experience AKI, including new or worsened chronic kidney disease, progression to end-stage kidney disease, cerebral and cardiovascular events,2 3 new infections and sepsis,4 5 gastrointestinal bleeding,6 malignancy,7 fracture risk8 and death.9 10

Delirium is also strongly associated with less favourable health outcomes, including new persistent cognitive impairment, neuro-psychiatric sequelae and greater health services utilisation.11 12 Recent evidence has described unique sub-phenotypes of delirium in critical illness, which may portend different prognoses and perhaps provide new targets for therapeutic intervention.13

Numerous experimental and clinical studies provide evidence of AKI-mediated crosstalk and distal organ dysfunction, particularly in the heart, liver and brain, believed to be largely mediated by systemic and regional inflammatory cytokines, oxidative stress and immune responses.14 Experimental models of ischaemic kidney injury report an increase in systemic and brain-specific inflammatory mediators, increased glial fibrillar acidic protein deposition in astrocytes, increased extravasation of Evans blue dye into the brain parenchyma implying compromised blood-brain barrier function and alteration to locomotor function following short-term exposures.15 These data imply that AKI may directly and indirectly mediate rapid inflammatory and functional changes in the brain, which may clinically manifest as delirium in acute and critical illness.16

Relatively few clinical studies have evaluated the association between AKI and incident delirium.17,21 Selected studies have identified surrogate markers of AKI (eg, serum urea) as risk factors for incident delirium in the development and validation of clinical risk prediction scores.22 23 Selected post hoc evaluations of prospective cohort studies and clinical trials have provided ancillary evidence that timely renal replacement therapy (RRT) may mitigate the association of AKI and delirium or coma.18 24

Most recent clinical practice guidelines for sedation and analgesia do not specifically comment on the potential incremental risk of delirium among critically ill patients with AKI or provide any specific direction on management.25 To date, little is known about the incidence of de novo delirium in this population. The temporal relationship between these two conditions, particularly the severity of AKI and the risk of delirium, remains unclear. The spectrum of clinical susceptibilities and factors associated with the development of delirium are also unknown. Furthermore, the provision of acute RRT—especially the timing of initiation and the modality of RRT—on the severity and duration of delirium is uncertain. These represent important knowledge and evidence care gaps, given emerging evidence suggesting a strong association between AKI and delirium. Accordingly, we aim to perform a systematic review to describe the association between AKI and delirium, with the goal of synthesising existing knowledge to enhance clinical recognition of this association and identify potential strategies for its mitigation.

Methods and analysis

Study design

We will perform a systematic review to identify and evaluate the association between AKI and delirium among patients with critical illness, following guidelines from the Cochrane Collaboration and the Center for Reviews and Dissemination. The review will be described using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses Protocols statement (online supplemental table 1).26 27

Study registration

This systematic review was registered with PROSPERO on 25 March 2025.

Search strategy

We planned to initially search in March 2025, and data analysis will begin in September 2025. The search strategy will be developed in consultation with an experienced research librarian (JYK), who will design and execute comprehensive searches in Ovid MEDLINE, Ovid Embase, CINAHL, Scopus, Web of Science Core Collection and Cochrane Library (via Wiley). To capture all relevant literature pertaining to AKI and delirium, relevant keywords and controlled vocabulary will be carefully selected. A combination of the following search themes will be used: (1) AKI, RRT; (2) critical care, critical illness, intensive care and intensive care units (ICUs) and (3) delirium (online supplemental table 2). Searches will range from inception to January 2025. In addition to subscription databases, the research team will review the first 200 results from Google Scholar for inclusion, as this has been demonstrated to be a reasonable number of results to screen, given the high overlap between Web of Science and Google Scholar.28 Bibliographies from included studies will be reviewed using the web-based tool Covidence (www.covidence.org).

Trial eligibility for screening and selection

Two authors will independently screen study titles and abstracts, followed by full-text review to determine whether the remaining studies meet the inclusion criteria using Covidence. Disagreements will be resolved through discussion. In the case of unresolved matters, a third reviewer will be consulted.

Inclusion criteria

Eligible studies will meet all of the following criteria: (1) clinical trial (randomised, cluster or before-and-after) or observational cohort (prospective or retrospective) that describes individual patient-level data; (2) reports the occurrences of both AKI and delirium, using validated definitions; (3) focused on a population admitted to a critical care or ICU (online supplemental table 3)29 and (4) published from inception to January 2025.

Exclusion criteria

We will exclude publications that are described as: (1) case-reports or case-series and (2) preclinical or experimental studies.

Data extraction

Each selected article will have data extracted from full texts using a standardised data extraction template in Microsoft Excel. A random 10% of data will be extracted in duplicate for reliability evaluation. The data extraction template will be pilot tested. One author will complete the data extraction. A second author will independently validate and verify the data extraction for a proportion of studies; disagreements will be resolved through discussion. In the case of unresolved matters, a third reviewer will be involved. The authors of the retrieved studies and/or documents will be contacted for further information if necessary. Primary outcome

The proportion of critically ill patients with AKI who develop delirium, compared with critically ill patients with AKI who do not develop delirium.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes will include: (1) the proportion of critically ill patients with AKI, stratified by severity of AKI (stages 1–3), who develop delirium (stratified by any, duration and severity), compared with critically ill patients with AKI who do not develop delirium; (2) baseline susceptibilities and clinical factors associated with the development of delirium among critically ill patients with AKI (including those that may be modifiable) and (3) the proportion of critically ill patients with AKI, stratified by receipt of acute RRT (including descriptions of indications, timing and RRT modality), who develop delirium (including descriptions of delirium severity and duration).

Patient outcomes

Patient outcomes will include: (1) mortality; (2) resolution of AKI and (3) changes in cognition or new/worsened cognitive impairment.

Health service outcomes

Health service outcomes will include: (1) use and duration of organ support (eg, non-invasive ventilation, invasive mechanical ventilation, RRT); (2) length of ICU and hospital stay and (3) change in baseline disposition (eg, discharge to skilled nursing facility).

Evaluation of study quality

Two authors will independently complete a risk of bias assessment for included studies, and disagreements will be resolved through discussion with a third reviewer, as needed. The risk of bias assessment will be done using Microsoft Excel.

Methodological quality will be rated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale (NOS) for observational studies,30 including the following domains: study selection (representativeness of the exposed cohort, selection of the non-exposed cohort, ascertainment of exposure and demonstration that the outcome was not present at the start of the study), comparability of cohorts and outcomes (assessment of outcomes, follow-up and adequacy of follow-up). Each study will be assessed as good quality, fair quality or poor quality based on the thresholds for converting the NOS to Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality standards.

Randomised controlled trials (RCTs) will be assessed using the Cochrane Risk-of-Bias (RoB 2) tool for randomised trials through the Distiller SR Tool for RCTs, if applicable.31 This includes the following domains: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective outcomes reporting and any other sources of bias. Each domain will be graded as ‘yes’, ‘no’ or ‘unclear’ if the risk for bias is deemed high, low or unknown, respectively. The study will be considered at high risk of bias if either allocation concealment or blinding of study participants domain is assessed as inadequate or unclear.

Data analysis and synthesis

Descriptive analyses will be performed on all articles, including summary statistics of design, patient characteristics and susceptibilities/exposures across included studies. We will describe the primary exposure/outcome of the association between AKI, including AKI severity and receipt of RRT, and delirium overall, pooled if feasible, and stratified by the presence of adjusted effect estimates. Moreover, we will describe clinical predictors and factors shown to be independently associated with delirium among critically ill patients with AKI, stratified by whether this may or may not be modifiable. Furthermore, we will describe the patient and health service outcomes stratified by AKI exposure and delirium events, pooled if feasible. Finally, we will leverage the search and data abstraction to potentially investigate the following additional questions, if possible: is there a validated clinical risk prediction model that can be readily implemented to identify critically ill patients with AKI at high risk for delirium? What factors may be potentially modifiable and actionable in clinical practice? This may further present an opportunity for prospective validation of clinical risk prediction for delirium in existing databases/datasets focused on AKI.

Study characteristics will be summarised for dichotomous outcomes using pooled proportions, risk ratios or ORs, and for continuous outcomes using weighted mean differences, with 95% CIs. Where data are reported as medians, they will be converted to means and SD. A pooled meta-analysis for reported outcomes will be conducted using the Der Simonian-Laird random-effects model. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses will be performed to assess potential differences across study designs (eg, RCTs vs observational studies). Data will be presented descriptively if meta-analysis cannot be performed (ie, if studies are clinically diverse, they have a high risk of bias based on risk of bias tools, etc). Heterogeneity between studies included in the review will be assessed by multiple means including visual inspection and using the I2 statistic. If significant heterogeneity is present, we will not report study results using meta-analysis. We will explore heterogeneity using subgroup analysis, as feasible. We will examine clinically distinct subgroup populations, as possible, to better explore the impact of AKI on delirium among critically ill patients. We will also assess for publication bias using a funnel plot (ie, >10 studies) and consider complementary approaches, such as Egger’s test, where appropriate, to explore potential small-study bias. All statistical analyses will be performed using STATA statistical software, V.17 (StataCorp LLC).

The strength of evidence will be assessed using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) tool.

Discussion

AKI is a common complication in hospitalised patients, particularly in critically ill populations and is a known clinical risk factor for the new onset of delirium. Nevertheless, there are still many gaps in our growing understanding of the pathobiology of kidney-brain crosstalk. Evaluation of the association between AKI and delirium is essential for timely identification of at-risk patients and to evaluate strategies that may mitigate delirium and improve patient outcome, as delirium is linked to various long-term adverse sequalae, including cognitive impairment and increased mortality.

This systematic review will include an extensive literature search to comprehensively summarise and synthesise current knowledge on the relationship between AKI and delirium among critically ill patients. The literature will be thoroughly screened and selected, with data extraction and risk of bias assessment performed for each study to ensure data quality. Additionally, prespecified analyses and publication bias assessments are planned, and the GRADE approach will be used to evaluate the certainty of evidence. This work aims to identify key knowledge gaps, guide future research, support health system planning and improve awareness of incident delirium in patients with AKI. Ultimately, it may help define best practices for identifying and managing delirium in this high-risk population.

Statistical aggregation will be limited by the quality of the included studies and regional disparities in the management of AKI. These limitations include heterogeneous populations, varying definitions of AKI and delirium, and differences in methods of reporting outcomes, particularly due to variation across health systems. Although differences may limit the quantitative synthesis of data, this is not the primary objective of this review. As the incidence of AKI is increasing over time,32 enhancing awareness of AKI at the bedside and influencing clinical practice to permit earlier recognition of AKI and delirium and resultantly improve the outcomes of critically ill patients.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethics approval is not required for this study, as all data included in this evaluation are already published, and our study will not directly involve human participants. Findings will be disseminated through academic conferences and published in a peer-reviewed journal.
