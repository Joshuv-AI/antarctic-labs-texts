Long-term outcomes of postoperative radiotherapy for patients with pIIIA-N2 non-small-cell lung cancer after complete resection and adjuvant chemotherapy (PORT-C): a single-centre, randomized, phase 3 trial
Authors: Yu Men, Yongxing Bao, Nan Bi, Zongmei Zhou, Jun Liang, Jima Lv, Qinfu Feng, Zefen Xiao, Yan Wang, Junling Li, Jie Wang, Shugeng Gao, Jie He, Zhouguang Hui, Luhua Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThe PORT-C trial was the first published phase III randomized clinical trial (RCT) to evaluate the role of postoperative radiotherapy (PORT) using intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT)/three-dimensional conformal radiation therapy (3D-CRT) in patients with resected pIIIA-N2 non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC). We aimed to assess the long-term outcomes of this RCT.MethodsPatients with pIIIA-N2 NSCLC treated with complete resection followed by four cycles of platinum-based chemotherapy between January 1, 2009 and December 31, 2017 were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to PORT or observation. Radiotherapy was delivered using a 6 MV-X ray linear accelerator via 3D-CRT or IMRT, with 2 Gy per fraction up to 50 Gy over 5 weeks. The primary endpoint was disease-free survival (DFS), analyzed using modified intent-to-treat (mITT). Secondary endpoints included overall survival (OS), locoregional recurrence-free survival (LRFS), distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS), and toxic effects. DFS, OS, LRFS, and DMFS rates were estimated using Kaplan–Meier method, compared using log-rank test, and modeled using Cox proportional hazards method. The patterns of first failures were analyzed using competing risk analyses. This trial is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT00880971.FindingsOverall, 394 patients were randomly allocated to the PORT (n = 184) or observation (n = 180) arms. The median follow-up time was 87.9 months (interquartile range [IQR] 72.2–113.8). In the mITT analyses, DFS showed no significant difference between the PORT and observation arms (hazards ratio [HR], 0.90; 95% CI, 0.70–1.12; p = 0.39). The 5-year DFS rates in the PORT and observation arms were 36% (95% confidence interval [CI], 28.9%–43.1%) and 31.5% (95% CI, 24.6%–38.4%), respectively; the 5-year OS rates were 64.7% (95% CI, 57.6%–71.8%) and 70.4% (95% CI, 63.5%–77.3%), respectively (p = 0.20). In the per-protocol analyses, 140 and 170 patients were included in the PORT and observation arms, respectively. PORT did not significantly improve DFS (HR, 0.80; 95% CI, 0.61–1.05; p = 0.11) or OS (HR, 1.09; 95% CI, 0.77–1.53; p = 0.63). Most patients died of cancer. However, more deaths due to cardiopulmonary disease were observed in the PORT arm than in the observation arm (6/184, 3.3% vs. 2/180, 1.1%). The first failure of locoregional recurrence (LR)-only was significantly lower in the PORT arm than in the observation arm (10.9%, 95% CI, 6.8%–15.9% vs. 18.9%, 95% CI, 14.0%–26.2%, p = 0.031). Only 1 patient (0.5%) in the PORT arm had grade 3 radiation pneumonitis. No radiotherapy-related grade 4 or 5 AEs were observed.InterpretationThe long-term results of the PORT-C trial indicated no DFS benefit from receiving PORT for patients with pIIIA-N2 NSCLC after complete resection and adjuvant chemotherapy.Funding10.13039/501100012166National Key R&D Program of China, 10.13039/501100001809National Natural Science Foundation of China, Capital’s Funds for Health Improvement and Research, Beijing Hope Run Special Fund of 10.13039/501100019870Cancer Foundation of China, and 10.13039/100018904Beijing Xisike Clinical Oncology Research Foundation.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed for studies before January 1, 2009, without language restrictions, using the terms “non-small cell lung cancer”, “complete resection”, and “postoperative radiotherapy”. Several meta-analyses have shown that postoperative radiotherapy (PORT) has no effect on the survival improvement for patients with non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) after complete resection. However, sub-group analysis based on the same dataset of these meta-analyses showed that the PORT with conventional radiotherapy might be beneficial for stage IIIA (N2) disease. The ANITA trial have shown that postoperative chemotherapy contributed to the improvement on 5 year survival rate by about 4% for patients with NSCLC after complete resection. But the overall survival rate was relatively low and the local recurrence was still the dominant failure pattern for stage IIIA (N2) disease even these patients received the postoperative chemotherapy. Also, the subgroup analysis of the ANITA trial and several smaller randomized controlled studies have shown that PORT improved the 5-year survival for patients with pIIIA-N2 NSCLC. The 3D conform radiotherapy (3D-CRT) and intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) can increase the radiation dose to the target volume while decreasing the dose to risk organs comparing with the conventional radiotherapy. So it is expected that PORT using 3D-CRT or IMRT after postoperative chemotherapy will improve the local control and survival for stage IIIA (N2) NSCLC.

Added value of this study

Our study presents the long-term follow-up results of the PORT-C trial. The findings are generally consistent with the previously published results. PORT still failed to confer a benefit in the primary endpoint of DFS. However, PORT did reduce locoregional recurrence. With the extension of follow-up, distant metastasis became the main factor affecting survivals. In terms of non-cancer-related deaths, cardiopulmonary-related mortality in the PORT arm appeared to be higher than that in the observation arm.

Implications of all the available evidence

The findings of our study further demonstrated that PORT could not improve DFS of pⅢA-N2 NSCLC patients after complete resection and adjuvant chemotherapy. Further research should focus on the heterogeneity of pIIIA-N2 NSCLC to identify populations more suitable for and more likely to benefit from PORT.

Introduction

Postoperative radiotherapy (PORT) for non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is not recommended for patients with stage pI-II or pN0-1 disease due to its detrimental impact on survival. However, the definitive role of PORT in pIIIA-N2 NSCLC, especially in patients receiving adjuvant chemotherapy, has been controversial for a long time. Although many retrospective studies have confirmed its potential value, two large phase III randomized controlled trials (RCTs), LungART and our PORT-C trial, both showed that PORT does not improve disease-free survival (DFS) for patients with pIIIA-N2 NSCLC after complete resection and adjuvant chemotherapy.

The PORT-C trial1 aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of PORT using three-dimensional conformal radiation therapy (3D-CRT) and intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) in patients with pIIIA-N2 NSCLC after complete resection followed by adjuvant chemotherapy. This trial included 364 eligible patients treated between January 1, 2009 and December 31, 2017. The initial results, reported in August 1, 2021 with a follow-up period of 46 months, showed no improvement in the primary endpoint, DFS, in modified intent-to-treat (mITT) analyses (3 year-DFS: PORT group, 40.5%; observation group, 32.7%; hazards ratio [HR] = 0.84, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.65–1.09, p = 0.20). However, in per-protocol (PP) analyses, PORT significantly improved DFS (42.8% vs. 30.6%, HR = 0.75, p = 0.05). The value of PORT in local control was evident, with the 3-year local recurrence-only rate significantly lower in the PORT arm (9.5% vs. 18.3%, Fine-Gray HR = 0.55, Gray’s test p = 0.04). No radiotherapy-related grade 4 or 5 adverse event (AE) were observed. After that, the LungART study published its research results. A total of 501 patients were enrolled in the LungART study to evaluate the value of 3D conformal PORT after complete resection. The results showed that PORT did not improve the 3-year DFS. However, the LungART study showed that PORT might lead to higher cardiopulmonary toxicity. At present, the study has not yet released its long-term follow-up data.

The current study provides a long-term follow-up of the PORT-C trial, reporting updated outcomes to further evaluate the efficacy of PORT for pIIIA-N2 NSCLC.

Methods

Trial design and eligibility

The details of the PORT-C trial design have been reported previously.1 This single-center RCT was conducted at our center in China, with approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee.

Eligible patients had pathologically proven and staged IIIA-N2 NSCLC (American Joint Committee on Cancer [AJCC] staging system, 6th edition before January 1, 2010 and 7th edition thereafter) after complete resection and four cycles of adjuvant chemotherapy. A comprehensive review ensured the inclusion of patients without recurrence. Eligible patients were aged 18–70 years, had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status (PS) ≤ 1, and adequate lung function. Patients were excluded if they had a history of other malignancies, underwent pneumonectomy or neoadjuvant chemotherapy, or had uncontrolled active infections. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Ethics

This study was approved by the ethics committee of Cancer Institute and Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, approval No.08-35/292, and was registered on ClinicalTrial.gov (NCT00880971) on April 13, 2009.

Random assignment

The Patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio using simple randomization to receive PORT or observation. An statistician generated the randomization schedule using simple randomization. The list was uploaded to the Interactive Web Response System (IWRS). Eligible patients were assigned unique randomization numbers sequentially through the IWRS immediately after screening confirmation. After obtaining informed consent from the patient, the researcher inputs the information into the internal randomization system online. The system automatically allocated treatment arms based on the preloaded random sequence. Given the open-label design, treatment assignments were not masked.

Pretreatment workup

All patients underwent the following pretreatment examinations: hematological examination (including blood routine, liver and renal function, tumor biomarkers,etc.), cardiopulmonary examination, contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) or positron emission tomography-CT (PET-CT), brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or CT (if MRI was not available), and radionuclide bone scans (omitted if PET-CT was performed).

Procedures

All patients underwent lobectomy or bilobectomy, with complete exploration and dissection of the mediastinal lymph nodes required at levels 4, 7, and 10 for right lung cancer and at levels 4 (if accessible), 5, 6, 7, and 10 for left lung cancer. Four cycles of platinum-based doublet regimens were mandatory as adjuvant chemotherapy.

Approximately six weeks after completing adjuvant chemotherapy, patients in the PORT arm received radiotherapy. The clinical target volume (CTV) included the ipsilateral hilum, subcarinal region, ipsilateral mediastinum (2R and 4R for right lung disease and 2 L, 4 L, 5, and 6 for left lung disease), and stump (only for central lesions). The planning target volume (PTV) was defined as the CTV plus 0.5 cm margins to account for tumor motion and setup variations. Radiotherapy was performed using a linear accelerator with a photon energy of 6 MV-X rays to deliver 3D-CRT or IMRT. The fractionation was 2 Gy per fraction, up to 50 Gy, over 5 weeks. Each treatment target and plan was reviewed by the center. CBCT is performed daily for the first five fractions, and then once a week thereafter.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was DFS, calculated from the date of randomization to the first date of recurrence or all-cause death, whichever occurred first. Secondary endpoints included overall survival (OS), locoregional recurrence-free survival (LRFS), distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS), and toxicity. OS was measured from the date of randomization to all-cause death or the last follow-up day. LRFS was measured from the date of randomization until local or regional recurrence or all-cause mortality. DMFS was measured from the date of randomization to the date of distant metastasis or all-cause death. Survivors were censored at the last follow-up if no corresponding event occurred. Treatment toxicities were evaluated according to the National Cancer Institute Common Toxicity Criteria (NCI CTC), version 3.0.

Post-treatment follow-ups were conducted every 3 months for the first 2 years, every 6 months from years 2–5, and annually thereafter. CT scans of the neck, chest, and abdomen are required for each review. Brain MRI and bone scans are reviewed once a year.

Statistical analysis

Based on our published retrospective study,2 to detect a difference of 14% in DFS (30% in the observation group vs. 44% in the PORT group) with a one-sided type I error of 0.025 with 80% power, and accounting for 10% ineligibility or loss to follow-up, we calculated a sample size of 390 patients.

The data cutoff for the analysis presented here was March 31, 2023. For comparisons between groups, categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test: Yates' continuity correction was applied to 2 × 2 contingency tables, while Pearson's chi-square test was used for tables larger than 2 × 2 (i.e., those with more than two rows or columns). All tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Survival was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, with a log-rank test used for group comparisons. The Cox proportional hazards model was applied to calculate HRs and 95% CIs. Survival rates were analyzed using a mITT approach. PP and as-treated (AT) populations were also calculated. Subgroup analyses were performed using Cox proportional hazards regression models to evaluate potential heterogeneity in treatment effects across subgroups. To formally assess the interaction between treatment groups and baseline characteristics, interaction terms (subgroup variable × treatment group) were incorporated into the Cox models. The significance of these interaction terms was tested to determine whether treatment effects differed across subgroups. Additionally, forest plots were generated to visually present hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals for each subgroup, along with interaction p-values, to evaluate the consistency of treatment effects. All subgroup analyses were considered exploratory, and no multiplicity adjustments were applied. A two-sided p-value < 0.10 was considered suggestive of potential interaction. Patterns of first failures were analyzed using competing risk analyses, with cumulative incidences estimated using the Aalen-Johansen estimator and evaluated using Gray’s test and the Fine-Gray regression model. Differences were considered statistically significant at a two-sided p value of 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using R, version 3.4.1 (R Foundation).

Role of the funding source

The funders of this study had no roles in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

In the PORT arm, 140 patients underwent PORT as per random assignment and were included in the PP analysis. Reasons for not undergoing radiotherapy were detailed in the previous published article.1 Thus, 170 patients comprised the observation group in the PP analysis. Treatment compliance was notably higher in the observation arm than in the PORT arm.

In the mITT analysis, all patients received R0 resection and completed four cycles of chemotherapy. Among the 140 patients who received PORT in the PORT arm, 125 (125/140, 89.3%) underwent IMRT and 15 (15/140, 10.7%) underwent 3D-CRT. The median administered dose was 50 Gy (IQR 50–50 Gy). The review of radiotherapy plans showed that all organs at risk (OARs) complied with the protocol constraints, with actual doses significantly lower than the limits. This resulted in a lower incidence of toxicities (Table 2). No radiotherapy-related grade 4 or 5 AEs were observed. Discussion

This is the first phase III RCT to report results of the 5-year survivals focusing on the value of PORT for completely resected IIIA-N2 NSCLC. Notably, the final results are partially consistent with the initial outcomes.1 The benefit of the primary endpoint DFS was not observed (p = 0.39 for the mITT population), nor was it observed for OS (p = 0.20 for the mITT population) or DMFS (p = 0.95 for the mITT population). However, even though LRs were significantly decreased by PORT, LRFS did not significantly improve in the mITT population after long-term follow-up, which differed from previous findings. This result is not surprising. Although PORT is beneficial for locoregional control, its effect on distant metastases is weak. With the extension of follow-up, distant metastasis has gradually become the main cause of death, diluting the survival benefits of PORT. This is evident in the LRFS curve, where the latter segments of the curve converge and intersect. Additionally, from the subgroup analysis, patients with positive visceral pleura or fewer PLNs derived a DFS benefit from PORT, likely due to the high risk of local recurrence and lower risk of distant metastasis, allowing the effect of local control to be exerted. In contrast, for patients with a lower risk of local relapse, such as those with maximum diameter of the tumor less than 3 cm and negative visceral pleura, and for those with a higher risk of distant metastasis, such as those with positive LVI, salvage radiotherapy may be a better choice than early radiotherapy for PORT, because of the harmful effect of OS by PORT. According to the current research results, adjuvant radiotherapy is not routinely recommended for patients with stage III-N2 NSCLC after radical resection. However, for some patients with high-risk factors for local-regional recurrence, adjuvant radiotherapy is recommended for consideration, such as R1/R2 resection, lymph node positivity with ≥4 metastatic nodes, or ypN2 status, etc. However, the results of our exploratory analysis suggested that, in addition to identifying which groups of patients need PORT, we should pay more attention to identifying those who do not require it. Moreover, it is worth noting that the efficacy of systemic therapy partially influences the final survival benefit of PORT. It can be seen that for PORT to be effective, it needs to be under the premise of effective systemic control. Therefore, with the improved effectiveness of new adjuvant treatment modalities in systemic control compared to traditional chemotherapy, the value of PORT as a local treatment deserves further exploration.

During the PORT-C trial, adjuvant chemotherapy was the standard of care for patients with resected IIIA-N2 NSCLC. However, the current treatment landscape has undergone significant changes, with adjuvant-targeted therapy and immunotherapy emerging as alternative options. ADAURA,3,4 a global multicenter, double-blind, randomized controlled phase III clinical trial, established osimertinib as the standard adjuvant treatment for patients with stage IB–IIIA EGFR-mutated NSCLC after complete resection. The recently published ALINA trial5 demonstrated that adjuvant alectinib significantly improved DFS compared to platinum-based chemotherapy in patients with resected ALK-positive stage IB-IIIA NSCLC. IMpower0106,7 and KEYNOTE 0918 are two key evidence-based studies on adjuvant immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). On one hand, due to these advancements in adjuvant treatments for IIIA-N2 NSCLC, the role of PORT has become more complex and less clear. The PORT-C trial could not determine the value of PORT following complete resection and targeted or ICIs therapy. On the other hand, both our PORT-C trial and the LungART study previously indicated that more effective systemic therapies are needed, and in this context, PORT may demonstrate its advantages. This is because more effective systemic therapy could substantially reduce distant relapse, creating opportunities for PORT to translate locoregional benefits into improved survival. For instance, ADAURA showed that only 15% of patients had DM, but over 60% of relapses occurred locally, emphasizing the effectiveness of targeted therapy for metastasis and the urgency of strengthening local treatment. Similarly, in the IMpower010 trial, nearly 60% of patients with treatment failure in both arms experienced locoregional relapse. Furthermore, radiation demonstrates a synergistic effect with ICIs, and an appropriate combination could yield satisfactory outcomes. Notably, the locoregional rates mentioned above, whether in ADAURA or IMpower010, referred to the population of all enrolled patients (stages IB–IIIA). These rates were higher in patients with stage IIIA-N2 disease.

The 5 year-OS rates were 64.7% and 70.4%, with median OS times of 98.8 months and 147.1 months in the PORT arm and observation arm, respectively. These results were unexpected. In our previously published article, we attributed this finding to a successful follow-up strategy and effective salvage treatment. Targeted therapy has a high response rate and is particularly effective. The rates of targeted therapy as salvage treatment in the PORT and observation arms were 56.9% (70/123) and 70.1% (89/127), respectively. This difference was significant (p = 0.042 for the mITT population), which may partly explain the negative OS difference and the inferior OS curve of the PORT arm. Additionally, compared with the Lung ART study, our PORT-C study enrolled a younger population, all of whom were Asian. The proportion of EGFR mutations in this population is higher. Patients with EGFR mutations have a high risk of recurrence, but salvage treatments are more effective. As can be seen from the survival outcomes of the PORT-C study and Lung ART, the PORT-C study appears to have a lower DFS but a higher OS.

The negative result of improving the primary endpoint DFS in our PORT-C trial highlights the need to select the best candidates who can truly derive a survival benefit from PORT. It would be arbitrary to deny the application of PORT for all patients with pN2 NSCLC. With the advancement of diagnosis and treatment technologies, patients' survival has been prolonged, and the impact of distant metastasis on survival has become more prominent. As a treatment focusing on local-regional control, PORT requires more precise selection of candidates than ever before. On the other hand, more attention may also need to be paid to identifying populations that are not suitable for postoperative radiotherapy. Overall, pN2 NSCLC is highly heterogeneous. Age,9 heavy smoking,10 multiple positive N2 stations11,12 extracapsular extension,13 involved lymph node ratio,14 metastatic lymph node number,10,15 and histologic subtypes16 have all been reported as risk factors for high local recurrence. ypN2 NSCLC refers to patients who receive systematic treatment before surgery and still have positive lymph nodes in the N2 station detected in post-surgery specimens. Compared with pN2 patients, ypN2 patients have a higher risk of relapse. Some retrospective studies have demonstrated the survival benefit of PORT.17, 18, 19, 20, 21 Based on several RCTs, neoadjuvant immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy has become the preferred standard therapy,22 and patients with ypN2 NSCLC require more attention. Additionally, some patients with poor tolerance for PORT are screened after neoadjuvant therapy. Surveys, particularly after the publication of the PORT-C and LungART trials, indicate that more clinicians now decide on the use of PORT based on risk factors.23,24 However, reliance solely on clinical factors is insufficient. Molecular residual disease (MRD) is associated with worse prognosis and has the potential to become a valuable biomarker for predicting disease recurrence after radical interventions and guiding the selection of PORT.25, 26, 27 MRD can be detected up to three months before clinical progression.28 As mentioned, deaths caused by distant metastasis diminish the benefits of PORT. Early detection and treatment of disease progression are therefore crucial, and MRD plays a critical role in this context.

The incidence of toxicities was higher in the lung-ART trial than in the PORT-C trial. According to our previously published data, acute toxicities, particularly cardiopulmonary toxicities, are low. This is largely due to modern radiation techniques, strict restriction on radiation doses to normal tissues, and smaller target volumes. Additionally, based on the analysis of existing problems in a meta-analysis published in 1998 and other research investigated later, radiotherapy techniques have a strong impact on outcomes. The Lung-ART PORT group had higher late grade 3–4 cardiopulmonary toxicities than the control group (11% vs. 5%), which was related to an excess of deaths. After long-term follow-up of the PORT-C trial, the most common causes of non-cancer-related deaths were cardiac and pulmonary diseases, possibly attributable to damage caused by PORT. A post hoc analysis of the PORT-C trial and contemporary retrospective data showed that heart dose was associated with long-term survival, while lung dose was linked to early survival.29 The boundary between early and long-term survival was 36 months. Radiation exposure weakens lung resistance to microorganisms, increasing the risk of non-cancer-related deaths.30 Low doses, such as lung V5, have been confirmed in previous studies to correlate significantly with pneumonitis31 and should be carefully considered during treatment planning. Radiation-induced heart disease (RIHD) typically occurs years after treatment. For long-term surviving tumor types, such as breast cancer and lymphoma, RIHD is a serious concern. For NSCLC, there is increasing recognition of the significance of RIHD, which is associated with inferior survival.32 Common RIHD includes acute coronary syndrome, myocardial infarction, arrhythmia, pericardial effusion, pericarditis, and congestive heart failure. Considering that patients with pIIIA-N2 can achieve long-term survival after radical therapy, especially as demonstrated in our PORT-C trial, where the median OS of the whole group reached 105.6 months if receiving PORT. The evaluation of cardiovascular disease risks should be performed. Heart doses, including the mean heart dose and cardiac subvolume doses, should be more strictly restricted and evaluated. Although the overall percentage of cardiopulmonary was low (3.3% in the PORT arm vs. 1.1% in the observation arm), it remained the most common cause of non-cancer-related deaths. It is time to discuss the potential benefit of reducing radiation volumes, as studies in the radical setting have shown no clear benefit to elective nodal irradiation.33 Applying similar principles in the adjuvant setting could further reduce cardiopulmonary toxicity. Finally, radiation technology has room for improvement. For example, proton radiotherapy offers better dose distribution curves, ensuring sufficient coverage of the target while reducing exposure to OARs, such as the lungs and heart.34 Additionally, respiratory-gated radiotherapy can provide more stable organ positioning, which may also help reduce OAR doses.

This study has some limitations. First, poor patient adherence affected the results. Second, the PORT-C trial was conducted using adjuvant chemotherapy. However, adjuvant systemic treatments have changed in recent years. Currently, adjuvant chemotherapy is no longer the sole treatment option for patients with stage pIIIA NSCLC. Adjuvant target therapy (for patients with EGFR mutations) and immunotherapy have become standard treatments. Third, with the release of results from Checkmate 816, neoadjuvant immunotherapy has been recommended. A subgroup of patients with ypN2 disease was not included in the PORT-C trial. Third, the molecular testing of the patients in our study was mostly done retrospectively. Fourth, the long recruitment period and the single center design were two additional limitations. Finally, these subgroup analyses are exploratory and were conducted without pre-specification as primary endpoint or adjustment for multiplicity. The multiple unadjusted statistical testing raises the possibility of false-positive results (Type I error). Therefore, any apparent subgroup differences should be viewed as preliminary and suggestive, requiring validation in future studies. They cannot be considered definitive evidence.

In conclusion, PORT did not improve the long-term DFS in patients with pIIIA-N2 NSCLC, but it did increase LR. Identifying a proper subgroup of patients who might benefit from PORT requires further studies for precise evaluation.
