Impact of treatment goals on outcomes in critically ill nonagenarians: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Nattaya Raykateeraroj, Nuanprae Kitisin, Dong Kyu Lee, Je Min Suh, Jake Hinton, Boris Waldman, David Pilcher, Rinaldo Bellomo, Laurence Weinberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe increasing number of critically ill nonagenarians admitted to intensive care units (ICUs) poses challenges in treatment decision-making. This study examines the short-term impact of treatment goals on ICU and hospital mortality and length of stay (LOS), incorporating APACHE III-J scores for risk adjustment.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included adults aged ≥ 90 years admitted to ICUs in Australia and New Zealand from 2010 to 2023, using data from the ANZICS Adult Patient Database. Patients were categorized by initial goal: full (n = 24,694) vs. limited (n = 10,690). Propensity score matching balanced baseline characteristics, resulting in 10,671 matched pairs. Mortality was analyzed using flexible parametric survival models. ICU and hospital LOS were assessed using log-transformed linear regression models. Competing risks regression was applied to adjust for early mortality bias. APACHE III-J scores were included as a key predictor.ResultsLimited treatment was associated with mortality risk that was highest early after admission and declined over time but remained consistently higher than with full treatment. ICU LOS (β = 0.966, 95% CI 0.943–0.990, p = 0.006) and hospital LOS (β = 0.944, 95% CI 0.919–0.969, p < 0.001) were significantly shorter in this group, primarily due to early death. Competing risks analysis confirmed a higher cumulative incidence of ICU mortality (sHR 1.717, 95% CI 1.555–1.900, p < 0.001) and hospital mortality (sHR 1.643, 95% CI 1.541–1.752, p < 0.001). APACHE III-J was an independent predictor of mortality.ConclusionsLimited treatment in critically ill nonagenarians was associated with higher ICU and hospital mortality. The excess risk of ICU mortality was greatest early after ICU admission and gradually attenuated over time, although it remained elevated throughout the ICU stay. These findings support early risk stratification and periodic reassessment of treatment goals as clinical conditions evolve.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07408-8.Keywords: Nonagenarians, Intensive Care Unit, Critical illness, Geriatrics, Aging

Background

The global aging population is projected to reach 2.2 billion by 2080, driven by advancements in healthcare, improved living conditions, and better disease management [1, 2]. Among this growing demographic, the number of nonagenarians is expected to nearly double from 16.3 million in 2015 to 30.9 million by 2030 [3]. This demographic shift has led to increased ICU admissions among nonagenarians, underscoring the demand for specialized and intensive care [4].

Despite advancements in care, nonagenarians remain a high-risk group in critical care settings, presenting unique challenges for healthcare systems [5–10]. The exponential growth of this population has driven a threefold increase in ICU referrals and bed utilization for patients aged 90 years and older, further highlighting the need for tailored treatment strategies [4, 11, 12]. It is important to establish treatment goals of care for every nonagenarian admitted to the ICU to avoid inappropriate or potentially harmful treatments. This approach helps clinicians decide, in collaboration with families, on the most appropriate care plans, even though these discussions can be very difficult.

A systematic review has shown that older patients are more likely to have limitations on life-sustaining treatment (LST), and that the frequency of such limitations has increased over time [13]. This observation may appear counterintuitive in the context of increasing global life expectancy and population longevity [1–3]. However, decisions regarding LST are influenced by multiple factors, including baseline health status, frailty, comorbidities, and patient preferences.

Some studies suggest that, even with less aggressive treatment, nonagenarians achieve ICU, hospital, and 30-day mortality rates comparable to those of the younger subgroup of older adults after adjusting for confounders [14, 15]. However, other research indicates that older ICU patients, particularly those with multiple comorbidities or functional decline, experience higher mortality and are more likely to require long-term care following discharge [16]. These findings highlight the need for a more nuanced and individualized approach to care in this population. Furthermore, data on the incidence and outcomes associated with limited treatment in nonagenarians remain scarce.

To address this gap, we conducted an observational study using retrospective data from the Australian and New Zealand Intensive Care Society (ANZICS) Adult Patient Database to examine the impact of treatment goals on ICU and hospital mortality and other key outcomes in critically ill nonagenarians [17]. By examining these issues, we aim to provide clinicians with more robust evidence to support discussions with patients and their families regarding the appropriate level of care, particularly in cases where limited treatment is anticipated to be associated with higher mortality. Although the precise magnitude and duration of this effect remain uncertain, our goal is to enhance clinical decision-making and improve communication around care goals in this vulnerable population.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective observational cohort study used de-identified data extracted in July 2024 from the Australian and New Zealand Intensive Care Society (ANZICS) Adult Patient Database (APD), a large clinical quality registry curated by the ANZICS Centre for Outcome and Resource Evaluation. The ANZICS APD [18] contains detailed demographic, clinical, and outcome information submitted by approximately 98% of intensive care units (ICUs) in Australia and 68% of ICUs in New Zealand [19]. The study included data from ICU admissions occurring between Jan 1, 2010, and Dec 31, 2023. Ethical approval was granted by the Alfred Hospital Ethics Committee (Project No. 253/24), which waived the requirement for informed consent due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of de-identified data. The study was registered with the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ACTRN12625000070437), and the protocol remained unchanged throughout the study period. This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [20].

Participants

We included all patients aged 90 years and older (nonagenarians) admitted to ICU during the study period. Treatment goals were defined as those clearly documented in the ANZICS database at the time of ICU admission. This reflects the patient’s care plan at the point of ICU entry; any subsequent changes to treatment goals during the ICU stay were not captured and should not be inferred. Patients were assigned to one of two groups based on these documented treatment goals: those receiving full active management (full treatment), defined as having no limitations on life-sustaining treatment, and those with a documented treatment limitation order (limited treatment), which may include restrictions on interventions such as mechanical ventilation, inotropes, or renal replacement therapy. These limitations were based on clinical judgement, prognosis, and/or discussions with patients or their families. Patients with a documented goal of “palliative care for a dying patient” were excluded due to their small numbers and non-comparability with those receiving active ICU-level care.

Procedures and variables

All data were collected by trained clinical staff at contributing ICUs and submitted to the ANZICS registry using standardized definitions. Variables extracted for this study included age, sex (assigned at birth), hospital admission source, ICU admission source, ICU and hospital admission and discharge dates, and discharge destination. Clinical information included illness severity as measured by the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) III-J score [21] and the presence of comorbidities, including chronic respiratory disease, cardiovascular disease, renal disease, liver disease, and malignancies (including metastatic solid cancers and hematological malignancies). Surgical status was categorized as elective (procedures that could be delayed for more than 24 h) or non-elective (procedures required within 24 h). The primary exposure variable was the treatment goal documented at ICU admission, categorized as full treatment or limited treatment based on the presence of a treatment limitation order. Missing data were described in Supplementary Table 1 and excluded from analysis without imputation.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were ICU mortality, defined as death during the ICU admission, and hospital mortality, defined as death before discharge from the hospital. Secondary outcomes included ICU and hospital length of stay (LOS), measured in days from ICU or hospital admission to discharge. Severity of illness, as measured by the APACHE III-J score, was incorporated into risk-adjusted models to account for differences in baseline acuity. Mortality outcomes were evaluated using time-to-event models, and analyses incorporated time-dependent effects and competing risks regression where appropriate.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using the MatchIt package [22] to balance baseline characteristics between the full treatment and limited-treatment groups. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression, incorporating age, sex, APACHE III-J score, admission type, and comorbidities. To determine the optimal matching approach, multiple methods with varying calipers and ratios were systematically assessed. A propensity density plot was used to evaluate overall propensity score balance and the common-support area, while a love plot visualized individual covariate balance before and after matching. After assessing balance and ensuring a sufficient number of matched pairs, nearest-neighbour matching (NNM) with a 1:1 ratio and a caliper of 0.1 was selected based on achieving the lowest standardized mean differences (SMDs) across covariates, as detailed in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Continuous variables were summarized as medians (IQR) and analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, while categorical variables were assessed using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. ICU and hospital LOS were log10-transformed to address skewness and enhance model performance, with results back-transformed for clinical interpretation [23]. Statistical significance was determined using a two-sided p < 0.05, based on null hypothesis significance testing.

ICU and hospital mortality were initially evaluated using Kaplan–Meier survival curves and log-rank tests. Cox proportional hazards models were tested to estimate hazard ratios (HRs); however, proportional hazards (PH) assumption violations were confirmed for both ICU and hospital mortality models (p < 0.001, Schoenfeld residuals), indicating time-dependent effects. Due to this, Flexible Parametric Models (FPMs) were selected as the primary survival analysis method. FPMs, implemented via the flexsurv package [24], allow for smooth baseline hazard estimation and effectively handle time-varying hazard ratios. Model selection was guided by Akaike and Bayesian Information Criteria to optimize fit and stability. Statistical methods for ICU and hospital mortality are summarized in Supplementary Table 2, with Kaplan–Meier survival curves and Schoenfeld residual plots provided in Supplementary Figs. 2–7. Full Cox proportional hazards model results are presented in Supplementary Table 3, and model selection details for flexible parametric survival analysis are shown in Supplementary Table 4.

A sensitivity analysis excluding elective surgical admissions was conducted. The same propensity score matching strategy and flexible parametric survival models were applied to this acute-admission cohort to assess the robustness of the primary findings.

Univariable and multivariable linear regression models were used to analyze ICU and hospital LOS, with log10 transformation applied to address skewness, while demographic variables, severity scores, and admission type were considered for inclusion via stepwise selection (p < 0.2 in univariable analyses). Multicollinearity was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). Results are reported as β-coefficients with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), t-values, and p-values.

To account for competing risks in ICU and hospital mortality on time to discharge, and therefore on LOS estimates, competing risks regression analysis using the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model [25] was performed. This approach estimated subdistribution hazard ratios (sHRs) by modeling the cumulative incidence function (CIF), which accounts for the probability of death over time and better contextualizes LOS differences between treatment groups. Analyses were conducted using the cmprsk package [26], with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

The total number of critically ill nonagenarian admissions generally increased over the study period (2010–2023) across 211 ICUs in New Zealand and Australia, rising from 1.22% of total admissions (1,323 out of 108,015) in 2010 to a peak of 1.93% (3,592 out of 186,366) in 2019, before slightly declining to 1.62% (3,105 out of 191,756) in 2023 (Supplementary Fig. 8). Despite the rise in admissions, survival rates among critically ill nonagenarians fluctuated over the study period (Supplementary Fig. 9). ICU mortality decreased from 9.98% in 2010 to 6.63% in 2023, while hospital mortality showed a general downward trend, declining from 21.69% in 2010 to 15.23% in 2023.

As shown in Fig. 1, after excluding 1,271 patients with missing data and 313 who received palliative care, the final cohort included 35,384 nonagenarians admitted between 2010 and 2023, with an ICU mortality of 6.19% and a hospital mortality of 14.49%. This cohort comprised 24,694 patients (69.79%) receiving full treatment and 10,690 (30.21%) with limited treatment. Propensity score matching yielded a matched cohort of 21,342 patients for further analysis.

ICU mortality

In the propensity-matched cohort, ICU mortality was higher in the limited-treatment group than in the full-treatment group (11.21% vs. 7.68%; Table 3). The effect of treatment goals on ICU mortality demonstrated clear time-varying patterns. As shown in Fig. 2A, survival probabilities began to diverge early after ICU admission. Over time, survival declined more rapidly in the limited-treatment group, while the full-treatment group maintained a higher survival probability.

Hospital mortality

Hospital mortality was also higher in the limited-treatment group than in the full-treatment group in the propensity-matched cohort (24.46% vs. 16.70%; Table 3). The association between treatment goals and hospital mortality also demonstrated time-varying effects. As shown in Fig. 3A, survival probability declined more rapidly in the limited-treatment group, while patients receiving full treatment maintained a higher survival probability over time. The separation between the survival curves persisted over time.

This pattern is further supported by the cumulative hazard function (Fig. 3B), which shows a persistently higher cumulative hazard for hospital mortality in the limited-treatment group, with increasing divergence between groups over time. Unlike ICU mortality, where the adjusted cumulative hazard ratio gradually attenuated over time, the risk of hospital mortality in the limited-treatment group remained elevated throughout the hospital stay.

After adjustment for sex and APACHE III-J score, the cumulative hazard ratio for hospital mortality remained higher in the limited-treatment group compared with the full-treatment group (reference HR = 1.0). Although the magnitude of this excess risk gradually declined over time, the hazard ratio remained approximately 1.4 at the end of the observation period, indicating a persistent but attenuating excess risk of hospital mortality (Fig. 3C).

Sensitivity analysis excluding elective surgical admissions

Restricting the analysis to acute ICU admissions yielded similar results to the primary analysis. The acute-admission cohort included 22,670 patients, of whom 9,400 (41.46%) had limited treatment and 13,270 (58.54%) received full treatment. After propensity score matching, 9,314 pairs (18,628 patients) were retained with well-balanced baseline characteristics. In the matched acute cohort, ICU mortality was 12.01% in the limited-treatment group versus 8.33% in the full-treatment group, while hospital mortality was 25.76% versus 18.02%, respectively (Supplementary Table 6). Flexible parametric survival models showed time-varying patterns consistent with the primary analysis, with persistently higher cumulative hazards for both ICU and hospital mortality in the limited-treatment group throughout follow-up (Supplementary Figs. 10–11).

Impact of treatment goals on ICU and hospital length of stay

In the propensity-matched cohort (Table 3), ICU LOS did not significantly differ between treatment groups (p = 0.452), while hospital LOS was significantly shorter in patients receiving limited treatment (p < 0.001). After adjusting for sex and APACHE III-J score, limited treatment was independently associated with a 3.4% reduction in ICU LOS (p = 0.006) and a 5.6% reduction in hospital LOS (p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 7).

Given the significantly shorter LOS in the limited-treatment group, a Fine–Gray competing risks analysis was performed to determine whether this was due to faster recovery or early mortality. The analysis demonstrated that limited treatment was significantly associated with an increased cumulative incidence of both ICU mortality (sHR 1.717, 95% CI 1.555–1.900, p < 0.001) and hospital mortality (sHR 1.643, 95% CI 1.541–1.752, p < 0.001), suggesting a higher likelihood of early death in this group (Supplementary Table 8).

A higher APACHE III-J score was independently associated with ICU mortality (sHR 1.036, 95% CI 1.034–1.040, p < 0.001) and hospital mortality (sHR 1.031, 95% CI 1.030–1.032, p < 0.001). In linear regression models, APACHE III-J was associated with longer ICU LOS but paradoxically with shorter hospital LOS. This discrepancy is likely explained by competing risks, as patients with higher APACHE III-J scores had increased early hospital mortality, which shortened their overall hospital stay duration.

Additionally, male sex was associated with a higher incidence of hospital mortality after adjustment. Further exploration of sex-based differences in baseline characteristics and clinical outcomes revealed that male sex was associated with greater disease severity and a higher burden of comorbid conditions (Supplementary Table 9).

Discussion

Key findings

Patients with documented limited treatment had higher ICU and hospital mortality than those receiving full treatment. Importantly, the excess risk of ICU mortality was most pronounced early after admission and gradually attenuated over time, although it remained consistently higher than in the full-treatment group throughout the ICU stay. A similar time-dependent pattern was observed for hospital mortality, with a progressively declining but persistently elevated risk in patients with limited treatment goals. These findings remained consistent in a sensitivity analysis restricted to acute ICU admissions.

Despite shorter ICU and hospital length of stay in the limited-treatment group, this difference was primarily explained by higher early mortality rather than faster clinical recovery or differences in care delivery. Higher APACHE III-J scores were independently associated with increased mortality and longer ICU stays, whereas shorter hospital LOS appeared to be largely driven by early mortality rather than improved recovery.

Male sex was associated with higher hospital mortality but not with ICU mortality, which may reflect a greater burden of comorbidities and baseline vulnerability rather than differences in ICU care.

Relationship of our findings to the literature

The prevalence of treatment limitation orders in ICU patients varies widely by region, with higher rates reported in North America (50.4%), Australia (50.8%), and Europe (43.6%), and much lower rates in Asia (12.8%) and the Middle East (3.1%), likely reflecting cultural and religious factors [27]. In our study, 30.2% of critically ill nonagenarians had documented limited treatment, and this group experienced significantly higher ICU and hospital mortality. These results align with previous work, including a large Australian cohort study of ICU patients aged ≥ 75 years, which found that limited treatment, rather than age or ICU type, was the strongest predictors of mortality [28].

While some studies suggest that nonagenarians may have comparable adjusted outcomes despite less aggressive ICU care, including lower use of mechanical ventilation, or renal replacement therapy [14, 15], our findings indicate that limited treatment at ICU admission is independently associated with poorer outcomes. This is consistent with evidence from broader ICU populations [29], an octogenarian cohort [30], and recent reviews focused on nonagenarians [31], all of which indicate that limited treatment is a key factor associated with mortality in very old patients.

Higher APACHE III-J scores were consistently associated with ICU and hospital mortality, supporting its role in early risk stratification in this population [21]. This aligns with prior studies supporting the predictive value of acute physiology scores, including APACHE III-J [32], Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II) [12, 33, 34], and the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) [34]. Other well-established risk factors, including advanced age [35], medical and non-elective surgical admissions [9, 12, 33, 36, 37], and comorbidity burden measured by indices such as the Charlson Comorbidity Index [38], have also been associated with higher mortality. Beyond these established risk factors, baseline frailty is increasingly recognized as a critical prognostic determinant in this age group. Emerging evidence suggests that among critically ill nonagenarians, frailty may be a stronger determinant of outcomes than acute illness severity alone [39], a finding further reinforced by a recent scoping review which reported consistent associations between elevated SAPS II, SOFA, and CCI scores and mortality, and identified frailty as a central contributor to prognosis in this population [40].

However, interpreting these mortality differences between treatment strategies requires consideration of whether prolonged survival translates into meaningful clinical benefit. In very old ICU populations, survival may occur alongside substantial morbidity, including post–intensive care syndrome (PICS), which encompasses physical, cognitive, and psychological impairments after critical illness [41, 42]. Prior studies have shown that many elderly ICU survivors do not return to their baseline functional status and may experience persistent reductions in quality of life after discharge [43, 44]. In our cohort, survival probability declined steeply early after ICU admission, and the gap between the survival curves gradually narrowed over time, suggesting that additional survival time in this population may not necessarily translate into meaningful recovery or improved well-being. Because our study did not capture patient-centered outcomes such as functional recovery, cognitive status, or health-related quality of life, the clinical significance of the observed survival differences remains uncertain.

Clinical implications

Limited treatment in critically ill nonagenarians was associated with higher ICU and hospital mortality, with a particularly elevated early ICU mortality risk. The time-dependent pattern of mortality suggests that patients who survive the initial critical phase may have a better chance of recovery, emphasizing the need for periodic reassessment of treatment goals based on evolving clinical conditions. These findings suggest that decisions regarding treatment intensity should be guided by evolving clinical risk rather than fixed determinations made at ICU admission.

While APACHE III-J was associated with increased ICU and hospital mortality and may support early risk stratification, it does not account for key geriatric factors such as frailty, functional status, or cognitive impairment. Clinical decisions regarding treatment intensity should therefore integrate both physiological severity and baseline vulnerability, ideally supported by validated frailty assessments.

Structured discussions on treatment goals at ICU admission, aligned with prognostic expectations and the patient’s evolving clinical status, should also consider differences in end-of-life care perspectives, religious beliefs, and healthcare policies. Incorporating these factors to support patient-centered approaches that respect individual and cultural values is crucial for guiding appropriate care adjustments throughout the ICU stay.

Directions for future research

Future studies should explore long-term outcomes and quality of life (QoL) among critically ill nonagenarians, particularly regarding functional recovery, cognitive status, and post-ICU independence. In addition to treatment goals (limited vs. full treatment), incorporating baseline frailty assessment is essential for understanding its prognostic value relative to acute illness severity. Prospective studies using validated frailty measures could help identify patients most likely to benefit from intensive care and clarify how frailty interacts with treatment decisions and outcomes. Furthermore, economic evaluations assessing the cost-effectiveness of different treatment strategies may help optimize ICU resource allocation and inform policy discussions on care for this high-risk population.

Strengths and limitations

This study is one of the largest cohort analyses of critically ill nonagenarians, utilizing 14 years of data from the ANZICS Adult Patient Database, which includes a diverse ICU population across Australia and New Zealand. Another key strength of this study is its novel focus on the association between APACHE III-J scores and outcomes in critically ill nonagenarians—an area that remains underexplored despite the widespread use of physiological severity scoring systems in clinical practice. The use of this standardized severity adjustment further enhances the reliability and generalizability of our findings. By leveraging a propensity score-matched cohort, we achieved a robust balancing of baseline characteristics between the full treatment and limited-treatment groups, minimizing confounding and ensuring comparability of outcomes. Methodological rigor was further enhanced by time-dependent hazard modeling to account for proportional hazards violations and competing risks regression to refine mortality and length of stay estimates.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be considered. First, as a retrospective observational study, causality cannot be definitively established, and residual confounders may still influence results despite propensity score matching. Second, reliance on a single regional database may limit generalizability to settings outside Australia and New Zealand. Third, excluding patients admitted primarily for palliative care and those with missing data may have introduced selection bias and potentially underrepresent the most vulnerable patients.

Additionally, frailty data were not consistently available in the dataset and could not be incorporated into our primary analysis. Given that frailty is increasingly recognized as a key prognostic factor in very old patients in the ICU, its absence may limit our ability to fully capture baseline vulnerability and interpret outcome differences across treatment groups. The database records treatment goals documented at ICU admission but does not capture when or where these decisions were established (e.g., outpatient setting, during hospital admission, or at ICU admission), nor the process through which such decisions were made. It also does not record subsequent changes in treatment goals during the ICU stay. As a result, we could not distinguish between pre-existing advance care decisions and limited treatment introduced during the acute hospital presentation, which may have influenced the observed associations. We also lack data on critically ill nonagenarians who were not admitted to the ICU, limiting insight into broader treatment decision-making processes.

Lastly, this study focuses on short-term outcomes (ICU and hospital mortality), without assessing long-term survival, functional recovery, or post-ICU quality of life. The dataset also lacked granular detail on crucial patient-specific information such as patient preferences, advance directives, or specific resuscitation decisions, limiting a comprehensive understanding of how individualized goals of care influence outcomes.

Conclusion

Limited treatment in critically ill nonagenarians was associated with higher ICU and hospital mortality. The excess mortality risk was most pronounced early after ICU admission and gradually attenuated over time, although it remained elevated throughout the ICU stay. These findings support the use of early risk stratification and periodic reassessment of treatment goals to ensure that care remains aligned with the patient’s evolving clinical condition.
