The Dutch Nationwide Pathology Databank and its central role in health research
Authors: Albert G. Siebers, Quirinus J.M. Voorham, Chantal C.H.J. Kuijpers, Annette H. Bruggink, Paul A. Seegers, Esther C. van den Broek, Menno Hoogeveen, Michiel van den Brand, Carolien H.M. van Deurzen, Robby E. Kibbelaar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Dutch Nationwide Pathology Databank (Palga) was established in 1971 and achieved nationwide coverage of all Dutch pathology departments in 1991. It serves as the central repository for pathology data across the Netherlands. Palga manages a nationwide IT infrastructure enabling the collection, structuring, and secure sharing of pathology data from all Dutch pathology departments. This unique infrastructure supports diagnostics in patient care by enabling pathologists to access nationwide patient pathology histories and by supporting standardized, structured reporting, particularly in population-based screening programs and oncological pathology. It also serves as a valuable resource for secondary data use, supporting scientific research and healthcare policy decision-making.This article provides an overview of the Palga infrastructure, with a focus on its embedded scientific database for secondary use. It describes how pathology data are collected, structured, and shared, and explains how data are securely processed in compliance with current legislation, with particular emphasis on safe secondary use.Secure secondary use of pathology data is expected to increasingly advance evidence-based healthcare research for the benefit of society. Palga's nationwide infrastructure provides substantial added value for patient care, health research, and healthcare quality improvement, while enabling responsible and socially valuable secondary data use through collaboration and robust legal and ethical governance.

Introduction

The Netherlands was one of the first countries to initiate computer-based registration of pathology diagnostics as early as the 1970s.1 Starting with only a limited number of pathology departments, a nationwide pathology network was established in 1991 under the name Palga Foundation (hereafter referred to as Palga).2 In general, pathologists diagnose medical conditions based on tissue and cellular material, including molecular diagnostics. Today, all Dutch pathology departments collectively generate a large volume of pathology diagnoses. These data are primarily used to support clinical decision-making in individual patient care (primary use), but they also serve broader purposes, such as clinical, epidemiological, and public health research (secondary use). Consequently, these data have become valuable for policymakers and healthcare quality monitoring, and with the introduction of the European Health Data Space (EHDS), they are increasingly relevant in an international context. They also play a key role in real-world evidence studies that provide insights into healthcare practices and outcomes in real-life settings.

Palga hosts the Dutch Nationwide Pathology Databank and supports both primary use in patient care and secondary use in research, with its embedded Palga Scientific Database (PScDb) specifically dedicated to the latter. As of mid-2026, this national pathology data collection, in a country with 18 million inhabitants, has accumulated to more than 92 million records from more than 15 million persons and has contributed to more than 750 scientific publications (https://www.palga.nl/jaarverslagen (available in Dutch)).

As healthcare technologies continue to advance and the number of clinical data sources grows, the need for high-quality, complete, and source-registered data—captured clearly, unambiguously, and only once—becomes increasingly important, also driven by stricter legal requirements. Compliance with the FAIR principles (Findability, Accessibility, Interoperability, and Reusability) is key to this effort. Palga contributes to this ambition by enabling nationwide standardized structured reporting (SSR) of pathology data, thereby supporting the development of a self-learning healthcare system.

The aim of this article is to provide an up-to-date description of the Palga infrastructure, including its technical, organizational, and legal framework, database composition, and methods for secure high-quality collection, processing, and reusing pathology data, highlighting developments since the widely cited article by Casparie et al. in 20072 and outlining the database's potential within the rapidly evolving healthcare landscape. Data collection, database composition, and quality assurance

Data collection

All 39 pathology departments in the Netherlands are connected to and participate in the Palga data infrastructure, resulting in one of the most comprehensive national pathology infrastructures worldwide with nationwide coverage. Every pathology department in the Netherlands uses a local (commercial) Laboratory Information Management System (LIMS) for primary registration and reporting of pathological specimens. The LIMS is integrated with and connected to what is referred to as the Palga Node (Fig. 1). Subsequently, each local Palga Node is linked to the Palga Hub, which serves as a secure gateway facilitating communication between departments across the country.Fig. 1Local LIMS and Palga Node.HIS: hospital information system; LIMS: laboratory information management system; SSR protocol module: standardized structured reporting protocol module.Fig. 1

The Palga Hub supports the primary use of pathology data in patient care, while also facilitating automated data collections for secondary uses, such as the National Cancer Registry (NCR), the Dutch Institute for Clinical Auditing3 and the registry of the national cervical screening program. It functions as a central index, with all patient-related data remaining at the source (i.e., the pathology department) (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Palga Nodes and Palga Hub for patient care.Fig. 2

In contrast, the PScDb is a central database used specifically for secondary purposes such as scientific research, quality monitoring, and healthcare policy development. This database is pseudonymized; personal identifiers are transformed into several distinct pseudonyms to ensure privacy protection. For example, one pseudonym is generated by combining date of birth, sex, and the first four letters of the surname by birth, whereas another uses the date of birth, sex, first initial, and the first eight letters of the surname by birth. Consequently, the former is less distinctive and more prone to duplication, whereas the latter is more distinctive but also more prone to pseudonym splitting—for example, when variations in the patient's name result in multiple pseudonyms.

Each Palga Node transfers data to the PScDb through a Trusted Third Party (TTP) (ZorgTTP, Houten, the Netherlands). Identifiable personal data are irreversibly pseudonymized in two steps, with the encryption key stored outside the Palga organization, ensuring that no one within Palga has access to identifiable data. In the first step, personal identifiers are converted at the source into a first-order pseudonym. This pseudonym is then further processed centrally by ZorgTTP to create a final Palga pseudonym (Fig. 3). There is no link to the original data, and original data are not stored during the process. This approach guarantees that the use of (pseudonymized) patient data is fully compliant with privacy legislation. Data structure and coding principles

Data exchange between the Palga Node and PScDb takes the form of a short extract from the pathology report (excerpt). This excerpt includes various patient pseudonyms, a report ID, metadata, and several free-text fields, such as clinical data, macroscopy, microscopy (with results from additional techniques, e.g., immunohistochemistry or molecular testing, when applicable), as well as the conclusion text. Furthermore, standardized structured variables are also included in the data when applicable.

After finalization of the pathology report, the pathologist labels each case in a standardized manner, summarizing the conclusion text in a condensed format referred to as the “Palga One-line Diagnosis Summary” (PODS), which will be discussed in more detail below.

Before pseudonymized excerpts enter the PScDb, they are collected in the Raw Datastore and processed using dedicated Extract-Transform-Load tooling. The PScDb is updated weekly, ensuring that the national database remains current and comprehensive.

Coded diagnostic reporting and standardized structured reporting

Data integrity and quality are essential for enabling effective analysis of pathology and molecular data for scientific purposes. Consequently, the implementation of data validation tools and SSR protocols is crucial. Palga places strong emphasis on both, ensuring consistency and reliability across all Dutch pathology departments.

Coded diagnostic reporting

During the authorization phase in the LIMS, the Palga Node checks the internal quality and completeness of the entered data, immediately flagging any discrepancies for prompt correction by the entrant. Once validated, the Palga Node generates and stores an excerpt of the final pathology report.

A key component of this excerpt is the PODS which can be regarded as a summary of the report's conclusion section. The PODS consists of a series of terms, resulting in a structured diagnostic line that, at a minimum, includes the anatomical location (topography) of the examined material, the procedure followed to obtain the material (technique), and at least one pathological diagnosis term, all separated by asterisks. When the conclusion text includes several diagnoses or spans multiple anatomical locations, more than one series of codes may be assigned to accurately represent all findings. Additional Palga terms exist for other elements, such as etiology, functional abnormalities, and other diseases. These Palga terms are listed in a codebook, known as the Palga Thesaurus (https://www.palga.nl/voor-pathologen/palga-on-line-thesaurus). The Palga terms are linked to an internal coding system and mapped to corresponding SNOMED CT codes. The Palga thesaurus facilitates both the searchability of the databases and the interoperability of the data.

As an example, a narrative conclusion text like “Cervical biopsy showing an hrHPV-positive adenocarcinoma” would be coded using Palga terms as: “cervix*biopsy*adenocarcinoma*high-risk HPV type positive” with the corresponding Palga codes automatically generated as “T83000*P11400*M81403*E33453M00021.”

Palga terms are based on (internationally) recognized coding systems, such as SNOMED and ICD-O, and are mapped through the Palga Thesaurus, which is publicly accessible and regularly updated. Today, the Palga Thesaurus is linked to SNOMED CT, ensuring future-proof (inter)national integration with other complementary data sources. Palga terms are automatically converted into corresponding Palga codes.

Unlike the Palga terms, the Palga codes are structured hierarchically, facilitating efficient retrieval of terms and ensuring coverage of synonymous terminology over time. For example, the anatomical location term for “colon” corresponds to Palga code T67000, whereas “ascending colon” is represented by T67200. Querying the database for T67 retrieves both terms. Similarly, synonymous pathology terms are grouped under a single Palga code, ensuring that all synonymous terms used in pathology diagnostics can be retrieved.

Groups of Palga codes can be aggregated into higher-order retrieval terms, which represent generic diagnostic or anatomical categories such as “all benign neoplasms,” “all primary carcinomas,” “all metastases,” or “complete digestive tract,” and procedures such as “all resections.” These retrieval terms enable efficient, consistent, and comprehensive searches across large datasets, thereby facilitating research.

Standardized structured reporting

The results of pathological examinations can be reported either in a narrative format or a standardized structured format. Traditionally, all pathology reports in the Netherlands were narrative, containing clinical data, the concluding diagnosis, and additional information on morphological (macroscopy and microscopy), immunological, and molecular characteristics, all documented in free-text fields. However, with the increasing complexity of healthcare, the semantic interoperability of diagnostic information is becoming increasingly important. At the same time, the demand for detailed pathological features continues to grow to ensure optimal diagnosis and effective patient management. Extracting clinically relevant information from narrative reports can be challenging and inefficient, both in daily clinical practice and for secondary use. In the era of more complex data and guidelines, narrative reports alone may no longer be sufficient.4

SSR addresses these challenges by using a predefined and structured reporting format, referred to as a Palga protocol. For reporting purposes, SSR automatically generates both the conclusion text and the corresponding PODS. This approach ensures consistency, completeness, and accuracy, thereby enhancing overall data integrity and quality.5, 6 This is associated with improved treatment options and patient outcomes.7 In the Netherlands, SSR is at its highest level, using fully standardized, evidence-based pathology protocols with discrete data elements to ensure complete, consistent, and high-quality reporting across all pathology departments.5, 9

Palga introduced the first protocols for SSR for colorectal and breast cancer resection specimens in 2009, ensuring their nationwide availability. To support increasingly complex histological, cytological, and molecular SSR protocols, Palga developed the Palga Protocol Module (PPM) in 2013. Built on the LogicNets Inc. platform (LogicNets Inc., Eindhoven, The Netherlands), the PPM is connected to the local LIMS and used for diagnostic reporting. All SSR protocols are validated through internal rules and control mechanisms. The PPM and SSR protocols have been CE-certified since 2019, demonstrating compliance with applicable regulatory requirements for safety and performance.

As of mid-2026, Palga maintains 34 operational SSR protocols, mainly focusing on oncological pathology and covering nearly all tumor types for which national treatment guidelines are available. These protocols include histology, cytology, and molecular diagnostics., In addition, a limited number of non-oncological protocols are available, such as for placental pathology and urine cytology. Although SSR protocols are widely implemented, their use is not mandatory. Nevertheless, in recent years, more than one-third of pathology reports have been generated using structured reporting.

All Dutch pathology departments have access to the PPM and SSR protocols via their LIMS. However, the actual usage varies by pathology department and by protocol. Whereas the use of SSR protocols is not mandatory in general, its use is strongly recommended in several Dutch national clinical guidelines such as those for lung cancer, endometrial carcinoma, breast cancer, ovarian carcinoma, and prostate cancer. However, for colon biopsies and cervical smears performed within the framework of the population-based screening programs, these SSR protocols are mandatory for each specimen type. Overall, adoption of SSR protocols is high and continues to rise, with more than 900,000 SSR pathology reports recorded in 2025. Ongoing initiatives aim to further increase adoption at both the pathology department level and the SSR protocol level.9, 10 Moreover, evidence from colorectal cancer demonstrates that SSR not only generates more complete and reliable data compared with traditional narrative reporting but also contributes to improved patient outcomes, including survival.7

Since 2022, the medical content of each national SSR protocol is approved by field-specific expert groups from the Dutch Society of Pathology. Each new SSR protocol is based on datasets from the International Collaboration on Cancer Reporting, Dutch national treatment guidelines from the Federation of Medical Specialists and according to the TNM Classification of Malignant Tumors published by the Union for International Cancer Control. These datasets define the mandatory core elements for each organ-specific minimal dataset. Users can request additional non-core elements, which can be configured at pathology department level. As guidelines and clinical practice evolve, the SSR protocols are continuously updated.

Like the narrative reports, the structured reports are mapped with SNOMED CT terminology bindings, next to the Palga terms.8 However, also each separate item recorded within the SSR protocols, will be linked with SNOMED CT. This offers several advantages. By assigning unique and unambiguous codes to specific medical concepts (e.g., anatomical locations, procedures, and pathological findings), SNOMED CT ensures semantic interoperability through the various data sources and enables integration across hospitals, healthcare providers, and research institutions, supporting patient-centered care even at an international level. For example, SNOMED CT includes patient-friendly terms, enabling patients to better understand their own health records in a personal health environment. It also provides a shared language in multidisciplinary team (MDT) meetings and Molecular Tumor Boards, reducing miscommunication and facilitating decision-making processes.

Data quality control, validation, and certification

The quality of reported data is crucial for ensuring reliable healthcare and scientific insight.

Effective quality control and validation processes are therefore essential to maintain consistency and reliability. Palga has developed dedicated software tools to automate these checks and validations, helping to safeguard high-quality data.

The first of these tools is the “Palga One-line Diagnosis Summary-Control Module” (PODS-CM). As the use of the PODS with Palga terms is mandatory, the PODS-CM is installed on each Palga Node to ensure compliance and to flag any incomplete or inconsistent use of Palga terms during the authorization phase of the electronic report. Reports flagged by the system cannot be authorized or submitted until corrections have been made. The PODS-CM verifies that the PODS adheres to the established structural and semantic standards defined by the Palga thesaurus, ensuring uniformity and completeness across pathology departments.

The second software tool is the Palga Frontend Control Module (PF-CM). Whereas the PODS-CM focuses on structural and content standards of the PODS, the PF-CM operates at a different level. It checks that mandatory personal and report data (e.g., date of birth) are entered correctly, formatted properly, and that all other relevant report fields are complete and accurate.

Palga ensures quality and secure handling of its systems and data. The organization is certified under ISO 27001 (information security management) and NEN 7510 (information security in healthcare), demonstrating adherence to rigorous standards for data protection and operational reliability.

Data governance, privacy, and ethical framework

Legal framework

In the European Union (EU), the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) has been effective since 2018 and provides the fundamental legal framework for the protection of personal data and privacy of all EU citizens. The GDPR sets strict requirements for the collection, storage, processing, and sharing of personal data.11 It requires a valid legal basis for data processing (such as explicit consent in certain contexts) and mandates that data be securely stored and not unlawfully shared. The GDPR also guarantees citizens' rights, including the right to access, correct, and object to the processing of their personal data.

The EHDS builds upon the GDPR. The EHDS aims to establish a common framework and infrastructure to facilitate the secure and seamless exchange of health data within the EU. This is intended not only to improve patient care but also to enable the (re)use of health data for research, policymaking, and innovation.

At the national level, the legal basis for use of health data in the Netherlands is based on the GDPR (Articles 5(1)(b) and 9) and national legislation (Article 7:458 of Dutch Implementation Act of the General Data Protection Regulation (UAVG)). The use of personal data for purposes, other than the original, generally requires consent. However, secondary use for scientific research may be permitted without explicit consent under specific conditions, provided that:•the data are used for scientific or statistical research in the public interest;•the data are pseudonymized to protect patient identity;•obtaining individual consent would be impracticable or disproportionate in relation to the research objectives;•the data are minimized, including only data necessary for the research purpose.

In contrast, if research cohorts are collected specifically by a research group or registry, and where patients were enrolled with explicit consent for the study purposes, the use of these data is limited to the primary purposes of the consent. In such cases, informed consent and/or other appropriate governance procedures are required for linking or combining these datasets with other sources, such as the PScDb, but always in accordance with applicable ethical and legal frameworks.

Together, these European legislations, complemented by national laws and regulations, form the legal and regulatory framework under which patients and citizens demand transparency, control, and public accountability regarding the (re)use of their health data, including for purposes beyond direct patient care.

Roles and responsibilities of Palga as public data infrastructure

Within the context of the GDPR, the participating pathology departments are the data controllers. They are primarily responsible for the collection and processing of their (own) data and for ensuring compliance with the GDPR.

In general, patient consent for the use of pathology data in the Netherlands follows an opt-out system. Patients are informed about the use of their data, which is included unless they explicitly object. The connected pathology departments, acting as data controllers, are responsible for ensuring that patients are adequately informed about this process.11, 12 Some pathology departments in the Netherlands are moving towards explicit (opt-in) consent, either alongside or replacing the traditional opt-out model. The upcoming EHDS, however, is generally oriented towards an opt-out approach, although individual countries are expected to apply their own interpretations.

Palga functions as data processor. As such, it is responsible for the secure handling of personal data and for ensuring full compliance with the GDPR. This includes guaranteeing that all data that are received and processed are stored securely, that access is restricted, and that any unauthorized use is prevented. Palga ensures that its data processing systems and protocols meet the requirements for data security, integrity and confidentiality.

In addition, Palga is exploring the possibility of expanding its role of Data Mediation Entity within the EHDS. In this role, Palga would support the structured and secure exchange of health data for secondary use, in compliance with EHDS requirements and in collaboration with the designated Health Data Access Body.

Data protection and security standards

Palga has the societal responsibility to ensure that pathology data are handled safely, and in compliance with current legislation. Building on its role as data processor and its potential future role as a Data Mediation Entity, as well as its responsibilities for data security, Palga implements concrete measures to protect the patient data including pseudonymization, strict access, and secure data storage. In addition, by aligning its SSR data with EHDS standards, Palga facilitates international data sharing while maintaining compliance with both GDPR and EHDS-specific requirements.

Governance and ethical review

As a national public data infrastructure, Palga is responsible for accountability and transparency towards its various stakeholders and has implemented a formal governance model. This includes various Palga Committees, working groups (some of which are in close cooperation with the Dutch Society for Pathology) and the Palga Advisory Board. Ethical review is performed by various internal committees before data are made available for secondary purposes. In this way, Palga adds public trust as a national data infrastructure, supporting patient care, scientific research, and health policy.

Data utilization and access

High-quality health research is essential for an efficient, effective, and self-learning healthcare system, driven by biomedical innovations such as personalized and precision medicine. National population-based registries play a crucial role in facilitating this kind of research by providing optimal health data.13 Palga supports these efforts by making pathology data available for health research at various levels of aggregation. Anonymized counts of specific pathology diagnoses (aggregated data) are publicly retrievable through the Palga Public Database (PPDb), ensuring privacy while still providing valuable insights. At a lower level of aggregation, more detailed data can be obtained from the PScDb. These two databases are discussed in more detail below.

Palga Public Database

The PPDb (https://www.palgaopenbaredatabank.nl/), which is derived directly from the PScDb, serves as an initial resource for researchers to explore the number of specific diagnoses available. This inherently indicates the potential availability of corresponding pathology materials. The PPDb contains data organized by Palga code, rather than at the patient level, with each code corresponding to an individual diagnostic event. This preliminary information helps researchers assess if the PScDb is a suitable source for their research questions and whether a formal application to Palga is warranted.

The data in the PPDb are fully anonymized and cannot be traced back to individual patients. The PPDb provides only aggregated numbers and can be searched using Palga terms and codes. Results may be stratified by year, age group, gender, and type of material (histology or cytology). To prevent the unintended identification of individuals, the PPDb does not contain results for queries yielding 10 or fewer cases.

Palga Scientific Database

The data from the PScDb are primarily intended for researchers, pathologists, medical specialists, epidemiologists, and other health scientists. Its use is governed by specific terms and conditions as described in the legal framework section above, but it also includes adherence to the Dutch Code of Conduct for Health Research (https://elsi.health-ri.nl/sites/elsi/files/2023-06/Code%20of%20Conduct%20for%20Health%20Research%202022.pdf).

Additionally, Palga provides datasets only if strict conditions for data usage are met, specifically:•the data may be used solely for the specific purpose outlined in the data request;•the data must not be shared with any third party that is not mentioned in the request;•publication of results in a peer-reviewed journal is mandatory, even in the case of negative results.

As described previously, the PScDb contains no directly identifiable personal information; but instead, pseudonyms are used to protect patient privacy. The database is updated weekly to ensure that the most recent nationwide pathology data are available.

Data access and governance

Over the last decades, Palga has consistently received 150–200 data requests per year. These can be submitted by anyone conducting scientific or health research, but always in collaboration with a pathologist affiliated with a pathology department in the Netherlands. These data requests are generally free of charge. Submissions are processed through a dedicated online application system, the Palga Portal (https://aanvraag.palga.nl).

After submission and signed acceptance of the terms of delivery, each request is reviewed by Palga's Committee for Research, Innovation, and Healthcare Quality Evaluation and the Privacy Committee to assess:•whether the requested data are appropriate and available in the PScDb to answer the research question;•scientific relevance;•research methodology;•compliance with European and national privacy legislation.

The Scientific Committee is composed of a MDT of experts, including pathologists, epidemiologists, and molecular biologists in pathology. The role of the Palga Privacy Committee is to protect patient privacy, but it does not replace an accredited Medical Research Ethics Committee or the Central Committee on Research Involving Human Subjects.

Requests for data from the PScDb typically fall into three main categories: exploratory requests, general data requests, and requests involving linkage to other research cohorts or registries, which will be discussed separately below.

Authorized Palga staff (data stewards) use SAS Enterprise Guide (SAS EG, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) to query the database for specific tissue and/or morphology (based on the terms and codes, captured in the PODS), time periods and patient characteristics such as age, sex, and other metadata. The availability of pseudonyms in the PScDb enables the data stewards to recognize reports belonging to the same person. However, researchers receive only randomly assigned study specific identification numbers, and never pseudonyms or any other patient-identifying information, ensuring that the data remain relevant while fully non-identifiable.

Exploratory data requests

When a search in the PPDb does not yield sufficiently specific or adequate results, researchers can submit an exploratory data request. These requests are typically aimed at gaining more detailed insights into the availability of specific pathology data and the feasibility of using the PScDb to address a research question, prepare a grant application, or submit a formal request for general data access or a linkage study.

The results of exploratory data requests typically include aggregated data tables (e.g., number of cases meeting specific criteria, often stratified) and do not provide access to any detailed individual-level data. Palga explicitly states that it is not responsible for the validity of the data in exploratory data requests and discourages their use for publication purposes, as they are primarily intended for internal research refinement. Because exploratory data have not undergone rigorous validation, it is important that users exercise caution when considering it for any formal analysis or public dissemination.

General data requests

General data requests involve targeted search strategies on specific pathology or patient groups, or combinations of these. Palga codes and retrieval terms are essential for efficient and reliable data retrieval and selection by the Palga data stewards. These codes and terms originate from the Palga terms recorded into the PODS by pathologists, underscoring the importance of accurate diagnostic coding rules and their standardization.

Requests are processed through the Palga Portal, which ensures a standardized and transparent procedure for submission and review. Researchers first register in the Palga Portal before submitting a request for data and/or pathology material. Each request is then evaluated by the Palga Scientific and Privacy Committee.

When approved, the Palga data stewards access and query the PScDb using SAS EG software. This allows them to efficiently retrieve and process pathology data based on specific strategies. Utilizing the advanced functionalities of SAS EG, data stewards can apply tailored search strategies, filter relevant records, and create dedicated datasets. These queries result in structured and validated datasets that meet the required standards for research, scientific analysis, and publication in peer-reviewed journals. Thus, a complete pathology patient history, including both pre-existing conditions and subsequent diagnostics, can be provided. This history can be tailored to specific anatomical locations or organ areas, or types of abnormalities (e.g., metastases).

Requesters can expect high-quality datasets, which include standard variables such as:•an anonymized random patient identification number;•excerpt sequence number, age, and sex;•excerpt-specific variables like the date and year of the pathological examination, type of examination (histology, cytology, autopsy), free-text field such as the conclusion text, and the various PODSs.

When constructing research datasets, Palga data stewards select excerpts and identify those belonging to the same individual using probabilistic linkage of pseudonymised patient characteristics across the records. To address the risk of duplicate pseudonyms from less distinctive identifiers or arising from incomplete or variable patient identifiers, an additional quality indicator (administrative_duplicate_probability) is used during data extraction and delivery. This indicator is based on probabilistic comparisons of pseudonymised patient characteristics across excerpts. Excerpts are linked when sufficient similarity is observed across key identifiers, whereas discrepancies in more weakly discriminative variables do not necessarily preclude a match (e.g., differences in initials due to use of first names versus formal names).

To balance the risk of false positives (incorrectly merging excerpts from different patients) and false negatives (incorrectly separating excerpts from the same patient), the variable “administrative_duplicate_probability” is assigned to all excerpts. This variable categorizes the degree of ambiguity in patient linkage into three levels, ranging from “1. non” to “3. present—likely”, with higher values indicating a higher likelihood of administrative duplication. The score is determined for both target and historical excerpts, and also reflects the internal consistency between multiple target excerpts where applicable, thereby providing researchers with an additional measure of linkage uncertainty.

Because the PScDb is updated weekly with nationwide coverage, it enables near real-time monitoring of diagnostic trends and volumes, disease incidence, quality indicators, and immediate impact of external events. For example, van Velthuysen et al.15 showed that pathology report volumes dropped by up to 88% during the first COVID-19 lockdown, with their results published shortly thereafter, thus demonstrating the rapid capture of nationwide diagnostic trends.

Long-term insights are also possible. A retrospective cohort study of women diagnosed with cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 3 demonstrated that this group remains at a persistently increased risk of developing HPV-related carcinomas and premalignancies in the anogenital and oropharyngeal regions, even after up to 20 years of follow-up.16

The data available in the PScDb can be used to study cost-effective clinical strategies as well. Vink-Börger et al. evaluated a two-antibody approach (PMS2 and MSH6) for mismatch repair testing in colorectal and endometrial cancer, comparing it with the traditional four-antibody method. Their findings showed that the two-antibody strategy provides a valid, cost-saving alternative to the standard approach.17

Data requests involving linkage to other research cohorts or registries

Patient cohorts from complementary data sources—such as cohorts specifically collected by research groups, individual researchers, registries, biobanks, or clinical trials—can be linked to the PScDb under defined conditions. This enables researchers to enrich pathology data with additional information, such as clinical outcomes, mortality, treatment, or lifestyle factors, thereby creating more comprehensive datasets, supporting more extensive analyses, and adding substantial scientific value.

Over the last decade, PScDb data have been successfully linked to a variety of complementary sources. Linkage is generally achieved through pseudonymized patient identifiers from both datasets, ensuring privacy while enabling integrated analyses.

Patient-identifying data from other sources are always submitted via ZorgTTP, a Trusted Third Party, which also handles pseudonymization for the Palga Nodes (see also the section: Data Collection). A key requirement for linking Palga datasets with external sources is that neither source enriches its database with data from the other. Furthermore, the researcher must comply with Palga's terms and conditions as well as those of the other data provider. Consequently, the researcher retrieves data from both sources and can combine them using a shared key. In this way, the data can be integrated without transferring Palga data to the external source or, conversely, data from the external source to Palga.

Many requests for data linkage focus on combining data from the NCR with Palga data. These mostly aim at combining survival or treatment data from the NCR with tumor-specific data, information on metastasis, coexisting conditions or other data not registered by the NCR as illustrated by a previous study linking both datasets.18

Other examples of established linkages involve connections with the National Business Intelligence Center for Healthcare in the Netherlands (Vektis),19 the Netherlands Perinatal Registry (PeriNed),20, 21 Statistics Netherlands (CBS),22 the Lifelines Cohort Study and Biobank (Lifelines),23 the PHARMO Data Network (PHARMO),24 the Dutch Archipelago of Ovarian Cancer Research (AOCR),25 Hereditary Breast and Ovarian Cancer in the Netherlands Study (HEBON),26, 27 and the Foundation For the Detection of Hereditary Tumors (StOET).28, 29 As noted earlier in the Legal Framework section, for linkages of cohorts specifically collected by research groups with the PScDb, explicit informed consent from participants is required.

Facilitating access to archived diagnostic tissue for research purposes

Millions of formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue samples and slides, originally collected for patient care, are stored in local pathology archives throughout the Netherlands. These residual materials are invaluable: they may be required for future diagnostics purposes such as additional testing or family screening in the context of hereditary diseases, but they also serve as a secondary-use biobank for research. The linkage between pathology data and the corresponding tissue samples is particularly important for studies on rare diseases.

To support researchers in identifying, locating, and accessing tissue samples, the Dutch National Tissue Portal (DNTP) was initiated by the Biobanking and Biomolecular Research Infrastructure—the Netherlands (BBMRI.nl). The project was developed in close collaboration with Palga, the Dutch Society for Pathology, and the University Medical Centers in the Netherlands.14

Since 2015, Palga has supported and facilitated the centralized DNTP portal (https://dntp.nl/), which is managed by DNTP. Whereas Palga provides the infrastructure and enables access, DNTP holds full responsibility for the centralized process of requesting, identifying, and distributing archived human tissue material for scientific research in the Netherlands.

Acting as the intermediary between researchers and each Dutch pathology department, DNTP streamlines access to relevant biomaterials by coordinating submissions through a single online portal. Palga data stewards further support this process by using information from the PScDb to identify and locate the materials in the respective pathology departments and submitting the requests to DNTP on behalf of researchers. Pathology departments can also receive logistical support from a dedicated UMC-based hub staff member provided to DNTP, who can manage the entire subsequent logistics process. Importantly, the DNTP portal infrastructure also functions as a track-and-trace system, enabling pathology departments to efficiently manage the distribution of material. Through this collaboration, DNTP and Palga provide a standardized, efficient interface between biomedical research and pathology services.

A request for relevant pathology material (e.g., FFPE blocks, (digitized) slides, or other samples) is often data-driven, following a data request from the PScDb. The researcher selects the relevant pathology excerpts from the provided dataset, and Palga data stewards forward the request to DNTP, referencing the corresponding study specific identification numbers. Once the PalgaIDs have been sent to the pathology departments, they may decide whether to cooperate and provide the requested materials to researchers along with the corresponding PalgaIDs, enabling linkage between the Palga data and the materials.

This process, facilitated by the collaboration between Palga, DNTP, and pathology departments, has enabled researchers to access highly specific pathology material for detailed analyses.

For example, De Jong et al. revised earlier pathology samples from young, chemotherapy-naïve patients with N0 triple-negative breast cancer, demonstrating that a high level of stromal tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes was associated with excellent long-term prognosis.30 In another study, Kemps et al.31 combined PScDb data linkage with the National Cancer Registry and the retrieval of rare Juvenile Xanthogranuloma specimens via the DNTP portal.

A further example is the study by De Back et al., who developed a FFPE-curated Consensus Molecular Subtype classifier from highly degraded RNA that has been extracted from FFPE-blocks of colorectal cancer tissue obtained via DNTP. This classifier accurately categorized tumors and predicted anti-EGFR therapy response.32

Finally, tissue materials obtained through DNTP from patients with HER2-positive metastatic breast cancer by Steenbruggen et al.33 were analyzed to characterize the tumor immune microenvironment. Differences in immune cell infiltration between primary tumors and metastases provided factors influencing overall survival and potential alternative treatment strategies. This integrated approach, supported by Palga's data stewardship and DNTP's centralized material management, provided valuable insights for improving diagnostics and treatment strategies.

Discussion

In the current timeframe, availability, accessibility, reliability, and quality of health data are increasingly important for innovative, evidence-based research, and for supporting a sustainable, future-oriented healthcare system. At the same time, citizens demand greater control over their personal data, whereas legislative frameworks emphasize stronger data protection and privacy safeguards. Palga, with its unique nationwide database for secondary use, operates precisely at this intersection, aiming to achieve an optimal balance between high-quality health research and robust privacy protection. Given this complex balance, both the opportunities offered by Palga and its PScDb, as well as the challenges need to be addressed. The present overview demonstrates that Palga has evolved into a nationwide infrastructure supporting primary patient care and enabling secondary use of pathology data through standardized coding, secure governance frameworks, and long-term longitudinal data collection. However, continued development is required to meet increasing demands for interoperability, artificial intelligence (AI) readiness, and privacy-preserving data reuse. To place these developments in an international context, it is informative to compare Palga with comparable pathology and cancer data infrastructures. Several countries have established such infrastructures, including the NHS Digital Pathology Programme in the United Kingdom, regional pathology infrastructures in Europe, and TCGA and CPTAC in the United States. The NHS Digital Pathology program primarily focuses on the large-scale digitization of histopathology workflows through whole-slide imaging, enabling remote reporting and the development of AI applications based on imaging data, whereas initiatives such as TCGA and CPTAC have concentrated on integrated multi-omics cancer datasets. A key distinguishing feature of Palga is that it was developed primarily as a nationwide clinical infrastructure with nationwide coverage of routine diagnostic pathology reports collected longitudinally across all pathology departments. This positions Palga as one of the few nationwide infrastructures providing longitudinal, text-based, real-world (structured) pathology data, whereas the other initiatives are more advanced in the large-scale integration of digital pathology imaging and AI-driven image analysis.

Opportunities and challenges

With Palga, the Netherlands is among the few countries with nationwide coverage of pathology data, providing an unparalleled resource for secondary data use. Combined with structured coding using PODS and the increasing adoption of SSR protocols, this enables high-quality data, interoperability, and reliable reuse of pathology data for research and healthcare purposes. Mapping with SNOMED CT further strengthens semantic interoperability by allowing systems to “speak the same language.” Despite challenges related to protecting patient privacy while facilitating secondary use, Palga ensures secure access by fully complying with the GDPR and proactively preparing for the EHDS requirements. These measures maintain the availability of rich pathology data for a wide range of research purposes, which is essential for patient care and a self-learning health system. The substantial scientific output, with over 750 publications facilitated by PScDb data to date, reflects the database's broad utility. Additionally, Palga's intermediary role provides access to unique archived tissue material via DNTP, supporting high-quality research.

Whereas these opportunities demonstrate Palga's strengths, several challenges remain. Although SSR protocols are used in over one-third of pathology reports and adoption is gradually increasing, the persistent use of narrative reporting and inconsistent adoption continue to affect data uniformity and quality.6 This lack of uniformity and quality not only hampers the efficiency and accuracy of data retrieval by data stewards but is further intensified by the expanding use of molecular diagnostics, which increases the pressure to maintain consistent, high-quality pathology reporting. The main obstacles slowing the further implementation of SSR protocols are clinicians' resistance to changing established reporting routines, incompatibilities with existing workflows, and the perception of increased workload.9 Although SSR protocols are available nationwide within the Palga infrastructure, their adoption in routine pathology practice depends on integration into local reporting workflows, individual pathologist preference and practice, and locoregional agreements. Previous studies have shown that successful implementation of SSR requires attention to workflow integration, stakeholder engagement, and reduction of perceived reporting burden.10 Ongoing initiatives by the pathology community aim to further increase SSR adoption and standardization.

Balancing patient privacy with research needs remains a dynamic challenge. In addition, the availability of tissue material for research is constrained by logistical, legal, and financial limitations faced by pathology departments that participate voluntarily in tissue provision. Addressing these challenges is essential to fully realize the potential of the PScDb for secondary data use and high-quality research.

Future directions

Digital microscopy, advances in molecular diagnostics, and the emerging role of AI will shape Palga's strategic priorities. Integration of histopathological and molecular data via the PPM enables a more comprehensive understanding of patient disease, improving diagnostic accuracy and supporting personalized treatment.

Palga currently leads in standardization, but further efforts are needed to strengthen interoperability and reliability of pathology data. Palga is structurally funded by the Dutch Ministry of Health, Welfare and Sport and most of Palga's resources are currently dedicated to maintaining the existing database, with limited investment in research and development of new functionalities. This allocation may constrain innovation and long-term sustainability. Whereas the use of the PPM largely depends on voluntary participation, broader adoption by the professional community will require further integration of the Palga databank and its infrastructure with laboratory management systems and reporting tools, as well as the inclusion of data from all types of pathology investigations, including histopathology, cytology, molecular diagnostics, immunohistochemistry, and digital slide images. This would enable the development of more complete and reliable datasets, supporting accurate analyses for healthcare evaluation and representative AI training, and facilitating wider multidisciplinary professional use.

AI offers interesting opportunities to further improve the value of pathology data for research. At present, AI is not used internally within Palga, but the Palga database represents a rich source for development of AI tools, particularly for research involving large language models (LLMs) applied to textual pathology data. LLMs may support several aspects of pathology workflows, including automated structuring of newly generated pathology reports and conversion of unstructured narrative reports into structured synoptic reports. The extraction of clinically relevant variables, often embedded in lengthy, heterogeneous texts, such as ER/PR status, Bethesda classification, Gleason score, and Breslow thickness may be facilitated. LLMs may also support standardized coding, reducing pathologists' workload. Finally, LLMs can assist in anonymization of free-text pathology reports by identifying and redacting sensitive information, such as names, dates of birth, or institutional identifiers. Together, these applications could substantially improve data quality, interoperability, safe sharing of datasets for research and AI development, while preserving the clinical relevance of the text. Recording which report elements were generated or supported by AI could further facilitate quality assurance, validation, auditing, and post-market surveillance of AI-assisted pathology workflows.

Currently, research projects initiated by academic centers and supported by Palga are exploring the development of a large-scale research database intended to support the development, training, and validation of LLM-based applications in pathology. Such a resource could provide curated, pseudonymized pathology text data suitable for model training, fine-tuning, and benchmarking. Challenges for LLM development on Dutch pathology text relate less to data volume than to the highly specialized terminology, extensive use of abbreviations, and heterogeneity of reporting practices across institutions and time periods.

Internationally, comparable initiatives demonstrate the feasibility and value of large-scale, well-curated biomedical datasets for AI development, including resources such as UK Biobank, Big Picture, and CPTAC. These initiatives illustrate the importance of data curation, harmonization, and well-defined metadata to support reproducible model development and clinical translation. Similar principles will be essential for a future Palga research dataset, which could provide a unique source of longitudinal, nationwide real-world pathology text data for LLM development and validation.

Although Palga provides nationwide access to pathology reports, whole-slide images are not currently part of the nationwide infrastructure. Palga was originally designed and implemented for the nationwide exchange and long-term storage of structured and narrative pathology reports rather than digital image data. Integrating whole-slide images presents substantial technical, legal, governance, and financial challenges, including the large storage requirements associated with whole-slide imaging, heterogeneity of digital pathology systems across institutions, and differences in local governance and data-sharing policies.

Recognizing the growing importance of digital pathology and AI-based image analysis, the Dutch pathology community has initiated projects to explore a nationwide image-sharing infrastructure for primary patient care. Such initiatives may provide the foundation for future secondary use of pathology images for research and AI development. However, these initiatives are currently in an exploratory and feasibility assessment phase, and no formal nationwide roadmap or implementation timeline has yet been established. The optimal technical architecture, governance model, and long-term storage strategy remain under active investigation.

In the future, combining structured pathology data from Palga with nationwide image-sharing infrastructures could substantially enhance opportunities for data-driven research and the development of AI-based diagnostic tools.

Overall, Palga provides a strong foundation for the secondary use of pathology data through its nationwide coverage, longitudinal data collection, and standardized reporting. Future integration of molecular data, digital pathology, and AI-based approaches will further increase the value of Palga for research and clinical innovation.

Conclusions

An ever-expanding nationwide pathology data infrastructure, such as Palga, is increasingly used for secondary purposes to advance evidence-based healthcare, research, and policy at both the individual and population level. The implementation of the EHDS is expected to further accelerate the responsible reuse of health data for the benefit of patients and society. Thanks to its nationwide coverage and robust infrastructure, Palga plays an indispensable role in patient care, health research, and healthcare quality improvement, while responsible and socially valuable secondary use of its pathology data relies on collaboration among healthcare providers, researchers, and policymakers, supported by strong legal and ethical governance. Building on Palga's achievements, the Netherlands is well-prepared to face future challenges in pathology data, allowing innovation and new opportunities while maintaining high data quality and patient safety.
