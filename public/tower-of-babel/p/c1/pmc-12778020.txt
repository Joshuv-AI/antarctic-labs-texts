A 15-Year-Old Girl with an Unusual Cause of Intermittent Gross Hematuria
Authors: Natasha Freeman, Namrata G. Jain, Satoru Kudose
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Visual Abstract

Case Presentation

A 15-year-old girl with a history of intermittent gross hematuria (three episodes over the course of 8 months) associated with viral and bacterial infection presented with diarrhea, abdominal pain, and intermittent arthralgia in her hands. She was found to have a Shiga-toxin Escherichia coli infection complicated by AKI and gross hematuria. Serum creatinine was 1.31 mg/dl with normal electrolytes, urine protein 0.2 g/g creatinine, hemoglobin of 10.0 g/L, platelet count 215×103/μl, haptoglobin 125 mg/dl, and lactate dehydrogenase 245 U/L. Urinalysis showed 3+ protein, large blood, 11–20 white blood cells and >50 red blood cells (RBCs) per high powered field with dysmorphic RBCs and a RBC cast. Serological workup revealed positive anti-nuclear antibody (titer 1:80), negative/normal complement levels C3 and C4, anti–double-stranded DNA antibody, anti-neutrophilic cytoplasmic antibodies, anti-streptolysin O antibody, and hepatitis B and C. Genetic testing (Natera Renasight) did not reveal pathogenic mutations.

Kidney biopsy (Figure 1) revealed diffuse endocapillary hypercellularity by monocytes (Figure 1), with negative conventional immunofluorescence and immunofluorescence on pronase-digested paraffin-embedded tissue. Ultrastructural evaluation revealed rare mesangial and subendothelial deposits (Figure 2). Although the lack of immunoreactivity makes definitive classification difficult, (1) endocapillary hypercellularity by monocytes and (2) discordance between the degree of hypercellularity and amount of deposits were suspicious for cryoglobulinemic GN (cryoGN). Such discordance likely reflects aggressive clearance by monocytes and is atypical for more common glomerulonephritides in children, such as IgA nephropathy.

Kidney biopsy revealed glomeruli with diffuse segmental to global endocapillary hypercellullarity by mononuclear leukocytes and a few neutrophils. The glomerular basement membranes showed rare segmental duplication. No significant mesangial hypercellularity, fibrin or immune thrombi, or crescents were seen. Mild tubulointerstitial scarring, minimal arteriosclerosis and arteriolosclerosis were present. Both conventional IF and IF performed on pronase-digested paraffin-embedded tissue was negative for all reactants (not shown). Original magnification 600×, periodic acid–Schiff stain. IF, immunofluorescence.

Ultrastructural examination demonstrated sparse minute subendothelial and mesangial electron dense deposits (arrows) without organized substructure (inset). The absence of substructure (seen in approximately 80% of cases) is likely due to sampling error in this case because of the rarity of deposits. Endothelial cell fenestrations were attenuated and rare endothelial tubuloreticular inclusions were present. Original magnification 12,000×.

Subsequently, serum cryoglobulin testing revealed a cryocrit of 1%, classified as type 3 cryoglobulinemia by immunofixation. Rheumatologic and infectious workups were negative. The patient was diagnosed with idiopathic type 3 cryoglobulinemia and was treated with steroids and rituximab (weekly ×4 at 375 mg/m2, then maintenance every 6 months), a regimen which has proven effective for mixed cryoGN in the adult literature.1 Sixteen months following biopsy, she remains with preserved kidney function.

Discussion

Cryoglobulins are immunoglobulins (Ig) that precipitate at <37°C and then redissolve at >37°C. Type 1 cryoglobulin is composed of monoclonal Ig produced by hematolymphoid neoplasm, whereas types 2 and 3 are composed of mixture of Igs, most commonly related to chronic infections and autoimmune diseases; type 2 can also be caused by monoclonal Ig, with half categorized as monoclonal gammopathy of renal significance.1 Mixed cryoglobulinemia classically manifests with purpuric rash, weakness, and arthralgia, but can also include skin ulcers, peripheral neuropathy, and GN, typically associated with low C4 and positive rheumatoid factor, sometimes associated with chronic infection, autoimmune disease, or hematologic malignancy.1,2 Diagnosis is made by positive serum cryocrit and immunofixation testing in the presence of end-organ damage, often detected by biopsy, although multiple serum tests may be necessary to exclude false-negative results.

Cryoglobulinemia is rare in children. Etiology is most commonly unknown, with a minority associated with infection. Renal involvement is rare, with less than 15 cases reported. Among the few with biopsy, one showed diffuse endocapillary hypercellularity and sparse immune deposits, as in our case.3
