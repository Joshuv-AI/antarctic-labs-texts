Development of a CRE/CREB-driven HBx responsive HBV cell culture reporter system for antiviral drug evaluation
Authors: Muhammad Atif Zahoor, Nahla FadlElMawla, Adrian Kuipery, Joshua B. Feld, Avisha Chowdhury, Alexander I. Mosa, Adam J. Gehring, Jordan J. Feld
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background & AimsChronic HBV infection is a leading cause of liver disease and cancer. Current therapies fail to eliminate covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA), underscoring the need for novel strategies. We aimed to develop a quantitative cell-based reporter system that detects HBx expression during HBV infection and is suitable for screening compounds with anti-HBV activity.MethodsWe generated an HBx-responsive cell line stably expressing nano-luciferase (nLuc) under the control of a viral cAMP-response element (vCRE) derived from the human T-cell leukemia virus-1 core promoter. The system was used to evaluate various drug inhibitors in HBV-infected cells.ResultsThe vCRE-nLuc system was confirmed to be responsive to HBx using pHBV1.3-wild-type, pHBV1.3-null-X and pMyc-HBx constructs (p <0.0001). HBV infection with both culture-derived and patient-derived clinical isolates (genotypes A-E) significantly increased luciferase activity (p <0.0001). Treatment with HBx inhibitors (siRNA, specific HBx inhibitors), as well as IFNα reduced luciferase production (p <0.0001). In contrast, antivirals that do not interfere with protein production (tenofovir), or viral entry (bulevirtide) showed no effect (p <0.05), underscoring the specificity of the system for identifying compounds that inhibit protein production and/or HBx function. Finally, HepG2.2.15 cells carrying HBV and transduced with vCRE-nLuc confirmed the system’s suitability for monitoring HBV infection.ConclusionsWe established a vCRE-nLuc-driven HBx-responsive cell line for quantitative monitoring of HBV infection and evaluation of antiviral drugs. This system holds potential for identifying new anti-HBV agents and advancing our understanding of HBV replication.Impact and implicationsThe HBx-responsive vCRE-nLuc reporter system provides a sensitive and scalable platform to monitor HBV infection and evaluate antiviral compounds targeting HBx function. Its ability to detect HBx activity from both laboratory and clinical HBV isolates underscore its translational relevance. By enabling selective screening of HBx-targeting agents, this system may advance efforts to silence covalently closed circular DNA and accelerate the development of curative therapies for chronic hepatitis B.

Introduction

Despite the availability of an effective vaccine, HBV remains a major health problem worldwide. Approximately 254 million people are currently living with chronic HBV infection and are at a greater risk of developing progressive liver injury with the potential to cause cirrhosis, and its complications, including hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).1,2 Currently, nuclecos(t)ide analogues and to a lesser degree PEGylated interferon-alpha (peg-IFNα) are used to treat chronic HBV. While long-term therapy can improve clinical outcomes, these treatments have limited efficacy against covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA) and rarely achieve a functional cure, defined as the loss of hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg).3,4 Consequentially, there is a pressing need for effective HBV model systems to aid in the discovery of potential therapeutic agents.4

HBV, a member of the Hepadnaviridae family, is an enveloped virus with a partially double-stranded DNA genome that replicates via reverse transcription of an RNA intermediate.5 Upon entry into hepatocytes via the Na+-taurocholate cotransporting polypeptide (NTCP) receptor, the partially double-stranded DNA genome is transported to the nucleus where it forms cccDNA, which serves as a template for transcription of five major viral RNA transcripts: precore (preC), pregenomic (pgRNA), large surface (preS1), middle surface (preS2), small surface (S), and HBx RNA.5 HBx, the shortest of these transcripts, encodes a 154-amino-acid protein that is important for viral replication and cccDNA maintenance.6 HBx contributes to hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) development by interacting with a multitude of host cellular proteins.7 The structural maintenance of chromosomes 5/6 complex (SMC5/6) is an essential regulator of host genome stability and a restriction factor for HBV cccDNA, limiting viral replication.8 HBx counters this restriction by interacting with the SMC5/6 complex and recruiting the DNA damage binding protein 1-Cullin 4 (DDB1-CUL4) E3 ubiquitin ligase, leading to its ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. This degradation relieves the suppression of HBV transcription, thereby enhancing viral replication.[8], [9], [10] Additionally, HBx acts as a transcriptional co-activator by binding to the basic domain-leucine zipper (bZIP) family, including cyclic AMP-response element (CRE)-binding protein (CREB) and CREB-binding protein (CBP/p300).11

The CREB and activating transcription factor (ATF) family of proteins play an important role in regulating gene expression, influencing cellular processes such as gluconeogenesis, lipid metabolism, and cell proliferation.12 Activation of the CREB/bZIP family of proteins involves phosphorylation of a key serine residue (serine 133 in CREB) induced by cAMP which allows CREB to recruit CBP/p300 to the CRE in target gene promoters, thereby activating transcription.12 The human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 (HTLV-1) promoter, located within the 5′ long terminal repeat, contains three conserved 21-base pair repeat elements, known as viral cAMP response elements (vCREs). Each vCRE is flanked by 5′ G-rich and 3′ C-rich sequences which serve as binding sites for the transcription factors CREB and ATF-1.13,14 The viral oncoprotein Tax, which is essential for HTLV-1 replication and pathogenesis, forms a stable ternary complex with phosphorylated CREB (Ser133), the coactivators CBP/p300, and the vCRE via minor groove interactions at the G/C-rich flanks. This quaternary complex is critical for the robust transcriptional activation of the viral promoter.[14], [15], [16]

Given that HTLV-1 Tax and HBV HBx are oncogenic regulatory transactivator proteins which share functional similarities,17 we hypothesized that HBx could activate vCRE-dependent and downstream gene transcription by utilizing the vCRE element within HTLV-1’s core promoter. In this study, we developed a reporter cell line stably expressing a nLuc gene driven by the vCRE promoter, which can be transactivated by HBx. We established a rapid biochemical assay that enables monitoring of HBV replication and evaluation of antiviral drug efficacy by quantifying luciferase activity. This novel quantitative HBV reporter assay offers a valuable tool for assessing viral replication and screening compounds that inhibit HBV protein expression and/or specifically target HBx activity.

Materials and methods

Cells

HepG-NTCP-A3/C2 cells18 were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagles Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Carlsbad, USA) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, Carlsbad, USA), L-glutamine, 100U penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco Fisher Scientific, Canada), 1 mM Sodium Pyruvate, 20 mM HEPES, essential and non-essential amino acids (Corning, Manasas, VA, USA), 2.5 μg/ml puromycin, and 5 μg plasmosin (Invivogen, Cedarlane, Canada). Huh7, HEK293T, HepAD38 and HepG2.2.15 cell lines were maintained in DMEM (Gibco, Carlsbad, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, Carlsbad, USA), 2 mmol/L L-glutamine, 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 μg/ml streptomycin (Gibco, Carlsbad, USA). Cell viability was determined using the Trypan blue exclusion method.

Plasmid construction

The plasmids pLVX-Puro, pHBV1.3-wt (wild type), pHBV1.3-null-X, pLV6.2-nano-Luciferase-ccd, pCMV4-Tax, psPAX2 and pCMV-VSV-G were obtained from Addgene (Addgene, Watertown, USA). The pHBV1.3-null-X carries two stop codons immediately in the first and second AUG that disrupted the HBx coding sequence essentially as described.19,20 The pcDNA3.1-cMyc-HBx (pMyc-HBx) plasmid was provided by Christine Neuveut.11,21 The pSHH2.1 plasmid was a kind gift from Stephen Urban.22 The pMA-RQ plasmid consisting of the minimal HTLV-1 core promoter termed as vCRE was synthetically designed and obtained from GeneArt (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA). The cytomegalovirus (CMV) immediate early enhancer/promoter was excised from the pLVX-Puro lentiviral vector using ClaI and BamHI restriction endonucleases and replaced with synthetic vCRE synthetic promoter, designated as pLV-vCRE-Puro vector. To construct the nLuc reporter construct, a DNA fragment containing the nLuc gene was PCR-amplified from the pLV6.2-NanoLuciferase-ccdB plasmid, using BamHI and SphI restriction sites with the following primers: 5’-GAGGATCCGCCACCATGGTCTTCACACTCGAAGATTTCG-3’ (forward) and 5’-CGGGCATGCTCCAGACTGCCTTGGGAAAAGCGCCTCCCCTACCCTTACGCCAGAATGCGTTCGCACAGCCG-3’ (reverse) and cloned into pLV-vCRE-Puro vector. The PGK promoter, which was disrupted during the cloning process, was restored through primer-mediated introduction of the PGK promoter sequence as indicated by the underline in the reverse primer sequence. The final reporter plasmid was designated as pLV-vCRE-nLuc and was verified by DNA sequencing. The pLV-CMV-nLuc-Puro vector, containing an intact CMV promoter, was generated by cloning the nLuc gene at the BamHI and SphI restriction sites and served as negative control.

Transfection, lentivirus generation and establishment of stable cell lines

HEK293T cells were transfected using lipofectamine 2000 as previously described.23 Briefly, 5x105 cells were plated in 6-well plate one day before transfection. The following day, the cells were transfected or co-transfected with 0.5 μg of pLV-vCRE-nLuc with or without 1 μg of pHBV1.3-wt or 1 μg of pHBV1.3-null-X or 1 μg of pMyc-HBx. The total amount of transfected DNA was kept constant by adding an appropriate amount of empty vector. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, the cells were washed once with 1X Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS), lysed, and subjected to luciferase assay. To generate lentiviral particles, 5x105 HEK293T cells were plated in a 6-well plate one day before transfection. The following day, the cells were transfected with 1 μg of the packaging vector psPAX2, 0.5 μg of pCMV-VSV-G, and 1.5 μg of pLV-vCRE-nLuc vector using lipofectamine 2000.23 Forty-eight hours post-transfection, the culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 2,250 rpm for 10 min to remove debris. The virus-containing supernatant (LV-vCRE-nLuc) was filtered through a 0.22-μm filter and either used directly to transduce target cells or stored at −80 °C for future use. HepG2.2.15 cells and HepG2-NTCP-A3/C2 were transduced with the LV-vCRE-nLuc. To obtain single-cell clones, HepG2-NTCP-A3/C2 cells transduced with LV-vCRE-nLuc, were trypsinized 2 days after transduction and seeded into 96-well plates at approximately 100 cells per plate. Individual colonies were picked after 10 days, expanded and a clone 1D6 was selected and designated as HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nLuc. HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nLuc stable cells were transiently transfected with lipofectamine 3000.23 Briefly, 5x105 HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nLuc cells were plated one day before transfection into 12-well plates. The following day, the cells were transfected with 1 μg of pHBV1.3-wt or 1 μg of pHBV1.3-null-X or 1 μg of pMyc-HBx. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, the cells were washed once with 1X PBS, lysed, and used for luciferase assay.

Nano-glo luciferase assay

Luciferase activity in cell lysates was measured using the Nano-Glo Luciferase Assay kit (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA), following the manufacturer's instructions. The data were normalized to the protein content, as described.23,24

HBV infection and quantification of HBV genome, cccDNA and RNA transcripts

HBV derived from HepAD38 (genotype D) cell culture supernatant was concentrated to remove debris and filtered through a 0.45-μm filter. Aliquots were stored at −80 °C. The HBV DNA genome copy number was quantified by qPCR and presented as genome equivalent per ml (GEq/ml). HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nLuc cells were seeded at 5x105 cells per mL in 24-well plate and infected with HepAD38-harvested HBV at multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100, 200, 500 or 1,000 GEq/ml in the presence of 2.5% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Millipore Sigma, Oakville, ON, Canada) and 4% polyethylene glycol (PEG 8000; Millipore Sigma, Oakville, ON, Canada) as described.18,25 The following day, the virus inoculum was removed, the cells were extensively washed with 1X PBS and cultured in the presence of 2.5% DMSO. Four days post-infection, extracellular relaxed circular viral DNA (rcDNA) was extracted from supernatants using the DNeasy blood & tissue kit (QIAgen, Canada) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and quantified using HBV 1844F 5’-GTTGCCCGTTTGTCCTCTAATTC-3’ as a forward and HBV 1745R 5’-GGAGGGATACATAGAGGTTCCTTGA-3’ as a reverse primer, as described.18 Total RNA was extracted from HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nLuc cells infected with HBV at various MOIs using the RNeasy Mini kit (QIAgen, Toronto, ON, Canada) according to manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration was quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, and 1 μg of total RNA was used for cDNA synthesis with VILO cDNA synthesis kit.18 HBV pgRNA, preS1, preS2 and HBx RNA transcripts were quantified by qPCR using QuantStudio 6 Flex system (Thermo Scientific, Whitby, ON, Canada). HBx RNA transcripts were determined after subtracting pgRNA, preS1 and preS2 as described.26 The cccDNA was quantified by TaqMan-based real-time PCR on a QuantStudio 6 Flex System (Thermo Scientific, Canada) using the following primers and probe: forward primer p1040 (5′-GTGGTTATCCTGCGTTGAT-3′), reverse primer p1996 (5′-GAGCTGAGGCGGTATCT-3′), and probe p1085 (5′-FAM-AGTTGGCGAGAAAGTGAAAGCCTGC-TAMRA-3′). Thermal cycling conditions were 95 °C for 15 min, followed by 50 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 63 °C for 70 s. Absolute cccDNA quantification was performed using serial dilutions of the pSHH2.1 plasmid as standards. To normalize for input cellular DNA, human β-globin was quantified by SYBR Green-based qPCR using the following primers: forward (5′-CAGGTACGGCTGTCATCACTTAGA-3′) and reverse (5′-CATGGTGTCTGTTTGAGGTTGCTA-3′), as previously described.18,22

Recombinant virus production and infection

Recombinant HBV particles were collected from the supernatant of pHBV1.3-wt or pHBV1.3-null-X plasmids transfected Huh7 cells essentially as described.27 Briefly, 1x106 cells were plated in a 6-well plate and the following day were transfected with 3 μg of pHBV1.3-wt or pHBV1.3-null-X molecular clones using Lipofectamine 2000. Four days after transfection, the recombinant virus (rHBV1.3-wt or rHBV1.3-Null-X) in the supernatant was concentrated with Intact Virus Precipitation Reagent (Thermo Scientific, Whitby, ON, Canada) according to manufacturer’s instructions. HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nLuc cells were infected with rHBV1.3-wt or rHBV1.3-Null-X for 6 days, followed by analysis of supernatant-derived rcDNA, cccDNA, pgRNA, luciferase activity, and HBV core antigen (HBcAg) expression as previously described.18 For HBx trans-complementation, HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nLuc cells were infected with rHBV1.3-ΔX for 4 days, as described above, followed by transient transfection with pHBx using Lipofectamine 3000. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, supernatant rcDNA, intracellular cccDNA, pgRNA levels, luciferase activity, and HBcAg expression were analyzed.

Immunofluorescence assay for HBcAg

HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nLuc cells infected with cell culture-derived HBV at various MOIs or with rHBV1.3-wt or rHBV1.3-Null-X (with or without pHBx transfection) viruses were fixed in 4% PFA at room temperature, washed with 1X PBS and permeabilized with 0.5 % Triton X-100. After blocking, cells were incubated with rabbit polyclonal HBV anti-Core antibody C149 as described28 followed by incubation with donkey anti-rabbit Alexa fluor-647 (Thermo Scientific, Canada). Nuclei were stained with DAPI, and the cells were imaged using EVOS FL Auto 2 imaging system (Invitrogen, Canada) as described18,25

Compounds and drug inhibition assays

The 50% inhibitory concentration (IC50) for nitazoxanide (Millipore Sigma, Oakville, ON, Canada), dicoumarol (Thermo Scientific, Whitby, ON, Canada), and pCREB-Ser133 inhibitor 666-15 (Millipore Sigma, Oakville, ON, Canada) was determined as described.[29], [30], [31], [32] Briefly, HBV-infected HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nLuc cells were either left untreated as controls or treated with nitazoxanide at 0.5, 1, 5, 10, 50, and 100 μM; dicoumarol at 50, 100, 200, 300, 500, and 1,000 μM; or the pCREB-Ser133 inhibitor 666-15 at 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 5, and 10 μM. Forty-eight hours after treatment, cell viability and luciferase activity were measured. For HBx inhibition, HBV-infected HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nLuc cells were treated with dicoumarol at 100, 200 and 300 μM or with nitazoxanide at 1, 5, and 10 μM as described.29,30 For inhibition of CREB activity, HBV-infected HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nLuc cells were treated with pCREB-Ser133 inhibitor 666–15 at 0.25, 0.5 and 1 μM for 48 h.31 DMSO was used as a solvent to dissolve the drugs and served as a control. For HBV entry inhibition, infection was performed in the presence or absence of bulevirtide (MyrB) at 1 μM as previously described.18 HBV-infected cells were treated with 100 or 1,000 U of recombinant human IFNα2 (rhIFNα2; GoldBio, St Louis, MO, USA) or 50 or 100 μM of tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF; Themo Scientific, Whitby, ON, Canada). The media was replenished on alternate days. The cells were lysed after 7 days, and the luciferase activity was measured. Extracellular HBV rcDNA in the culture supernatants was quantified as described above. HEK293T cells (2.5 × 105) were plated in a 12-well plate one day before transfection. The following day, the cells were transfected or co-transfected with pCMV-nLuc or pLV-vCRE-nLuc and pCMV4-Tax-wt using Lipofectamine 2000 as described above. Transfections were performed in the presence or absence of nitazoxanide (10 μM), dicoumarol (300 μM), or pCREB-Ser133 inhibitor 666-15 (1 μM) for 48 h. Cell viability and luciferase activity were measured, and western blot analyses were performed. HepG2.2.15 cells transduced with LV-vCRE-nLuc were treated with DMSO or nitazoxanide or dicoumarol or pCREB-Ser133 inhibitor 666-15 at different concentrations as described above and subjected to luciferase assays.

Short-interfering RNAs

HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nLuc cells were infected with HBV for 4 days as described above and transfected with 50 nM of HBV-specific siRNA or non-targeting scramble control using DharmaFECT 1 according to manufacturer’s instructions (GE Healthcare Dharmacon, Lafayette, CO, USA). The HBV-specific siRNA (5′-ACCUCUGCCUAAUCAUCUC-3′) targets nucleotides 1825–1843 (Fig. 3A), a region shared by all five HBV RNA transcripts: the 3.5 kb precore, 3.5 kb pgRNA, 2.4 kb PreS1, 2.1 kb PreS2/S, and 0.7 kb HBx as described.25,33 After 24 h of transfection, cells were washed, trypsinized, re-seeded, and cultured for up to 8 days after transfection. The media was changed on alternate days. Extracellular HBV rcDNA in the supernatant at day 2, 4, 6, and 8 was quantified by qPCR and the cells were lysed for luciferase activity. At day 4 post-siRNA transfection, HBV-infected HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nLuc cells were further transfected with either an empty vector or a plasmid encoding the Tax protein. After 48 h, cells were harvested and subjected to western blot analysis, pgRNA quantification or luciferase assay.

HBV-infected serum samples

In compliance with institutional guidelines and approval from the Research Ethics Board (ID #: 18-5748) University Health Network (UHN), and with written informed consent, serum samples were collected from chronically infected HBV patients (genotypes A-E) at the Toronto Centre for Liver Disease as described.18,34 Serum samples were filtered through 0.22-μm syringe filter and buffer exchanged using Amicon Ultra 0.5 μM filter units (Millipore Sigma, Oakville, ON, Canada) by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 30 min18 and used to infect HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nL at 500 GEq/ml for 24 h. The next day, the virus inoculum was removed, and the infected cells were maintained for 10 days in the presence of 2.5% DMSO. The cell lysates were prepared, and luciferase activities were measured.

Western blotting and antibodies

HEK293T and HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nLuc cells were lysed with cell culture lysis 5X buffer (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA) and the protein concentrations were determined by bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay (Thermo Scientific, Whitby, ON, Canada) as described.23 Cell lysates were resolved by 10-12% SDS-PAGE and transferred to methanol soaked immune-blot PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad, Toronto, ON, Canada). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for one hour at room temperature, washed, and incubated with the following primary antibodies: mouse monoclonal anti-CREB (35-0900), rabbit monoclonal anti-Phospho-CREB pSer133 (MA511192), mouse monoclonal anti-HBcAg (MA17607), mouse monoclonal anti-β-actin (MA1744) from Thermo Scientific (Thermo Scientific, Whitby, ON, Canada), rabbit polyclonal anti-Smc6 (AB155495), and rabbit monoclonal anti-HBx (AB309352) from Abcam (Waltham, MA, USA). Secondary antibodies, HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit or goat anti-mouse (Thermo Fisher Scientific), were used, and protein bands were visualized using chemiluminescence (Thermo Scientific, Whitby, ON, Canada), as described.23

Statistical analysis

Student’s t test, one-way or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparison test or regression analysis were carried out using GraphPad Prism (San Diego, CA, USA) as described.18,35 Data are shown as mean ± SD. Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of HBcAg or DAPI and the intensity of protein bands were quantified using ImageJ, as previously described.23

Results

Responsiveness of vCRE promoter to HBx in HEK293T and HepG2-NTCP cells

The HTLV-1 Tax protein is well known to bind CREB family transcription factors, leading to vCRE-mediated transactivation.13,23,36 Building on this mechanism, we developed an HBx-responsive, cell-based reporter system to monitor HBV infection via a quantifiable nLuc assay driven by vCRE and CREB. We engineered a synthetic vCRE sequence derived from the HTLV-1 long terminal repeat, containing nine tandem repeats of the 21-bp enhancer element (5’-CAGGCGTTGACGACAACCCCT-3’), each flanked by G/C-rich regions and the HTLV-1 TATA box. This vCRE was cloned upstream of the nLuc gene in a lentiviral vector (pLV-vCRE-nLuc). Upon delivery into mammalian cells, the vCRE acts as a cis-regulatory element to drive nLuc expression. We hypothesized that this construct would serve as a reporter of HBx-mediated transcriptional activation, enabling luminescence-based detection of HBx activity (Fig. 1A). To evaluate the responsiveness of vCRE-nLuc cassette to HBx, we transfected HEK293T cells with pLV-vCRE-nLuc alone or co-transfected them with pMyc-HBx, pHBV1.3-wt or pHBV1.3-null-X plasmids. The results demonstrated that HBx and pHBV1.3-wt plasmids induced the expression of nLuc by more than 5- and 4.5-fold, respectively, which were significantly different from the control (p <0.0001). In contrast, pHBV1.3-null-X, which lacks an intact HBx, did not enhance vCRE-mediated luciferase expression (Fig. 1B). Western blot analyses confirmed increased CREB phosphorylation at Ser133 in the presence of Myc-HBx or pHBV-1.3-wt, but not with pHBV1.3-null-X (Fig. 1C), confirming HBx-driven transactivation of vCRE-mediated luciferase activity. Notably, a size shift in anti-HBx western blot was observed due to the Myc tag on HBx. Next, we generated a HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nLuc stable cell line through lentiviral-mediated transduction, followed by single-cell cloning through limiting dilution (Fig. 1D). A single-cell clone, 1D6 (HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nLuc) was selected for transient transfections. The results revealed that transfection with pMyc-HBx led to a 5.18-fold increase whereas pHBV1.3-wt induced a 4.38-fold increase in luciferase activity (Fig. 1E; p <0.0001). Additionally, CREB phosphorylation at Ser133 was also elevated in response to pMyc-HBx and pHBV1.3-wt (Fig. 1F). Both pMyc-HBx as well as pHBV1.3-wt-mediated luciferase activities were significantly different from the control and from each other (p <0.0001). Increasing multiplicity of HBV infection correlates with enhanced luciferase activity in HBV-infected HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nLuc cells, and this effect is mediated by HBx

To evaluate whether HBV infection in HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nLuc cells induces enhanced luciferase activity, we infected HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nLuc cells with HepAD38-derived genotype D virus at an MOI of 100, 200, 500 and 1,000 GEq/ml for 4 days in the presence of PEG (Fig. 2A) as described.18 The results showed a significant, MOI-dependent increase in luciferase activity compared to control (Fig. 2B; p <0.0001). Western blot analysis corroborated these findings, demonstrating increasing levels of phosphorylated CREB (pCREB-Ser133) with increasing HBV MOI (Fig. 2C). Although HBx and HBcAg were undetectable by western blot analyses with the tested antibodies (data not shown), immunofluorescence data revealed an MOI-dependent increase in HBcAg expression in HBV-infected cells (Fig. 2D). To confirm that luciferase activity was driven by HBV replication, we assessed HBV replication through multiple approaches. Given the well-documented challenge of detecting HBV-derived HBx, we utilized its known repressive effect on the Smc6 complex as indirect evidence of its presence (Fig. 2C).10,37 Additionally, we observed a significant, MOI-dependent increase in HBV DNA levels in cell culture supernatants, accompanied by a corresponding rise in intracellular cccDNA levels (Fig. 2E,F; p <0.0001). Moreover, we quantified HBx RNA transcripts in HBV-infected HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nLuc cells and found a strong correlation (r2 = 0.95; p <0.005) between enhanced luciferase activity and the level of HBx RNA transcripts as the MOI increased (Fig. 2G). To further validate the role of HBx on vCRE-mediated luciferase activity, we generated recombinant HBV-wt (rHBV1.3-wt) and HBV-null-X (rHBV1.3-null-X) viruses from pHBV1.3-wt and pHBV1.3-null-X molecular clones, respectively, and infected HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nLuc cells (Fig. 2H). Infection with rHBV1.3-wt significantly increased luciferase activity, levels of rcDNA in the supernatant, pgRNA abundance, and HBcAg expression, whereas rHBV1.3-null-X had no such effect (Fig. 2I–M). To further confirm that the reduction in HBV transcription and replication was specifically due to the lack of HBx, HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nLuc cells infected with rHBV1.3-null-X were transfected with a plasmid encoding HBx (Fig. 2H). Ectopic expression of HBx partially restored luciferase activity, rcDNA levels in the supernatant, pgRNA levels, and HBcAg expression (Fig. 2I–M), demonstrating that HBx can complement these functions in trans. However, no significant differences in cccDNA levels were observed among cells infected with rHBV1.3-wt, rHBV1.3-null-X, or rHBV1.3-null-X complemented with HBx (Fig. 2N), indicating that HBx is dispensable for cccDNA formation. Together, these data demonstrate that while HBx is not required for the establishment of cccDNA in HepG2-NTCP cells, it plays a critical role in promoting HBV transcription and replication, which can partially be rescued by trans-complementation. HBV-specific siRNA-mediated knockdown of HBx reduces luciferase activity in HBV-infected HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nLuc cells

To investigate whether HBx knockdown via HBV-specific siRNA reduces the luciferase activity in HBV-infected HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nLuc cells, we infected HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nLuc cells with HBV for 4 days before transfecting them with either HBV-specific siRNA, targeting nucleotides 1825–1843, common to all HBV RNA transcripts (Fig. 3A) or non-targeting scramble siRNA control for 24 h.25 The next day, the cells were trypsinized, re-seeded and processed for luciferase activity at 2, 4, 6, and 8 days post-transfection (Fig. 3B). Both HBV-specific and control siRNAs had no impact on cell viability (Fig. 3C). No significant effect on luciferase activity was observed on day 2 post-transfection with HBV-specific siRNA. However, luciferase activity was reduced by approximately 10%, 60%, and 80% on days 4, 6, and 8, respectively, compared to both the HBV-only and non-targeting siRNA controls (Fig. 3D; p <0.0001). Western blot analysis revealed that suppression of HBx by the HBV-specific siRNA restored Smc6 protein levels and decreased CREB phosphorylation activity in HBV-infected HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nLuc cells (Fig. 3E). Quantification of band intensity showed a de-repression in Smc6 protein at days 4, 6, and 8 post-siRNA treatment in HBV-infected cells (Fig. 3F; p <0.0001). As expected, siRNA treatment reduced HBV DNA levels by 27%, 46%, 82% and 92% on day 2, 4, 6 and 8 after treatment, respectively (Fig. 3G; p <0.0001). To determine whether HBV-specific siRNA interferes with the vCRE-nLuc reporter construct, HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nLuc cells were first infected with HBV and subsequently transfected with HBV-targeting siRNA, as described above. Four days after siRNA transfection, cells were transfected with either an empty vector or a plasmid expressing the HTLV-1 Tax protein. Luciferase assays performed 48 h later demonstrated that the vCRE-nLuc reporter remained responsive to Tax, as evidenced by increased luciferase activity and enhanced phosphorylation of CREB at Ser133 (Fig. 3H,I). These results confirm that the HBV-specific siRNA effectively knocked down the pgRNA (Fig. 3J, p <0.0005) without interfering with the reporter system. Dicoumarol (3,3′-methylenebis [4-hydroxycoumarin]), a small-molecule compound belonging to the dihydroquinolizinone class of chemicals which acts as an inhibitor of NAD(P)H:quinone oxidoreductase (NQO1) by competing with NAD(P)H for binding to NQO1 has been reported to degrade HBx protein.30 To evaluate its effects, HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nLuc cells were infected with HBV for 4 days and subsequently treated with 100, 200 and 300 μM of dicoumarol for 48 h. Dicoumarol treatment resulted in a dose-dependent reduction in luciferase activity (Fig. 4F; p <0.0001) with no effect on cell viability (Fig. 4G). These data suggest that these drugs do not interfere with promoter activity. Collectively, these data confirmed the reliability of our reporter system for quantifying HBx activity and demonstrate its potential utility in screening anti-HBx drugs.

Discussion

Current anti-HBV therapies, including nucelos(t)ide analogues and IFNα, fail to eliminate HBV cccDNA from infected hepatocytes and rarely achieve functional cure characterized by HBsAg loss. Consequently, patients often require long-term treatment, and the risk of liver cancer persists in those who remain HBsAg positive. This highlights the urgent need for novel antiviral strategies aimed at achieving HBsAg loss, ideally through clearance or silencing of cccDNA. While numerous agents are under investigation for their curative potential, only a few potent antiviral compounds have been identified. High-throughput screening systems have proven transformative in the discovery of direct-acting antivirals for HCV infection and could similarly accelerate the identification of novel anti-HBV agents, particularly those with novel mechanisms of action.41 We describe an HBx-responsive vCRE/CREB-driven cell culture reporter system designed to quantify HBV infection and evaluate new antiviral compounds. Importantly, the system specifically relies on the production of functional HBx protein, making it particularly useful for identifying inhibitors of cccDNA transcriptional activity. Such inhibitors are likely to play a critical role in curative antiviral strategies for chronic HBV.

Despite significant advances in the basic understanding of HBV replication, the development of effective therapeutics remains challenging, partly due to the lack of suitable reporter systems. While some recombinant reporter HBV genomes and cell lines are already in use,42,43 their utility is limited. Insertion of reporter genes into the HBV genome often disrupts replication and infectivity, owing to the compact nature of the genome and overlap of its open reading frames.42,44 A hybrid reporter system based on DDB-1 and HBx interaction has been described, which requires transient co-transfection of a split nLuc. However, compared to this transient HBx-DDB1 system,29 our reporter system stably expresses the HBx-responsive vCRE-nLuc cassette, eliminating the need for transfection step(s) and better mimicking the cellular interactions that occur under natural infection conditions. As a result, the luciferase read-out more accurately reflects the inhibitory effects of drugs. Furthermore, the transient HBx-DDB1 reporter system is limited to identifying agents that interfere with the HBx-DDB1 protein-protein interaction. In contrast, our reporter system relies on intact functional HBx, meaning that any treatment inhibiting HBx production (via transcription or translation) or its functional activity (e.g. protein-protein interactions or transactivation) will inhibit luciferase production, as demonstrated in our evaluation of agents with known mechanisms of action.

We showed that specific inhibitors of HBx, nitazoxanide and dicoumarol, both inhibited luciferase production, underscoring the utility of the system for identifying HBx-targeting compounds. Given the role of HBx in stabilizing cccDNA and preventing its transcriptional silencing by the SMC5/6 complex,8,10,37 HBx inhibitors may serve as promising antiviral target. Importantly, our system can also identify inhibitors acting upstream of HBx production, which may be more relevant for achieving functional HBV cure. Supporting this notion, siRNA targeting HBV transcripts (including HBx) inhibited luciferase activity. Likewise, IFN, which is known to have multiple antiviral functions, including degrading cccDNA and limiting its transcriptional activity, also inhibited luciferase production.3,4,45 In contrast, TDF, a potent reverse transcriptase inhibitor that effectively blocks HBV DNA production, did not affect luciferase production at physiological doses, as it does not influence cccDNA abundance or its transcriptional activity.

Our HBx-responsive cell line, where luciferase is driven by vCRE, leverages the CREB-HBx interaction.11,21 While HTLV-1 Tax protein can transactivate vCRE 45- to 60-fold,46 HBx achieves a 5.13-fold activation in HEK293T cells, likely due to its N-terminal transcriptional repressor.47 This level of activation is sufficient to quantify HBV activity, with infection at different MOIs inducing a 10- to 45-fold increase in luciferase activity in HepG2-NTCP-vCRE-nLuc. HBx recruits CREB and ATF2 to HBV enhancer I48 and CBP/p300 to the HBV minichromosome, correlating with replication.49 These findings explain why inhibiting CREB results in a reduction in HBV DNA production. Importantly, because the system relies on CREB signaling,12 CREB inhibitors will reduce luciferase activity. Thus, positive hits from an initial screen will need further characterization to confirm anti-HBV effects rather than simply anti-CREB activity. We leveraged the fact that HBx promotes proteasomal degradation of the SMC5/6 complex10 to demonstrate that inhibiting HBx production, without affecting CREB activity, will reduce luciferase activity and derepress SMC6. This was clearly shown with high-dose TDF, which inhibited luciferase activity and reduced CREB phosphorylation, but did not affect SMC6 levels, suggesting an effect on CREB activity rather than HBx production. HBx is a multifunctional viral protein essential for HBV replication and cccDNA transcriptional activity in vivo but has little or no significant effect on viral replication efficiency in certain poorly differentiated hepatoma cell lines.6,7 It enhances viral transcription by modulating the cellular CREB pathway, specifically by inhibiting the dephosphorylation of CREB at Ser133 via protein phosphatase 1.21 Thus, the elevated Ser133 phosphorylation observed in our study likely reflects HBx-mediated stabilization of CREB activity through sustained phosphorylation at Ser133. In our recombinant HBV infection studies, the absence of HBx markedly impaired the production of rcDNA and pgRNA. However, ectopic expression of HBx in trans partially rescued both viral DNA replication and transcription, consistent with its known augmentative role.50 The incomplete restoration is likely attributable to transfection efficiency. Notably, cccDNA levels remained unchanged between HBV1.3 and HBx-deficient HBV and were unaffected by HBx complementation, suggesting that HBx is dispensable for cccDNA formation but critical for its transcriptional activity.6

Moreover, since our cell culture reporter system is HBx- and CREB-dependent, it is well suited for identifying compounds that specifically target HBx, as well as any compounds that work upstream of HBx production and thus limit HBx quantity (e.g. cccDNA or HBV RNA-targeting agents). A potential limitation of our system is that while HBx-mediated luciferase fold changes are statistically significant, they are not as substantial in absolute terms as is seen with some other screening systems. Therefore, further screening, particularly in primary human hepatocytes, is necessary for validation. However, our reporter system offers several advantages, including the ability to use patient-derived viruses from all genotypes and cell lines with stable HBV production. Additionally, it enables rapid quantification of observed effects across a wide dynamic range. Since patient isolates can be evaluated, the system could also be used for more direct clinical applications, such as understanding treatment non-response. This could be particularly useful in the future if novel agents are not universally active. Although HBx primarily originates from cccDNA, it can also be derived from integrated HBV DNA.51 Therefore, there is a theoretical possibility of observing false negative results in screening if a compound inhibits cccDNA but does not affect HBx produced from integrated DNA. This is likely only a concern if cells that stably produce HBV with known integrations are used. In systems with initial infection, as would be the case with the patient-derived virus, most, if not all, HBx will come from cccDNA.

In conclusion, the HepG2-NTCP-nLuc-vCRE reporter cell line offers a simple, rapid, and direct approach to monitor HBV replication and is well suited for antiviral drug screening. This system is particularly valuable for identifying novel agents that target HBx, the viral regulatory protein essential for cccDNA transcription and HBV replication, which is likely to be critical for the development of curative therapies.
