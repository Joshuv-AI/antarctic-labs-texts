Characterization of Matrix-Adapted PEG Isolation of Exosome-Like Nanovesicles from Pandanus conoideus
Authors: Lely Rahmawaty, Annisa N. Az Zahra, Triati D. K. Wungu, Anggraini Barlian, Suprijadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles (PDENs) have recently gained prominence as biocompatible, renewable nanocarriers, but lipid-dense tropical plants remain an underutilized vesicle reservoir and present substantial hurdles for efficient isolation. Here, we establish Pandanus conoideus (red fruit), a Papuan endemic with unusually high carotenoid, tocopherol, and unsaturated fatty acid content, as a new source of PDENs and develop a matrix-adapted polyethylene glycol (PEG 6000) workflow for their scalable isolation from fresh fruit (RFF), dried simplicia (RFS), and long-stored red fruit oil (RFO). Differential centrifugation coupled with matrix-specific PEG precipitation and syringe filtration yielded RFO-derived PDENs (RFO-PDENs) with exosome-like hydrodynamic diameters (predominantly 30–150 nm by nanoparticle tracking analysis), low polydispersity, and spherical, bilayered morphology by transmission electron microscopy, whereas RFF and RFS favored larger, more polydisperse vesicle/aggregate populations. Optimization uncovered narrow matrix-dependent PEG “windows”, with 8–10% PEG producing the most homogeneous, highly concentrated RFO-PDENs fraction (up to 1.2 × 1010 particles/mL) using only benchtop equipment, thus enabling straightforward scale-up. Untargeted LC–HRMS metabolomics of bulk RF-PDEN preparations revealed a complex metabolite landscape dominated by fatty acids and oxylipins, fatty acid amides, sphingolipids/ceramides, amino acids, and cyclic peptides, together with signature P. conoideus antioxidants (α-tocopherol, α/β-carotene) and dermatologically relevant metabolites such as azelaic acid. However, the presence of highly abundant signals with plausible nonendogenous or process-related origins (e.g., 4-undecylbenzenesulfonic acid) indicates that these data represent a composite PDENs preparation rather than a definitive catalog of enclosed vesicular cargo, and potential coprecipitation of matrix- or process-derived components cannot be excluded. Complementary label-free proteomics identified a low-complexity proteome enriched in plastid/mitochondrial ribosomal proteins, Cytochrome b 6f complex subunit VI, and a dense cohort of lectin and lectin receptor-like kinases that is consistent with translation/redox-associated components and a lectin-enriched, signaling-candidate vesicle surface, while remaining preliminary and qualitative in nature. Collectively, these findings show that matrix-optimized PEG precipitation enables isolation of a high-yield RF-PDEN reservoir from challenging, lipid-rich red fruit oil with preserved membrane architecture and a composition consistent with bioactive lipid and antioxidant enrichment, while underscoring that proposed antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, barrier-repair, or depigmenting applications of RFO-PDENs remain hypothesis-generating and will require future functional validation.

Introduction

Plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles (PDENs) have recently gained attention as a new class of natural nanoscale carriers, offering an attractive alternative to mammalian exosomes because of their favorable safety profile, low immunogenicity, and negligible risk of zoonotic pathogen transmission. Comparable to their mammalian counterparts, PDENs encapsulate a broad repertoire of bioactive molecules, including proteins, lipids, and multiple RNA species, enabling them to participate in intercellular communication and regulate recipient cell behavior. Collectively, these features position PDENs as promising platforms for applications in drug delivery, functional foods, and nanomedicine.

Indonesia harbors underexplored botanical resources that may provide valuable sources of PDENs. Among these, Pandanus conoideus (red fruit), a Papuan endemic traditionally used as both food and medicine, is particularly noteworthy yet remains unexplored for PDENs isolation. Red fruit oil contains an exceptionally high proportion of lipids (≈98–99%) and is particularly rich in essential unsaturated fatty acidsoleic (omega-9), linoleic (omega-6), and α-linolenic (omega-3), which have been linked to improvements in serum LDL levels and cardiovascular health. Notably, red fruit is also an extraordinary source of carotenoids and tocopherols; its total carotene content (6,447.90–8,223.15 μg/g) , is reported to be roughly 22–40-fold higher than that of carrots, and together with its vitamin E fraction, these constituents confer potent antioxidant capacity linked to antidiabetic, , tissue-repair, , and renoprotective activities. In parallel, red fruit extracts exhibit broad bioactivity, including anti-inflammatory and anticancer effects in gastrointestinal and other models, ,, immunomodulator, as well as antimelanogenic and skin-brightening properties comparable to hydroquinone-based formulations, mediated in part through modulation of NF-κB, COX-2, and melanogenesis-related signaling pathways (e.g., PKA, ERK, AKT). , While recent formulation efforts have incorporated red fruit extracts into engineered nanosystemssuch as thermosensitive nanoemulgels and self-nanoemulsifying drug delivery systems (SNEDDS)to improve the solubility and stability of lipophilic components, ,,, these studies emphasize externally constructed systems. Collectively, this distinctive compositional and bioactivity profile suggests that P. conoideus represents a promising candidate for the discovery and characterization of bioactive PDENs.

Preliminary research was conducted to explore whether Pandanus conoideus contains a distinctive PDEN population, given the numerous studies reporting its health-related bioactivities. Owing to the plant’s annual fruiting cycle in remote Papuan forests, fresh fruit and simplicia were unavailable at the onset of this study; therefore, red fruit oil obtained from the 2022 production year and stored at 10–15 °C was utilized. Unexpectedly, initial fndings revealed the presence of PDENs in red fruit oil that had been stored for approximately three years, prompting a broader investigation. Therefore, this study aimed to define the PEG 6000 concentration that most effectively enables precipitation-based isolation of plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles from three P. conoideus matrices, using their physicochemical and molecular signatures as benchmarks. In parallel, we assessed the native morphology of red-fruit-derived exosome-like nanovesicles (RF-PDENs) using a vacuum-filtration method that captures vesicles through size-and adsorption-dependent retention as an orthogonal comparative approach. PEG-mediated precipitation involves the addition of PEG, typically in the presence of salt, to decrease nanoparticle solubility through steric exclusion and osmotic effects, thereby promoting aggregation and enabling recovery of extracellular vesicles (EVs/exosomes) by low-speed centrifugation. ,,, This strategy is extensively applied for virus concentration, exosomes, and PDENs because it is operationally simple and obviates the need for ultracentrifugation. Subsequent filtration steps (commonly 0.45 μm followed by 0.22 μm) are frequently incorporated to remove larger debris or microvesicles and to sterilize or clarify PDEN preparations. Moreover, PEG-based precipitation is cost-effective and amenable to large sample volumes, supporting its potential translation into scalable and industrially relevant isolation workflows.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of RF-PDENs

Matrix- and PEG-Dependent Isolation of RF-PDENs

Plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles (PDENs) were isolated from Pandanus conoideusred fruit fresh (RFF), simplicia (RFS), and oil (RFO)using differential centrifugation followed by matrix-adapted PEG 6000 precipitation. Tailored pretreatment, PEG concentration, and resuspension volume were necessary for each matrix, particularly for the viscous, lipid-rich RFO, to achieve well-dispersed nanoscale vesicles. RFF, RFS, and RFO-PDENs at 5% and 10% served as a preliminary benchmark formulation, and subsequent optimization used PSA, NTA, and TEM to identify PEG conditions that maximized exosome-like size, homogeneity, and particle yield across matrices.

Dynamic light scattering revealed a strong dependence of particle size characteristics on PEG concentration in all matrices (Table , Figure a–b). PSA histograms were analyzed assuming an approximately normal particle size distribution, which enabled estimation of the median diameter from the cumulative undersize (D50), the modal diameter from the peak maximum, and the associated peak frequency. In combination with the SD of the main peak, these parameters (Table ) were then used to pinpoint the PEG concentration that produced the most monodisperse, optimally sized RF-PDENs population. In RFF, increasing PEG from 3 to 15% shifted the main size mode from 230 nm (170–270 nm, SD 53 nm) to 280 nm (250–315 nm, SD 33 nm), with a transient shift to smaller particles at 4% PEG (119 nm, 100–135 nm, SD 18 nm) that coincided with an elevated PDI (1.2), indicating that the apparent size reduction arose within a highly heterogeneous population. RFS exhibited progressive enrichment of larger vesicles at higher PEG concentrations, with mean diameters increasing from 225–320 nm (SD 23–38 nm) at 3–4% PEG to 385–525 nm (SD 42–81 nm) at 8–15% PEG, while PDI remained at 0.4–0.6 and count rates peaked at 8–10% PEG, consistent with preferential precipitation of submicron aggregates. By contrast, RFO produced the smallest and most uniform vesicles across the PEG series, maintaining mean diameters of 100–183 nm with peak diameters of 131–143 nm, low PDIs of 0.2–0.3, and comparatively small SDs (3–8 nm at 3–12% PEG), together with high count rates (204–519 kCPS), indicative of a narrowly distributed nanoscale vesicle population, except at 15% PEG where the SD increased to 42 nm and larger particles appeared (Table , Figure b).

These trends indicate that the combined filtration-PEG workflow fractionates RF-derived vesicles by size and aggregation state: low PEG (3–5%) favors enrichment of sub-200 nm particles in RFF and RFO, whereas higher PEG (≥8–10%) shifts recovery toward larger vesicles and aggregates, particularly in RFS and RFF, as reflected in the broader SDs and modestly elevated PDIs. The behavior parallels recent observations that plant matrix composition and processing state (fresh vs dried) strongly modulate the yield and physicochemical properties of exosome-like nanoparticles, as seen for Portulaca oleracea-PDENs, where fresh and dried materials yielded distinct size and zeta potential profiles despite being isolated with a common ultracentrifugation-based protocol. Together, these data support the conclusion that PEG-assisted precipitation is not “one-size-fits-all” for plant matrices; instead, matrix-dependent thresholds must be defined to balance yield against aggregation and coprecipitation of nonvesicular macromolecules.

NTA Reveals Size-Defined RF-PDENs Subpopulations

Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA) of RF-PDENs isolated under matrix-specific PEG conditions quantified particle number and hydrodynamic size within two windows: 30–150 nm and 150–300 nm (Table , Figure c–m). PBS controls contained only background-level particles (≈4.5–5.9 × 106 particles/mL) with mean diameters of 116 ± 21 nm and 212 ± 40 nm, confirming negligible vesicle-like material in buffer alone and providing a baseline for instrument noise. All RF formulations showed 2–4-log higher particle concentrations, up to 108–1010 particles/mL, demonstrating efficient enrichment of RF-associated nanovesicles rather than nonspecific contaminants.

Across matrices, mean diameters in the 30–150 nm window were tightly clustered around 100–114 nm with SDs of 25–28 nm, while those in the 150–300 nm window were centered at 199–212 nm with SDs of 36–41 nm, revealing two relatively well-resolved nanovesicle subpopulations consistent with exosome-sized vesicles and larger vesicles or aggregates (Table ). RFF (3%) and RFS (4%) yielded comparable mean sizes in both windows (114 ± 25 and 212 ± 41 nm for RFF; 110 ± 26 and 207 ± 38 nm for RFS) and particle concentrations on the order of 108 particles/mL, with a shared modal diameter of 165 nm, demonstrating efficient enrichment of RF-associated nanovesicles rather than nonspecific contaminants and falling within the size and concentration ranges reported for other plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles, such as cabbage EVs.

The oil-based formulations showed a more pronounced exosome-like signature. RFO (8%) and RFO (10%) displayed mean diameters of 100 ± 28/209 ± 40 nm and 101 ± 26/199 ± 36 nm in the small and large windows, respectively, with very high particle concentrations (6.9 × 108 and 5.4 × 108 particles/mL for RFO 8%; 1.2 × 1010 and 5.8 × 109 particles/mL for RFO 10%) and modal diameters of 86–95 nm, indicating that these conditions maximize the yield of ∼100 nm RF-PDENs while maintaining relatively narrow SDs.

Storage history appears to modulate particle number and the relative contribution of the two size windows but does not appear to shift the core exosome-like diameter range, suggesting that RFO-derived vesicles possess a comparatively robust membrane architecture. Taken together, these NTA data identify RFO 8–10% as a high-yield, exosome-sized nanovesicle preparation suitable as a lead candidate for downstream biological and formulation studies, while also aligning with previous reports of high-concentration PDEN suspensions obtained from other edible fruits, such as Physalis peruviana.

Morphological Validation and Comparison to Established RF-PDENs

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was employed to validate PDENs’ morphology and assess the impact of the isolation method and PEG concentration on vesicle integrity (Figure ). Across all matrices, vacuum-filtered samples contained vesicles but also dense background structures and broad particle size distributions, reflecting the coisolation of debris and aggregates (Figure a–c). In RFF, vesicles appeared embedded in amorphous material, and low PEG (3–4%) yielded poorly contrasted particles on noisy backgrounds, while 5% PEG produced a dominant ≈300 nm structure surrounded by smaller vesicles, consistent with PEG-induced aggregation rather than a clean 50–150 nm PDENs population (Figure d–f).

Vacuum-filtered RFS showed similar issues: it contained irregular, dense particles with few clearly delineated vesicles (Figure b). At 3% PEG, fields were dominated by aggregates with only sporadic nanoscale vesicles, indicating inefficient selective precipitation. Although 4–5% PEG improved the visibility of 90–120 nm vesicles, large, bright aggregates persisted, pointing to mixed populations and incomplete removal of nonvesicular material (Figure g–i). By contrast, vacuum-filtered RFO already displayed numerous well-spaced spherical vesicles with diameters of ∼80–150 nm and relatively clean backgrounds, consistent with PSA data showing smaller, more monodisperse particles (Figure c). PEG treatment further improved RFO preparations: 8% PEG generated abundant 70–120 nm vesicles with minimal aggregation, and 10% PEG produced fields dominated by individual ∼100–110 nm vesicles with intact, circular membranes, in line with efficient precipitation of exosome-sized vesicles (Figure d–f).

Under matrix-adapted PEG conditions, refined-oil RF-PDENs consistently formed predominantly spherical vesicles in the 70–120 nm range, matching the 30–150 nm particle-size window typically ascribed to exosome-like nanovesicles and exhibiting high scattering intensities on PSA/KCPS, together with clearly resolved bilayer morphology and abundant particles by TEM. Taken together, these orthogonal size and morphology readouts indicate that the chosen PEG concentration supports efficient enrichment of nanoscale vesicles with exosome-like dimensions rather than larger debris or aggregates. Morphologically, RFO-PDENs resemble the cup- to sphere-shaped PDENs reported for Carica papaya and Solanum nigrum, which were likewise enriched in 100–200 nm vesicles by ultracentrifugation and membrane-based isolation, supporting the view that oil-derived nanovesicles from P. conoideus fall within the same general PDEN size and shape regime across different botanical sources. , While direct compositional equivalence cannot be inferred from morphology alone, these convergent PSA, KCPS, and TEM features strengthen the interpretation that RFO-PDENs represent bona fide plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles suitable for cross-study comparison.

Positioning RF-PDENs in the Broader Exosome and Nanoplatform Landscape

The integrated PSA, NTA, and TEM data identify RFO as the most suitable RF-PDENs matrix, yielding the smallest, most narrowly distributed vesicles with low PDI, high particle number, and clean exosome-like morphology. RFF and RFS, in contrast, are enriched with larger, more polydisperse particles and aggregates, suggesting that these matrices act primarily as reservoirs for coarser vesicles or nonvesicular assemblies, analogous to how dried plant states can shift the composition and biophysical behavior of PDENs. The matrix and storage dependence observed here resonates with recent reviews emphasizing that plant species, tissue type, processing (fresh vs dried), and isolation method jointly define the functional landscape of plant-derived ELNs. ,

PEG-based precipitation is increasingly recognized as a practical, scalable alternative to ultracentrifugation for enriching extracellular vesicles and plant-derived nanovesicles, with several studies emphasizing that both matrix type and PEG concentration must be empirically tuned to balance yield, purity, and vesicle integrity. Reviews of clinical-grade EV workflows and general EV purification strategies highlight polymer precipitation as a low-equipment, high-throughput method that can rival or surpass ultracentrifugation in particle recovery while remaining compatible with downstream molecular and functional analyses. In the plant nanovesicle field, Kumar et al. exploited PEG 6000 not only as a molecular crowder to precipitate turmeric- and pepper-derived ENVs but also as a fusogenic agent to assemble hybrid vesicles, achieving markedly enhanced encapsulation of bioactives, which underscores the versatility of PEG-mediated workflows for complex botanical matrices. Consistent with these reports, systematic variation of PEG 6000 across fresh fruit, dried simplicia, and red fruit oil matrices in the present study revealed matrix-specific “optimal windows”, with 8–10% PEG yielding the highest concentration and most homogeneous population of exosome-like vesicles from lipid-rich red fruit oil, analogous to the 8–10% PEG regimes that have been identified as optimal for plasma and other biofluids (Figure c, j–k).

Identification of RF-PDENs

Metabolomic Composition of RF-PDENs

The LC–HRMS analysis of RF-PDENs revealed a highly complex metabolomic profile that is broadly consistent with the multifunctional cargo reported for plant extracellular vesicles, while still reflecting contributions from the surrounding matrix and isolation process. The total ion chromatogram (TIC) displayed well-resolved peaks over approximately 0.5–20 min retention time and showed high chromatographic reproducibility across independent preparations (coefficient of variation < 5% for total ion current, n = 3) (Figure a), indicating stable performance of the LC-HRMS method under matrix-adapted PEG isolation conditions. Using accurate mass (mass error < 5 ppm), isotopic pattern matching, and mzCloud-based spectral library searches (scores ranging from 30.6 to 99.9%), 247 metabolites were tentatively assigned, spanning carotenoids, tocopherols/tocotrienols, fatty acids and oxylipins, sphingolipids and ceramides, fatty acid amides, amino acids and peptides, and diverse secondary metabolites (Supporting Information). Because these annotations are based on untargeted profiling of bulk preparations rather than purified vesicle cores, they should be interpreted as a composite “metabolite landscape” of RF-PDEN preparations rather than as a definitive catalog of enclosed vesicular cargo, in line with previous reports of complex, matrix-dependent profiles in untargeted metabolomics studies. ,,

The RF-PDEN metabolite pool covered a wide dynamic range. Highly abundant species included 4-undecylbenzenesulfonic acid (3.50 × 109 AU, 23.94% of total signal), caprolactam (1.54 × 109 AU, 10.53%), and cyclo­(Leu)6 (1.09 × 109 AU, 7.46%), whereas functionally interesting but less abundant components, such as α-tocopherol (5.47 × 106 AU) and carotenoids (α-carotene and β-carotene, each 1.21 × 105 AU), were also detected (Table ). The very high signal attributed to 4-undecylbenzenesulfonic acid is notable because sulfonated aromatics are rarely reported as endogenous plant metabolites and may instead reflect processing-related contaminants or coprecipitating surfactant-like material. Accordingly, these species cannot at present be interpreted as definitive exosomal cargo, and the current data set should be regarded as including both vesicle-associated and coisolated metabolites. Future work incorporating orthogonal purification strategies (e.g., size-exclusion chromatography or density gradients) will be needed to more clearly discriminate vesicle-enriched metabolites from matrix-derived or process-derived coisolates. ,

Hierarchical clustering analysis was visualized as a heatmap to illustrate relative metabolite abundance patterns within RF-PDEN preparations (Figure b), and selected metabolites were grouped into functional categories based on reported biochemical pathways and pharmacological activities (Figure c–e). The largest cluster comprised metabolites associated with metabolism, nutrient transport, and neuroactive functions (66% of the selected signal intensity in Figure c and 18% of the overall composition in Figure d), dominated by amino acids such as isoleucine (3.95 × 108 AU, 2.70% of total signal) and l-phenylalanine (2.97 × 108 AU, 2.03%), along with choline (1.29 × 108 AU) and the cyclic peptide cyclo­(Leu)6 (1.09 × 109 AU). These observations are consistent with the emerging view that plant-derived vesicle preparations can contain amino acids and small peptides, , but the current data do not resolve whether these molecules are selectively packaged into nanovesicles or are largely coisolated from the surrounding matrix; targeted compartment-specific studies will be required to address this distinction.

A second major category comprised metabolites with previously reported anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory activities (30% of the functional signal in Figure c and 34% of the overall composition in Figure d), including unsaturated fatty acids (oleic acid, 4.85 × 107 AU; linoleic acid, 3.77 × 106 AU) and the dicarboxylic acid azelaic acid (2.54 × 107 AU). Oleic and linoleic acids are well-established lipid mediators that can modulate inflammation via mechanisms such as attenuation of NF-κB activation, reduction of pro-inflammatory cytokine production (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α), and alteration of lipid membrane properties in target cells, ,, while topical linoleic acid has been reported to enhance ceramide synthesis, promote keratinocyte differentiation, and accelerate wound healing by stimulating fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition. ,, Azelaic acid, a saturated nine-carbon dicarboxylic acid, has been used clinically for conditions such as acne, rosacea, and melasma due to its antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and antikeratinizing effects, including inhibition of tyrosinase and modulation of keratinocyte differentiation. ,, In light of these prior reports, the presence of linoleic/oleic acid and azelaic acid at appreciable signal intensities in RF-PDEN preparations suggests a plausible molecular basis for some of the antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, depigmenting, and wound-healing activities historically attributed to P. conoideus extracts (Figure e); ,, however, in the absence of direct functional assays and in view of potential coisolation of matrix components, these links should be considered working hypotheses rather than demonstrated properties of RF-PDENs.

Antioxidant and protective molecules formed a distinct cluster (40% of the functional signal in Figure c and 0.08% of the overall composition in Figure d), dominated by α-tocopherol (5.47 × 106 AU), α-tocotrienol (1.52 × 106 AU), α-carotene (1.21 × 105 AU), β-carotene (1.21 × 105 AU), and D-(+)-malic acid (4.97 × 107 AU). The detection of tocopherols and carotenoids within RF-PDEN preparations is consistent with their status as signature antioxidants of Pandanus conoideus fruit, which contributes to its characteristic red-orange pigmentation and documented radical-scavenging capacity. , α-Tocopherol and α-tocotrienol act as chain-breaking antioxidants within lipid bilayers, and carotenoids such as α- and β-carotene are efficient singlet-oxygen quenchers that can influence redox-sensitive signaling pathways. ,, Taken together, these observations suggest that RF-PDEN preparations may provide a vesicle-associated reservoir of lipophilic antioxidants; however, their exact localization (encapsulated vs surface-associated vs matrix-derived) and contribution to antioxidant or anti-inflammatory function remain to be tested experimentally.

The presence of carotenoids and tocopherols in RF-PDEN preparations, despite their very low relative abundance (tocopherols/tocotrienols ≈ 0.05% and carotenoids/terpenoids ≈0.03% of the total signal), is notable given the propensity of these lipophilic compounds to undergo oxidative degradation during isolation and storage. The fact that these molecules remain detectable after PEG precipitation and LC-HRMS analysis suggests that the matrix-adapted isolation protocol can preserve at least a fraction of these sensitive antioxidants, potentially through protection within the lipid-rich domains of the nanovesicles. By analogy with other exosome-like systems, encapsulation of carotenoids and tocopherols within vesicular structures could, in principle, enhance their stability and bioavailability by providing a protective microenvironment that limits exposure to light, oxygen, and enzymatic degradation (Figure e); however, this proposed mechanism has not yet been validated for RF-PDENs, and dedicated functional and pharmacokinetic studies will be required to substantiate it.

A smaller but functionally interesting fraction corresponds to bioactive signaling and protein-modulating molecules (15% of the signal in Figure b–c), represented by caprolactam (1.54 × 109 AU), piperidine (2.18 × 108 AU), and bethanidine (4.08 × 108 AU). While caprolactam is widely known as an industrial nylon-6 precursor, its occurrence and potential role in biological systems remain underexplored. Piperidine and related N-heterocycles are alkaloid motifs with neuroactive properties, and their detection in RF-PDEN preparations is consistent with the possibility that P. conoideus fruit contains alkaloid-related compounds that may modulate cellular signaling pathways. Bethanidine is a guanidine derivative historically used as an antihypertensive agent due to its ability to inhibit norepinephrine release from sympathetic nerve terminals. The presence of these alkaloid-like and guanidine-containing species in RF-PDEN preparations raises the possibility that such metabolites could influence cardiovascular or neuroendocrine function; however, their endogenous origin in the plant, their specific association with vesicles versus bulk matrix, and their biological relevance in the context of RF-PDENs remain speculative and require further targeted investigation.

The exosome membrane and lipid-structure cluster (33% of structural signal in Figure c and 12.4% of overall composition in Figure d) was dominated by 1-stearoylglycerol (1.52 × 108 AU), hexadecanamide (1.12 × 108 AU), stearamide (1.61 × 108 AU), and several ceramides, including Cer­(d18:0/14:0) (3.03 × 108 AU), Cer­(d18:0/16:0) (1.41 × 108 AU), and Cer­(d18:0/17:0) (1.78 × 108 AU). This sphingolipid- and fatty amide-rich profile is highly characteristic of exosome membranes and reflects the critical role of ceramides in exosome biogenesis and structural integrity. ,, The enrichment of ceramides and sphingoid bases in RF-PDENs also has important implications for vesicle stability and bioavailability. Ceramide-rich membranes exhibit enhanced resistance to enzymatic degradation and oxidative stress, thereby prolonging the circulation half-life of nanovesicles in biological fluids. , Furthermore, ceramides can modulate the interaction of exosomes with target cells by influencing membrane fusion, receptor binding, and endocytosis pathways. By analogy, the ceramide content of RF-PDENs may enhance their interaction with epithelial barriers during topical or oral administration and could, in principle, contribute to penetration or transcytosis, thereby improving the delivery efficiency of encapsulated bioactive cargo; however, these ideas are currently inferential and will require direct testing in appropriate skin and intestinal models before any claims about barrier repair or delivery efficacy can be substantiated.

Taken together, the LC-HRMS data indicate that RF-PDEN preparations contain a diverse set of metabolites, including amino acids, cyclic peptides, bioactive lipids, ceramides, and low-abundance antioxidants, in a pattern that is broadly consistent with both the known composition of P. conoideus fruit and general features of plant-derived extracellular vesicle preparations. At the same time, the detection of highly abundant species with a plausible nonendogenous or processing-related origin, such as 4-undecylbenzenesulfonic acid and possibly some alkaloid-like or guanidine-containing metabolites, highlights an important limitation of the current matrix-adapted PEG protocol: coprecipitation of soluble matrix components and process-derived contaminants cannot be fully excluded. Consequently, the present metabolomic profile should be viewed as a composite description of RF-PDEN preparations rather than as a definitive list of stably enclosed vesicle cargo, and any mechanistic or application-oriented interpretations based on these data should be regarded as hypotheses that await confirmation through refined purification workflows and direct functional validation.

Protein Yield of Matrix-Adapted RF-PDENs (BCA Assay)

The total protein content of Pandanus conoideus–derived exosome-like nanovesicles (RF-PDENs) was quantified by BCA assay to evaluate the influence of source matrix and PEG concentration on vesicle-associated protein recovery and integrity. To ensure comparability across matrices, samples were normalized using a 10× (1:9) dilution based on intrinsic composition differences (fresh pulp ∼80–85% water, crude drug ∼ 28.5% dry matter, and oil fraction ∼ 6–8.7%). Under these standardized conditions, RF-PDENs isolated from fresh pulp (RFF, 3% PEG), crude drug (RFS, 4% PEG), and refined oil (RFO, 8% PEG) exhibited comparable protein concentrations below 100 μg/mL (96.63, 66.77, and 80.89 μg/mL, respectively; Table , Figure b). This consistency indicates that matrix-adapted PEG precipitation enables reproducible vesicular protein recovery across distinct starting materials when input normalization is applied, in agreement with previous extracellular vesicle studies demonstrating that controlled sample loading and solution conditions reduce variability across heterogeneous biological matrices. Comparable protein levels have also been reported for plant-derived nanovesicles, such as those from Aloe vera, where BCA-quantified vesicular protein was at the concentration of 120 μg/mL, supporting that this concentration range is sufficient for downstream functional and cell-based assays. This supported downstream functional assays, further indicating that this concentration range is sufficient for biological applications.

Notably, the refined oil matrix also offers practical advantages, including reduced processing complexity and improved filtration efficiency, which may contribute to more consistent vesicle recovery. Collectively, these findings show that both matrix normalization and PEG optimization are critical determinants of RF-PDEN yield, while refined oil represents an efficient and potentially more stable platform for vesicle isolation and downstream applications.

Lectin and Lectin Receptor-Like Kinases as Surface-Associated Candidates

The most functionally specialized class within the RF-PDENs proteome comprises lectin-domain–containing proteins and lectin receptor-like kinases, including legume lectin domain proteins, Jacalin-type lectins, L-type and G-type lectin receptor-like kinases, and lectin receptor-type protein kinases homologous to monocot species such as Zea mays, Triticum aestivum, Cocos nucifera, Oryza brachyantha, Digitaria exilis, Panicum spp., and Eragrostis curvula. Quantitatively, these lectin/RLK proteins represent the largest functional category, accounting for ∼45% of the RF-PDENs protein groups (Supporting Information). Many of these lectin proteins harbor hydrophobic signal peptides and transmembrane segments, placing them in plasma membrane-associated receptor complexes in GO-like cellular component annotations, with primary molecular functions assigned to carbohydrate binding and receptor serine/threonine kinase activity (Figure d). Within the limitations of shallow coverage, this pattern is consistent with a lectin-enriched, surface-associated protein cohort and suggests that RF-PDENs may present candidate receptor architectures at their outer membrane, rather than establishing a fully defined, signaling-competent receptor platform.

Lectin receptor-like kinases (LecRLKs) are recognized as key pattern recognition receptors and sensors at the plant cell surface, where they detect extracellular ATP, lipids, or pathogen-associated molecular patterns and transduce signals that activate ROS production and immune responses. L-type and G-type LecRLKs, in particular, are known to initiate defense signaling and stress-adaptation responses when they engage their cognate ligands. Their apparent enrichment on RF-PDENs surfaces raises the possibility that P. conoideus nanovesicles may retain receptor architectures capable of engaging glycan and receptor networks on mammalian skin cells, analogous to how plant-derived vesicles have been proposed to interact with host immune and barrier cells via conserved carbohydrate and lipid motifs. ,, However, additional targeted proteomics and functional assays will be required to establish whether they mediate specific recognition or signaling events in the recipient cells.

From a dermatological perspective, the presence of jacalin- and legume-type lectins, together with lectin receptor-type kinases, is potentially relevant because lectins have been implicated in the modulation of cytokine signaling, promotion of keratinocyte migration, and control of macrophage polarization in both plant- and mammalian-derived EV studies. The RF-PDENs lectin signature, therefore, suggests a plausible mechanistic basis for immunomodulatory and barrier-interacting activities, where glycan-mediated recognition at the stratum corneum and viable epidermis could, in principle, facilitate vesicle uptake and tuning of inflammatory responses; nonetheless, these ideas remain putative and require rigorous experimental confirmation.

Integrated Proteomics Functional Interpretation

Integrating the BCA and LC–HRMS/MS data, matrix-adapted PEG precipitation yielded RF-PDENs with (i) matrix-normalized protein content for RFF, RFS, and RFO at moderate PEG levels, (ii) an apparent increase in protein recovery from refined oil at 10% PEG that reflects both higher PEG6000 concentration and larger input volume rather than PEG concentration alone, and (iii) a discrete, low-complexity proteome enriched in organelle-derived ribosomal and redox-related proteins together with lectin-domain receptor-like kinases. Taken together, these observations indicate that the present preparations are characterized by moderate protein yields and a sparse proteomic profile dominated by organelle-associated and membrane-related proteins, which is compatible with a vesicular origin and qualitatively aligns with trends reported for plant extracellular vesicles and other small-EV preparations, while remaining subject to cautious interpretation given the shallow proteomic depth and the influence of input volume on yield.

The available GO-like annotations indicate an over-representation of terms related to translation and stress responses (ribosomal and RF1-like proteins), photosynthetic electron transport and electron-transfer activity (Cytochrome b 6f), and cell recognition, defense response, carbohydrate binding, and receptor kinase signaling (lectin/RLKs). Within the constraints of low peptide counts and modest sequence coverage, these annotations are best viewed as pointing to possible functional themesrather than demonstrating fully defined translation, redox, or signaling “modules” in RF-PDENsand thus provide qualitative hypotheses about vesicle-associated protein functions rather than definitive evidence of coordinated molecular machineries.

When considered in the context of skin biology, these combined annotations can be interpreted as preliminary indicators that RF-PDENs may carry proteins linked to redox processes, cellular stress responses, and carbohydrate-mediated recognition, which are conceptually relevant to antioxidant and immunomodulatory pathways reported for other plant-derived vesicles. ,, At present, however, the proteomics data are best regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and any proposed links to dermato-therapeutic activities such as anti-inflammatory, barrier-supporting, antiaging, or wound-healing effects should be treated as putative and subject to rigorous confirmation in targeted proteomic and functional assays (Figure e).

Collectively, the characterization and identification data support a schematic model in which Pandanus conoideus-derived exosome-like nanovesicles (PDENs) arise from endomembrane compartments and are released into the extracellular space, transporting both membrane and luminal cargos (PDENs) (Figure ). Matrix-adapted PEG 6000 precipitation (3–15%) generated distinct vesicle populations from the three P. conoideus matrices: RFF produced broadly polydisperse particles of roughly 170–360 nm, RFS produced 225–245 nm vesicles enriched in larger aggregates, and the lipid-rich RFO gave a high-yield pellet of nanoscale vesicles with an average diameter of ∼100 nm and a predominant 30–150 nm subpopulation by NTA, characteristic of exosome-like morphology. The magnified RFO-PDENs show a sphingolipid- and ceramide-rich bilayer studded with lectins, encapsulating amino acids and cyclic peptides (metabolic and neuroactive cargo), unsaturated fatty acids and azelaic acid (anti-inflammatory and barrier-modulating lipids), and tocopherols/carotenoids (antioxidant pigments), which together suggest a plausible mechanistic basis for antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and skin-repair activities of RF-PDENs but do not, given the current proteomics depth, establish a definitive molecular mechanism.

Conclusion

Matrix-adapted PEG 6000 precipitation enabled the isolation of plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles from Pandanus conoideus fresh fruit, dried simplicia, and long-stored red fruit oil using only benchtop-scale procedures, satisfying a key practical requirement for scalable botanical vesicle workflows. Across matrices, integrated particle-size analysis, nanoparticle tracking, and transmission electron microscopy identified red fruit oil (RFO) as the most favorable source, yielding abundant 70–120 nm vesicles within the 30–150 nm exosome-like size window, with narrow dispersity and clearly resolved bilayer morphology, whereas fresh fruit and simplicia preparations contained larger, more heterogeneous vesicle and aggregate populations. Untargeted LC-HRMS metabolomics and label-free proteomics indicated that RF-PDEN preparations contain small-molecule and protein signatures consistent with P. conoideus composition and general features of plant-derived extracellular vesiclesincluding sphingolipids/ceramides, long-chain fatty acid amides, unsaturated fatty acids, azelaic acid, carotenoids, tocopherols, organelle-derived ribosomal proteins, redox-associated components, and lectin/lectin receptor-like kinasesbut also revealed highly abundant signals with plausible nonendogenous or process-related origins (e.g., 4-undecylbenzenesulfonic acid) and coisolation/depth-of-coverage limitations. Consequently, the present metabolomic and proteomic profiles should be viewed as composite, preliminary descriptions of RF-PDEN preparations rather than definitive catalogs of stably enclosed vesicular cargo, and any mechanistic or application-oriented interpretations derived from these data remain hypothesis-generating and require future functional validation. Within this framework, the convergence of matrix-optimized, scalable PEG isolation with exosome-like vesicles displaying lectin-enriched surfaces and molecular signatures consistent with bioactive lipids and antioxidants suggests that P. conoideus oil-derived RF-PDENs may represent a promising nanocarrier system for subsequent mechanistic, functional, and formulation studies in nutraceutical, dermal, and anti-inflammatory contexts, and offers a conceptual blueprint for adapting PEG-based approaches to other compositionally complex, lipid-dense botanicals.

Materials and Methods

Isolation of RF-PDENs

Plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles (PDENs) were obtained from three P. conoideus matricesred fruit oil (RFO), dried simplicia (RFS), and fresh fruit (RFF)by differential centrifugation followed by PEG-mediated precipitation, using matrix-tailored pretreatment steps, PEG 6000 concentration optimization, and adjusted resuspension volumes. Fresh red fruit (Pandanus conoideus) purchased from CV Cahya Herbal Indonesia (in July 2025) and red fruit simplicia, (purchased in August and December 2025), were obtained from a local plantation in Nabire (Central Papua) and Sorong (Southwest Papua), Indonesia, whereas red fruit oil (manufacturing date: February 25, 2022; stored at 4 °C) was sourced from PT Tri Rahardja Karanganyar, Surakarta, Indonesia (Figure ). The species identity was confirmed based on macroscopic morphological characteristics by a botanist from the Bandung Institute of Technology (ITB), Indonesia. The macroscopic morphology of fresh red fruit, simplicia, and red fruit oil is shown in Supporting Information.

Matrix-specific workflows for isolation of Pandanus conoideus-derived exosome like nanovesicles (RF-PDENs). Schematic overview of sample preparation from red fruit fresh pulp (RFF), dried simplicia (RFS), and red fruit oil (RFO), including extraction or dispersion in PBS/ethanol, mechanical processing (blending, grinding, sieving), and differential centrifugation (2000 × g, 6000 × g, 10 000 × g) to obtain red fruit pellets. RF-PDENs are then isolated either by a matrix-adapted PEG 6000 precipitation routecomprising pellet resuspension in PBS, PEG addition, and overnight incubation at 4 °C, clarification at 8000 × g, and sequential filtration through nylon mesh/coarse filter paper and PES 0.45/0.22 μm membranesor by a vacuum-filtration route involving serial filtration (0.45, 0.22, 0.10, and 0.03 μm), washing, and recovery of RF-PDENs into PBS.

The isolation procedure was adapted from Kalarikkal et al. (2020) and further optimized for red fruit-derived samples. To standardize the isolation procedure, dilution ratios and filtration steps were optimized prior to polyethylene glycol (PEG) precipitation (Figure ). For fresh (RFF) and simplicia (RFS) preparations, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) was used as the extraction medium to maintain vesicle stability in aqueous matrices. In contrast, oil-based preparations (RFO) were first dispersed in ethanol to overcome their hydrophobicity and promote efficient release and suspension of PDENs from the lipid-rich matrix. A consistent material-to-solvent ratio was maintained across all matrices, with input amounts adjusted to account for differences in physical form so that isolation conditions remained comparable. Fresh red fruit (RFF) was homogenized in PBS with a blender for 5 min, diluted 1:9 (v/v) in PBS, and passed through a sieve to remove coarse debris. Red fruit oil was initially diluted 1:9 (v/v) in ethanol and then further diluted 1:1 (v/v) with PBS, whereas red fruit simplicia (RFS) was suspended directly at 1:9 (v/v) in PBS.

All suspensions were processed by sequential centrifugation at 2,000 × g for 10 min, 6,000 × g for 20 min, and 10,000 × g for 40 min at 4 °C using Centrifuge 5810 R (Eppendorf, Germany) and/or Centrifuge Z 36 HK (HERMLE, Germany) to remove cells and debris. Before PEG addition, the clarified samples were passed through nylon mesh to eliminate coarse particulates. The resulting supernatants were combined with PEG 6000 (w/v) and incubated at 4 °C for 12–24 h to promote PDENs precipitation, after which vesicles were pelleted by centrifugation at 8,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. Pellets were resuspended in PBS using matrix-specific volumes10 mL for RFO-PDENs, 30 mL for RFS-PDENs, and 30 mL for RFF-PDENsyielding visibly robust pellets in all three matrices (Figure ).

RFF samples were first clarified using standard filter paper, whereas RFO and RFS samples were passed through Whatman No. 1 paper. After this, all PDENs preparations were further purified by sequential syringe filtration through 0.45 and 0.22 μm poly­(ether sulfone) (PES) membranes. For the vacuum-filtration workflow, postcentrifugation supernatants were processed through Whatman paper, followed by PES membranes of progressively smaller pore sizes (0.45, 0.22, 0.10, and 0.03 μm). The retained material was washed with phosphate-buffered saline to remove soluble impurities, and the final PDENs-enriched fraction was resuspended in sterile PBS. All PDENs suspensions, irrespective of the isolation route, were stored at 4 °C prior to physicochemical characterization and functional assays.

Characterization of RF-PDENs

Optimization of PEG Concentration Based on PSA

Particle size distribution of RF-PDENs was initially assessed using a Particle Size Analyzer (View Sizer 100, HORIBA Scientific, Japan) (The Research Center for Nanosciences and Nanotechnology, Institut Teknologi Bandung). Samples were dispersed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4) and appropriately diluted to ensure optimal measurement conditions. PSA was first performed on RF-PDENs isolated via vacuum filtration to confirm the presence of nanoscale vesicle populations derived from P. conoideus. Subsequently, PSA analysis was conducted on RF-PDENs isolated using varying PEG concentrations (3%, 5%, 8%, 10%, 12%, and 15%) to evaluate size distribution profiles and determine the optimal isolation conditions. Data interpretation relies on comparing particle size distribution curves, with particle diameter (nm) plotted on the x-axis and relative frequency (%) on the y-axis. Peak (mode)-size- and -number-based mean/median values were used to characterize particle populations. Particle concentration and size uniformity were evaluated using count rate and polydispersity index (PDI) values. As PDENs analysis requires accurate representation of nanoscale populations, interpretation focused primarily on number-based metrics and distribution shape rather than direct comparison with intensity-based DLS Z-average values, which are biased toward larger particles. Detailed quantitative parameters are provided in the Supporting Information, while representative distribution profiles are presented in the main text for qualitative comparison.

Appearance and Morphology

The morphology of RF-PDENs was assessed by transmission electron microscopy (The Research Center for Nanosciences and Nanotechnology, Institut Teknologi Bandung). In brief, RF-PDENs suspensions were applied to carbon-coated copper grids and negatively stained to enhance vesicle contrast for imaging. Excess liquid was removed using filter paper, followed by staining with phosphotungstic acid. The grids were allowed to air-dry prior to imaging. TEM observations were performed using a Hitachi HT7700 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi High-Tech, Japan) operated at an accelerating voltage of 100 kV. Images were acquired in bright-field mode with a representative scale bar of 100 nm, and image orientation was maintained at 0° rotation during acquisition.

Size Distribution and Particle Concentration of Selected RF-PDENs (NTA)

Nanoparticle tracking analysis using the View Sizer 3000 (HORIBA Scientific, Japan) was subsequently performed on RF-PDENs obtained under the optimal PEG concentration selected based on PSA results (Dermama Biotech Lab, Solo-Indonesia). Prior to analysis, the instrument was equilibrated, and the measurement chamber was thoroughly rinsed with ultrapure water, following the manufacturer’s protocol. Calibration was performed using 100 nm polystyrene microsphere standards. After calibration, the chamber was flushed with ultrapure water and loaded with diluted samples, with all samples diluted 50-fold prior to analysis.

Measurements were conducted under controlled temperature conditions. Particle tracking was performed across two predefined size ranges (30–150 nm and 150–300 nm) to capture both small and larger vesicle populations. For each sample, multiple video recordings were acquired under automatic detection settings. Particle size distribution, modal particle size, D10/D50/D90 values, and particle concentration were calculated using the instrument software with logarithmic binning enabled. PBS was used as a diluent control, and background particle counts were subtracted from all sample measurements.

Protein Quantification

Total protein content of RF-PDENs preparations was determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) colorimetric assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. 23227), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Bovine serum albumin standards prepared by serial dilution were used to generate a calibration curve. A fresh BCA working reagent was obtained by combining Reagent A and Reagent B at a 50:1 (v/v) ratio, and 25 μL of each RF-PDENs sample or BSA standard was dispensed into a 96-well plate, followed by 200 μL of working reagent; all measurements were performed in triplicate. After incubation at 37 °C for 30 min, absorbance at 595 nm was recorded, and protein concentrations were interpolated from the standard curve and reported as total protein per sample.

LC-MS-Based Metabolomic Profiling

LC-MS-based metabolomic profiling of RF-PDENs was performed using a Thermo Scientific Vanquish Horizon UHPLC system coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris 240, equipped with an OptaMax NG heated ESI source operating in positive and negative modes with polarity switching. RF-PDENs suspensions (2 mL) were vacuum-concentrated to dryness and reconstituted in 1 mL LC-MS grade methanol, vortexed (3000 rpm, 1 min), centrifuged (12,000 g, 10 min, 4 °C), and the supernatant was filtered through 0.20 μm nylon filters into LC vials. Metabolites were separated on an Accucore Phenyl Hexyl column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 2.6 μm) at 40 °C with a mobile phase of water (0.1% formic acid, A) and acetonitrile (0.1% formic acid, B) at 0.3 mL min–1 using a gradient from 5% to 90% B over 16 min, held for 4 min, then re-equilibrated to 5% B (total run time: 25 min); injection volume was 5 μL for RF-PDENs, pooled QC, and procedural blanks. Full MS/dd-MS2 data were acquired at 60,000 (full MS) and 30,000 (MS2) resolution (m/z 67–1000), with a maximum injection time of 100 ms, intensity threshold of 5000, mass tolerance of 5 ppm, stepped NCE 30/50/70, and source settings of 3500 V/2500 V (positive/negative), sheath gas 35 AU, auxiliary gas 7 AU, sweep gas 1 AU, ion transfer tube at 300 °C, and vaporizer at 320 °C.

Untargeted metabolomics data were processed in Thermo Scientific Compound Discoverer (CD, v3.3) using a standard untargeted workflow with peak detection, RT alignment, and feature grouping applied separately to positive and negative data sets before merging. Peak picking used a minimum intensity of 1.0 × 105 counts and S/N ≥ 3, with RT alignment (maximum shift 0.5 min, 5 ppm mass tolerance) referenced to QC injections. Features present in <50% of samples per group or absent in QCs were removed. Procedural blanks were used for background filtering, retaining only features with at least 3-fold higher mean intensity in RF-PDENs samples and absent/below detection in ≥80% of blanks. Adduct/isotope grouping (5 ppm, RT tolerance 0.05 min) consolidated common adducts ([M + H]+, [M + Na]+, [M–H]−, etc.) and isotopologues into single compounds while clustering coeluting in-source fragments. MS/MS-based identification relied on mzCloud and ChemSpider-linked databases (including flavonoids, lipid, and natural product libraries), with Level 1–2 annotations defined according to MSI/ACS practice. Level 1 required RT and MS/MS agreement with authentic standards (mass error ≤ 3 ppm, MS/MS similarity ≥ 90%), and Level 2 required high-quality library matches (mass error ≤ 5 ppm, similarity ≥ 80%) plus consistent adduct/isotope patterns. Level 3–4 features (tentative class or unknown) were retained for statistics but not structurally interpreted in detail. The analyses included GO enrichment (ranked by protein count and categories with ≥ 2 proteins per pathway), heatmap, and radar (spider) chart (based on hypothesis).

LC-MS-Based Proteomic Analysis

LC-MS-based proteomic analysis of RF-PDENs was carried out on a Thermo Scientific Vanquish Horizon UHPLC system hyphenated to an Orbitrap Exploris 240 high-resolution mass spectrometer equipped with an OptaMax NG heated ESI source. In brief, 0.2 mL of RF-PDENs was mixed with 0.5 mL of RIPA lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA-2Na, 1% Triton X-100) and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. Proteins in the supernatant were precipitated overnight with ice-cold acetone at −20 °C, washed three times with acetone, and resolubilized in 8 M urea. The solution was sonicated on ice, centrifuged again (12,000 g, 15 min, 4 °C), and the resulting supernatant was diluted with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate. Disulfide bonds were reduced with 100 mM dithiothreitol (10 μL, 1 h, 55 °C) and alkylated with 20 mM iodoacetamide (5 μL, 1 h, 37 °C, in the dark), followed by reprecipitation with cold acetone, vacuum concentration, and reconstitution in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate. Proteins were then digested with sequencing-grade trypsin at a 1:50 (w/w) enzyme-to-substrate ratio for 16 h at 37 °C, after which peptides were acidified with trifluoroacetic acid to approximately pH 3, centrifuged (14,000 g, 15 min, 4 °C), filtered through 0.20 μm PVDF membranes, and 10 μL aliquots were injected for LC-MS analysis.

Peptide separation was achieved on a Thermo Scientific Acclaim PepMap 100 C18 column (150 mm × 1.0 mm, 3 μm) held at 30 °C, using water with 0.1% formic acid (mobile phase A) and acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid (mobile phase B) at 75 μL min–1. The LC gradient consisted of an initial hold at 5% B for 1 min, a linear increase from 5% to 50% B over 30 min, a 2-min plateau at 50% B, a ramp to 90% B over 2 min, and re-equilibration at 5% B to 45 min. The Orbitrap Exploris 240 operated in data-dependent mode (Full MS/dd-MS2) with positive ionization, acquiring full MS scans at 120,000 resolution (fwhm at m/z 200) over m/z 350–1200, with a maximum injection time of 100 ms, an intensity threshold of 8000, and selection of precursors with charge states 2–6 within a 1.0 m/z isolation window and 5 ppm mass tolerance. MS2 spectra were recorded at 15,000 resolution using higher-energy collisional dissociation with a normalized collision energy of 30 and nitrogen as the collision gas. The H-ESI source parameters were: a spray voltage of 4000 V, sheath gas at 10 arbitrary units (AU), auxiliary gas at 5 AU, sweep gas at 1 AU, and ion transfer tube temperature of 300 °C.

Raw data sets were processed in Thermo Proteome Discoverer 2.5 using Sequest HT and a workflow aligned with established plant proteomics studies of exosome-like nanovesicles. Spectra were searched against a UniProtKB reference proteome restricted to Viridiplantae (downloaded on a specified date), optionally augmented with a Pandanus conoideus protein set when available, plus a standard contaminants database. A reversed-sequence decoy database was generated for false discovery rate (FDR) estimation. Trypsin was specified as the protease with up to two missed cleavages, carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification, and methionine oxidation and N-terminal acetylation were specified as variable modifications. Peptide-spectrum matches were filtered using a target–decoy strategy to achieve FDR ≤ 1% at both PSM and peptide levels (Percolator), and proteins were reported at ≤1% protein-level FDR. Only high-confidence peptides (Sequest HT cross-correlation scores appropriate for high-resolution data, typically XCorr ≥ 1.9 for z = 2 and ≥2.3 for z = 3, and posterior error probabilities corresponding to ≥95% confidence) were accepted. Proteins included in quantitative analyses were required to be supported by at least two unique, high-confidence peptides, whereas single-peptide identifications were retained only when they showed high-quality MS2 fragmentation, mass error ≤ 5 ppm, and plausible biological relevance. These single-peptide proteins were explicitly flagged as lower confidence and excluded from quantitative comparisons. ,
