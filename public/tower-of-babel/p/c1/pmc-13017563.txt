ChemR23 prevents phenotypic switching of vascular smooth muscle cells into macrophage-like foam cells in atherosclerosis
Authors: Bryce R Evans, Julia Schulz, Vasiliki Triantafyllidou, Anais Yerly, Manovriti Thakur, Nico Angliker, Mark Siegrist, Yvonne Jansen, Yi Yan, Sanne L Maas, Christoph Gold, Floriana M Farina, Batoul Bayer, Alexander Bartelt, Christian Weber, Justus Wettich, Lars Maegdefessel, Nadia Sachs, Marc Schindewolf, Drosos Kotelis, Heidi Noels, Donato Santovito, Emiel P C van der Vorst, Yvonne Döring
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsHaematopoietic ChemR23 deficiency was shown to reduce atherosclerotic lesions by increasing M2 macrophages, but conflicting results in systemically deficient mice suggest a cell-specific function of ChemR23. Therefore, we aimed to study the role of ChemR23 particularly on vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) in atherosclerosis.Methods and resultsMice with a non-haematopoietic cell ChemR23 deficiency due to bone marrow transplantation of apolipoprotein E deficient bone marrow into irradiated ChemR23e/e Apoe−/− double deficient recipient mice (Apoe−/− ►ChemR23e/e Apoe−/−) were fed a western diet (WD) for 6 or 12 weeks. Subsequent analysis revealed an increased lesion size and enhanced VSMC proliferation and VSMC foam cells in Apoe−/− ►ChemR23e/e Apoe−/− mice. Bulk RNA sequencing of adventitia-stripped aortas of Apoe−/− ►ChemR23e/e Apoe−/− mice exposed an increase in gene expression of synthetic VSMC markers such as Lgals3 and Cd68, while contractile genes were downregulated. Likewise, single-cell transcriptome data from advanced human atherosclerotic plaques uncovered the highest ChemR23 expression in contractile VSMCs, while its expression in synthetic VSMCs was markedly reduced. In vitro, treatment of human aortic smooth muscle cells (HASMCs) with α-N-ethyl-3-(4-(trifluoromethyl)phenyl)acetamide (α-NETA), a small molecule inhibitor of ChemR23, increased synthetic gene expression but downregulated expression of transforming growth factor beta (TGFB), ATP Binding Cassette Subfamily A Member 1, ATP binding cassette Subfamily G Member 1, and scavenger receptor Class B Type 1. Further, α-NETA–treated HASMCs downregulated TGFB secretion, increased cholesterol uptake but decreased cholesterol efflux, and showed enhanced cell proliferation. Agonizing ChemR23 with its bona fide ligand chemerin 9 (C9) had no effect on synthetic gene expression but mitigated the effects of α-NETA on gene expression, cholesterol uptake, efflux, and cell proliferation. In vivo, both α-NETA and C9 treatment of Apoe−/− mice over 4 weeks WD revealed therapeutic potential. C9 reduced general inflammatory burden, while α-NETA promoted an atheroprotective M2 macrophage phenotype.ConclusionThese findings suggest a critical role of ChemR23 in regulating VSMC phenotype switching thereby affecting atherosclerosis and suggest ChemR23 as a therapeutic target to either modulate inflammation (C9) or macrophage polarization (α-N-ethyl-3-(4-(trifluoromethyl)phenyl)acetamide; α-NETA) in atherosclerotic disease.Keywords: ChemR23, Atherosclerosis, Vascular smooth muscle cell, Phenotypic switching

Introduction

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) is a major contributor to morbidity and mortality worldwide, leading to clinical events such as myocardial infarction and stroke. Underlying mechanisms of ASCVD involve a complex interplay between endothelial cells (ECs) lining the arterial walls, immune cells infiltrating the sub-endothelial space, and vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) proliferating and migrating from the media to form the fibrous cap of an atherosclerotic lesion.1 While macrophages have long been considered the primary cellular driver of plaque development, recent studies increasingly recognize VSMCs as critical and plastic contributors to both early and advanced stages of atherosclerosis.2 VSMCs, which make up the media, are essential for maintaining vascular tone and regulation of blood pressure via their contractile properties.3 In healthy vessels, they are characterized by spindle-shaped morphology and expression of contractile markers such as smooth muscle alpha actin 2 (ACTA2) and myosin heavy chain 11 (MYH11).4 During atherogenesis, VSMCs undergo phenotypic modulation, characterized by downregulation of contractile genes, increased migratory capacity, and acquisition of synthetic, osteogenic, or even macrophage-like features.5,6 For example, lesional VSMCs may express CD68 and adopt foam cell characteristics due to upregulated scavenger receptors (e.g. CD36) and impaired cholesterol efflux via ATP binding cassette subfamily A member 1 (ABCA1) and ATP binding cassette subfamily G member 1 (ABCG1).7–11 Key regulators of these changes in VSMC phenotype are transforming growth factor beta (TGFB1) and upregulation of the Krüppel-like factor 4 (KLF4).7,8,12

In this context, the chemerin/ChemR23 (CMKLR1) axis has emerged as a potential modulator of both inflammation and vascular function.13 ChemR23 is a G protein–coupled receptor expressed in adipose tissue, liver, pancreatic β-cells, macrophages, and cells of the human and mouse vasculature.14–18 Its primary ligand, chemerin (encoded by RARRES2), is activated via proteolytic cleavage into bioactive forms such as chemerin-9 (C9) and C15 (C15).19–24 C9 in particular binds ChemR23 with high affinity and exerts anti-inflammatory effects, including in regard to vascular inflammation, neurodegeneration, and metabolic disease.25–27 Beyond chemerin, ChemR23 also binds the specialized pro-resolving lipid mediator resolvin E1 (RvE1), promoting leucocyte clearance and limiting inflammation.27–30 For instance, ChemR23 is upregulated by shear stress on ECs, thereby affecting β2 integrin activation via CCRL2, which may mediate monocyte recruitment independent of canonical ligand-receptor signalling.31 Moreover, circulating chemerin levels are elevated in patients with acute coronary syndrome, aortic stenosis, and abdominal aortic aneurysm,16,32 while single nucleotide polymorphisms in the CMKLR1 locus associate with reduced inflammation and interleukin (IL)–6 levels in adipose tissue in obese individuals33,34 ChemR23-mediated signalling thus spans both pro-resolving and potentially pro-atherogenic roles, depending on cellular context, ligand availability and disease stage.

Recent literature has presented further conflicting findings on ChemR23’s role in atherosclerosis. Our own work demonstrated that haematopoietic deletion of ChemR23 skews macrophages towards an M2 phenotype and reduces lesion size, suggesting a pro-inflammatory role for ChemR23 in immune cells.13 In contrast, Laguna-Fernández et al.29 reported that systemic deletion of ChemR23 increased early lesion development, implying atheroprotective effects of ChemR23, particularly in non-haematopoietic cells such as ECs or VSMCs. Furthermore, others reported that C9 treatment reduces lesion burden in Apoe−/− mice, highlighting potential atheroprotective effects of ChemR23 activation.27 Similar contradictions have been observed in studies of vascular calcification and stiffness in obesity.35–37 These discrepancies likely reflect cell- and context-specific roles of ChemR23, particularly in vascular vs. haematopoietic compartments,38,39 as also outlined by research showing that RvE1–ChemR23 interaction reduces proliferation and preserves contractile identity by inhibiting Wnt7a/β-catenin and NF-κB signalling in hypertension.30,34

Taken together, these studies underscore the complexity of ChemR23 biology in atherosclerosis and demand further understanding of its cell-specific roles—particularly in VSMCs—to resolve these conflicting observations and to define ChemR23 as a possible viable therapeutic target in ASCVD. In this study, we specifically investigate the vascular (non-haematopoietic) role of ChemR23 in atherosclerosis, with a focus on its impact on VSMC phenotypic switching, foam cell formation, and lesion development.

Methods

Mice

ChemR23e/e Apoe−/− and Apoe−/− mice as described in our previous study were used to investigate the role of ChemR23 in atherosclerosis.13 The animals were bred in the local animal facility under specific pathogen–freestatus and fed a normal laboratory diet (KLIBA Nafag 3437) before the start of the experimental diet. For atherosclerosis studies, mice were fed a WD containing 21% fat and 0.15–0.2% cholesterol (Sniff TD88137), starting at 8–10 weeks of age for 6 or 12 weeks. Animals were sex and age matched also in adherence to the guidelines as described in the ATVB Council Statement.40 All animal experiments were approved by the local ethical committees (Amt für Veterinärwesen, Kanton Bern, BE105-2020 and BE70-2023, and Regierung von Oberbayern, Sachgebiet 54, Germany), and all procedures conformed to the guidelines from Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Parliament on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes. For bone marrow transplantation (BMT) for non-haematopoietic cell ChemR23 deficiency, bone marrow cells (3 × 106/mouse) from Apoe−/− littermate controls were flushed from the femur and tibia cavities and subsequently administered to ChemR23e/e Apoe−/− and Apoe−/− recipient mice by lateral tail vein injection. This was done immediately after a second lethal dose of whole-body irradiation (2 × 6.5 Gy), while the first was 4 h prior. After 4 weeks of recovery, the mice were placed on WD for 6 or 12 weeks, as described above. For haematopoietic cell ChemR23 deficiency, bone marrow cells (3 × 106/mouse) from ChemR23e/e Apoe−/− mice or from Apoe−/− littermate controls were flushed from femur and tibia cavities and subsequently administered to Apoe−/− recipient mice by lateral tail vein injection 1 day after a lethal dose of whole-body irradiation (2 × 6.5 Gy). After a 4 week recovery, mice were placed on WD for 6 weeks, as described above. To investigate the activation and inhibition of ChemR23 in vivo, Apoe−/− mice were implanted subcutaneously with an osmotic mini-pump (Alzet, Cupertino, CA, USA; model 2004). Pumps contained either 7.7 μg/kg/h of C9 in 10% 2-Hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin (β-cyclodextrin, Merck Darmstadt, Germany) in sterile H2O or 10 mg/kg/day α-NETA (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) in 10% β-cyclodextrin.27,38,41 As a control, Apoe−/− were implanted with pumps containing 10% β-cyclodextrin. Mice were then placed on a WD for 4 weeks before euthanasia and analysis. The doses of C9 and α-NETA were selected based on data from previous studies.27,39,42 For the implant, mice were anaesthetized by intraperitoneal administration of a mixture of fentanyl (0.05 mg/kg), midazolam (5.0 mg/kg), and medetomidine (0.5 mg/kg) mixed in NaCl (0.9%) to a volume of 3 μL/g body weight. The anaesthesia was reversed with a mix of buprenorphine 0.2 mg/kg, atipamezole 2.5 mg/kg, and flumazenil 0.5 mg/kg mixed in NaCl (0.9%) and administered subcutaneously. Generally, mice were euthanized by overdosing with a mix of xylazine (8 mg/kg) and ketamine (100 mg/kg) in sterile water to a volume of 3 μL/g body weight and administered via intraperitoneal injection.

Bulk RNA sequencing

Aortas obtained from six mice from our BMT model fed a WD for 6 weeks, three from Apoe−/−►ChemR23e/eApoe−/−, and three from Apoe−/−►Apoe−/− were used to dissect the aorta under a dissection microscope, preserving the media and intima layers. Isolated aortic segments were promptly stored in RNAlater™ (Merck Darmstadt, Germany #R0901) at 4°C for subsequent RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted by first homogenizing the tissue using a Pellet Pestle™ Cordless Motor (K749540-0000) with Fisherbrand™ RNase-Free Disposable Pellet Pestles (12-141-364) from the media and intima layers using RNeasy Fibrous Tissue Mini Kit (QIAGEN Hilden, Germany #74704). The quantity and quality of the purified total RNA was assessed using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Qubit 3.0 fluorometer with the Qubit RNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA #Q32855) and an Advanced Analytical Fragment Analyzer System using a Fragment Analyzer RNA Kit (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA #DNF-471), respectively. Sequencing libraries were made with input RNA ranging from 270–562 g using an Illumina TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA USA #20020595) in combination with TruSeq RNA UD Indexes Set A (Illumina, 20020492) according to Illumina’s guidelines. The cDNA libraries were evaluated using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Qubit 3.0 fluorometer with the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA #Q32854) and an Agilent Fragment Analyzer (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) with a HS NGS Fragment Kit (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA #DNF-474), respectively. The cDNA libraries were pooled and sequenced with a loading concentration of 300 pM, 50 bp paired-end and dual indexed, using a shared Illumina NovaSeq 6000 S1 Reagent Kit v1.5 (100 cycles; Illumina 20028319), on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 instrument. The quality of the sequencing runs was assessed using Illumina Sequencing Analysis Viewer (Illumina version 2.4.7) and all base call files were demultiplexed and converted into FASTQ files using Illumina bcl2fastq conversion software v2.20. A minimum of 36 million reads/cell were generated for each library. The quality control assessments, generation of libraries and sequencing was carried out at the Generation Sequencing Platform, University of Bern. The reads were mapped to the reference genome using HiSat2 v. 2.2.1.43 featureCounts v. 2.0.144 was used to count the number of reads overlapping with each gene as specified in the genome annotation (Mus_musculus.GRCm38.102). The Bioconductor package DESeq2 v1.36.045 was used to test for differential gene expression between the experimental groups. clusterProfiler v4.4.446 was used to identify gene ontology (GO) terms containing unusually many differentially expressed genes. Gene set enrichment analysis47 was run in clusterProfiler v4.4.446 using gene sets from KEGG48 and MSigDB.49 An interactive Shiny application v1.6.0 (Web Application Framework for R 2022, https://shiny.posit.co.) was set up to facilitate the exploration and visualisation of the RNA-seq results. All analyses were run in R version 4.2.0 (22 April 2022) (R Core Team 2022). The data are available under the GSE309322 accession number, and all differentially expressed genes are also included in a Supplementary material online, Excel.

Histology and immunofluorescence

Atherosclerotic lesion size was assessed by analysing cryosections of the aortic root by staining for lipid depositions with Oil-Red-O or haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining as described.50 In brief, hearts with the aortic root that had been fixed overnight with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) were embedded in Tissue-Tek O.C.T. compound (Sakura, Shinkawa, Chuo-Ku Tokyo, Japan) for cryosectioning. Oil-Red-O+ atherosclerotic lesions were quantified in 4 μm transverse sections and averages were calculated from two to three sections. Aortic arches with the main branch points (brachiocephalic artery, left subclavian artery, and left common carotid artery) were fixed with 4% PFA and embedded in paraffin. Lesion size was quantified after H&E staining of 5 μm longitudinal sections. Masson’s Trichrome staining was used to visualize and quantify total extracellular matrix (ECM) within the plaques.51 To visualize collagen content in atherosclerotic lesions, Picrosirius Red staining was performed on paraffin-embedded aortic root sections using Direct Rot 80 dye (25% dye content, Merck).

To assess the cellular composition or inflammation of atherosclerotic lesions, aortic root sections were blocked with 10% bovine serum albumin (BSA)(1%) horse serum (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany #H0146-10ML) in Phosphate-buffered-saline (PBS) before being stained overnight with a selection of the following antibodies: anti-ICAM1(1:100) (BD Pharmingen, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA clone: 3E2), anti-α-smooth muscle actin (αSMA, 1:1000) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA clone: 1A4), anti-Mac2 (1:400) (BioConcept, Allschwil, Basel-Land, Switzerland clone: M3/38), anti-CD36 (1:100) (Novus Biologicals, Centennial, CO, USA clone: D-2712), anti-ABCA1 (1:100) (Novus Biologicals, Centennial, CO, USA clone: HJ1), anti-CD206 (1:100) (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA clone: MR5D3), and anti-CD68 (1:100) (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA,USA: clone Y1/82A). Co-staining with LipidSpot™ (70065-T, Biotium, Fremont, California, USA) was done for 10 min. Nuclei were counter-stained by 4′,6- Hoechst 33342 (1:2000) (H3570 Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). After incubation with a secondary FITC- or Cy3-conjugated antibody (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, California, USA) or Cy5-conjugated antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Baltimore Pike, West Grove, PA, USA) for 30 min at room temperature, the sections were embedded with Immu-Mount™ (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Analyses were performed using a Nikon ECLIPSE Ti-E fluorescence microscope and Nikon DS-Ri2 camera. Image analysis was performed using Image J software. For each mouse and staining two to three root sections were analysed and data were averaged.

Cholesterol/triglyceride measurements from plasma

Plasma triglyceride (TG) and total cholesterol (TC) levels were quantified using enzymatic assay kits from Roche Diagnostics, Basel Switzerland (Triglycerides: #20767107 322; Cholesterol Gen.2: #03039773 190) and the Calibrator f.a.s. (#10759350 190). Plasma samples were diluted 1:5 in 0.9% NaCl for TG measurements and 1:20 for TC measurements. Measurements were performed in 96-well flat-bottom plates (Corning, New York, NY, USA #3595) using a Varioskan Lux plate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). No lid was placed on the TG plate during incubation to avoid spill-over. All samples were read and evaluated according to the manufacturer’s guidelines and the established in-house protocol.

Human single-cell RNA-seq dataset analysis

We retrieved single-cell gene expression data from a set of carotid endarterectomy specimens from three patients generated as part of Alsaigh et al.52 and available through Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, accession code: GSE159677). Clinical and demographic characteristics are reported in the derivative publication.52 We followed the analytical pipeline provided by the authors at https://github.com/TorkamaniLab/Carotid.Art.Dis.scRNA_Seq.53 Briefly, we performed the preliminary steps using Monocle54 by filtering and down-sampling the datasets to account for different cell counts between the atherosclerotic core (AC) and proximal adjacent (PA) areas of surgical specimens. From the initial 51 981 cells, we filtered out cells based on the number of genes (<200 or >4000) and mitochondrial RNA content (>10%). The 45 826 retained cells were further down-sampled to achieve an equal cell number (n = 2850) across patients and tissues, to avoid discrepancies in size and number of cells may produce significant analytical biases influencing cluster analysis, particularly in assessing plaque vs. healthy tissue differences. By recombining similar cells and removing small cell clusters (n < 250), the threshold for keeping a partition was that at least 250 cells be present in that partition in both AC and PA samples. After excluding doublets and contaminated droplets, this pipeline resulted in 13 267 cells, consistent with the original publication.53 We normalized the feature expression measurements for each cell by the total expression, multiplied by a scale factor (10 000 by default), and log-transformed the result. We identified the 2000 highly variable genes. A principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on these scaled data, and 50 principal components were selected for the neighbour graph and the uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). Based on marker genes provided by Alsaigh et al.52 and established in the literature, we annotated seven broad cell types: ECs, B cells, macrophages, contractile and synthetic VSMCs, and T and natural killer (NK) cells. Besides the original expression analysis as in Alsaigh et al.,52 we used the zero-preserving adaptively thresholder low-rank approximation (ALRA) imputation algorithm55 to enhance sensitivity by recovering missing values in scRNA-seq data while preserving true biological zeros. The number of non-zero values rose from 6.61 to 32.48% by using k = 57 (automatically chosen).

Human bulk RNA-seq dataset analysis

Bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data from n = 202 human carotid artery plaques were obtained from patients who underwent carotid endarterectomy for high-degree stenosis of the internal carotid recruited in the Munich Vascular Biobank.56 Written informed consent was given by each patient, and the study protocol was approved by the local Regional Ethical Committee, and all the procedures complied with the declaration of Helsinki. The raw bulk RNA-seq data underwent initial quality control and normalization using the edgeR package. Variance-stabilizing transformation and trimmed mean of M-values were applied to remove lowly expressed genes. Outlier detection was conducted at both stages, leading to the removal of several outliers’ post-normalization. PCA revealed batch effects, which were controlled for in downstream analysis. Pairwise correlation coefficients were computed using Spearman’s correlation test on normalized gene expression, using a Python pipeline (version 0.13.1) to load and merge normalized gene expression data with metadata. Linear regression lines with 95% confidence intervals were used for graphical presentation in the scatter plots.

Cell culture

Human aortic smooth muscle cells (HASMCs) (#CC-2571 Biosystems Switzerland AG, Muttenz, Switzerland) and complementing full smooth muscle cell growth medium-2 were purchased from Lonza (Basel, Switzerland #CC-3182). Cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. Upon reaching 80–90% confluence, cells were trypsinized and passaged. Cells used for experiments were between Passages 6 and 9 to maintain the true phenotype between experiments. HASMCs were cultured in 12-well plates (Corning®, New York, NY, USA) at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well or in 96-well plates (Corning®, New York, NY, USA) at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well, ensuring optimal conditions for reaching confluency. The cells underwent treatment with either 100 ng/mL tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α (PeproTech, Cranbury, NJ, USA, #300-01A-100UG) or 100 ng/mL interferon (IFN)-γ (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA #570206) or were treated with the vehicles dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) or Milli-Q H2O to serve as controls, for a duration of 4 h. Subsequently, the initial medium was discarded, and the cells were introduced to a fresh medium containing either 10 μM α-NETA or 100 nM C9, which was maintained overnight. All cell culture experiments were performed using biological replicates, with each replicate representing an independent sample from a separate biological preparation. Differences in replicate numbers between conditions reflect practical limitations such as sample availability and the inclusion of additional treatments in follow-up experiments, but all treated groups were consistently compared with matched untreated controls from the same experimental batches to ensure valid normalization and comparability. Further, cells underwent the following downstream analysis: fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS), reverse transcriptase-polymerase chain reaction (RT–PCR), cell proliferation, multiplex profiling, wound healing assay, cholesterol uptake and efflux assay, and multiplex kinome activity profiling.

Generation and validation of CMLKR1-silenced HASMCs

Short-hairpin RNA (shRNA) sequences targeting distinct regions of the CMLKR1 transcript #274 (shCMKLR1#274—TRCN0000011274) and #733 (shCMKLR1#733—TRCN0000367733) or a scrambled control (shSCR—SHC002V) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA. Lentivirus containing pLKO.1-shRNA plasmids targeting ChemR23 (CMKLR1) were produced by transfecting packaging cells (293T) according to a standard co-transfection system with CaCl2 method, as previously applied.57 Viral supernatants were concentrated via polyethylene glycol precipitation, and viral titres were measured using quantitative PCR (qPCR) Lentivirus Titer Kit (ABM, Richmond, BC, CA, USA Cat. # LV900), following the manufacturer’s instructions. For silencing CMLKR1 in HASMCs, cells were transduced with purified lentiviral particles and selected with puromycin (1 µg/mL) for 48 h. Knockdown efficiency was assessed by qPCR and immunofluorescence.

Further, transduced HASMCs were seeded in 12-well chamber slides (Viatris, Canonsburg, PA, USA 81201-IBI) at 1 × 105 cells/well. Following overnight culture, cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min at room temperature, permeabilized using 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min, and blocked with 10% BSA (1%) horse serum (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany H0146-10ML) in PBS before being stained with a primary antibody against anti-CMKLR1 (Lucerna Chem, Luzern, Switzerland #ab230442, 1:100) 1 h at room temperature. Slides were washed and incubated with a Cy5-conjugated antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch), nuclei were counter-stained with 4′,6-Hoechst 33342 (1:2000) (H3570 Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). Images were acquired using a Nikon ECLIPSE Ti-E fluorescence microscope and Nikon DS-Ri2 camera. Image analysis was performed using Image J software.

Flow cytometry

Ethylenediaminetetraacetate (EDTA)–buffered blood (Sarstedt, Nümbrecht, Germany) was obtained from Apoe−/−► Apoe−/− control or Apoe−/− ► ChemR23e/e Apoe−/− mice and selected blood parameters were determined using an automated blood cell counter ProcyteDx (Idexx). Blood then underwent erythrocyte lysis and blocked in PBS containing 2% BSA (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), 2% mouse serum (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 2% rabbit serum (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), and 2% human serum (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), before 10 min incubation with fluorescently labelled antibodies (1:100) against CD45 (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). clone: 30-F11, BUV395), CD80 (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). clone: 16-10A1, BUV737), Gr-1 (Ly-6G/Ly-6C) (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA clone: RB6-8C5, Brilliant Violet 421), CD4 (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). clone: RM4-5, V500), CD8a (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). clone not specified, BV711), CD3e (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA clone: 145-2C11, FITC), CD45R (B220) (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA clone: RA3-6B2, PE-Cy7), CD115 (c-fms) (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA, clone: AFS98, PE), CD11b (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA clone: M1/70, APC), and counting beads (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA), followed by a 5 min incubation with the alive/dead cell marker 7-aminoactinomycin D (1:1000) (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) to identify live leucocytes. Cells were maintained in a FACS buffer of Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS; Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA: 14175129) containing 0.075% BSA (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and 37.5 µM EDTA (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). The data were analysed using the FlowJo software and the absolute count was obtained using the counting beads.

To study intracellular TGFB1 expression in HASMCs, cells were gently dislodged using an enzyme-free Cell Dissociation Buffer in HBSS (Gibco, St. Louis, MO, USA 13150016). The cells were then fixed in 4% PFA and rendered permeable with a 1% Triton™ X-100 (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany 1086431000) solution. For the specific detection of TGFB1 at the intracellular level, cells were stained using the TGFB1 monoclonal antibody (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA clone: TB21, 1:200). The secondary antibody was goat anti-mouse IgG Alexa 647 (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA 1:500). The analytical phase was conducted utilizing the FACS SORP LSR II, with subsequent data analysis performed in FlowJo™ Software (version 10, BD Life Sciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA).

Generation of murine bone marrow–derived macrophages

Murine bone marrow–derived macrophages (BMDMs) were generated from the femur and tibia of Apoe−/− mice. The bones were cut at both ends, and bone marrow was flushed out with ice-cold sterile PBS using a 10 mL syringe with a 25G needle. The cells were dispersed by gentle pipetting, filtered through a 100 µm strainer, and centrifuged at 220 g for 5 min. The cells were resuspended and maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA 21870076) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA #F7524), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA 15140122), 1% GlutaMAX™ (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA 35050061), 10 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acidbuffer (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany #83264), and 50 ng/mL macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) (PeproTech, Cranbury, NJ, USA Cat. no. 315-02) and seeded in large petri dishes for suspension culture (Greiner, St. Gallen, Sitzerland Cat. no. 7.639 161). Macrophages were differentiated for 9 days under these conditions in a humidified 5% CO2, 95% air incubator at 37°C with regular medium changes. Adhered macrophages were detached by incubation for 5 min at 37°C with pre-warmed, sterile Lidocaine solution [4 mg/mL Lidocaine (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany Cat. no. L5647-15G)/10 mM EDTA/PBS (without Ca2+ and Mg2+)] after having washed the cells with PBS. The cells were then harvested, washed, counted, and seeded in 10 cm petri dishes (Greiner, St. Gallen, Sitzerland Cat. no. 7.633 181) for suspension culture (3 × 106 cells/dish) and incubated overnight. For stimulation, cells were treated either with 10 μM α-NETA58,59 100 nM C9,59,60 or corresponding vehicles only (control) in full growth medium overnight. For flow cytometry, macrophages were harvested and then stained (1:100) for CD11b (clone M1/70, APC-conjugated; Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA 17-0112-82), CD206 (clone MR6F3, PE-conjugated; Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA,USA 12-2061-82), F4/80 (clone BM8, eFluor 450-conjugated; Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA 48-4801-82), CD301 (clone MGL1/MGL2, PE-Cy7-conjugated; Lucerna Chem, Luzern, Switzerland #145706), MHC class II (BV711-conjugated), CD80 (BUV737-conjugated; BD Biosciences,Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA #612773), and CD45 (BUV395-conjugated; BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA #564279).

RNA extraction and RT–qPCR

HASMCs were seeded in a 12-well plate (Corning®) at 1 × 105 cells per well, allowed to grow until confluency, and maintained in full medium. The cells underwent treatment with either 100 ng/mL TNF-α (PeproTech, 300-01A-100UG) or 100 ng/mL IFN-γ (BioLegend, 570206) or were treated with the vehicles DMSO or Milli-Q H2O to serve as controls, for 4 h. Subsequently, the initial medium was discarded, and the cells were introduced to a fresh medium containing either 10 μM α-NETA or 100 nM C9, which was maintained overnight. RNA was isolated using the Promega RNA Miniprep (Z6011) Systems and quantified using a NanoDrop™ 2000/2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). RNA (200 ng) was then converted to cDNA using a reverse transcriptase enzyme, iScript (Bio-Rad 1708891). RT–qPCR was performed using custom TaqMan™ probes for 18S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) (Hs03003631_g1), ABCG1 (Hs00245154_m1), ABCA1 (Hs01059101_m1), cluster of differentiation 68 (CD68; Hs02836816_g1), collagen Type I alpha 1 (COL1A1; Hs00164004_m1), LGALS3 (Galectin 3; Hs00173587_m1), KLF4 (Hs00358836_m1), CD36 (scavenger receptor Class B Member 3; Hs00354519_m1), scavenger receptor Class B Type 1 (Hs00969821_m1), and transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGFB1; Hs00998133_m1) in combination with the TaqMan™ mastermix (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA #4461882) with quantification performed in a QuantStudio 6 system (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA), loading 20 ng of cDNA. The relative gene expression levels were calculated using the comparative cycle treshold method, and the data were normalized to the expression of a housekeeping gene 18S.61

Cell proliferation

The proliferation of HASMCs was evaluated using the CellTrace™ CFSE Cell Proliferation Kit from Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA (#C34554). Initially, 18 µL of DMSO was added to a vial of CellTrace CFSE staining solution to prepare the stock solution. This was further diluted with 20 mL of PBS (pre-warmed to 37°C) to achieve a final concentration of 5 µM for the staining solution. HASMCs, at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/mL, were centrifuged for 5 min at 220 g, and the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellet was then resuspended in 10 mL of the prepared CellTrace CFSE staining solution and incubated for 20 min in a 37°C water bath. Following incubation, the cells were centrifuged again for 5 min at 300 g, resuspended in complete HASMC growth medium (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) and seeded into a 12-well plate at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL per well. The cells underwent treatment with either 100 ng/mL TNF-α (PeproTech, Cranbury, NJ, USA #300-01A-100UG) or 100 ng/mL IFN-γ (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA #570206) or were treated with the vehicles DMSO or Milli-Q H2O to serve as controls, for a duration of 4 h. Subsequently, the initial medium was discarded, and the cells were introduced to a fresh medium containing either 10 μM α-NETA or 100 nM C9, which was maintained overnight. Following treatment, cells were incubated for 24 h at 37°C. Subsequently, cells were harvested using enzyme-free Cell Dissociation Buffer in HBSS (Gibco, part of Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and fixed in 4% PFA. The final analysis was conducted using a flow cytometer SORP LSR II, employing a 488 nm excitation wavelength and emission filters suitable for fluorescein detection, and analysed in FlowJo (v10).

Wound healing assay

The effect of TNF-α, IFN-γ, α-NETA, and C9 on cell migration in HASMCs was determined via a scratch assay using an IncuCyte system (IncuCyte S3_Live-Cell Analysis System, Fluorescence microscope). HASMCs were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well and allowed to grow until confluency and then serum starved for 24 h. The cells were then treated with TNF-α (100 ng/mL) or IFN-γ (100 ng/mL) for 4 h before being washed (PBS) and treated with α-NETA (10 μM), C9 (100 nM), or left untreated as a control overnight. A scratch was manually created in each well, using a wound maker. Cells were washed with serum-free medium to remove cell debris and treated with growth medium or serum-free medium, the latter acting as a negative control. The plate was placed in the IncuCyte system for live-cell imaging. Images were captured at 1 h intervals over 24 h and analysed to calculate the percentage of wound closure using the IncuCyte® Software (v2020C).

Cholesterol uptake and efflux assay

The Cholesterol Efflux Assay Kit of Merck/Sigma-Aldrich (Cat. No. MAK192) was used in combination with HASMCs, and it was processed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, HASMCs were seeded in a white 96-well plate (NUNC 136101) at 1 × 104 cells per well, allowed to adhere. The cells underwent treatment with either 100 ng/mL TNF-α (PeproTech, Cranbury, NJ, USA #300-01A-100UG) or 100 ng/mL IFN-γ (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA #570206) or were treated with the vehicles DMSO or Milli-Q H2O to serve as controls, for 4 h. Subsequently, the initial medium was discarded, and the cells were introduced to a fresh medium containing either 10 μM α-NETA or 100 nM C9, which was maintained overnight. After washing with serum-free medium, the cells were incubated with labelled cholesterol for 1 h, washed, and treated with human serum (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA #H4522) as a cholesterol acceptor for 4 h. After the incubation, the supernatants were transferred to a new plate, and fluorescence was measured at 482 nm excitation and 515 nm emission. The cell layer was solubilized with the provided cell lysis buffer and incubated on a shaker for 30 min, after which the fluorescence was measured at 482 nm excitation and 515 nm emission. The percentage of cholesterol efflux was calculated using the formula: 100 × [fluorescence intensity of the medium/(fluorescence intensity of the medium + cell lysate)].

For cholesterol uptake, the procedure was identical as above, with the only difference that the cell layer was washed with serum-free medium and then left with 100 μL of serum-free medium while measuring the fluorescence at 482 nm excitation and 515 nm emission. The percentage of cholesterol uptake was calculated using the formula: 100 × [fluorescence intensity of the cell layer/(fluorescence intensity of the medium + cell layer)].

Collagen production

HASMCs were treated with either 100 ng/mL TNF-α (PeproTech, 300-01A-100UG) or 100 ng/mL IFN-γ (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA #570206) or were treated with the vehicles DMSO or Milli-Q H2O to serve as controls, for 4 h. Subsequently, the initial medium was discarded, and the cells were introduced to a fresh medium containing either 10 μM α-NETA or 100 nM C9, which was maintained overnight. Collagen production by HASMCs was analysed in supernatant using a commercial Sircol™ Collagen Assay Kit (BioVendor R&D, Brno Czech Republic #S1000). Potential for collagen degradation was measured using the EnzChek™ Gelatinase/Collagenase Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA,USA #E12055) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Western blot

HASMCs were lysed in RIPA buffer (1% Triton X-100, 1%; 1% sodium deoxycholate; 0.1% SDS; 150 nM NaCl; 10 nM Tris, pH 7.4), supplemented with phosphoSTOP™ (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) and cOmplete™ Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min and then centrifuged at 14 000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA,USA). Five µg of protein lysates were resolved by SDS-PAGE using Bolt™ 4–12% Bis-Tris Plus gels with a 3-(N-morpholino)propanesulfonic acidSDS running buffer (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, California, USA) under reducing conditions. Proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) using the Trans-Blot Turbo™ transfer system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA in tris-buffered saline with tween 20 (TBS-T) for 1 h at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies against: extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK1)/2 (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA, #AF1576, 1:1000), Phospho-ERK1/2 (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA #AF1018, 1:1000), and GAPDH (Santa Cruz, Dallas, Texas, USA #sc-365062, 1:2500) diluted in blocking buffer overnight at 4°C. After washing, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-Rabbit IgG (Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA, USA #7074S, 1:2500) in TBS-T for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were developed using SuperSignal™ West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA), and chemiluminescence was visualized in a ChemiDoc™ Imaging System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Multiplex bead array analysis

Supernatant from cultured cells or plasma from blood samples was collected and analysed using either standard Legendplex assay kits (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA #740446 and #740450) to quantify the concentrations of multiple cytokines and chemokines simultaneously. This was performed according to the manufacturer protocol and samples were analysed using the Cytek® Aurora flow cytometry platform. Results were quantified against a standard curve using the Qognit software provided by BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA.

Multiplex kinome activity profiling

Serine–threonine kinase (STK) profiles were determined using the PamChip® microarray platform with the PamStation®12 (PamGene International, ‘s-Hertogenbosch, Netherlands). Protein was first isolated from HASMCs cultured in 12-well plates (Corning, New York, NY, USA) at a density of 1 × 105 and treated with either 10 μM α-NETA or 100 nM C9 overnight. To isolate the protein HASMC cells were exposed to the lysis buffer (M-PER™ Mammalian Protein Extraction Reagent (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA,USA, Cat # 78501) containing protease inhibitors (Halt™ Protease Inhibitor Cocktail; Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA,USA, Cat # 78425) and phosphatase inhibitors (Halt™ Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail; Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA,USA, Cat # 78428). Per array, 2 μg of protein and 400 μM ATP were added. An antibody mix was supplemented to the STK chip to detect the phosphorylated sites. Phosphorylation intensity was quantified (and corrected for local background) using the BioNavigator software (version 6.3, PamGene International, ‘s-Hertogenbosch, Netherlands). Upstream Kinase Analysis (functional scoring method, PamGene, ‘s-Hertogenbosch, Netherlands), was used to rank kinases based on combined specificity scores (peptides linked to a kinase, derived from six databases) and sensitivity scores (treatment control differences). The median final score of the kinase with a score >1.2 and with adjusted P-values for multiple comparisons by the false discovery rate of <0.05 are depicted in a heat map. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed on the differentially expressed kinases (median final scores >1.2 and adjusted P-value <0.05) using the R package cluster Profiler 4.0.46 The R package disease ontology semantic and enrichment analysis62 was utilized to analyse the biological complexities in which these kinases correlate with multiple annotation categories, which was visualized in a network plot with the help of the R package Reactive Pathway Analysis (ReactomePA) v1.44.0.63

Statistics

Data are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Data were first tested for a normal distribution before being compared via unpaired Student’s t-test between two groups for normally distributed data or Mann–Whitney U test for non-parametric data. Comparison of more than two groups was performed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Holm–Šídák’s post-hoc test, or Kruskal–Wallis H with Dunn’s post-hoc test for non-parametric data. Analysis of more than three groups with multiple variances was subjected to a two-way multivariate analysis of variance (ANOVA). A P < 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference. Sequencing data: A Spearman correlation analysis was performed to assess the relationship between two genes, from the human bulk RNA-seq data, followed by the generation of a regression plot with a 95% confidence interval. Mouse Bulk RNA-seq was analysed via the Benjamini–Hochberg method to adjust P-values in GO pathway analysis for bulk RNA-seq data.

Results

Non-haematopoietic cell ChemR23 deficiency increases atherosclerotic plaque size and VSMC foam cell content after 6 and 12 weeks WD

To investigate the role of ChemR23 on non-haematopoietic cells during atherogenesis, we performed BMT by injecting apolipoprotein E deficient (Apoe−/−) bone marrow into lethally irradiated Apoe−/− (Apoe−/−►Apoe−/−) or Apoe−/− ChemR23-deficient (Apoe−/−►Apoe−/− ChemR23e/e) recipients. Mice were placed on a western diet (WD) for either 6 or 12 weeks (Figure 1A; see Supplementary material online, Figure S1A). Body weight, leucocyte counts, and plasma lipid levels were assessed but did not differ between groups at 6 and 12 weeks WD (see Supplementary material online, Tables S1 and S2). However, atherosclerotic plaque size was significantly increased in both the aortic root and arch in ChemR23-deficient recipients at both timepoints (Figure 1B and C; see Supplementary material online, Figure S1B and C). At 6 weeks, the percentage of Mac2+ cells and ICAM1 expression in the aortic root were unaltered (Figure 1D and E). After 12 weeks, both parameters were modestly decreased in ChemR23-deficient animals (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1D and E). In contrast, α-αSMA–positive VSMCs were significantly augmented in lesions of Apoe−/−►Apoe−/− ChemR23e/e mice at both 6 and 12 weeks of WD feeding (Figure 1F; see Supplementary material online, Figure S1F), suggesting that loss of ChemR23 expression on VSMCs may enhance their proliferation or their migration into the intima. To this end, we examined the proportion of VSMC-derived foam cells identified by co-staining for αSMA and LipidSpot™, and found them to be significantly higher in ChemR23-deficient lesions at both timepoints (Figure 1G; see Supplementary material online, Figure S1G). ECM content, assessed by Masson’s Trichrome staining, was increased in aortic root plaques of Apoe−/−►Apoe−/− ChemR23e/e animals compared with control mice after 6 weeks WD feeding (Figure 1H). To more specifically assess collagen deposition, we performed Sirius Red staining, which did not reveal any significant difference in collagen content between the groups (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1H and I). These findings suggest that the increased blue staining observed in Masson’s Trichrome is likely to reflect general ECM accumulation rather than a true increase in fibrillar collagen. Taken together, these results indicate an important role of ChemR23 in orchestrating VSMC phenotype and their contribution to atherogenesis at early and advanced stages.

Murine ChemR23-deficient VSMCs exhibit a gene expression profile resembling that of a synthetic VSMC phenotype

To further investigate the role of ChemR23 in VSMC phenotype regulation, we performed bulk RNA-seq on adventitia-stripped aortas from Apoe−/−►Apoe−/− ChemR23e/e and Apoe−/−►Apoe−/−, control mice after 6 weeks of WD feeding (n = 3 per group). Differential gene expression analysis revealed significant transcriptional changes in ChemR23-deficient aortas, with pathway enrichment pointing to altered cholesterol metabolism, TGF-β signalling, and pathways relevant to ASCVD (see Supplementary material online, Figure S2A). A more detailed analysis at the specific transcript level revealed increased expression of genes associated with VSMC phenotype switching like Lgals3 and Cd68, and a concurrent decrease in markers of the contractile VSMC phenotype, such as Timp4 and Myocd (see Supplementary material online, Figure S2B). Expression of genes involved in VSMC foam cell formation, including Lgals3, was also upregulated (see Supplementary material online, Figure S2C). Analogous, pro-migratory and pro-proliferative transcripts such as Pdgfrb and Col1a1 were significantly increased in the ChemR23-deficient group (see Supplementary material online, Figure S2D). These transcriptomic findings are consistent with increased phenotypic modulation and foam cell–like behaviour of VSMCs in the absence of ChemR23, as also observed at the histological level in Figure 1.

Contractile VSMCs reveal the highest expression of ChemR23 in human lesions

To explore the relevance of our findings in human disease, we analysed ChemR23 expression in a single-cell transcriptome dataset of advanced human carotid atherosclerotic lesions (GSE159677).52 Using UMAP, we identified major vascular and immune cell populations, including VSMCs, ECs, macrophages, T cells, and NK cells, based on canonical marker gene expression (Figure 2A and B). Within the VSMC population, two distinct sub-clusters corresponding to contractile and synthetic phenotypes were identified. These were defined by the expression of established markers (contractile: ACTA2, MYH11, TAGLN, and CARMN; synthetic: KLF4, CD68, DCN, and COL1A1) (Figure 2B). Further, analysis of CMKLR1 (ChemR23) expression revealed that expression was highest in contractile VSMCs, lower in synthetic VSMCs and lymphoid cells, and intermediate in macrophages and ECs (Figure 2C and D). Compared with adjacent not-diseased surgical specimens, CMKLR1 (ChemR23) expression was higher in VSMCs and ECs within the atherosclerotic lesion, while it was lower in lesional macrophages (Figure 2E). A relevant expression of CMKLR1 was also confirmed in another independent dataset of human carotid atherosclerotic lesions (GSE253904, Supplementary material online, Figure S2E and F). These single-cell transcriptomic data confirm that ChemR23 is enriched in contractile VSMCs and provide human evidence supporting a potential role in VSMC phenotypic modulation.

ChemR23 inhibition promotes phenotypic switching and downregulates TGFβ1 in human VSMCs

To examine the role of ChemR23 in human VSMCs, we treated HASMCs in vitro with α-NETA (a ChemR23 antagonist42), C9 (a ChemR23 agonist20,27), or vehicle control. Treatments were applied either at baseline or after 4-hour pre-activation with TNF-α or IFN-γ to mimic an inflammatory environment. Real-time qPCR analysis revealed that α-NETA treatment increased KLF4 expression under all conditions, while C9 alone did not alter KLF4 levels but prevented α-NETA–induced KLF4 upregulation (Figure 3A). We next analysed the expression of CD68 and LGALS3 (Mac2), genes previously found to be upregulated in ChemR23-deficient murine aortas. α-NETA significantly increased both transcripts at baseline (Figure 3B and C). C9 alone had no significant effect, and co-treatment with C9 suppressed the α-NETA threonine kinase induced upregulation of CD68 (Figure 3B and C). Due to its upstream role in maintaining a contractile phenotype, we also examined the expression of TGFB1.64,65 α-NETA reduced TGFB1 messenger RNA (mRNA) expression across all conditions, including baseline and cytokine-activated cells, while C9 had no effect alone but restored TGFB1 levels when co-administered with α-NETA (Figure 3D). Flow cytometric analysis of intracellular TGFβ1 protein and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay measurement of secreted TGFβ1 confirmed reduced protein levels following α-NETA treatment, which were maintained in the presence of C9 (Figure 3E and F). To assess potential changes in collagen regulation downstream of ChemR23 signalling, we examined COL1A1 mRNA expression, collagen production, and degradation in α-NETA–treated HASMCs. None of these parameters were significantly altered compared with vehicle-treated cells (see Supplementary material online, Figure S4A–C). These findings are consistent with in vivo Sirius Red staining (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1H and I), which revealed no significant difference in fibrillar collagen content between groups. Since downregulation of TGFB1 is also known to abolish a contractile phenotype in VSMCs,66 we explored downstream signalling by multiplex kinase activity profiling. Here, α-NETA treatment was associated with increased ERK1/2 activity, while C9 promoted AKT1/2 activation (see Supplementary material online, Figure S3A). These findings were consistent with our RNA-seq data from murine aortas, which showed downregulation of genes associated with AKT signalling and predominant upregulation of genes linked to the ERK1/2 cascade in ChemR23-deficient samples (see Supplementary material online, Figure S3B and C).

Taken together, these data demonstrate that ChemR23 inhibition in human VSMCs promotes a synthetic transcriptional programme, characterized by ERK1/2 activation, reduced TGFβ1 signalling, and increased expression of markers such as KLF4, CD68, and LGALS3, without directly altering collagen production. These results align with our in vivo findings and support the role for ChemR23 in maintaining contractile VSMC identity.

ChemR23 silencing promotes a synthetic VSMC phenotype via ERK1/2 signalling and alters key gene expression regulators

To complement our pharmacological studies, we generated a loss-of-function CMKLR1 model in HASMCs by lentiviral transduction of two shRNAs targeting distinct regions of the mRNA transcript (sh#274 and sh#733). Validation of the model was obtained at the RNA level by qPCR, showing a robust reduction in CMKLR1 mRNA compared with scrambled short hairpin (SCR) shRNA-transduced controls (shSCR; see Supplementary material online, Figure S5A), and at the protein level by immunofluorescence, demonstrating a marked decrease in ChemR23 signal in HASMCs transduced with both shRNAs, when compared with shSCR control (see Supplementary material online, Figure S5B). Based on our prior in vivo and pharmacological findings, we next assessed whether ChemR23 knockdown alone modulates the expression of key genes involved in VSMC phenotypic switching. As measured by qPCR, CMKLR1 silencing significantly reduced MYOCD expression, a critical transcription factor maintaining contractile VSMC identity, while KLF4, CD68, and LGALS3 were robustly upregulated (Figure 4A–D). Consistent with our in vivo data, these results suggest a shift towards a synthetic/foam cell–like phenotype. This was further corroborated by western blot analysis, which showed increased phospho-ERK1/2 in HASMCs upon CMKLR1 silencing compared with shSCR-transduced cells (Figure 4E), thus further validating our results obtained with the multiplex kinase activity assay upon α-NETA treatment.

ChemR23 inhibition promotes VSMC proliferation but not migration in vitro

Given the increased lesional VSMC content observed in ChemR23-deficient mice (Figure 1F), combined with the ERK1/2 activation upon ChemR23 knockdown (Figure 4E), we next assessed whether ChemR23 signalling influences VSMC migration or proliferation. HASMCs were treated with α-NETA or C9 at baseline or following cytokine activation and subjected to wound healing and proliferation assays. Wound closure assessed by IncuCyte imaging over 24 h revealed no significant effect of α-NETA, C9, or their combination on HASMC migration compared with vehicle-treated controls. Migration was only affected under serum-free conditions, as expected (see Supplementary material online, Figure S6A–C).

In contrast, α-NETA treatment significantly enhanced HASMC proliferation, both at baseline and under inflammatory conditions, as measured by carboxyflurorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (CFSE)dilution via flow cytometry. This proliferative effect was attenuated by co-treatment with C9, while C9 alone had no significant impact (Figure 5A and B). These results are supported by pathway enrichment analysis from our kinase activity profiling, which identified several α-NETA–responsive kinases involved in proliferation (see Supplementary material online, Tables S3 and S4).

Together, these data suggest that ChemR23 signalling restrains VSMC proliferation, and that its disruption may contribute to increased VSMC accumulation observed in vivo.

Inhibition of ChemR23 on human aortic VSMCs modifies LDL uptake and efflux

To test whether ChemR23 signalling regulates VSMC foam cell formation, as suggested by our in vivo findings (Figure 1G), we assessed cholesterol uptake and efflux in HASMCs treated with α-NETA, C9, or both—at baseline and after pre-activation with TNF-α or IFN-γ.

Cholesterol uptake was significantly increased in α-NETA–treated HASMCs while C9 treatment alone did not alter uptake, but co-treatment with C9 effectively blocked the α-NETA–induced increase (Figure 6A). CD36, a key scavenger receptor implicated in lipid uptake, was also upregulated by α-NETA at the mRNA level, an effect again neutralized by co-treatment with C9 (Figure 6B). Pro-inflammatory cytokines did not further increase CD36 expression, suggesting a ceiling effect under these conditions. In line with these in vitro findings, VSMCs in aortic root lesions of and Apoe−/−►Apoe−/− ChemR23e/e mice showed a significant upregulation of CD36 protein expression compared with VSMCs of Apoe−/−►Apoe−/− animals (Figure 6C), reinforcing a regulatory role for ChemR23 in modulating cholesterol uptake by VSMCs in vivo.

In contrast, α-NETA treatment markedly reduced cholesterol efflux capacity in HASMCs, independent of inflammatory stimulation (Figure 7A). Co-treatment with C9 failed to rescue efflux in resting cells but successfully restored efflux capacity in activated HASMCs, underscoring an active role for ChemR23 in preserving efflux under inflammatory stress. This effect was mirrored at the gene expression level. α-NETA suppressed the expression of the HDL receptor SR-BI, and the efflux transporters ABCA1 and ABCG1, while C9 restored their expression (Figure 7B–D). Inflammatory stimulation did not amplify these effects, again suggesting a saturation point in gene regulation. Consistently, ABCA1 protein expression was reduced in lesional VSMCs of ChemR23-deficient mice in vivo (Figure 7E), supporting a functional role for ChemR23 in maintaining cholesterol efflux capacity.

Finally, analysis of transcriptomic data from human carotid plaques (Munich Vascular Biobank56) revealed strong positive correlations between CMKLR1 (ChemR23) and ABCA1 (ρ = +0.32, P < 0.001) as well as ABCG1 (ρ = +0.57, P < 0.001) (Figure 7F and G). Systemic ChemR23-targeting differentially affects lesion development and cellular composition

Previous work from our group demonstrated that ChemR23 promotes atherosclerosis via its actions on haematopoietic cells—particularly macrophages and plasmacytoid dendritic cells,13 whereas our current findings uncover a protective role for ChemR23 in VSMCs. These results highlight the importance of cell-specific analyses to fully understand the diverse functions of ChemR23 in atherosclerosis. To investigate the therapeutic potential of systemic ChemR23 modulation, we implanted osmotic mini-pumps subcutaneously in adult Apoe−/− mice delivering either vehicle, the ChemR23 agonist C9, or the antagonist α-NETA,27,38,41 followed by 4 weeks of WD feeding (Figure 8A). While body weight, leucocyte counts, and lipid profiles remained unaffected (see Supplementary material online, Table S5), C9 treatment significantly reduced atherosclerotic lesion size in the aortic root, whereas α-NETA showed a trend towards reduction (Figure 8B). Despite the observed decrease in lesion size, the composition of atherosclerotic plaques was affected differently by each treatment. While Mac2+ cells remained unchanged across groups (Figure 8C), VSMC numbers were unaltered in C9-treated mice but significantly reduced in α-NETA–treated animals (Figure 8D). This contrasts with the increase in VSMCs observed in our non-haematopoietic cell ChemR23-deficient model (Figure 1F). To investigate this discrepancy, we quantified VSMC-derived foam cells and found them significantly reduced in α-NETA–treated mice (Figure 8E). These findings suggest that systemic ChemR23 inhibition impacts VSMC behaviour differently than vascular-specific ChemR23 loss, potentially due to overriding effects on immune cells. Following this hypothesis, we revisited our earlier finding that ChemR23 deficiency in macrophages is atheroprotective fostering enhanced cholesterol efflux and an M2 phenotype13 and treated Apoe−/− BMDMs with C9 and α-NETA. Supporting our previous findings, α-NETA treatment of Apoe−/− bone marrow–derived macrophages upregulated CD206 (a marker of M2 polarization) in vitro (Figure 8F), a phenotype we also confirmed in aortic root lesions of treated animals (Figure 8G). Moreover, re-analysis of our previously published data on haematopoietic ChemR23 deficiency13 revealed similar reductions in VSMC and VSMC-derived foam cell numbers in vivo (see Supplementary material online, Figure S7), reinforcing that macrophage phenotype dominates plaque composition under systemic ChemR23 blockade.

Interestingly, C9 treatment reduced lesion size without affecting VSMC or macrophage plaque content (Figure 8B–D). Instead, C9 treatment significantly reduced circulating levels of inflammatory cytokines and atheropromoting mediators, including TNF-α, GM-CSF, IL-1α, and IL-23 (Figure 8H).67–70 In contrast, α-NETA treatment in the same setting did not significantly alter systemic cytokine levels, suggesting that its effects are more cell- or tissue-specific, and likely driven by modulation of local immune cell phenotype rather than systemic inflammation.

These findings illustrate that systemic ChemR23 targeting can beneficially modulate atherosclerosis, but the net outcome is context-dependent. α-NETA reduces VSMC-derived foam cells by affecting the macrophage phenotype, whereas C9 lowers the inflammatory burden without altering plaque cellularity, possibly via vascular or immune cell stabilization. Therefore, precise cell- and context-specific targeting of ChemR23 may offer distinct and complementary therapeutic strategies for ASCVD.

Discussion

The role of the ChemR23 axis in atherosclerosis has remained controversial, with evidence supporting both anti- and pro-atherogenic functions depending on cellular context and disease stage. Previous work from our group demonstrated that haematopoietic deficiency of ChemR23 reduces atherosclerotic lesion size by promoting anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages,13 while other studies have suggested conflicting outcomes upon systemic ChemR23 loss.29 Clinical data further complicate this picture, showing increased ChemR23 protein levels in unstable human carotid plaques without changes in mRNA expression,71 indicative of post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms that are currently poorly understood.

Our present study now provides novel insights by revealing that ChemR23 has a previously unappreciated protective role in lesional VSMCs. Our data show that ChemR23-deficient VSMCs adopt a synthetic, macrophage-like foam cell phenotype in atherosclerotic lesions. Mechanistically, this phenotypic switch is associated with enhanced KLF4 expression and diminished TGFβ signalling, coupled to a switch to ERK1/2 pathway activation (see Supplementary material online, Figure S8), consistent with prior findings on the pro-synthetic effects of ERK signalling.33,72 This data places ChemR23 as a critical regulator of VSMC plasticity and lipid handling,12,73 and aligns with previous work highlighting VSMC plasticity and its central role in lesion progression and instability.74 In addition, elevated KLF4 and reduced MYOCD expression have been consistently associated with a synthetic phenotype in VSMCs, as well as enhanced foam cell formation, underlying their significant contribution to plaque instability.75–78 Furthermore, microRNA-dependent regulation of KLF4 and MYOCD has been implicated in plaque development in hypertension-associated vascular remodelling, linking systemic factors to vascular cell reprogramming.34

The downregulation of TGFB1, a key stabilizer of the contractile phenotype,79 following ChemR23 inhibition by α-NETA suggests that ChemR23 acts as a protective checkpoint against pathological VSMC phenotypic switching. Interestingly, our kinase profiling data demonstrate that C9 and α-NETA treatments selectively activate distinct kinase pathways (AKT vs. ERK), potentially explaining divergent downstream effects observed upon agonist or antagonist engagement of ChemR23.

Beyond transcriptional changes fostering a synthetic VSMC phenotype, increased VSMC proliferation upon ChemR23 inhibition via α-NETA aligns with prior observations highlighting the ERK1/2 signalling pathway as a critical mediator of VSMC proliferation.80 Nevertheless, previous reports have also suggested opposing roles of ChemR23 agonism on proliferation and migration: Sato et al.27 demonstrated that treatment of HASMCs with C9 inhibited platelet derived growth factor (PDGF)-induced proliferation and migration by suppressing ERK1/2 and promoting AKT signalling. In contrast, our data indicate that C9 treatment alone does not significantly alter HASMC proliferation or migration, possibly due to differences in experimental conditions such as the absence of PDGF stimulation. Nevertheless, C9 treatment prevented the pro-proliferative effects of α-NETA, supporting the concept that ChemR23 signalling restricts VSMC proliferation. These findings complement recent studies showing that chemerin/ChemR23 signalling regulates arterial stiffness and vascular tone and reactivity, possibly through effects on calcium handling and smooth muscle contraction pathways.73 The underlying kinase profiles observed in our study (see Supplementary material online, Figure S3A) show that α-NETA increased ERK1/2 activation, whereas C9 preferentially activated AKT signalling without inducing ERK pathways, partially aligning with findings by Sato et al.27 The discrepancies regarding ERK1/2 activation by C9 might be explained by differences in ligand concentration, stimulation duration, and particularly cell type–specific receptor dynamics, as prior studies primarily relied on macrophages or transfected cell lines and not VSMCs.81,82 Additionally, we employed multiplex kinase activity profiling, which simultaneously analyses multiple STK phosphosites, as opposed to the more targeted western immunoblotting used previously. Collectively, these insights reinforce the notion that ChemR23 signalling pathways regulating VSMC proliferation are highly context-dependent, influenced significantly by experimental conditions, cell type specificity, and ligand-receptor dynamics.

Moreover, our findings on lipid metabolism highlight significant reductions in cholesterol efflux mediators ABCA1 and ABCG1 alongside increased CD36 expression upon ChemR23 blockade or deletion. These observations closely mirror data from human carotid plaques, supporting clinical relevance. Consistent with prior evidence linking these mediators to foam cell formation and lesion instability,83,84 our findings strongly support ChemR23's critical regulatory function in lipid homeostasis.

To further explore the therapeutic implications of systemic ChemR23 modulation, we treated Apoe−/− mice with either C9 (ChemR23 agonist) or α-NETA (ChemR23 antagonist) for 4 weeks. Consistent with previous studies,27,85 C9 treatment significantly reduced lesion size, likely due to an overall decrease in systemic inflammatory cytokines; TNF-α, IL-23, IL-1α, and GM-CSF. The latter is consistent with findings from genetic studies showing that individuals with higher ChemR23 protein expression exhibit reduced inflammatory cytokines.33 Interestingly, general plaque characteristics, including Mac2+ cells and VSMC content, were largely unaffected by C9, suggesting that systemic anti-inflammatory effects might be driving lesion reduction independent of plaque composition changes. Surprisingly, systemic α-NETA administration also reduced lesion size and showed a trend towards lower circulating inflammatory cytokines, contradicting our initial findings from non-haematopoietic ChemR23–deficient mice. Notably, α-NETA significantly decreased lesional VSMCs, particularly VSMC-derived foam cells. To resolve this discrepancy, we conducted a detailed analysis of macrophage polarization in these lesions, revealing an increased proportion of anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages following systemic α-NETA treatment, a finding recapitulated in vitro using BMDMs. These results align with previous reports describing ChemR23 as a marker of pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages86 and support earlier work from our group showing that haematopoietic ChemR23 deficiency reduces lesion size through promotion of M2 macrophages.13 We thus propose that systemic ChemR23 inhibition predominantly affects lesion development through macrophage polarization towards an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, indirectly preventing excessive VSMC phenotypic switching and foam cell formation.87 Whether prolonged α-NETA treatment might eventually lead to increased VSMC proliferation and foam cell generation offsetting these initially beneficial effects, requires additional investigation.

Current literature on ChemR23’s role in VSMC phenotype switching is limited and mainly centres on osteogenic differentiation and vascular calcification. Studies by Magnus Bäck’s group88–90 have shown that ChemR23-deficient VSMCs retain a more contractile phenotype and resist phosphate- or vitamin D3-induced calcification. Interestingly, activation of ChemR23 by chemerin or RvE1 also protects against calcification in ChemR23-competent cells and mice. These seemingly opposing findings suggest that ChemR23 deficiency may prevent pro-osteogenic signalling, while its activation could engage alternative protective pathways—highlighting the signalling complexity of ChemR23 and the need for further mechanistic investigation. However, direct comparison with our data is challenging, since only one study examined ChemR23 deficiency on an Apoe−/− background investigating aortic valve calcification, while the others utilized a C57BL/6 background without a pro-atherogenic WD.88–91 Moreover, Carracedo et al.89 did not find differences in Bmp2 mRNA expression, consistent with our bulk RNA-seq data from adventitia-stripped mouse aortas, but reported changes in Runx2 and Acta2 expression, which we did not observe. Collectively, these studies highlight the significance of the ChemR23 axis in osteogenic differentiation of VSMCs; however, its role in macrophage-like phenotypic switching and foam cell formation in VSMCs, as presented in our study, remains unique and cannot be directly extrapolated from the existing osteogenic-focused literature.

Overall, our study underscores the therapeutic potential of selective ChemR23 modulation to concurrently control vascular and inflammatory responses in atherosclerosis. A major strength lies in our integration of multiple complementary methodologies, including murine models, bulk and single-cell RNA-seq, and functional in vitro assays using primary human cells, enhancing the translational relevance and robustness of our conclusions. Given the receptor’s dual role—limiting VSMC-driven plaque instability and promoting anti-inflammatory macrophage phenotypes, specific ChemR23 agonists may stabilize advanced plaques and limit cardiovascular events, particularly in patients with inflammatory or metabolic comorbidities.

However, certain limitations merit consideration. The absence of lineage-tracing experiments represents a significant limitation, potentially introducing bias in interpreting the cellular origin of foam cells within lesions. While our immunofluorescent staining strongly suggests the presence of VSMC-derived foam cells, this approach alone does not unequivocally exclude contributions from other cell types like ECs. Such uncertainty introduces potential bias. The degree of this bias is difficult to quantify without lineage-tracing mouse models, but could lead to an overestimated role of VSMC phenotypic switching and its contribution to lesion development. Further, LGALS3, while commonly used as a macrophage marker, is also expressed by phenotypically modulated VSMCs. This overlap complicates accurate quantification of macrophages within the lesion, as LGALS3⁺ VSMCs may be misclassified as macrophages. As such, our macrophage counts may be overestimated, potentially underrepresenting the contribution of macrophage-like VSMCs.

In addition, our non-haematopoietic cell ChemR23-deficient mouse model introduces potential bias from incomplete cell-specific targeting. As our human single-cell data indicate EC expression of ChemR23 that increases during disease, we cannot exclude EC contributions to observed phenotypes. Such lack of cell type specificity could bias interpretations towards overstating VSMC-specific effects. Conditional cell-specific ChemR23 knockout models are required to precisely delineate these contributions. Moreover, employing α-NETA as a ChemR23 antagonist introduces another potential source of bias due to known off-target effects, especially at higher doses (e.g. ALDH1A1, ChAT modulation).38,58 However, our findings closely mirrored genetic knockout and knockdown data consistent with other models like Experimental Autoimmune Encephalomyelitis.38,58 At the moment α-NETA remains the only commercially available antagonist of ChemR23. Lastly, the relatively short-term intervention period in our systemic ChemR23-targeting experiments could introduce bias related to timing and disease stage specificity. Short-term treatments likely emphasize early-stage inflammatory modulation and macrophage polarization, possibly masking or understating potential long-term consequences such as enhanced VSMC proliferation and foam cell formation. Long-term interventions at different disease stages could be helpful to fully capture the complex, potentially biphasic effects of ChemR23 modulation.

In conclusion, our findings identify ChemR23 as a pivotal regulator of VSMC plasticity and cholesterol handling, two key processes that govern plaque stability in atherosclerosis. By preventing VSMC phenotypic switching and foam cell formation, ChemR23 preserves vascular integrity and may serve as a critical checkpoint in lesion progression. Importantly, ChemR23 blockade in macrophages promotes an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype that dampens foam cell burden and lesion development, while systemic activation with C9 reduces circulating inflammatory cytokines, further limiting vascular inflammation. These results highlight ChemR23 as a promising therapeutic target with dual actions on both vascular and immune cells, while clinical translation will likely require cell type–specific targeting strategies. Future work focusing on conditional knockout models, lineage tracing, and translational approaches will be essential to fully harness ChemR23 modulation as a therapeutic avenue in atherosclerosis and cardiovascular disease.

This study highlights the critical role of ChemR23 in VSMC phenotypic switching. VSMCs lacking ChemR23 are more prone to transform into macrophage-like foam cells, contributing to plaque progression. These findings suggest that ChemR23-targeted therapies, such as C9 or α-NETA, may influence VSMC behaviour, with C9 reducing systemic inflammation and thereby atheroprogression, while α-NETA promotes lesional atheroprotective M2 macrophages, which hinder VSMC foam cell formation. These insights into ChemR23’s regulation of VSMC plasticity provide potential avenues for therapeutic intervention in managing plaque stability and progression in atherosclerotic patients.
