Using a consensus machine learning approach to identify host biomarkers associated with treatment response in pulmonary tuberculosis
Authors: Ruiqi Zhang, Fusheng Yao, Wenqi Liu, Qiao Lin, Yuanyuan Huang, Min Huang, Junyan He, Guobao Li, Guoliang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe lack of reliable biomarkers to monitor treatment response constitutes a major challenge in tuberculosis (TB) management, often leading to under- or overtreatment with the standard six-month regimen. Here, we integrated amino acid and trace element profiles, along with four machine learning-based feature selection methods, to develop an evaluation model. Additionally, we investigated the dynamic expression patterns of genes associated with the identified features during TB treatment to gain functional insights. The consensus feature selection from the four methodologies identified 34 potential biomarkers. Notably, three trace elements—Antimony (Sb), Copper (Cu), and Strontium (Sr)—were consistently highlighted as significant biomarkers across all employed methods. Re-analysis of two public RNA-seq datasets revealed that genes associated with these three trace elements yielded higher Area Under Curve values in distinguishing cured TB from untreated TB. Subsequent gene expression profiling showed dynamic expression patterns for genes linked to these biomarkers, predominantly involving pathways of programmed cell death, signal transduction, and immune response. These findings suggest that the combination of Sb, Cu, and Sr may serve as a novel laboratory criterion for monitoring TB treatment response, providing a practical tool to guide clinical decisions and reduce adverse outcomes.Keywords: machine learning, biomarkers, tuberculosis, treatment response

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is a chronic infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), posing a significant threat to human health worldwide. It is estimated that approximately one-quarter of the global population is infected with Mtb [1]. Despite the notable improvement in TB cure rates achieved through the Directly Observed Treatment Short-course strategy endorsed by the World Health Organization, the success rate of the standard six-month treatment for newly diagnosed TB remains between 70% and 85% [1]. Additionally, there is a recurrence rate of 2.3%–6.5%, with most recurrences being drug-resistant TB [1–3]. These phenomena not only complicate the diagnosis and treatment of TB, but also underscore the importance of accurately identify noncured TB and cured TB to end the TB epidemic.

The evaluation of cured TB currently relies on three main criteria: standardized anti-TB treatment, chest X-ray and/or CT scan results, sputum smear, and sputum culture results [4]. However, these methods have certain limitations. For instance, sputum culture, considered the gold standard for diagnosing TB, requires a long time—usually over two weeks—to obtain results. Sputum smear also has low sensitivity [5]. Most importantly, the absence of Mtb in the sputum does not mean that the bacteria have been completely eliminated [6]. Furthermore, the interpretation of chest X-ray and CT scan results depends on the subjective judgment and experience of doctors [7]. Consequently, these factors may result in patients who are not fully cured continuing to be sources of TB transmission. The lack of rapid and accurate diagnostic methods could also lead to over-treatment, increasing drug-related mortality, and imposing additional physical and economic burdens on patients [8, 9].

In recent years, the role of metabolism in activating immunity and host defense against TB infection has garnered significant attention. Throughout the dynamic progression of TB infection, notable fluctuations in ion and amino acid levels in the human body occur [10, 11]. These changes are not only intricately linked to the survival and dissemination of Mtb but also offer novel insights for the diagnosis and treatment of TB. Our recent studies have indicated that metabolic imbalances of amino acids and trace elements during TB infection could be a key factor in the disease’s development and can serve as effective biomarkers for distinguishing TB from healthy controls [12, 13]. These studies suggest that during the process of TB treatment, the levels of amino acids and trace elements may alter, potentially serving as biomarkers for cured TB.

Machine learning, a subset of artificial intelligence, presents significant advantages in handling vast, intricate, and diverse datasets [14]. Through supervised learning, machine learning models can pinpoint disease-related biomarkers and construct efficient disease diagnostic classifiers [15, 16]. However, there remains a gap in research concerning the identification of noncured and cured TB markers using machine learning models.

In this study, we integrated amino acid and trace element profiles with multiple machine learning algorithms for robust feature selection. This integration led to a novel and powerful approach for monitoring TB treatment and established a theoretical foundation for a precision evaluation system, thereby enhancing the accuracy and objectivity of current cured TB assessment methods.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shenzhen Third People’s Hospital (approval number: 2021–016). All experiments were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The participants involved in this study were recruited from Shenzhen Third People’s Hospital between January 2021 and May 2021 according to the following criteria:

(i) definite TB: those who displayed a positive detection for chest radiography, sputum culture, IGRA, and acid-fast bacilli staining were included;

(ii) the cured TB group: individuals who had received a standard six-month regimen of anti-TB treatment, exhibited negative cultures, and showed no evidence of TB relapse were included.

According to the criteria above, we recruited 155 participants, including 126 TB participants and 29 cured TB participants (Table 1). The enrolled participants did not present with extrapulmonary TB, HIV co-infection, or other complications such as hypertension. Only three individuals had a history of smoking in the TB group. Whole-genome sequencing identified all MTBC strains as drug-sensitive and belonging to Lineage 2 (L2), specifically the modern Beijing family of Mtb. A 5 ml blood sample was collected from each participant using vacuum blood collection tubes (BD Vacutainer). Plasma was separated using centrifugation at 500 g for 5 min and stored at −80 °C before use.

Targeted ion and amino acid profiling

Blood or plasma samples (40 μl) were processed in 96-well plates with the addition of 20 μl of 10% (w/v) sulfosalicylic acid containing internal standards. After vortexing for 30 s, 120 μl of aqueous solution was added, followed by another 30 s of vortexing. Samples were then centrifuged at 4000 g for 30 min at 4 °C to remove proteins and particulates. In total, 80 μl supernatant was transferred to clean vials for ultra-high performance liquid chromatography coupled mass spectrometer (UHPLC–MS) analysis. Separation was achieved on an Acquity UHPLC HSS T3 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm) with a flow rate of 0.5 ml/min and a column temperature of 55 °C. A gradient elution program optimized for both ions and amino acids was employed, using water with 0.05% heptafluorobutyric acid and 0.1% formic acid (v/v) as mobile phase A, and acetonitrile with the same additives as mobile phase B. Multiple reaction monitoring was used to monitor analyte transitions, with key mass spectrometry parameters set as follows: curtain gas flow of 35 l/min, medium collision gas, GS1 and GS2 flows of 60 l/min each, ion spray voltage of 5500 V, and a temperature of 600 °C. Quantification was performed using the area ratios of analyte peaks to their respective internal standards, with the aid of Analyst software, a targeted compound list, and library. Quality control measures, including the use of blank and quality control samples, were implemented to ensure the accuracy and reproducibility of the method.

Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis and initial screening of significant features

Orthogonal partial least-squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was performed on MetaboAnalyst (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca), a web-based platform that offers various modules for statistical, biomarker, pathway, enrichment, etc. We calculated the Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) scores for each feature, which indicate the contribution of each feature to discriminating between different groups. Following this, we built a predictive model using the first principal component (PC1) and performed 10-fold cross-validation to gauge the model’s performance. To further enhance the model’s reliability and avoid potential overfitting, we conducted 100 permutations to validate the model’s robustness.

Multivariable features selection and model building

To identify biomarkers capable of effectively differentiating cured TB, all features—including amino acid concentrations and trace element levels—were first standardized using Z-score normalization to ensure comparability across variables. We subsequently conducted both univariate and multivariate analyses utilizing four distinct feature selection methods. The intersection of features selected by these techniques was considered as the most representative and important feature combination. We divided the data into a 70% training set and a 30% test set to validate the feature selection and classification performance in four machine learning models. Given the severe class-imbalance in our dataset, Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was used to oversample the cured TB group on the train set, ensuring a balanced number of samples across different classes. Following this, parameters were tuned using 10-fold cross-validation on the training set, and the models were evaluated on the test set.

For random forest (RF) feature selection, we used the Boruta package in R, a feature importance evaluation strategy based on RFs. Following 300 iterations of normalized permutation importance assessments, the “confirmed” features were retained. This entire process was repeated 100 times to ensure robustness, and features that were selected on at least 10 occasions were carried forward for further analysis. To construct the RF model, we optimized two key parameters: ntree (ranging from 100 to 2000 in increments of 250) and mtry (1–4). After evaluating the performance of these models, we selected the optimal combination based on our criteria. Additionally, a probability threshold of 0.5 was selected. Details parameter optimization can be found in Table S1.

The Rpart-based feature selection and diagnostic model relied on the “Rpart” package in R. Model training was conducted using the “caret” package, specifying the rpart method for decision tree construction. Hyperparameter tuning was executed to refine the model’s complexity and predictive power. Parameters such as minsplit, minbucket, and maxcompete were fine-tuned to achieve an optimal balance between complexity and performance. A rigorous 10-fold repeated cross-validation strategy was implemented to ensure the model’s generalizability, and the final model was selected based on the Area Under Curve (AUC) score.

We applied the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) for feature selection and classification using the “glmnet” package in R. LASSO was integrated into binomial logistic regression to pinpoint relevant features by pushing noninformative coefficients towards zero. The regularization parameter, λ, was determined through 10-fold cross-validation, using binomial deviance as the evaluation metric. A probability threshold of 0.5 was applied for classifying individuals as having untreated or cured TB.

XGBoost, an algorithm based on gradient boosting decision trees, was utilized for both prediction and feature ranking. The “XGBoost” package in R was used for feature selection, and the optimal regularization parameters were identified through grid search on the training subset. Feature importance was determined by the frequency of feature usage in splitting data points and their impact on model performance. We designated a threshold where features that enhance branch accuracy by over 5% were classified as “important features” and used these to build the final model. Details parameter optimization can be found in Table S2.

To improve prediction accuracy, we developed an ensemble model by averaging the prediction scores from the four individual models for each sample in the test set. We then used MedCalc software to plot ROC curves based on these ensemble prediction scores and constructed confusion matrices using the threshold of 0.5, thereby assessing the predictive capabilities of the ensemble model. The Precision-Recall (PR) curves were generated using the “PRROC” package. Variable importance for features selected by the four algorithms was calculated using the varImp function from the caret package.

External validation in whole blood RNA-seq data

To explore the gene expression changes associated with the trace elements Sb, Cu, and Sr, we began by downloading genes related to these elements from the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (http://ctdbase.org/) and formatted them into GMT files. For gene set variation analysis (GSVA), we employed the “GSVA” package in R, utilizing the normalized GSE89403 RNA-seq dataset. To understand the potential roles of these trace elements, we identified differentially expressed genes (P-value <.05, |log2(Fold Change)| ≥ 1) using the “limma” package on the GSE89403 dataset, and then conducted functional enrichment analysis with DAVID (v6.7; http://david.abcc.ncifcrf.gov). Significantly enriched terms were determined based on a P-value <.05.

For validating the diagnostic targets of these trace elements in cured TB, we employed two transcriptional datasets: GSE89403 as the training set and GSE40553 as the test set. We integrated these datasets using RANK-in (http://www.badd-cao.net/rank-in/), a tool for microarray and RNA-seq data integration, and developed an RF model for this purpose. Additionally, we applied the Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) algorithm to interpret the genes selected by the RF model, utilizing the “fastshap” package in R.

Correlation analysis

We calculated the correlation coefficients between pairs of features using “Hmisc” packages in R and considered associations with a P-value <.05 as significant.

Statistical analysis

In this study, we employed two-tailed test for normally distributed data. For data with unequal variances or nonparametric data, we employed Welch’s correction and the Mann–Whitney U test, respectively. The P-value <.05 was recognized as significant.

Results

Integrated profiles of amino acid and trace element in TB and cured tuberculosis groups

To directly compare the differences in amino acids and trace elements content and their transporter efficiency between plasma and whole blood in TB and cured TB samples, we conducted multivariate pattern recognition analyses. Initially, we applied the OPLS-DA method to filter out orthogonal variables unrelated to the classification variable, thus obtaining reliable information on the differences between TB and cured TB. The score plots from the OPLS-DA analysis clearly demonstrate significant differences in amino acids and trace elements composition, as well as their transporter efficiency, between TB and cured TB in plasma and whole blood (Fig. 1A–C). To prevent overfitting, we performed 100 permutations tests for each model (Fig. 1D–F). The results revealed great predictive performance and absence of overfitting, with intercepts of R2 and Q2 in the plasma model standing at 0.036 and −0.078, respectively. Similarly, for the whole blood model, the intercepts of R2 and Q2 were 0.114 and −0.165, respectively, while in the transporter efficiency model, they were 0.093 and −0.157.

Based on the VIP values, we further selected components that potentially play important roles in distinguishing between the cured TB and TB groups. Using the criterion of VIP ≥ 1, we identified a total of 38 features: 11 in plasma (Fig. 1G), 19 in whole blood (Fig. 1H), and 8 related to transporter efficiency (Fig. 1I). These results suggest that there are significant differences in the composition of amino acids and trace elements, as well as their transporter efficiency, between TB and cured TB groups.

Amino acids and Trace elements selected using supervised machine learning approaches.

Based on the initial selection of 38 potential features, we further employed four distinct machine learning algorithms to refine our feature set and establish evaluation models.

The Boruta, rpart, LASSO, and XGBoost algorithms selected 28, 4, 22, and 5 features, respectively (Fig. 2A). Notably, three of these features were consistently selected across all four models. Subsequent differential analysis revealed that the cured TB group had significantly lower plasma concentrations of Cu, as well as significantly elevated plasma levels of Sb, and significantly elevated transport efficiency of Sr (Fig. 2B). Pairwise Jaccard similarity comparisons revealed that feature sets selected by Boruta–LASSO and Rpart–XGBoost exhibited higher agreement, with coefficients of 0.500 and 0.471, respectively. In contrast, the overall Cohen’s kappa across all four feature selection methods was 0.0894, indicating substantially divergent mechanisms underlying feature importance evaluation and selection among the different techniques (Fig. S1).

To elucidate the significance of these selected features, we constructed a correlation coefficient matrix using the features identified by the four machine learning techniques. As depicted in Fig. 2C, significant positive correlations were observed among blood amino acids. Similarly, the transport efficiencies of trace elements such as magnesium, zinc, Cu, and iron also exhibit positive correlations.

Performance of cured tuberculosis classification using selected features

To compare the performance of four feature selection methods, we compared their classification results on the test set (Fig. 3A–D). Our analysis revealed that the RF model demonstrated superior performance, achieving the highest AUC of 0.986 (95% CI: 0.897–1.000), although the LASSO and XGBoost models also exhibited a notable AUC of 0.889 (95% CI: 0.795–0.989) and 0.902 (95% CI: 0.776–0.970), respectively. Conversely, the rpart model displayed the least impressive performance, with AUCs of 0.779 (95% CI: 0.630–0.889). Given that multivariate methods can identify features with varying degrees of accuracy, we further explored ensemble learning algorithms (Fig. 3E). This ensemble model demonstrated an enhanced AUC of 0.966 (95% CI: 0.864–0.997), compared to rpart, LASSO, and XGBoost models. Further confusion matrices indicated RF model boasted the highest accuracy of 0.844 (95% CI: 0.705–0.935), accompanied by a substantial decrease in the misclassification rates for both untreated TB and cured TB. Notably, the RF model achieved perfect accuracy of 1.000 (95% CI: 0.631–1.000) for predicting cured TB and a commendable accuracy of 0.811 (95% CI: 0.648–0.920) for predicting untreated TB (Fig. 3B). We also calculated Area Under the PR Curve (AP score) to profile model reliability. We found RF and Ensemble methods achieved high AP scores of 0.956 and 0.882, respectively (Fig. S2A). Notably, SMOTE did not significantly alter overall accuracy, but led to a noticeable improvement in sensitivity (recall)—highlighting its utility in enhancing the detection of minority class (Fig. S2B–D; Tables S3 and S4).

To mitigate the risk of the final model being too dependent on any one feature, we performed a univariate analysis (Fig. 3F–H). The results revealed that each of the three features had AUC values >0.75, with plasma Sb achieving the highest AUC score. Notably, the features consistently chosen by all four algorithms also emerged as the most significant in the variable importance analysis (Fig. 3I). This indicates that the model developed with the three features commonly identified across algorithms demonstrated robust classification capabilities.

Dynamic expression analysis of trace element-related genes in response to antituberculosis treatment

Finally, we explored the dynamic transcriptional profiles of genes associated with three key trace elements throughout the course of TB treatment. By re-examining RNA-seq data from a public dataset (GSE89403), we assessed the expression trends of these genes. Through GSVA analysis, we detected significant fluctuations in the expression of genes related to Sb and Cu across different stages of TB treatment (Fig. 4A and B). In contrast, genes linked to Sr exhibited minimal variation, which may be attributed to the sparse representation of Sr-associated genes in our dataset (Fig. 4C). We then applied RF as a classification tool, leveraging its high accuracy in MS datasets. This approach yielded a model with an AUC of 0.923 in the training cohort (GSE89403) and 0.886 in the validation cohort (GSE40553) (Fig. 4D). To dissect the individual impact of each gene within the RF model and its role in predictive outcomes, we conducted SHAP analysis. The resultant plots consistently highlighted CDKN3 as the pivotal gene, exhibiting the highest SHAP value for forecasting TB treatment success (Fig. 4E and F). Further differential expression analysis revealed that 27.6%, 16.5%, and 10.0% of genes associated with Sb, Cu, and Sr, respectively, experienced substantial changes following TB cure (Fig. 4G). These genes were predominantly involved in pathways such as “apoptosis”, “tuberculosis”, “TNF signaling pathway”, and “Th17 cell differentiation” (Fig. 4H). These findings suggest a close relationship between trace element-related genes and immune responses, with their expression profiles dynamically altering during TB treatment, potentially offering novel insights into the pathogenesis and therapeutic mechanisms of TB.

Discussion

TB, though a chronic infectious disease, is entirely curable with proper treatment. However, the lack of standardized laboratory criteria for discharging TB patients presents a challenge in ensuring their safe release from treatment. This predicament not only risks premature discharge of patients who have not fully recovered, thus heightening the potential for Mtb transmission in the community, but also imposes unnecessary extended treatment on patients who have actually achieved recovery [8, 9]. As a result, the exploration of biomarkers that can accurately predict TB treatment response has become a crucial research prior.

Blood, serving as a conduit between various tissues and organs in the human body, can sensitively reflect changes in one’s health status through its compositional nuances [17, 18]. By comparing and analyzing the disparities in trace elements and amino acid content within blood and plasma samples, as well as their transport efficiency of untreated TB patients and those who have been cured of TB, we have uncovered three potential trace elements that may serve as distinguishing markers between these two conditions.

Importantly, this study has transcended the confines of merely analyzing differences in individual metabolite content. Instead, it has harnessed advanced machine learning techniques to delve into the intricate interplay among metabolites. Furthermore, we employed four supervised machine learning models to validate these features. Among them, the RF model stood out, demonstrating exceptional performance. The RF model achieved a 100% accuracy rate in predicting cured TB. Although each of the machine learning models selected amino acids as an important feature, the intersection of the selected features from all four models did not include amino acids. Instead, it consisted of three trace elements: the levels of Cu and Sb in plasma, as well as the ratio of Sr. These biomarkers showed high sensitivity and specificity in distinguishing cured TB from untreated TB.

Cu is an essential trace element for the growth and development of all organisms, including Mtb [19, 20]. On one hand, Mtb can utilize Cu as a cofactor for the superoxide dismutase SodC and cytochrome c oxidase. Through this process, Mtb reduces the levels of superoxide in its cytosol and extracellular environment, thereby defending against the killing effects of ROS and promoting Mtb survival [21, 22]. However, on the other hand, excessive Cu may disrupt the Fe–S clusters in the enzyme HemN, leading to a decrease in heme synthesis, further reducing the activity of peroxidase and nitric oxide reductase [23]. This mechanism may lead to the increased toxicity of reactive species and thus decreased bacterial survival. Therefore, an elevation in copper levels in TB patients can effectively hinder the Mtb growth, whereas cured TB patients exhibit a decrease in Cu content as a result of the absence of Mtb.

Sr is a vital trace element that modulates the kinetics of immune cells, encompassing the behavior of T cells, cytokine production, and the NF-κB signaling pathway [24]. Research has elucidated that Sr not only diminishes oxidative stress but also possesses notable antioxidant capabilities. Its role in inhibiting the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines is especially pivotal, conferring an anti-inflammatory impact [25]. This beneficial effect is largely attributable to Sr’s regulatory influence on intracellular calcium concentrations, which subsequently impacts T cell responses and the synthesis of anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as TGF-β and IL-10, thereby assuming a critical role in mitigating the exaggerated immune responses characteristic of autoimmune and chronic inflammatory disorders [26, 27]. Furthermore, Sr mitigates the activity of inflammatory mediators by downregulating the expression of key pro-inflammatory agents, including IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α, primarily through the NF-κB pathway [28, 29] . Concurrently, Sr can augment the production of anti-inflammatory cytokines like IL-10, thereby reinforcing the inhibition of inflammation [30]. While a direct link between Sr and TB has not been established by research, the cytokine storm is a hallmark of TB pathology. Consequently, elevated levels of Sr may contribute to the reduction of inflammation and Mtb clearance. Thus, elevated levels of Sr may be instrumental in improving the disease state.

Similarly, Sb also plays an essential role in the clearance of intracellular bacteria, including Leishmania spp, it exerts its effects through the regulation of the immune system [31]. However, its role in TB infection has not yet been elucidated.

Furthermore, three trace elements related genes showed dynamic expression during the TB treatment, and mainly in specific cellular death and immune response pathways, suggesting their crucial role in TB infection. The inherent stability of these trace elements like Sb, Cu, and Sr in biological matrices, combined with the high precision of modern analytical techniques such as ICP-MS, provides a robust foundation for their biomarker potential.

This study has two main limitations. First, the small sample size, particularly in the cured TB group (n = 29), is a major constraint. Although we applied SMOTE and cross-validation to avoid the effect of majority class on Classifier [32–34], and this method improved sensitivity without significantly affecting accuracy, we need to further expand the cohort to enhance the generalizability of the findings reliably. These results should be considered proof-of-concept, and larger multicenter prospective studies are needed to confirm clinical applicability. Second, the inference of trace element-related gene expression was predicated on external transcriptomic data, as opposed to direct measurement of trace elements and concomitant RNA changes within the same cohort. This approach may weaken the direct evidence linking trace element biomarkers to gene expression, due to potential heterogeneity in host genetics, MTBC lineages, or clinical management. Future work should incorporate direct quantification of trace elements together with transcriptomic and metabolomic profiling in a larger, well-defined cohort from a homogeneous population. Such an approach is essential to derive physiologically relevant insights, establish causal associations, and mitigate confounding.

Conclusion

In summary, our multi-element biomarker panel, integrated with an interpretable machine learning framework, offers a feasible strategy for improving treatment monitoring and outcome prediction in TB care. By providing objective, quantitative, and dynamic readouts of host response to therapy, this approach could complement—and in some settings potentially reduce reliance on—traditional microbiological or imaging-based assessments.
