IGF2BP3 promotes the muscle invasion of bladder cancer via PPARγ dysregulation
Authors: Jing-yi Cao, Hua Peng, Guang-yue Wang, Ping-An Chang, Wen-Tao Yao, Qian Wang, Jie-Zhou, Gui-hua Zhang, Heng Wang, Jia-Cheng Wu, Sha-Sha Li, Quan-lan Gao, Chang-song Pei, Qi-chao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBladder cancer (BC) is a common urinary-tract malignancy that, once it progresses to the muscle‐invasive form (MIBC), adopts a markedly more aggressive behavior and is linked to significantly poorer patient outcomes. Despite extensive investigation, the molecular drivers of MIBC progression have yet to be fully elucidated. In this study, we demonstrate that IGF2BP3 is markedly upregulated, whereas PPARγ is significantly downregulated, in MIBC tissues compared with non–muscle–invasive disease, and that these reciprocal expression changes correlate with enhanced muscle invasion and adverse clinical outcomes. Comprehensive in vitro analyses reveal that elevated IGF2BP3 modulates BC cell motility, with overexpression enhancing migration and invasion and knockdown reducing these processes, while effects on proliferation and apoptosis were minimal. Mechanistic investigations further indicate that IGF2BP3 manipulation in vitro modulates PPARγ mRNA levels and PPARγ knockdown or overexpression respectively augments or abrogates IGF2BP3‐driven cell motility. Collectively, our findings define a novel IGF2BP3/PPARγ signaling axis that contributes to the aggressive behavior of MIBC and suggest that targeting this pathway may offer new avenues for therapeutic intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-04648-3.Keywords: Bladder cancer, IGF2BP3, Muscle-invasive, PPARgamma

Introduction

Bladder cancer (BC) places a substantial global burden, with over 500,000 new cases diagnosed each year. In the United States alone, it ranks as the sixth most frequently identified cancer in men and is responsible for significant mortality [1, 2]. Urothelial carcinoma—the predominant histological form—can be classified into non-muscle-invasive (NMIBC) and muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) [3]. Although NMIBC accounts for approximately 75% of cases, MIBC—observed in 25–30% of patients, with around 5% presenting distant metastases—exhibits rapid progression, frequent recurrence, and dismal clinical outcomes [4]. Despite advances in diagnostic imaging and therapeutic interventions, the molecular mechanisms underlying the invasive behavior of MIBC remain poorly defined, underscoring an urgent need for novel mechanistic insights and therapeutic targets [5].

Recent studies have increasingly recognized the pivotal role of post-transcriptional regulation in cancer progression, particularly through N6-methyladenosine (m6A) RNA modification-the most abundant internal modification in eukaryotic mRNAs. The m6A machinery comprises methyltransferase “writers”, demethylase “erasers”, and m6A-binding “readers,” which decode the methyl mark to regulate RNA metabolism, stability, and translation [6, 7]. Dysregulation of m6A imprinting has recently been implicated in the initiation and progression of bladder cancer, influencing transcriptomic plasticity, tumor stemness, and therapeutic response. Among the readers, the Insulin-like Growth Factor 2 mRNA-binding Protein family (IGF2BP1/2/3) recognizes m6A-modified transcripts via the consensus GGm6AC motif and enhances their stability and translational efficiency, thereby amplifying oncogenic signaling pathways that drive proliferation, migration, invasion, and immune evasion [8–10]. In particular, IGF2BP3-an oncofetal RNA-binding protein-has emerged as a central effector of the m6A epitranscriptomic machinery and a pivotal post-transcriptional regulator in multiple malignancies, including lung, breast, and colorectal cancers, where it stabilizes mRNAs encoding pro-tumorigenic and immune-modulatory factors [11, 12]. Despite accumulating evidence implicating m6A dysregulation in bladder cancer, the specific contribution of IGF2BP3 to the muscle-invasive phenotype and its mechanistic relationship with downstream targets remain poorly defined.

In parallel, Peroxisome Proliferator-Activated Receptor Gamma (PPARγ) is a ligand-activated nuclear receptor that governs cellular differentiation, lipid metabolism, and inflammatory responses [13]. Interestingly, emerging evidence suggests that PPARγ expression inversely correlates with bladder cancer invasiveness [14]. It is abundant in non–muscle-invasive luminal tumors yet lost in aggressive, muscle‐invasive basal subtypes. In basal progenitor cells, PPARγ activation drives differentiation into non‐invasive superficial urothelial cells, but when these cells are primed by injury, PPARγ instead promotes the emergence of immune‐deserted luminal tumors with invasive capabilities [15–17]. As these tumors progress, loss of PPARγ accompanies a switch back to a basal, highly invasive phenotype. Although IGF2BP2 have been shown to bind and stabilize PPARγ mRNA in hepatic and macrophage models—modulating lipid metabolism and inflammatory activation [18, 19], respectively—IGF2BP3 is a close paralog within the same family. Given their conserved RNA-binding domains [20], it is plausible that IGF2BP3 similarly associates with the PPARγ transcript to regulate its stability and contribute to bladder cancer invasiveness.

We therefore hypothesize that IGF2BP3 underpins bladder cancer’s transition to the muscle-invasive phenotype by regulating PPARγ expression and functional activity. To test this hypothesis, our study systematically evaluated the expression profiles of IGF2BP3 and PPARγ in clinical BC specimens and correlated these with invasion metrics and patient outcomes. Complementary in vitro experiments assessed IGF2BP3’s impact on tumor cell migration and invasion, while mechanistic investigations examined whether these effects were mediated through modulation of PPARγ. Collectively, our integrated approach reveals a novel IGF2BP3/PPARγ signaling axis that underlies the aggressive phenotype of MIBC and offers promising avenues for the development of targeted therapies in this challenging disease.

Methods

Study design and datasets

Patients and clinical tissue specimen collection

A total of 12 bladder tissue samples were prospectively collected at Xuzhou Cancer Hospital: four NMIBC specimens, four MIBC specimens, and four normal control mucosa samples obtained from adjacent non-neoplastic bladder tissue. All specimens were obtained at the time of surgical resection between January and June 2024. The clinicopathological characteristics of the study cohort are summarized in Table S1.

Shortly after surgical removal, specimens were washed in cold phosphate-buffered saline, bisected, and handled as follows: one half was rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and kept at − 80 °C for later RNA and protein extraction; the other half was immersed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin overnight, then processed into paraffin blocks for histological and immunohistochemical studies. A board-certified urological pathologist then re-reviewed all cases to assign tumor stage and grade according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 8th -edition TNM criteria and the 2016 WHO classification.

This study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Xuzhou Cancer Hospital Ethics Committee (approval no. 2024-02-001-K01), and all participants provided written informed consent before inclusion.

Study design and analytical workflow

To provide an overview of the study design, we constructed a schematic diagram summarizing the sequential analytical and experimental workflow (Fig. 1). Initially, differentially expressed (DE) m6A regulators were identified from TCGA-BLCA and GSE120736 datasets, followed by survival screening that highlighted IGF2BP3 as a key prognostic m6A-related regulator. IGF2BP3 expression was validated by immunohistochemistry (IHC) in clinical samples, and its biological functions in cell proliferation, apoptosis, migration, and invasion were examined in vitro. Integrative analysis of TCGA, GSE120736, and GSE90639 datasets was then used to identify potential downstream targets, leading to the identification of PPARγ, which was further validated by IHC and rescue experiments.

Overall workflow of the study design

Data acquisition and bioinformatics analysis

Gene expression count tables and their associated clinical profiles for bladder urothelial carcinoma (406 tumors and 40 matched normal tissues) were retrieved from the TCGA-BLCA resource via the GDC portal. Raw counts were converted into transcripts per million (TPM), log₂(TPM + 1)-scaled, and the dataset was narrowed to the 446 samples with complete clinical records. Expression profiles for non–muscle-invasive versus muscle-invasive tumors (GSE120736) and RIP-seq of Flag-tagged IGF2BP1/2/3 in HEK293T cells (GSE90639) were obtained from GEO in MINiML format. All matrices were log₂-transformed as needed; unnormalized data were quantile-normalized (normalize.quantiles, preprocessCore); probe IDs were mapped to gene symbols per platform annotations (multi-mapping probes discarded, duplicated entries averaged); and batch effects within and between platforms were removed using removeBatchEffect (limma), with normalization and batch correction confirmed via boxplots and PCA. Differential expression analysis was performed using limma (|log₂FC| > 1, adjusted p < 0.05). Candidate effectors were nominated by intersecting TCGA-BLCA and GSE120736 DEGs with RIP-seq–identified IGF2BP targets using a Venn diagram analysis. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated by stratifying patients at median expression, with differences tested by the log-rank test and hazard ratios (95% CI) calculated via univariate Cox regression. All analyses were performed in R v4.3.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded bladder specimens were sectioned at 4 μm, dewaxed in xylene, and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series to water. Heat-induced antigen retrieval was carried out in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0; Beyotime) in a microwave for 20 min, then endogenous peroxidase was blocked with 3% H₂O₂ in methanol for 10 min at room temperature. To reduce non-specific binding, slides were pre-incubated in 5% bovine serum albumin in PBS for 30 min before being incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti-IGF2BP3 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-271548; 1:200) and rabbit anti-PPARγ (Abcam, ab59256; 1:200). After three PBS rinses, sections were treated for 30 min at room temperature with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (ZSGB-BIO, PV-9001; 1:500). Chromogenic detection used DAB substrate (ZSGB-BIO, ZLI-9018) until brown signal appeared, and nuclei were counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin. Negative controls omitting primary antibody were run in parallel. For each case, three separate sections were evaluated by three pathologists blinded to clinical data. Immunoreactivity was semi-quantitatively scored by the percentage of positive tumor cells: 0 (< 10%), 1 (10–25%), 2 (26–50%), 3 (51–75%), and 4 (> 75%). Finally, slides were digitized on an Olympus VS120 slide scanner (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Cell culture and transfection

Immortalized human ureteral epithelial SV-HUC-1 cells and the bladder carcinoma line 5637 were obtained from Zhongqiao Xinzhuo Science & Technology (Shanghai, China). 5637 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 (Vicmed Biotech, Xuzhou, China), while SV-HUC-1 cultures used Ham’s F-12 K medium (Gibco). In both cases, media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Beyotime, China). Cultures were kept in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Medium was refreshed every 24–48 h, and cells were passaged at ~ 80–90% confluence, typically every 2–3 days. For lentiviral infection, 5637 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 3–5 × 10⁴ cells/mL and allowed to adhere overnight until reaching approximately 20–30% confluence. Lentiviral constructs for IGF2BP3 overexpression (LV-IGF2BP3) and corresponding control virus (CON335), as well as RNAi lentiviruses targeting IGF2BP3 (LV-IGF2BP3-RNAi) with negative controls (CON313), were provided by Shanghai Jikai Gene. Before infecting the cells, the culture medium was refreshed with complete medium containing 40 µL of the manufacturer’s infection enhancer. Lentiviral stocks were adjusted to an MOI of 5 and applied to the cultures, which were incubated at 37 °C for 12 h. Afterward, the medium was replaced with standard complete medium, and infection efficiency was quantified approximately 72 h later. All experiments were carried out in three independent replicates.

Cell-counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cells in the exponential growth phase were collected by centrifugation and resuspended in serum-free RPMI-1640. The concentration was adjusted to 3 × 104 cells/mL, and 100 µL aliquots were plated in six replicate wells of a 96-well microplate. After allowing the cells to adhere and reach roughly 80% confluence over 12–16 h, each well was supplemented with 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (Sigma, MO, USA). Plates were then incubated for 30–120 min until an orange tint developed, at which point absorbance at 450 nm was recorded. Initial trials established the ideal incubation time, and thereafter optical density measurements were taken daily for analysis.

5-Ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay

Cells were plated in 24-well dishes and exposed to 300 µL of 50 µM EdU solution (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China) for a two-hour pulse. Afterward, samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, then briefly agitated on a shaker to remove excess fixative and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 10 min. Following thorough PBS washes, the cells were incubated in the dark with 200 µL of Apollo® reaction mix for 30 min, counterstained with 200 µL Hoechst 33,342 for another 30 min, and finally visualized under an Olympus IX73 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Wound healing test

A 6-well plate was pre-marked on its underside with at least three equidistant lines (0.5–1.0 cm apart). Cells, seeded at a density of 5 × 10^5 per well, were cultured for 24 h until confluence reached approximately 90%. The culture medium was then aspirated, and a uniform scratch was generated along the pre-marked lines using a sterile pipette tip. After washing with PBS, complete medium supplemented with serum was replenished, and the wound area was documented at 0, 12, and 24 h to monitor cellular migration and closure.

Transwell assay

Transwell inserts were placed in 24-well plates, and the upper chambers were coated with 80 µL of extracellular matrix gel diluted 1:10. After gentle rocking to distribute the gel evenly, plates were incubated overnight. Following 12–24 h of serum starvation, cells were trypsinized, washed, and resuspended in 2mL of serum-free medium containing bovine serum albumin. A 200 µL aliquot of this suspension was added to each insert, while 600µL of complete medium was placed in the well below. Plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Afterward, non-invading cells on the upper membrane surface were removed, and cells that had traversed to the underside were stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min prior to counting.

TUNEL staining

Approximately 1 × 105 cells were seeded onto glass coverslips in 24-well plates and transduced with either IGF2BP3 overexpression vectors, IGF2BP3-targeting shRNA, or corresponding controls. Seventy-two hours later, cultures were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, then permeabilized on ice for 5 minutes in 0. % Triton X-100/0.1% sodium citrate. Apoptotic nuclei were detected using a TUNEL assay kit (Yeasen Biotech, China) according to the supplied protocol, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Five randomly chosen fields per coverslip were imaged under an Olympus fluorescence microscope, and the apoptotic rate was calculated as the number of TUNEL-positive nuclei divided by the total DAPI-stained nuclei. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT‐PCR)

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China), using chloroform for phase separation and isopropanol for precipitation, followed by ethanol washes to concentrate and purify the samples. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from the purified RNA using the Takara Reverse Transcription Kit (Takara, Kyoto, Japan). Gene expression was then quantified by SYBR Green–based real-time PCR (Takara, Kyoto, Japan), and relative levels were calculated via the 2^–ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences utilized were as follows: for IGF2BP3, forward 5′-ACGAAATATCCCGCCTCATTTAC-3′ and reverse 5′-GCAGTTTCCGAGTCAGTGTTCA-3′; for PPARγ, forward 5′-ACCAAAGTGCAATCAAAGTGGA-3′ and reverse 5′-ATGAGGGAGTTGGAAGGCTCT-3′; and for GAPDH, forward 5′-GGAAGCTTGTCATCAATGGAAATC-3′ and reverse 5′-TGATGACCCTTTTGGCTCCC-3′.

Western blotting (WB)

Tissue specimens and cultured cells were lysed in RIPA buffer, and the cleared supernatants were quantified for protein concentration. Equal amounts of lysate were denatured, separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. To reduce background, membranes were blocked in 5% skim milk for 2 h at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against IGF2BP3 (1:1 000; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), PPARγ (1:1 000; Abcam) and β-actin (1:5 000–10 000; Abmart). After extensive washes, blots were probed for 1 h at room temperature with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (VICMED VP6022). Chemiluminescent signals were captured on an Odyssey CLX Infrared Imaging System, and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ (v1.8.0_112). For targets at different molecular-weight ranges, the PVDF membrane was cut before primary-antibody incubation, and the resulting pieces were probed in parallel. Uncropped scans of each membrane piece—showing full edges and molecular-weight markers for all biological replicates—are provided in Supplementary Fig. S1.

Statistical analysis

All data processing and statistical tests were carried out in R (v4.3), GraphPad Prism (v8.0.2, GraphPad Software, CA, USA), and SPSS (v22.0). Results are reported as mean ± standard error of the mean. For pairwise comparisons, we used Student’s t-test or, when normality assumptions were violated, appropriate nonparametric alternatives; differences among three or more groups were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance. Forward stepwise Cox regression was performed to identify independent prognostic markers in bladder cancer. A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

IGF2BP3 expression is elevated in BC and predicts adverse clinical outcomes

By interrogating 20 canonical m6A regulators in the TCGA-BLCA cohort, we found that several readers (IGF2BP1, IGF2BP3, YTHDF1, YTHDF2) and RNA-binding proteins (RBM15, RBM15B, HNRNPC) were significantly upregulated in bladder cancer tissues compared with matched normal urothelium, whereas multiple writers and readers—including METTL3, WTAP, ZC3H13, YTHDC1, YTHDC2—as well as demethylases (FTO and ALKBH5) and RNA-binding proteins (HNRNPA2B1 and EIF3A) were significantly downregulated (Fig. 2A). To hone in on those linked specifically to muscle invasion, we then applied hierarchical clustering to the GSE120736 dataset and observed that four readers—IGF2BP1, IGF2BP3, RBM15 and YTHDF1—were consistently overexpressed in MIBC compared to NMIBC (Fig. 2B). Univariate Cox regression identified IGF2BP3 as a significant predictor of overall survival (Fig. 2C), and Kaplan–Meier analysis confirmed that high IGF2BP3 expression portends significantly poorer survival (Fig. 2D), thereby establishing IGF2BP3 overexpression as a key mediator of muscle invasion and adverse prognosis in bladder cancer.

To validate our transcriptomic findings from TCGA-BLCA and GSE120736, we performed immunohistochemical staining for IGF2BP3 on an independent cohort of bladder cancer specimens comprising NMIBC and MIBC cases. IHC analysis revealed a stepwise increase in IGF2BP3 expression from normal bladder tissue to NMIBC and further to MIBC (Fig. 2E). Quantitative assessment of mean optical density confirmed that IGF2BP3 levels were minimal in normal tissues, moderately elevated in NMIBC, and highest in MIBC, thereby corroborating its tumor-specific upregulation and strong association with muscle invasion in bladder cancer.

IGF2BP3 promotes BC cell migration and invasion

To elucidate the role of IGF2BP3 in BC, we first established stable overexpression and knockdown models in 5637 cells via lentiviral transduction. The successful modulation of IGF2BP3 was confirmed by Western blot, which showed corresponding changes in protein levels, and by RT-qPCR, which revealed significant alterations in mRNA expression under overexpression (oeIGF2BP3) and knockdown (shIGF2BP3) conditions (Fig. 3A, B). Subsequently, wound-healing assays revealed that IGF2BP3 overexpression markedly enhanced the migratory capacity of 5637 cells, as evidenced by a faster wound closure rate, whereas silencing IGF2BP3 significantly impaired migration compared to controls (Fig. 3C). Consistently, Transwell assays demonstrated that IGF2BP3 overexpression increased both migration and invasion of 5637 cells, while knockdown reduced these processes (Fig. 3D). These data collectively suggest that IGF2BP3 promotes the metastatic potential of BC cells by enhancing migration and invasion.

IGF2BP3 does not significantly affect BC cell proliferation or apoptosis

To further investigate the role of IGF2BP3 in BC cell proliferation, we employed CCK-8 and EdU assays. Neither oeIGF2BP3 nor shIGF2BP3 altered cell viability or EdU incorporation compared with respective controls (Fig. S2A, B). Apoptosis measured by TUNEL staining likewise showed minimal differences across groups (Fig. S2C). These results indicate that IGF2BP3 does not substantially impact proliferation or apoptosis in BC cells, suggesting its effects are predominantly on migration and invasion rather than growth or survival.

PPARγ as a potential downstream target of IGF2BP3 in BC

Building on our functional data, we next sought to identify the key downstream effector(s) through which IGF2BP3 drives muscle invasion. To this end, we overlaid three datasets: (i) TCGA-BLCA differentially expressed genes, (ii) RIP‐seq targets of Flag‐tagged IGF2BP1/2/3 in HEK293T cells (GSE90639), and (iii) NMIBC versus MIBC DEGs from GSE120736. This integrative Venn analysis yielded 25 overlapping candidates (Fig. 4A). Univariate Cox regression analysis of these 25 genes in the TCGA cohort then pinpointed PPARγ as the only significant prognostic marker (Fig. 4B). Kaplan–Meier survival curves confirmed that low PPARγ expression portends poorer overall survival compared with high expression (Fig. 4C). Consistent with its proposed role in muscle invasion, PPARγ mRNA levels were significantly reduced in MIBC compared with NMIBC samples in the GSE120736 dataset (Fig. 4D). Immunohistochemical analysis further revealed strong PPARγ staining in adjacent normal urothelial tissues, moderate staining in NMIBC, and markedly decreased staining in MIBC sections (Fig. 4E). Together, these data nominate PPARγ as a functionally relevant downstream target of IGF2BP3 in bladder cancer progression.

IGF2BP3 regulates PPARγ to promote bladder cancer cell migration and invasion

Building on our identification of PPARγ as a prognostic IGF2BP3 target, we next tested whether PPARγ mediates IGF2BP3-driven motility. qRT‐PCR confirmed that IGF2BP3 overexpression downregulated PPARγ mRNA, whereas knockdown upregulated it (Fig. 5A). In Transwell assays, PPARγ overexpression attenuated the increased motility caused by IGF2BP3, while PPARγ silencing rescued the reduced motility in IGF2BP3-silenced cells(Fig. 5B). Likewise, wound‐healing assays showed that PPARγ overexpression slowed gap closure in IGF2BP3-overexpressing cells, whereas its knockdown accelerated migration in IGF2BP3-silenced cells (Fig. 5C). These data demonstrate that PPARγ acts downstream of IGF2BP3 to mediate its pro-migratory and pro-invasive effects in bladder cancer cells.

Discussion

Our data reveal that IGF2BP3 is associated with a more aggressive bladder-cancer phenotype and with lower PPARγ levels in clinical specimens. Tumors with high IGF2BP3 expression showed poorer clinical outcomes and displayed enhanced migratory and invasive behavior, suggesting that highlighting IGF2BP3 as a potential prognostic correlate and a testable therapeutic hypothesis in muscle-invasive disease. Rescue experiments further suggest that part of the motility phenotype is PPARγ-dependent: restoring PPARγ attenuated the pro-migratory effect of IGF2BP3, whereas silencing PPARγ rescued the reduced migration caused by IGF2BP3 knockdown [21]. Together, these findings support a working model in which IGF2BP3 dampens PPARγ signaling to facilitate invasive behavior in bladder cancer.

Despite IGF2BP3’s minimal impact on BC cell proliferation and apoptosis, its significant effect on cell migration and invasion underscores its role in regulating metastasis rather than cell growth. This observation aligns with the known functions of RNA-binding proteins, which often modulate processes such as cell motility and invasiveness rather than promoting survival. The findings are compatible with post-transcriptional modulation of PPARγ by IGF2BP3 in the context of cell motility. Our findings are consistent with post-transcriptional down-modulation of PPARγ by IGF2BP3 and implicate this axis in cell migration and invasion. Definitive evidence for direct RNA binding and mRNA stability effects is pending, and in-vivo studies are needed before inferring metastatic behavior or therapeutic efficacy. These findings highlight the IGF2BP3-PPARγ axis as a hypothesis-generating, potentially actionable pathway warranting further preclinical evaluation [22].

From a translational perspective, our findings suggest that the IGF2BP3-PPARγ axis may represent a promising biomarker and therapeutic target in bladder cancer. The stepwise upregulation of IGF2BP3 and concomitant loss of PPARγ from normal urothelium to MIBC indicate potential utility in disease classification and risk stratification. Moreover, pharmacologic activation of PPARγ through clinically approved agonists could, in principle, restore anti-invasive signaling in tumors with high IGF2BP3 expression [23]. These insights provide a rationale for exploring IGF2BP3-PPARγ-targeted strategies as part of precision therapeutic approaches in bladder cancer.

This study has limitations. First, the cohort of clinical specimens was small and single-center, limiting statistical power and generalizability. Second, our functional work relied on in-vitro assays; in-vivo validation of the IGF2BP3–PPARγ axis is warranted. Third, the in vitro functional assays were conducted in a single bladder cancer cell line (5637), which may restrict the generalizability of our findings. Although this model is well established for mechanistic exploration, validation in additional NMIBC- and MIBC-derived lines (such as T24 or UMUC3) will be important to confirm the reproducibility of the IGF2BP3–PPARγ regulatory axis. Finally, mechanistic claims such as direct binding and RNA-stability regulation require dedicated assays (e.g., RIP-qPCR/CLIP-seq and mRNA decay experiments).

In summary, we delineate an IGF2BP3–PPARγ axis that preferentially influences migration and invasion rather than cell growth in bladder cancer. By coupling clinical observations with functional epistasis, our study highlights IGF2BP3 and PPARγ as linked correlates of aggressive disease and nominates this pathway as a testable target for therapeutic modulation in muscle-invasive bladder cancer.

No new DNA/RNA sequencing datasets or other data types subject to mandatory deposition were generated. Data sharing is not applicable to this article with respect to new sequencing datasets, as no such datasets were generated or analysed during the current study. The underlying study-generated materials (qPCR Ct tables, densitometry spreadsheets, and assay readouts), which include de-identified clinical variables from a small, single-center cohort (n = 12), are not publicly posted due to institutional IRB and privacy restrictions, but are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request for non-commercial academic research.
