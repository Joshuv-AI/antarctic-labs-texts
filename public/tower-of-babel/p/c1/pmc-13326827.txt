Impact of Recanalization on Liver Hypertrophy after Portal Vein Embolization and the Role of Re‐Embolization
Authors: Masao Uemura, Shimpei Otsuka, Ryo Ashida, Katsuhisa Ohgi, Mihoko Yamada, Rui Sato, Takeshi Aramaki, Akifumi Notsu, Katsuhiko Uesaka, Teiichi Sugiura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPortal vein recanalization following portal vein embolization (PVE) can impair future liver remnant (FLR) growth; however, the clinical significance of recanalization and the utility of re‐embolization remain unclear. This study aimed to assess the incidence of recanalization after PVE, its impact on liver regeneration, and the efficacy of re‐embolization.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 249 consecutive patients who underwent hepatectomy following right PVE between 2010 and 2022. Recanalization was classified as major (section or segment) or minor (less than one segment). In this study, FLR was defined as the left hemiliver for standardization across different surgical procedures. Liver hypertrophy was assessed using the degree of hypertrophy (DH) and the kinetic growth rate (KGR). Re‐embolization was performed in selected patients with insufficient FLR growth due to recanalization.ResultsRecanalization occurred in 89 patients (36%), including 55 minor and 34 major recanalizations. DH and KGR differed significantly among the three groups. Subsequent post hoc analysis revealed that major recanalization had significantly lower DH (p = 0.002) and KGR (p = 0.003) than non‐recanalization. Seven patients underwent re‐embolization, which significantly improved the FLR ratio (34.2% to 39.8%, p = 0.003) without complications.ConclusionsMajor recanalization after PVE was significantly associated with impaired liver regeneration. Re‐embolization is a potentially effective strategy for managing insufficient FLR growth due to recanalization, particularly in cases of major recanalization.

Introduction

Portal vein embolization (PVE) is an established preoperative procedure that reduces the risk of liver failure by inducing hypertrophy of the future liver remnant (FLR) before major hepatectomy [1, 2]. Various factors influence the extent of FLR hypertrophy, including patient age, nutritional status, comorbidities, liver disease, liver function, prior chemotherapy, and initial FLR volume [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. Notably, in addition to these background factors, recanalization of the embolized portal vein has been reported to significantly decrease the hypertrophy rate, potentially compromising the planned surgical strategy [5]. However, the clinical characteristics of recanalization, including its incidence, risk factors, and impact on surgical outcomes, have not been systematically evaluated in a large cohort.

Re‐embolization (re‐PVE) is a potential therapeutic option for patients with insufficient FLR growth due to recanalization. Animal studies have demonstrated that repeated PVE promotes liver regeneration; however, the efficacy and safety of re‐PVE have not yet been evaluated in clinical practice [9, 10]. This therapeutic strategy may be particularly important for patients with biliary tract cancer, for whom radical hepatectomy represents the only curative treatment option. Insufficient hepatic reserve, including recanalization‐related failure of liver hypertrophy, can result in postoperative liver failure in patients undergoing major hepatectomy. Therefore, we hypothesized that re‐PVE in patients with recanalization may enhance outcomes and improve subsequent surgical results.

The present study aimed to (1) elucidate the incidence and risk factors of recanalization after PVE in a large cohort, (2) quantitatively assess the impact of recanalization on liver regeneration, and (3) evaluate the efficacy and safety of re‐PVE.

Material and Methods

Patients

Consecutive patients who underwent PVE of the right portal branch before right hemihepatectomy or right trisectionectomy between 2010 and 2022 at Shizuoka Cancer Center were included. Patients who underwent PVE for branches of segment 4 were excluded. Patients who were scheduled for right hemihepatectomy or trisectionectomy after right PVE but did not undergo hepatectomy due to the identification of unresectable disease were excluded. We followed the Brisbane 2000 terminology for defining the liver anatomy, type of hepatectomy, and recanalization area [11]. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Shizuoka Cancer Center (J2022‐185‐2022‐1‐3). This study was conducted in accordance with the STROBE guidelines [12].

PVE Procedure

PVE was performed 3–4 weeks before the planned operation when the FLR ratio was estimated to be < 40% of the total liver volume or when the patients had poor functional hepatic reserve [8]. All PVE procedures were performed under local anesthesia by experienced radiologists using the ipsilateral transhepatic approach, a standardized method demonstrated to be safe at our institution. Following portal venography, gelatin sponge particles mixed with contrast medium were injected to embolize the right portal vein branches. After embolization, final portography confirmed the occlusion in all right portal branches of the right hemiliver (segments 5–8 in the Couinaud classification), while unrestricted portal flow of the left hemiliver and caudate was confirmed [11, 13]. Finally, one or two metallic coils were placed in the puncture tract for hemostasis. On the first day post‐PVE, ultrasound examination confirmed no recanalization of the right portal vein branch, anterior segment branch, or posterior segment branch. In patients with obstructive jaundice, PVE was performed after the serum total bilirubin levels had decreased to < 2.0 mg/dL following biliary drainage [8]. In the portal vein anatomy, based on previous studies, bifurcation type was classified as normal, whereas trifurcation or independent right posterior types from the main portal vein were altogether classified as variant [14, 15].

Volumetric Assessment

Using a standardized protocol, contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CT) was performed before and 2 weeks after PVE [8]. The FLR was defined as the left hemiliver (segments 2, 3, and 4 in the Couinaud classification) for standardization across different surgical procedures [11, 13]. The total liver and FLR volumes were measured independently by two experienced surgeons using dedicated software (SYNAPSE VINCENT; Fuji Film Co., Tokyo, Japan). The FLR ratio and degree of hypertrophy (DH) were calculated using the following formulae [7, 16]:

FLR ratio (%) = (FLR volume/total liver volume) × 100.

DH (%) = FLR ratio after PVE—FLR ratio before PVE.

Using standardized liver volume in Japanese people, the kinetic growth rate (KGR) was calculated by the following formulae [17, 18]:

Standardized total liver volume (ml) = 706.2 × body surface area (m2) + 2.4.

sFLR ratio (%) = (FLR volume/standardized total liver volume) × 100.

KGR (%) = (sFLR ratio after PVE—sFLR ratio before PVE)/time elapsed since PVE (weeks).

Assessment of Liver Function

An indocyanine green (ICG) retention test was performed before and 2 weeks after PVE (and re‐PVE, if applicable) using a standard dose of 0.5 mg/kg body weight. The indocyanine green clearance of true future liver remnant (ICGK‐F) was calculated as ICGK × future liver remnant ratio, according to the surgical procedure. Based on our institutional criteria and previous studies, a cut‐off value of 0.05 was used as the minimum acceptable ICGK‐F for major hepatectomy [8, 19, 20].

Evaluation of Recanalization

Recanalization was assessed using contrast‐enhanced CT 2 weeks after PVE. Recanalization was defined as the presence of contrast enhancement in the area of the embolized right portal branches demonstrating macroscopic portal venous flow during the portal phase, in the absence of arterial hyperperfusion during the arterial phase [5]. Recanalization on CT was evaluated by one surgeon under the supervision of radiologists, and cases with uncertain interpretations were resolved through consultation with the radiologists. Moreover, recanalization was classified as major (section or segment) or minor (less than one segment) according to the recanalized area (Figure 1). Multiple minor recanalized areas were also defined as minor recanalization.

Re‐PVE Procedure and Preoperative Assessment

Re‐PVE was considered for patients with ICGK‐F < 0.05 and confirmed recanalization. Among these patients, candidates for re‐PVE were selected after the surgical team carefully evaluated their clinical condition. The procedure was performed using the same embolization materials as the initial PVE but through different puncture routes due to the presence of previous coils. The preprocedural planning included three‐dimensional (3D) image reconstruction of the optimal route selection (Figure 2). Follow‐up and liver function tests were performed 2 weeks after re‐PVE. Regardless of whether re‐PVE was performed, gadolinium ethoxybenzyl diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid–enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (Gd‐EOB‐DTPA–enhanced MRI) was conducted preoperatively in all patients without contraindications to evaluate the feasibility of curative resection.

Statistical Analyses

Categorical variables were compared using Fisher's exact test or the chi‐square test, as appropriate. All continuous variables are expressed as medians with ranges and were compared between two groups using the Mann–Whitney U test. The Kruskal‐Wallis test was used to compare continuous variables among three groups, and the Steel‐Dwass test was used for post hoc analysis. To identify predictors of DH, a multivariate linear regression analysis was conducted using variables including recanalization. Standardized beta coefficients (β) were calculated to assess the relative contribution of each predictor. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify predictors of major recanalization. For laboratory parameters, cut‐off values were determined using normal limits at our institution. Medians were used as cut‐off values for age, body mass index, FLR before PVE, and ICGK. Variables with p < 0.2 in univariate analysis were entered into the multivariate model. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was used to compare paired data before and after PVE (and re‐PVE). All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.1.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient Characteristics

Between 2010 and 2022, 271 consecutive patients underwent right PVE. Of these, 22 patients did not undergo hepatectomy due to the identification of unresectable findings: peritoneal dissemination (n = 8), distant lymph node metastases (n = 8), liver metastases (n = 4), locally advanced disease (n = 1), and positive peritoneal lavage cytology (n = 1). Hepatectomy was performed in 249 patients as planned, resulting in an overall resection rate of 91.9%. Of these, seven patients required re‐PVE before hepatectomy due to insufficient liver hypertrophy associated with recanalization. This study focused on the 249 patients who underwent hepatectomy following right PVE. Details of the patient characteristics are shown in Table 1. Most patients had perihilar cholangiocarcinoma or liver metastases, most underwent right hemihepatectomy, and approximately one‐third required combined bile duct resection and reconstruction. Preoperatively, 40% of the patients underwent biliary drainage and 15% underwent chemotherapy. The median duration between initial PVE and hepatectomy across all patients was 27 days.

Incidence and Impact of Recanalization

Recanalization was observed in 89 patients (36%), with minor and major recanalization observed in 55 and 34 patients, respectively (Table 2). There were statistically significant differences among the three groups in median DH and KGR (Kruskal‐Wallis test, p = 0.001 and p = 0.003, respectively). Subsequent post hoc analysis using the Steel‐Dwass test revealed that major recanalization was associated with a significantly lower median DH (p = 0.002) and KGR (p = 0.003) than non‐recanalization. Although previous study reported that the formation of porto‐portal collaterals attenuated liver hypertrophy [21], no porto‐portal collaterals were observed on CT in the present study.

There were no significant differences in other variables, including anticoagulant use and portal vein variant.

Outcome of Re‐PVE

Re‐PVE was performed in seven patients (five with major and two with minor recanalization) who showed insufficient FLR growth due to recanalization (Table 5). The median duration between the first PVE and re‐PVE was 27 days. All re‐PVE procedures were performed without complications. After re‐PVE, the FLR ratio significantly increased from after the first PVE (34.2% to 39.8%, p = 0.003). In particular, the FLR ratio after re‐PVE significantly improved after the first PVE in patients with major recanalization (29.5% to 43.3%, p = 0.031), whereas no significant improvement was observed in those with minor recanalization (Figure 3). Among patients with major recanalization, those who underwent re‐PVE had significantly lower FLR volumes and ratios than those who did not (data not shown). In case 2, the tumors were enlarged and unresectable on CT after re‐PVE. Tumor shrinkage of tumors was achieved following systemic chemotherapy. Subsequently, hepatectomy was performed following another PVE.

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that 36% of patients who underwent hepatectomy following right PVE experienced recanalization, which was significantly associated with impaired FLR hypertrophy. To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive analysis to investigate the efficacy of re‐PVE as a treatment strategy for recanalization in a large cohort of 249 patients. Notably, major recanalization was significantly associated with impaired liver regeneration compared to non‐recanalization. Re‐embolization showed a particular benefit in selected cases of major recanalization, suggesting important implications for post‐PVE management strategies.

Previous studies have reported varying recanalization rates, ranging from 0.9% to 39.8%, primarily attributed to differences in embolic materials [5, 7, 17, 22]. Our relatively high recanalization rate (36%) may be attributed to the use of absorbable gelatin sponge particles. While N‐butyl‐2‐cyanoacrylate (NBCA) is effective for inducing FLR hypertrophy [23], it has several drawbacks. PVE with NBCA is often performed via a contralateral approach [24], which carries the risks of complications in the remnant liver, such as biliary bleeding, arterio‐portal shunt formation, or portal thrombosis. To protect the remnant liver, an ipsilateral approach is preferred. However, using NBCA on the ipsilateral side requires Amplatzer plugs or occlusion balloons to prevent reflux into the portal vein of the FLR [25], making the procedure costly. Therefore, we adopted the ipsilateral approach using a gelatin sponge, as our standard technique. In this study, despite the higher recanalization rate, most patients achieved sufficient liver hypertrophy and underwent planned hepatectomy without requiring re‐PVE. To further reduce recanalization rates while maintaining safety, we have recently introduced PVE using a gelatin sponge with balloon occlusion [26].

A key finding of this study is that major recanalization was an independent negative predictor of DH. This observation is mechanistically plausible. PVE aims to induce hypertrophy by redirecting portal blood flow from the embolized lobe to the FLR [3, 27, 28]. This abrupt increase in portal perfusion is known to trigger a cascade of regenerative events, including the upregulation of various humoral mediators and subsequent activation of intrahepatocyte signal transduction [3]. However, the occurrence of major recanalization likely counteracts this intended hemodynamic shift, thereby attenuating these crucial regenerative stimuli and resulting in an impaired hypertrophic response.

A higher initial FLR ratio was also identified as an independent negative predictor of DH. This finding is consistent with previous reports and can be explained by the fundamental mechanism of PVE [4, 8]. A larger baseline FLR inherently means that the volume of the liver to be embolized is smaller. Consequently, the magnitude of portal blood flow redistribution, the primary trigger of hepatic regeneration, is diminished, leading to a weaker regenerative stimulus and a less pronounced hypertrophic response. This suggests that while a high initial FLR is favorable for safety, it may paradoxically limit the regenerative potential achievable with PVE. In patients with favorable liver function, as in this study, the FLR criteria for PVE may be modified.

Although no direct reports have linked low ChE levels to recanalization, our study identified this association. Low ChE levels can serve as a surrogate marker for impaired hepatic synthetic function, potentially reflecting a fragile hemostatic balance. In such a state, hyperfibrinolysis may lead to premature lysis of the thrombus around the embolic material [29, 30]. Consequently, for patients with low ChE levels, the use of permanent embolic materials is recommended to prevent recanalization and ensure sufficient liver regeneration. In contrast, the prothrombin time (PT) and albumin levels, as traditional markers of synthetic function, were not associated with major recanalization. This discrepancy is likely due to selection bias in our cohort. Since the study consisted exclusively of candidates for major hepatectomy (Child‐Pugh class A), PT values were normal in nearly all cases (abnormal in only 1.6%), leaving insufficient variance to detect a difference. Furthermore, albumin levels were likely confounded by inflammation. The cohort included a high proportion of patients with cholangiocarcinoma (45%) and undergoing preoperative biliary drainage (40%). As albumin acts as a negative acute‐phase reactant, its levels may have reflected the presence of cholangitis rather than pure hepatic reserve. While no significant difference was observed regarding portal vein variants, future studies with larger cohorts are needed to further elucidate the impact of anatomical and technical factors on recanalization.

In this study, we evaluated liver hypertrophy using DH, which is more clinically relevant for assessing the planned residual liver volume and ratio than the “hypertrophy ratio” used in our previous report [8]. In that previous analysis, ALP and ChE were identified as independent predictors of insufficient hypertrophy, as assessed by the hypertrophy ratio, using logistic regression with a dichotomized endpoint. However, they were not significant predictors for DH in the current multivariate linear regression analysis. Linear regression using continuous outcomes preserves more statistical information than logistic regression with dichotomized endpoints, which may explain the different predictors identified. Prospective validation with standardized methodology and larger patient cohorts is warranted to establish consistent predictors.

In the present study, recanalization was assessed using enhanced CT. However, a previous study has demonstrated the utility of Gd‐EOB‐DTPA–enhanced MRI for evaluating post‐PVE changes [31]. Although Gd‐EOB‐DTPA–enhanced MRI was not routinely performed after PVE in our cohort due to cost and our protocol, it may offer superior evaluation of both the FLR function and recanalization, identified by the enhancement gradient or demarcation line in the hepatobiliary phase. Furthermore, particularly when surgery is delayed for any reason, Gd‐EOB‐DTPA–enhanced MRI can also play a crucial role in oncological surveillance.

Re‐embolization has demonstrated remarkable effectiveness in patients with insufficient FLR growth due to major recanalization. Despite technical challenges due to previous coil placement, we successfully performed the procedure through alternative portal branches in seven patients through meticulous preprocedural planning, including 3D CT‐based route simulation. However, as in case 2, the prolonged waiting period for surgery remains a significant oncological concern. In patients undergoing re‐PVE, except for case 2, the median waiting time from the initial PVE to surgery was 60 days. Optimizing this interval is critical. Therefore, the strategy should be individualized based on risk profiles. In patients at high risk of recanalization or those requiring substantial liver hypertrophy, upfront use of non‐absorbable embolic materials or PVE combined with hepatic vein embolization (liver venous deprivation) may be considered [32]. Associating liver partition and portal vein ligation for staged hepatectomy (ALPPS) may be an option when rapid and substantial hypertrophy is required, particularly when conventional PVE is unlikely to achieve adequate FLR growth [33]. For patients with insufficient hypertrophy due to recanalization, early detection and timely intervention minimize surgical delay and potentially improve oncological outcomes. Further studies are needed to establish evidence‐based selection criteria for these strategies.

The present study had some limitations. First, its retrospective single‐center design and small number of re‐embolization cases may limit its generalizability. Second, selection bias may have existed in the re‐PVE group, as the candidates were chosen based on a comprehensive clinical assessment. Third, our evaluation timing at 2 weeks after PVE was relatively early compared to that in previous studies, although this enabled prompt decision‐making for additional interventions [5, 17, 22]. Fourth, we did not identify any clear porto‐portal collaterals on CT in this study. However, it cannot be proven that there was no non‐visible collateralization or recanalization on CT because enhanced MRI after PVE or intraoperative portography was not routinely performed. Fifth, interobserver reproducibility for the assessment of recanalization was not formally evaluated. Although difficult cases were discussed with radiologists, systematic evaluation using multiple independent reviewers with kappa statistics was not performed. Sixth, as this was an exploratory analysis, the predictors identified may differ from those in previous studies depending on outcome definitions and analytical methods. Finally, we did not compare our strategy with other interventions and embolic materials for FLR growth. Prospective studies are required to establish standardized protocols for the management of FLR hypertrophy, including consideration of recanalization.

In conclusion, recanalization following right PVE was significantly associated with impaired liver regeneration, particularly in recanalized areas of liver section or segment. Re‐PVE has been indicated as a safe and effective strategy for managing insufficient FLR growth due to major recanalization. Further prospective validation is required to elucidate its benefits.
