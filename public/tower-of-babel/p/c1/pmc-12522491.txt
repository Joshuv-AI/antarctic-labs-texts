Quantifying human pluripotent stem cell attributes with population balance modeling
Authors: Aedan Brown, Demetrios M. Stoukides, Emmanuel S. Tzanakakis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe manufacturing of stem cell-based therapeutics hinges on models linking product characteristics to bioprocess conditions and cellular physiology. Typical quantitative models of cellular systems rely on population-average properties but fail to inform about the state of the cells based on critical quality attributes. Frameworks capturing the inherent heterogeneous nature of isogenic stem cell populations depend on physiological state functions (PSFs), which represent distributions of rates of cellular content change, division and differentiation. Such modalities can offer invaluable biological insights and facilitate the robust biomanufacturing of stem cell products, but obtaining the PSFs presents significant challenges.MethodsPopulation balance equation (PBE) modeling was implemented to derive stem cell PSFs. Their extraction from experimental data requires the acquisition of relevant distributions for newborn and dividing cells. These subpopulations for human embryonic and induced pluripotent stem cells were identified and analyzed with multiplex flow cytometry. Solution of the PBE model featuring appropriate PSFs was achieved via interval-of-quiescence techniques.ResultsRate distributions of synthesis, division and differentiation of human stem cells are presented. These PSFs were calculated for the first time capitalizing on experimental analysis of stem cell ensembles, including mitotic and newborn cells. Here, the PSFs were linked to the pluripotency marker POU5F1 (OCT4) as a descriptor of hPSC state and they followed a similar unimodal distribution over the OCT4 cargo for the stem cell lines examined. Exogenous lactate, which decelerates growth without affecting pluripotency marker expression, suppressed the PSF range revealing notable differences across the stem cell lines. The findings also pointed to line-specific effects induced by stressors such as high extracellular lactate. Lastly, the derivation was demonstrated of PSFs based on intracellular NANOG utilizing the PSF distributions extracted for OCT4.ConclusionsThe work provides a first account of the derivation of rate distributions – rather than population averages – of stem cell physiological properties including division and change in OCT4 content. This enables the implementation of modeling frameworks for the rigorous quantitative description of hPSC populations that is important for addressing fundamental biological questions about pluripotency and differentiation, and critical in the biomanufacturing of hPSC-based therapeutics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04668-w.

Introduction

Cell populations are characterized by innate heterogeneity with intrinsic properties such as the content of various proteins, RNA, and DNA presented as distributions rather than singular values. These are linked to the growth, protein synthesis, and differentiation kinetics, which however are treated as average characteristics of sizable populations largely ignoring the diversity of ensembles of isogenic cells. Human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) are heterogenous affecting their fate decisions [1]. Despite their simplicity, population-average approaches including non-corpuscular models fail to fully describe essential properties of hPSC cultures. For instance, cell growth is typically modeled as a function of the concentration of a single substrate (e.g., Monod kinetics) although hPSC growth depends on multiple substrates (e.g., glucose, glutamine) and growth factors (e.g., basic FGF). In cell therapy settings, the quality of the cells is assessed on attributes (critical quality attributes or CQAs) such as pluripotency marker expression which enter the quality target product profile (QTPP) for quality-by-design (QbD) analysis in commercial settings. Knowing how such CQAs evolve with time and understanding influencing factors are important to advance the manufacturing of hPSC-based therapeutics.

Corpuscular and structured models comprising population balance equations (PBEs), which entail cell trait distributions including expressed marker proteins, are well-suited for describing hPSC populations. Such models encompass intrinsic physiological state functions (PSFs) [2] capturing the rates of division, cell growth or protein synthesis, partitioning, and differentiation as distributions. Knowledge of the PSFs allows the description and prediction of dynamics of hPSC ensembles and the association of single-cell to population-wide properties. The PSFs are valid constants for a given physiological state of a cell even when the population is in a transient mode with the overall distribution of physiological states changing with time [2], for example, during hPSC differentiation. The PSFs offer a rigorous way to depict the culture dynamics on first principles.

PBE-based models are multiscale and thus suitable for combining various processes transpiring at different temporal (e.g., gene expression, cell mitosis) and spatial scales (e.g., single cell-specific vs. population-wide events). The versatility of cell PBEs has been demonstrated with their application in microbial [3, 4], yeast [5] and animal cell systems [6, 7]. Specifically for hPSCs, material partitioning during mitosis and the ensuing population diversity [8] have been described using PBEs, as well as the link between the propensity for hPSC differentiation and culture density [9]. Calculation of the PSFs involves solving a challenging inverse problem [10–12], but in most instances the form of PSFs is assumed introducing suboptimal accuracy. Effort then focuses on solving the PBE to obtain the distribution of the cell population based on a single or multiple traits. For example, a two-PBE model has been implemented for cell cycle, proliferation and differentiation [9] of hPSCs in culture, where an analytical form of each PSF was assumed including the PSF for differentiation described by first-order kinetics.

Yet, the derivation of the PSFs is possible as demonstrated for microbial [13, 14] and yeast [5] cell populations under balanced growth. Here, we provide a first account of the theoretical derivation of PSFs for hPSCs including the rates of synthesis, division and differentiation over a variable(s) describing the state of the cells (state vector). The theoretical framework is implemented considering the cellular content of the transcription factor POU5F1 (OCT4), which is a CQA of pluripotent hPSC populations. The PSFs of the increase in OCT4 content and division intensity are determined experimentally for human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) and embryonic stem cells (hESCs) under routine maintenance conditions and in excess lactate. A downward shift in the PSFs of the rates of intracellular OCT4 increase and hPSC division reflects the observed deceleration in hPSC growth in the presence of exogenous lactate. The results yield insights on the physiology of cultured hPSCs and are aligned with previous studies on the role of an optimal intracellular OCT4 levels for maintenance of pluripotency. This framework can be applied to the derivation of PSFs based on multiple cell traits, thereby improving the predictive capacity and accuracy of relevant PBE models as digital twins in the biomanufacturing of hPSC-based therapeutics.

Materials and methods

Human pluripotent stem cell culture

Human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) H9 (passages 60–70) and induced pluripotent cells (hiPSCs) IMR90-4 (passages 40–50) were expanded and maintained in hPSC medium (StemMACS iPS-Brew XF, Miltenyi Biotec, Cambridge, MA) in dishes coated with Matrigel (Corning, Corning, NY). The cells were passaged upon incubation with Accumax (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and replated at 2 × 104/cm2 in hPSC medium with 10 µM ROCK inhibitor Y-27,632 (for at least 24 h). Sodium L-lactate (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) was dissolved in hPSC media to a final concentration as stated. Cell counts and viability were determined by Trypan Blue dye (ThermoFisher) exclusion using a cell counter (TC20, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). The cells are routinely checked for mycoplasma by qPCR (Takara Bio USA, San Jose, CA) and for karyotypic abnormalities as we reported [15].

Flow cytometry

In preparation for flow cytometry, cells were harvested after treatment with Accumax and centrifugation at 500xg for 5 min. The cell pellet was washed by resuspending in PBS and spun down before the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Alfa Aesar, Tewksbury, MA) in PBS for 10 min. Cell permeabilization, EdU (5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine) staining and detection were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol using the Click-iT™ EdU Alexa Fluor™ 647 Flow Cytometry Assay Kit (ThermoFisher). Antibodies were added for: phosphorylated histone H3 (pHH3; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA), and OCT4 (Miltenyi). Cells were also treated with 4 µg/ml Hoechst 33,342 (ThermoFisher) for 15 min. Samples stained with antibodies and corresponding isotypes (BD Pharmingen™ Alexa Fluor® 647 IgG1 Isotype Control) were processed on an Attune NxT flow cytometer (ThermoFisher) and the results were analyzed with the FCS Express software (v. 7, De Novo Software, Glendale, CA).

Cell cycle analysis

The G2/M phase length was determined by mitotically arresting the cells with 100 ng/ml KaryoMAX™ colcemid (ThermoFisher) after a 60-min pulse of 10 µM EdU and subsequent washes with culture medium to remove unincorporated EdU [9]. Treated cells were maintained for up to 12 h. The cells were analyzed at different times as stated by staining with EdU and Hoechst 33,342 and running flow cytometry yielding the temporal evolution of the fraction of EdU+ cells. Initially, the fraction of EdU+ cells remained constant. This period represents the duration of the G2/M phase [16]. Subsequently, the fraction of EdU+ cells with >1x DNA content increased eventually reaching a plateau. This time span corresponds to the length of the S phase.

Extraction of distributions for newborn and dividing cells

Pluripotent cells were plated for 48 h before being incubated with EdU. The duration of the EdU incubation was equal to the length of the G2/M phase determined as described above. Then, the cells were harvested at different time points as stated, fixed and stained with Hoechst 33,342 and antibodies for pHH3 and OCT4. To extract the OCT4 distribution of newborn cells from flow cytometry data, EdU+ cells with 1x DNA content were gated based on Hoechst staining. Analogously, dividing cells were gated as those which were positive for pHH3 with 2x DNA (Hoechst staining) [17].

Statistical analysis

Results are expressed as mean ± SD, unless stated otherwise, from at least three independent experiments with each experimental point analyzed in triplicates. Comparative analysis was carried out using Prism (v.9 GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA).

Extraction of PSFs for self-renewing stem cells in culture

Having established a workflow for the calculation of PSFs, the rates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:R\left(x\right)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varGamma\:\left(x\right)$$\end{document} were determined for hPSCs cultured under routine maintenance conditions. The extraction of the actual PSFs is contingent on the acquisition of the distributions nT, nd and nnb. Moreover, the content of OCT4 was considered as the state attribute of the hPSCs linking the PSFs to the cells’ pluripotent state. OCT4 is highly expressed in undifferentiated stem cells and germline cells but is downregulated upon lineage commitment [21, 22]. It is considered a master regulator of transcriptional networks determining the self-renewal of stem cells [23]. For this purpose, cultured H9 hESCs and IMR90-4 hiPSCs were analyzed by flow cytometry to obtain single-cell multiplex information.

While the acquisition of the total distribution nT of OCT4 for the population of hPSCs is straightforward, obtaining the OCT4 profile nd requires the identification of the mitotic cell subpopulation. To this end, dividing hPSCs display phosphorylated histone H3 (pHH3) [17] and DNA content that is double that of cells residing in the G1 phase (Fig. 2A). Thus, the distribution nd was extracted for pHH3+ cells residing in the right hand-side mode of the bimodal distribution of DNA (2x amount of DNA). In both H9 hESC and IMR90 hiPSC cultures the fraction of mitotic cells identified was 2.5–3.5%. All dividing cells were positive for OCT4 with a distribution that exhibited limited dispersion compared to that of the total population (nT).

On the other hand, extraction of the respective distribution for newborn cells, nnb, requires knowledge of the duration of the various cell cycle phases. For this reason, cells were exposed to an EdU pulse under growth-arresting conditions. The subpopulation of EdU+ cells, particularly with 2x DNA content, was recorded over time (Suppl. Figure 6–7) [9]. Cells uptake EdU during active DNA synthesis (S phase). In the presence of the mitosis inhibitor colcemid, EdU+ cells transition through the S phase and into the G2/M stage where their DNA amount is twice greater than that for cells in the G1 phase but their entry into the G1 phase is prevented. As a result, EdU+/2x DNA cells accumulate until their fraction reaches a plateau. The temporal evolution of this subpopulation allows calculation of the lengths of the G2/M and S phases. By extension, the duration of G1 can be estimated by subtracting the time spans of G2/M and S phases from the cell doubling time, which is determined experimentally. For both H9 hESCs and IMR90 hiPSCs, the G2/M and S phases exhibited similar lengths, 5 h and 7 h, respectively. The slight difference in doubling time between the two hPSC lines (20.8 h for IMR90 vs. 18.8 h for H9 cells) translated to a similar difference in the duration of their G1 phase.

Knowledge of the intervals of the various cycle segments allowed proper timing of sampling the cultured cells to obtain the distribution for the newborn stem cells. Cells exposed to an EdU pulse were sampled 5 h later (G2/M phase length) and stained with the Hoechst 33,342 dye. At this time point, cells which incorporated EdU at the end of the S phase, transitioned through the G2/M phase and their daughter cells just about entered the G1 phase. Hence, EdU+ cells with 1x DNA content were considered as newly divided cells (Fig. 2B). For the two hPSC lines examined here, newborn cells accounted for 5% of the population and over 90% were OCT4+.

Before calculating the rate of change in OCT4 content and division intensity, the requisite distributions were rescaled given that only cells with OCT4 expression beyond 99% of the isotype control samples were considered as positive in line with standard practice in flow cytometry analysis. Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:F\left(c\right)$$\end{document} was computed as the ratio of the number of truncated data points to the number of total points collected. This rescaled PDF is equal to the true PDF of the data but is only defined above the isotype-based cutoff, and thus the PSFs is as well. The rescaled PDFs obtained from multiple datasets were averaged to obtain a single density function for each cell subpopulation (Fig. 2C-D). Because of the cutoff based on the isotype control, the rescaled PDFs exhibit fluctuations at the lower end of the OCT4 range as seen primarily for the nT and nnb distributions. Yet, these do not translate to prominent undulating patterns of the calculated PSFs (see below).

Subsequently, the PSFs were calculated for IMR90 and H9 cells (Fig. 2E-F). Similar to the results obtained using synthetic PSFs (Fig. 1), the information beyond the 90th percentile of OCT4+ cells exhibits variability which is more challenging to interpret. For both hPSC lines considered here, the division intensity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varGamma\:\left(OCT4\right)$$\end{document} exhibits a sharp rise peaking close to the 90th quantile and subsequently dips. Even at the highest OCT4 content, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varGamma\:\left(OCT4\right)$$\end{document} is still greater than that for cells at the lower end of OCT4 range, where newly divided cells reside. Calculation of OCT4-dependent PSFs for self-renewing stem cells with exogenously added lactate

Lactate, a product of glycolytic metabolism, slows down the proliferation of various cell types. Pluripotent hPSCs rely on glycolysis for their homeostasis [24] and, similar to other cells, lactate decelerates their expansion [25]. When hPSCs were maintained in medium with added lactate, their average growth rate decreased from 3.4 × 10− 2 h− 1 for H9 cells and 3.3 × 10− 2 h− 1 for IMR90 cells to 1.9 × 10− 2 h− 1 for both lines. Yet, pluripotency was maintained as assessed by the fraction of OCT4+ cells after 3 days of culture. In the presence of lactate, over 94% of the cells displayed OCT4.

Transformation of OCT4-based PSFs to NANOG-dependent PSFs

The requisite distributions for the calculation of the PSFs are PDFs and as such, they can be transformed to obtain the corresponding distributions dependent on another cell trait. For instance, the transcription factor NANOG serves as an alternative marker of stem cell pluripotency, interacting with OCT4 in global epigenetic regulatory networks which repress developmental genes and regulate cell cycle transitions [26, 27]. Here, a transformation function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:f$$\end{document} that relates NANOG to OCT4 should be obtained i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:OCT4\:=\:f\left(NANOG\right)$$\end{document}. Then, a distribution can be transformed from OCT4 to NANOG as the state variable:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{n}_{i}\left(NANOG\right)={\text{n}}_{i}\left(OCT4\right)\bullet\:\frac{df\left(NANOG\right)}{dNANOG}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{n}_{i}$$\end{document} is any of the total, newborn, and dividing cell distributions.

Then, the cell PSFs based on NANOG can be calculated as already described or by converting the PSFs for OCT4:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{PSF}_{NANOG}\left(NANOG\right)={PSF}_{OCT4}\left(f\left(NANOG\right)\right)$$\end{document}

Here, PSFNANOG and PSFOCT4 are the PSF expressions with NANOG or OCT4 content as the independent variable, respectively. Of note, the distributions \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{n}_{i}$$\end{document} are PDFs whereas R and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varGamma\:$$\end{document} are not. To this end, the mapping between NANOG and OCT4 was represented using a linear transformation of flow cytometry data for each hPSC line (Supp. Figures 8–9) allowing the calculation of the distributions of NANOG and the respective PSFs (Fig. 4). When compared to the corresponding distributions of OCT4, the ones based on NANOG content play out over lower protein values and the same is observed for the derived PSFs. These results show that once PSFs are extracted for a cell trait, derivation for an alternate dependent trait is straightforward hinging on knowledge of the relation between them. This not only facilitates PSF calculations but addresses the issue of re-acquiring the newborn and dividing cell distributions for a particular marker, which presents considerable challenges.

Discussion

In this work, the derivation and calculation based on experimental information is reported of the hPSC growth rate and division intensity linked to OCT4 content as the physiological state variable. The PSFs are assessed at a single cell- rather than population-level unlike common empirical approaches (e.g., Monod kinetics) relating population-averaged growth to bioprocess operation-relevant variables such as substrate concentration. The latter associate the evolution of cell ensemble size to adjustable variables including feed concentration, but provide no information about the state of the cells, their heterogeneity and critical quality attributes. The specific rate of change in OCT4 content, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:R$$\end{document}, and the division intensity, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varGamma\:$$\end{document}, can be used, for instance, in PBE models which capture important process traits.

Traditional first principle-based models entail material balances on the cells, and various substrates and metabolic products, most often including the product of interest (e.g., recombinant antibody). A major limitation of these models becomes apparent when applied to bioprocesses for cellular therapeutics where the cells are the actual product. This limitation is exacerbated in stem cell populations, which exhibit diverse content and responses to the extracellular milieu despite their isogenicity. To this end, our findings show that the division intensity follows a distribution rather than being monodisperse. For both H9 and IMR90 hPSCs, the form of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varGamma\:\left(OCT4\right)$$\end{document} shows that there is an OCT4 level at which the division rate is the highest. While less distinct for H9 than IMR90 hPSCs, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varGamma\:\left(OCT4\right)$$\end{document} decreases at higher content of OCT4. These results are aligned with experimental evidence relating OCT4 to stem cell self-renewal with an optimal range of OCT4 content for proliferation [28, 29], and cells with lower OCT4 amounts become trophectoderm while those with elevated OCT4 adopt other fates [28, 29].

In addition, the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:R\left(OCT4\right)$$\end{document} conforms to a similar pattern exhibiting a peak in OCT4 production rate around the middle of the OCT4 range. Whereas information on the hPSC production rate of OCT4 is not available, there are reports of an ideal range of OCT4 acting as a master regulator of stem cell pluripotency. Knocking down OCT4, which should suppress its rate of increase, induces endoderm differentiation [30]. As intracellular OCT4 is maintained above a threshold for preservation of the pluripotent state, the rate of OCT4 production may be reduced to divert resources to other essential cellular processes. This may also serve the tight regulation of OCT4 levels in conjunction with the action of other pluripotency transcription factors including SOX2 and NANOG. Indeed, these transcription factors partake in a highly interconnected pluripotency gene regulatory network controlling the OCT4 content [31, 32]. Moreover, OCT4 is targeted by multiple E3 ligases for ubiquitin-mediated degradation [33] further regulating OCT4 content during self-renewal [34, 35]. Deubiquitinating enzymes such as ubiquitin-specific protease 3, can counteract E3 ligases adding another layer of OCT4 control in stem cells [33]. OCT4 is also phosphorylated by c-Jun N-terminal kinases [36] and ERK [37] further increasing its proclivity for degradation.

The determination of the PSFs as demonstrated in this work, opens avenues for their application to describe hPSC populations and other therapeutically useful cell types in various applications from biomanufacturing to tissue engineering. In contrast to previous studies where the PSFs considered in animal cell PBEs were either theoretical (e.g., based on mass transport through a spherical cell [38]) or derived experimentally for bacteria, the PSFs here were directly extracted from cultures of hPSCs. Additionally, the generation is demonstrated of PSFs which depend on critical quality attributes (e.g., OCT4) that can now be captured dynamically via PBEs enabling outcome prediction and control of hPSC bioprocesses.

Estimation of PBE functions from relevant distribution data has been examined previously [10, 11, 39–41], mostly in non-biological settings including phenomena with particle aggregation and breakup, as well as in cell processes including bioreactor cultivation of stem cells as clusters [12]. The reported kernels [41–43] were calculated using “total” distribution data often making simplifying assumptions about the form of the PBE in order to solve the associated inverse problem [40–43]. Here, this challenge was largely addressed by considering hPSC subpopulations as reported for bacterial communities [11] analyzed with flow cytometry. The use of EdU and pHH3 to identify mitotic and newborn cells can be multiplexed for acquiring the distribution of various cell attributes, not only OCT4 as we reported here. Thus, PSFs can be calculated based on a state vector comprising these attributes.

Such multi-dimensional PBEs can be solved using interval-of-quiescence calculations [44] as reported here. This method is suitable for modeling processes spanning temporal and spatial scales, and it allows for the incorporation of stochastic phenomena [9, 19, 45]. The solution involves the generation of several sample trajectories as in Monte Carlo-based models. To this end, PSFs resulting from solving the PBE and the assumed synthetic PSFs aligned in the 10th – 90th percentile range of the state variable for the underlying total distribution. Yet, the solutions tended to degrade outside of this range similar to prior reports [40]. The accuracy of the scheme improved as the ensemble size increased. Improvements were also observed upon averaging across multiple simulation replicates (Suppl. Tables 2 and 4).

The differentiation PSF \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:D\left(x\right)$$\end{document} for hPSCs undergoing specification was also derived. The relative contributions of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:D\left(x\right)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varGamma\:\left(x\right)$$\end{document} to the PBE reflect the culture conditions and the balance between self-renewal and commitment. Higher \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:D\left(x\right)$$\end{document} values with lower \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varGamma\:\left(x\right)$$\end{document} are expected when cells undergo differentiation as shown experimentally [28, 29]. Extracting the D(x) under different conditions will be helpful to better understand the differentiation process, guide its optimization, and accelerate the development of relevant bioprocesses.

Conceivably, the PBE framework can be extended to higher-dimensional state vectors that allow for a richer description of stem cells, such as modeling multiple markers of pluripotency and differentiation. Such implementation is associated with challenges on both theoretical and experimental fronts [46]. On the theory side, analytical estimation of PSFs may scale poorly from one dimension to many. Experimentally, estimating a high-dimensional distribution over many markers may be challenged by an incomplete sampling of the joint distribution. Monte Carlo-based algorithms such as the one employed here can alleviate some of these challenges, but both portions provide directions for future work.

Conclusions

This work expands the repertoire of tools for modeling cell populations, which are naturally heterogeneous. Such tools further our knowledge of complex processes such as hPSC self-renewal and differentiation, with direct applications to the biomanufacturing of relevant therapeutics. The determination of PSFs linked to physiologically important characteristics of hPSCs such as OCT4 offers deep insights in stem cell biology, which may be impossible to obtain based solely on experimental approaches. Moreover, these can also contribute to the development of digital twins for prediction and control of stem cell bioprocesses in conjunction with QbD strategies.
