Evolutionary and Functional Insights of Leucine‐Rich Alpha‐2‐Glycoprotein 1 in Cetacean Wound Healing: The First Identification of LRG1 in the Bottlenose Dolphin (Tursiops truncatus)
Authors: Takahisa Hamano, Seiji Hobo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCetaceans exhibit remarkable wound‐healing abilities, an adaptation critical for survival in their aquatic environment. This study provides the first identification of leucine‐rich alpha‐2‐glycoprotein 1 (LRG1) in the bottlenose dolphin (Tursiops truncatus), focusing on its potential role in wound healing. LRG1, a member of the leucine‐rich repeat family, is involved in immune regulation, angiogenesis, and tissue remodeling in terrestrial mammals. We extracted and analyzed LRG1 from T. truncatus to detect basic evolutionary insights on cetacean wound healing. Comparative analyses indicated that T. truncatus LRG1 exhibits similarities with its terrestrial counterparts but may offer distinct adaptational characters. These findings represent a pivotal first step toward elucidating the molecular evolutionary mechanisms of cetacean wound healing and highlight LRG1 as a promising target for future veterinary clinical applications.Keywords: cetacean, LRG1, molecular evolution, wound healing

Cetaceans are mammals that evolved from terrestrial animals approximately 50 million years ago (Thewissen and Bajpai 2001) and have fully adapted to aquatic environments. Various physiological adaptations have occurred during their transition from land to sea, including changes in their respiratory, circulatory, sensory, and integumentary systems. These adaptations show the distinct challenges of the aquatic environment, which is completely different from land. Notably, the skin system of cetaceans differs owing to its exposure to water rather than air. Cetaceans are known for their remarkable wound‐healing abilities (Wells et al. 2008), which differ from those of terrestrial animals at the molecular level (Kang et al. 2023). A recent study has identified cetacean‐specific modifications in key blood coagulation and immune pathways, suggesting that distinct evolutionary pressures may have driven their enhanced regenerative capacity (Kang et al. 2023).

Leucine‐rich alpha‐2‐glycoprotein 1 (LRG1) is a member of the leucine‐rich repeat (LRR) family and is prominently expressed in the liver and neutrophils of humans (Camilli et al. 2022). Its various functions include preventing cell death in association with cytochrome c, promoting pathological angiogenesis in tumors, supporting epithelial cell proliferation, and contributing to wound‐healing (Camilli et al. 2022). Research on genes involved in the biological response of cetaceans after injury has revealed the unique characteristics of coagulation factors and transforming growth factor‐β (TGF‐β) (Kang et al. 2023); however, the role and presence of cetacean LRG1 remains unexplored. In this study, we aimed to provide an evolutionary medicine perspective on cetacean LRG1 and to elucidate its biochemical properties through in silico evolutionary analysis, ultimately seeking to identify potential clinical applications.

Most of the methods used in this study are similar to those used in previous studies (Hamano et al. 2023; Hamano et al. 2024). First, the liver was obtained postmortem (more than 12 h, less than 24 h after death) from the carcass of a female bottlenose dolphin (Tursiops trucatus), which was born and raised in an aquarium and had died of a disease (Nueangphuet et al. 2022). The liver tissue was immersed in RNAlater (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Total RNA was extracted using the PureLink RNA mini kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and single‐stranded cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript II 1st strand cDNA synthesis kit (TaKaRa, Shiga, Japan). Primers were manually designed based on gene sequences encoding human and mouse LRG1 proteins registered in the NCBI database. Consequently, the gene sequence encoding the T. trucatus LRG1 (dLRG1) was identified. The cDNA encoding dLRG1 was cloned into pcDNA3.4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using the In‐Fusion Assembly enzyme (TaKaRa) and mass‐cultured in DynaCompetent Cells Jetgiga DH5α (Funakoshi, Tokyo, Japan). The mass‐cultured plasmid was transfected into Chinese Hamster Ovary (CHO) cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and an expression procedure was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions. CHO cells were grown at 37°C in an 8% CO2 humidified environment for transfection. From the second day after transfection, the cells were cultured at 32°C in a 5% CO2 humidified environment. On the ninth day, the CHO cells and supernatant were harvested and centrifuged at 2000 × g for 10 min to separate the cells. The supernatant was purified with a co‐carrier using a Talon Spin Column and HisTALON Buffer set (Takara). A portion of the purified protein was treated with PNGase F (Funakoshi) at 37°C for 18 h. Sodium dodecylsulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) analysis was performed on the purified product treated with glycosidase under both reducing and nonreducing conditions. NuPAGE MES SDS Running Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for electrophoresis at 200 V for 30 min. The molecular weight was calculated using the Novex Sharp Pre‐stained Protein Standard (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples on the polyacrylamide gel were transferred to Invitrogen polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) Paper Sandwich 0.45‐μm membranes (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in NuPAGE Transfer Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 30 V for 60 min. CBB‐R250 was used to stain PVDF membranes. In addition, the major band in the lane where the co‐purified product was electrophoresed under reducing conditions was excised and sequenced using a protein sequencer (SHIMADZU, PPSQ‐33A). Finally, an in silico analysis was performed based on the elucidated dLRG1 sequence. The tools used in silico were as follows: Signal IP (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/SignalP-5.0/) (Almagro Armenteros et al. 2019) for the prediction of signal peptide in dLRG1, Protoparam tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/) (Gasteiger et al. 2005), and the InterPro protein family database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/) (Blum et al. 2021) for the prediction of function of dLRG1, MUSCLE algorithm in MEGA (https://www.megasoftware.net/) (Kumar et al. 2018) for the alignment of LRGs, SWAKK (https://ibl.mdanderson.org/swakk/) (Liang et al. 2006) to calculate Ka/Ks for each codon between bovine LRG1 and dLRG1, HOMCOS (https://homcos.pdbj.org/?LANG=ja) (Kawabata 2016) for the 3D homology modeling of LRGs, eF‐surf (https://pdbj.org/eF-surf/top.do) (Kinoshita and Nakamura 2005) for the calculation of electrostatic surface potential of proteins, and STRING (https://string-db.org/) (Szklarczyk et al. 2023) for the interaction analysis of LRGs. Sequences from humans (NCBI Accession number: NP_443204.1), mice (NP_084072.1), rats (NP_001009717.1), and cows (NP_001039642.1), whose confirmed sequences are registered in the NCBI database, were used as terrestrial animal LRG1 references. RNA sequence datasets from the blood (Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) Accession number: GSE138765) and skin (GSE138935) of T. truncatus samples (Morey et al. 2016; Neely et al. 2018) were obtained from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The expression level of TGF‐β1 (NW_017843033.1;256917‐264560), SMAD1 (NW_017842585.1;22822632‐22900225), MAPK14 (NW_017844288.1;1166976.1242649), NF‐κB1 (NW_017842585.1;18126186‐18247777) and STAT3 (NW_017843467.1;1176797‐1243109) in each data set was compared with that of LRG1 (NW_017844100.1;209965‐214471) by calculating the Spearman's rank correlation coefficient (r).

To the best of our knowledge, this study presents the first report of total RNA extraction from the liver of a T. trucatus carcass and dLRG1 expression in mammalian cells, based on the first identified genetic information. The dLRG1 sequence is shown in Figure 1. The identified cDNA sequence consisted of 1041 base pairs, predicting a protein size of 38.39658 kDa and a theoretical isoelectric point of 8.32. The signal peptide was predicted to cleave between the 35th and 36th amino acids, and the protein sequencer demonstrating the signal peptide was as predicted. The dLRG1 amino acid sequence, predicted from the cDNA sequence, exhibited eight LRR structures, consistent with other animal species. According to the InterPro classification, the protein product is an LRR and transmembrane domain‐containing protein. SDS‐PAGE results showed that the major band was 40‐50 kDa under both reducing and nonreducing conditions (Figure 1). In addition, glycosidase treatment shifted the major band to 30‐40 kDa. The results of protein sequencing suggested that the protein constituting the major band in SDS‐PAGE was the target protein. The alignment results showed that the amino acid residues at positions 66, 109, 162, 192, 209, 234, 312, 333, and 334 were shared by the other four terrestrial animals but were different only in T. trucatus (Figure 1). STRING database analysis results suggested that this protein may bind with endoglin, adhesion G protein‐coupled receptor L1‐3, and lipopolysaccharide binding protein (Figure 2). RNA expression analysis of the skin transcriptome showed a weak positive correlation between LRG1 and SMAD1 expression (r = 0.200876) and a weak negative correlation between LRG1 and MAPK14 expression (r = −0.3448). Similarly, RNA expression analysis of the blood transcriptome showed weak positive correlations between the LRG1 and MAPK14 (r = 0.237485), LRG1 and NF‐κB1 (r = 0.361668), and LRG1 and STAT3 (r = 0.319781). However, it is important to note that the samples were not classified by clinical status in the original study (Morey et al. 2016; Neely et al. 2018).

Human LRG1, with a deglycosylated molecular weight of 34–36 kDa, increases to 55–60 kDa when glycosylated (Camilli et al. 2022). In contrast, while the deglycosylated dLRG1 expressed in this study showed a similar result, the size of the glycosylated recombinant dLRG1 was slightly smaller than that of the human one. Human LRG1 is known to have glycans at asparagine positions 79, 186, 269, and 325 and threonine position 37 (Yang et al. 2023). These residues were the same as those in dLRG1. The glycosylation of LRG1 differs depending on the cell from which it is secreted. For example, LRG1 produced by neutrophils differs from that produced in the liver (Camilli et al. 2022). These results suggest that recombinant dLRG1 synthesized in CHO cells may exhibit a glycosylation pattern different from that of human LRG1.

Calculation of Ka/Ks for each site, except for the signal peptide between cattle and bottlenose dolphins, showed that the Ka/Ks of codons encoding amino acid residues 68, 74, 83–92, 137–142, 144, 145, 203–206, and 235 exceeded 1.0 (Figure 3). Codon sites with a Ka/Ks greater than 1.0 may be under positive natural selection (Goldman and Yang 1994; Yang and Nielsen 2000). However, some of these substitutions may also reflect neutral evolution rather than adaptive divergence. Alternatively, even if the substitutions arose from adaptive divergence, these codon sites may still be subjected to bilateral selection pressures driven by evolutionary factors in both cattle and T. truncatus. Furthermore, the possibility of unilateral selection pressure arising from cattle domestication or T. truncatus adaptation to the aquatic environment cannot be excluded.

Calculated Ka/Ks for each site except the signal peptide between bovine LRG1 and dLRG1. The codons encoding amino acid residues 68, 74, 83–92, 137–142, 144, 145, 203–206, and 235 had Ka/Ks ratios > 10.

Interspecies comparison of the electrostatic surface potentials showed that the broad, negatively charged region within the horseshoe‐shaped molecular structure is highly conserved among species, although to different degrees (Figure 2). Previous studies have shown that this site binds to cytochrome c and inhibits cell death (Camilli et al. 2022), suggesting its essential role remains unchanged in T. trucatus. However, the 192nd amino acid residue, located within the horseshoe shape, differed between species. It was a threonine in the other four terrestrial animal species but isoleucine in T. trucatus. While this study does not include functional assays, our computational findings suggest that the unique mutation at residue 192 in T. truncatus may have arisen either through neutral evolution or as part of an adaptive response to the species' aquatic environment. The electrostatic surface potential revealed another difference between other species. The sides of the horseshoe‐shaped curve were neutral to negatively charged in humans, mice, and rats but positively charged in cows and T. trucatus, which are clearly different. Although the molecular function of this site in LRG1 has not yet been elucidated, it may have a unique function in the Cetartiodactyla.

STRING correlation analysis showed that dLRG1 may be strongly related to the TGF‐β signaling pathway, as in humans. In several animal species, including humans, the TGF‐β signaling pathway is deeply involved in tissue repair and anti‐inflammation, such as fibroblast activation (Camilli et al. 2022). It should be noted that the interpretation of RNA expression analysis from publicly available datasets in this study is limited because the health status information of subjects from the original research was unknown. However, we showed possible correlations between the expression level of LRG1 and various inflammatory genes in T. truncatus. LRG1 expression may affect various signaling pathways in a tissue‐dependent manner. Advanced research into LRG1 as a biomarker for various diseases in humans is underway (Camilli et al. 2022). This study provides a preliminary investigation into the potential clinical relevance of LRG1 as an inflammation‐related marker in T. truncatus, analogous to its role in humans. Further studies may be undertaken to reveal the functional molecular mechanism of this protein in T. truncatus, including in vitro scratch assays (Jonkman et al. 2014).
