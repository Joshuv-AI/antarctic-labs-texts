Upper Airway Volume Predicts Brain Structure and Cognition in Adolescents
Authors: Adway Kanhere, Nithya Navarathna, Paul H. Yi, Vishwa S. Parekh, Jerrah Pickle, Christine C. Cloak, Thomas Ernst, Linda Chang, Dongdong Li, Susan Redline, Amal Isaiah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
RationaleOne in 10 children experiences sleep-disordered breathing (SDB). Untreated SDB is associated with poor cognition, but the underlying mechanisms are less understood.ObjectivesWe assessed the relationship between magnetic resonance imaging–derived upper airway volume and children’s cognition and regional cortical gray matter volumes.MethodsWe used 5-year data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development study (N = 11,875 children; 9–10 yr old at baseline). Upper airway volumes were derived using a deep learning model applied to 5,552,640 brain magnetic resonance imaging slices. The primary outcome was the Total Cognition Composite score from the NIH Toolbox (NIH-TB). Secondary outcomes included other NIH-TB measures and cortical gray matter volumes.Measurements and Main ResultsThe habitual snoring group had significantly smaller airway volumes than nonsnorers (mean difference, 1.2 cm³; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.0–1.4 cm³; P < 0.001). Deep learning–derived airway volume predicted the Total Cognition Composite score (estimated mean difference, 3.68 points; 95% CI, 2.41–4.96 points; P < 0.001) per one-unit increase in the natural log of airway volume (∼2.7-fold raw volume increase). This airway volume increase was also associated with an average 0.02-cm³ increase in right temporal pole volume (95% CI, 0.01–0.02 cm³; P < 0.001). Similar airway volume predicted most NIH-TB domain scores and multiple frontal and temporal gray matter volumes. These brain volumes mediated the relationship between airway volume and cognition.ConclusionsWe demonstrate a novel application of deep learning–based airway segmentation in a large pediatric cohort. Upper airway volume is a potential biomarker for cognitive outcomes in pediatric SDB, offers insights into neurobiological mechanisms, and informs future studies on risk stratification.

At a Glance Commentary

Scientific Knowledge on the Subject

Sleep-disordered breathing affects up to 17% of children and is associated with poor cognition. Although surgical removal of tonsils and adenoids can improve symptoms through upper airway expansion, current clinical decisions often rely on subjective assessments. Although polysomnography remains the gold standard for diagnosis, its adoption is limited by resource constraints and access barriers.

What This Study Adds to the Field

This study demonstrates that magnetic resonance imaging–derived upper airway volumes, obtained through deep learning analysis of 5.5 million brain magnetic resonance imaging slices from nearly 12,000 children, predict cognitive performance and regional brain volumes. Smaller airway volumes were associated with lower cognitive scores across multiple domains, particularly affecting processing speed and attention. The relationship between airway volumes and cognition was mediated by gray matter volumes in the frontal and temporal brain regions. These results highlight upper airway volume as a surrogate marker of neurocognitive risk in pediatric sleep-disordered breathing and they also demonstrate the feasibility of large-scale airway analysis using deep learning, offering insights into the biological pathways linking airway anatomy to cognitive outcomes.

Sleep-disordered breathing (SDB), characterized by habitual snoring and respiratory pauses, affects up to 17% of children (1) and arises from recurrent upper airway collapse during sleep (2). Untreated SDB is associated with poor cognition, problem behaviors, and impaired quality of life (1). Cognitive deficits and problem behaviors are the most described primary outcomes of SDB-related clinical trials (3, 4). Providing a mechanistic basis for these relationships, several recent studies have identified regional morphometric alterations in the brain, especially in the regions responsible for attention and behavioral regulation (5–7).

The surgical removal of tonsils and adenoids results in the improvement of pediatric SDB by upper airway expansion (8). Adenotonsillectomy is the most common surgical procedure in children younger than 15 years, with more than 500,000 procedures performed annually (9). Most children undergo adenotonsillectomy without objective assessments, as clinicians rely on parent-reported symptom burden and subjective grading of tonsil size (10). Although clinical guidelines recommend polysomnography (PSG), which remains the gold standard for diagnosing SDB severity (11), real-world adoption remains limited because of resource constraints and access barriers (12). Notably, although PSG effectively quantifies respiratory disturbances and predicts various health outcomes, standard PSG metrics alone may not fully capture the complexity of neurocognitive outcomes (13, 14). This highlights the potential value of complementary objective measures to enhance clinical decision making.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) identifies upper airway anatomy in children with SDB (15, 16). Quantitative upper airway assessments could delineate anatomic features of SDB while reducing PSG-related costs. Linking imaging-derived anatomic features of SDB could provide additional biological insight into its neurocognitive impacts and inform future investigations into targeted treatment strategies.

The ABCD (Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development) study, comprising ∼12,000 children, presents a unique opportunity to examine the relationship between upper airway volume and brain outcomes in a large, diverse cohort. We sought to opportunistically derive upper airway volumes from brain MRIs performed in the ABCD study using segmentation algorithms and deep learning. Therefore, the primary aim of this study was to assess the relationship between MRI-derived upper airway volume and children’s cognition. Furthermore, we also aimed to investigate the relationship between upper airway volume and regional cortical gray matter volumes and (3) assess the potential mediation effects of regional brain volumes on the relationship between airway volume and cognitive outcomes. We hypothesized that upper airway volumes would be positively associated with cognitive measures. Furthermore, we also hypothesized that regional brain volumes would mediate the relationship between upper airway volumes and cognitive performance.

Methods

Data Source

The ABCD study (https://abcdstudy.org) is the most extensive national, prospective, observational study of children’s brain development from age 9–10 years until adulthood (17). Release 5.0 included 11,875 children in Year 1 (baseline), the near-complete cohort in Year 3 (2-yr follow-up), and the partial cohort in Year 5 (4-yr follow-up). Parental permissions and child assents were obtained from caregiver–child dyads, and all study procedures were approved by the central (University of California, San Diego) and local (University of Maryland, Baltimore) institutional review boards. Children with serious psychiatric and neurological diagnoses were excluded. Given the start of the pandemic in the middle of the study, some in-person procedures were converted to remote assessments. The current dataset included the following dependent variables obtained without preprocessing: age in months, sex assigned at birth, race, site, participant, and visit ID (Years 1, 3, and 5). The combined pretax household income and the highest educational achievement of the primary caregiver were recorded. History of snoring was derived from the Sleep Disturbance Scale in Children (18) as reported by the primary caregiver and recategorized using clinically acceptable thresholds of none, nonhabitual (<3 nights/wk), and habitual (⩾3 nights/wk) snoring (19). Participant age, sex assigned at birth, and race and ethnicity were reported by the primary caregiver. The body mass index was calculated from the weight and height in the ABCD dataset and converted to a z-score (20). The ABCD study sample size detects small to medium effects related to exposures over the study’s duration while accounting for up to 10% attrition (21).

Assessment of Cognitive Function

The ABCD study used the NIH Toolbox (NIH-TB) Cognition Battery, a computer-based, child-friendly, standardized, and validated measure of cognitive performance (22). The NIH-TB includes seven domains and three composite scores. The Picture Vocabulary Test pairs pictures with audible descriptions. The Flanker Inhibitory Control Test assesses the inhibition of attention to irrelevant aspects of a task that presents a central target with flanking stimuli to the left and right. The Dimensional Change Card Sort Test matches a target stimulus to the appropriate shape/color. The List Sorting Working Memory Test assesses the reproduction of visually and orally presented items in order of size. The Picture Sequence Memory Test involves arranging a set of pictures in the order of a sequence stated in an audio file. The Oral Reading Recognition Test assesses single-word pronunciation. The Flanker, Dimensional Change Card Sort, Picture Sequence Memory, List Sorting, and Pattern Comparison tests contribute to the Fluid Composite score. The Crystallized Composite score is derived from the Picture Vocabulary and the Oral Reading Recognition tests. The Total Cognition Composite includes all seven domains. Models examining the association between airway volume and cognition included the age-corrected score. Brain volume and mediation models included the age-uncorrected scores. A detailed description of cognitive testing is provided in the Supplementary Methods.

ABCD Imaging Protocol

The ABCD imaging protocol and related technical parameters (23) consolidate the previous neuroimaging protocols associated with the PING (Pediatric Imaging, Neurocognition, and Genetics) Study (24) and the Human Connectome Project (https://www.humanconnectome.org) (25). Three 3-Tesla scanners were utilized: Siemens Prisma (Siemens Medical Solutions), General Electric Discovery MR750 (GE Healthcare), and Philips Achieva (Koninklijke Philips). The ABCD study harmonizes high-resolution T1- and T2-weighted head MRIs collected from 21 U.S.-based sites acquired in the same year as the cognitive testing and centrally processed (23, 26). The T1-weighted images acquired using adult-size multichannel coils were based on magnetization-prepared 180-degree radiofrequency pulses and rapid gradient-echo (MP-RAGE) sequence (https://abcdstudy.org/images/Protocol_Imaging_Sequences.pdf). Morphometric cortical reconstructions were performed using Freesurfer v5.3.0 (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu) and projected on the Destrieux atlas with 74 parcellations/hemisphere (27). Further details concerning the imaging acquisition, preprocessing, and segmentation of brain regions are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Deep Learning Segmentation Model Development and Airway Volume Calculation

We obtained Year 1 and Year 3 T1-weighted brain MRIs from 50 randomly selected children from the ABCD dataset (100 MRIs total, 25,600 slices; 26 male [52%]; mean baseline age, 9.8 yr [95% confidence interval (CI), 9.6–10.0 yr]; 34 White [68%]; 10 habitual snorers at baseline [20%]). An otolaryngology resident (J.P.) performed manual upper airway segmentation using standardized anatomical boundaries from the superior turbinates to the inferior endplate of the C4 vertebral body (Figures 1A–1C and see Figure E1 in data supplement). The segmentation was jointly supervised by an attending radiologist (P.H.Y.) and a pediatric otolaryngologist (A.I.), requiring approximately 45 minutes per scan for 37.5 hours. Selection criteria included: 1) complete T1-weighted MRI data for both Years 1 and 3; and 2) absence of significant motion artifacts preventing accurate annotation. Thirty MRI scans (7,680 slices) from 15 children were set aside as a held-out testing set. The remainder (70 MRI scans from 35 children; 17,920 slices) were used to construct five datasets comprising 5, 10, 20, 40, and 70 scans each. These datasets were then used for segmentation model training to evaluate the impact of training dataset size on ultimate segmentation performance.

For deep learning model training, we used nnU-Net, a state-of-the-art, self-configuring segmentation method that spans automated image preprocessing, algorithm architecture, and training settings (28). Each model developed on the training datasets of varying sizes was tested on the hold-out set of 30 MRIs. Performance was evaluated using mean Dice similarity scores, which measure the spatial overlap between a model-predicted segmented area and an expert-segmented area designated as the ground truth. A Dice score of 0 indicates no overlap between the two regions (entirely dissimilar segmentations), whereas a score of 1 indicates perfect overlap (identical segmentations) (29). The Dice scores were compared between models trained on the dataset with 70 MRIs to those trained with 5, 10, 20, and 40 MRIs (Figures 1D–1J). Dice coefficients on the held-out test set were consistently high across all sample sizes, ranging from 0.838 (n = 5) to 0.845 (n = 70) (Figures 1K and 1L). We modeled this relationship further using a power law function: An = Amax − C/np, where An is the Dice coefficient, n is the number of training samples, Amax is the asymptotic maximum performance, C is a scaling constant, and p is the power exponent. Curve fitting yielded Amax = 0.85, C = 0.04, and p = 1.00. This model predicts only marginal improvements in Dice coefficients with larger datasets, suggesting that our approach achieves robust performance (Figures 1M–1O).

To account for potential scanner effects, we validated our deep learning model’s performance separately for each scanner type (Siemens Prisma, GE Discovery MR750, and Philips Ingenia). The model demonstrated robust performance across platforms, with no statistically significant differences in segmentation accuracy (ANOVA, F[2,47] = 1.57; P = 0.23). The upper airway segmentation model was then applied to all available 21,649 MRI scans (5,552,640 slices) to calculate upper airway volumes for the entire ABCD cohort. The airway voxel volumes were extracted using the pyRadiomics library (v3.1.0), validated by the Imaging Biomarker Standardization Initiative (30). We used nnU-Net v1 for all model training and predictions with Python v3.9. The model training leveraged a Google Cloud Virtual Machine with four NVIDIA V100 GPUs. In comparison, the segmentation and radiomics computations on 21,649 scans were done on a local compute cluster with eight NVIDIA A6000 GPUs. Technical details regarding the segmentation algorithm are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Statistical Analysis

Numeric and categorical data were summarized yearly using means (95% CI) and counts (%). For univariate models, we used ANOVA and Tukey-Kramer test for post hoc pairwise comparisons. For multivariable models, we used linear mixed-effects regression models. We paid attention to the parsimony and robustness of the models and followed best practice recommendations (31). The primary outcome was the NIH-TB Total Cognition Composite age-corrected score. We used temporally matched MRI and cognitive data from the same assessment wave for all analyses. This allowed us to examine longitudinal relationships between upper airway volume and individual cognitive measures when available across multiple waves.

Established relationships between social determinants and neurodevelopmental trajectories guided our selection of socioeconomic covariates (32). Total pretax household income and highest educational attainment of the primary caregiver were included as primary socioeconomic indicators based on their documented associations with children’s cognitive development, academic achievement, and brain structure. Household income, categorized into 10 levels, captures access to material resources, health care, educational opportunities, and environmental exposures that may influence upper airway development and brain maturation. Caregiver educational attainment, converted to approximate years of education, is a proxy for health literacy, parenting practices, and intergenerational transmission of educational advantages. These socioeconomic measures were included as fixed effects in linear mixed-effects regression models alongside other developmentally relevant covariates: race and sex assigned at birth (to account for known developmental differences), body mass index z-score (given established relationships with both brain development and airway volume), time of assessment (to control for developmental stage), and type of assessment (in-person, remote, or hybrid to account for test environment). The models included nested random effects for subject identification within the study site to account for individual trajectories and site-specific variations in socioeconomic distribution. This comprehensive covariate structure was maintained across all analyses examining relationships with cognitive performance and regional brain volumes, as well as in mediation analyses investigating pathways between airway volume, brain structure, and cognitive outcomes, to ensure consistent control for socioeconomic influences on developmental trajectories. In addition, models concerning cortical volumes included age and MRI serial numbers as fixed and random effects. The linear mixed-effects models were created using R v4.3.2 (https://cran.r-project.org) and lme4 (33).

We hypothesized that regional brain volumes would mediate the relationship between upper airway volume and cognitive performance, particularly in areas susceptible to SDB. Our mediation model tested whether specific brain regions (orbital gyri, temporal poles, and anterior cingulate cortices) are intermediate regions linking airway anatomy to cognitive outcomes. These regions were selected based on (1) their known susceptibility to intermittent hypoxia in SDB (2, 34) their established roles in cognitive function (35, 36), and (3) previous literature showing their structural alterations in SDB (6).

We used the mediation package (37) to assess the relationships between airway volume (exposure) and cognitive test scores (outcome) attributable to regional brain volumes (mediator). This pathway is a conceptual extension of Baron and Kenny’s well-known method (38). The mediation package was initially described for studies with randomized exposures to best estimate a potential causal relationship, although we do not assume them. The mediation effect was calculated using the bias-corrected bootstrap approach (n = 1,000 replicates) (39). Although a sensitivity analysis is recommended for mediation analyses testing causal hypotheses, the observational nature of this study assumes unmeasured confounding. The multivariate models iteratively analyzed each regional cortical volume separately (74 for each hemisphere). All P values were adjusted for false discovery (40). Mediation effects were calculated as percentage mediated (95% CI). The mediation analysis is further illustrated in the data supplement.

Results

The ABCD study 5.0 release included 11,868 patients at baseline. The follow-up waves included 9,158 (Year 3) and 3,471 (partial Year 5) children. Scan information was available for 11,771 (Year 1), 8,123 (Year 3), and 3,045 (Year 5) children. A total of 1,190 scans were manually excluded based on additional quality control measures, including inadequate slices, noise, poor quality, or field-of-view truncation. The final MRI-based dataset included 21,749 scans, out of which 100 scans were set aside for deep learning model training. The missingness in the sample is shown in detail in Figure E2. The average age at baseline in the ABCD study was 119.0 (interquartile range, 14) months. Boys comprised 52%. The ABCD cohort was 74.2% White, 16.0% Black, and 9.4% other at baseline. Households earning $100,000–$200,000 in total pretax household income constituted the largest group (∼28%). The marginal effects of airway volume on cognitive test scores derived from the linear mixed effects models are shown in Figures 4D–4M. Eight of the 10 models demonstrated a significant positive relationship between airway volume and cognitive test scores (Figure 4N). The most robust relationship was observed between airway volume and Total Cognition Composite score, with a mean difference of 3.7 test score points (95% CI, 2.4 to 5.0; P < 0.001) per 1-unit increase in the natural log of airway volume, corresponding to an approximate 2.72-fold increase in raw airway volume. Even when there was a discernible and positive impact of the year of testing for some variables (Figure 4H), a separate relationship was identified between airway volume and the Pattern Comparison Processing Speed test, with a mean difference of 2.8 test score points (95% CI, 1.5 to 4.1; P < 0.001) per 1-unit increase in the natural log of airway volume—corresponding to an approximate 2.72-fold increase in raw airway volume. There were no statistically significant time × airway volume interactions in any of the models. Although habitual snorers had significantly lower airway volumes, snoring weakly predicted the Total Cognition Composite score in the entire cohort (mean difference, −1.0 points; 95% CI, −1.9 to −0.2; P = 0.02). However, this relationship was mitigated among children comprising nonhabitual and habitual snorers (mean difference, 1.3 points; 95% CI, 0.5 to 3.1; P = 0.16).

The marginal relationships between airway volume and whole and regional brain volumes are summarized in Figures 5A–5C. The top five regional brain volumes ranked by the strength of their associations with airway volume are shown in Figure 5B. We identified significant associations between airway volume and regional brain volumes (Figure 5C) but not with whole brain volume (mean difference, 0.01 cm³; 95% CI, −1.17 to 1.19 cm³; P = 0.94). All reported differences reflect the change in brain volume per 1-unit increase in the natural log of airway volume (approximately a 2.72-fold increase in raw volume). The strongest associations were observed for the right temporal pole (mean difference, 0.02 cm³; 95% CI, 0.01 to 0.02 cm³; P < 0.001), the right orbital gyrus (mean difference, 0.11 cm³; 95% CI, 0.07 to 0.15 cm³; P < 0.001), and the left orbital gyrus (mean difference, 0.11 cm³; 95% CI, 0.08 to 0.14 cm³; P < 0.001).

Last, Figures 5D and 5E summarize the extent of the associations between airway volumes and individual cognitive scores mediated by regional cortical gray matter volumes. The mediation effects are most pronounced for the left and right orbital gyri, the right and left temporal poles, and the left and right anterior cingulate gyri, for all NIH-TB outcomes. For example, the volume of the left orbital gyrus mediated 17.9% (95% CI, 12.0–34.5%) of the relationship between the airway volume and the Total Cognition Composite score. The smallest mediation effects were identified for the Dimensional Change Card Sort, Pattern Comparison, and Picture Sequence Memory tests.

Discussion

Based on a large longitudinal sample of children, this study provides evidence for the relationship between MRI-derived upper airway volume and cognitive outcomes. Gray matter volumes in the orbitofrontal, temporal, and anterior cingulate brain regions mediate the relationship between upper airway volumes and cognitive outcomes. Current clinical decision making often relies heavily on parent-reported symptoms alongside objective measures when available. These results show that a biomarker derived from upper airway morphology can enhance our understanding of the biological mechanisms linking pediatric SDB to cognitive outcomes. Future research could explore more accessible imaging, such as office-based ultrasonography of the upper airway, representing a cost-effective translation of the results in this study, especially for surgical planning.

Upper airway volume has been previously implicated as the anatomic basis of airflow restriction in SDB (15, 41). Both static and dynamic MRIs have demonstrated restricted upper airway volumes in SDB. More recently, automated segmentation of the upper airway using deep learning has provided performance comparable to manual segmentation (42).

Selecting 50 children (resulting in 100 MRIs and 25,600 slices) for segmentation training represents a balance between annotation quality and effort. Each scan required approximately 45 minutes of expert annotation time, with joint supervision from radiology and pediatric otolaryngology attendings, ensuring high-quality ground truth data. This intensive manual process totaled 37.5 hours of specialized annotation time. Our systematic evaluation of variations in training dataset sizes demonstrated that segmentation performance plateaued early, with Dice scores showing minimal improvement beyond our smallest dataset. This finding aligns with recent advances in computer vision models, where modern deep learning architectures have demonstrated an ability to learn effectively from limited but well-annotated datasets (43, 44). The robust performance across all training set sizes and the substantial time investment required for expert annotation support our methodological choice as both practical and technically sound for this specific anatomical segmentation task.

An association between snoring and upper airway volumes in the ABCD cohort is consistent with the pathophysiology of SDB (45). This relationship may also capture some of the adverse outcomes of SDB observed in previous studies related to snoring frequency in children (14). Next, given the age-dependent natural craniofacial expansion, airway volumes derived using deep learning increased steadily with age. We also identified the smallest airway volumes in Black children, consistent with the previously recognized highest risk for snoring (46). However, this relationship was also attributed to lower socioeconomic status, which might lead to higher airway inflammation rates related to poor housing (47).

We show positive associations between airway volumes and most cognitive test scores measured using the NIH-TB cognitive battery. The strongest association was observed for the Total Cognition Composite score, which spans contributions from all seven domain test scores. This finding is supported by well-described associations between SDB and cognition in children (14). Other composite scores—Fluid and Crystallized—were also affected. Among the domain scores, the most discernible associations were identified between upper airway volumes and the Pattern Comparison test. This NIH-TB test assesses processing speed, which requires attention. Both cognitive functions use the lateral prefrontal cortex as a neurobiological substrate and are also sensitive to intermittent hypoxia (6). In contrast, the relationship between upper airway volume and Picture Sequence Memory test results, a surrogate for episodic memory, was not statistically significant. This finding is consistent with prior studies that found short-term recall, specifically visual memory, appears to be preserved in SDB (48).

The relationship between airway volumes and brain structure was notable for regional differences in gray matter volumes, consistent with previous studies of brain structure in children with SDB (5, 6, 34). The strongest associations were identified within the frontal and temporal lobes (e.g., orbitofrontal gyri and temporal poles), consistent with a previous study linking brain morphometric characteristics of the frontal lobe with problem behaviors in the ABCD cohort (6). The temporal pole is associated with high-level cognitive processes: visual processing for complex objects and face recognition, autobiographic memory, naming and word-object labeling, semantic processing, and socioemotional processing (36). The orbitofrontal gyri have a dominant role in emotional processing, social cognition, and inhibitory control (35). Although problem behaviors were not explicitly studied, the association between upper airway volume and the Flanker test (representing response inhibition) underscores the associations between SDB and cortical regions that mediate emotional regulation (6). The mediation effects related to the anterior cingulate cortices support emotional processing and behavioral regulation and reinforce previous findings (49). The relationships between the cognitive function and volumetric findings reflect the potential susceptibility of these brain regions to the effects of intermittent hypoxia on cognitive outcomes in SDB (50).

Although the current study demonstrates a previously undescribed relationship between airway volume and cognitive test scores, the structural and functional correlates of upper airway morphometry still need to be clarified. The reduction in airway volumes may correlate with SDB, supported by the observed association between airway volumes and snoring. However, the absence of polysomnography in the ABCD study does not allow us to evaluate associations with the severity of typical physiological measures that represent obstructive respiratory events and hypoxemia. Parental reports of snoring in adolescents may underestimate snoring, particularly because parents and teenagers often sleep in separate rooms; the absence of reported snoring may underestimate its true prevalence. As habitual snorers comprise children with upper airway obstruction spanning the full spectrum of SDB from snoring to severe OSA, our observed associations may be stronger in a sample of children with proven OSA. Although prior studies have shown only weak or null associations of polysomnographic measures with cognition (3, 13), we could not compare the predictive value of MRI to polysomnography for cognitive outcomes in this large sample. It is possible that factors influencing airway development in utero or during early life also influenced brain development, and our findings do not indicate causal relationships. Our imaging dataset is confined to measurement of upper airway volume and is limited by lack of individual contributions of the tonsils, adenoids, and other upper airway structures such as the tongue base (51). Sex differences associated with pubertal onset can influence airway volumes, which can modify the results. Our findings also highlight the complex interplay between socioeconomic factors, brain structure, and cognitive outcomes in adolescent development. However, our analysis was limited by the categorical nature of income data and the lack of neighborhood-level socioeconomic indicators.

Future studies should consider more granular measures of socioeconomic status, including neighborhood characteristics, environmental exposures, and healthcare access patterns. In addition, investigating how socioeconomic factors might moderate the relationship between airway volume and cognitive outcomes could provide valuable insights for targeted interventions. Understanding these relationships is particularly important, given the potential long-term implications for cognitive development and academic achievement in children from disadvantaged backgrounds. Children with smaller airway volumes may maintain greater airway patency by augmenting upper airway reflexes during sleep (52). Finally, smaller upper airway volumes may increase exposure to hypoxia resulting from airflow limitation akin to the association between lung or bronchial volumes and cognitive performance in adults with asthma (53).

The strengths of the current study include the large dataset that approximates the socioeconomic and racial diversity of the United States, its longitudinal nature, the robust control of covariates, the use of validated psychometric tests, and the use of unified protocols for imaging. Limitations include using a single-site segmentation and missing data for some cognitive test scores because of the inability to perform some tests remotely during the pandemic. We focused on gross upper airway volumes rather than specific radiomic features of the upper airway that could provide more mechanistic insights (16, 54).

Imaging the upper airway and using simple volumetric calculations may, in the future, help stratify risk or tailor treatment strategies, pending further validation of their predictive value for individual outcomes. Further work in this area could expand to using child-friendly, inexpensive, and easily accessible upper airway imaging tools, such as ultrasound. The association between airway volumes and cognitive outcomes highlights the potential of radiomics in bridging imaging phenotypes with clinically relevant endpoints in SDB. This approach could complement existing diagnostic tools, such as polysomnography, by offering new insights into the relationship between upper airway volume and neurocognitive outcomes.

Future research directions include the evaluation of airway volumes from inexpensive and child-friendly imaging modalities, such as ultrasound, in clinical decision making for SDB management, such as determining surgical candidacy for adenotonsillectomy. In addition, exploring the integration of these imaging biomarkers with other clinical and physiological parameters provides more comprehensive risk stratification models for SDB in children. The convergence of artificial intelligence, advanced imaging techniques, and clinically meaningful outcomes could support future research to refine SDB diagnosis and management via precision medicine strategies. However, prospective studies and clinical validation in diverse settings are essential before these approaches can be integrated into routine clinical practice.
