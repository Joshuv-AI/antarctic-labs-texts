Déjà vu! A Case of Recurrent Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatohepatitis 70 Days After Liver Transplantation
Authors: Matthew Still, April Gettelfinger, Victor Akinditan, Abbas Syed, Ashley Eggert, Tanner Thornsberry, Jacob Ciricillo, Divya Sharma, Amoah Yeboah-Korang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) recurrence following liver transplantation occurs in 11%-24% of recipients, typically months to years posttransplant. Early recurrence (<6 months) is extremely rare. We present a 40-year-old woman with obesity and remote kidney transplant who underwent liver transplantation for MASH cirrhosis. At 10 weeks posttransplant, she developed acute cellular rejection and 68% steatosis, progressing to >95% steatohepatitis with F1 fibrosis within 6 days on corticosteroid treatment. Multimodal intervention, including semaglutide, resulted in histologic improvement (steatosis reduced to 30%, ballooning resolved) with sustained biochemical normalization. This case highlights ultra-rapid MASH recurrence and favorable histological response to early multimodal metabolic intervention.

INTRODUCTION

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease is increasingly prevalent, affecting more than one-third of the world's adult population.1,2 Progression to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) occurs in 20%–30% of individuals with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, and is currently the leading indication of liver transplantation (LT) among women and individuals older than 65 years old.3–5

Recurrent MASH occurs in up to 24% of recipients, and is typically more aggressive with accelerated fibrosis progression.6,7 Despite this, recurrent MASH-related cirrhosis develops in <2% of transplant recipients, recurrent disease-related graft failure in 3%–9% of patients, and re-transplantation due to recurrent disease occurs in <1% of patients.6 The most common etiologies for recurrent MASH following LT are posttransplant metabolic disturbances.8 Additional factors include the reversal of the catabolic state seen in cirrhosis, improved appetite and weight gain, and certain immunosuppressive agents.9

Early MASH recurrence (<6 months post-LT) is rarely reported in the literature. We present a unique case of a patient who underwent LT for MASH cirrhosis and subsequently developed recurrent MASH of the donor organ within 10 weeks posttransplant.

CASE REPORT

A 40-year-old woman with class III obesity (body mass index [BMI] 41.0 kg/m2), previous sleeve gastrectomy, obstructive sleep apnea on continuous positive airway pressure with suboptimal adherence, kidney transplant in 2016 for chemotherapy-related nephrotoxicity (Burkitt lymphoma) complicated by tacrolimus-induced thrombotic microangiopathy, and MASH/hepatitis C virus cirrhosis (complicated by hepatocellular carcinoma) underwent deceased donor LT in January 2025. Baseline metabolic parameters demonstrated total cholesterol 168 mg/dL, low-density lipoproteins 78 mg/dL, high-density lipoproteins 33 mg/dL, and triglycerides 286 mg/dL, and no diabetes. Initial allograft biopsy at transplant demonstrated <5% macrosteatosis without fibrosis (Figure 1).

Initial donor liver biopsy at time of transplant showing (A) minimal macrovesicular steatosis (<5%) without inflammation or ballooning (H&E, 20×) and (B) absence of fibrosis (Masson trichrome, 20×).

Ten weeks posttransplant, she had rising liver enzymes: aspartate aminotransferase (AST) 73 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 99 U/L, compared with 34 and 48 2 weeks earlier. At that time, BMI was 38.7 kg/m2 and fasting glucose was 74 mg/dL. Her immunosuppression at that time consisted of mycophenolate, prednisone, and belatacept. Ultrasound demonstrated increased hepatic echogenicity. Liver biopsy revealed moderate acute cellular rejection (ACR) and severe steatosis (∼68%) without fibrosis (F0; Figure 2). She was admitted for treatment of ACR and initiated on pulse-dose corticosteroids. Given her previous history of tacrolimus-induced microangiopathy, everolimus was started but later transitioned to cyclosporine due to leukopenia.

First posttransplant biopsy at 10 weeks showing (A) severe macrovesicular steatosis (68%) with moderate acute cellular rejection characterized by portal and perivenular lymphocytic infiltrate (H&E, 20×) and (B) minimal fibrosis (F0; Masson trichrome, 20×).

Despite treatment, liver enzymes worsened (AST 180, ALT 272). Repeat biopsy after 6 days demonstrated severe steatosis (>95%) with ballooning degeneration consistent with steatohepatitis (Figure 3). There was minimal portal tract chronic inflammation with rare lymphocytes and patchy pericellular fibrosis (F1), without evidence of ongoing ACR. She was started on semaglutide 0.25 mg weekly, icosapent ethyl 2 g twice daily, and vitamin E 800 International units daily, in addition to cyclosporine, mycophenolate, and prednisone taper at discharge. Semaglutide was titrated to 1 mg weekly by August 2025. Over the subsequent 60 days, her liver enzymes improved significantly, with AST and ALT decreasing into the 60s U/L. Surveillance biopsy performed in July 2025 showed reduction in steatosis to 30%, resolution of ballooning degeneration, and stable pericellular fibrosis (Figure 4). Moderate ACR was concurrently identified and treated with pulse corticosteroids. In January 2026, her liver enzymes were completely normal. At 6-month follow-up, her BMI was 36.9 kg/m2, with normal HbA1c and creatinine. Lipid panel demonstrated total cholesterol 263 mg/dL, low-density lipoproteins 224 mg/dL, high-density lipoproteins 39 mg/dL, and triglycerides 295 mg/dL. She transitioned to tirzepatide 2.5 mg weekly in December 2025 and increased to 7.5 mg weekly by the end of January 2026 (Table 1).

Second biopsy 6 days later demonstrating (A) severe steatosis (>95%) with hepatocyte ballooning degeneration consistent with active steatohepatitis (H&E, 20×) and (B) development of patchy pericellular fibrosis (F1) (Masson trichrome, 20×).

Follow-up biopsy at 4 months posttreatment demonstrating (A) significant reduction in steatosis to 30% with resolution of ballooning degeneration (H&E, 20×) and (B) stable focal pericellular fibrosis (F1) without progression (Masson trichrome, 20×).

DISCUSSION

This case demonstrates ultra-rapid MASH recurrence, with progression from <5% steatosis to severe steatohepatitis (>95%) with F1 fibrosis within 10 weeks posttransplant. Early recurrence (<6 months post-LT) is rarely reported in the literature, making this case particularly notable.6

An important consideration is whether the rapid increase in steatosis from 68% to >95% was attributable to corticosteroids rather than representing true recurrent MASH. Corticosteroids drive hepatic fat accumulation through increased de novo lipogenesis, upregulation of fatty acid transporters, and insulin resistance.9 However, steroid-induced steatosis typically lacks histological features of steatohepatitis.9,10 The repeat biopsy demonstrated ballooning degeneration and patchy pericellular fibrosis (F1), features that reflect lipotoxic hepatocellular injury and cytoskeletal disruption characteristic of MASH pathophysiology rather than steroid-induced steatosis.10 Furthermore, pericellular fibrosis is a histological signature of MASH rather than other causes of hepatic injury.10 Notably, the patient already had 68% steatosis at the first biopsy, indicating an established disease process that was likely exacerbated by corticosteroids.

Additional patient factors likely contributed, including obesity, obstructive sleep apnea, and metabolic syndrome. Modest weight reduction (16 lbs or 7.8% of total body weight loss) likely contributed to histological improvement, though persistent dyslipidemia suggests inadequate metabolic control. In the posttransplant period, reversal of the catabolic state associated with cirrhosis leads to improved appetite and weight gain.8 Calcineurin inhibitors such as cyclosporine contribute to insulin resistance, hypertension, and dyslipidemia.11 This patient's baseline immunosuppression included belatacept, which has improved metabolic outcomes vs calcineurin inhibitors.12 The development of rapid MASH recurrence despite this metabolically favorable agent underscores the dominant role of the patient's preexisting risk factors and the subsequent addition of high-dose corticosteroids and cyclosporine.

This case demonstrates robust histologic response to early multimodal therapy. Reduction in steatosis from >95% to 30% with resolution of hepatocyte ballooning represents meaningful histologic improvement. Despite the absence of FDA-approved therapies for posttransplant MASH recurrence, this patient achieved sustained biochemical improvement with combined metabolic therapy, including a GLP-1 receptor agonist, icosapent ethyl, and vitamin E.13,14

This case underscores the challenge of distinguishing steroid-induced steatosis from recurrent MASH in the early posttransplant period and highlights the importance of histologic confirmation when clinical trajectories are atypical. Rapid progression to steatohepatitis with fibrosis within 10 weeks suggests recurrent disease can occur earlier than traditionally appreciated, particularly in patients with significant metabolic risk.

Although causality cannot be established, these findings support early, aggressive metabolic intervention and a high index of suspicion in high-risk patients. Further studies are needed to define optimal screening and management of ultra-rapid MASH recurrence after LT.
