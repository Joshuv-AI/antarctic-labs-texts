Comparative Evaluation of Deep-Reasoning Large Language Models for Ophthalmic Emergencies
Authors: Xuanqiao Lin, Yizhou Yang, Yuecheng Ren, Lan Wang, Lei Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate contemporary deep-reasoning large language models (LLMs) for early assessment of ophthalmic emergencies using sequential, workflow-mimicking information levels.DesignCross-sectional, vignette-based, head-to-head comparative evaluation.SubjectsThirty-four deidentified emergency ophthalmology teaching cases curated from a publicly accessible repository.MethodsEach case was reconstructed into 3 sequential information levels (level 1 [L1]: history; level 2: basic examination; level 3 [L3]: specialist examination). Six LLMs (Doubao, DeepSeek, Kimi-2, ChatGPT-5, Gemini-3, and Grok-4), operating in deep-reasoning mode, generated outputs that were independently scored by 2 ophthalmologists. Diagnoses were graded as fully correct, partially correct, or incorrect; triage category (typical vs. atypical emergency) was rated as correct or incorrect. Ancillary test recommendations were mapped to a prespecified 10-category taxonomy and classified as undertesting, exact match, or overtesting. A four-level composite outcome integrated diagnostic correctness, triage accuracy, and testing.Main Outcome MeasuresDiagnostic correctness (fully correct, partially correct, incorrect), triage-category accuracy, ancillary test recommendation patterns, and composite outcome (ideal, safe but overtesting, potentially dangerous, intermediate).ResultsAcross 612 model–case–level outputs, 46.9% of diagnoses were fully correct, 24.5% partially correct, and 28.6% incorrect. Fully correct diagnoses increased from 43.1% at L1 to 53.9% at L3 (P = 0.048). Overall triage category accuracy was 85.3% (range, 76.5%–94.1% across models; P = 0.003) and did not differ across information levels (P = 0.89). Ancillary test recommendations most commonly reflected undertesting (51.0%), followed by overtesting (27.5%) and exact matches (21.6%) (P < 0.001 across models). In generalized estimating equation pairwise comparisons, ChatGPT-5 showed higher odds of a fully correct diagnosis than DeepSeek (odds ratio [OR], 3.54; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.49–8.43) and Gemini-3 (OR, 2.24; 95% CI, 1.31–3.83), and lower odds of potentially dangerous composite outcomes than DeepSeek (OR, 0.28; 95% CI, 0.10–0.74) and Gemini-3 (OR, 0.31; 95% CI, 0.11–0.89).ConclusionsDeep-reasoning LLMs demonstrated high triage category accuracy and moderate diagnostic performance for ophthalmic emergencies, with diagnostic correctness improving at higher information levels. However, ancillary testing patterns varied substantially, and ideal composite safety profiles were uncommon, supporting cautious, supervised deployment with explicit guardrails governing workup recommendations.Financial Disclosure(s)The author has no/the authors have no proprietary or commercial interest in any materials discussed in this article.

Ophthalmic emergencies require rapid triage and precise decision-making to prevent irreversible vision loss.1,2 These cases range from typical emergencies, such as acute angle-closure glaucoma and open-globe injuries, to atypical presentations that mimic benign conditions yet harbor sight-threatening pathology.3 However, the limited availability of ophthalmologists in general emergency departments often delegates initial triage to nonspecialist physicians,4,5 increasing the risk of both underevaluation—leading to delayed treatment of critical conditions such as ocular infections, vascular occlusion, or orbital infection5—and overevaluation, which may result in low-value testing and unnecessary referrals.6 This expertise gap underscores the need for accessible, expert-level clinical decision support systems.

Recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI), particularly large language models (LLMs), offer a promising solution for addressing gaps in clinical expertise.7,8 Unlike image-based computer vision systems that analyze data such as fundus photos, LLMs excel at complex logical reasoning and knowledge synthesis. Their strong performance on medical licensing examinations highlights their potential to process multifaceted clinical information and support decision-making in high-stakes environments, including emergency ophthalmology.9 Emerging studies have explored LLM applications in ophthalmology, primarily focusing on diagnosing conditions from static, full-text cases.10,11 However, a substantial gap remains between existing research designs and routine clinical practice, where diagnostic reasoning is a dynamic, stepwise process from history-taking to examination and specialist assessment.12 Most current AI studies evaluate LLMs under “complete information” scenarios, failing to assess performance amid the uncertainty inherent in initial patient encounters, where limited history and basic ocular findings guide early decision-making. Furthermore, although diagnostic accuracy is frequently reported, few studies systematically evaluate cost-effectiveness, clinical safety, or potential biases toward “overtesting” (resource overutilization) or “undertesting” (risk of missed diagnoses).

To address the gap between static AI evaluation and dynamic clinical workflows, this study introduces a sequential, tiered information framework based on high-quality clinical teaching cases. We aimed to evaluate the LLM performance in early ophthalmic emergency triage and to determine whether their recommended diagnostic workups are safe, effective, and clinically appropriate. By comparing LLM-suggested diagnoses and workup recommendations with reference clinical outcomes, this study provides preliminary insights into the safety characteristics and potential clinical utility of LLM-based decision support, informing the responsible development and implementation of future clinical AI systems.

Methods

Study Design and Ethics

This study was a cross-sectional evaluation of LLMs for the early assessment of ophthalmic emergencies. Standardized case vignettes with sequential levels of clinical information were constructed to approximate routine emergency workflows, in which clinicians progressively gather patient history, basic ocular examination findings, and, when available, specialist investigations. All cases were derived from deidentified teaching materials or publicly accessible clinical resources, with all potentially identifiable information removed prior to use. Because the study involved no real-time patient care and no identifiable patient data, it was deemed exempt from institutional ethics committee review by the Institutional Review Board of the Eye, Ear, Nose, and Throat Hospital of Fudan University in accordance with institutional policy and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Case Selection and Case Construction

Cases were identified from EyeRounds.org, a publicly accessible online teaching repository maintained by the Department of Ophthalmology and Visual Sciences at the University of Iowa. Ophthalmic emergency case reports were screened to identify cases suitable for vignette construction. The inclusion criteria were: (1) an acute presentation requiring same-day or urgent ophthalmic assessment; (2) sufficient information to reconstruct a coherent clinical history and examination; and (3) availability of a reference diagnosis and an expert-recommended diagnostic workup. Nonacute, purely elective, or follow-up cases were excluded. Each candidate case was independently reviewed and refined by 2 board-certified ophthalmologists, with discrepancies resolved by consensus. The final data set comprised 34 cases, categorized as typical or atypical ophthalmic emergencies according to predefined criteria. Typical ophthalmic emergencies were defined as time-critical, vision-threatening conditions with relatively classic acute presentations that usually require rapid emergency department evaluation and urgent ophthalmology consultation within hours. Atypical ophthalmic emergencies were defined as urgent conditions that still warrant same-day ophthalmologic assessment but present with subtler or more nonspecific histories and basic examination findings. These presentations may be mistaken for benign or nonophthalmic conditions, placing them at higher risk of underrecognition and undertriage.

For each case, data were extracted from the original case narratives and organized into a three-level, stepwise information structure designed to mirror routine clinical reasoning. Level 1 (L1) included only the presenting complaint and relevant medical, ocular, and social history. Level 2 incorporated L1 information plus key bedside examination findings reasonably available in a general emergency department (e.g., visual acuity, intraocular pressure, extraocular movement, and pupillary responses). Level 3 (L3) further added the results of specialist ophthalmic examinations obtained in the real case (e.g., detailed slit lamp biomicroscopy and gonioscopy). Additional ancillary testing (e.g., B-scan ultrasonography, OCT, or visual field testing) was not included.

For each case, the expert panel designated the categories performed or clearly indicated in routine clinical management as the reference ancillary test set. LLM Selection and Prompting Protocol

Six commercially available deep-reasoning LLMs from major providers in the United States and China were evaluated: Doubao (ByteDance), DeepSeek (DeepSeek AI), Kimi-2 (Moonshot AI), ChatGPT-5 (OpenAI), Gemini-3 (Google), and Grok-4 (xAI). Each model was accessed via its official interface and run in its dedicated deep-reasoning or “thinking” mode. All models were evaluated using the same set of English-language ophthalmic emergency cases to minimize potential bias from translation. For every case–model interaction, a new dialog session was initiated, and the same standardized prompt was used (Supplementary Materials, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). All evaluations were conducted between December 1 and 5, 2025, and model versions reflected the releases available during this period.

Outcome Definitions and Scoring

All outputs were independently assessed by 2 ophthalmologists, with disagreements resolved by consensus and, when necessary, adjudicated by a third reviewer. All model outputs were anonymized prior to evaluation, and graders were blinded to the identity of the LLM generating each response. Graders evaluated responses based solely on the clinical information presented at each stage and were not informed of the study hypotheses or comparative analyses.

Diagnostic Correctness

For each model output, the primary diagnosis was compared with the expert reference. Diagnostic performance was graded using a three-level scale: (1) fully correct, when the diagnosis, eye laterality (when applicable), and key etiologic or anatomical details matched the reference; (2) partially correct, when the core disease entity was correctly identified but essential qualifiers (e.g., specific cause, subtype, or anatomical details) were incomplete or inaccurate; and (3) incorrect, when the proposed diagnosis differed from the reference disease entity, even if some descriptive details were correct.

Triage Category Accuracy

Each case was assigned a reference triage category (typical vs. atypical ophthalmic emergency) by the expert panel before model testing. Model outputs were scored as correct if the suggested triage category matched the reference and incorrect otherwise, yielding a binary triage outcome for each model–case–level combination.

Ancillary Test Recommendation Patterns

Ancillary tests recommended by each model were mapped to categories A–J, as described above. For each output, we calculated the number of ancillary test categories recommended by the model and by the expert reference and derived a utilization ratio (model-recommended categories divided by reference categories). Test recommendations were then classified into 3 patterns: undertesting (ratio < 1), exact match (ratio = 1), and overtesting (ratio > 1).

Composite Safety Outcome

To approximate overall clinical safety, we constructed a four-level composite outcome integrating diagnostic correctness, triage-category accuracy, and ancillary test utilization. Each output was classified as: (1) ideal, defined as a fully correct diagnosis, correct triage category, and exact ancillary test match; (2) safe but overtesting, defined as a correct triage category and at least a partially correct diagnosis (fully or partially correct) with overtesting of ancillary test categories; (3) potentially dangerous, defined as any incorrect diagnosis or triage category in the presence of undertesting; or (4) intermediate, encompassing all remaining combinations that were neither clearly ideal nor clearly unsafe. The composite outcome was designed as a rule-based integration of diagnostic correctness, triage accuracy, and testing behavior to approximate overall decision safety within a simulated clinical framework. Because no validated weighting scheme exists for balancing these dimensions in ophthalmic emergencies, all components were intentionally treated equally to preserve transparency and reproducibility.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 22.0; IBM Corp.) and R statistical software (version 4.3.3). Outcomes were summarized as counts and percentages. Descriptive differences in outcome distributions across information levels and models were assessed using chi-squared tests; pairwise chi-squared comparisons applied Holm adjustment for figure annotation. For primary inferential cross-model comparisons accounting for within-case correlation, generalized estimating equations (GEE) with a binomial distribution and logit link were fitted using an exchangeable working correlation structure, with clustering at the case level. Inferential analyses were prespecified for clinically actionable binary endpoints (fully correct diagnosis, triage category accuracy, undertesting, and potentially dangerous outcome), whereas multicategory distributions were primarily reported descriptively to limit multiplicity and preserve interpretability. For information-level stratified analyses (L1–L3), ChatGPT-5 was specified as the reference model. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and two-sided P values are reported for GEE analyses. A 2-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Diagnostic Performance

Across all information levels combined (612 model–case–level outputs), 46.9% of diagnoses were fully correct, 24.5% partially correct, and 28.6% incorrect (Table 2; Fig 1A). When analyzed according to sequential information levels, the proportion of fully correct diagnoses increased from 43.1% at L1 to 43.6% at level 2 and 53.9% at L3 (P = 0.048; Fig 2). Diagnostic accuracy differed significantly across models (P < 0.001; Table 2), with ChatGPT-5 showing the largest proportion of fully correct diagnoses (65.7%) and the lowest error rate (17.6%) in this sample. In GEE pairwise comparisons, ChatGPT-5 had higher odds of a fully correct diagnosis than DeepSeek (OR, 3.54; 95% CI, 1.49–8.43; P = 0.004), Gemini-3 (OR, 2.24; 95% CI, 1.31–3.83; P = 0.003), and Kimi-2 (OR, 2.86; 95% CI, 1.27–6.43; P = 0.011). The difference between ChatGPT-5 and Doubao did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.062). Triage Category Accuracy

Overall triage accuracy was 85.3% (522/612 outputs) and differed significantly across models (P = 0.003; Table 2; Fig 1B). Correct triage rates were generally high, ranging from 76.5% for Kimi-2 to 94.1% for ChatGPT-5. In contrast to diagnostic performance, triage accuracy remained stable across information levels, with comparable proportions of correct assignments at L1, L2, and L3 (86.3%, 84.8%, and 84.8%, respectively; P = 0.89; Fig 2). Generalized estimating equations pairwise comparisons indicated that Doubao had higher odds of correct triage than Kimi-2 (OR, 3.18; 95% CI, 1.27–8.00; P = 0.014), while other pairwise differences did not reach statistical significance (Tables S2 and S6, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org).

Ancillary Test Recommendation Patterns

Ancillary test recommendations were categorized according to the prespecified taxonomy (Table 1). Undertesting was the most frequent pattern (51.0%), followed by overtesting (27.5%) and exact matches (21.6%) (Table 2; Fig 1C). The overall distribution of these patterns did not differ significantly across information levels (P = 0.12; Fig 2), indicating that additional clinical detail mainly shifted which tests were requested, rather than the overall tendency to underinvestigate or overinvestigate. In contrast, test-utilization patterns varied substantially across models (P < 0.001; Table 2). In GEE analyses, ChatGPT-5 was significantly less likely to undertest compared to DeepSeek (OR, 0.43; 95% CI, 0.21–0.89; P = 0.022) and Gemini-3 (OR, 0.18; 95% CI, 0.08–0.38; P < 0.001). Tables S3 and S7 (available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org) report the full GEE results and information-level stratified comparisons.

Composite Outcomes

Across all outputs, the composite safety outcome indicated that the ideal scenario occurred in 7.5%, safe but overtesting in 16.8%, potentially dangerous combinations in 20.4%, and intermediate patterns in 55.2% (Table 2; Fig 1D). The distribution of composite outcomes differed significantly across models (P < 0.001). ChatGPT-5 and Doubao produced relatively few potentially dangerous outputs (10.8% and 9.8%, respectively), whereas DeepSeek and Gemini-3 had higher proportions (30.4% and 28.4%, respectively), with Grok-4 and Kimi-2 showing intermediate performance. In GEE pairwise comparisons focusing on the potentially dangerous outcomes, ChatGPT-5 demonstrated lower odds of a potentially dangerous outcome than DeepSeek (OR, 0.28; 95% CI, 0.10–0.74; P = 0.010) and Gemini-3 (OR, 0.31; 95% CI, 0.11–0.89; P = 0.029). Differences between ChatGPT-5 and the remaining models were not statistically significant. Tables S4 and S8 (available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org) provide the complete results and information-level stratified analyses.

Discussion

This study evaluated 6 LLMs for the early assessment of ophthalmic emergencies using 34 clinical cases presented across 3 sequential information levels. All models were tested in their deliberate or deep-reasoning modes. Overall, the models demonstrated moderate to good diagnostic and triage performance; however, substantial variability was observed in ancillary test recommendations and composite safety profiles. Among the evaluated deep-reasoning systems, ChatGPT-5 exhibited, within this study sample, a comparatively more safety-oriented profile, combining comparatively higher diagnostic accuracy with fewer potentially dangerous combinations of diagnostic, triage, and testing errors.

Comparison with Existing Literature

Our findings are broadly consistent with prior studies indicating that contemporary LLMs can achieve and, in some cases, approach ophthalmology trainee- or specialist-level performance on structured questions and clinical cases. Antaki et al13 reported that ChatGPT achieved >50% accuracy across ophthalmology examination questions, with performance varying by subspecialty. Thirunavukarasu et al14 subsequently demonstrated that GPT-4 significantly outperformed GPT-3.5 on 347 ophthalmology questions (61.7% vs. 48.4%). In more complex clinical scenarios, Milad et al15 and Mikhail et al16 showed that GPT-4 can generate coherent diagnostic reasoning and next-step recommendations in challenging ophthalmic cases, although occasional hallucinations and overconfident errors were observed. Beyond ophthalmology, several studies have evaluated LLMs for diagnosis and triage in general emergency medicine, reporting triage accuracies in the 80% to 90% range and performance comparable to laypersons and, in some settings, approaching that of clinical physicians.17 In eye-specific emergency settings, Lyons et al18 and Kreso et al found that GPT-4-based tools could reach high diagnostic and triage accuracy relative to ophthalmology trainees and established telephone triage systems. The present study extends this literature by directly comparing multiple deep-reasoning LLMs using a sequential information framework and by explicitly incorporating ancillary test utilization and a composite safety endpoint into model evaluation.

Contribution of LLMs in Ophthalmic Emergency Settings

Our findings highlight the potential value of LLMs as adjunctive decision-support tools in emergency ophthalmology, particularly in settings with limited on-call ophthalmology coverage. Prior studies have documented the scarcity of ophthalmologists in general emergency departments and the consequent reliance on nonspecialist physicians for the initial triage of ocular complaints, which can result in both underevaluation and overevaluation.4,19 In our study, even under history-only conditions (L1), several models achieved fully correct diagnoses in over 40% of cases and correct triage decisions in more than 80% of outputs, with ChatGPT-5 reaching a triage accuracy of 94.1% across all information levels. These results suggest that, when provided with structured clinical information, LLMs can approximate expert-level reasoning in prioritizing ophthalmic emergencies, potentially assisting nonspecialists in recognizing sight-threatening conditions that require urgent referral. Notably, triage accuracy remained relatively stable across L1 to L3, whereas diagnostic accuracy improved with additional examination and test information. This pattern implies that key “red-flag” historical features may be sufficient for an LLM to identify most emergencies, even when detailed ophthalmic examination or imaging is unavailable. In resource-limited emergency settings, LLMs could therefore be deployed primarily as triage and prioritization assistants, enabling frontline clinicians to identify patients who require immediate attention before specialist consultation or equipment is accessible.

Ancillary Test Recommendations and Cross-Linguistic Differences

A distinctive finding of this study is that undertesting was more common than overtesting across all models, with approximately half of the outputs recommending fewer ancillary test categories than the expert reference. From a safety perspective, this is concerning, as omitted imaging or laboratory tests may delay the recognition of conditions such as open-globe injury, endophthalmitis, or vascular occlusion. However, models differed markedly in their “testing style”: some (for example, ChatGPT-5 and Doubao) exhibited a more overinclusive or balanced approach, whereas others (such as DeepSeek and Gemini-3) were strongly conservative, frequently recommending fewer tests than clinically indicated.

These differences likely reflect both technical factors and the clinical cultures embedded in their training data.20 Notably, 3 of the evaluated LLMs were developed by China-based companies, and their default assumptions about test ordering may implicitly mirror practice environments in mainland China, where emergency physicians often face high patient volumes, uneven resource distribution, and limited access to advanced ophthalmic imaging.21 In such settings, it is common to prioritize a minimal but “sufficient” set of tests combined with empirical treatment, rather than performing exhaustive diagnostic confirmation for every case. By contrast, many Western tertiary centers adhere more strictly to guideline-based, evidence-driven practice, emphasizing comprehensive diagnostic evaluation before initiating definitive therapy.22 Consequently, the more conservative testing patterns of Chinese-language models and the more expansive test panels from some English-language models may mirror these regional practice norms. These findings highlight that LLM behavior is shaped by both biomedical knowledge and the implicit health-system context within their training data.23 Any deployment of LLM-based tools in ophthalmic emergency care should therefore incorporate local calibration of test-ordering thresholds, rather than directly adopting defaults optimized for a different healthcare environment.

Composite Safety and Model Selection

As argued by Sendak et al,24 the evaluation of clinical AI should move beyond single-dimensional metrics, such as accuracy, and adopt multidimensional safety indicators, emphasizing that accuracy alone does not ensure clinical safety. Our composite outcome, which integrates diagnosis, triage, and ancillary testing, serves as a pragmatic proxy for overall clinical safety. The ideal scenario remained rare (<10% of outputs), while approximately one-fifth of outputs were classified as potentially dangerous combinations. These findings highlight that high diagnostic or triage accuracy alone does not guarantee a safe overall care pathway.

Within this set of LLMs, ChatGPT-5 (and, to a lesser extent, Doubao) generated more ideal and “safe but overtesting” outputs and fewer potentially dangerous combinations, whereas DeepSeek and Gemini-3 generated the largest share of potentially dangerous patterns. Previous meta-analytic evidence suggests that, in many emergency and acute-care decision settings, a slightly overinclusive diagnostic strategy can reduce the risk of serious adverse events compared with conservative undertesting approaches.25 For pilot implementation in emergency eye care settings, this implies that model selection should not rely solely on headline diagnostic accuracy. Instead, health systems should consider composite safety behavior, tolerance for overtesting, and the feasibility of human oversight.26 In resource-limited environments, a slightly overinclusive model that minimizes dangerous undertesting scenarios may be preferable, provided that its recommendations are guided by locally agreed care pathways and are consistently reviewed by clinicians.

Implications for Clinical Application

This study demonstrates that contemporary deep-reasoning LLMs can meaningfully contribute to the early assessment of ophthalmic emergencies, extending beyond the scope of isolated examination-style questions. Using expert-curated cases with sequentially presented information levels, we show that these systems can maintain high triage accuracy and achieve moderate-to-good diagnostic performance while also generating structured ancillary test recommendations and composite safety profiles. In settings with limited onsite ophthalmology coverage, such models could be embedded as supervised decision-support tools, enabling nonspecialist clinicians to identify vision-threatening presentations sooner, prioritize referrals, and avoid incomplete workups. The inclusion of both Chinese- and English-language models also highlights a broader conceptual implication: LLMs not only encode medical knowledge but also reflect the clinical cultures and resource norms present in their training environments. This creates an opportunity to deliberately “tune” models toward locally appropriate testing thresholds and risk tolerance, supporting their evolution into configurable, context-aware assistants for emergency eye care.

Methodological Interpretation of Outcome Measures

The grading framework used in this study should not be interpreted as a validated measure of clinical outcomes. The diagnostic and testing categories were operational constructs developed to enable structured comparison of LLM reasoning under standardized conditions. Consistent with this perspective, the Decision support systems driven by AI reporting guidelines, early clinical studies of AI systems represent a developmental and exploratory phase analogous to phase 1 to 2 trials, where structured performance assessment and safety-oriented evaluation precede large-scale efficacy testing. Such studies commonly employ pragmatic or proxy endpoints to characterize system behavior within simulated or limited clinical environments, acknowledging that direct clinical outcome validation is premature at this stage.27 Accordingly, partially correct diagnoses may still lead to appropriate triage and downstream management in emergency care, and risk-averse overtesting may in some scenarios represent a defensible strategy to avoid missed vision-threatening disease. Because diagnostic accuracy, triage decisions, and testing behaviors represent complementary aspects of clinical reasoning rather than independent endpoints, an integrated measure was introduced to summarize overall decision patterns within the simulated workflow. The composite outcome, therefore, represents an exploratory safety proxy integrating these dimensions rather than a clinically validated endpoint. Because no validated weighting framework exists for balancing these components in ophthalmic emergency decision-making, they were treated equally to preserve transparency and reproducibility, consistent with recommendations for early-stage clinical AI evaluation.

Limitations and Challenges

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the modest sample size with few rare or atypical presentations may not fully capture critical failure modes (particularly in rare or diagnostically ambiguous emergencies where even experienced clinicians can disagree). This reflects the study design, which intentionally used expert-curated emergency teaching cases to enable controlled sequential evaluation across standardized information levels rather than data set–scale benchmarking. Accordingly, findings should be interpreted as exploratory comparative note signals that require confirmation in larger prospective datasets. Second, cases and reference labels were derived from a single repository and curated by one study team, potentially introducing selection bias and limiting generalizability, despite adherence to standardized procedures. Third, the vignette-based design precluded assessment of how clinical practice factors, such as time pressure or clinician trust, affect tool adoption. Finally, the evaluation relied on a single prompting strategy and excluded multimodal inputs (e.g., fundus photography or OCT), potentially underestimating optimal model performance. Consequently, practical challenges related to governance, medico-legal accountability, bias mitigation, and postdeployment error surveillance remain important considerations for any clinical implementation in practice.

In summary, this evaluation of 34 ophthalmic emergency cases shows that LLMs can achieve moderate-to-good diagnostic performance and consistently high triage accuracy. However, substantial variability remains in ancillary test recommendations and overall safety profiles. Undertesting remains common, and only a minority of outputs achieve an ideal combination of correct diagnosis, appropriate triage, and fully aligned test strategies, even in deep-reasoning modes. Among the 6 models tested, ChatGPT-5 exhibited a more safety-oriented pattern, with higher diagnostic accuracy and fewer potentially dangerous combinations, whereas several other models favored more conservative test utilization at the cost of increased risk of unsafe underevaluation. These findings suggest that deep-reasoning LLMs may serve as supervised decision-support tools for triaging ophthalmic emergencies in resource-limited settings; however, they are not a substitute for specialist care and require local calibration alongside robust human oversight.
