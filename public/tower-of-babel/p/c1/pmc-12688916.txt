Giant fibroepithelial stromal polyp of vulva: a case report
Authors: Biki Kumar Sah, Abhishek Kumar Sah, Smriti Karki, Upasana Lamichhane, Ramesh Shrestha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Fibroepithelial stromal polyps (FESPs) are rare benign mesenchymal tumors of the vulva, usually measuring 1–2 cm and often discovered incidentally. Lesions larger than 5 cm are uncommon, and those exceeding 20 cm are extremely rare, with very few reported cases in the literature.Case presentation:We report the case of a 24-year-old female who presented with a gradually enlarging vulvar mass over 4 years. Initially small, the lesion eventually grew to approximately 20 × 15 cm, causing difficulty in walking and mild discomfort. On physical examination, a large, pedunculated mass was noted arising from the right lateral vulvar wall, extending postero-inferiorly into the gluteal cleft and medial aspects of both thighs. Preoperative pelvic Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) revealed a well-defined, homogeneously enhancing mass measuring 13 × 12.5 × 14.8 cm, suggestive of a giant fibroepithelial stromal polyp. The mass was surgically excised, and histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of fibroepithelial stromal polyp.Discussion:Giant vulvar fibroepithelial stromal polyps are exceptionally rare, particularly those measuring more than 20 cm. These lesions can present with significant morbidity due to their size and location, posing both diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. This case highlights the importance of considering FESP in the differential diagnosis of large vulvar masses and the role of imaging and histology in accurate diagnosis and management.Conclusion:Early identification and surgical excision of large fibroepithelial stromal polyps are crucial to prevent complications and improve patient quality of life. This case adds valuable insight into this rare clinical entity.

Introduction

Fibroepithelial stromal polyp, a benign skin lesion, is a type of mesenchymal tumor. It is hormone-sensitive, so it tends to occur more in reproductive-age females or females under hormonal replacement therapy (HRT). It occurs in about 25% of female population but it typically occurs in vagina less frequently than in the cervix and vulva, and is rarely seen in extra genital regions[1,2]. The case of fibroepithelial polyp of vulva was first described by Ostor et al in 1988[3]. The size is generally between 1 and 2 cm but rarely exceeds greater than 5 cm in diameter. Though the exact reason of its origin is not known, it is proposed that obesity, hormone factors or insulin resistance might be the reasons for its substantial growth. It may vary from small, polypoidal-pedunculated, mostly found as solitary hyperpigmented to hypopigmented lesions. Here, we present a unique and probably the first case of a giant fibroepithelial stromal polyp of the vulva from Nepal, measuring a size of 15-20 cm in diameter. This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE checklist[4].

Case presentation

A 24-year-old unmarried, non-smoker, non-alcoholic, female presented to the Out Patient Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology with complaint of a huge swelling in vulval area for 4 years. The swelling was initially small but gradually increased to its current size leading to ambulatory difficulty and mild pain. She had a normal menstrual cycle, normal bowel and bladder habits, with no medical comorbidities, past surgical history, or family history of similar vulvar swelling. Due to its giant size, she was compelled to skip schooling due to difficulty in walking and associated pain.

On examination, her general condition was fair, thin-built, with BMI 18.5 kg/m2. Vital signs were normal, and physical examination findings were unremarkable. Her breasts and thyroid were normal, and there were no palpable peripheral lymph nodes. On local examination, there was a huge, solitary, non-tender, polypoidal mass measuring approximately 20 × 15 cm, arising from the right side of the vulva with a smooth surface. It was soft to firm in consistency with no increase in local temperature over the mass (Fig. 1). There was a small superficial ulcer over the inferior aspect of the mass, measuring approximately 1 × 1 cm, characterized by a punched out edge. The floor was covered with red granulation tissue, accompanied by minimal foul-smelling discharge. Systemic examinations yielded normal results, and baseline preoperative investigations were within normal limits.

Preoperative Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) of pelvis with bilateral hip joints, revealed a homogeneously enhancing, well defined, pedunculated, mass measuring 13 × 12.5 × 14.8 cm. The mass arose from the right lateral wall of vulva, protruding postero-inferiorly into gluteal cleft and medial aspect of bilateral thighs, most likely a giant fibroepithelial stromal polyp of the vulva. The Uterus exhibited normal size with unremarkable bilateral adnexae (Fig. 2). Upper abdominal sonography scan and chest X-ray scan showed normal findings. The patient underwent a wide local excision of the vulvar mass, ensuring a 1.5-2 cm tumor free surgical margin up to the inferior layer of urogenital diaphragm. V-Y flap reconstruction was performed to repair the perineal defect. Minimal blood loss was reported. At 2 weeks postoperative, a three cm defect of the reconstructed perineal wound was observed, healing by secondary intention. Consequently, her postoperative Clavien Dindo complication was categorized as Grade II. The Foley catheter was removed on third postoperative day, and the rest of her postoperative days were uneventful. After 3 months of follow-up, there was a significant recovery.

On gross examination, the mass measured 20 × 15 × 9 cm, and on serial sectioning, the tissue appeared soft gray-white to yellowish glistening, along with minute cystic spaces with hemorrhagic material (Fig. 3). Histo-pathology of the section revealed keratinized stratified squamous epithelial along with hypo- and hypercellular areas. Spindle and stellate shaped cells were observed in a loose collagenous myxoid stroma. Area of hyalinization, eosinophilic deposits and large areas of dissecting collagen bands infiltrating adipose tissue were also seen, suggesting a giant fibroepithelial stromal polyp of the vulva (Figs 4 and 5). Discussion

Fibroepithelial stromal polyp is a soft, benign mesenchymal tissue tumor generally found in submandibular region, axilla, neck and inguinal areas. It can also be found in vagina, cervix and vulva but needs to be distinguished from other benign or malignant mesenchymal tumors. This case report presents a case of fibroepithelial stroma polyp of the vulva measuring 20 cm which is exceedingly giant compared to the other fibroepithelial stromal polyps, which rarely exceed the size of 5 cm as reported in the literature[2]. Elliott and Elliott has mentioned polyps ranging from 0.5 to 5 mm in mature women[5]. The exact etiology of these stromal polyps is unknown. However, these polyps have been found to be predominantly hormonally sensitive, mostly to oestrogen and progesterone, triggering their growth, and are therefore mostly in reproductive-aged females or during pregnancy. In pregnancy, they are often multiple and regress spontaneously after delivery[6]. There are few studies regarding pathophysiology of fibroepithelial polyp. One of the study done by Safoury et al showed tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL) and mast cells play important role in development of polyp. This study was done analyzing skin sections from 15 patients with help of enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay and reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction[7]. Another study done by Farag et al proved insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) plays key role in susceptibility of fibroepithelial polyp with help of gene polymorphism analysis[8].

Ostor et al first described fibroepithelial stromal poylps of vulva and vagina in 13 cases series, with their differentiation from sarcoma botryoides. These polyps mostly presented with swelling, while two of them presented with vaginal bleeding[1]. Ogura et al described a case of 60-year-old woman with a fibroepithelial stromal polyp of vagina who presented with abnormal vaginal bleeding[9]. In our case, swelling in the vulvar region was the only symptom that led her to visit the hospital.

The differential diagnosis that should be considered for vulvar mass include angiomyofibroblastoma, aggressive angiomyxoma, sarcoma, botryoid embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma, perineurinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, cervicovaginal myofibroblastoma, etc. There are many vulvovaginal soft tissue lesions with similar features, so it is important to consider them before making a definitive diagnosis. The Diagnostic algorithm required to clinch a diagnosis includes a detail history, physical examination, radiological investigations, and histopathological examination.

It is important to determine the origin and extent of the lesion, along with its blood supply, before operative intervention. Easily accessible ultrasonography can be considered the initial radiological test before CT or MRI, providing limited information[10]. Ultrasonography can assess size, blood flow status, and follow-up for gynecologic malignancies[11]. MRI is superior to CT scan for evaluating soft tissue tumors, offering accurate data on size, site, underlying soft tissue involvement, lymph node status, and lesion extent. T1-weighted MRI finding of fibroepithelial stroma of polyp shows abundant fibrous tissue as linear hyperintense areas within the lesion, whereas in T2-weighted MRI shows abundant fibrous tissue as stratiform hypointense areas surrounded by patchy hyperintense areas[12].

Histopathological examination establishes the final diagnosis among the various differentials of fibroepithelial stromal polyp of the vulva, playing a crucial role in excluding malignancies. Typical histopathological features include benign mesenchymal tissue with spindle-nucleated cells and fine chromatin loosely arranged between myxoid stroma. Regarding Immunohistogical markers it shows positive stains with Desmin, Estrogen Receptor (ER), Progesterone Receptor (PR), variable with vimentin, occasional with actin and focal rarely with MyoD1 but also shows negative stains mostly with Smooth Muscle Actin (SMA), High Mobility Group A2 (HMGA2), S100, cytokeratin and myogenin.[13,14]. To confirm that the polyp is not derived from smooth muscle tissue, immunohistochemistry was performed, showing a negative result for the smooth muscle marker, i.e., desmin; it is different from the study done by Dura et al which revealed fibroepithelial polyp positive for desmin for estrogen receptor[15,16].

Management of fibroepithelial stromal of polyp of vulva depends on patient’s health awareness. In this case, the patient presented to hospital late when the polyp was about 20 cm in size. Optimal treatment involves establishing a definitive diagnosis of fibroepithelial polyp of the vulva through detailed history, physical examination, imaging, and histopathological examination. Treatment options include surgical excision with primary closure or flap reconstruction for large masses, or excisional biopsy for tiny lesions.[17,18]. In our patient, surgical excision of polyp was performed with V-Y flap reconstruction of the perineal wound defect to its giant size causing difficulty in walking and mild pain. Other treatment option include minimal invasive solution, cauterization, and cryotherapy; but these options were not applicable in our patient due to large size of polyp. Patient complete recovered after 1 month to her normal state.

Conclusion

Giant fibroepithelial stroma of polyps of the vulva are rare. Diagnosis should be made considering all the history, physical examination and radiological imaging. Management involves surgical excision with primary closure or reconstruction without compromising the patient’s reproductive health.
