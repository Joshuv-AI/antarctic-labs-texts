Metformin enhances external urethral sphincter integrity and restores continence via AMPK activation in a rat model of stress urinary incontinence
Authors: Yuting Xu, Xuhong Li, Alvaro Munoz, Li Jiang, Lihua Huang, Yanhua Zhou, Liping Zhu, Youbo Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStress urinary incontinence (SUI) is primarily caused by structural and functional damage to the external urethral sphincter (EUS). In previous studies, metformin, a known AMP‐activated protein kinase (AMPK) activator, has shown repair effects after skeletal muscle injury. This study explored its therapeutic effect and molecular mechanisms in SUI associated with EUS injury.MethodsThe SUI model was established by double vaginal dilation. Rats were assigned to sham, vehicle‐treated, or metformin‐treated (200 mg/kg/day, oral) groups. Urodynamic testing was performed after 7 and 14 days. Subsequently, EUS tissues were isolated for histological (hematoxylin and eosin [H&E], Masson's), apoptotic (TUNEL), ultrastructural (transmission electron microscopy [TEM]), and molecular analyses. Finally, C2C12 myotubes cells were treated with varying concentrations of metformin to evaluate the expression of phosphorylated AMPK (p‐AMPK) and MYH1/2, which are markers of skeletal muscle fiber type and regeneration.ResultsAbdominal leak point pressure (ALPP) and bladder leak point pressure (BLPP) decreased after double vaginal dilation, suggesting that SUI was established. After treatment for 7 and 14 days, respectively, metformin significantly increased ALPP and BLPP (p < 0.05) without affecting maximum bladder capacity (p > 0.05). Masson's staining and TEM revealed that metformin improved disorganized EUS architecture. Further, Western blot analysis demonstrated that metformin increased the expression of MYH1/2 and p‐AMPK in vivo and in vitro. Meanwhile TUNEL assay showed that metformin treatment reduced apoptotic cells in the EUS from SUI rats.ConclusionsThese results indicate that metformin enhances EUS repair to improve SUI via activating the AMPK signaling pathway. These findings provide new insights into therapeutic strategies for SUI.Keywords: AMPK signaling pathway, external urethral sphincter, metformin treatment, stress urinary incontinence

BACKGROUND

Stress urinary incontinence (SUI) is defined as the demonstrable involuntary leakage of urine during increased intra‐abdominal pressure, occurring in the absence of detrusor contraction, and typically provoked by physical maneuvers, including coughing, sneezing, or physical exertion. 1 , 2 A nationwide population‐based epidemiology study in China revealed SUI as the predominant subtype of urinary incontinence, demonstrating an overall prevalence of 7% among adult women. 3 Previous studies have shown that factors such as middle to advanced age, high body mass index (≥24 kg/m2), ethnicity, literacy, vaginal delivery number, chronic cough, diabetes mellitus, and smoking are all associated with an increased risk of SUI. 4 With global population aging and the rising burden of chronic diseases, the incidence and severity of SUI have increased, significantly compromising quality of life and imposing substantial socioeconomic costs. 5 , 6 Therefore, in‐depth research on the etiology and prevention of SUI is of great clinical value and social significance.

Normal urinary continence requires the coordinated contraction of the internal urethral sphincter (IUS) and the external urethral sphincter (EUS), along with the activity of pelvic floor muscles, to maintain urethral closure pressure above intravesical pressure, thereby ensuring effective urine storage within the bladder. 7 , 8 The EUS plays a critical role in urinary continence by generating reflex contractions in response to increased abdominal pressure, thereby substantially enhancing urethral closure pressure. 9 , 10 , 11 In this sense, vaginal delivery can be a primary cause of injury to the EUS, as excessive mechanical stress during childbirth labor can overstretch and damage the EUS, levator ani, nerves, and associated pelvic floor structures for support. 12 In multiparous women, these muscles often show strain, fiber loss, and disorganization, leading to impaired urethral closure and SUI. 13 Thus, restoring the structure and function of the EUS is critical for an effective therapy in women with SUI.

Surgical approaches, such as Burch colposuspension, pubourethral sling procedures, and mid‐urethral sling techniques, are commonly employed for patients with moderate‐to‐severe SUI or those who do not respond to conservative treatment. 14 Despite these interventions revealing a clear efficacy, they are associated with surgical risks and postoperative complications. 15 In contrast, approximately 70% of patients present a mild‐to‐moderate SUI, for whom conservative, nonsurgical treatments are typically the first‐line choice. 16 These include pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT), biofeedback devices, electrical or magnetic stimulation, and pharmacotherapy. 1 , 17 The therapeutic success of these approaches is contingent upon sustained patient adherence, yet suboptimal compliance frequently compromises their clinical outcomes. 18 Therefore, pharmacological intervention may be considered as a necessary therapeutic strategy to optimize the outcomes of conservative management. For instance, a double‐blind, multicenter randomized controlled trial demonstrated that the combination of PFMT with duloxetine, the principal pharmacological agent for SUI, yields significantly better outcomes compared to either intervention. 19 However, its clinical utility is often limited by adverse effects such as nausea, vomiting, dizziness, anxiety, and even sexual dysfunction, which frequently lead to treatment interruption. 20 , 21 These limitations highlight the urgent need for novel, safe, and effective agents capable of promoting EUS repair and restoring continence.

Metformin, a drug with blood glucose reducing effects, is widely used as a first‐line therapy for type 2 diabetes and has also demonstrated therapeutic potential in a range of conditions, including cancer, aging, and polycystic ovary syndrome. 22 , 23 , 24 Recently, metformin has been reported to enhance myofiber membrane integrity, promote muscle regeneration, and improve neuromuscular deficits in mouse models of Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD). 25 Because restoration of the EUS is essential for the effective treatment of SUI, we hypothesize that metformin may contribute to structural and functional recovery of the EUS and thereby ameliorate SUI.

This study utilized a double vaginal‐dilation rat model that closely replicates clinical SUI, 26 with the objective of systematically evaluating the functional effects and molecular mechanisms of metformin, with a focus on EUS injury and repair. Our results offer a novel therapeutic avenue for the treatment of SUI.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental animals

Female Sprague–Dawley rats (8 weeks old, weighing 230 ± 20 g), were obtained from the Department of Animal Science at Central South University. Animals were housed and maintained in a specific pathogen‐free grade vivarium facility in the same university. A total of 48 female Sprague–Dawley rats were used in this study. Twelve rats (6 sham and 6 SUI) were used to validate model establishment. The remaining 36 rats were randomly allocated to sham, SUI + vehicle, and SUI + metformin groups (n = 12 each). The overall experimental design is summarized in Figure S1.

Establishment of the SUI rat model

The SUI model was developed by intraperitoneal injection of 1% sodium pentobarbital followed by insertion of a modified 8F double‐lumen balloon catheter into the vagina. The catheter was carefully anchored to the skin surrounding the vaginal region using absorbable 3–0 sutures. Vaginal dilation was simulated by inflating the balloon with 3 mL of water. A 120‐g weight was then suspended from the distal end of the catheter to ensure stability. To enhance reproducibility and reliability, this procedure was repeated after 3 days to establish the vaginal distension injury. In the sham group, the balloon was not inflated with water, whereas all other steps remained consistent with those of the SUI group. To confirm the establishment of the SUI model, six rats from each of the sham and SUI groups were randomly selected for urodynamic evaluation and histological examinations.

Urodynamic tests

Rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 10% urethane (1.2 g/kg; Aladdin, Shanghai, China). After the bladder was emptied, an F3 ureteral catheter was inserted and connected to a microinjection pump and a pressure transducer, and connected to a multichannel physiological recorder (ALC‐CMG system, Shanghai Aikotai Biological Technology Co., Ltd., China). Sterile saline was infused into the bladder at a constant rate of 0.2 mL/min. Infusion was ceased upon the appearance of the first drop of urine at the urethral meatus. The infused volume at this point was recorded as the maximum bladder capacity (MBC), with the corresponding intravesical pressure defined as the bladder leak point pressure (BLPP). Once MBC was determined, the bladder was emptied again and refilled with 50% of the previously measured MBC volume. Subsequently, pressure was applied to the lower abdomen of the rat to simulate a Valsalva maneuver. The abdominal leak point pressure (ALPP) was recorded as the intravesical pressure at which the first drop of urine appeared at the external urethral orifice. For each urodynamic parameter (MBC, BLPP, and ALPP), two consecutive measurements were obtained per animal and averaged for statistical analysis.

Treatment with metformin in SUI rats

After SUI model establishment, rats were randomly assigned to three groups: sham (n = 12), vehicle‐treated (n = 12), and metformin‐treated (200 mg/kg, oral gavage, n = 12). Metformin was dissolved in sterile water and administered once daily. The dosage of 200 mg/kg was selected as it is a well‐established protocol widely utilized in Sprague–Dawley rat models of various diseases. 27 , 28 , 29 Furthermore, pharmacokinetic evidence confirms that this dosage maintains tissue exposure within a therapeutically relevant, non‐suprapharmacological range, with hepatic concentrations remaining below 1 mM, thereby ensuring efficacy while avoiding off‐target toxicity. 30 At each posttreatment time point (day 7 and day 14), six rats per group were randomly selected to undergo urodynamic evaluation and were then euthanized for subsequent analyses.

Animal euthanasia and tissue preparation

At the designated endpoints (day 7 or day 14), animals were killed by CO2 inhalation (40% chamber volume per minute). Death was confirmed by cessation of respiration and cardiac activity, and cervical dislocation was applied, if necessary, in accordance with the Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Experimental Animals (GB/T 39760‐2021). EUS, liver, and kidney tissues were immediately harvested. For histological and immunohistochemical analyses, tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4°C, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. For transmission electron microscopy (TEM), fresh EUS tissues were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (pH 7.4) at 4°C. For Western blot, tissues were snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C.

Histological and apoptotic staining

Masson's trichrome staining (Servicebio, China) and TUNEL assay (Servicebio, China) were performed to assess tissue damage and apoptosis using EUS sections, respectively. To evaluate potential toxicity toward other organs, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was conducted on liver and kidney tissues following standard protocols (Servicebio, China).

Immunohistochemistry

The EUS sections were subjected to immunohistochemistry using an anti‐MYH1/2 antibody (Abcam, UK; 1:100), following the protocol described by Gao et al. 31 Three nonconsecutive sections per animal were analyzed to ensure representativeness and account for tissue variability. Images were captured using a digital slide scanner (Servicebio, China).

Transmission electron microscopy analyses

To examine the ultrastructure of the EUS, tissue samples were fixed in electron microscopy fixative (Servicebio, China), postfixed with 1% osmium tetroxide, and dehydrated through a series of graded ethanol–acetone solutions. The specimens were then embedded in epoxy resin and sectioned into ultrathin slices (60–80 nm). After staining, sections were examined using a transmission electron microscope (TEM, Hitachi, Japan).

Cell culture

To determine the effects of metformin on myogenic differentiation, C2C12 mouse cells (Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shanghai, China) were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) containing 100 IU/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Gibco). To induce myogenic differentiation, cells were maintained at 37°C in 5% CO2 and allowed to reach 70%–80% confluence before the culture medium was replaced with differentiation medium containing 2% horse serum (Gibco). The differentiation process was monitored daily using light microscopy, with the formation of multinucleated myotubes typically observed by day 5. During the myotube formation phase, metformin was added to the differentiation medium at various concentrations (0, 100, 200, 400, 800, and 1600 μmol/L) and incubated for 24 h. At the end of treatment, cells were immediately harvested for subsequent analyses.

Evaluation of compound C effects on the AMPK pathway

Upon observation of early myotube formation, the cultured cells were randomly divided into four groups and treated in serum‐free medium as follows: the control group received no intervention; the compound C group was treated with 10 μmol/L compound C to inhibit the AMPK pathway; the metformin group received 400 μmol/L metformin (the concentration was determined from previous experiments for inducing myotube formation); and the metformin + compound C group received both 10 μmol/L compound C and 400 μmol/L metformin. All treatments were performed with three biological replicates, and after 24 h, the cells were harvested for molecular evaluations.

Western blot characterizations

Either EUS tissues or C2C12 cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors to extract total protein. Western blot was performed as previously described. 32 The following primary antibodies were used in this study: anti‐GAPDH (Proteintech, 60004‐1‐Ig; 1:1000), anti‐AMPK (Proteintech, 80209‐5‐RR; 1:1000), anti‐p‐AMPK (CST, 2535; 1:1000), anti‐Bcl‐2 (Proteintech, 26593‐1‐AP; 1:1000), anti‐Bax (Proteintech, 50599‐2‐Ig; 1:1000), and anti‐MYH (Abcam, ab37484; 1:1000). Briefly, primary antibody incubation was performed overnight followed by 1‐h incubation with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies (mouse or rabbit; Beyotime, China) at room temperature. After three washes, bands were detected using an ECL kit (Biosharp, USA) and imaged with a UVP ChemStudio PLUS system (UVP, USA).

Immunofluorescent assay for MYH1/2 in EUS sections

Immunofluorescence staining was performed as previously described. 31 Briefly, slides were washed thrice with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) containing 0.1% Triton X‐100 (PBS‐T) followed by permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X‐100 for 15 min. After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at room temperature for 1 h, sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibody against MYH1/2 (Abcam; 1:100), and images were analyzed using a digital slide scanner (3DHISTECH Ltd., Hungary).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software, and the data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the data. For comparisons between two groups, an independent t‐test was conducted if the assumption of homogeneity of variance was met following Levene's test; otherwise, a corrected t‐test was employed. For comparisons involving three or more groups, one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was initially performed. If the ANOVA results indicated statistical significance and homogeneity of variance (confirmed by Levene's test), post‐hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) test. In cases where homogeneity of variance was violated, the Games–Howell test was used. Statistical significance was considered when p < 0.05.

RESULTS

Decreased ALPP/BLPP and EUS damage in SUI rats

Urodynamic testing showed significantly reduced BLPP and ALPP compared to the sham group. The BLPP in the SUI group (28.08 ± 1.33 mmH2O) was significantly lower than that in the sham group (43.13 ± 1.54 mmH2O) (p < 0.01). Similarly, the ALPP in the SUI group (35.93 ± 0.85 mmH2O) was also significantly decreased compared to that in the sham group (50.72 ± 1.16 mmH2O) (p < 0.01). No significant difference in MBC was observed between the SUI group (1.73 ± 0.57 mL) and the sham group (1.83 ± 0.39 mL) (Figure 1A). In contrast with the sham group, Masson's staining indicated that SUI rats displayed relaxed and disorganized striated muscles with unclear smooth muscle boundaries and increased thickness (Figure 1B). Immunohistochemistry evaluations against MYH1/2 showed significant damage to the EUS of SUI rats and decreased expression of the protein (Figure 1C). TUNEL staining revealed an increased number of positive apoptotic signals in the EUS following SUI induction compared to sham rats (p < 0.001; Figure 1D).

Both ALPP and BLPP were significantly improved by metformin treatment

On posttreatment day 7, the SUI + MET group exhibited a significant recovery in leak point pressures compared to the SUI group. The BLPP in the SUI + MET group increased to 34.76 ± 0.83 mmH2O, which was significantly higher than that of the SUI group (29.78 ± 1.03 mmH2O) (p < 0.001), although it remained lower than the sham (45.14 ± 0.54 mmH2O) (p < 0.001). Meanwhile, ALPP was significantly elevated in the SUI + MET group (41.88 ± 0.78 mmH2O) compared to the SUI group (37.36 ± 0.66 mmH2O) (p < 0.001). These values remained lower than those observed in the sham group (52.10 ± 1.04 mmH2O).

This therapeutic effect was sustained and further evident by day 14. The SUI + MET group (37.93 ± 1.86 mmH2O) continued to show significantly higher BLPP values than the SUI group (33.87 ± 1.20 mmH2O) (p < 0.01), approaching but not fully reaching the level observed in the sham group (45.34 ± 1.05 mmH2O). A similar pattern was observed for ALPP, which was significantly increased in the SUI + MET group (46.94 ± 0.88 mmH2O) compared to the SUI group (41.84 ± 0.86 mmH2O) (p < 0.001), showing a trend toward the sham group (52.08 ± 1.44 mmH2O).

Regarding MBC, no statistically significant differences were observed among the three groups at both day 7 and day 14. The MBC values on day 7 were 1.96 ± 0.74 mL (sham), 1.54 ± 0.20 mL (SUI), and 1.76 ± 0.52 mL (SUI + MET), respectively; the values on day 14 were 2.11 ± 0.31 mL (sham), 1.75 ± 0.41 mL (SUI), and 2.05 ± 0.67 mL (SUI + MET) (Figure 2).

Urodynamic assessment in sham, SUI, and SUI + metformin (SUI + Met) rats at 7 and 14 days postmodeling. Maximum bladder capacity (MBC; n = 6; p > 0.05, ns, not significant). At both 7 and 14 days, bladder leak point pressure (BLPP) decreased in the order sham > SUI + Met > SUI (n = 6; **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001). Abdominal leak point pressure (ALPP) exhibited a similar trend (n = 6; **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).

The morphology of the EUS was significantly improved in SUI rats after metformin treatment

First, to determine the potential organ toxicity of metformin in SUI rats, hepatic and renal tissues were examined after 14 days of administration. No significant histological or morphological abnormalities were observed, suggesting that metformin at this dose does not induce hepatic or renal toxicity (Figure S2). Then, EUS sections were evaluated using Masson's trichrome staining in the sham, SUI + vehicle, and SUI + metformin groups. On day 7, the SUI + vehicle group showed marked fiber disarray and indistinct skeletal‐smooth muscle boundaries, along with evident relaxation. The SUI + metformin group exhibited partial improvement in fiber alignment despite persistent relaxation (Figure 3A). On day 14, although relaxation was less pronounced in the SUI + vehicle group compared to day 7, fiber arrangement remained disordered and structural integrity was not fully restored (Figure 3A). In contrast, in the metformin‐treated group, muscle layers appeared compact and well organized, closely resembling that of the sham group (Figure 3A). To further quantitatively assess EUS structure, muscle thickness was measured at the 3, 6, 9, and 12 o'clock positions, and the average value was calculated to represent overall EUS thickness. Metformin treatment significantly reduced EUS thickness and improved fiber alignment, indicating alleviation of muscle relaxation, with more pronounced effects observed on day 14 (p < 0.05).

On day 7, MYH1/2‐positive signals were markedly reduced in both SUI + vehicle and SUI + metformin groups (Figure 3B). By contrast, on day 14, a notable increase in MYH1/2 immunoreactivity was determined in the SUI + metformin group relative to the SUI + vehicle group, approaching the expression levels observed in the sham group (Figure 3B). Western blot analysis, using GAPDH as a loading control, demonstrated that after 14 days of metformin treatment, MYH1/2 expression in EUS tissues was significantly restored to the levels comparable to those observed in the sham group (p < 0.05), based on three independent replicates (Figure 3C).

Metformin treatment improves the EUS microstructure in SUI rats

Using TEM, we found that the EUS myofibers of sham rats displayed intact ultrastructure, with well‐organized myofibrils, symmetrically distributed sarcomeres, and clearly defined Z lines and M bands (Figure 4). By contrast, the SUI day 1 group exhibited a pronounced structural disruption, including disorganized sarcomeres, asymmetric alignment, and shortened or distorted Z lines and M bands (Figure 4). After the 14‐day metformin treatment, partial recovery was observed in the SUI + vehicle group, although irregular myofibril orientation and localized sarcomeric disarray persisted, whereas in the SUI + metformin rats, most of the EUS myofibrils were well aligned, with preserved sarcomeric symmetry and minimal distortion, closely resembling the sham group (Figure 4).

Metformin alleviates apoptosis rate in the EUS via activation of the AMPK signaling pathway in SUI rats

To determine an antiapoptotic effect of metformin, TUNEL staining showed that on day 7 the SUI group had a significant increase in apoptosis compared to the sham group (p < 0.001), whereas metformin treatment in SUI rats markedly reduced apoptotic signals (p < 0.001), indicating an early protective effect (Figure 5A). Although a reduction in apoptotic signals was observed in the SUI + vehicle group on day 14, the signals remained increased when compared to those in the sham group (p < 0.01). In contrast, apoptotic signals in the SUI + metformin group were comparable to those in the sham group, with no significant difference, suggesting a sustained suppression of apoptosis (Figure 5A). Furthermore, Western blot analysis revealed that the Bcl‐2/Bax ratio was significantly decreased in the SUI + vehicle group after 14 days, whereas the SUI + metformin rats had an increased ratio near to normal levels (Figure 5B).

Western blot analysis demonstrated that p‐AMPK levels were further elevated on day 14 in the SUI + metformin group compared to the SUI + vehicle group (Figure 5C), whereas total AMPK expression remained relatively stable across all three groups.

Metformin promotes myogenic differentiation and expression of MYH1/2 in C2C12 cells via AMPK activation

After induction of differentiation with horse serum, cells were treated with different concentrations of metformin (0, 200, 400, 800, 1600 μmol/L) for 24 h, and among these concentrations, 400 μmol/L metformin produced the most pronounced myogenic differentiation, as evidenced by increased MYH1/2 protein levels assessed using Western blot (Figure 6A). Quantitative immunofluorescence analysis also revealed that 400 μmol/L metformin significantly promoted myogenic differentiation, as reflected by a higher number of myotubes and increased MYH1/2‐positive area relative to total field area compared to untreated SUI controls (p < 0.05; Figure 6B).

Subsequently, total AMPK and p‐AMPK levels were examined in response to increasing concentrations of metformin (0, 100, 200, 400, 800, and 1600 μmol/L) using Western blot analysis (Figure 6C). A dose‐dependent increase in p‐AMPK was observed, whereas total AMPK expression remained largely unchanged, supporting the activation of AMPK signaling (Figure 6C). To further validate an AMPK involvement, a pharmacological inhibition with compound C was performed. The metformin‐induced upregulation of MYH1/2 expression was attenuated by compound C, whereas treatment with compound C alone led to a statistically significant reduction in MYH1/2 (Figure 6D). Consistent with these findings, Western blot results showed that metformin‐induced phosphorylation of AMPK was markedly suppressed by compound C (Figure 6E).

DISCUSSION

Although SUI affects a large proportion of urinary incontinence patients, effective and safe pharmacological treatments remain limited in current clinical practice. Our study is the first to demonstrate that metformin, an activator of AMPK, significantly improves the morphological integrity of the EUS and enhances ALPP and BLPP in a SUI rat model. Mechanistically, additional experiments revealed that metformin activates the AMPK signaling pathway in the EUS, upregulates MYH1/2 expression, and inhibits apoptosis, thereby promoting functional restoration of EUS and improving urinary continence. These results underscore the potential of metformin as a safe and effective candidate for clinical intervention in SUI patients.

Obstetric trauma is a major cause of SUI, and although various animal models have been developed to study SUI, the double vaginal distension rat model remains the most commonly used approach to simulate this condition. 26 , 33 , 34 A significant decrease in BLPP and ALPP is a crucial indicator for confirming SUI in rats. As a key part of the urethra, the EUS is prone to injury during vaginal distension, and this can lead to pelvic floor muscle damage and reduced support, thereby increasing the risk of SUI. 35 Such injury may involve both muscle and nerve tissue and manifest as alterations in gene and protein expression, as well as an increase in apoptosis. 36 , 37 In the employed double vaginal distension model, we observed significant reductions in BLPP and ALPP as early as 1 day postinduction, accompanied by pronounced morphological damage, decreased MYH1/2 protein expression, and increased signals of apoptosis in the EUS. These findings support the use of our model as a reliable platform for future studies investigating drug mechanisms and therapeutic efficacy.

The urethral sphincteric complex functions as the ultimate biomechanical governor for urinary continence, with the EUS acting as its voluntarily modulated effector. Thus, as a key muscle for continence, we focused on evaluating SUI‐induced alterations in the EUS. Crucially distinct from the autonomic internal sphincter, the EUS—composed of striated muscle fibers innervated by somatic motor neurons of the pudendal nerve (S2–S4)—executes a stress‐responsive urethral closure via rapid tetanic contractions. 38 , 39 , 40 This inherent neurophysiological specialization underpins our strategic focus on the pathophysiology of the EUS as a cardinal therapeutic target for SUI.

Previous studies have demonstrated a protective effect of metformin on skeletal muscle. For instance, Langone et al. 41 reported that metformin mitigated toxin‐induced muscle degeneration, with treated mice exhibiting greater resilience and reduced muscle damage. Similarly, metformin has been shown to attenuate skeletal muscle atrophy in rats via ZEB1‐mediated mechanisms. 42 Building on these findings, our study revealed that metformin also exerts significant therapeutic effects in a rat model of SUI characterized by an injured EUS. Notably, our results show that metformin treatment for 7 days led to partial recovery of EUS function, whereas a 14‐day regimen resulted in near‐complete restoration of MYH1/2 expression and marked EUS functional improvement. This time‐dependent efficacy may be attributed to the pharmacokinetics of metformin and the biological timeline required for effective muscle regeneration in rodents.

It should be noted that the follow‐up period in the present study was intentionally limited to 14 days based on the intrinsic characteristics of the double vaginal distension–induced SUI model. In our previous work using the same model, although muscle spindle morphology had not fully recovered by day 21, urodynamic parameters had already normalized to sham levels at this time. 43 This phenomenon, in which functional recovery precedes full structural recovery, is also consistent with previously published studies employing vaginal distension–based SUI models. 44 Therefore, restricting the observation period to within 14 days enables more sensitive detection of treatment‐associated effects before substantial spontaneous functional recovery occurs.

Our results support the daily use of metformin to effectively prevent apoptosis in the EUS of SUI rats after 14 days of treatment, suggesting a potential antiapoptotic mechanism that underlies its therapeutic effect. Mechanical forces play a critical role in maintaining tissue homeostasis; however, excessive mechanical stretching—such as that experienced by the pelvic floor muscles during childbirth, chronic stress, or experimental vaginal distension—can disrupt the intracellular equilibrium and trigger apoptosis when the mechanical load exceeds cellular tolerance. 45 In this sense, the antiapoptotic effect of metformin observed in this study is consistent with previous findings in other mechanostress‐related models. For instance, Ge et al. 46 demonstrated that metformin exerts neuroprotective effects by inhibiting propofol‐induced apoptosis in mouse hippocampal neurons HT‐22 through the neuronal downregulation of caveolin‐1.

The effect of metformin on apoptosis appears to be highly context‐ and cell type‐dependent. Although our results and several studies highlight its antiapoptotic and tissue‐protective roles in nonmalignant tissues, other reports have shown that metformin can induce apoptosis and inhibit proliferation in different cancer cell types in a dose‐dependent manner. 47 This differential and contradictory effect may stem from the distinct metabolic and signaling landscapes of normal versus malignant cells, as well as the cellular microenvironment and the status of AMPK and mTOR pathways. 48 Despite these effects, such selectivity underscores the therapeutic versatility of metformin, enabling it to suppress unwanted cell death during tissue repair while promoting apoptosis in pathologically proliferating cells.

To further underscore the mechanism implicated in muscle repair, our in vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed that metformin exerts restorative effects via AMPK activation, leading to increased MYH1/2 expression and reduced apoptosis in EUS muscle cells. These results highlight the potential therapeutic value of targeting the AMPK‐MYH1/2 axis in the context of EUS skeletal muscle injury and dysfunctions associated with SUI. Although the precise pharmacokinetic distribution of metformin within pelvic floor tissues has not been fully characterized, metformin is well known to exert systemic metabolic effects and activate AMPK signaling across multiple skeletal muscle compartments following oral administration. 49 Given that the EUS is composed of striated skeletal muscle fibers, it is biologically plausible that metformin reaches and acts upon this tissue through systemic circulation. Indeed, we observed increased AMPK phosphorylation within the EUS following metformin treatment, providing molecular evidence of effective AMPK activation in this tissue. Collectively, our results expand the theoretical foundation for SUI treatment and support further research into the long‐term efficacy and safety of metformin for the clinical management of SUI.

Our findings highlight metformin as a promising candidate for drug repurposing in SUI therapy. Coupled with metformin's extensive clinical use and favorable safety profile, these preclinical data from animal models provide a foundation for initiating randomized controlled clinical trial for SUI therapy. However, whether metformin is effective for male SUI remains unknown. Male SUI is predominantly caused by iatrogenic intrinsic sphincter deficiency, most commonly following radical prostatectomy. 50 , 51 Based on the EUS‐restorative effects observed in our study, we speculate that metformin may play a role in male SUI, which needs further validation in male animal models and clinical studies. Importantly, our data further indicate AMPK signaling as a central regulator of EUS repair, consistent with prior evidence that adequate AMPK activity is essential for myogenic differentiation and muscle repair, whereas its impairment exacerbates muscle degeneration. 52 , 53 Thus, other agents targeting AMPK signaling represent promising therapeutic candidates for SUI.

Despite the unique and original observations of this study about the clear therapeutic effects of metformin on the functionality and morphology of the EUS, several limitations in this study should be acknowledged. First, the pathophysiology of SUI involves a complex interplay of pelvic floor muscles and nerve innervation. Our study focused primarily on the EUS and did not investigate potential changes in other components, such as the levator ani muscle or pudendal nerve. Second, regarding the mechanism, we focused on the canonical AMPK signaling pathway to establish a therapeutic rationale. Future studies utilizing high‐throughput sequencing (e.g., RNA‐seq) are needed to comprehensively elucidate the molecular mechanisms. Third, we employed a single, standard therapeutic dose to provide proof‐of‐concept evidence; a detailed dose–response analysis remains to be explored. Finally, our functional assessments were limited to classical urodynamic evaluations, and future studies should incorporate additional measures such as urinary continence behaviors (e.g., sneeze tests or pad tests in rats) to more comprehensively assess continence function.

CONCLUSION

We successfully established a SUI model using a double vaginal distension method and further demonstrated that metformin exerts significant therapeutic effects. Mechanistically, metformin seems to promote morphological and functional repair of the injured EUS and reduces apoptosis rate through the activation of the AMPK signaling pathway, leading to improved ALPP and BLPP in SUI rats. These findings highlight the translational potential of repurposing metformin as a therapeutic strategy for SUI.
