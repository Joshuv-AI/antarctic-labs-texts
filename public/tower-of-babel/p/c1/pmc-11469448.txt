Multifaceted roles of NADPH oxidases in the growth and pathogenicity of Beauveria bassiana
Authors: Shengan Zhu, Jing Wang, Yu Liu, Dan Jin, Xingyou Luo, Min Wan, Yanhua Fan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTReactive oxygen species (ROS), synthesized by the nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase (Nox) complex, are vital molecules in biological cells, influencing various physiological processes such as fungal growth, development, and virulence. Beauveria bassiana, an entomopathogenic fungus, is a promising biopesticide for agricultural, forestry, and urban pest control. This study focuses on the characterization of NADPH oxidases (Noxs) in B. bassiana. Gene expression profiles of Noxs in B. bassiana (BbNoxs) were analysed using RT-qPCR. Knockout strains of single BbNoxA, BbNoxB, BbNoxR, and double BbNoxA and BbNoxB were constructed via homologous recombination, and their phenotypic characteristics were examined. Fungal virulence was evaluated using Galleria mellonella larvae, and infection structures formation and penetration ability were assessed on cicada wings. ROS production and actin assembly during fungal growth and infection were detected using staining and marker methods. Expression analysis revealed significant upregulation of BbNoxs during fungal growth and infection. Compared to the wild-type strain, single knockouts (ΔBbNoxA/B/R) and double knockout (ΔBbNoxAB) of BbNoxs exhibited reduced conidial yields, accelerated conidial germination rates. Deletion of BbNoxB or BbNoxR decreased fungal virulence compared to the WT strain in topical inoculation experiments. Additionally, loss of BbNoxB or BbNoxR impaired infection structures formation, penetration ability, ROS production, and actin aggregation during fungal infection. BbNoxs are crucial for fungal growth, development, and virulence in B. bassiana, playing essential roles in infection structures formation, penetration, ROS production, and actin assembly. Understanding their functions provides insights into B. bassiana’s pathogenic mechanisms.

Introduction

ROS, encompassing singlet oxygen (1O2), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), superoxide anion (O2−), hydroxyl radical (•OH), emerge as byproducts from oxygen’s energy or electron transfer [1,2]. ROS play a pivotal role in the intricate balance of cellular processes within live cells [3,4]. With heightened chemical reactivity, they participate in diverse oxidative reactions arising from these processes [5–9]. Most ROS can act as signalling molecules or second messengers for intracellular communication when maintained at appropriate concentrations [10,11]. Nox is a transmembrane protein, capable of transferring electrons from NADPH, an important electron donor on one side of the membrane, to oxygen molecules on the other side. It catalyzes the generation of superoxide anions, serving as a significant source of ROS and the only enzyme directly responsible for ROS production in organisms [12–14]. Nox proteins alone have no catalytic activity and can exert catalytic function only when the Nox catalytic subunits and regulatory subunits combine to form a stable Nox complex [15].

The Nox family represents a group of evolutionarily conserved enzymes found across various eukaryotic organisms [16]. Nox was originally identified in mammalian phagocytes as crucial mediators of host defence mechanisms against microbial pathogens, the most well known is Nox2 (gp91phox) [17]. In the last decades, the new Nox isoforms have since been recognized in a wide range of organisms, from mammals to plants and fungi [18]. Differences were observed in the composition and the number of Noxs in different species. Thirteen plant Nox members, also named respiratory burst oxidase homologues (RBOHs), from RBOHA to RBOHL, and RBOHN had been identified and characterized so far [19–23]. In fungi, NoxA, NoxB and NoxC were identified. NoxA and NoxB closely resemble human gp91phox and have clear functions, whereas NoxC shares a common motif with human Nox5, yet its function remains elusive [10]. Despite the evolutionary divergence of eukaryotic organisms, the conservation of Nox proteins underscores their ancient origin and fundamental roles in cellular physiology and adaptation.

Fungal pathogens employ sophisticated strategies to breach host defences [24]. Appressoria are specialized infection structures that enable the penetration of host tissues, and their formation is finely orchestrated by a myriad of molecular signals, among which ROS emerge as crucial players [25]. Within filamentous fungi, ROS synthesis mediated by Nox complex plays a crucial role in both virulence and cellular differentiation. ROS production is essential for the development of appressoria in Magnaporthe grisea (Cooke) Saccardo (Magnaporthales: Pyriculariaceae) [26,27], as well as for reinforcing penetration pegs in Verticillium dahliae Klebahn (Glomerellales: Plectosphaerellaceae) [28] and Alternaria alternata (Fries) Keissler (Pleosporales: Pleosporaceae) [29]. In Pyricularia oryzae Cavara (Magnaporthales: Pyriculariaceae), disruption of ROS production using the diphenyleneiodonium (DPI) inhibitor results in compromised conidiospore germination and abnormal appressorial morphology, emphasizing ROS’s role in regulating polarized fungal growth through F-actin cytoskeleton remodelling [26]. The non-pathogenic phenotype observed in a ΔnoxB disruption mutant of A. alternata, characterized by reduced ROS production and an inability to penetrate the host cuticle [30], aligns with findings in Puccinia triticina Eriksson (Pucciniales: Pucciniaceae). In the latter, ROS production is crucial for establishing a dominant polarity axis in actively growing regions of the fungus [31]. ROS generated within the appressorium stimulate cytoskeletal remodelling by directly influencing proteins such as gelsolin, which participates in actin severing and rapid F-actin polymerization [31]. In M. grisea, the cooperative activity of Nox2 and its regulatory subunit NoxR is vital for the formation of the septin ring at the appressorium base [27].

Beauveria bassiana (Balsamo) Vuillemin (Hypocreales: Cordycipitaceae) is an entomopathogenic fungus and is known for its broad-spectrum insecticidal properties. It is used to control a variety of agricultural, forestry, and urban pests [32]. Upon fungal conidia being attached onto insect cuticle, it germinated to form infection structures, which is able to breach insect cuticle. In this work, we characterized three Nox genes in B. bassiana. Our results showed that three genes are involved in early conidiation and conidial germination. BbNoxB and BbNoxR play important roles in ROS production, actin assembly and infection structures formation during fungal infection process. These results highlight crucial contribution of BbNoxs in asexual development and pathogenicity of B. bassiana.

Materials and methods

Strains and culture conditions

The wild-type strain B. bassiana ARSEF 2860 (referred to as WT) was obtained from the Robert W. Holley Center for Agriculture and Health (Ithaca, NY, USA) and was grown in potato dextrose broth (PDB, agar-free) medium or on PDB agar medium (PDA, solidified with 1.5% (w/v) agar) at 26°C. Escherichia coli DH5α was cultured in Luria-Bertani medium (LB, agar-free) or on LB agar medium (LB, supplemented with 1.5% (w/v) agar) containing antibiotics based on plasmid resistance and used for plasmid manipulations. Agrobacterium tumefaciens AGL-1 was cultured in Yeast Extract Mannitol Broth medium (YEB, agar-free) or on YEB agar medium (YEB, supplemented with 1.5% (w/v) agar) containing antibiotics based on plasmid resistance and used for fungal transformations.

Expression profiles of BbNoxs

The well-studied Nox complex-related genes of Fusarium oxysporum Schlechtendal (Hypocreales: Nectriaceae), FoNoxA, FoNoxB, and FoNoxR, were utilized as queries to identify homologues in B. bassiana genome [33]. The relative expression of BbNoxs was analysed via RT-qPCR using template of synthesized cDNA from extracted RNA of B. bassiana inoculated on cicada wings or one-fourth SDA. Briefly, after inoculation on cicada wings for 12 h on PDA or SDA for 5, 10, 15 d, the total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent, and cDNA was synthesized using the HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+gDNA wiper) (Vazyme, R323–01) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RT-qPCR was performed using Bio-Rad CFX manager 3.0 Detection System with SYBR Green (Bio-Rad, USA), according to manufacturer’s instructions. The relative expression was calculated using the cycle threshold (Ct) 2−ΔCt method with normalization to B. bassiana 18S rRNA (BBA_07911) gene. Construction of BbNoxs gene deletion, complementation and lifeact-GFP strains

For targeted deletion of BbNoxs, both upstream and downstream (~2000 bp) of deletion regions for each gene were amplified by PCR using B. bassiana genomic DNA as the template and cloned, respectively, into EcoRI- and XbaI- digested pK2-sur vector through homologous recombination. Validated correct vector was transformed into A. tumefaciens AGL-1 for targeted gene disruption by homologous recombination as described [34]. For gene complementation, the sequence contained the ORF, promoter (~2 kb upstream of ATG) and terminator (~0.5 kb downstream of the stop codon) was amplified and cloned into pK2-bar digested with EcoRI. The correct recombinant plasmid was transformed into ΔBbNoxs mutants using A. tumefaciens-mediated transformation system as described above. To construct lifeact-GFP strain, the plasmid pK2-bar-lifeact-linker-GFP was constructed as follows. The short lifeact (17-aa small peptide MGVADLIKKFESISKEE, a F-actin marker) and GFP (including an N-terminal GDPPVAT linker) were amplified separately by PCR using the plasmid containing lifeact and GFP as the templates and cloned into EcoRI digested pK2-bar. The corrected recombinant plasmid was used to transform the WT and ΔBbNoxs mutant strains. Conidial yields and germination rate were assessed as described by Jinfeng Chen [35].

Insect bioassays

Fungal virulence was analysed by tropical infection and intrahemocoel injection. The mature conidia were harvested from 14 d-old culture on PDA with 0.05% Tween-80. The 6th instar larvae of Galleria mellonella (Linnaeus) (Lepidoptera: Pyralidae) were procured from Tianjin Huiyude Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Tianjin, China) and maintained in a 26°C incubator without any additional rearing, before being utilized to assess the virulence of fungal strains. For tropical infection, larvae were immersed in conidial suspension (15 ml of 1 × 107 conidia/ml) or equal volume of 0.05% Tween-80 (control group) for approximately 15 s. For intrahemocoel injection, conidial suspension (3 µl of 1 × 105 conidia/ml) was injected into larvae using the automated syringe pump. Each treatment was replicated three times, with 33 larvae per replicate. All treated larvae were transferred into 15-cm petri dishes and incubated at 25ºC. Mortality was monitored daily starting at 60 h post infection and 48 h post injection until all larvae had died. The LT50 (median lethal time) was calculated as a valid measure of virulence through survival curve analysis using the Kaplan – Meier model.

For observation of fungal growth, the fungi-killed larvae cadavers were transferred onto damp filter paper in new petri dishes at 25°C and monitored daily. To assess conidial production on cadavers, larvae cadavers 15 d post death were thoroughly vortexed in 0.05% Tween-80 for 5 min, and the conidia was counted by a haemocytometer.

Infection structures formation assay

To observe fungal infection structures formation, fungal conidia were inoculated into the cicada wings as follows. The cicada wings were sterilized in 6% H2O2 for 1 h and washed three times with sterilized water to remove the residual H2O2, and then left inside the clean bench until completely dry. The conidial suspensions (20 µl of 1 × 107 conidia/ml) were applied onto the cicada wings to induce infection structures formation. After incubation for 12 h at 26°C, the infection structures were microscopically observed. The experiment was repeated three times.

Cuticle penetration assay

Conidial suspensions (2 µl of 1 × 107 conidia/ml) were inoculated on sterile filter paper discs (Φ = 1.0 cm) placed on cicada wings (on a PDA plate). After inoculation for 3 d at 26°C, the PDA plates were incubated for five additional days after removing cicada wings, and the size of the fungal colonies was measured and photographed.

Hydrolytic enzyme activity assay

Proteases and lipase activity assay

Plates for protease and lipase activity detection were prepared according to the methods of Deb and Dutta [36] and Mustafa and Kaur [37], respectively. The conidial suspension (2 µl of 1 × 107 conidia/ml) were inoculated onto the centre of plates and incubated for 10 days at 26°C, and the size of hydrolysis zones produced by enzymes was measured and photographed.

Chitinase activity assay

The dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method was used to measure the chitinase activity as described by Miller [38] with some modifications. In brief, colloidal chitin (100 µl, 5% (w/v)) was mixed with crude enzyme (100 µl) and incubated for 1 h at 45°C. DNS reagent (200 µl) was added, and after heating to 100°C, absorbance was measured at 545 nm with a Varioskan™ LUX multimode microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). One unit of chitinase enzyme activity was established as the enzyme amount causing the liberation of 1 µg N-acetyl-D-glucosamine within an hour.

ROS detection

To quantify ROS in germinated conidia, fungal conidia were cultured in PDB for 12 h and resuspended in H2DCFDA solution (10 µM) or PBS (control) and then incubated in the dark at 28°C for 20 min. After being washed twice with PBS, the samples were transferred to an ELISA plate for fluorescence intensity measurement using a Varioskan™ LUX multimode microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), with excitation at 488 nm and emission measured at 525 nm.

For detection of ROS in fungal infection structures, fungal strains were inoculated on cicada wings for 12 h and then incubated with 0.5% (w/v) NBT solution in the dark at 26°C for 20 min. After the dyeing, the samples were washed twice with PBS and microscopically observed.

Actin observation

The conidial suspensions of all tested strains (1 × 107 conidia/ml) were, respectively, inoculated onto PDA plates or cicada wings. After incubation at 26°C for 12 h, the green fluorescence signals from fungal cells were observed using the confocal fluorescence microscope (Leica SP8, Leica Microsystems, Heidelberg, Germany), and the fluorescence intensity was measured using Image J software.

Statistical analysis

Graphical representation and statistical analysis of the data were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc). Differences between two groups were assessed using Student’s t test and indicated by asterisks (*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01). Differences among more than two groups were evaluated using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Significant differences between groups were denoted by different lowercase English letters (p < 0.05). Mean ± SD was used to represent the data in its entirety.

Results

Gene expression profiles of BbNoxs in B. bassiana

Three homologous genes of Nox in B. bassiana, BBA_07926, BBA_00765, and BBA_03772, were identified and designated as BbNoxA, BbNoxB, and BbNoxR, respectively. BbNoxA and BbNoxB consist of 557 and 576 amino acids, respectively, both featuring three domains: a transmembrane, a FAD_binding_8, and a NAD_binding_6. BbNoxR is a 490-amino acids protein with two TPR (tetratrico peptide repeat region) domains and a PB1 (phox and bem1) domain (Fig. S1A). Phylogenetic tree analysis revealed that the proteins BbNoxA and BbNoxB exhibited a close evolutionary relationship with homologs in Beauveria brongniartii RCEF 3172, rather than in other ascomycete fungi. Conversely, BbNoxR was closely related with homologues found in ascomycetous fungi (Fig. S1B-D).

The expression patterns of BbNoxs were analysed. To assess the potential role of BbNoxs in fungal infection, wild-type B. bassiana conidia were inoculated onto cicada wings to simulate penetration of insect cuticle. RT-qPCR results revealed that three BbNox genes exhibited significant upregulation at 12 h post inoculation on cicada wings compared to control treatment (on cellophane membrane) (Figure 1a). Additionally, when cultured on 1/4 SDA for 5–15 days, the expression of three BbNoxs genes exhibited an increase tendency with prolonged growth time (Figure 1b). BbNoxs are involved in fungal growth and development

BbNoxs knockout strains were constructed (Fig. S2-S3). The loss of BbNoxA/B/R led to a 35.88–58.26% reduction of conidial yields 5 days post inoculation. However, at 10th and 15th day, the conidial yields of three single gene deletion mutants were similar to that of the WT. For the double deletion mutant (ΔBbNoxAB), the conidial yields at 10th and 15th day were reduced by 47.9% and 41.9%, respectively, compared to the WT (Figure 2a). In the germination assay, all BbNoxs knockout strains (ΔBbNoxA, ΔBbNoxB, ΔBbNoxR, and ΔBbNoxAB) exhibited accelerated germination rate compared to the WT, with reductions of 28.04%, 26.12%, 17.60%, and 20.20% in half-germination times (GT50s), respectively (Figure 2b-f). BbNoxs contribute to fungal virulence

In the topical inoculation experiment, the virulence of ΔBbNoxB, ΔBbNoxR and ΔBbNoxAB was significantly decreased compared to the WT and complementary strains (Figure 3a-d). Deletion of BbNoxA has no significant effect on fungal virulence. The half-lethal times (LT50s) for ΔBbNoxB, ΔBbNoxR, and ΔBbNoxAB were 391.7 ± 124.8, 278.0 ± 56.5, and 360.4 ± 64.0 h, respectively, with 141.50%, 71.48%, and 122.19% increase compared to the WT (162.2 ± 16.6 h) (Figure 3e). However, the intrahemocoel injection experiment showed no difference in virulence between the mutants and the WT (Figure 3f-g). It was observed that there was sparse hyphal growth on cadavers killed by the mutants 8 d post-death, in stark contrast to the WT and complementary strains which produced a heavy layer of fungal growth on the cadavers (Figure 3h). BbNoxs required for infection structures formation and penetration

To further explore the possible mechanism involved in the compromised fungal virulence of mutants, infection structures formed on cicada wings were observed (Figure 4a). The results revealed a significant reduction in the infection structures formation of ΔBbNoxB, ΔBbNoxR, and ΔBbNoxAB mutants by 74.9%, 48.8%, and 73.4%, respectively, compared to the WT (Figure 4b). Additionally, the penetration ability of fungal strains against cicada wings were evaluated by observing the colony formed on PDA after it penetrated the wings. Successful penetration and removal of cicada wings led to the growth of WT and ΔBbNoxs mutants on PDA medium. However, the knockout mutants, with the exception of ΔBbNoxA, exhibited significant reductions in colony size by 50.0% compared to the WT (Figure 4c-d). Deletion of BbNoxs did not affect the activities of protease and lipase but significantly decreased chitinase activity by 36.2%, 22.4%, and 20.7% for ΔBbNoxA, ΔBbNoxB, and ΔBbNoxR respectively (Figure 4e, S4A-C). Discussion

ROS production regulated by Noxs is necessary for fungal developmental processes including mycelia growth, differentiation, conidiation, and infection structures formation of plant pathogenic fungi [39]. Our results indicated that BbNoxs of entomopathogenic fungus B. bassiana were associated with ROS production and regulated early conidiation and conidial germination. Among them, BbNoxB and BbNoxR play more important roles during infection process in ROS production and infection structures formation.

Our results showed the relative expression level of BbNoxB and BbNoxR was significantly increased when B. bassiana grown on cicada wings. Similar results were found for the nox2 in M. oryzae, which was upregulated in infection processes [40]. In addition, deletion of BbNoxB or BbNoxR decreased fungal virulence by topical infection, similar to the result yielded by double knockout of BbNoxA and BbNoxB. However, virulence of all the BbNoxs knockout mutant strains was similar to WT by intrahemocoel injection method. These results suggested that BbNoxB and BbNoxR were essential for penetration, and BbNoxA may be functionally redundant in fungal penetration of such barriers. In phytopathogenic fungi Colletotrichum gloeosporioides (Penzig) and Saccardo (Glomerellales: Glomerellaceae) and Colletotrichum scovillei Damm, P.F. Cannon & Crous (Glomerellales: Glomerellaceae), noxR or noxB (nox2) were necessary for fungal penetrating intact plant tissues such as fruits and leaves [41,42]. Deletion of nox2 or noxR in C. scovillei caused loss of fungal virulence when inoculated on intact pepper fruits [42]. These findings indicated that noxB (nox2) or noxR plays a key role in breaking host barriers to establish primary infection.

Appressoria formation and hyphal penetration are crucial steps for fungi to establish primary infection and enter the host internal tissues. Our results showed that loss of BbNoxB or BbNoxR caused obvious decrease in infection structures formation and penetration capacity. Furthermore, similar result was found in double knockout mutant ΔBbNoxAB. The finding was in accordance with our hypothesis that BbNoxA may be functionally redundant in infection structures formation. These results provided evidence for the roles of BbNoxB and BbNoxR in pathogenicity by regulation of appressorium formation and hyphal penetration. Our results were consistent with findings in C. gloeosporioide, in which pathogenicity of ΔnoxB, ΔnoxR and ΔnoxAnoxB are all decreased due to their defects in the penetration processes [41]. In addition, similar results were found in C. scovillei [42], nox2 or noxR affected fungal virulence by regulating appressorial development and penetration. These findings together indicated that the mechanism of noxB (nox2) or noxR regulating the infection processes may be similar in both phytopathogenic and entomopathogenic fungi.

Producted ROS via Nox complex alter the redox balance inside the cells, thereby mediating cell signal transduction and regulating various cellular functions [43–45]. In C. gloeosporioides, noxB and noxR were associated with ROS production in germinated conidia, inactivation any of them weakened ROS generation of hyphal tips [41]. Our results indicated deletion of BbNoxA resulted in slight decrease of ROS in mature or germinated conidia, however no significant change was observed when cultured on cicada wings. Deletion of BbNoxB or BbNoxR caused significant decrease of ROS in germinated conidia and when grow on cicada wings, however, no significant change in mature conidia. We hypothesized that the dormancy state of mature conidia required only low level of ROS. However, upon encountering nutrient substrates, such as artificial culture media and insect tissues, fungal conidia begin growth or invasive cycle. At this juncture, fungal cells need a relative high ROS level to activate downstream signalling pathways and subsequently modulating vital life processes. Therefore, BbNoxB and BbNoxR are postulated to play pivotal roles in regulating ROS levels throughout this intricate process.

The dynamic interplay between F-actin depolymerization and polymerization is particularly critical for the life cycle of filamentous fungi [46–48]. As these fungi navigate their environment, encountering diverse challenges and host tissues, the modulation of F-actin dynamics becomes a central orchestrator of their growth and pathogenicity. We found that significant decreased F-actin polymerization in ΔBbNoxB and ΔBbNoxR compared with the WT and ΔBbNoxA. This maybe resulted from the decrease of ROS level in both ΔBbNoxB and ΔBbNoxR mutants. Roughly similar results were found in C. gloeosporioides, F-actin showed a diffused distribution in polarized mycelia and appressoria when loss of noxB or noxR [39]. In M. oryzae, nox2 and noxR were essential for assembly of a toroidal F-actin network at the point of penetration peg emergence [26]. These findings revealed that NoxB (Nox2) and NoxR play a key role in F-actin organization, which is important for fungal growth and infection.

Taken together, we conclude that BbNoxB and BbNoxR regulate the ROS production of germinated conidia and are necessary for F-actin organization in B. bassiana. Fungal growth and development were affected by ROS by altering cellular redox homoeostasis. Our study extends the understanding of the molecular mechanism of Noxs regulating the growth and pathogenicity of entomopathogenic fungi.
