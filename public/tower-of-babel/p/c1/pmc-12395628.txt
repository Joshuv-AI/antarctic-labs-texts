A comparative study of capacitation-mediated changes in whole mouse sperm proteome
Authors: Jae Yeon Hwang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMammalian spermatozoa acquire fertilizing ability in response to environmental factors enriched in the female reproductive tract, a process called capacitation. During capacitation, sperm undergo physiological changes that are accompanied by functional regulation of sperm proteins. However, the mechanism by which capacitation orchestrates sperm protein functions to modulate physiological characteristics remains unclear. Here, I analyzed capacitation-mediated global proteomic changes in mouse spermatozoa to unravel the underlying molecular association with the biological processes in sperm capacitation. I quantitatively compared 4,587 proteins identified by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. Among them, the amounts of 47 and 180 proteins were reduced to over 1.5-fold (p < 0.05) and 1.3-fold (p < 0.1), respectively, and those of 11 and 60 proteins were increased over 1.5-fold (p < 0.05) and 1.3-fold (p < 0.1), respectively, in capacitated mouse sperm. Functional annotation of proteins reduced in capacitated sperm revealed that these proteins could be associated with lipid metabolism, RNA processing, and remodeling of the nuclear envelope structure. This result suggests that reactive oxygen species might be more generated for cholesterol efflux and the nucleus might undergo decondensation to form pronucleus in sperm during capacitation. In addition, functional annotation of proteins of which levels are increased in capacitated sperm represents that they could be involved in sperm structure. This study expands the molecular contribution to modulation of sperm functions and provides new insights into potential biological processes involving regulatory molecular machinery in capacitated sperm.

Introduction

In mammals, spermatozoa are ejaculated into the female reproductive tract and migrate toward the fertilization site to fertilize the oocytes (Hwang and Chung 2023). In the female reproductive tract, mammalian sperm gradually acquire fertilizing ability through a process called capacitation (Chang 1951). Capacitated spermatozoa undergo physiological changes, such as hyperactivated motility and acrosome reaction (Austin and Bishop 1958; Yanagimachi 1970), which are essential for normal sperm fertility. Although the importance of capacitation has been highlighted over the last half-century, how capacitation induces global molecular changes to modulate physiological functions in mature sperm is still not well understood.

Mammalian spermatozoa undergo several molecular and cellular events during capacitation in response to environmental factors in the female reproductive tract. For example, high levels of albumin induce cholesterol efflux from the sperm plasma membrane, altering membrane fluidity during capacitation (Travis and Kopf 2002). In addition, bicarbonates initiate the protein kinase A (PKA) signaling pathway, which potentiates global tyrosine phosphorylation (Visconti et al. 1995; Visconti et al. 1995). Simultaneously, intracellular alkalinization activates a sperm-specific ion channel, cation channel of sperm (CatSper), to initiate Ca2+ signaling, which is essential for sperm hyperactivation (Ren et al. 2001; Kirichok et al. 2006). In addition, capacitation-mediated Ca2+ signaling triggers acrosomal exocytosis (Fraser 1998; Ded et al. 2020). These molecular changes have been well-characterized and broadly applied to evaluate successful sperm capacitation. However, how molecular changes in individual proteins contribute to sperm function during capacitation remains largely unknown.

Spermatozoa are currently known to have limited ability for transcription and translation (Miller et al. 2005; Zhao et al. 2006). Thus, sperm could rely on post-translational modifications (PTMs) or degradation of proteins (Baker 2016) to regulate their physiological functions during capacitation. Previous studies have reported that CATSPER1, a pore subunit of the CatSper channel, undergoes protein degradation during capacitation (Chung et al. 2014; Ded et al. 2020), which is negatively associated with successful sperm migration in the female reproductive tract (Ded et al. 2020). These results highlight that the degeneration of certain proteins is highly associated with sperm function for successful fertilization. Which sperm proteins, however, undergo degradation and how the protein degeneration is associated with biological processes in capacitated sperm have not been well studied.

Here, I analyzed global changes in the sperm proteome during capacitation. Using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), I compared whole sperm proteomes before and after inducing capacitation. The whole proteomic analyses identified 4,587 proteins in total, of which over 100 proteins were significantly downregulated in mouse sperm during capacitation. Functional annotation revealed that these proteins contribute to lipid metabolism, RNA splicing, nuclear envelope remodeling, which expands our knowledge of the molecular contribution to physiological changes in mouse sperm during capacitation.

Results

Capacitation alters whole proteome profiling in mouse spermatozoa

To understand capacitation-associated global protein changes in sperm, I quantitatively compared the whole sperm proteome before and after inducing in vitro capacitation (Figure 1; Supplementary Table S1). In vitro capacitation in this study successfully induces protein tyrosine phosphorylation (pTyr; Figure 1A and B) and acrosome reaction (Figure 1B and C) in mouse sperm. A total of 4,587 proteins were identified from LC-MS/MS analysis. In capacitated sperm, ∼1% (n = 47) and ∼3.8% (n = 180) of total proteins were reduced by over 1.5 – (p < 0.05) and 1.3-fold (p < 0.10), respectively (Figure 1B; Supplementary Table 1). The relative abundances of 11 (∼0.2% of all proteins, p < 0.05) and 60 (∼1.3% of all proteins, p < 0.10) proteins were increased by over 1.5 – and 1.3-fold in capacitated sperm, respectively (Figure 1B; Supplementary Table 1). Considering the marginal de novo protein synthesis in mature sperm and the normalization methods applied here, the proteins with increased fold changes in capacitated sperm could be degenerated barely than the other proteins, which remains the proteins to relatively higher abundances in capacitated sperm. Sperm capacitation reduces proteins to regulate lipid metabolism and RNA processing

To understand how capacitation-mediated protein reduction contributes to sperm biological processes, I subjected the proteins that had over 1.5-fold reduction (n = 47; p < 0.05) in capacitated sperm to functional annotation (Figure 2; Supplementary Figure S1; Supplementary Table 2). Functional annotation revealed that 66 gene ontologies were significantly enriched (Figure 2A). Interestingly, multiple ontologies could be categorized as lipid metabolism – and RNA processing-related (Figure 2B–D; Supplementary Figure S1). Ten (38.46%) and two (13.33%) ontologies were related to lipid metabolism among the significantly enriched biological process (BP, n = 26) and molecular function (MF, n = 15) ontologies, respectively (Figure 2D). In particular, ontologies for the negative regulation of very-low-density lipoprotein particle clearance (GO:0010916; log2(fold enrichment, FE) = 8.05), chylomicron remnant clearance (GO:0034382; log2FE = 7.56), and phospholipid efflux (GO:0033700; log2FE = 6.46) in BP and lipase inhibitor activity (GO:0055102; log2FE = 7.56) in MF were significantly enriched. This result suggests that lipids and lipoprotein vesicle metabolisms could be altered in capacitated sperms. In addition, 24% (n = 6) and 20% (n = 3) of the enriched BP and MF ontologies, such as the positive regulation of mRNA splicing, via spliceosome (GO:0048026; log2FE = 6.01) and U1 snRNP binding (GO:1990446, log2FE = 7.05), were related to RNA processing (Figure 2D). This result indicates that capacitation reduces protein levels to manage the mRNA splicing process in sperm. Enriched cellular component (CC) ontologies including methylosome (GO:0034709, log2FE = 6.51), U1 snRNP (GO:0005685; og2FE = 6.06), and U2 snRNP (GO0005686; log2FE = 5.92) also support the possibility that mRNA splicing process could be altered in capacitated sperm (Supplementary Figure S1).

Next, I examined each reduced protein included in the enriched ontologies to further understand how the individual proteins are associated with each other. Each of the eight proteins was included in the enriched BP and MF ontologies, which were categorized as lipid metabolism and RNA processing (Figure 3A and B). Two lipoproteins APOC2 and APOC3, and glutathione proteins including GSTA3 and GSTM7 were included in the ontologies for lipid metabolism. Spliceosome subunits (such as SF3B6 and SNRPA) and regulatory factors for splicing (ALYREF and RBMXL1) were included in the ontologies for RNA processing. The detected proteins are not exclusively expressed in the testis (Figure 3B), suggesting that lipid metabolism and RNA processing, which is not specific in testis, may be altered in capacitated sperm. Clustering analysis of the target proteins further supported the functional association of these proteins (Figure 3C and D). Notably, several glutathione transferases were reduced in capacitated sperm (Figure 3C), which might be associated with increased levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in capacitated sperm to cause oxidative stress (Aitken et al. 2022). Proteins to manage nucleus architecture are reduced in capacitated sperm

To further understand how capacitation-mediated proteomic changes affect biological events in mature sperm, I subjected proteins of which amounts are relatively smaller reduced (fold change, FC > 1.3; p < 0.1) to functional annotation. Functional annotation represented 68 BP, 56 CC, and 32 MF ontologies were significantly enriched (Figure 4A). Like the former functional annotation (Figure 2), multiple ontologies related to lipid metabolism and RNA processing were significantly enriched (Supplementary Figures S2 and S3). Interestingly, five BP (7.35%) and three MF (9.38%) ontologies were related to the nuclear membrane (Figure 4B–E). In particular, BP ontologies for nuclear pore localization (GO:0051664, log2FE = 6.11) and nuclear envelope organization (GO:0006998, log2FE = 5.11) and MF ontologies for structural constituent of nuclear lamina (GO:0160123, log2FE = 7.38) were significantly enriched, indicating that the nuclear envelope architecture could be remodeled during capacitation. Enriched CC ontologies including lamin filament (GO:0005638, log2FE = 5.79) and nuclear lamina (GO:0042382, log2FE = 5.15), also support the possible alteration of nuclear membrane structure in capacitated sperm (Supplementary Figure S3). Next, I performed gene expression and interaction analyses of the proteins included in the enriched ontologies related to the nuclear membrane architecture (Figure 5). Nineteen proteins were included in the nuclear membrane-related BP and MF ontologies (Figure 5A–C). Especially nuclear lamina proteins (LMNA and LMNB1), heterochromatin proteins (CBX1 and CBX5), and pre-RNA processing proteins (RBMXL1, NUDT21, and HNRNPD) were present in the enriched ontologies (Figure 5A). Tissue expression profiling demonstrates that the genes encoding the target proteins are not exclusively expressed in the testis, suggesting that their testis-specific functions could be marginal (Figure 5C). Interaction analysis demonstrated that the target proteins are clustered in the nuclear lamina and paraspeckle structures (Figure 5D), supporting the expected remodeling of the nuclear membrane and heterochromatin status in capacitated sperm. Sperm structural proteins are relatively more preserved during capacitation

Our proteomic comparison revealed that relative abundances of ∼0.2% (n = 11) and ∼1.3% (n = 60) proteins were increased by over 1.5-fold (p < 0.05) and 1.3-fold (p < 0.1), respectively, in capacitated sperm (n = 60; Figure 1E), indicating these proteins could be relatively preserved well during capacitation. As 11 proteins that increased by over 1.5-fold in capacitated sperm were not annotated with enriched gene ontologies, we performed functional annotation with the proteins of which relative abundance is increased by over 1.3-fold (p < 0.1, n = 60; Figure 1E). Functional annotation of the proteins revealed that six BP, eight CC, and three MF ontologies were significantly enriched (Figure 6A; Supplementary Table 2). Notably, half of the enriched CC ontologies, including sperm head–tail coupling apparatus (GO:0120212; log2FC = 5.76) and sperm flagellum (GO:0036126; log2FC = 3.15), were associated with sperm tail structure (Figure 6B). Twelve proteins were included in the sperm tail-related ontologies and, especially, genes encoding DNAI1, DNAL4, and DNAJB13 express exclusively in the testis (Figure 6C and D). Interaction analysis of these 12 proteins revealed that dynein proteins (DYNLT1B, DNAI1, and DNAL4) and connecting piece proteins (DNAJB13 and PMFBP1) clustered together (Figure 6E). This clustering pattern suggests that proteins of the axoneme and connecting piece could be more stable with marginal degradation during capacitation. Discussion

Capacitated sperm develop hyperactivated motility for successful navigation to the fertilization site (Hwang and Chung 2023) and undergo acrosomal reaction, which is indispensable for sperm-egg fusion (Miyata et al. 2024). These physiological changes have been expected to be strongly associated with functional changes in sperm proteins during capacitation. Considering the limited transcription and translation processes in mature sperm, one possible way to modulate the molecular functions of sperm proteins would be to change their PTM during capacitation. Activated FER tyrosine kinase potentiates global tyrosine phosphorylation of sperm proteins during capacitation (Alvau et al. 2016). In addition, the presence of other types of PTMs in sperm proteins, such as acetylation, glycosylation, and ubiquitination (Baker 2016), suggests that PTM patterns could be altered during capacitation to modulate sperm protein function. A recent study demonstrated that capacitated bull sperm undergo changes in oxidative PTMs, S-nitrosylation, and S-glutathionylation of several proteins (Mostek-Majewska et al. 2023), supporting the potential role of PTMs in modulating sperm capacitation in mammals.

Other ways to regulate protein function during capacitation would be the degradation or truncation of sperm proteins. Recent studies have examined proteomic changes during capacitated in mouse (Skerrett-Byrne et al. 2022), humans (Castillo et al. 2019; Chhikara et al. 2023), bulls (Chen et al. 2022), pigs (Zigo et al. 2024), and ram (Chen et al. 2024) sperm. In a mouse sperm proteom study, Skerrett-Byrne et al., revealed that ∼1% and ∼3.5% of proteins were changed over 2 – and 1.3-fold, respectively, (Skerrett-Byrne et al. 2022) which is comparable to this study. Although the list of individual proteins eiher up-regulated or down-regulated proteins in capacitated sperm is quite different, proteins related to RNA processing (SF3B1, HNRNPA3, HNRNPK, and HNRNPD), chromatin compaction (PRM2), redox process (GSTA3) were down regulated in capacitated sperm (Skerrett-Byrne et al. 2022) as similar to this study. Interestingly, in human, proteins associated with RNA processing (HNRNPK, FAM71B, and THO5) and redox process (GSTM3) are also reduced in capacitated sperm (Castillo et al. 2019; Chhikara et al. 2023). In addition, levels of axonemal proteins, RSPH1 and TUBA1A, are increased in human sperm after capacitation. The shared protein level changes suggests molecular regulation underlying RNA processing, antioxidation, and axonemal function could be conserved in human and mouse sperm during capaciation. On the other hands, levels of peri-axonemal proteins, such as AKAP3, ODF2, and AKAP4, are reduced in capacitated human sperm (Castillo et al. 2019), which is not observed in mouse sperm capaciatiton (Skerrett-Byrne et al. 2022). The difference suggests that proteomic alteration and resulted biological processes could be species-specific in sperm capaciation. In this study, quantitative comparison of the whole sperm proteome also revealed that a larger number of proteins were significantly reduced rather than increased (Figure 1), indicating that degeneration of sperm proteins could be active during capacitation. A recent proteomic study demonstrated that only a small number of proteins undergo proteasome-mediated degradation in capacitated boar sperm (Zigo et al. 2024), suggesting that various proteases may modulate protein degradation in mammalian sperm capacitation. However, how capacitation-mediated protein reduction affects physiological changes in sperm remains to be further understood.

For successful capacitation, Spermatozoa undergo cholesterol efflux, which could modulate transmembrane cell signaling by increasing membrane fluidity (Travis and Kopf 2002). Notably, cholesterol in the sperm plasma membrane should be oxidized by ROSs to bind successfully to its acceptors, such as albumin, during capacitation (Aitken and Nixon 2013). The oxidation by ROSs increases the hydrophilicity of cholesterol, enhancing its binding to albumin (Brouwers et al. 2011). ROS levels increase in capacitated sperm, which are mainly generated from the electron transport chain in the mitochondria (Aitken 2017). Notably, our proteomic analysis demonstrated that proteins that regulate redox processes, including glutathione S-transferases (GSTs; GSTA3, GSTM7, and GSTP1), were reduced in capacitated sperm (Figures 2 and 3). Considering the physiological importance of ROSs, the reduced abundance of GSTs may contribute to increased ROS levels in sperm during capacitation. However, as excessive ROSs could cause lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial damage, resulting in cell death (Wang et al. 2023), whether reduced GST levels indicate either increased ROS levels for successful capacitation or induced cell death for sperm selection needs to be further clarified.

The sperm head carries a haploid paternal genome with an extremely condensed structure by wrapping DNA into a male germ cell-specific nuclear protein called protamine (Okada and Yamaguchi 2017). Once a capacitated sperm successfully enters the oocyte, the sperm nucleus is decondensed and transiently forms a pronucleus transiently, which fuses with the maternal pronucleus to generate a zygotic nucleus (McLay and Clarke 2003). In this process, sperm nuclear envelopes are removed and reorganized by maternal nuclear lamina proteins. Simultaneously, protamine is replaced with histones, which changes epigenetic status of the paternal genome prior to fusion with the maternal pronuclei (Okada and Yamaguchi 2017). These dynamic changes in nuclear structure and chromatin status are crucial for the successful completion of fertilization (McLay and Clarke 2003; Johnson et al. 2011). Interestingly, our proteomic analyses demonstrated that nuclear lamina proteins (LMNA and LMNB1) as well as chromatin remodeling proteins (CBX5, CBX1, and ADNP) were weakly reduced in capacitated sperm (Figures 4 and 5). LMNA and LMNB1 form intermediate filaments that control the integrity of nuclear architecture (Wilson and Foisner 2010). This nuclear cytoskeletal structure interacts with DNA via heterochromatin proteins, including CBX5, to modulate 3D genome organization and function (Wong et al. 2022). Thus, the reduced nuclear lamina proteins and heterochromatin proteins elucidate that sperm could start to decondense their heads for pronucleus formation by remodeling nuclear and chromatin structures during capacitation. A few studies have also demonstrated that sperm chromatin becomes less compact in human and ram sperm after capacitation, without DNA damage (De Lamirande et al. 2012; Peris-Frau et al. 2021). In addition, a previous mouse sperm proteome study reported that the abundance of PRM2, which condenses DNA extremely in sperm head, decreases levels of during capacitated (Skerrett-Byrne et al. 2022). These studies also support the possibility that capacitated sperm initiate to decondense their nuclei to prepare for pronucleus formation after fertilization.

In this study, I analyzed the proteomic changes in mouse sperm during capacitation. This study contributes to understanding the underlying molecular association with the biological processes involved in sperm capacitation. More detailed studies to clarify the molecular processes predicted by the downregulated proteins in sperm will help expand our understanding and contribute to resolving fertilization issues in both society and industry (Hwang 2024; Lee and Hwang 2024).

Materials and methods

Animals

Wild-type C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Charles River Laboratory (Wilmington, MA, USA) and Samtako Bio-Korea Inc. (Osan, Republic of Korea). The mice were managed according to the guidelines approved by the Institute of Animal Care and Use Committee of Yale University (#20079) and Pusan National University (#PNU-2023-0386).

Mouse sperm preparation

Mouse sperm were collected from the caudal region of the epididymis of adult mice, as previously described (Hwang et al. 2022; Hwang et al. 2023). The epididymis was collected from euthanized male mice and cut into 2–3 pieces in M2 medium (EMD Millipore, Burlington, MA) to allow the sperm to swim out. To induce capacitation, collected sperm were incubated in human tubular fluid (HTF; EMD Millipore) at 3.0 × 106 cells/mL concentration for 90 min at 37°C, 5% CO2 incubation as a previous study (Hwang et al. 2022).

Antibodies and reagents

Mouse monoclonal anti-pTyr (clone 4G10, #96215), anti-acetylated tubulin (clone 6-11B-1, T6793), and anti-LMNB1 (clone 3C10G12, 66095-1-Ig) were from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA), Sigma Aldrich (St Louis, MO, USA), and Proteintech (Rosemont, IL, USA), respectively. Goat anti-mouse IgG conjugated with Alexa 568 (A-11004), wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) conjugated with Alexa 647 (W32466), and Hoechst (H1398) were from ThermoFisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (115-035-003) was from Jackson ImmunoResearch (West Grove, PA, USA).

Sperm immunoblotting

Sperm immunoblotting was performed as similar to previous study (Hwang et al. 2022). Briefly, 1.0 × 106 cells of sperm were lysed with 30 μl of 2X lithium dodecyl sulfate (LDS) for 10 min with vortexing at room temperature followed by centrifugation at 18,000 x g, 4°C for 10 min. Supernatant was mixed to 50 mM dithiothreitol and denatured by boiling at 75°C for 10 min. Denatured protein samples were subjected to SDS-PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membrane (BioRad, CA, USA) followed by blocking using 5% skim milk in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) supplemented with 0.05% Tween 20 (TBS-T; Duksan, Ansan, Republic of Korea) for an hour at room temperature. Blocked membranes were incubated with primary at 4°C overnight. Mouse monoclonal anti-pTyr (1:2,000), anti-LMNB1 (1:500), and anti-AcTub (1:20,000) were used for primary antibody. The membranes were washed with TBS-T and incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG for an hour at room temperature. After washing with TBS-T, membranes were developed using SuperSignal West Dura ECL solution (Thermo).

Sperm fluorescence imaging

Sperm fluorescence imaging was performed as previously (Ref). Collected sperm were washed with PBS and attached on the glass coverslip by centrifugation at 700 x g for 5 min followed by fixation using 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 10 min at room temperature. Fixed coverslips were washed with PBS and permeabilized using 1% Triton-X100 in PBS for 10 min at room temperature. Permeabilized coverslips were washed with PBS and blocked using PBS containing 10% normal goat serum (Sigma Aldrich) for 1 h at room temperature. The coverslips were incubated with mouse anti-pTyr (1:200) in blocking buffer at 4°C overnight. The coverslips were washed with PBS and incubated with Alexa 568-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (1:1,000) for an hour at room temperature. Immunostained coverslips were washed with PBS. Either immunostained or permeabilized coverslips were incubated with WGA conjugated with Alexa 647 (1:1,000) and Hoechst dye (5 μg/ml) for 20 min at room temperature to stain acrosome and DNA, respectively. The coverslips were washed with PBS followed by mounting on glass slide using Vectasheild (Vector Laboratory). Fluorescence-stained coverslips were imaged using Zeiss LSM900 with Plan-Apochromat 63x/1.4 and C-Apochromat 40x/1.2 objectives (Carl Zeiss).

Sperm protein extraction for LC-MS/MS analysis

The collected non-capacitated and capacitated sperm cells were lysed to extract the whole proteome for LC-MS/MS, as previously described (Hwang et al. 2019), with modifications. Briefly, sperm from five male mice were pooled and each half was prepared for non-capacitated sperm and capacitated sperm per each replication. Prepared sperm samples were washed with PBS and lysed with 8M urea buffer with brief sonication followed by gently rocking at 4 °C for overnight. Lysates were centrifuged at 18,000 x g, 4°C for 1 h. Supernatants were mixed with trichloroacetic acid and incubated overnight at 4°C to precipitate the extracted sperm proteins. The precipitates were washed with acetone and resuspended in 8M urea. The collected sperm proteins were analyzed by LC-MS/MS. Sperm lysates from three sets of non-capacitated and capacitated groups were subjected for LC-MS/MS analysis.

Liquid-chromatography tandem mass spectrometry

Protein samples were reduced, alkylated, and then trypsin treated for 16 h at 37°C. The prepared peptide samples were purified with a SOLA HRP 96 well plate C18 cartridge (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), reconstituted in 50 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate, and labeled with a Tandem Mass Tags (TMT) reagent. The labeled peptides were pooled and fractionated using an Ultimate 3000 HPLC (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with an ACQUITY UPLC Peptide CSHTM C18 column (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA). The fractionated peptides were reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid and analyzed using Orbitrap Q Exactive HF-X (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled with Vanquish Neo UPLC (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A trap column (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and an analytical column (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used to separate the peptides.

Database search and data processing

The LC-MS/MS raw files were processed using the COMET search engine (Eng et al. 2013). The mouse protein database was downloaded from UniProt (UP000000589; https://www.uniprot.org/proteomes/UP000000589). Search conditions were limited to fully-tryptic peptides with a length of 7–50 amino acids, and allowing up to two missed cleavages. Cysteine alkylation (+57.0214 Da), methionine oxidation (+15.9949 Da), and N termini, lysine TMT (+229.163 Da) were considered as peptide modifications. False discovery rate was applied at 1% each at the spectrum, peptide, and protein levels. Protein levels were normalized by quantile normalization. Differentially expressed proteins were defined as those with fold changes greater than 1.3 fold (p < 0.1). Among them, proteins of which levels are altered over 1.5-fold with p < 0.05 and over 1.3-fold with p < 0.01 were considered for largely and mildly altered proteins, respectively, during sperm capacitation.

Functional annotation

Functional annotation of the identified target proteins was performed as described previously (Hwang et al. 2022; Kim and Hwang 2024). Briefly, lists of proteins that were significantly reduced or increased in capacitated sperm were compared using the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID; https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) (Huang et al. 2009; Sherman et al. 2022) for functional annotation. Gene ontologies with a fold enrichment greater than two-fold (p < 0.05) were considered significant. The significantly enriched gene ontologies in each term (BP, CC, and MF) were categorized using REVIGO (http://revigo.irb.hr) (Supek et al. 2011) and visualized in semantic spaces using R software.

Tissue expression profiling

The tissue expression levels of genes encoding proteins included in the significantly enriched gene ontologies were obtained from the ENCODE project in the NCBI Gene database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gene). The enriched expression levels of the target genes in the testes were calculated by normalizing testicular expression levels to the sum of the expression levels in either entire adult tissues or adult and embryonic tissues.

Interaction analysis

The physical and functional networks of the proteins detected from the grouped ontologies were examined using STRING (https://string-db.org/) (Szklarczyk et al. 2023). Clustering of target proteins was performed using the k-means clustering module incorporated into STRING.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the permutation-based Student’s t-test to compare normalized protein levels acquired from LC-MS/MS analysis. Statistical comparison of acrosome reaction rate between non-capacitated and capacitated sperm was carried out using Student’s t-test. Fisher’s exact test was used to examine the functional enrichment of gene ontologies.
