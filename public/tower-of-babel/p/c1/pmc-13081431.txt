Comparative performance of large language models for patient-oriented support in dental trauma emergencies
Authors: Emre Sözen, Hasan Akpınar, Sevda Yaman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDental trauma represents a common emergency condition requiring rapid and accurate guidance to prevent permanent damage. In urgent scenarios where access to dental professionals may be delayed, large language models (LLMs) have the potential to provide patients with timely and relevant information. The aim of this study was to comparatively evaluate the dentist rated performance of five widely used LLMs in answering frequently asked questions related to dental trauma emergencies across three predefined components: accuracy, comprehensiveness, and clinical applicability.MethodsBased on the guidelines of the International Association of Dental Traumatology (IADT), the ToothSOS application, and frequently asked patient questions, 27 open-ended questions in Turkish were prepared and divided into five clinical subcategories: avulsion, post-replantation care, luxation, fractures, and other traumas. The questions were posed to the models ChatGPT-4o, Claude 3.5, DeepSeek, Microsoft Copilot, and Gemini 2.0 Flash. Twenty experienced dentists evaluated the responses using a 5-point Likert scale, with the evaluators jointly considering three predefined components: accuracy, comprehensiveness, and clinical applicability. A total of 2,700 individual ratings were analyzed using the Friedman test, Bonferroni-corrected Wilcoxon test, and Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC).ResultsA significant difference in overall performance was observed among the models (p < 0.001). ChatGPT-4o achieved the highest mean score (4.63 ± 0.57), whereas Gemini received the lowest score (3.87 ± 0.91). Claude, DeepSeek, and Copilot demonstrated similar, moderate performance, ranging approximately between 4.1 and 4.3. Median values were 5 (IQR 4–5) for ChatGPT-4o and Claude and 4 (IQR 3–5) for the other models, indicating that the responses of ChatGPT-4o and Claude were rated more consistently high. Within each model, no statistically significant differences were observed in mean Likert scores across clinical subcategories (p > 0.05).ConclusionsAlthough LLMs may not be able to replace professional clinical examination, they may serve as a rapid supportive source of patient-oriented information in dental trauma emergencies where access to a dentist is limited. However, model dependent differences, highlight the need for regular verification before patient facing use.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08037-8.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) technologies have shown rapid advancement in dentistry, particularly in diagnostic imaging, disease detection, and treatment planning [1]. Recent developments in natural language processing have enabled large language models (LLMs) to generate real-time, patient-oriented responses, facilitating access to health information and supporting patient education and clinical decision support [2, 3].

LLMs have increasingly been explored as patient facing informational tools, particularly for addressing frequently asked questions posed by children and their caregivers regarding oral health, preventive practices, and common clinical procedures [2]. Their ability to generate accessible, non-technical explanations has positioned LLMs as potential adjuncts for patient education and communication in routine dental contexts [4].

Despite their potential benefits, LLMs present important limitations in healthcare applications [5]. One major concern is the phenomenon of hallucination, in which models may generate inaccurate or fabricated information [6]. In addition, most LLMs are trained on datasets with a temporal cutoff, limiting their ability to reflect the most current clinical guidelines and evidence. These limitations are particularly critical in time sensitive conditions such as dental trauma, where inaccurate or outdated guidance may negatively affect prognosis, underscoring the need for cautious and well validated use in clinical contexts [7, 8].

Accordingly, the use of LLMs in patient facing clinical contexts requires appropriate regulatory oversight and ongoing validation, supported by safety oriented mechanisms such as auditability, traceability, and predefined supervision triggers. These safeguards are particularly important in emergency scenarios, where uncertainty and time pressure may amplify the risks associated with unverified or context insensitive outputs [9].

Several widely used LLMs, including ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Microsoft Copilot, and DeepSeek, are increasingly accessed by the public for health related inquiries. These models differ in training strategies, update frequencies, and safety oriented design features, which may influence the quality and clinical relevance of the information they provide [10–13].

Alongside these general purpose systems, domain-specific digital tools developed for dental traumatology have also emerged, designed to address trauma related queries based on established clinical guidelines such as those of the International Association of Dental Traumatology (IADT) [14].

Dental trauma represents a common global health problem, affecting approximately one in five individuals during their lifetime [15]. Such injuries frequently occur in children and adolescents and may result in significant functional, aesthetic, and psychosocial consequences. Because dental trauma often occurs outside clinical settings, patients and caregivers commonly seek immediate guidance to understand injury severity, initial management steps, and potential treatment options [16, 17]. Recent comparative research has evaluated advanced chatbot based systems for traumatic dental injury questions, particularly in the primary dentition, using the 2020 IADT guidelines, and has reported model dependent differences in accuracy, completeness, and readability of responses [18].

LLMs hold substantial potential for delivering patient oriented information in dental trauma emergencies, where timely and accurate guidance may help reduce anxiety and support informed decision making [19, 20]. However, despite growing interest in LLM based medical applications, there remains a lack of systematic, dentist rated comparative evaluations assessing their performance in providing patient-oriented dental trauma information in non-English languages, including Turkish.

Accordingly, this study aims to comparatively evaluate the performance of five LLMs ChatGPT (GPT-4o, OpenAI), Claude (Claude 3.5, Anthropic), DeepSeek (DeepSeek AI), Microsoft Copilot (publicly accessible web version, Microsoft), and Gemini (Gemini 2.0 Flash, Google) as rated by experienced dentists, using a holistic performance framework encompassing accuracy, comprehensiveness, and clinical applicability.

The null hypothesis of this study was that there would be no statistically significant differences among the evaluated LLMs across these three performance components.

Materials and methods

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Afyonkarahisar Health Sciences University (Protocol No: 2024/355). Informed consent was obtained electronically from all participating dentist evaluators prior to their inclusion in the study.

Question development process

In this study, the responses generated by LLMs to frequently asked patient questions regarding dental trauma were evaluated. The question pool was compiled from multiple sources: frequently asked questions obtained using the keyword “dental trauma” on the website alsoasked.com, the guidelines of the IADT, and the ToothSOS mobile application. Questions derived from these sources were selected based on their relevance to commonly encountered dental trauma scenarios and were standardized to ensure consistent structure, wording, and level of clinical detail. The questions were reviewed by the researchers (E. S. and H.A.) based on clinical relevance and clarity. The final list was organized to include clear, comprehensive, and meaningful questions. However, the question set was not subjected to formal pilot testing or external content validation, as the questions were designed as standardized prompts to elicit LLM responses rather than to function as a psychometric instrument.

Consequently, 27 open ended questions were compiled in a standardized format to ensure consistency. These questions were categorized into five subgroups: avulsion (8 questions), post-replantation care/complications (6 questions), luxation (3 questions), fractures (4 questions), and other traumas/general findings (6 questions). Each clinical subcategory was designed to reflect key guideline based concepts relevant to that trauma type, including emergency management, post-injury care, follow-up recommendations, and potential complications. The unequal distribution of questions across subcategories was intentional and based on conceptual and guideline driven scope rather than numerical symmetry. Different trauma types vary in clinical complexity, number of management steps, and diversity of potential patient concerns. Consequently, certain trauma categories allow the formulation of a greater number of independently distinguishable patient-oriented questions, whereas others involve fewer separable management components from a patient perspective. This distinction reflects differences in clinical content density rather than differences in clinical importance. Therefore, the number of questions in each subcategory was determined according to conceptual coverage rather than equal numerical representation.

The question development process was designed to reflect real world patient information needs during dental trauma emergencies. Accordingly, open ended, patient style questions were intentionally selected instead of expert formulated or prompt optimized queries. This approach aimed to simulate realistic emergency scenarios in which patients typically seek immediate, non-technical guidance without structured prompting or repeated refinement of their questions. By avoiding prompt engineering and maintaining a standardized question format, the study sought to evaluate the baseline patient facing response quality of the LLMs under conditions that closely resemble real world patient use.

Acquisition of LLM responses

Each question was posed to five different LLMs. Data collection was conducted between February 1–5, 2025, to ensure consistency in evaluation conditions. The models included ChatGPT-4o, DeepSeek, Gemini 2.0 Flash, Claude 3.5, and Microsoft Copilot, all of which were publicly accessible through standardized web based user interfaces at the time of data collection. These models were selected because they were directly available to end users without the need for API integration, local deployment, or technical configuration, thereby reflecting real world patient accessible AI tools. The selection of these models was based on their widespread public accessibility, contemporary relevance, and their ability to represent diverse development approaches within the current LLM landscape. All included models are readily accessible through publicly available, browser based web applications and are commonly used by the general population, making them suitable for evaluating realistic patient facing information delivery scenarios. Furthermore, the inclusion of models developed by different organizations was intended to allow a comparative assessment across heterogeneous LLM systems rather than multiple versions derived from a single provider, thereby enhancing the interpretability of the findings.

The models were accessed exclusively through their official web interfaces using default settings, without the use of any browser extensions or prompt optimization techniques. Although prompt structure and session independence were standardized, generation parameters such as temperature or sampling settings could not be controlled through the web interface; therefore, inherent output variability could not be fully eliminated. To minimize potential variations related to model updates or deployment changes, all responses were collected within a restricted time frame. Each question was posed once to each model in a fresh session within this data collection window to approximate common real world patient use scenarios. Response consistency across repeated sessions or different time points was not evaluated, as the primary aim was to enable a controlled cross-model comparison of baseline performance under standardized conditions.

Each question was posed in a new, independent chat session for each model, with no prior conversational context or memory carried over between interactions. The full set of patient style questions is provided as Supplementary Material 1 in their original Turkish form. The English translations of these questions are provided separately as Supplementary Material 2 to enhance international comprehensibility. Only the corresponding Likert scores assigned by the evaluators were retained for statistical analysis.

Both the questions and model responses were generated and evaluated entirely in Turkish. No formal cross-language translation or validation protocol was applied, which is acknowledged as a limitation of the study. No additional prompts or prompt optimization techniques were used; questions were presented to the models in their raw form to reflect baseline real world performance, as guiding prompts may artificially inflate response quality and do not reflect typical patient behavior in acute dental trauma scenarios.

The participating dentists evaluated the responses independently and in a randomized order. Only the question texts and standardized scoring instructions were provided, and no additional guidance was given during the evaluation process. All responses were anonymized and presented to the evaluators in a randomized order, and the evaluators were fully blinded to the identity of the source model.

Participation in the study was entirely voluntary. All evaluators were provided with an electronic informed consent form via Google Forms, which outlined the study’s purpose, scope, and methodology. Consent was obtained electronically prior to participation, and evaluators were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time without providing a reason. All study data were collected online using Google Forms (Google LLC, Mountain View, CA, USA).

The questionnaire was sent to the dentist evaluators on February 15, 2025. No fixed time limit was imposed for completion, and all responses submitted by April 15, 2025 were included in the analysis.

Evaluation criteria and scoring method

Responses generated by the five different LLMs were independently evaluated by 20 experienced dentists using a 5-point Likert scale. Since each evaluator rated all models across all questions, a total of 2,700 observations were included in the analyses (20 evaluators × 5 models × 27 questions). This methodological approach aligns with recent studies that employed structured, clinician rated assessment protocols for evaluating LLM responses in dental contexts [4, 18].

For each response, evaluators assigned a single overall score reflecting performance. While scoring, they were instructed to jointly consider multiple predefined dimensions: the factual accuracy of the information provided, the comprehensiveness of the response and the extent to which it adequately addressed the question, and its applicability to emergency dental trauma scenarios.

Responses were evaluated using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = completely incorrect, 5 = completely correct). For the purposes of clinical interpretation, these anchor points were operationalized as follows: a score of 1 indicated a response that was incorrect, misleading, or potentially harmful; a score of 3 indicated a response that was partially correct but incomplete or lacking sufficient clinical relevance; and a score of 5 indicated a response that was accurate, comprehensive, and clinically appropriate.

In addition to overall mean scores, we reported the proportion of responses receiving a score of 1, predefined as incorrect, misleading, or potentially harmful. We also reported the proportion of responses scoring 1–2. These proportions were calculated separately for each model using 540 ratings per model (20 raters × 27 questions) as the denominator.

No formal calibration session was conducted prior to scoring. Instead, all evaluators received standardized scoring instructions, and inter-rater agreement was subsequently assessed using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC).

For example, a response that correctly emphasized the urgency of replantation for an avulsed permanent tooth but omitted key practical guidance such as appropriate actions when immediate replantation is not feasible, recommended storage media, or essential post-replantation follow-up would be expected to receive an intermediate score. In contrast, a response providing complete, guideline aligned information across these aspects would receive the highest score.

The evaluation of LLM responses was guided by the 2020 IADT guidelines, which are internationally recognized and widely adopted as a reference standard for the management of traumatic dental injuries. These guidelines are developed through expert consensus and evidence based recommendations and are commonly used in both clinical practice and academic research to inform decision making in dental trauma care [14, 20]. Nevertheless, it is acknowledged that clinical guidelines, including those issued by the IADT, may contain areas of limited specificity or variability in recommended management depending on clinical context. As such, LLM responses addressing topics where guideline recommendations are broad or open to interpretation may reasonably differ in phrasing or emphasis. Accordingly, during expert evaluation, responses were assessed not for verbatim adherence to guideline text but for overall consistency with guideline principles and safe clinical practice.

Statistical analysis

In the data analysis process, descriptive statistics were first calculated for the overall performance scores of the LLMs. For each model, the mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, and maximum values were determined. The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and since the assumption of normal distribution was not met, non-parametric methods were employed instead of parametric tests. Although Likert scale data are ordinal by nature, both median (IQR) and mean (SD) values were reported to provide complementary descriptive summaries. Linear mixed-effects modeling was used as an approximation to facilitate relative comparisons between models and clinical subcategories, given the repeated measures structure of the data.

The Friedman test was used to examine overall and subcategory based differences among the models, while pairwise comparisons were performed using the Bonferroni-corrected Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using the ICC based on a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement and average measures, as all responses were evaluated by the same group of raters and overall agreement among evaluators was of interest. The obtained ICC values were interpreted according to Fleiss’s criteria: <0.50 = poor, 0.50–0.75 = moderate, 0.75–0.90 = good, and > 0.90 = excellent agreement.

Additionally, to account for the repeated-measures structure of the data and to examine interactions between models and clinical subcategories, a General Linear Mixed Model (GLMM) was employed. In the mixed-effects model, evaluator and question were included as random effects, and Likert-scale scores were treated as continuous outcomes to facilitate comparative modeling, while the primary inferential analyses remained non-parametric (Friedman and Wilcoxon tests), which are fully appropriate for ordinal data [21, 22]. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0. To assess the statistical power of the study, a post-hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 (Cohen’s f = 0.25). The resulting statistical power was 82%, and the significance level was set at p < 0.05.

The distribution of overall performance scores across all LLMs was assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test, which indicated that the normality assumption was not met (p < 0.001). Additionally, Levene’s test revealed unequal variances (F = 25.14, p < 0.001).

The ≤ 8 years group included 11 evaluators (55%), and the > 8 years group included 9 evaluators (45%). Mann–Whitney U tests revealed no significant differences in overall scores between less- and more-experienced dentists for any model (all p > 0.05).

Overall model performance differed significantly among the five LLMs (Friedman test, p < 0.001), with an effect size of Kendall’s W = 0.36, indicating a moderate effect size. ChatGPT-4o ranked highest with the greatest consistency, followed by Claude 3.5 and Copilot, which demonstrated similar intermediate performance, then DeepSeek, and finally Gemini, which ranked lowest. This rank order (ChatGPT-4o > Claude ≈ Copilot ≈ DeepSeek > Gemini) remained stable across all clinical subcategories. Overall, ChatGPT-4o demonstrated the highest performance across all trauma types, whereas Gemini consistently ranked lowest, particularly in luxation related scenarios (Table 3; Fig. 1).

Inter-rater reliability was assessed using the ICC with a two-way mixed-effects model, consistency type, and average measures (ICC[3,k] according to the Shrout and Fleiss [23] convention). This model was selected because the same fixed set of 20 raters evaluated all responses, and the primary interest was in rating consistency rather than absolute agreement. The necessary data for ICC calculation were obtained from the complete dataset of 2,700 individual ratings (20 evaluators × 5 models × 27 questions), with each evaluator independently providing a single overall Likert score for every LLM response. This created a full 20 × 135 rating matrix (20 evaluators × 135 total responses), which served as the input for the ICC analysis. Overall ICC was 0.58 (95% CI: 0.52–0.64), indicating moderate agreement according to Fleiss’s criteria. Model-specific analyses showed the highest ICC for ChatGPT-4o (0.65; 95% CI: 0.58–0.71), indicating greater inter-rater consistency for responses generated by this model (Table 5).

Discussion

Delays in accessing a dentist during emergency dental trauma situations can pose serious risks to patient management. In such scenarios, LLMs may serve as supplementary tools capable of providing timely and relevant information to patients. Dental trauma represents not only a frequently encountered clinical condition but also one of the most commonly searched oral health related topics online [24]. Early and appropriate intervention, particularly in children, can significantly improve outcomes by reducing risks such as pulp necrosis, infection, and adverse effects on developing permanent teeth [25]. Trauma to primary teeth may also influence eruption patterns, space maintenance, speech, and nutrition. Furthermore, dental trauma constitutes a significant source of psychological stress for both patients and caregivers. Accordingly, access to accurate and timely information during emergencies may provide both clinical and psychological support [25].

The increasing accessibility of LLMs in healthcare highlights their potential utility in time critical scenarios such as dental trauma. However, risks related to hallucinations and outdated training data necessitate continuous evaluation of accuracy and reliability [26, 27]. Although newer LLM versions demonstrate improved overall performance, content dependent variability persists, particularly in high stakes clinical contexts [28]. In such settings, average performance metrics alone may fail to capture rare but potentially harmful outputs, underscoring the importance of risk aware evaluation frameworks and human oversight [29].

Previous studies evaluating LLM performance in dental traumatology have employed diverse methodological approaches, including binary questions, open ended formats, mixed item designs, simulated clinical cases, and image based inputs [18, 19, 30–33]. Collectively, this literature demonstrates that reported performance varies substantially depending on question format, input modality, clinical complexity, and model version. Accordingly, direct comparison across studies requires careful consideration of methodological differences.

In this context, the findings of the present study demonstrate that dentist rated performance differs significantly among the evaluated LLMs. Accordingly, based on the statistical analyses, the null hypothesis was rejected.

Several earlier investigations reported lower performance of previous LLM versions, particularly ChatGPT-3.5, in dental trauma related topics compared with other areas of pediatric dentistry [2, 34]. In contrast, more recent studies suggest improved outcomes with advanced ChatGPT based architectures. Consistent with these observations, the present study found that ChatGPT-4o outperformed Gemini across all clinical categories. Similar findings have been reported by Kuru et al. [30] and Sezer et al. [4], while Kapoor et al. [35] further demonstrated higher accuracy, clarity, and clinical relevance for ChatGPT based models. Together, these findings support the interpretation that architectural advancements may contribute to improved patient oriented information delivery, although variability remains.

Recent comparative work in dental traumatology indicates that despite performance gains, limitations related to readability, misinformation risk, and clinical actionability persist, particularly in emergency contexts [36]. Importantly, even models demonstrating high average performance may generate responses rated as potentially harmful, emphasizing the need for cautious interpretation before patient facing use.

The evaluation of LLM responses in the present study was guided by the 2020 IADT guidelines, which provide an internationally recognized reference standard for traumatic dental injury management. While comprehensive, these guidelines include recommendations that are intentionally broad or context dependent. Consequently, LLM generated responses may vary in emphasis or phrasing while remaining clinically acceptable. Accordingly, responses were evaluated for overall consistency with guideline principles and safe clinical practice rather than strict verbatim adherence [14].

Avulsion related questions, which are supported by clear, time critical recommendations in the IADT guidelines, yielded the highest performance across models, with ChatGPT-4o demonstrating superior overall performance and greater response consistency. This finding aligns with previous reports showing concordance between ChatGPT responses and dental trauma cases [37, 38]. The clarity and urgency emphasized in avulsion management may partly explain the higher performance observed in this category.

Post-replantation care and complication related questions represented the lowest performing clinical category overall; however, compared with the other evaluated models, ChatGPT-4o provided the highest rated responses within this domain. The relatively lower performance observed in replantation may be explained by the inherent complexity of post-replantation management. Previous literature has reported that although ChatGPT generally provides guideline consistent recommendations in this area, deficiencies may occur in specific details such as antibiotic selection and splint duration [37]. The present findings appear consistent with these observations.

Luxation injuries, encompassing multiple subtypes and variable prognoses, represented the most challenging category. In the present study, ChatGPT-4o again outperformed other models, whereas Gemini and Copilot demonstrated comparatively limited performance. Similar difficulties in luxation management, particularly intrusion injuries, have been reported previously [39], likely reflecting the complexity and heterogeneity of these trauma types.

Fracture related questions revealed moderate performance across models, with ChatGPT-4o provided higher-scoring responses but all systems showing limitations in treatment specificity. Prior studies similarly report that LLMs may offer accurate but relatively superficial information in fracture classification and management [2].

Other trauma related questions, including soft tissue injuries and general trauma assessment, showed less pronounced performance differences among models. ChatGPT-4o remained the highest performing model, while Gemini scored lowest. This pattern is consistent with literature suggesting strong explanatory capacity but variable response quality across heterogeneous trauma scenarios [37].

The features of the present study lies in its evaluation of commonly encountered dental trauma scenarios in Turkish using a prompt-free, patient-oriented format. Demonstrating measurable performance differences in a less widely studied language such as Turkish represents a methodological strength, supporting the need for language specific validation beyond English dominant settings. This observation aligns with recent bilingual research reporting lower accuracy for ChatGPT-4o in Turkish (84.0%) compared to English (87.8%) when answering multidisciplinary dental questions [40]. Nevertheless, generalization to other languages should be approached cautiously, as linguistic structure, cultural context, and training data distributions may influence model behavior.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The question set was not subjected to formal pilot testing or external content validation, which may have influenced question clarity or scope. The restricted data collection window precluded assessment of subsequent model updates and temporal response stability. Expert based Likert scoring introduces inherent subjectivity, as no checklist based gold standard was applied, and ratings may partially reflect perceived clarity in addition to factual correctness. In this context, the moderate inter-rater agreement observed in this study may reflect variability in clinical judgment rather than inconsistency among evaluators. In open ended trauma scenarios, distinctions between levels of response adequacy can reasonably be interpreted differently by experienced clinicians. Therefore, small differences between models should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, although LLM outputs were evaluated under expert supervision, they represent generalized medical information, and inappropriate interpretation in real world patient facing contexts may lead to clinically significant errors without professional oversight. Finally, the public availability of IADT guidelines and ToothSOS resources raises the possibility of prior training exposure, which may influence apparent guideline alignment.

Future studies may incorporate direct comparisons between human generated answers and LLM responses to further explore the alignment between AI generated information and human clinical reasoning in dental trauma scenarios. Studies with larger samples and alternative methodological designs may help enhance the generalizability of these findings. In addition, building on the present study’s focus on comparative baseline performance under standardized conditions, future research should examine the impact of prompt variability, model updates, and temporal changes in model behavior.

Conclusion

This study provides a structured, dentist rated comparative evaluation of five widely used LLMs in delivering patient oriented information on dental trauma emergencies based on Turkish language, open ended patient style questions simulating real world emergency inquiries. Among the evaluated systems, ChatGPT-4o demonstrated the highest overall performance in terms of accuracy, comprehensiveness, and clinical applicability, with consistently superior ratings across all trauma subcategories. Claude and DeepSeek showed comparable intermediate performance, followed by Copilot, whereas Gemini yielded the lowest overall scores, particularly in more complex trauma scenarios such as luxation.

Despite high overall mean scores, the existence of responses rated 1–2 underscores the need for cautious interpretation of model outputs.

Beyond identifying performance differences at a defined time point, this study contributes a reproducible framework for evaluating AI generated emergency dental trauma information in non-English contexts. While LLMs cannot replace professional clinical examination and treatment, they may serve as a complementary informational resource when immediate dental care is unavailable.
