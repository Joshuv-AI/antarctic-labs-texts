Novel technique for leadless pacemaker implantation in the small right atrial appendage: a case report

Introduction

Transvenous pacemakers have traditionally been used to treat bradyarrhythmias. However, complications related to the leads and device pockets have been reported; lead-related and subcutaneous pocket-related complications occur in approximately 8% and 11% of patients 5 years after implantation, respectively.1 Leadless pacemakers can reduce these complications compared with transvenous pacemakers.2 An atrial leadless pacemaker (Aveir AR [Abbott]) has recently become available, enabling atrial pacing and maintaining strict atrioventricular synchronization.3 Although the efficacy and safety of leadless pacemakers have already been reported, anatomical variations present challenges during implantation in some cases.4

Here, we present a specific bailout technique involving partial rotation while maintaining deflection applied in a markedly small right atrial appendage (RAA).

Case presentation

A 66-year-old man visited the emergency department of our hospital complaining of dizziness. A 12-lead electrocardiogram showed junctional rhythm at 40 beats/min (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1A). He had a history of isolated adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) deficiency and previous ablation for atrial fibrillation.

Considering the risk of pocket infection associated with chronic steroid therapy for ACTH deficiency and the patient’s preference, Aveir AR was implanted for symptomatic sick sinus syndrome.5 This study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee.

A contrast injection into the RAA at right and left anterior oblique 30° angles was performed using a 4-Fr pigtail catheter, confirming that the RAA was significantly small (Figure 1A). Preoperative contrast-enhanced CT, performed for left atrial thrombus evaluation before atrial fibrillation ablation, had already suggested a small RAA; however, the degree of procedural difficulty was not anticipated. The Aveir AR delivery catheter was guided to the RAA base using deflection manoeuvres (Figure 1B). However, upon deflection release, the catheter tip slipped towards the superior vena cava (SVC). Despite attempting multiple deflection angles, the tip consistently slipped towards the SVC upon release (Figure 1C). To address this issue, a bailout strategy involving limited pre-rotation was applied: the chevron marker was rotated by one-quarter turn while maintaining catheter deflection to achieve initial myocardial engagement and prevent catheter displacement (Figure 2B). Under fluoroscopy, the catheter tip was confirmed not to be displaced from the RAA base. After the one-quarter turn while maintaining catheter deflection, mapping measurements showed favourable impedance and threshold values (390Ω and 1.75 V/0.4 ms, respectively). Even after releasing the deflection, the catheter tip position remained stable at the RAA base. Subsequently, the catheter deflection was released and chevron marker was rotated by 1 1/2 turns under fluoroscopy (Figure 2C).

While confirming catheter stability fluoroscopically, we simultaneously confirmed the current of injury (COI), pacing threshold, and impedance (Figure 3).6 Electrical parameters were analysed at each stage to ensure safe myocardial engagement (see Supplementary material online, Table S1). Before rotating the lever while maintaining deflection, the values of each parameter were as follows: pacing threshold of 1.75 V/0.4 ms, sensing amplitude of 1.3 mV, and impedance of 300Ω. No increase in the S-wave was observed in the commanded intracardiac electrogram (Figure 3A). However, following the one-quarter turn while maintaining deflection, S-wave elevation from the baseline and sufficient COI were observed (Figure 3B). This phenomenon was similarly observed after catheter deflection release. The impedance increased from 300Ω to 390Ω after the one-quarter turn and remained stable thereafter. In contrast, a transient increase in the threshold was observed after releasing the deflection and 1 1/2 rotations (4.0 V/0.4 ms). However, one minute later, the threshold improved to 1.75 V/0.4 ms (Figure 3C). After confirming that the tip position of Aveir was not misaligned and all parameters were acceptable, we switched to the tethered mode. The release knob was rotated to detach the Aveir AR without displacement. The final pacing threshold after release was 1.25 V/0.4 ms (Figure 3D). We placed an Aveir VR at the right ventricular apical septum because of a Wenckebach-type atrioventricular block at a 110-bpm heart rate. The total procedure and fluoroscopy times were 126 and 36 min, respectively. During the operation, vital signs remained stable. Post-operative transthoracic echocardiography revealed no pericardial effusion. The postoperative fluoroscopic image and electrocardiogram are shown in Figure 4 and Supplementary material online, Figure S1B. Two months after implantation, atrial pacing parameters remained stable (threshold 0.5 V/0.4 ms, sensing amplitude 0.7 mV, impedance 320Ω), and symptoms resolved.

Discussion

We present a specific bailout technique applied in a markedly small RAA. To prevent catheter tip slip at the small, slippery RAA base, part of the outer helix was screwed into the myocardium before releasing the catheter deflection. We thus successfully implanted the Aveir AR safely.

According to the recommended implantation procedure for the Aveir AR system, pre-mapping is performed after release of deflection. However, releasing catheter deflection consistently resulted in persistent catheter tip instability with slippage towards the SVC. To overcome this phenomenon, we rotated the chevron marker by one-quarter turn before release of deflection.

The risk of perforation is the main concern when initiating screw placement while maintaining deflection. The structural design, controlled rotation, myocardial thickness, and optimal outer helix contact with the myocardium are critical factors that could have allowed us to safely implant Aveir AR in the small RAA. First, Aveir AR has a distal grind shape at the outer helix tip, which may reduce the risk of perforation compared with Aveir VR.7 In porcine RAA testing, myocardial perforation occurred with Aveir VR but not with Aveir AR after several rotations (see Supplementary material online, Video S1). Second, the number of rotations was important. The deflection was maintained for one-quarter turn only. Therefore, the outer helix tip was retained within the myocardium. However, this retention prevented tip slippage and enabled smooth placement. If the sole purpose is securing the tip, increasing the rotation may not be necessary. Third, the device was intentionally placed at the RAA base, rather than in the deep RAA, where the myocardium is thinner and more perforation- or dislodgement-prone.8 In addition, deep RAA dislodge more easily than base RAA. Knops et al.3 reported higher rates of postprocedural dislodgement (4.4%) for leadless pacemakers implanted in the mid-to-deep RAA than for those in other locations (0.9%). When attempting to place the Aveir AR in the deep RAA, the RAA rotated with the Aveir device (see Supplementary material online, Video S2). Subsequently, switching to the tether mode allowed the RAA to untwist. Therefore, in the deep RAA, rotating the outer helix only twisted the appendage without creating sufficient screw fixation. This was likely the cause of frequent deep RAA dislodgement. Fourth, maintaining deflection while applying excessive outer-helix contact to the myocardium may increase the perforation risk. Here, the COI was low, and the S-wave was at the baseline level prior to screwing the outer helix. However, atrial wave height, threshold, and impedance were adequate (Figure 3A). These findings indicate adequate myocardial contact without excessive force, underscoring the importance of confirming electrical parameters before screw deployment. The RA and RAA volumes were 123.1 and 1.81 ml indicating that the RAA was extremely small, while the RA was large (Figure 5).9 Owing to anatomical reasons, leadless pacemaker implantation may sometimes be difficult; therefore, preoperative imaging may be advantageous for treatment selection.

We describe a bailout technique involving limited pre-rotation under maintained deflection for Aveir AR implantation in an extremely small RAA. This modified approach does not replace the manufacturer-recommended implantation sequence and should be considered strictly as a bailout strategy in highly selected anatomical conditions.
