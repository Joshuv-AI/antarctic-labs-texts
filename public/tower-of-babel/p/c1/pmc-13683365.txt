Enhancing anatomical recognition by surgeons during pelvic lymph node dissection using artificial intelligence
Authors: Daichi Kitaguchi, Shin Takenaka, Yasukazu Nakanishi, Shun Yamaguchi, Tomohiro Noda, Suguru Odajima, Junki Onishi, Yuki Koike, Kohei Hirose, Madoka Kataoka, Mitsumasa Homma, Atsushi Kouno, Hiroki Matsuzaki, Nobuyoshi Takeshita, Hitoshi Masuda, Hiroshi Tanabe, Yuichiro Tsukada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe lateral pelvis is a critical anatomical region in colorectal, gynecological, and urological surgeries. However, its anatomical complexity and variability pose significant challenges for pelvic lymph node dissection (PLND). This study aimed to develop an artificial intelligence (AI) model to identify key anatomical structures relevant to PLND and evaluate whether AI assistance enhances surgeons’ ability to recognize pelvic anatomical features. Thirty-six surgeons representing colorectal, gynecological, and urological specialties, with varying experience levels, reviewed 640 video snippets (0.5 s each) from PLND procedures. The model was trained on 23,259 annotated and 653 unannotated images extracted from 293 PLND procedure videos. Threefold cross-validation yielded Dice similarity coefficients of 0.6483 for the ureter, 0.8654 for the obturator nerve, 0.8619 for the external iliac artery, and 0.8736 for the external iliac vein. Across all structures, AI assistance led to a significant improvement in sensitivity and specificity among participating surgeons (p < .001). Our findings suggest that the proposed AI model may assist surgeons in identifying pelvic anatomical structures across different specialties and experience levels. Further studies using continuous intraoperative workflows will be required to determine its impact on clinical practice.Subject terms: Anatomy, Medical research, Urology

Introduction

Pelvic lymph nodes are a common site of metastasis in various malignancies, including rectal, prostate, bladder, cervical, and endometrial cancers1–5. Pelvic lymph node dissection (PLND), also referred to as lateral lymph node dissection in colorectal surgery, reduces local recurrence and improves recurrence-free survival6–13. However, given the anatomical complexity of the pelvis and its proximity to vital structures such as the ureters, nerves, and major vessels, PLND remains technically demanding and carries a significant risk of intraoperative injury14,15. These challenges highlight the need for innovative technologies to enhance the safety and precision of PLND procedures, such as the application of computer vision-based surgical guidance systems.

Artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning-based computer vision techniques such as semantic segmentation, has recently been applied to surgical navigation for minimally invasive procedures16,17. AI-based semantic segmentation models can automatically identify critical anatomical landmarks during surgery, thus helping in the prevention of intraoperative injuries and supporting intraoperative decision-making by providing a clearer understanding of the correct dissection planes and cutting lines. Such computer vision-based approaches have demonstrated the potential to improve intraoperative awareness, support real-time surgical guidance, and enhance safety during complex laparoscopic and robotic surgical procedures.

Training high-performance AI models requires datasets that capture a wide range of anatomical and procedural variations18. To develop AI-assisted navigation systems for minimally invasive PLND, models trained on cross-disciplinary datasets spanning colorectal surgery, gynecology, and urology are expected to enhance recognition performance and system generalizability. The present study aimed to develop an AI model for identifying key anatomical structures relevant to PLND using such multidisciplinary data. Additionally, we evaluated the model’s target structure recognition performance and investigated whether its use could improve surgeons’ ability to identify anatomical structures in the pelvis, stratified by specialty and clinical experience.

Results

Model training and validation

The training dataset used to develop PelviX3Net comprised 23,259 annotated and 653 unannotated images (i.e., negative training data), extracted from 293 surgical videos of minimally invasive PLND. The dataset was stratified by primary disease and surgical specialty: PLNDs performed by colorectal surgeons (128 videos), gynecologists (121 videos), and urologists (44 videos) contributed 8546 annotated and 197 unannotated, 9936 annotated and 323 unannotated, and 4777 annotated and 133 unannotated images, respectively. Threefold cross-validation performed on the training dataset demonstrated that the model achieved DSCs of 0.6483, 0.8654, 0.8619, and 0.8736 for the ureter, obturator nerve, external iliac artery, and external iliac vein, respectively.

Discussion

We developed a deep learning-based semantic segmentation model to identify key structures, including the ureter, obturator nerve, external iliac artery, and external iliac vein, during PLND procedures. The developed model demonstrated high recognition accuracy and significantly enhanced surgeons’ ability to identify target structures, resulting in notable improvements in sensitivity and specificity when AI assistance was employed. Thus, the developed AI model could support surgeons in identifying anatomical structures during minimally invasive PLND, potentially aiding in the correct identification of dissection planes and reducing the risk of intraoperative organ injury.

According to a report from the World Health Organization on patient safety, despite the awareness of adverse effects, surgical errors continue to occur at a high rate. Reportedly, 10% of preventable patient harm in healthcare occurred in surgical settings19. Suliburk et al. found that 56.4% surgical care-related adverse events were associated with human error. Among these, cognitive errors accounted for the largest share at 51.6%, whereas technical errors were responsible for only 10.420. This suggests that several surgery-related adverse events could be prevented using technological interventions targeting human cognition. Additionally, the ureter, obturator nerve, and iliac vessels are critical anatomical structures in the pelvis and the target organs recognized by our developed AI model. Injuring these structures can lead to serious complications, including major bleeding, renal dysfunction, and lower limb dysfunction. Such injuries can directly result in life-threatening events or postoperative deterioration affecting the patient’s quality of life. The developed AI model is expected to assist surgeons’ cognition of these critical structures during PLND, thereby minimizing preventable injuries.

The use of AI assistance significantly improved the sensitivity of target structure recognition. Although this suggests faster and more accurate intraoperative identification of critical landmark structures, a potential risk of increased false positives (FPs) remains, since the AI might display incorrect locations, which could lead to misrecognition of dissection planes. Therefore, it is desirable to enhance sensitivity without compromising specificity. However, in this study, specificity remained unchanged and was significantly improved with AI assistance. Thus, the developed AI does not mislead surgeons but instead helps in reducing misidentifications. Notably, the additional benefit in specificity for inexperienced trainees was significant, suggesting that the AI effectively aided in minimizing misidentification among trainees. These results imply that the potential negative effects of using the system during surgery, such as increasing complications owing to anatomical misidentification or confounding the surgeon’s judgment, are negligible.

The anatomical complexity of neurovascular structures in the narrow lateral pelvis can lead to incomplete lymph node dissection and lateral local recurrence5. A thorough understanding of pelvic anatomy and accurate target lateral node identification are essential to ensure complete lymph node dissection and avoid neurovascular structure damage. To address these technical challenges, novel surgical navigation techniques have been developed. One example is stereotactic navigation, an established computer-aided surgical navigation system used in orthopedic surgery, neurosurgery, and otolaryngology that is also applied for navigation in PLND21. Additionally, three-dimensional (3D) models and holographic navigation, which involve highly detailed virtual and physical 3D models constructed from computed tomography/magnetic resonance imaging, have been used for pelvic surgery navigation22,23. Although the pelvis is less susceptible to pneumoperitoneum than is the abdominal cavity, precise alignment of the pelvis with preoperative images is difficult because of intraoperative organ deformation and positional shifts. Consequently, intraoperative navigation with millimeter-level accuracy has not been achieved. AI does not rely on preoperative image information because it analyses intraoperative images in real time. Intraoperative AI use is considered the most feasible approach, since it allows for real-time analysis that accounts for organ deformation and positional changes during surgery. Recent advances in computer vision have greatly expanded its clinical utility in laparoscopic and robotic surgeries, enabling more accurate anatomical recognition and safer intraoperative navigation.

When deploying intraoperative AI navigation systems in real surgical settings, user interface design represents an important challenge. Excessive highlighting of anatomical structures may obstruct the surgical field, whereas insufficient visualization may cause surgeons to overlook critical structures. From the perspective of preventing oversights and enhancing recognition, simply allowing the overlay to be turned on and off may not be ideal, because navigation support should be available even when the surgeon does not actively request it. One possible approach is to present the AI output on an auxiliary display. In robotic surgery, the information could be shown using an auxiliary display within the surgical console, whereas in laparoscopic surgery it could be displayed on a secondary monitor adjacent to the main surgical monitor. Alternatively, continuous overlay on the main surgical monitor may also be feasible if the visualization is carefully designed to avoid obstructing the operative field. Regardless of the display method, visualization parameters such as color selection, transparency, and the intensity of segmentation masks should be optimized so that anatomical guidance remains visible while minimizing interference with the surgeon’s view. Future studies should also investigate adaptive visualization strategies such as dynamic modulation of mask transparency or use of intermittent or pulsatile overlays according to the surgical context in order to maximize visibility while minimizing distraction and obstruction of the operative field.

This study had some limitations. First, this study was observational and was not conducted in an actual surgical setting. Therefore, the developed model was not used during real surgeries, and its impact on clinical outcomes was not evaluated. This reflects the artificial nature of the experimental setting. In clinical practice, surgeons perform stepwise dissection while accumulating contextual anatomical information throughout the procedure under a 3D view, which likely enhances their ability to accurately recognize anatomical structures. In contrast, the present study used short, context-free two-dimensional (2D) video snippets to evaluate surgeons’ performance. This simplified experimental design was chosen to enable a controlled comparison between surgeons and the model; however, it may have underestimated the surgeons’ actual performance. Accordingly, the comparison between human performance and the model in this study should be interpreted with caution. In addition, although AI assistance improved anatomical recognition performance, the magnitude of improvement was moderate, and further refinement of both the accuracy of the model and the design of the user interface will be necessary to achieve clinically meaningful real-time surgical support. Future studies using continuous intraoperative video streams and context-aware models will be necessary to evaluate the real-world clinical utility of AI systems designed to assist surgeons by highlighting critical anatomical structures during PLND. Second, the sensitivity for certain critical anatomical structures, particularly the ureter, remained lower than that for other structures. Because ureteral injury is a serious complication in pelvic surgery, further improvement in the model’s performance for ureter recognition will be necessary before routine clinical implementation. Therefore, the current system should be considered an assistive tool rather than a fully reliable autonomous guidance system. Future efforts to improve ureter recognition may include the accumulation of additional annotated datasets and the incorporation of temporal and contextual information from continuous intraoperative video streams. Third, the current model recognizes only the ureter, obturator nerve, external iliac artery, and external iliac vein. In the future, additional anatomical structures in the lateral pelvis, particularly the internal iliac vessels and pelvic floor landmarks, should be incorporated to establish a more comprehensive navigation system for PLND. Fourth, the training dataset was fully anonymized and did not include detailed clinical metadata such as lymph node status, nodal size, pathological findings, or prior surgical history. Therefore, it was not possible to stratify the dataset according to lymph node burden, pathological status, or surgical complexity. In addition, detailed information regarding anatomical variations and patient demographics was not available. Although the test dataset was collected from multiple institutions and included different types of pelvic surgery, all cases were derived from a Japanese population. Therefore, the generalizability of the model to populations from other geographic and ethnic backgrounds remains to be validated in future studies. Nevertheless, the AI model developed for PLND in a cross-disciplinary manner is highly innovative. PLND involves navigation of complex anatomy and challenging procedures; thus, there is a high demand for intraoperative navigation systems.

In conclusion, the AI model developed in this study may assist surgeons in recognizing target anatomical structures and reducing misrecognition. Further studies using real surgical workflows will be required to determine its impact on clinical outcomes.

Methods

Study design

This multi-center, prospective observational study evaluated the performance of a Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) developed by Jmees Inc.

Ethics statement

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the ethics committee of the National Cancer Center Hospital East, Kashiwa, Japan (registration number 2024-05). The surgical videos used for model development were anonymized, without including personal information. Written informed consent was obtained from all surgeons participating in the study. This study adhered to the provisions of the Declaration of Helsinki (1964, revised in Brazil in 2013)24.

Semantic segmentation model

A deep learning-based semantic segmentation model, named PelviX3Net, was developed to analyze videos of minimally invasive PLND and automatically recognize key anatomical structures involved, including the ureter, obturator nerve, external iliac artery, and external iliac vein. The model used a semantic segmentation algorithm that interprets the meaning of each pixel in an image and displays the recognized structures as pixel aggregates. The UNet + + (Nested U-Net) architecture25 was employed for semantic segmentation, with EfficientNetV2-L26 serving as the backbone network in the encoder; it was pretrained using ImageNet-21K (https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2104.1097227). Implementation details for PelviX3Net are provided in Table S1.

Training dataset

Anonymized videos of minimally invasive PLND, including laparoscopic and robot-assisted procedures, were converted to MP4 format at 30 frames/s. The videos were then divided into static image units, and images depicting the lateral pelvis were randomly selected for subsequent annotation. These videos contained no information related to nationality, healthcare facilities, or timing of surgery, ensuring complete anonymity. Because the dataset was fully anonymized, detailed clinical metadata such as lymph node status or pathological findings were not available. In addition, detailed patient demographics and surgical history (e.g., primary vs revisional surgery) were also not available in the dataset.

Annotation

Twelve annotators participated in the study, including four colorectal surgeons: two laparoscopic skill-certified surgeons and two board-certified surgeons; five gynecologists: three board-certified specialists in gynecological oncology and two board-certified gynecologists; and three urologists: one board-certified urology instructor and two board-certified urologists. These annotators were responsible for generating the ground-truth annotations for model training and were not involved as participants in the surgeon performance evaluation experiment. The annotators manually labeled pixels corresponding to the target structures in the images using the Computer Vision Annotation Tool (CVAT; CVAT.ai, Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA). All annotated images were double-checked by surgeons to ensure quality, and any discrepancies in annotation coverage were resolved through discussion. The annotated images were then used as training data for PelviX3Net. Figure 1 shows representative annotated images. Additionally, to reduce FPs, unannotated static images that showed no target structures despite depicting the lateral pelvis were included as negative training data for PelviX3Net.

Test dataset

Anonymized videos of PLND performed between April 2009 and September 2024 were collected from multiple hospitals across Japan. From these, 30 cases comprising 10 rectal resections, 10 total hysterectomies, and 10 radical prostatectomies were selected for the test dataset. Selection criteria were as follows: minimally invasive procedures (i.e., laparoscopic or robot-assisted), exclusion of cases already included in the training dataset, suitability for target structure recognition (i.e., no strong halation, blurring, or focus issues), and adult patients aged >18 years.

All videos used in this study were 2D recordings. 3D stereoscopic recordings were not included in order to maintain consistency in image characteristics across the dataset. Patient sex and the recording camera system manufacturers, namely Olympus Corporation (Tokyo, Japan), Karl Storz SE & Co. KG (Tuttlingen, Germany), Stryker Corporation (Kalamazoo, MI, USA), and Intuitive Surgical, Inc. (Sunnyvale, CA, USA), were adjusted during case selection to reflect real-world data distribution. Table S2 shows a detailed breakdown of case distributions by each factor.

From each video, eight 0.5-s snippets (i.e., 15 consecutive frames) containing the target structures and eight 0.5-s snippets without the target structures were randomly extracted, resulting in a total of 480 snippets (240 with and 240 without the target structures) from all 30 cases.

AI performance metrics

The dice similarity coefficient (DSC) was employed to evaluate the performance of the developed model in the semantic segmentation task. For validation within the training dataset, a threefold cross-validation approach was used. The test data comprised 0.5-s video snippets (i.e., 15 consecutive frames) with or without the target structure. For snippets containing the target structure, correct recognition (i.e., true positive, TP) was defined as achieving DSC above a predetermined threshold on five or more consecutive frames within the 15-frame sequence; otherwise, it was considered a false negative (FN). For video snippets without target structures, correct non-recognition (i.e., true negative, TN) was defined as the model not detecting any pixels corresponding to the target structure for any continuous sequence of five frames within the 15-frame clip. Otherwise, the case was considered FP. The DSC threshold for each target structure, predetermined based on the surgeons’ questionnaire results, was set at 0.4, 0.2, 0.4, and 0.4 for the ureter, obturator nerve, external iliac artery, and external iliac vein, respectively. Surgeon participants

Thirty-six surgeons from multiple hospitals across Japan participated in the study. In colorectal surgery, participants included four laparoscopic skill-certified surgeons with 10–17 years of clinical experience, four board-certified surgeons with 4–25 years of clinical experience, and four non-board-certified surgeons with 2–4 years of clinical experience. In gynecology, participants included four board-certified specialists in gynecological oncology with 12–16 years of clinical experience, four board-certified gynecologists with 5–6 years of clinical experience, and four non-board-certified gynecologists with 1–4 years of clinical experience. In urology, participants included four board-certified urology instructors with 13–25 years of clinical experience, four board-certified urologists with 8–10 years of clinical experience, and four non-board-certified urologists with 2–4 years of clinical experience. These categories represent different levels of surgical experience, ranging from trainees to highly specialized surgeons with advanced certification. In Japan, these certifications require substantial surgical training and procedural case experience as part of the qualification process.

The sample size was determined based on the results of a prior pilot trial. Using these preliminary data, we performed a power analysis to calculate the sample size required to detect a statistically significant difference using Student’s t-test, ultimately determining that 36 participants would be sufficient.

Surgeon performance with and without AI

As shown in Fig. 2, the monitors were arranged side-by-side. In the no-AI-assisted condition, the right monitor displayed nothing; in the AI-assisted condition, both monitors displayed the same snippet, but the right monitor was designed to superimpose a segmentation mask inferred and generated by the developed model. Each participating surgeon evaluated 8 snippets per case; for 10 cases, this resulted in 80 snippets per target structure, and 4 target structures were included. Consequently, 320 snippets were evaluated in the no-AI-assisted condition; 160 snippets contained the target structures, and 160 did not. Subsequently, 320 snippets (i.e., eight snippets × 10 cases × 4 target structures) that did not overlap with the previous snippets were evaluated in the AI-assisted condition. Colorectal surgeons, gynecologists, and urologists evaluated snippets extracted from the surgical videos of rectal resections, total hysterectomies, and radical prostatectomies, respectively.

All video data were obtained from PLND procedures; therefore, the participating surgeons were aware of the surgical context. However, the specific stage of the procedure was not disclosed to the participants. During the evaluation, the AI output was visualized using color overlays, and the target anatomical structure being assessed in each task was specified to the participants. Each snippet was displayed in a loop, allowing repeated observation by the surgeons. In the dataset, the annotated anatomical structures were at least partially exposed in the surgical field, as completely buried structures would not be identifiable by either surgeons or the AI model.

When a surgeon identified a target structure, they made a single-point plot in the region of the target structure for the last frame of a set of 15 consecutive frames. No plot was made if the target structure was deemed absent. For snippets containing target structures, if the surgeon made a plot in the correct region, it was classified as TP; otherwise, it was considered FN. For snippets without target structures, if the surgeon did not make a plot, it was classified as TN; otherwise, as FP. The sensitivity and specificity of the surgeons’ recognition of target structures were calculated accordingly.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was the sensitivity of all participating surgeons in recognizing each target structure, with and without AI assistance. The secondary endpoint was the specificity, also with and without AI assistance. Sensitivity and specificity were further analyzed in a subgroup analysis wherein the participating surgeons were divided into board-certified specialists and trainees without board certification.

Statistics

Numerical data, including sensitivity and specificity, are presented as means with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) or as means ± standard deviation (SD), as appropriate. Comparisons were performed using Student’s t test. All p values were two-sided, and those <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).
