Substrate pre-stretch reprograms macrophage behavior through surface topographical remodeling
Authors: Gang Yang, Yi Han, Tao Tang, Yong-fu Xiong, Jun-wei Wang, Xiao-song Qing, Chen Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mechanical regulation of macrophages is increasingly recognized, but whether a mechanically preconditioned surface remains biologically instructive after loading has ceased is unclear. Here, we developed a custom uniaxial pre-stretch device to generate polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) membranes with defined tensile histories and used this system to examine how substrate mechanical regulates macrophage behavior. Atomic force microscopy showed that pre-stretch reduced membrane step height and surface roughness without significantly changing elastic modulus or water contact angle, indicating selective remodeling of surface topography. RAW264.7 macrophages cultured on pre-stretched membranes exhibited increased cell and nuclear spreading, reduced roundness, and decreased F-actin intensity, accompanied by enhanced cell–substrate adhesion and increased migratory activity. Pre-stretched surfaces also promoted phagocytosis and reactive oxygen species generation without affecting proliferation. In addition, macrophages on pre-stretched membranes showed elevated expression of TNFα and IL-1β and increased TNF-α secretion, whereas Arg1 and IL-10 were not enhanced, indicating a shift toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype. These findings demonstrate that pre-stretched PDMS membranes function as mechanically encoded interfaces that direct macrophage morphology, motility, functional activation, and inflammatory polarization. More broadly, this study identifies substrate mechanical as a previously underappreciated regulator of macrophage mechanoimmunology and provides a useful platform for biomaterials research, disease modeling, and drug screening.

Introduction

The gastrointestinal tract is a mechanically active organ in which immune regulation occurs within a continuously changing physical environment.1 During normal physiological function, intestinal tissues are repeatedly exposed to deformation associated with peristalsis, luminal flow, pressure fluctuation, and wall stretch, while the epithelial barrier, stromal compartment, microbiota, and immune cells are all embedded in this dynamic setting.2 Increasing attention has therefore been given to the concept of gut mechanoimmunology, which emphasizes that physical cues are not merely background features of the intestine but active regulators of mucosal immunity, tissue homeostasis, and disease progression.3 More broadly, mechanosensing in the gastrointestinal tract has been implicated in barrier maintenance, inflammatory regulation, and tissue remodeling, supporting the view that mechanical context is an essential part of intestinal physiology and pathology.4

Among gut-resident immune populations, macrophages are particularly important because they integrate immune defense with tissue maintenance and repair. Intestinal macrophages represent one of the largest mononuclear phagocyte populations in the body and are central to maintaining mucosal homeostasis through pathogen clearance, removal of cellular debris, regulation of inflammatory responses, and support of epithelial regeneration5, 6, 7, 8, 9. At the same time, these cells are highly plastic and can adopt inflammatory or reparative programs depending on the local microenvironment, making them key determinants of whether gastrointestinal tissues progress toward persistent inflammation or restoration of homeostasis10, 11, 12. Their importance is further underscored in intestinal disorders, where dysregulated macrophage function contributes to barrier damage, aberrant immune activation, and impaired healing.

Mechanical regulation of macrophage behavior is now increasingly appreciated, yet an important gap remains in understanding how macrophages response to surfaces roughness cues.13 Previous studies have shown that macrophages are responsive to tissue-level physical cues, including matrix stiffness, shear stress, pressure, and cyclic stretch, and that these cues can influence cell spreading, migration, inflammatory signaling, phagocytosis, cytokine production, and polarization state14, 15, 16, 17, 18. This has led to the broader recognition of macrophage mechanoimmunology as a framework in which force-sensitive receptors and cytoskeletal–nuclear signaling pathways translate physical inputs into immune outcomes. However, most existing studies examine macrophages either under active mechanical loading or on substrates defined by static material properties. Much less is known about whether a surface that has been mechanically preconditioned before cell seeding can preserve instructive biophysical information that subsequently shapes macrophage morphology, motility, and immune phenotype.

Advances in microfluidic chips and mechano-dynamic culture devices have substantially expanded the experimental toolbox available for mechanobiology research.19 By enabling precise control of strain, deformation, fluid flow, and substrate mechanics, these platforms offer reductionist yet physiologically relevant systems for investigating how physical forces regulate cell behavior.20 Among the materials used in such systems, polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) has been particularly valuable in chip-/device-based culture platforms, including microfluidic and organ-on-chip systems, owing to its mechanical compliance, optical transparency, biocompatibility, and ease of fabrication.21 PDMS membrane-based platforms have also been widely used in mechanical stretch studies, where they provide a convenient substrate for applying defined tensile deformation while supporting live-cell imaging and quantitative analysis of force-responsive behavior, as exemplified by the Flexcell system.22 Building on this framework, we developed a PDMS membrane-based pre-stretch device that serves as a controllable model of a mechanically encoded interface, in which tensile preconditioning may generate persistent surface-associated cues that remain after subsequent cell seeding. This design makes it possible to test whether macrophages respond not only to active loading, but also to the mechanical cues embedded in a substrate, thereby defining a distinct mode of immune mechanoregulation.

In this study, we developed a pre-stretch device to generate PDMS membranes with defined tensile histories of 10% and 20% strain and used this system to examine how mechanically preconditioned surfaces regulate macrophage behavior after cell seeding. Atomic force microscopy revealed that pre-stretching significantly reduced membrane step height and surface roughness, whereas contact angle and elastic modulus remained unchanged, suggesting that tensile preconditioning mainly altered surface topography rather than wettability or bulk stiffness. RAW264.7 macrophages cultured on the pre-stretched membranes displayed increased cell and nuclear spreading, reduced roundness, and decreased F-actin levels, indicating pronounced changes in cell morphology and cytoskeletal organization. These changes were accompanied by enhanced focal adhesion formation, increased migration and expansion, reduced membrane staining intensity, elevated phagocytic activity, and increased intracellular ROS, without detectable effects on proliferation. Moreover, PCR and ELISA analyses showed that the pre-stretched membranes promoted macrophage polarization toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype. Collectively, these results suggest that the mechanical cues of a substrate can be retained as persistent surface-associated cues that direct macrophage morphology, motility, functional activity, and inflammatory polarization.

Materials and methods

Pre-stretch device fabrication

Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) membranes were prepared using a standard elastomer base/curing agent system (Dow Corning, SYLGARD184) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, the base and curing agent were mixed at a ratio of (10:1, w/w), degassed under vacuum, cast into a flat mold, and cured at 60 °C for 6 h to obtain thin PDMS membranes. The cured membranes were cut into rectangular strips of defined dimensions and mounted onto a custom-built uniaxial pre-stretch device. The membrane thickness is 0.5 mm, the modulus of the PDMS membrane is 4.1 MPa. After the PDMS membrane is stretched, it will be fixed in the device and keep the stretched state to keep the topological stable, which will not be released after stretch. The pre-stretch device was fabricated via 3D printing, which consisted of two opposing clamps that fixed both ends of the PDMS membrane and enabled controlled uniaxial elongation along the longitudinal axis. Stretch was applied by increasing the distance between the clamps, thereby generating tensile preconditioning of the membrane before cell seeding.

Cell culture

RAW264.7 cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA) and maintained in Advanced Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM; Invitrogen) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS; Fisher Scientific, Houston, TX) and 1 % (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin antibiotic mixture. Cells were seeded onto PDMS membrane coated with 10 mg/mL poly-L-lysine at 37 °C for 4 h, then washed by PBS for 3 times and cells used at 80 % confluence. Cultures were maintained at 37 °C incubator supplied with 5 % CO2, washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Corning, 46-013-CM), and passaged using a medium-flush method.

Atomic force microscopy characterization of PDMS membranes

Surface topography and local mechanical properties of PDMS membranes were characterized using atomic force microscopy (AFM; Bruker NanoWizard V) equipped with a Bruker SNL-10 cantilever. Before measurement, PDMS membranes from the 0%, 10%, and 20% pre-stretch groups were carefully mounted on glass-bottom dishes or rigid sample holders to minimize sample movement during scanning. All samples were measured under the same environmental conditions, and at least three randomly selected regions were analyzed for each membrane group.

For topographical analysis, the membrane surface was scanned in PeakForce tapping mode to obtain height images. The scan size, scan rate, and resolution were kept consistent among all groups to allow direct comparison of surface morphology. Representative AFM height images were collected from multiple regions of each sample. Step height and surface roughness were quantified from the height images using the AFM instrument software. Surface roughness was calculated as the average roughness or root-mean-square roughness within the selected scan area, and step height was determined from line-profile analysis across representative surface features. The same image-processing and background-flattening parameters were applied to all groups before quantitative comparison.

For local mechanical characterization, AFM-based force spectroscopy was performed using the same cantilever. Force–distance curves were acquired at multiple positions on each PDMS membrane surface. The cantilever spring constant and sensitivity were calibrated before measurement according to the manufacturer's instructions. During force spectroscopy, the loading force, approach/retraction speed, and indentation range were kept constant for all samples. The local Young's modulus was calculated by fitting the approach portion of the force–distance curves using the Sneddon contact model. Curves with unstable baselines, obvious adhesion artifacts, or poor fitting quality were excluded from analysis. Multiple regions and multiple force curves were analyzed for each sample, and the averaged values were used for statistical analysis. Data from the 0%, 10%, and 20% pre-stretch groups were compared to determine whether pre-stretch-induced topographical remodeling was accompanied by changes in local substrate stiffness.

Single-cell force spectroscopy (SCFS)

For single-cell force spectroscopy (SCFS), individual RAW264.7 cells were attached to a ConA-functionalized tipless cantilever (Bruker NPO-10). To achieve stable and specific cell capture, the cantilevers were sequentially coated with biotin-BSA, streptavidin, and biotin-ConA, thereby promoting strong binding to cell-surface glycoconjugates. During cell attachment, the cantilever was gently brought into contact with a single cell using a loading force of 5 nN for 30 s, followed by a 10 min stabilization period to allow firm adhesion and minimize cell detachment during subsequent measurements. Successful attachment was confirmed under an inverted microscope, and cells exhibiting lateral displacement were excluded from analysis. The cell-bearing cantilever was then used to probe cell–substrate detachment in PBS (pH = 6.8) under identical loading conditions. Both the approach and retraction velocities were set to 5 μm/s to minimize hydrodynamic drag and ensure reproducible force–distance measurements. All SCFS experiments were performed under temperature-controlled conditions at 37 °C. Adhesion forces were extracted from the force–distance curves using Nanoscope Analysis software (Bruker).

F-actin staining

Cells were fixed with pre-chilled 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min at room temperature and then permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (Sigma, T8787) in PBS for 10 min. Samples were subsequently blocked with 1% normal donkey serum and gamma globulins (NDS) in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. F-actin was stained with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated phalloidin (1:400) for 1 h, and nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Invitrogen Molecular Probes, D3571) for 5 min according to the manufacturer's instructions. Between each step, the samples were washed three times with PBS for 5 min to remove unbound molecules. Macrophage morphology was imaged using a Zeiss fluorescence microscope. Cell morphology was quantified using ImageJ, and roundness was used as a morphological parameter for analysis. For each experimental condition, at least 30 cells from three independent experiments were analyzed.

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) measurement

Intracellular ROS levels were detected using a DCFH-DA-based fluorescent probe according to the manufacturer's instructions (Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit, Beyotime Biotechnology, China). RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and exposed to the indicated treatments. After treatment, the medium was removed and the cells were washed once with PBS. DCFH-DA was diluted 1:1000 in serum-free DMEM to a final concentration of 10 μM, added to the cells at 500 μL per well, and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cells were then washed three times with serum-free DMEM to remove excess extracellular probe. Fluorescence images were acquired immediately using an inverted fluorescence microscope under identical exposure conditions. For each group, 100 randomly selected cells were analyzed, and intracellular ROS levels were quantified based on DCF fluorescence intensity using ImageJ software.

Macrophage phagocytosis assay

Macrophage phagocytic activity was evaluated using pHrodo™ Red BioParticles (Thermo Fisher Scientific, P35361), which emit fluorescence in acidic phagosomes following particle uptake. RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 10^4 cells per well and allowed to adhere for at least 1 h before the indicated treatments were applied for 2 h pHrodo BioParticles were resuspended in PBS at 1 mg/mL, briefly vortexed, diluted 1:10 in complete culture medium, and added to the cells. After incubation at 37 °C for 2 h, cells were washed with PBS to remove non-internalized particles and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:1000) for 5 min. Fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope under identical conditions. For each group, at least 200 cells were randomly selected, and phagocytic activity was quantified as the percentage of cells containing intracellular red fluorescence.

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation on PDMS membranes was assessed using MTS assay (Promega, G3582) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RAW264.7 cells cultured on the 0%, 10%, and 20% membranes were incubated with the assay reagent for 1 h, and absorbance was recorded using plate reader (Bio-Rad, USA) at 490 nm. Results were normalized to the non-stretched control group and expressed as relative cell proliferation.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from RAW264.7 cells cultured on different PDMS membranes using RNA extraction kit (ThermoFisher Biotechnology, USA). RNA concentration and purity were determined by nano-drop (ThermoFisher Biotechnology, USA), and equal amounts of RNA were reverse-transcribed into cDNA using reverse transcription kit (Bio-rad). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using SYBR Green or probe-based master mix (Bio-rad), on a PCR instrument. The expression of macrophage polarization-related genes, including TNFα, IL-1β, Arg1, and IL-10, was measured using gene-specific primers. Enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The secretion of TNF-α and IL-10 was measured in culture supernatants using commercial ELISA kits (Abclonal, RK00016 and RK03242) according to the manufacturer's instructions. After macrophages were cultured on the different PDMS membranes for 48 h, supernatants were collected, centrifuged to remove debris, and subjected to ELISA. Absorbance was read at 570 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad), and cytokine concentrations were calculated from standard curves.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by appropriate post hoc tests. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Pre-stretch decreases the roughness of PDMS membrane surface

To generate mechanically preconditioned substrates, we developed a custom uniaxial stretching device capable of applying defined tensile strain to thin PDMS membranes before cell seeding (Fig. 1a). In this design, the PDMS membrane was fixed between two opposing clamps, and tensile loading was applied along a single axis by increasing the clamp-to-clamp distance, thereby elongating the membrane in the longitudinal direction. In the schematic, L1 represents the initial length of the effective PDMS membrane region, whereas L2 denotes the total clamp distance after stretching. The applied tensile strain was calculated as the relative elongation of the effective membrane region along the loading axis, according to the equation strain (%) = (L2 − L1)/L1. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was performed to assess both the surface topography and local mechanical properties of PDMS membranes following tensile preconditioning. AFM height images were acquired using a Bruker SNL-10 cantilever in surface scanning mode to visualize the membrane topography (Fig. 1b). Relative to the non-stretched control, the 10% and 20% pre-stretched membranes exhibited a smoother surface morphology, characterized by reduced topographic irregularities in the height maps. Consistent with these observations, quantitative analysis 10 different positions on the membrane after stretch, which demonstrated that both step height and surface roughness were significantly decreased after pre-stretch, with a greater reduction in the 20% group than in the 10% group (Fig. 1c and d). These findings indicate that tensile preconditioning is sufficient to remodel the surface microtopography of PDMS membranes in a strain-dependent manner. To further examine whether these topographical changes were accompanied by altered surface mechanics, AFM force measurements were used to determine the local elastic modulus of the membranes. No significant difference in modulus was detected among groups (Fig. 1e), indicating that pre-stretch predominantly changed surface topography without measurably affecting local stiffness.

To determine whether these changes were accompanied by alterations in other material properties, contact angle measurements were performed by placing a water droplet on the membrane surface and measuring the contact angle formed at the solid–liquid interface (Fig. 1b–f). In contrast to the marked changes in surface topography detected by AFM, the water contact angle did not differ significantly among the non-stretched, 10%, and 20% pre-stretched groups, indicating that pre-stretching did not measurably affect the surface wettability of the PDMS membrane. In sum, these results indicate that tensile preconditioning primarily remodels the surface topography of the PDMS membrane while preserving its bulk mechanical and interfacial properties, thereby establishing a well-controlled mechanically encoded substrate for subsequent macrophage studies.

Quantitative analysis confirmed that pre-stretched membranes significantly increased macrophage spreading area in a strain-dependent manner, with the largest cell area observed in the 20% group (Fig. 2b). Nuclear area was also significantly increased on pre-stretched membranes relative to the non-stretched control (Fig. 2c), indicating that surface mechanical affects not only whole-cell morphology but also nuclear architecture. In parallel, cell roundness was significantly reduced after pre-stretch (Fig. 2d), consistent with a transition from a more compact morphology toward a more extended and irregular shape.

To determine how pre-stretched membrane effect to actin polymerization, F-actin fluorescence intensity was quantified as shown in Fig. 2e. Macrophages cultured on pre-stretched membranes exhibited significantly lower F-actin intensity than those on non-stretched membranes, with the lowest level observed in the 20% group (Fig. 2e). Together, these results indicate that tensile preconditioning of PDMS membranes promotes macrophage spreading and nuclear expansion while reducing cell roundness and F-actin abundance, demonstrating that substrate mechanical cue is sufficient to induce pronounced morphological and cytoskeletal remodeling.

We next examined whether the increased adhesion was accompanied by altered migratory behavior using live-cell imaging and single-cell tracking. After the membrane was pre-stretched, macrophages were seeded on surfaces with different roughness, live-cell imaging tracking cell random migration for 6 h, and take pictures every 5 min. Then analyze the cell migration by nuclear tracking. Representative migration trajectories showed that macrophages cultured on the 20% pre-stretched membrane displayed a broader movement range than those on the non-stretched and 10% groups (Fig. 3b). Quantitative analysis further demonstrated that cell migration velocity was significantly increased on the 20% pre-stretched membrane (Fig. 3c). Consistently, accumulated migration distance was also significantly elevated in this group (Fig. 3d), indicating enhanced motile activity on the pre-stretched surface.

By contrast, Euclidean distance and directionality did not show significant differences among groups (Fig. 3e and f), suggesting that substrate pre-stretch primarily increased the extent and speed of macrophage movement rather than imposing a persistent directional bias. Together, these data show that mechanically preconditioned PDMS membranes promote stronger macrophage adhesion and greater migratory activity, supporting the idea that substrate roughness regulates cell–surface interaction and downstream cell behavior.

We next examined whether these changes were accompanied by altered macrophage effector function. Quantification of phagocytic activity showed that macrophages cultured on pre-stretched membranes exhibited significantly enhanced phagocytosis relative to the non-stretched control, with a more pronounced increase at 20% strain (Fig. 4C). In parallel, intracellular ROS levels were also elevated on the pre-stretched membranes, particularly in the 20% group (Fig. 4D), indicating that mechanically encoded surfaces promote a more activated cellular state.

To determine whether substrate pre-stretch broadly affects cell growth, we further assessed macrophage proliferation via MTS assay. In contrast to the marked effects on membrane staining, phagocytosis, and ROS generation, no significant difference in proliferation was detected among the groups (Fig. 4E). Together, these findings indicate that pre-stretched PDMS membranes selectively enhance macrophage functional activation, characterized by increased phagocytic activity and oxidative response, without significantly altering proliferative capacity.

Pre-stretched surfaces bias macrophages toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype

To determine whether mechanically preconditioned PDMS surfaces alter macrophage inflammatory polarization, we examined the expression of representative pro-/anti-inflammatory markers in RAW264.7 cells cultured on non-stretched and pre-stretched membranes. qRT-PCR analysis showed that the expression of the pro-inflammatory markers TNFα and IL-1β was significantly increased on pre-stretched membranes, with the strongest induction observed in the 20% group (Fig. 5A). To further validate these findings at the protein level, we quantified cytokine secretion by ELISA. Consistent with the gene-expression data, TNF-α secretion was significantly elevated in macrophages cultured on pre-stretched membranes, particularly in the 20% group (Fig. 5B). By contrast, IL-10 levels were not significantly increased by pre-stretch and remained largely unchanged across groups (Fig. 5C). These results support the conclusion that mechanically encoded PDMS surfaces preferentially promote pro-inflammatory activation.

Taken together with the earlier observations of increased spreading, focal adhesion, migration, phagocytosis, and ROS generation, these data indicate that pre-stretched substrates drive macrophages toward a more activated and pro-inflammatory state. As summarized schematically in Fig. 5D, tensile preconditioning of the PDMS membrane generates a surface state that suppresses quiescent or less activated macrophage behavior while promoting focal adhesion, migration, phagocytic activity, and inflammatory polarization. Collectively, these findings identify substrate rougnhess as an instructive regulator of macrophage immune state.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that the pre-stretching substrate can serve as an instructive regulator of macrophage behavior even in the absence of continuous external loading. Using a custom uniaxial pre-stretch device, we generated PDMS membranes with defined tensile preconditioning and found that pre-stretch reduced membrane step height and surface roughness without significantly altering bulk modulus or wettability. When macrophages were cultured on these pre-stretched membranes, they exhibited increased spreading and nuclear area, reduced roundness and F-actin intensity, enhanced focal adhesion, migration, phagocytosis, and ROS generation, and a shift toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype. Taken together, these findings indicate that prior tensile loading can be retained as a persistent surface-associated cue that reshapes macrophage morphology, function, and immune state.

A key significance of this work lies in its conceptual distinction from conventional stretch-based mechanobiology. Most previous studies have focused on how macrophages respond to active mechanical stimulation, such as cyclic stretch, compression, or fluid shear.23 In contrast, our study was designed to ask whether a surface that has already undergone deformation can remain biologically instructive after the loading process has ended. This question is important because, in living tissues, immune cells frequently encounter matrices and interfaces that have been mechanically remodeled by prior deformation, injury, fibrosis, surgical manipulation, or device implantation.24 Thus, cells may respond not only to ongoing force, but also to the mechanical memory embedded in a surface. Our results support this idea by showing that tensile preconditioning alone is sufficient to alter PDMS topography and subsequently bias macrophage behavior. In this sense, the pre-stretched membrane represents a reductionist model of a force-imprinted interface, extending the scope of immune mechanobiology from real-time force sensing to the recognition of mechanically encoded surface states. Since PDMS surfaces may show variable cell attachment depending on surface treatment and hydrophobicity. Therefore, surface coating was used to ensure consistent baseline cell attachment across all PDMS groups. Importantly, all experimental groups received the same coating procedure, so the observed differences are attributed to the pre-stretch-remodeled surface condition rather than differential coating exposure.

The present findings also provide insight into how such mechanically encoded interfaces may regulate macrophage activation. Because pre-stretch primarily altered surface topography, while leaving modulus and wettability unchanged, the observed cellular responses are more likely to arise from changes in topography-dependent cell-surface interaction than from bulk mechanical or interfacial differences. This distinction is important, as it suggests that subtle structural remodeling of a substrate can reorganize macrophage adhesion and shape downstream cytoskeletal and functional responses.25 Consistent with this interpretation, macrophages on pre-stretched membranes showed greater spreading, reduced roundness, increased focal adhesion, and decreased F-actin intensity, indicating a substantial reorganization of cell architecture. These structural changes were accompanied by enhanced migration, phagocytosis, ROS production, and increased expression of pro-inflammatory markers, together suggesting that the altered surface state promotes a more activated macrophage phenotype. Although the precise mechanotransductive pathways remain to be defined, the data support a model in which pre-stretch-induced surface remodeling modifies macrophage adhesion and structural organization, thereby priming inflammatory function.

From a mechanical perspective, pre-stretching may partially flatten surface asperities and reduce microscale height variation on the PDMS membrane, thereby changing how macrophages physically engage with the substrate. A smoother surface may increase the effective contact area between the cell membrane and material surface, allowing more stable adhesion formation and stronger cell–substrate force transmission. This altered mechanical coupling could explain the enhanced adhesion force, increased spreading, and greater migratory activity observed on pre-stretched membranes. Meanwhile, changes in adhesion geometry and local traction distribution may reorganize the actin cytoskeleton and transmit mechanical signals to the nucleus, contributing to nuclear spreading and downstream inflammatory activation. Therefore, pre-stretch-induced topographical remodeling may regulate macrophage behavior not simply as a passive surface change, but by reshaping the mechanical boundary condition through which cells sense, generate, and transmit forces.

Beyond its mechanistic implications, this work may contribute to clinical research and biomedical engineering by introducing pre-stretch as a controllable strategy for generating immune-regulatory interfaces. In many biomedical settings, including gastrointestinal implants, wound-healing materials, soft biointerfaces, and organ-on-chip systems, surfaces are exposed to deformation during fabrication, handling, implantation, or physiological use. Such mechanical preconditioning is usually treated as a passive background factor, yet our findings suggest that it may actively influence subsequent immune responses.26 The pre-stretch approach described here therefore provides a simple way to engineer surface states that modulate macrophage behavior without requiring continuous mechanical loading during culture. This concept may be useful in the design and evaluation of biomaterials where immune activation must either be enhanced, such as for clearance-oriented applications, or minimized, such as for inflammation-sensitive interfaces. More broadly, it suggests that the mechanical past of a material should be considered an integral design variable in translational bioengineering.

The pre-stretch device may also serve as a versatile platform for future disease modeling and drug screening. Many pathological conditions are associated with repeated tissue deformation and altered surface structure, including intestinal inflammation, fibrosis, postoperative remodeling, and mechanically stressed mucosal interfaces. By providing a reductionist system in which prior tensile loading can be imposed in a controlled manner, this platform could be used to investigate how mechanically remodeled surfaces influence immune behavior under disease-relevant conditions. It may also be integrated with epithelial cells, fibroblasts, extracellular matrix coatings, inflammatory mediators, or microbial components to build more physiologically relevant in vitro models. In addition, because macrophage morphology, phagocytosis, oxidative stress, and inflammatory polarization respond robustly to the preconditioned substrate, the system could be adapted for screening anti-inflammatory drugs, immunomodulatory biomaterials, or compounds targeting adhesion- and mechanotransduction-related pathways. Thus, beyond establishing a new mode of macrophage mechanoregulation, this study provides a practical framework for connecting mechanically encoded interfaces to mechanobiology, disease modeling, and translational bioengineering.

Conclusion

In conclusion, substrate pre-stretch creates a persistent mechanically encoded surface state that remodels PDMS topography and directs macrophage behavior even after external loading is removed. This preconditioned interface enhances macrophage spreading, adhesion, migration, phagocytosis, ROS generation, and biases cells toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype. These findings identify substrate rouhness as an important regulator of macrophage mechanoimmunology and highlight pre-stretched surfaces as a useful platform for biomaterials design, disease modeling, and drug screening.
