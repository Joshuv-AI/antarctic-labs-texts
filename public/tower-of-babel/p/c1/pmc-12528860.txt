Pediatric Orthopaedic Clinical Peer-review Triggers and Ongoing Professional Practice Evaluation (OPPE) Metrics: An Ohio Statewide Consensus
Authors: Julie B. Samora, Kevin Baker, Kerwyn Jones, Michael Vish, Susan P. Tourner, Michael Vossmeyer, Nichole Liston, Todd F. Ritzman, Michael T. Bigham
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTwo critical mechanisms to measure competency in pediatric orthopaedic surgery are case-based clinical peer review and ongoing professional practice evaluation (OPPE). However, we currently lack a standard set of triggers or metrics to inform competency evaluation in pediatric orthopaedic surgery. The purpose of this study was to seek consensus on the most important triggers for case-based peer review and metrics for OPPE in pediatric orthopaedic surgery.MethodsThis consensus project involved a modified Delphi approach to achieve consensus among orthopaedic surgeons and peer review leaders from multiple children's hospitals in our state, via the Ohio Peer Review Collaborative (OPRC). The OPRC identified candidate metrics and triggers, and using 2 rounds of e-voting, the candidate metrics and triggers were ranked in a Likert-like fashion until consensus agreement was reached on the importance of each individual measure.ResultsTwenty-six (26) candidate OPPE metrics were identified initially, with 17 reaching consensus as important or very important. Eighty-four (84) candidate triggers were suggested for case-based peer review. After voting, 37 triggers reached consensus as important or very important. The OPRC assigned metric and trigger definitions for each consensus item to permit standardization in measurement and reporting.ConclusionsA multidisciplinary statewide clinical peer review collaborative has identified a consensus set of case-based peer review triggers and OPPE metrics that may serve as a foundational data set to inform competency measurement in pediatric orthopaedic surgery on a national level.Key Concepts(1)It is important to continually measure competency in pediatric orthopaedic surgery.(2)We sought to use the Ohio Peer Review Collaborative to identify triggers for clinical peer review and metrics for ongoing professional practice evaluation (OPPE).(3)A total of 17 OPPE metrics reached consensus, and 37 triggers reached consensus, indicating that there is a robust desire to measure peer performance.Level of EvidenceV

Introduction

Clinical peer review in medicine serves to evaluate and improve the quality of patient care by assessing the competence and performance of healthcare providers. It involves the review of medical decisions, diagnoses, and treatments by peers within the same specialty. Associated federal statutes, including the Health Care Quality Improvement Act (HCQIA), encourages peer review and provides legal protections for those involved [1]. State law in Ohio, and many other states, addresses the confidentiality and immunity of participants in medical peer review [2]. Accrediting bodies such as The Joint Commission and the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) have standards for hospitals, including requirements for an effective peer review process to ensure ongoing quality improvement in healthcare delivery [3,4]. A statutory and moral peer review imperative exists, and many consider peer review a continuum of quality performance tracking.

One element of the peer review continuum is the professional practice evaluation (PPE) and associated PPE metrics. Ongoing professional practice evaluation (OPPE) “identifies professional practice trends that may impact the quality and safety of care and applies to all practitioners granted privileges via the Medical Staff requirements” [3]. Related, the Focused Professional Practice Evaluation (FPPE) is a “process whereby the medical staff evaluates the privilege-specific competence of a practitioner who lacks documented evidence of competently performing the requested privilege(s) at the organization” [5]. Whereas the OPPE is widely considered to be a regular monitoring of care related to privileges, the FPPE is intentionally focused on either a new privilege, a new procedure, or any instance where there may be concern with a practitioner's ability to provide safe, high-quality care. Another element of the peer review continuum recognizes case-based peer review. Herein, there are various triggers for case-based peer review, including adverse events, poor outcomes, deviations from the standard of care, patient complaints, and concerns voiced by other healthcare professionals that warrant a specific and in-depth review of a provider's care around a specific patient's care.

Pediatric orthopaedic surgery has a long history of quality and improvement initiatives, both at the local hospital level and through national professional societies. The Pediatric Orthopaedic Society of North America (POSNA) created the POSNA Safe Surgery Program (PSSP) to ensure the highest quality of care for trauma, spine, sports medicine, hip/lower extremity, hand/upper extremity, and neuromuscular patients [6]. However, these national entities do not uniquely define pediatric orthopaedic specialty-specific OPPE metrics or case-based peer review triggers. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) published an information statement on the orthopaedic surgeon's role in medical peer review, declaring that medical peer review is an important part of physician licensing and regulation, and encouraging active participation in the peer review process [7]. The American College of Surgeons (ACS) launched the Children's Surgery Verification Quality Improvement Program in 2017, which developed standards, required rigorous data collection, a robust performance improvement process, and external peer review verification [8]. More recently, the ACS released updated guidelines aimed at promoting surgeons' lifelong competency, which recommend a series of career trajectory competency tests integrated with OPPE programs [9].

There are not yet standardized or federated OPPE metrics or case-based peer review triggers for pediatric orthopaedic surgery. Facing variability among children's hospitals in peer review processes and triggers, the Ohio Peer Review Collaborative (OPRC) was formed in November 2023, and prioritized the sharing of peer review structures and processes, while seeking consistency in OPPE metrics and peer review triggers. The OPRC sought to summarize the OPPE metrics and peer review case-based triggers used across the state's children's hospitals and subsequently achieve a consensus set of OPRC-recommended metrics and triggers.

Materials and methods

Ohio Peer Review Collaborative

The OPRC was established in November 2023 and chartered with four clear goals: To learn better practices in systems/processes from one another; to strengthen the quality of OPPE metrics and case-based peer review triggers; to establish benchmarking for OPPE metrics and case-based peer review triggers (i.e. percentages of cases reviewed, percentage of review cases with findings); and to contribute to the literature on elements along the continuum of peer review.

The OPRC members include six children's hospitals in Ohio: Akron Children's Hospital, Cincinnati Children's Hospital, Dayton Children's Hospital, Nationwide Children's Hospital, ProMedica Russel J. Ebeid Children's Hospital, and University Hospitals Rainbow Babies and Children's Hospitals. Representatives from participant hospitals include physician medical staff leaders involved with elements of peer review, quality and/or safety leaders, and leaders or staff with specific duties related to professional practice evaluations or peer review.

Institutional participation in the OPRC is voluntary, as it is not a covered entity under a patient safety organization (PSO). The improvement work seeking consensus was deemed non-humans subject research and thus exempt from institutional review board oversight.

Candidate OPPE metrics and case-based peer review triggers

To introduce the consensus-seeking process, an existing OPRC scheduled monthly meeting was used to discuss the candidate metric/trigger identification process, agree on pediatric orthopaedic surgery as the specialty of interest, and share the timing and process for the anticipated solicitation of OPPE metrics and case-based peer review triggers. Thirty days prior to the first round of voting, a request for candidate metrics was distributed via email to all OPRC institutions to submit OPPE metrics and peer review triggers used for peer review programs in pediatric orthopaedic surgery. Fifteen days later, a second request reminder was emailed to all OPRC members. An aggregate metric candidate list was compiled from submitted OPPE metrics and case-based peer review triggers in use by OPRC institutions plus metrics and triggers extracted from the medical literature using internet searches, ChatGPT, and published medical literature. Amongst the candidate elements, some metrics or triggers were suggested as being more applicable to a specific provider type (e.g. relevant specifically to an advanced practice provider- (APP)), while others were not specific. Some candidate metrics with specific time limits or durations identified were preserved (i.e. unplanned return to the operating room in 7 days or 14 days or 30 days), while others that seemed more policy-driven locally were collapsed into a single metric (i.e. chart closure in 24 hours or 48 hours or 2 business days). Ultimately, a final candidate list of OPPE metrics and triggers was compiled, representing the input from all OPRC member hospitals.

Consensus process

The modified Delphi technique was chosen to guide the consensus process as it is a proven methodology to achieve consensus in healthcare [10,11]. A priori, the OPRC agreed to include both OPRC participants and pediatric orthopaedic department chairs/division directors (or designees).

Once a full candidate metric and trigger list was generated, Microsoft Forms (Redmond, WA) was used to distribute and tabulate Round 1 voting. In addition to candidate metrics listed, Round 1 voting respondents were encouraged to add additional candidate metrics for consideration and voting in subsequent rounds. Each metric or trigger was scored for inclusion as a meaningful measure of quality by survey respondents using a five-point Likert-like scale from strongly agree (5), agree (4), neutral (3), disagree (2), and strongly disagree (1).

Round 1 and round 2 voting

Both OPPE metrics and peer-review triggers underwent 2 rounds of voting, though they were presented separately to provide distinction between the two when voting. During Round 1 voting all candidate metrics were scored and new metrics were added. Metrics with ≥70% of responses with a score of 4 or 5 were deemed “consensus agreement” and were retained on the final list. Conversely, metrics with <50% of responses with a 4 or 5 were deemed “consensus disagreement” and were discarded. Metrics with ≥50% and <70% of responses with a 4 or 5 score were retained for Round 2 voting. Round 1 voting was open for 14 days, with 1 reminder sent 1 week after Round 1 voting opened.

During Round 2 voting the metrics lacking consensus were ranked as were newly suggested metrics from Round 1 voting. Identical Likert-like ranking was used in Round 2 voting, though no option for suggesting new metrics was available in Round 2 voting. Round 2 voting also was open for 2 weeks, with 1 reminder 7 days after Round 2 voting opened. Participating in Round 1 voting was not a prerequisite for participating in Round 2 [12].

Case-based peer review triggers

The initial solicitation process identified 84 separate case-based triggers submitted for review by participants. Three of the triggers were the same or similar, and those three triggers were combined for the review process, yielding 81 candidate metrics for Round 1 voting.

Discussion

In this study, we were able to use a statewide peer review collaborative to develop a set of consensus OPPE metrics and peer review triggers for pediatric orthopaedic surgery.

Lucian Leape asserted that “honest feedback from peers can make the difference between a good doctor and a great one.” W. Edwards Deming, a staunch advocate for performance measurement, contended that “without data, you're just another person with an opinion.” Leape and Deming were both right and artfully described the continuum of peer review and its criticality to the quality of a healthcare system. Despite the importance of peer review, effective peer review is time intensive. As such, it is imperative that there be value derived for the institution, peers, and the provider. Hence, it is necessary that OPPE metrics and case-based triggers are meaningful, accurate, and actionable. The OPRC has leveraged robust and proven consensus methodology to articulate a set of pediatric orthopaedic surgery OPPE metrics and case-based triggers that meet this standard.

Data become meaningful when the practitioners in the group have agreement that the metric upon which they are evaluated is a true reflection of their ability to provide quality care to their patients. This study leveraged the OPRC network to achieve consensus across both clinical experts and peer review experts. The use of a mission-driven group like the Ohio Peer Review Collaborative provides many benefits. These include a high level of expertise and experience in the process of clinical peer review, a broad perspective and pool of expertise from which to make recommendations, and the ability to foster innovation. However, the use of a varied, multi-disciplinary group to suggest or affirm metrics or triggers, including an unknown number of pediatric orthopaedic surgeons, can also be seen as a weakness. While this may be true, it is possible that the consensus may be even stronger because it has passed the specialty clinicians' meaningfulness test and the peer review experts' feasibility test. As an example, an orthopaedic surgeon who is evaluated on a return to the operating room within a specific time frame for loss of fixation will be more amenable to the pursuit of practice improvement than a metric that evaluates their patients’ compliance with postoperative care bundles. The former metric is directly related to the care provided by the surgeon, and the latter is not.

This study speaks to the desire by leaders and peer review stakeholders to robustly measure peer performance, with 17 OPPE metrics and 37 case-based triggers achieving consensus agreement. Accuracy and actionability of the peer review data are foundational to OPPE metric and case-based peer review trigger endorsement. Though a healthy peer review program avoids the assignment of “blame” in lieu of quality improvement prioritization, less trusted peer review programs might create undue conflict between providers amidst any uncertainty of data accuracy. Further, OPPE metrics are intended to track performance over time in an aggregated fashion. A resulting inaccurate or unactionable measurement falls short of informing a clinical leader of subtle or non-subtle situations where improvement actions are needed.

The Delphi process is designed to evaluate each metric or trigger independently. However, in clinical practice, there are some events that may occur more than once—like infection, unplanned return to the operating room, or readmission—before rising to the level of concern. Establishing minimum thresholds for the frequency of such events may help better define when they should prompt review. Additionally, it would be beneficial to consider establishing consensus clusters of triggers that, when they occur together, warrant peer review. Incorporating these refinements would better align the review process with real-world clinical complexity and enhance the sensitivity of the peer review triggers.

Limitations

This study is not without limitations. This consensus set of OPPE metrics and case-based triggers was generated among a limited sample of Ohio Children's Hospitals. As a result, the consensus priorities in Ohio and the OPRC may over- or under-represent the non-Ohio peer review priorities. The specific makeup of the group members may influence the quality, applicability, and scope of the recommendations. Not all members of the Ohio Peer Review Collaborative were pediatric orthopaedic surgeons. This may limit the strength of the recommendations. Additionally, metrics were not selected or prioritized to overtly include feasibility of measurement – potentially yielding important but not usable triggers or metrics. The OPRC did not prioritize or rank any specific structure, process, or outcomes of a peer review program, but merely measures. And lastly, the authors recognize that well-conceived consensus metrics or triggers will be of limited value in a system that does not prioritize the value of peer review or support the culture of continuous improvement through peer review. The culture of peer review in the participating organizations was not measured or assigned any associated priority in this consensus process.

Conclusion

The Ohio Peer Review Collaborative has aligned a statewide cohort of clinical and peer review leaders from children's hospitals in Ohio to thoroughly explore and achieve consensus on a meaningful set of OPPE metrics and case-based peer review triggers for the specialty of pediatric orthopaedic surgery. This may serve as a foundational data set to inform competency measurement in pediatric orthopaedic surgery on a national level.
