Effect of laparoscopic anatomical hepatectomy for hepatocellular carcinoma based on individualized three-dimensional reconstruction model of portal vein region
Authors: Tingting Cao, Haisong Xu, An Zhang, Wenhao Huang, Gongze Peng, Tianchong Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) accounts for 90% of primary liver tumors. With the development of laparoscopic technology, laparoscopic liver resection is safer and more effective compared to traditional open surgery. However, HCC tumors are often located near major blood vessels and bile ducts, which increases the difficulty of precise resection. To address these challenges, computer-based three-dimensional (3D) liver reconstruction technology has been widely applied. This technology converts two-dimensional imaging data into detailed three-dimensional anatomical models, clearly displaying the vascular anatomy within the liver, accurately locating the lesion, defining the surgical margin, and estimating the liver volume. Its intuitive and multi-angle visualization enhances surgical understanding and decision-making, providing crucial support for preoperative assessment, surgical planning, and intraoperative navigation.Three-dimensional watershed analysis based on hepatic veins and hepatic pedicles is one of the two major strategies for guiding anatomical liver resection. However, the existing articles lack a comparison between different methods. Therefore, we conducted a comparative study of two different resection methods for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) to determine their advantages in terms of clinical outcomes.MethodsPatients diagnosed with hepatocellular carcinoma and treated with laparoscopic hepatectomy at Shenzhen People’s Hospital from 2018 to 2021 were the subjects of a retrospective study of clinical data. Two separate groups of participants, one with venous access and the other with hepatic pedicle access, were evaluated at the same time. Data was collected during the operation, immediately after the procedure, and at 36 months to compare the two surgical approaches.ResultsNo statistically significant difference in preoperative data was found between the two groups in this study, which included 78 patients. With a p-value of only 0.033, the hepatic pedicle group lost 50 ml less blood than the vein group. In comparison to the hepatic vein group, the hepatic pedicle group had substantially lower levels of ALT (P = 0.022), AST (P = 0.036), and postoperative complication rate (P = 0.031). The hepatic pedicle group showed a 78.4% survival rate and a 56.1% tumor-free rate, whereas the hepatic vein group demonstrated a 70.7% overall survival rate and a 36.6% tumor-free survival rate. Tumor-free survival was significantly longer in the hepatic pedicle group (P = 0.040), while overall survival did not differ significantly between the two groups. (P > 0.05).ConclusionsThree-dimensional (3D) reconstruction-guided hepatic pedicle laparoscopic anatomical hepatectomy was safe and effective and demonstrated favorable perioperative and oncologic outcomes, revealing the clinical value of 3D visualization in anatomical liver resection.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), accounting for 90% of primary liver tumours, is the most common histological subtype of primary liver cancers. Globally, HCC ranks as the seventh most common malignancy and the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths, with over 600,000 new cases diagnosed annually [1]. Liver cancer has risen from the third leading cause of cancer deaths in 2018 to the second in 2020, with China contributing 24% of global new cancer cases and 30% of cancer-related deaths [2]. Notably, nearly half of new HCC diagnoses occur in China, revealing the substantial regional burden of this disease [3].

With the advancement of laparoscopic, liver resection has become much safer and more effective compared with traditional open hepatectomy or non-anatomical resection [4]. However, Patients with liver cancer whose tumors often located close to major blood vessels and bile ducts, creating a major challenge for achieving precision-based resection [5, 6]. To address these challenges, precise preoperative evaluation and surgical planning methods were applied to create computer-based 3D liver reconstructions. These models allow surgeons to visualize hepatic anatomy, assess tumor location, and plan resection strategies with greater accuracy and confidence [7, 8]. Unlike the three-dimensional images generated in the brain, computer-generated models provide stable, reproducible, and shareable references for the entire surgical team, reducing uncertainty and improving intraoperative guidance [9]. For more advanced image-guided applications, augmented reality (AR)-assisted systems can further align virtual liver models with intraoperative views, enhancing precision in laparoscopic hepatectomy [10–12]. Despite these advances, recurrence still occurs in more than 70% of patients within five years following surgery. Therefore, medical three-dimensional reconstruction visualisation technology (3D-RVT) has been increasingly adopted to improve preoperative planning and surgical precision through the conversion of two-dimensional imaging data (ultrasound, CT, and MRI) into detailed 3D anatomical models [13, 14]. By analysing large volumes of image data, predicting anatomical structures, and identifying vascular patterns, the 3D reconstruction system assists surgeons in making more accurate and individualized surgical plans. Compared with traditional 2D imaging techniques, 3D-RVT has many advantages, including he ability to clearly display intrahepatic vascular anatomy, accurately localize lesions, define surgical margins, and estimate liver volume. Its intuitive and multi-angular visualization enhances surgical understanding and decision-making. This technology enables the simulation of surgical procedures, providing critical support for preoperative assessment, surgical planning, and intraoperative navigation [15]. Among them, 3D watershed analysis based on hepatic veins and 3D watershed analysis based on hepatic pedicle represent two major strategies for guiding anatomical hepatectomy. However, limited studies have directly compared these two techniques, and their respective clinical advantages remain unclear. To address this gap, we analyzed HCC patients who underwent different surgical modalities to evaluate and compare the clinical outcomes of hepatic vein-based and hepatic pedicle-based hepatectomy.

Methods

Patient cohort selection

A retrospective analysis was conducted on 148 patients who had elective laparoscopic hepatic resections at the Shenzhen People’s Hospital’s Department of Hepatobiliary and Pancreatic Surgery between 2018 and 2021. Based on the availability of preoperative 3D reconstruction data, 78 patients who met the inclusion criteria were identified and categorized into either the 3D hepatic vein-guided group or the 3D hepatic pedicle-guided cohort. Patients were included if they met the following criteria: (1) histopathological confirmation of HCC; (2) laparoscopic hepatectomy performed as the primary surgical method; (3) preoperative use of 3D visualization technology; (4) preoperative liver function grade A; (5) underwent regional or segmental liver resection. The exclusion criteria were: (1) patients who underwent major hepatectomy (e.g., hemihepatectomy) or irregular resection; (2) pathological diagnosis other than HCC; (3) previous liver surgery for tumor or other indications; (4) recurrent HCC at the time of surgery [16, 17] (Fig. 1). The selection of surgical technique was not randomized. The decision was made collectively by the surgical team according to tumor location and size, vascular invasion, hepatic functional reserve, and patient comorbidities. Specifically, the hepatic pedicle approach was preferred for tumors confined to a specific segment or pedicle region, while the hepatic vein approach was selected when the tumor was adjacent to or invaded hepatic venous structures. This nonrandomized selection ensured patient safety and maintained consistency with individualized surgical planning [47, 48].

Conduct process

Surgical system

All included patients underwent surgery using the 3D Laparoscopic hepatectomy navigation system (3D-LHNS), which had been developed by the Shenzhen Institute of Advanced Research, Chinese Academy of Sciences team (software copyright No.2018SR840555). This system was applied during the original surgical procedures to assist in intraoperative localization and navigation, and relevant imaging and reconstruction data from the system were retrospectively collected and analyzed for this study.

Preoperative preparation

Imaging acquisition and three-dimensional reconstruction

All patients underwent preoperative imaging, including contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Imaging acquisition focused on the hepatic arterial system, portal vein, hepatic veins, bile ducts, and tumor to ensure adequate visualization of small vascular branches [18, 19]. These images were imported into the 3D-LHNS system, which automatically segmented [18] (Fig. 2).

Using the reconstructed model, tumor volume, the shortest distance between the tumor and major vascular structures, and the estimated future liver remnant volume were calculated. A minimum remnant liver volume of ≥ 40% was required. Key anatomical landmarks, including Glissonean pedicle branches and major hepatic veins, were marked to guide intraoperative navigation [20, 21].

Preoperative assessment and surgical strategy selection

Liver function was evaluated using the Child-Pugh classification, and only patients with Child-Pugh class A were included. Hepatic functional reserve was further assessed using the indocyanine green retention rate at 15 min (ICG-R15) [17]. The surgical approach was selected based on tumor location and vascular involvement: (1) A hepatic pedicle-guided approach was preferred when the tumor was confined to a single liver segment or portal territory without hepatic vein invasion. (2) A hepatic vein-guided approach was selected when the tumor was adjacent to or invading a major hepatic vein, to preserve venous outflow.

Surgical procedure

All procedures were performed laparoscopically following standardized operative principles by the same experienced surgical team. The extent of liver resection and patient positioning were determined based on preoperative 3D visualization analysis, tumor location, vascular anatomy, and estimated postoperative functional liver reserve to ensure adequate residual liver volume [19]. Although both groups followed standardized laparoscopic techniques, they differed in the primary anatomical landmark used to guide liver transection.

Hepatic pedicle-guided laparoscopic anatomical hepatectomy

This approach uses the Glissonean pedicle (portal vein, hepatic artery, and bile duct) as the primary anatomical landmark and follows the sequence: pedicle control → ischemic demarcation → parenchymal transection.

Patient positioning and port placement

Patients were placed in a supine reverse Trendelenburg position (approximately 30°), with lateral tilt adjusted according to tumor location. A standardized five-port technique was used, with a 30° laparoscope inserted through the umbilical port. The main working port was positioned 2 cm below the xiphoid process, and auxiliary ports were placed along the right costal margin, right anterior axillary line, and left costal margin.

Exposure and isolation of the target hepatic pedicle

Using an extrahepatic Glissonean dissection technique guided by the Laennec membrane (“six gates” concept), the hepatoduodenal ligament was lowered to expose first-order pedicles. Based on preoperative 3D planning, the target segmental pedicle was carefully isolated using an ultrasonic scalpel while avoiding injury to adjacent structures [22, 23].

Pedicle clamping and ischemic boundary identification

The target hepatic pedicle was temporarily clamped with a bulldog clamp, with clamping time limited to ≤ 15 min. After 5–10 min, a clear ischemic demarcation line appeared on the liver surface and was marked using electrocautery to define the transection plane.

Parenchymal transection and tumor resection

Parenchymal transection was performed stepwise from the liver surface toward the deeper parenchyma along the ischemic boundary defined by the three-dimensional model, with priority given to small intraparenchymal vessels [19]. During transection, segmental and subsegmental Glissonean branches identified on the 3D reconstruction were sequentially exposed, clipped, and divided, while adjacent uninvolved pedicles were preserved. After complete resection, the specimen was retrieved in a protective bag through an enlarged umbilical incision. Intraoperative ultrasonography confirmed the absence of residual tumor.

Hemostasis and drainage

Minor bleeding was controlled using bipolar coagulation. Exposed portal vein branch stumps were reinforced with sutures when necessary. A closed suction drain was placed beneath the transection surface and removed once no bleeding or bile leakage was detected.

Hepatic vein-guided laparoscopic anatomical hepatectomy

This approach uses the hepatic vein as the primary anatomical landmark and follows the sequence: vein localization → transection plane definition → parenchymal transection along the vein.

Vein localization and transection plane planning

Patient positioning and port placement were similar to the pedicle-guided approach, with minor adjustments based on the target hepatic vein. The hepatic vein was localized using intraoperative ultrasonography combined with preoperative 3D reconstruction, typically at a depth of 1–3 cm from the liver surface. A thin layer of superficial parenchyma was divided to expose the venous wall.

Based on the spatial relationship between the tumor and hepatic vein shown on the 3D model, the hepatic vein was defined as the boundary for the transection plane and marked on the liver surface [24].

Parenchymal transection and vein preservation

Parenchymal transection proceeded toward the hepatic vein using an ultrasonic scalpel. Venous branches < 2 mm were coagulated, whereas branches ≥ 2 mm were clipped and divided. Upon exposure of the main hepatic vein, a vessel loop was used for gentle traction to prevent thermal injury. If the tumor invaded the vein wall, only the involved portion was resected and the remaining vein was repaired to preserve drainage. Intraoperative ultrasonography was used to confirm patency of the preserved hepatic vein and venous continuity was restored to preserve drainage [25].

Hemostasis and drainage

Venous branch stumps were sutured to prevent bleeding or air embolism. Hemostasis and abdominal drainage were performed similarly to the pedicle-guided approach, with particular attention to the hepatic vein region.

Validation of 3D reconstruction

To validate the accuracy and clinical relevance of the 3D reconstruction, intraoperative findings and postoperative specimens were compared with the preoperative 3D model. The consistency between the simulated resection plane and actual tumor boundaries was confirmed by intraoperative ultrasound and macroscopic examination of the resected specimen (Fig. 3). This process ensured that the 3D reconstruction accurately reflected the real hepatic anatomy and guided precise tumor excision.

Surgical team and quality control

All procedures were performed by the same experienced surgical team. Preoperative 3D planning, intraoperative navigation, and postoperative verification were applied consistently to ensure procedural standardization and reproducibility.

Data acquisition and postoperative monitoring

Patient data were retrospectively collected from clinical database of Shenzhen People’s Hospital. Preoperative Data that was retrieved from medical records included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), routine blood tests results, liver function assessments, tumor markers, and infectious disease screenings. Intraoperative data obtained from surgical and anesthesia records included the type of surgical procedure, operative time, intraoperative blood loss, and blood transfusion status. Pathological data from pathology reports including tumor number, size, volume, the degree of differentiation. Tumor staging was determined according to the 8th edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM classification for hepatocellular carcinoma [26]. Postoperative data collected from hospital records included laboratory results on postoperative day five, duration of hospital stays, and postoperative complications. Follow-up data included recurrence-free survival and overall survival [14, 21].

In general, follow-up information was obtained from the hospital’s electronic system and verified via telephone contact every 3 months. High-risk cases included patients with TNM stage III or above, tumor diameter > 5 cm, poorly or undifferentiated histology, or the presence of satellite lesions or lymph node metastasis. For non-high-risk cases, ultrasound and AFP testing were performed every 3 months, while enhanced CT or MRI, together with chest X-ray or CT, were conducted every 6 months within 2 years after surgery. The time to recurrence or death was recorded, and for patient lost to follow-up, the date of last contact was documented [18]. The following covariates were collected and later used for propensity score estimation: gender, age, body mass index (BMI), liver cirrhosis (Texture), HBV infection, hemoglobin (HGB), albumin (ALB), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total bilirubin (TB), prothrombin time (PT), alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), tumor number, and tumor volume. Because of the very limited number of cases with hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection in our center, this variable was not included in the present analysis. The primary outcomes assessed were recurrence-free survival and overall survival between the two patient cohorts [14].

Postoperative complications were defined and classified according to the Clavien-Dindo classification system, which categorizes complications based on the required therapeutic interventions [27]. Minor complications (Grade I-II) were managed conservatively or with simple medication, while major complications (Grade ≥ III) required invasive procedures, intensive care, or were life-threatening.

Statistical analysis

To reduce selection bias inherent in this retrospective study, propensity score matching (PSM) model was performed. Propensity scores were estimated using a logistic regression model that included variables such as age, sex, BMI, liver function indicators, and tumor characteristics. Patients in the hepatic vein and hepatic pedicle groups were matched 1:1 using the nearest-neighbor method with a caliper of 0.2, without replacement. This approach minimized potential confounding factors and improved the comparability between the two surgical groups (Tables 1 and 2).

Quantitative variables were expressed as means ± standard deviations or median (ranges), depending on whether the distribution, and categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between groups were conducted using the independent-samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables, and Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Survival analyses were performed using the Kaplan-Meier method, with differences compared by the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression model was used to identify prognostic factors, with results reported as hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using SPSS 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Patients characteristics

A total of 82 patients with hepatocellular carcinoma were included in the study, comprising 42 patients in the hepatic vein-guided group and 40 patients in the hepatic pedicle group. As shown in Table 1, baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were generally comparable between the two groups. However, a significant difference was observed in AFP level.

After 1:1 propensity score matching, 78 patients remained in the final analysis, including 41 patients in the hepatic vein-guided group and 37 patients in the hepatic pedicle group. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were well balanced between the two groups, with no statistically significant differences observed across measured variables (Table 2).

Laparoscopic anatomical hepatectomy

In this study, we selected the same group of surgeons with rich experience in liver surgery to perform the operation. Taking 3D reconstruction anatomical hepatectomy based on hepatic pedicle for HCC as an example, the specific surgical procedures were as follows: according to different preoperative 3D visualization models, the surgical model with more normal liver function was selected; Partial hepatic parenchymal resection was performed along the resection range according to the surgical plan, and the bleeding was stopped carefully at the cut surface. The pathological diagnosis of the specimen was HCC.

Perioperative comparison (Table 3)

The hepatic pedicle group demonstrated intraoperative outcomes compared with the hepatic vein group. Blood loss was significantly lower in the hepatic pedicle group (200 mL [IQR 100–280] vs. 250 mL [IQR 150–400], P = 0.033). Although the rates of blood transfusion rate (5.4% vs. 9.8%, P = 0.768) and conversion to laparotomy (0 vs. 4.9%, P = 0.520) were lower in the hepatic pedicle group, these differences were not statistically significant. The mean operation time was longer in the hepatic pedicle group (73.7 ± 76.9 min vs. 240.7 ± 86.2 min, P = 0.08), but the difference was not significant.

Short-term and long-term postoperative outcomes (Table 3)

Within 5 days postoperatively, the hepatic pedicle group showed significantly lower median ALT (79.0 IU/L [IQR 64.0–97.5] vs. 98.0 IU/L [IQR 73.5–152.5], P = 0.022), AST (59.0 IU/L [IQR 44.0–77.0] vs. 68.0 IU/L [IQR 47.0–125.5], P = 0.036), and a lower incidence of operation-related complications (8.1% vs. 26.8%, P = 0.031) compared with the hepatic vein group (Table 2). According to the Clavien-Dindo classification, minor complications, including pleural effusion, wound infection, and pulmonary infection, occurred in 12.2% vs. 2.7% (P = 0.252) and major complications, including liver failure, abdominal hemorrhage, and bile leakage, in 14.6% vs. 5.4% (P = 0.333) of patients in the hepatic vein and hepatic pedicle groups, respectively, with no statistically significant differences between groups.Pathological outcomes are summarized in Supplementary Table 3. The R0 resection rate was 82.9% in the hepatic vein-guided group and 91.9% in the hepatic pedicle group (P = 0.399). The median surgical margin distance was significantly wider in the hepatic pedicle group (1.2 cm [IQR 1.1–1.3]) than in the hepatic vein-guided group (0.9 cm [IQR 0.8–1.2], P = 0.016). The hepatic pedicle-guided approach was associated with a significantly wider surgical margin and a numerically higher R0 resection rate compared with the hepatic vein-guided approach, suggesting potentially improved oncologic clearance, although the difference in R0 status did not reach statistical significance.

Among the 78 patients, 27 (34.6%) experienced recurrence and 20 (25.6%) died during follow-up. The median follow-up duration was 36 months in both groups, with no significant difference between the hepatic vein-guided and hepatic pedicle groups (P = 0.355). The 3-year recurrence-free survival rate was 75.7% in the hepatic pedicle group and 56.1% in the hepatic vein group, while the overall survival rates were 78.4% and 70.7%, respectively. Recurrence-free survival was significantly longer in the hepatic pedicle group (HR = 0.445, P = 0.040), whereas the difference in overall survival did not reach statistical significance (HR = 0.659, P = 0.355) (Fig. 4).

Discussion

With continuous advances in medical imaging, 3D reconstruction has become an essential tool for surgical planning. By converting 2D images into accurate 3D models, it enhances visualization of complex hepatic structures and supports precise preoperative assessment, intraoperative navigation, and postoperative evaluation [28–30]. In hepatobiliary surgery, 3D reconstruction helps define vascular territories and predict blood supply, optimizing resection while preserving functional parenchyma. The choice between portal vein- or hepatic pedicle-based 3D analysis depends on tumor location and vascular anatomy [31].

In laparoscopic hepatectomy, defining the correct plane of liver dissection is essential for achieving anatomical resection. The hepatic vein system serves as the liver’s primary outflow tract, with the middle hepatic vein (MHV) forming the natural boundary between the left and right lobes and draining with the left medial and right anterior segments [24, 32, 33]. In hepatic vein-based hepatectomy, the MHV is used as a critical anatomical landmark for defining the resection plane, ensuring precise removal of tumor-bearing venous territories while preserving adequate venous drainage in the remaining liver. Based on the “cone unit” theory, the hepatic pedicle approach in laparoscopic anatomical hepatectomy enables precise management of the Glisson hepatic pedicle (including portal vein, hepatic artery and bile duct) of the target liver segment. By achieving regional blood flow occlusion, this method determines the ischemic boundary and guides the dissection plane with high accuracy. Using Laennec membrane as an anatomical landmark and employing an extrathecal dissection technique, it minimizes parenchymal injury and has demonstrated excellent efficacy in laparoscopic liver segmentectomy [22, 23, 34].

The impact of surgical techniques on prognosis remains contentious. Although follow-up did not reach 5 years, three-year RFS and OS were selected as primary survival endpoints because they reflect the period during which most recurrences occur and are widely accepted as meaningful mid-term outcomes in hepatocellular carcinoma surgery. In this study, the hepatic pedicle group exhibited less postoperative elevation in ALT and AST levels than the hepatic vein group, indicating reduced hepatic injury and better postoperative liver function. Additionally, the hepatic pedicle approach was associated with less intraoperative blood loss (by approximately 50 ml, P = 0.033), a higher recurrence-free survival rate (by about 19%, P = 0.040), and a modest improvement in overall survival (by around 8%) [35, 36]. This may be explained by its ability to achieve true anatomic resection along the portal territories, allowing complete excision of the tumor-bearing inflow area. By clearly defining ischemic boundaries, this approach minimizes residual microscopic disease and reduces intrahepatic metastasis risk. In contrast, the hepatic vein-based approach focuses on venous drainage preservation but may not fully encompass the tumor’s portal inflow region, potentially increasing the risk of recurrence. These differences in resection logic may explain the substantial survival benefit observed in the hepatic pedicle group. In hepatic vein-based hepatectomy, recurrence most commonly occurs intrahepatically along the branches of the portal vein, which is the main route for intrahepatic metastasis [25, 37]. Because this approach disregards the classic segmental classification of Makunouchi and Couinaud, it may fail to fully remove the portal territory of the affected segment, leading to recurrence in adjacent areas [38]. Previous reports indicate that 5-year recurrence rates after hepatectomy can reach 60–80%, with intrahepatic recurrence being the dominant pattern [39]. Clinically, vein-based hepatectomy has value in selected cases where tumors invade major hepatic veins. It allows safe ligation of the involved venous trunk while preserving uninvolved segment (such as IV, V, or VIII), and maintaining venous drainage, thus reducing intraoperative bleeding and postoperative liver failure [40, 41]. These differences emphasize that while vein-based resection may be appropriate in complex cases, pedicle-guided hepatectomy provides more complete anatomical and oncologic clearance. Several recent studies have further confirmed the survival benefits of anatomical or pedicle-based resection for HCC. Wang et al. [42].demonstrated that laparoscopic anatomical segmental and subsegmental hepatectomy, guided by precise Glissonean pedicle dissection through the Laennec membrane, enables accurate and safe resection across liver segments S1-S8. Their study confirmed that pedicle-based anatomy improves surgical accuracy and standardization, aligning closely with our results. Similarly, Kang et al. [43] reported that AR achieves superior perioperative and long-term survival outcomes compared to non-anatomical approaches by ensuring complete removal of tumor-bearing portal territories. A meta-analysis of 22 propensity score-matched studies demonstrated that AR significantly improved both overall survival and recurrence-free survival, particularly in patients with tumors ≤ 5 cm and non-cirrhotic livers [44]. Furthermore, Viganò et al. [45] observed that compression technique anatomic resection achieved higher 5-year survival (77% vs. 60%) and lower local recurrence (4% vs. 20%) compared to non-anatomic resection. Here, we found that the hepatic pedicle approach was associated with a significantly wider surgical margin and a numerically higher R0 resection rate, although differences in R0 status did not reach statistical significance. Collectively, these findings suggest that the hepatic pedicle-guided approach enhances oncological clearance and long-term survival by enabling precise removal of tumor-bearing territories while preserving healthy parenchyma.

In routine clinical practice, combined landmark strategies incorporating portal territory guidance and hepatic vein landmarks are commonly used during laparoscopic hepatectomy. In the present study, hepatic pedicle-guided and hepatic vein-guided approaches were analyzed separately to enable a standardized comparison of two core anatomical guidance principles under a unified three-dimensional reconstruction framework. The observed advantages of hepatic pedicle-guided resection in terms of reduced blood loss and improved recurrence-free survival suggest that, when combined landmark strategies are employed, portal pedicle guidance may serve as the primary determinant of the resection territory, with hepatic vein guidance acting as an adjunct to optimize safety margins. This interpretation is consistent with contemporary expert consensus recommendations on laparoscopic portal territory-guided anatomical hepatectomy [46].

The main limitation of this study is the relatively prolonged operative duration, which may increase the risk of postoperative complications, thrombosis, and an elevated risk of anesthesia-related issues. Nevertheless, the rate of postoperative complications did not differ significantly between the two groups. It is also important to acknowledge this retrospective study may be subject to selection bias, as the surgical approach was chosen based on tumor size, location, and vascular involvement. The limited sample size may further restrict generalizability. Future multicenter prospective studies with larger cohorts and extended follow-up are needed to validate these findings. Because the follow-up duration did not reach 5 years, late recurrences of hepatocellular carcinoma may not have been fully captured. Although the study was underpowered to detect statistically significant differences in rare outcomes, the magnitude and consistency of the observed difference in major complications suggest potential clinical relevance and warrant validation in larger, prospective cohorts. Future multicenter prospective studies with larger cohorts and extended follow-up are therefore needed to confirm these results. Microvascular invasion in the remnant liver could not be evaluated because standardized pathological assessment of non-tumorous liver tissue was not routinely available, representing an important limitation of this study. Additionally, hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection was not analyzed due to its low prevalence in our cohort. Other potential prognostic factors, such as patient lifestyle, genetic predisposition, and adjuvant therapy variations, were not included and warrant further investigation [17].

Conclusions

Under a unified preoperative three-dimensional reconstruction framework, hepatic pedicle-guided laparoscopic anatomical hepatectomy was safe and feasible and was associated with reduced intraoperative blood loss, better postoperative liver function preservation, and improved recurrence-free survival compared with hepatic vein-guided resection. These findings underscore the role of three-dimensional reconstruction in supporting precise and reproducible anatomical liver resection in laparoscopic surgery.
