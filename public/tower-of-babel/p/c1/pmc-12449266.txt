Apoplastic metabolomics reveals sugars as mesophyll messengers regulating guard cell ion transport under red light
Authors: Yotam Zait, Mengmeng Zhu, Eigo Ando, Yunqing Zhou, Adi Yaaran, Sunheng Yon, Mami Okamoto, Yuki Hayashi, Masami Y Hirai, Timothy Jegla, Toshinori Kinoshita, Sixue Chen, Sarah M Assmann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGuard cell pairs in the leaf epidermis enclose stomata, microscopic pores mediating CO2 uptake and water loss. Historical data suggest that signals from interior mesophyll tissue may modulate guard-cell regulation of stomatal apertures, but the molecular identity of any metabolite-based signals has remained elusive. We discovered that extracellular (apoplastic) fluid from Arabidopsis thaliana and Vicia faba enhances red-light-induced stomatal opening. Our extensive metabolomics analyses identified 448 apoplastic metabolites; among these, both sugars (photosynthetic products) and maleic acid increased under red light and caused enhanced stomatal opening. Immunohistochemical assays demonstrated sucrose upregulation of H+-ATPase phosphorylation, indicating increased ATPase activity. Patch clamp assays revealed that sucrose inhibits slow anion currents, thus opposing anion efflux. These impacts occurred at sucrose concentrations matching those present endogenously under red light. These regulatory influences promote guard-cell solute import and retention, which drive stomatal opening. Our research thus addresses the decades-long question concerning the existence, identity and mechanistic impact of mesophyll messengers that coordinate photosynthesis with stomatal response.Subject terms: Plant signalling, Extracellular signalling molecules

Main

Mesophyll cells and guard cells are both indispensable components of plant leaves. Mesophyll cells comprise the majority of leaf cells, with a primary function in photosynthesis. Under illumination, there is increased photosynthetic demand for atmospheric CO2, which enters the leaf through microscopic stomatal pores, delimited by surrounding guard cell pairs. Light induces stomatal opening by activation of guard-cell H+-ATPases and closure of anion efflux channels; these responses hyperpolarize the guard-cell plasma membrane, providing a driving force for K+ uptake through voltage-regulated inward K+ channels. Solute increase drives osmotic uptake of water, leading to guard-cell outbowing and pore opening, thus increasing stomatal conductance for both CO2 uptake and water vapour loss, with consequent impacts on photosynthesis and plant water status1–4.

Several decades ago, a remarkably close coordination was observed between the magnitude of stomatal conductance and mesophyll photosynthetic capacity in a wide range of species5—a relationship that has been confirmed in a recent meta-analysis6. One mechanism of coordination between mesophyll photosynthesis and stomatal conductance involves guard-cell sensing of intercellular CO2 concentrations7, which are directly altered by mesophyll carbon capture. However, classical studies have suggested that there is also metabolite-based signalling between guard cells and mesophyll cells, that is, a ‘mesophyll messenger’. For example, in 1992, enhanced stomatal responses to light were demonstrated in Commelina communis epidermal peels upon incubation with medium pre-conditioned with mesophyll cells8. In 2013, through insertion of permeable or impermeable physical barriers between isolated mesophyll and epidermis, evidence was provided for a mesophyll metabolite that promoted stomatal opening in response to low CO29. Exposure to mesophyll tissue from the same or different species can be equally effective in promoting stomatal responses to light and CO210,11, indicating cross-species conservation of the mesophyll signal(s). Some mesophyll-synthesized metabolites, for example, malate and fumarate, have been reported to negatively regulate stomatal opening under certain conditions12–15. However, the features and identity of mesophyll messengers, including those that open stomata, remain largely unknown.

Stomatal opening is stimulated by both blue and red light16–18. Although the specific blue-light response mediated by phototropin photoreceptors has greater quantum efficiency, it also saturates at low light intensities19. Thus, under natural conditions, non-phototropin-associated stomatal opening, which can be assayed as a red light response, is paramount, but has received less attention. It has been proposed that sensing of photosynthetically driven decreases in intercellular CO2 is the major driver of the red light response20–22; however, experiments in which stomatal opening occurs in response to red light in isolated epidermal peels19,23,24 refute this conclusion, establishing that there is guard-cell autonomous perception of red light. The phototropin-mediated response is saturated at low light intensities, and guard-cell photosynthesis is required for full stomatal opening18,19,25. Given the primacy of the latter stomatal response under light intensities that drive maximal photosynthesis, and that media from red-light-treated mesophyll is more effective in stimulating guard-cell swelling than media from blue-light-treated mesophyll26, we hypothesized that assessing apoplastic metabolites under red light could lead to identification of the elusive mesophyll messenger(s). Accordingly, we applied an integrated approach, combining physiological measurements, comprehensive metabolomics and ion transport assays, which revealed sugars and maleic acid as apoplastic mesophyll messengers that enhance stomatal apertures under illumination.

Results

Apoplastic fluid from illuminated leaves enhances stomatal opening

Vicia faba and Arabidopsis thaliana are both established model species in stomatal physiology. We adapted a centrifugation method27 for collection of plant apoplastic fluid and applied it to each species. The g force we used for collection was 3–5× lower than has been reported to suffice for avoidance of symplastic contamination28–30. Apoplastic fluid collected from V. faba leaves exposed to red light promoted red-light-induced stomatal opening in isolated V. faba epidermal peels (Fig. 1a), and similarly, A. thaliana apoplastic fluid enhanced stomatal opening in that species (Fig. 1b). Reciprocal experiments with apoplastic fluid from V. faba and A. thaliana indicated a cross-species effect of the red-light-induced apoplastic fluid on stomatal opening in both species (Fig. 1c,d). Apoplastic fluid was therefore considered to contain mesophyll messenger(s). Stomatal apertures were not enhanced in controls treated with the extraction solution alone or with apoplastic fluid obtained from leaves kept in darkness (Fig. 1a–d and Extended Data Fig. 1a).

Metabolomics analyses reveal candidate mesophyll messengers

Apoplastic fluid contains macromolecules (for example, proteins) and small molecules (for example, metabolites). To dissect this complex sample, we fractionated apoplastic fluid into <3 kDa and >3 kDa fractions. The small-molecule fraction (<3 kDa) had an effect in promoting stomatal opening under red light that was comparable to the entire non-fractionated mesophyll apoplastic fluid (Fig. 1e). Free ions, peptides and metabolites are expected components of the small-molecule fraction. Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) showed no significant difference in the ionome between dark- and red-light-induced V. faba apoplastic fluids (Supplementary Table 1). Furthermore, apoplastic fluid extracted from red light-treated V. faba and then subjected to heat denaturing (Fig. 1f) or protease treatments (Fig. 1g), produced comparable stimulation of red-light-induced stomatal opening as the untreated apoplastic fluid, suggesting that peptides were not involved. We therefore focused our search for the mesophyll messenger on small organic molecules. After a pre-test that confirmed the hydrophilic nature of the mesophyll messenger (Extended Data Fig. 1b,c), we applied reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and identified the fractions effective in enhancing red-light-induced stomatal opening. The effective fractions (Extended Data Fig. 1d,e) were combined for each species and subjected to metabolomics analyses.

We harnessed three complementary metabolomics platforms to characterize the apoplastic metabolomes of both V. faba and A. thaliana. Targeted gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) detected a total of 105 metabolites, targeted LC–MS/MS identified 293 metabolites, and untargeted GC–TOFMS identified 181 metabolites (Fig. 2a,b), with a total of 448 unique metabolites identified (Supplementary Tables 2–4). This is the most extensive description of the plant apoplastic metabolome so far30. The characterized apoplastic metabolomes of these two species are summarized in Fig. 2c,d, and we reasoned that mesophyll messengers would be represented in this list of compounds.

Due to the cross-species promoting effect of the apoplastic fluids, we first identified metabolites that exhibited the same trend of fold change in the red light vs dark metabolome across the two species (Supplementary Table 5). This list of 126 metabolites includes amino acids, carbohydrates, carboxylic acids, phytohormones and related metabolites, nucleotide-related compounds and signalling molecules. However, not all of the fold changes were statistically significant (P < 0.05). We next narrowed down candidates on the basis of: (1) the quantification results showing the same trend of fold change across multiple MS platforms, regardless of the P values in t-test; or (2) the quantification results from at least one platform showing statistical significance (P < 0.05 in t-test) for both A. thaliana and V. faba (Supplementary Table 5). Just 17 metabolites met these criteria; we chose to focus on hormone-related metabolites (threonic acid and 5-deoxy-5-methylthio-adenosine) and, on the basis of previous stomatal literature, the hormone-related metabolite methyl indole acetic acid31–33 and primary metabolites (the sugars sorbose, sucrose, fructose, glucose; fumarate and its isomer maleic acid34; and malate) (Fig. 2e and Extended Data Fig. 2).

Sugars and maleic acid enhance red-light-induced stomatal opening

We assessed three hormone-related metabolites: threonic acid, which is induced by overexpression of the jasmonate transducing protein, CORONATINE INSENSITIVE1 (ref. 35), and arises from ascorbate metabolism36; the auxin methyl indole acetic acid (ME-IAA); and 5′-methyl-thioadenosine, which is produced by metabolic pathways synthesizing ethylene, nicotinamide and polyamines and is recycled back to methionine in the Yang pathway37. Of these, only 5′-methyl-thioadenosine affected apertures, and only in V. faba (Extended Data Fig. 2). Among primary metabolites, 1 mM maleic acid, an isomer of the tricarboxylic acid cycle metabolite fumarate that increases in A. thaliana leaves under high light34, enhanced stomatal opening in both species. Fumarate14 or malate12 at 1 mM concentration had no effect under our conditions (Extended Data Fig. 2).

Most significantly, low mM concentrations of sucrose, fructose, glucose and sorbose consistently promoted stomatal opening in both species (Fig. 3a–d, and Extended Data Figs. 2 and 3a,b). Apoplastic sucrose concentrations have been reported to range from 0.4 mM to 5.5 mM (refs. 28,38,39), that is, the same concentration range at which we detect enhancement of stomatal opening (Figs. 3 and 4). Given that sugars, unlike maleic acid, are primary products of photosynthesis, and thus appealing candidates for mesophyll–guard-cell coordination, we targeted them for detailed analyses. Fructose and sucrose elicited a similar dose–response curve in both A. thaliana and V. faba (Fig. 3a–d), as expected for a genuine mesophyll messenger (Fig. 1). In both species, sugar regulation of the red-light responses was non-monotonic, with low concentrations showing the greatest enhancement and higher concentrations showing little to no enhancement of stomatal opening (Fig. 3a–d). We posit that at higher solute concentrations there is a biophysical effect in which water is withdrawn from guard cells due to the low apoplastic osmotic potential arising from the high external sugar concentration; this opposes guard-cell inflation and stomatal opening.

We applied GC–MS/selective reaction monitoring (SRM) to directly measure glucose, fructose and sucrose concentrations in V. faba apoplastic fluid over a time course. We observed that all three sugars increased in concentration over the duration of red-light exposure. Sucrose showed the most dramatic increase (Fig. 3e), with mean concentrations of 0.36 mM after 60 min and 1.9 mM after 120 min, matching the concentrations (Fig. 3c,d) and time course (Extended Data Fig. 3c) at which we observed enhancement of stomatal opening by exogenous sucrose.

We focused on sucrose in subsequent experiments. Sucrose also enhanced red-light-induced stomatal opening in intact V. faba leaves. A 1 mM concentration of sucrose fed through the petiole increased stomatal conductance in gas exchange experiments under red light (Fig. 3f) but not darkness (Extended Data Fig. 3d), consistent with the positive impact observed in isolated epidermal peels (Fig. 3c,d and Extended Data Fig. 3c). By contrast, the metabolically inactive sugars, mannitol and sorbitol, failed to enhance stomatal conductance or stomatal opening in epidermal peels (Figs. 3f and 4a). As expected for an osmotic effect, a high (100 mM) concentration of sucrose or mannitol depressed stomatal conductance (Fig. 3e), mirroring the impact of high sugar concentrations on stomatal apertures (Figs. 3a–d and 4a).

Notably, guard-cell photosynthesis was not required for sucrose enhancement: N-(3,4-dichlorophenyl)-N-dimethylurea (DCMU), an inhibitor of Photosystem II, did not prevent exogenous sucrose from enhancing stomatal apertures in isolated epidermal peels (Fig. 4b); however, as expected from previous data implicating guard-cell chloroplasts in the stomatal red-light response19,25, DCMU did reduce overall stomatal apertures in isolated epidermal peels under red light, as previously observed23,24,40. A role of PhyB in red-light promotion of stomatal opening has been proposed41; however, at least under our conditions, we did not observe a difference in the sucrose effect on stomatal apertures in epidermal peels from phyB mutants vs Col-0 (Fig. 4c), or significant differences in stomatal conductance responses to red light in phyB mutants vs wild-type Col-0 (Fig. 4d). These results are consistent with a report in which guard-cell-specific knockdown of phyB did not affect stomatal responses40.

Previous reports in the literature42 have suggested a role for hexokinase as a guard-cell sugar sensor. However, neither treatment with N-acetyl-glucosamine (NAG), a pharmacological inhibitor of hexokinase43, nor genetic overexpression of hexokinase42 impacted sucrose enhancement of the guard-cell red-light response (Fig. 4e,f), ruling out a role for hexokinase.

Guard-cell ion transporters are regulated by sucrose

Our data suggest a specific role for the products of photosynthesis as mesophyll messengers. While this has been previously hypothesized, previous reports have promoted sucrose as a source of energy and/or carbon skeletons18,44. In contrast, our further findings reveal regulatory effects of sugars on the activity of guard-cell ion transporters. During stomatal opening, H+ extrusion by guard-cell H+-ATPases hyperpolarizes the membrane potential45, both creating a driving force for K+ uptake and activating voltage-gated inward K+ channels. Consequent K+ influx osmotically drives water influx, guard-cell swelling and stomatal opening. We tested whether the proton pump is involved in sugar enhancement of stomatal apertures by evaluating the sucrose effect in a T-DNA insertional mutant46 of the major guard-cell plasma membrane H+-ATPase, AHA1. Overall levels of stomatal opening were reduced in the aha1-6 mutant, as expected (Fig. 5a). The magnitude of the sucrose enhancement was also diminished, implicating the H+-ATPase as a component in the sucrose effect. H+-ATPase phosphorylation is critical for H+-ATPase activation under blue-light-stimulated stomatal opening mediated by phototropin photoreceptors47,48. Red light has been observed to stimulate phosphorylation of the guard-cell H+-ATPase in a fluence-dependent manner but only in intact leaves, suggesting the obligate involvement of a mesophyll messenger49,50. Remarkably, exogenous sucrose at a low pH, typical of the apoplast51, was able to stimulate H+-ATPase phosphorylation even in isolated guard cells (Fig. 5b,c and Extended Data Fig. 3e), consistent with sucrose as a key mesophyll messenger. We observed that sucrose-induced H+-ATPase phosphorylation was significant at 1 mM sucrose and saturated at 30 mM sucrose (Fig. 5d), and the phosphorylation time course at 1 mM sucrose (Fig. 5e) was comparable to the time course of stomatal conducance enhancement by sucrose (Fig. 3f).

The enhancement of stomatal apertures by sucrose was not completely eliminated in the AHA1 H+-ATPase mutant (Fig. 5a). We next tested the hypothesis that sucrose inhibits opening of SLAC1 anion channels. Anion channels such as SLAC1, when activated, mediate anion efflux and consequent membrane depolarization, opposing inward K+ channel voltage activation and K+ influx and directly resulting in anion efflux; the combined effect drives guard-cell water loss that opposes stomatal opening. Pharmacological knockout of slow anion currents with the anion channel blocker anthracene-9-carboxylic acid (9-AC)52 eliminated the sucrose enhancement effect in isolated epidermal peels (Fig. 6a), suggesting that SLAC1 inhibition is a key component of the sucrose response. Consistent with this result, genetic knockout of SLAC1 by T-DNA mutagenesis (Fig. 6b) essentially eliminated the sucrose enhancement effect, both in isolated epidermal peels and intact A. thaliana leaves (Fig. 6b,c). These results indicate that anion channels function as negative regulators of stomatal opening, consistent with our previous observation that anion channel inhibition by 9-AC opposes ABA inhibition of stomatal opening52. Similarly, inhibition of slow anion channels has also been observed to promote blue-light-stimulated stomatal opening53.

Discussion

Although major advances have been made to understand the roles of various factors responsible for regulation of stomatal opening, we are still far from understanding the entirety of this process. Red-light illumination has a major role in regulation of stomatal movement17,19, with guard-cell chlorophyll23,25,49,54 clearly implicated in the response and a role of phytochrome B under debate40,41,55. Alternatively, it has been proposed that red-light-induced stomatal opening is not a photobiological effect, but rather an indirect effect mediated by a photosynthetically induced decrease in intercellular carbon concentration (Ci), which guard cells sense, resulting in an increase in stomatal aperture20. However, this conclusion has been contested on the basis of other studies indicating the presence of a response to red light independent of intercellular CO2 concentrations56–58. In previous work, we discovered that red-light-induced stomatal opening in Arabidopsis is weak or absent if the plants are grown in a low-light, low-humidity environment24, which may help to explain previous suggestions that guard cells do not directly sense red light.

Key mechanistic information on the red-light response49,50 shows that red light enhances phosphorylation of the H+-ATPase, a post-translational modification known to activate proton pumping and thus promote stomatal opening. Interestingly, this effect could only be detected in intact leaves, where it was inhibited by DCMU, suggesting a possible role for the mesophyll in this response. Indeed, the possibility of mesophyll cell metabolite(s) acting as ‘mesophyll messengers’ in stomatal regulation has been discussed for decades5,8–10, particularly in the context of red-light-induced stomatal movements9,26.

Our present work identifies some of these mesophyll messengers. Ions, particularly Ca2+, have been identified as long-distance signals59 with substantive impacts on stomatal apertures60, and an increasing number of apoplastic peptides have been identified as ligands for receptor-like kinases61. However, we found no evidence supporting ions (Supplementary Table 1) or peptides (Fig. 1f,g) as mesophyll messengers, despite their regulatory roles in other stomatal responses62,63.

We next turned to analysis of the apoplastic metabolomes of both A. thaliana and V. faba, producing a comprehensive dataset as a rich resource for future studies of apoplastic signalling (Fig. 2a–c). According to the Metabolomics Standards Initiative criteria64,65, we have achieved the most rigorous identification (‘level 1’; that is, with authentic standards) for 293 metabolites, and level 2 identification (that is, with fragmentation spectra matching spectral libraries) for the remaining 155 metabolites. Our GC–MS/SRM experiments identified sucrose, fructose and glucose at level 1. The total of 448 unique metabolites from the combined A. thaliana and V. faba datasets (Fig. 2a) is the largest so far; by comparison, ref. 30 used one platform (GC–MS) and identified the same three sugars at level 1 and 51 apoplastic metabolites at level 2; of these, 41 metabolites were also detected in our metabolomes.

Our multiplatform metabolomic analyses showed manifold changes in apoplastic metabolites in both A. thaliana and V. faba after red-light treatment (Supplementary Tables 2–5), including in malate and fumarate. In early research, 82 mM malate was shown to activate QUAC-type (rapid) anion channels of V. faba12, implicating a role of high malate concentrations in stomatal closure rather than stomatal opening. Consistent with that conclusion, we did observe that 100 mM malate inhibited stomatal opening (Extended Data Fig. 2), although this could be an osmotic effect. We also observed that red light elevated apoplastic malate concentrations in V. faba (Fig. 2e), leaving open the possibility that a regulatory role of this metabolite is species dependent. Fumarate at 1 mM had no effect in either V. faba and A. thaliana (Extended Data Fig. 2), consistent with a previous report of 5 mM fumarate having no effect on stomatal apertures under white light in tomato, although that report found that fumarate at 10–20 mM promoted stomatal closure in that species14.

Among metabolites meeting our selection criteria, only maleic acid (Extended Data Fig. 2) and sugars enhanced stomatal opening in both plant species. Interestingly, a previous report estimated apoplastic maleic acid in P. vulgaris at 0.45 mM (ref. 66), suggesting that our 1 mM maleic acid treatment is within the physiological range, although the molecular mechanism underlying its effect on stomatal apertures remains to be elucidated. Given their clear association with photosynthetic output, we focused on sugars as key mesophyll messengers. We showed that their effect extends across two species (Figs. 2–4 and Extended Data Fig. 3a,b), consistent with the cross-specific effect of apoplastic fluid (Fig. 1) and the previously reported cross-species effect of mesophyll signals10,11. This suggests that the sugar effect may be universal across species, as also observed for the photosynthesis–stomatal conductance relationship first uncovered in ref. 5. It is notable that sugars as mesophyll messengers provide a direct ‘read-out’ of mesophyll photosynthetic rates. Reported apoplastic sucrose concentrations are 0.4–5.5 mM (refs. 28,38,39), matching the range at which we detected enhancement of stomatal opening (Figs. 3–5a). Our own quantification of endogenous apopolastic sugars confirmed similar sugar concentrations (Fig. 3e). Sugars increased over a red-light time course, with sucrose in particular showing elevated values (Fig. 3e). Sucrose administration did not suffice to cause stomatal opening in darkness (Extended Data Fig. 3d), suggesting that the sugars are not simply providing energy to fuel stomatal opening, as was previously proposed18,44. Indeed, we observed two regulatory effects of sugars. First, sucrose promoted phosphorylation of the H+-ATPase (Fig. 5), which increases H+-ATPase activity47–49. In fact, this sucrose effect may explain why red light fails to induce H+-ATPase phosphorylation in isolated epidermal peels49. Second, sucrose inhibited slow anion channels (Fig. 6), an effect that opposes both anion loss and the depolarization arising from anion efflux. Our data show that the Arabidopsis SLAC1 slow anion channel plays a major role in the sucrose response (Fig. 6b,c); it is possible that the other Arabidopsis slow anion channel, SLAH3 (ref. 67), also makes a minor contribution. Activation, not inhibition, of slow anion channels has been observed to require ATP68. Moreover, an insightful early study69 deduced that the requirement for ATP reflects a role of phosphorylation in channel regulation rather than a role of ATP as an energy source, and phospho-regulation of SLAC1 is now well documented1,70,71. Therefore, we conclude that apoplastic sugars play a genuine signalling role in stomatal aperture regulation. Both H+-ATPase activation and anion channel closure would promote K+ influx through hyperpolarization-activated K+ channels, a central process driving stomatal opening.

We observed that higher concentrations of sugars are not effective in enhancing stomatal opening. Although we strictly cannot rule out a signalling role of sucrose in guard-cell deflation, as proposed in refs. 42,72, our data imply that results at higher sugar concentrations arise from a biophysical, that is, osmotic effect, as this phenomenon is also observed with high concentrations of the metabolically inactive sugars mannitol (Fig. 3e) and sorbitol (Fig. 4a); analogous impacts of mannitol have been observed in F. excelsior leaves73. Indeed, high sucrose concentrations accumulating around transpiring guard cells have been hypothesized to constitute an osmotic feedback mechanism limiting transpirational water loss, for example, under high vapour pressure difference38,39, and we did observe such inhibition by 100 mM sucrose or mannitol in gas exchange experiments (Fig. 3f).

In summary, we posit that at low apoplastic sucrose concentrations, sucrose enhancement of stomatal opening reduces CO2 limitation on photosynthesis, while at high apoplastic sugar concentrations, indicative of photosynthetic saturation, further stomatal opening is opposed. Thus, stomatal apertures are fine tuned to an optimal level that provides sufficient CO2 for photosynthesis without excessive water loss. Our experiments have identified sugars as mesophyll messengers which, via activation of the guard-cell H+-ATPase and inhibition of slow anion channels, promote stomatal opening under red-light conditions conducive for photosynthesis. Identification of the signalling cascade between sucrose perception and ion transport regulation, mechanistic explanation of the maleic acid response and assessment of the roles of the hundreds of other apoplastic metabolites we have identified, including the potential identification of additional mesophyll messengers, will be intriguing topics for future study.

Methods

Plant growth

A. thaliana seeds were plated on plates containing half-strength Murashige and Skoog (MS) basal salt mixture along with 10 g l−1 sucrose and stratified at 4 °C for 10 days to promote a high rate and synchrony of germination. Seedlings were then transplanted to soil (Metro-Mix 360 SC and Sunshine Mix #1, mix at 1:1, Sun Gro) in trays (21″ × 10.5″ × 2.5″, Thunder Acres; 40 seedlings per tray).

For apoplastic fluid collection, A. thaliana plants were grown in a growth chamber with 8 h/16 h day/night light cycles at a white light intensity of 125 µmol m−2 s−1. Plants were watered once a week. A. thaliana used for stomatal bioassays in response to red light were grown in a growth chamber with an 8 h/16 h day/night light cycle at a white light intensity of 300 µmol m−2 s−1 and 22 °C/20 °C day/night temperature cycles and relative humidity of 50–60%. Plants were watered twice a week. Fully expanded leaves from ~4-week-old plants were used.

V. faba plants were grown individually in pots (4″ × 4″ × 3.5″; Greenhouse Megastore) filled with soil (Metro-Mix 360, Sun Gro). For apoplastic fluid collection and red-light stomatal assays, V. faba plants were grown in a growth chamber with 8 h/16 h day/night light cycles at a white light intensity of 125 µmol m−2 s−1 and 22 °C/20 °C day/night temperature cycles. Plants were watered once a week. Fully expanded leaves from ~3-week-old plants were used.

Stomatal bioassays in response to red light

Leaves were excised before lights turned on in the chamber. Epidermal peels were prepared following the method of ref. 74. Epidermal peels were incubated in 5 mM KCl, 1 mM CaCl2 and 10 mM MES-KOH at pH 6.15 in the dark for 1.5–2 h to ensure stomatal closure. Then peels were transferred to a solution containing 5 mM KCl, 0.1 mM CaCl2 (as mock control) or tested sample (for example, red-light-induced apoplastic fluid or fractions from apoplastic fluid; Fig. 1 and Extended Data Fig. 1a).

For tests of selected metabolites, epidermal peels were transferred to a solution containing 5 mM KCl, 0.1 mM CaCl2, 10 mM MES-KOH at pH 6.15 as mock opening solution, apoplastic fluid obtained from plants treated with red light as control, or to the solutions containing metabolites being tested. The metabolites were dissoved at various concentrations in a buffer containing 5 mM KCl, 0.1 mM CaCl2 and 10 mM MES-KOH at pH 6.15.

Peels were placed under red light provided by an ABI LED red light bulb (JacobsParts) at an intensity of 125 μmol m−2 s−1. The emission spectrum of this red-light source is given in Extended Data Fig. 5. Stomata were imaged after 3 h red-light treatment or over the indicated time course (Extended Data Fig. 3c) by light microscopy (Nikon Diaphot 300 or Revolve ECHO Microscope, Model R4 with attached camera (Nikon E990)). Stomatal aperture measurements were performed using Fiji (ImageJ2 v.2.14.0; https://fiji.sc). Image acquisition and analysis were performed blinded. At least three independent biological replicates were performed for each treatment.

Emission spectrum of the red light source.

The ABI® LED red light source was quantified using a LI-180 Spectrometer (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA).

Metabolites that were tested were: fructose (Sigma-Aldrich, 57-48-7), malic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, 97-67-6), sorbose (Sigma-Aldrich, 87-79-6), threonic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, 7306-96-9), methyl indole acetate (Sigma-Aldrich, 1912-48-7), 5′-deoxy-5′methylthio-adenosine (Sigma-Aldrich, 2457-80-9), maleic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, 110-16-7), fructose, glucose and sucrose.

Gas exchange measurements were conducted using an LI-6800 portable gas-exchange system (LI-COR) on petiole-fed leaves of 5-week-old plants. Petioles were immersed in buffer solutions containing 0, 1 or 100 mM of either sucrose or mannitol, and excised in-solution to prevent embolism formation. Leaves were kept in the buffer with only the petioles submerged for 1 h in the dark to acclimate. Afterwards, they were clamped in the LI-6800 chamber with a 2 cm2 leaf chamber, CO2 concentration of 420 µmol mol−1, leaf temperature of 25 °C for V. faba and 22 °C for Arabidopsis, relative humidity maintained at 60% and a flow rate of 500 μmol s⁻¹. Following 30 min in the dark, leaves were exposed to 400 μmol m⁻² s⁻¹ of 100% red light (λ 620–750 nm) for 1 h. Stomatal conductance (gsw) was recorded every minute using the LI-COR autologging mode.

Apoplastic fluid preparation from A. thaliana and V. faba

Apoplastic fluids were prepared as previously described27, with modifications. Plants were placed under red light at an intensity of 125 μmol m−2 s−1 for 3 h. Meanwhile, another set of plants was placed in the dark for 3 h, from which the control apoplastic fluids were collected. Leaves were detached immediately after either dark or red-light treatment and briefly rinsed with deionized water. After immersing leaves in infiltration solution containing 5 mM KCl and 0.1 mM CaCl2, vacuum was applied for 75 s and then slowly released. Excess fluid on the infiltrated leaves was then removed using Kimwipes (Kimberly-Clark). Apoplastic fluids were collected from leaves by centrifugation for 10 min at room temperature using a speed setting of 3 (~1,400 rotations per minute (r.p.m.), corresponding to ~200 g) on an IEC clinical tabletop centrifuge (International Equipment) with #215 4-place central rotor. Previous research utilizing V. faba and other species confirmed that at g forces <1,000, there is negligible cytoplasmic contamination of the apoplastic fluid28. For A. thaliana, 700 g has been used to collect symplast-free extracellular vesicles29, and 1,000 g was recently reported to be appropriate for collection of apoplastic fluid from this species30, confirming the suitability of our protocol. Our yield of apoplastic fluid was ~0.4 ml g−1 A. thaliana leaves (fresh weight) and ~1.0 ml g−1 of V. faba leaves (fresh weight).

Ion content measurement

Ion contents of apoplastic fluids from V. faba incubated in darkness or under red light were measured using inductively coupled plasma-atomic emission spectrometry (XSERIES 2ICP-AES, Thermo Fisher) at the Laboratory for Isotopes and Metals in the Environment, Department of Geosciences at Pennsylvania State University.

Treatment of apoplastic fluid

To characterize the features of the effective components in apoplastic fluid that promote red-light-induced stomatal opening, heat and pepsin (Sigma-Aldrich, P6887) treatments were performed on red-light-treated apoplastic fluid samples from V. faba. Heat treatment consisted of heating samples at 95 °C in a water bath for 5 min. Pepsin digestion (final pepsin concentration 4 mg ml−1) was performed at 37 °C for 1 h. Treated apoplastic fluids were immediately used for stomatal responses to red light, with untreated apoplastic fluid as control.

Fractionation of apoplastic fluid using centrifugal filter, solid-phase extraction and HPLC

To separate apoplastic fluid samples into macromolecular and small-metabolite fractions, centrifugation using an Amicon Ultra filter (4 ml or 15 ml) with a 3-kD-cut-off membrane (EMD Millipore) was performed at 4 °C. Flow-through from the centrifugal filter contains small molecules (<3-kD fraction), whereas sample remaining above the filter membrane (>3 kD) contains macromolecules.

To further identify hydrophobicity of mesophyll messenger(s), HyperSep Retain PEP cartridge (Thermo Fisher) was used for fractionation. Briefly, the column was conditioned using methanol once, then deionized water twice. Red-light-induced apoplastic fluid (5 ml) from V. faba was then applied onto the column. Flow-through was collected. Methanol (5% methanol, 95% water) was used to wash the column and was collected. Then 25%, 50%, 75% and 100% methanol were applied onto the column and fractions (1 ml for each) were collected. All samples (original, flow-through, wash and all fractions) were lyophilised (Labconco). One ml of 5 mM KCl and 0.1 mM CaCl2 was used to reconstitute each sample for stomatal aperture assays.

Reverse-phase chromatography (RP-HPLC) uses water and organic solvent (for example, methanol) as the solvent system, and a column embedded with resins containing small hydrophobic groups, which efficiently separates analytes with varying degree of hydrophobicity. An UltiMate 3000 HPLC system (Thermo Fisher) was used to collect fine fractions of V. faba or A. thaliana apoplastic fluid. Lyophilised apoplastic fluid was first reconstituted with solvent A (0.1% trifluoride acid (TFA) in 10% methanol). Then 80 µl was injected and separated on a Gemini C18 column (particle size 5 µm, pore size 110 Å, 250 mm length, 4.6 mm diameter, Phenomenex) using a gradient with solvent B (0.1% TFA in 90% methanol) at a flow rate of 0.5 ml min−1. The HPLC gradient started at 100% A for 5 min, followed by ramping from 0% B to 30% B over 40 min, then ramping to 100% B over 5 min, holding at 100% B for 5 min, and then returning to 0% B and holding at 0% B for 5 min. HPLC fractions were collected, lyophilised and reconsititued using a solution containing 5 mM KCl and 0.1 mM CaCl2. Reconstitued fractions were then tested for enhancement of red-light-induced stomatal opening in V. faba epidermal peels as described above. Eluates between 6.5–9 min for A. thaliana apoplastic fluid and 6.5–10 min for V. faba apoplastic fluid were effective in promoting stomatal opening under red light (Extended Data Fig. 1d) and were thus collected for metabolomics analysis.

Metabolomics analysis of effective HPLC fractions of A. thaliana and V. faba apoplastic fluid

Effective HPLC fractions of red-light-induced A. thaliana and V. faba apoplastic fluids were prepared as above. Parallel fractions from dark-treated apoplastic fluids that eluted in the identical time windows were taken as controls. Five biological replicates of each sample (that is, dark-treated A. thaliana (AtD), red-light-treated A. thaliana (AtRL), dark-treated V. faba (VfD) and red-light-treated V. faba (VfRL)) fractions were analysed on three different metabolomics platforms: GC–MS (targeted, GCMS-TQ8040, Shimadzu) at the RIKEN Center for Sustainable Resource Science (Japan), GC–MS (untargeted, Pegasus IV GC–TOFMS, LECO) at the University of California Davis, and LC–MS (targeted, QTRAP4000, Sciex) at the University of Florida. The data are summarized in Supplementary Tables 2–4.

For targeted analysis on the GCMS-TQ8040, adonitol was first added to each sample as an internal standard, then all samples were lyophilised. Methoxamine reagent (MOX, 100 µl; Thermo Fisher) was then added to each lyophilised sample, followed by overnight incubation at 30 °C on a shaker (1,200 r.p.m.). Then, 50 µl of N-methyl-N-trimethylsilyltrifluoroacetamide plus 1% trimethylchlorosilane (Thermo Fisher) was added to each sample, followed by shaking at 1,200 r.p.m. at 37 °C for 30 min. After spin-down, the supernatant of each sample was transferred to a glass vial. One microlitre of each sample was then injected into a Shimadzu GCMS-TQ8040 with a temperature gradient: 60 °C for 2 min, ramping to 330 °C at 15 °C min−1, followed by 330 °C for 3 min.

For targeted analysis on the QTRAP4000, effective fractions were lyophilised, redissolved in 10% methanol (with lidocaine as internal standard) and vortexed for 15 min at room temperature. After centrifugation (4 °C, 13,000 g) for 15 min, supernatants were transferred to glass vials for HPLC–MRM–MS analysis. Samples were separated on an Eclipse XDB-C18 column (diameter 4.6 mm, length 250 mm, particle size 5 μm) with 0.1% formic acid in water as solvent A and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile as solvent B. The HPLC gradient started at 1% solvent B for 5 min, followed by ramping from 1% B to 99.5% B over 41.5 min, holding at 99.5% B for 4.5 min, and then returning to 1% B over 0.3 min and holding at 1% B for 8.7 min. The flow rate was 0.5 ml min−1. The mass spectrometer conditions were: 30 psi curtain gas, 50 psi GS1, 55 psi GS2, ion source voltage 4,500 V, with a TurboIon electrospray ionization interface temperature of 350 °C. Multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) transitions were distributed into different periods on the basis of the compounds’ retention time to increase the number of compounds detected in a single run75.

For untargeted analysis on the Pegasus IV GC–TOFMS, samples were derivatized as previously described76 and then injected (0.5 µl of each sample) into an Agilent 6890 gas chromatograph (Agilent). Separation was performed on an Rtx-5Sil MS column (length 30 m, internal diameter 0.25 mm, 0.25 µm film made of 95% dimethyl/5% diphenylpolysiloxane; Restek) with a 20-min temperature gradient: 50 °C for 1 min, ramping to 330 °C at 20 °C min−1, followed by 330 °C for 5 min. The transfer line temperature between the gas chromatograph and the mass spectrometer (Pegasus IV GC–TOFMS, LECO) was set to 230 °C. Mass spectra were acquired at a mass range of 80–500 Da (17 spectra per second, −70 eV ionization energy and 1,800 V detector voltage) with ion source at 250 °C. Data processing and metabolite identification were performed as previously described76.

Metabolomics data analysis

For analyses on targeted metabolomics platforms (QTRAP4000 and GCMS-TQ8040), metabolites were identified by paired mass/charge (m/z) ratios of the precursor ion and a selected product ion along with the chromatographic retention time, as acquired from analysis of authentic compounds (standards). For quantification of QTRAP4000 data, the peak area of each metabolite from each sample was first normalized to the internal standard. For quantification of GCMS-TQ8040 data, the peak area of each metabolite from each sample was first normalized by the LOWESS/Spline normalization algorithm with a pooled QC sample using MRMPROBS77. For analysis on the untargeted metabolomics platform (Pegasus IV GC–TOFMS), peak height was reported because peak heights are more precise for low-abundance metabolites than peak areas, due to the larger influence of baseline determinations on areas compared with peak heights78. Peak heights were log2 transformed for statistical analysis. Fold change was calculated using the average value of each group (dark and red light) and Student’s t-test was performed to assess the statistical significance of the difference between the two groups. Metabolites with a fold change >1.2 or <0.8 are defined as significantly changed metabolites between dark and red-light treatments. According to the Metabolomics Standards Initiative64,65, our targeted LC–MS analyses are at confidence ‘level 1’ identification based on fragmentation spectra from authentic standards. Our GCMS-TQ8040 and GC–TOFMS identifications are based on fragmentation pattern-matching against the Smart Metabolites Database V2 (Shimadzu, https://www.shimadzu.com/an/products/gas-chromatograph-mass-spectrometry/gc-ms-software/smart-metabolites-database/index.html) and in-house mass spectral libraries at the West Coast Metabolomics Center, respectively, and can thus be considered as ‘level 2’ identification.

Quantification of apoplastic sugar concentrations

Apoplastic fluid of V. fabia leaves was collected as previously described79 using indigo carmine (Thermo Scientific, 860-22-0) to calculate the dilution factor. Analysis of sugars in the apoplastic fluid was carried out using a GC–MS/MS system that consisted of a gas chromatograph Trace 1300 coupled with a triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer TSQ 9000 (Thermo Scientific). The sample was spiked with a mixture of internal standards (glucose-13C6, fructose-13C6, sorbitol-13C6), followed by evaporation with dry nitrogen and chemical derivatization, namely, conversion to oximes with hydroxylamine and afterwards, trimethylsilylation. The derivatized samples were separated on a BPX5 capillary column (30 m × 0.22 mm, 0.25 µm, SGE) using hydrogen as a carrier gas. The mass spectrometer was operated in positive electron impact ionization and SRM mode. Nitrogen (>99.999% purity) was used as a collision gas. Analytes were quantified against calibration curves of the internal standards of the corresponding carbohydrates. The acquisition was controlled using Xcalibur 4.0, and the data were analysed using TraceFinder software v.5.1 SP1 (Thermo Scientific).

Immunohistochemical analyses of guard-cell plasma membrane H+-ATPase phosphorylation status

Immunohistochemical analyses using Arabidopsis epidermal fragments were conducted as described previously with slight modifications47,80. A. thaliana Col-0 plants were grown as previously described49. Plants 4–6-weeks of age were used for the experiments. In brief, the epidermal fragments were kept in the basal buffer (5 mM MES-1,3-bis[tris(hydroxymethyl)methylamino] propane, 50 mM KCl and 0.1 mM CaCl2) and treated with sucrose or mannitol at the indicated concentration in the dark. The buffer pH was adjusted to 5.5 or 6.5 with bis-tris propane. The phosphorylation level of the guard-cell plasma membrane H+-ATPase was estimated as described previously49 using an antibody that recognizes phosphorylated H+-ATPase Thr-948, at a dilution of 1:1,000. Three or more experiments were performed, with at least 30 stomata analysed at each measured timepoint. The specimens were observed under a fluorescence microscope (BX50, Olympus) with a narrow excitation band-pass filter set: BP460–480HQ, BA495–540HQ (U-MGFPHQ, Olympus) for Alexa Fluor 488 using an Hg arc lamp as a source of excitation light. Fluorescence images were collected using a CCD camera system (DP72, Olympus) and processed using DP2-BSW software (Olympus). For estimation of fluorescence intensities, all images were taken at identical exposure times (334.79 ms).

Preparation of V. faba guard-cell protoplasts

V. faba guard-cell protoplasts were prepared largely as we previously described74. Briefly, per preparation, 5 healthy, young and fully expanded leaflets were excised into 2 cm × 0.5 cm pieces using a scalpel. Major veins were removed using a razor blade, and leaf pieces were then blended in cold distilled H2O with an Osterizer blender (Sunbeam) for 45 s to isolate epidermal fragments. Epidermal fragments were then collected by filtration on a 100-μm nylon mesh and washed thoroughly with distilled H2O to remove damaged mesophyll cells. Washed epidermal peels were incubated in 10 ml of Enzyme Solution 1 for 40 min under darkness at 29 °C, with shaking at 140 excursions per minute. Next, the osmolality of the solution was adjusted to 510 mOsmol kg−1 by adding 30 ml basic solution, followed by shaking for 5 additional minutes. After collecting the partially digested epidermal tissue on a 100-μm nylon mesh and washing with basic solution, the peels were treated with 10 ml of Enzyme Solution 2 for 1.5–2 h at 20 °C in darkness, with shaking at 40 excursions per minute. Once approximately half of the guard-cell protoplasts had started to round up, the epidermal fragments were collected on a 30-μm nylon mesh and washed gently with basic solution into 50 ml volume. The filtrate was centrifuged at 150 × g for 5 min and the small pellet of guard-cell protoplasts concentrated at the bottom was carefully preserved during removal of the supernatant. The pellet was washed again in 50 ml basic solution and re-centrifuged before collecting the purified guard-cell protoplasts in 3–5 ml final volume of solution. The protoplasts were kept on ice in darkness for 1 h to recover before electrophysiological recording.

The basic solution consisted of 0.45 M mannitol, 0.5 mM CaCl2, 0.5 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM ascorbic acid, 10 µM KH2PO4 and 5 mM MES, pH 5.5 (adjusted with KOH). Enzyme Solution 1 was made in basic solution/water 55/45 (v/v) and contained 0.7% (w/v) Cellulysin cellulase Trichoderma viride (Thermo Fisher), 0.1% PVP40, 0.25% BSA (bovine serum albumin) and 0.5 mM ascorbic acid, pH 5.5 (KOH). Enzyme Solution 2 was made in 100% basic solution and contained 1.5% Onozuka Cellulase RS (Yakult), 0.03% Pectolyase Y-23 (Thermo Fisher), 0.25% BSA and 0.5 mM ascorbic acid, pH 5.5 (KOH).

Electrophysiology of V. faba guard-cell protoplasts and Xenopus oocytes

We followed a previously established protocol to record anion channel currents of V. faba guard-cell protoplasts81. A 100 μl aliquot of protoplasts was transferred into 1.9 ml of external control solution containing 30 mM CsCl, 1 mM CaCl2, 2 mM MgCl2 and 10 mM MES, pH 5.6 (adjusted with Tris base) at a final osmolality of 490 mOsmol kg−1 (adjusted with d-sorbitol). The pipette solution consisted of 150 mM CsCl, 5.864 mM CaCl2, 2 mM MgCl2, 6.7 mM EGTA, 10 mM HEPES and 2 mM Mg-ATP, pH 7.1 (adjusted with Tris-HCl) at a final osmolality of 510 mOsmol kg−1 (adjusted with d-sorbitol). This combination of divalent cations and EGTA yields 2 μM cytosolic free Ca2+. Recording electrodes made from capillary glass tubes (34500, Kimble) were pulled with two-stage puller PP-83 (Narishige) and fire polished with micro-forge MF-900 (Narishige) before coating with melted wax (Kerr). Such electrodes filled with the abovementioned pipette solution gave an average pipette resistance of 6–8 MOhms in the external control solution. A 1 M KCl agarose bridge was used to connect the bath to an Ag-AgCl ground wire placed in 1 M KCl solution. Liquid junction potential was corrected and the slow/fast capacitance transients were compensated by the functions of Axon pClamp software (Molecular Devices). Recordings were conducted using Multiclamp 700A amplifier (Axon Instruments). Data were sampled at 10 kHz and filtered at 1.4 kHz.

After the whole-cell configuration was formed, protoplasts were incubated for 5 min to equilibrate the cytosol with the pipette solution. The holding potential for recordings was 40 mV and 6-s voltage sweeps were applied from 80 mV to −160 mV at −40-mV intervals. The first voltage family, applied 5 min after achieving the whole-cell configuration, was designated as the initial condition (T0). Subsequent voltage family recordings (T1, T2.) were initiated 3 min after solution changes. The seal resistance of each protoplast was checked before and after every voltage-step protocol to confirm stability. Average values of steady-state currents for I–V curves were taken within the 10-ms interval just before the end of each voltage step. Data from each protoplast were normalized relative to current amplitude at −160 mV.

Xenopus oocyte recordings were performed as previously described82–91, with methodological details provided in Extended Data Fig. 4.
