Long‐Term Outcomes After Catheter‐Directed Thrombolysis or Mechanical Thrombectomy in Intermediate‐High–Risk Pulmonary Embolism: A Propensity‐Matched Analysis From a TriNetX Database
Authors: Chilingarashvili Giorgi, Laurent Bertoletti, Jara‐Palomares Luis, Belinda Rivera‐Lebron, Shameek Gayen, Pourshahid Omid, D'Silva Reeve, Lijo Illipparambil, Sheila Weaver, Mario Naranjo‐Tovar, Vien Truong, Ahmed El‐Moghraby, Parth Rali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCatheter‐directed thrombolysis (CDL) and mechanical thrombectomy (MT) are increasingly used for intermediate–high–risk pulmonary embolism (PE), yet comparative long‐term outcomes remain uncertain. We aimed to compare the effectiveness and safety of CDL versus MT in real‐world clinical practice. Using the multi‐institutional TriNetX network, we identified adults with acute intermediate–high–risk PE treated with CDL or MT. One‐to‐one propensity score matching was performed across demographics, comorbidities, and laboratory variables. Outcomes were assessed up to 2 years post‐index. The primary outcome was all‐cause mortality, while secondary outcomes included hospitalization, major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), pulmonary hypertension (PH), and bleeding. After matching, 1290 patients remained in each group with balanced baseline characteristics, with a median follow‐up of approximately 1.5 years. Long‐term mortality was similar between CDL and MT (3.1% vs. 3.5%; p = 0.061). MACE rates were comparable (5.2% vs. 4.6%; p = 0.933), with no significant difference in PH (3.0% vs. 3.24%; p = 0.352). CDL was associated with higher hospitalization rates (2.2% vs. 0.9%; p = 0.026), while bleeding events were low and similar between groups. In this large real‐world cohort of patients with intermediate–high–risk PE, CDL and MT demonstrated broadly comparable long‐term outcomes, suggesting no clear superiority of either strategy and supporting ongoing clinical equipoise. Ongoing randomized trials are needed to further inform optimal management.

Introduction

Pulmonary embolism (PE) remains a major cause of cardiovascular death across the world, ranking among the top three in cardiovascular fatality rates [1, 2]. Appropriate treatment relies on accurate risk stratification, with current 2026 AHA/ACC/ACCP/ACEP/CHEST/SCAI/SHM/SIR/SVM/SVN guidelines defining a spectrum from low‐ to high‐risk PE subgroups, as well as therapeutic anticoagulation strategies [3]. Among these, the intermediate‐high risk group, referred to those hemodynamically stable patients (defined as systolic blood pressure > 90 mmHg) with right ventricular (RV) dysfunction plus elevated biomarkers (troponin or brain natriuretic peptide), represents a particularly vulnerable cohort, with reported mortality between 3% and 14% [3].

Anticoagulation is the standard therapy for PE; however, some intermediate‐high‐risk patients may deteriorate rapidly and require reperfusion strategies. Although systemic thrombolysis is the treatment of choice in patients with high‐risk PE, catheter‐directed therapies—such as catheter‐directed thrombolysis (CDL) and mechanical thrombectomy (MT)—have gained increasing relevance in patients with contraindications to systemic thrombolysis, as they offer minimally invasive alternatives and lower bleeding risk [4].

Observational and small early randomized studies suggest that CDL reduces clot burden and RV strain with low bleeding complications [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10], whereas MT offers rapid hemodynamic recovery with shorter hospital stays and financial burden [11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17].

Despite growing evidence of therapeutic strategies, direct comparative data between CDL and MT remain limited in the intermediate‐high risk PE cohort, particularly for long‐term outcomes. Preliminary evidence hints at similar short‐term safety profiles, but possible differences in chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) risk remain unknown [18, 19]. These gaps underscore the need for robust real‐world comparative studies to inform evidence‐based management of intermediate‐high risk PE.

Methods

Study Design and Population

We conducted a multicenter retrospective cohort study using the TriNetX US Collaborative Network (TriNetX LLC, Cambridge, MA), integrating de‐identified electronic health records from 67 healthcare organizations across the United States, from both academic and community institutions. All data are standardized using common ontologies such as the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10‐CM), Current Procedural Terminology (CPT), and RxNorm vocabularies [20, 21]

We identified adults ( ≥ 18 years) with intermediate–high–risk PE (January 1, 2021, to December 1, 2025) using ICD‐10 codes for acute PE with markers of RV strain or myocardial injury (acute cor pulmonale, acute respiratory failure, elevated BNP, or troponin). Treatment groups were assigned by CPT/ICD‐10‐PCS codes (Figure 1 and Supporting Information S1: Table 2), requiring the reperfusion procedure to occur within 48 h of the index PE diagnosis to ensure linkage. Patients receiving both CDL and MT during the same admission and those with high‐risk features (hemodynamic instability or cardiac arrest) were excluded. The index date was the first qualifying procedure, and outcomes were evaluated up to 2‐years post‐index.

Flow diagram of study cohort.

Outcomes

The primary outcome—all‐cause mortality was studied up to 2‐years following the index procedure. Secondary outcomes included the development of pulmonary hypertension (PH), major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE composite of death, transient ischemic attack [TIA], cerebrovascular accident [CVA], cardiac arrest, or acute myocardial infarction [AMI]), and gastrointestinal bleeding events. Outcomes were identified as new clinical events (through ICD codes) occurring during the follow‐up time. To reduce coding bias, we excluded patients with a prior history of the same event before index admission.

Statistical Analysis

Between‐group differences were evaluated using t‐tests or Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests, as automatically selected by TriNetX based on distributional properties. Confounding was minimized with 1:1 nearest‐neighbor propensity score matching (caliper 0.1), using a logistic model including age, sex, race/ethnicity, and major comorbidities (Supporting Information S1: Table 1). This yielded 1290 well‐balanced patients per group. Outcomes were analyzed within TriNetX Advanced Analytics: risks with 95% CIs, risk differences/ratios/odds ratios, and time‐to‐event analyses via Kaplan–Meier curves with log‐rank testing and Cox proportional‐hazards models after confirming proportionality. TriNetX applied standard censoring at the last clinical encounter. To assess the robustness of the primary findings, two pre‐specified sensitivity analyses were performed. First, a subgroup analysis was conducted restricting the cohort to patients aged > 65 years, given the higher procedural risk and distinct comorbidity burden in this population. Second, the procedure exposure window was narrowed from 48 to 24 h after the index PE diagnosis code, to ensure that only emergent, acute‐indication procedures were captured and to minimize inclusion of delayed or semi‐elective interventions. Both sensitivity analyses applied the same 1:1 propensity score matching algorithm and outcome definitions as the primary analysis.

Results

Post‐Match Characteristics

After 1:1 propensity‐score matching (1290 per group), covariate balance was excellent (standardized mean differences < 0.1). Age, sex, and race were closely aligned. Comorbid disease difference was statistically nonsignificant. These results confirm robust balance across all domains, supporting unbiased outcome analyses (Supporting Information S1: Table 1). The propensity score model yielded a C‐statistic of ~0.6, suggesting good discriminative performance. Balance diagnostics are illustrated in a Love plot (Figure 2).

Propensity score density distribution before and after matching. Propensity score distributions are shown for the catheter‐directed thrombolysis (CDT; Cohort 1) and mechanical thrombectomy (MT; Cohort 2) groups before and after 1:1 matching. Prior to matching, the two cohorts exhibited divergence in propensity score density, indicating baseline imbalance in covariate distributions. Following matching, the distributions nearly overlapped, demonstrating successful covariate balance and improved comparability between treatment groups.

Outcomes

The median follow‐up was approximately 1.5 years for all outcomes (514.2 ± 301.6 days, CDL cohort; 412.7 ± 299.2 days, MT cohort). All‐cause mortality at the end of 2‐year follow‐up occurred in 3.4% of CDL and 3.5% of MT patients (HR 1.29, p = 0.06), MACE at the end of 2‐year follow‐up occurred in 5.2% versus 4.6% (HR 0.99, p = 0.93), and CTEPH developed in 3% % versus 3.24% (HR 0.86, p = 0.35) for CDL and MT, respectively (Figure 3).

Safety outcomes: GIB was comparable (1.2% vs 1.4%; p = 0.53). Kaplan–Meier survival analyses demonstrated no significant difference in long‐term survival between the CDL and MT cohorts. Overall, 2‐year survival probability remained closely aligned (96.6% for CDL vs. 96.5% for MT; p = 0.06). Cumulative survival curves showed parallel trajectories, with no divergence throughout the follow‐up period (Figure 4).

Kaplan−Meier curve by outcome. This composite panel displays Kaplan–Meier survival estimates for six prespecified outcomes in the propensity‐matched cohort following catheter‐directed thrombolysis (CDT) or mechanical thrombectomy (MT). Across most endpoints—including all‐cause mortality, gastrointestinal bleeding, MACE, and pulmonary hypertension—the cumulative incidence curves between CDT and MT remained largely parallel without statistically significant divergence. Overall, these curves illustrate comparable long‐term safety and effectiveness between CDT and MT across multiple clinically relevant domains.

Results were consistent across both sensitivity analyses. When the procedure window was restricted to within 24 h of PE diagnosis (CDL n = 1003; MT n = 1003), 2‐year survival was 89.4% for CDL versus 91.4% for MT, with a hazard ratio of 1.33 (95% CI 0.97–1.83; p = 0.076), consistent in direction but not reaching statistical significance. In the age > 65 subgroup (CDL n = 713; MT n = 713), 2‐year survival was 84.7% versus 87.9%, respectively (HR 1.29, 95% CI 0.95–1.77; p = 0.104). Across both analyses, the directional trend favoring MT was preserved, yet neither reached statistical significance, supporting the robustness of the primary results.

Discussion

In this large, multicenter, real‐world cohort of patients with intermediate–high‐risk PE, both interventional treatments, CDL and MT, provide similar long‐term efficacy and survival in intermediate–high‐risk PE, with only modest differences in hospitalization profiles. The overall results support therapeutic equipoise between these two contemporary reperfusion approaches. Collectively, these survival analyses reinforce that CDL and MT yield equivalent long‐term mortality and cardiovascular outcomes, supporting clinical equipoise between these reperfusion strategies for intermediate–high‐risk PE. The relatively low 2‐year mortality observed in this analysis should be interpreted in the context of patient selection. Patients treated with CDT are unlikely to represent the full spectrum of acute PE, as use of this therapy during the study period was generally limited to selected patients based on risk profile, clinical status, contraindications to systemic thrombolysis, and local expertise. As a result, outcomes in this cohort may reflect both treatment effect and selection of patients considered suitable for catheter‐based intervention. Therefore, these findings should not be extrapolated directly to unselected all‐comer acute PE populations.

Our analysis offers one of the most comprehensive real‐world comparisons to date between CDL and MT for intermediate–high‐risk PE, with extended follow‐up. While multiple studies have demonstrated the acute hemodynamic benefits and favorable safety profile of MT in acute PE, evidence on its long‐term clinical outcomes remains comparatively limited [8, 11, 12, 13, 22, 23]. The absence of a statistically significant difference in mortality aligns with current literature suggesting that both catheter‐based interventions can deliver durable benefit. A PERT cohort small retrospective analysis of 147 patients found no significant difference in all‐cause mortality when comparing MT and CDL at the end of 1 year [24]. Our analysis cohort contains a multicentric retrospective cohort with a large number of patients and a longer follow‐up time. The convergence of survival curves and hazard ratios near unity in our data indicates that once patients are stabilized and receive definitive intervention, the choice of modality has less impact on long‐term survival.

Monteleone et al.'s retrospective analysis from the Truveta database, comparing USCDT to MT among all‐PE patients, demonstrates a better safety profile for USCDT [25]. This analysis cannot be compared to our analysis, as we restricted the population to intermediate‐high risk PE. Kucher et al. compared USAT to heparin (UFH) performing ULTIMA trial, demonstrating USAT superiority in RV dilation reversion but no difference in mortality or bleeding at 90 days [6]. Averginos et al. explored the difference between USAT and SCDT, conducting SUNSET PE trial, demonstrating no difference in 90‐day outcomes [26]. PEERLESS trial, a recent largest RCT, exploring short‐term outcomes of large‐bore MT versus CDL, demonstrated procedural and resource‐use advantages of MT (shorter ICU stays, lower clinical deterioration) over CDL, without mortality difference at 30 days [27]. Recent trials such as the STORM‐PE RCT, comparing AC versus MT, demonstrated that MT superiority in RV strain improvement at 48 h compared to anticoagulation alone, without increased early major adverse events at 7 days [5]. While most trials focused on short‐term endpoints, our data extend beyond the acute phase and demonstrate that procedural advantages do not appear to translate into long‐term survival or cardiopulmonary differences, thereby reinforcing the concept of therapeutic equivalence between the two interventions.

CDL and MT have rapidly evolved as complementary front‐line therapies for intermediate–high‐risk PE, bridging the gap between systemic thrombolysis and anticoagulation alone. CDL achieving effective lysis with reduced systemic exposure but despite that low bleeding risk remains, as shown in ULTIMA, SEATTLE II, and OPTALYSE‐PE trials [7, 28, 29]. In contrast, MT enables immediate thrombus extraction without local fibrinolysis, as demonstrated in the FLARE trial and FLASH registry [12, 13]. This approach is the best choice for patients with contraindications to systemic thrombolysis or with high‐risk or active bleeding.

The uniformly low long‐term PH event rates observed in both groups in our study align with previously reported rates. Published studies have reported the incidence of CTEPH to range between 0.4% and 6.2% [30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35]. The absence of a difference in long‐term PH risk between CDL and MT is both clinically relevant and pathophysiologically consistent with current understanding of post‐PE vascular remodeling. CTEPH is increasingly recognized as a sequela of persistent microvascular obstruction due to residual thrombi, endothelial dysfunction, and maladaptive remodeling of the pulmonary vasculature [36, 37, 38]. Furthermore, Meneveau et al. demonstrated that elevated residual pulmonary vascular obstruction (RPVO) is an independent prognostic factor for mortality, reinforcing the concept that chronic PH may be influenced by microvascular and endothelial injury rather than by persistent large‐vessel obstruction [39]. More recently, the HI‐PEITHO RCT compared ultrasound‐facilitated CDL plus anticoagulation with anticoagulation alone in 544 patients with intermediate‐risk PE, demonstrating a lower 7‐day composite of PE‐related death, cardiorespiratory decompensation or collapse, or recurrent PE (4.0% vs. 10.3%; RR 0.39; p = 0.005), without intracranial hemorrhage or a significant increase in major bleeding [40].

The PE‐TRACT trial (NCT05591118) is an ongoing multicenter, randomized study comparing CDL versus anticoagulation alone in patients with intermediate‐risk PE. It will also address uncertainties about the long‐term benefits of the therapies for intermediate‐high risk PE. PRAGUE‐26 (NCT05493163) is expected to complete in 2028 and enroll 558 patients, exploring 1‐ and 2‐year outcomes. USAT IH‐PE (NCT06143969) is a multi‐center study reporting short‐ and long‐term outcomes. STRARIFY‐II (NCT06453876) comparing MT, CDL, and AC for the short‐term outcomes. ESCADlys‐PE (NCT06487052) single‐center RCT actively recruits patients to evaluate the effectiveness of CDT. All these trials indicate growing interest toward intermediate‐high risk PE due to clinical gaps. Over the next few years, all these trials will shape evidence‐based guidance on the optimal role of catheter‐based reperfusion in intermediate‐risk PE. Strength and Limitations

One of the most important strengths of our work is that we have synthesized a large real‐world data, nationally representative cohort that involves the largest comparative analyses of CDL and MT to date. Robust 1:1 propensity‐score matching with excellent variable balance, strengthening internal validity. We extend the scope of prior trials and provide clinically meaningful insight into long‐term safety and effectiveness. The inclusion of multiple clinically relevant endpoints further enhances the breadth and applicability of the findings.

Several limitations should be considered while interpreting the data. First, this was a retrospective observational study conducted using electronic health record data from the TriNetX network. As such, ICD coding inaccuracies or misclassification bias are possible; however, this limitation is mitigated by the large number of patients included in the analysis, as other authors have shown when validating ICD‐based analyses in other diseases. Second, despite achieving excellent covariate balance through the PSM algorithm, unmeasured confounding factors cannot be excluded. Third, not having detailed information on clot burden, RV strain pattern, PESI/BOVA scores, and laboratory values, limits our ability to fully analyze intermediate–high‐risk PE. Fourth, procedural details for CDL dosing, infusion duration, device type, or MT system used were unavailable, precluding more detailed comparisons. Long‐term follow‐up in TriNetX is limited by patient attrition and variable data capture across sites, which may lead to underestimation of outcomes such as PH. In addition, the platform's constraints prevented detailed subgroup or sensitivity analyses, limiting broader generalizability.

Conclusion and Future Direction

In this real‐world cohort of intermediate‐high–risk PE, CDL and MT demonstrated similar long‐term outcomes, indicating no clear advantage of one strategy over the other. We hope, present analysis reinforces the paradigm that both CDL and MT are safe, effective, and complementary reperfusion strategies. The choice between modalities should consider individual bleeding risk, clot morphology, RV dysfunction severity, and institutional expertise. Our findings, together with emerging randomized evidence, support the integration of both techniques within structured PERT pathways, emphasizing multidisciplinary decision‐making, post‐procedure follow‐up, and long‐term surveillance for pulmonary vascular sequelae.
