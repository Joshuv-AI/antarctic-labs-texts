Effect of Semaglutide on Epicardial Adipogenesis and hiPSC-Atrial Cardiomyocytes New Hope in Targeting Epicardial Adipose Tissue
Authors: Bénédicte Gaborit
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: adipogenesis, epicardial fat, metabolism, semaglutide

The accumulation of epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) around the heart, particularly in patients with cardiovascular disease, and its degree of inflammation has emerged as a key risk factor linked to adverse cardiac outcomes.1 In addition to its role as an energy storage system, it has been found to contribute to the development and progression of coronary artery calcification, inflammation, and plaque vulnerability. Rather than having distinct boundaries, the epicardium shares an unobstructed microcirculation with the underlying myocardium, and in healthy conditions, produces cytokines that nourish the heart. However, in chronic inflammatory disorders, the epicardium becomes a site of impaired adipogenesis, leading to the secretion of proinflammatory adipokines that can disrupt atrial and myocardial function.2

In this issue of JACC: Basic to Translational Science, Basdas et al3 explored how semaglutide—a glucagon-like peptide receptor agonist 1 (GLP-1-RA) that has been shown to improve insulin sensitivity and reduce EAT volume in type 2 diabetes patients4—modulates proinflammatory adipogenesis in EAT. The authors focused on understanding the effect of semaglutide on the expression of key adipogenic, inflammatory, metabolic, and beiging markers in EAT, and how these pathways may impact neighboring atrial cardiomyocytes (human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived atrial cardiomyocytes) via paracrine signaling.

Using biopsies obtained from patients undergoing open-heart surgery or isolated stromal cells from subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) and EAT, they showed the modulation of proinflammatory epicardial adipogenesis by semaglutide. Primary cultures of EAT and SAT stromal cells from 20 patients were induced to adipogenesis by insulin dexamethasone IBMX troglitazone for 15 days, with or without semaglutide treatment. Inflammation markers, such as interleukin 6, cluster of differentiation 68, and secretory phospholipase A2 group IIA, were significantly higher in EAT than SAT after adipogenesis induction. Semaglutide treatment decreased fatty acid binding protein 4 (FABP4) and perilipin 1 messenger RNA levels in EAT, and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha and interleukin 6 in SAT.

The same group confirmed that semaglutide modified exosomes-protein cargo on EAT explants.5 This drug increased secretion of gelsolin, an antithrombotic protein, by EAT, modulated CD11b on neutrophils, its migration and endothelial adhesion, induced by adiposity protein, FABP4, or a chemoattractant. These are encouraging findings because semaglutide could promote antithrombotic and anti-inflammatory effects on EAT. This is of particular interest because the subpopulation of type 2 diabetes mellitus with a null calcium score but with low-grade inflammation and high EAT volume could particularly benefit from this drug.6

Regarding proteomics, adipogenesis-induced cells under semaglutide treatment showed an increased level of caveolin 1, a major coat protein for caveolae formation, Additionally, scaffold analysis showed voltage-dependent anion channel 3 in 50% of the semaglutide-treated adipogenesis group. This protein is in the outer membrane of mitochondria, regulating the membrane potential, and is thought to protect mitochondria from oxidative stress. Recently, a growing body of evidence has shown that EAT has a beige phenotype, and contains innate type 2 lymphoid cells that could be activated by alarmins (interleukin 33), and can induce the beiging of fat cells.7 In coronary artery disease patients, EAT GLP-1 receptor was associated with genes involved in fatty acid oxidation and white-to-brown fat differentiation.8

The authors demonstrated an upregulation of calcium-activated potassium channel subunit alpha-1 messenger RNA expression levels in cardiomyocytes after being exposed to an extracellular medium of epicardial adipogenized stromal cells. Moreover, they were slightly increased by semaglutide treatment without reaching statistical significance, displaying perhaps the possible protective effect over the electrophysiological remodeling on the myocardium induced by paracrine activity of EAT.

The research provides compelling evidence that semaglutide can reduce the induction of proinflammatory adipogenesis in EAT, demonstrated by a decrease in the expression of markers such as FABP4 and secretory phospholipase A2 group IIA. The demonstration that semaglutide’s effects extend beyond the adipose tissue itself, and modulate EAT to release signals that impact the surrounding cardiomyocytes, likely contributing to improved myocardial function or reduced cardiac stress, needs further research and confirmatory findings. This could also be explored in patients with no obstructive coronary artery disease or in primary prevention. One should also investigate the effect of semaglutide on mature adipocytes, and the bidirectional effect of atrial cardiomyocytes on adipocyte function.

This work makes an important contribution to understanding how metabolic interventions like the GLP-1-RA semaglutide can impact EAT ectopic fat depot and its associated proinflammatory pathways, which are thought to influence myocardial function. This study might suggest semaglutide as a preventive therapeutic strategy for reducing inflamed epicardial fat genesis. These findings have the potential to inform future clinical strategies focused on reducing inflammation in fat depots as a way to mitigate cardiovascular disease, particularly in the context of metabolic disorders like obesity and type 2 diabetes. Further research is needed to identify additional molecular mechanisms and determine the broader impact of GLP-1 receptor activation on EAT and cardiomyocytes.
