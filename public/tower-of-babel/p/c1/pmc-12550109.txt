Introduction

Biliary ileus is an uncommon form of mechanical bowel obstruction resulting from the migration of gallstones into the intestinal lumen. Its incidence is estimated at 30–35 cases per million hospital admissions, representing approximately 1–3 % of all intestinal obstructions [1,2]. Historically, diagnosis has been delayed due to the non-specific and insidious clinical presentation [3].

The advent of point-of-care ultrasound (PoCUS) and improved diagnostic imaging has facilitated earlier detection of biliary pathology, contributing to a reduced incidence of complications [4]. However, the condition retains a high mortality rate, primarily owing to delayed diagnosis and the vulnerable nature of the affected population, which often includes elderly patients with multiple comorbidities such as diabetes, hypertension, obesity, and chronic cardiopulmonary conditions [4]. Gallstone ileus primarily affects elderly women, reflecting their increased lifetime prevalence of cholelithiasis [5]. The underlying pathogenesis is strongly associated with recurrent or chronic calculous cholecystitis, which promotes inflammation and adhesion formation between the gallbladder and adjacent gastrointestinal structures. Over time, this leads to erosion and the creation of a bilioenteric fistula, most commonly between the gallbladder and duodenum [5,6].

Through this abnormal communication, large gallstones may migrate into the intestinal lumen, where they can become impacted and lead to mechanical bowel obstruction. While most small gallstones pass uneventfully, only a small fraction (around 6 %) result in clinical obstruction, typically in the terminal ileum, the site of impaction in up to 70 % of cases [7].

In their nationwide analysis, Halabi et al. confirmed this female predominance and emphasized the need for individualized surgical management strategies based on comorbidities and patient condition [6]. Similarly, Elabsi et al. reported a typical delay of 2–5 days between symptom onset and presentation, underlining the importance of clinical vigilance. Their case also illustrated that simple enterolithotomy with deferred cholecystectomy remains a safe and effective initial approach, with spontaneous fistula closure occurring in up to 50 % of cases [7].

Stones often become impacted at sites of anatomical narrowing, particularly the terminal ileum or ileocecal valve. When the duodenum is involved, the condition is referred to as Bouveret's syndrome [[8], [9], [10], [11]].

Clinically, biliary ileus presents with signs of mechanical bowel obstruction—diffuse abdominal pain, bloating, vomiting, and cessation of bowel movements. Occasionally, it may present atypically with gastrointestinal haemorrhage or peritonitis secondary to bowel perforation. The mortality rate remains significant, ranging from 10 % to 13 %. [4,5]

Case presentation

An 85-year-old male with a 30-year history of arterial hypertension and known but untreated cholelithiasis was referred to the Emergency Department (ED) by his general practitioner for suspected intestinal obstruction. On arrival, the patient reported diffuse abdominal pain, with localized tenderness in the mesogastrium and left lower quadrant, accompanied by absence of bowel movements and flatus.

On clinical examination, the abdomen was distended but soft, with localized tenderness and hypoactive bowel sounds. Laboratory tests showed leucocytosis (WBC 14,200/μL), neutrophilia (77.9 %), and hyperglycaemia (168 mg/dL).

Given the ambiguous clinical picture, PoCUS was performed at bedside. The ultrasound revealed gallstones within the gallbladder, aerobilia, and a 3 cm hyperechoic structure in the right lower quadrant, consistent with an intraluminal gallstone in the ileum (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). The adjacent bowel loops appeared dilated, thick-walled, and non-peristaltic.

Intraluminal gallstone visualised within a dilated intestinal loop. The image demonstrates a hyperechoic focus with posterior acoustic shadowing, consistent with migrated biliary calculus.

Ultrasonographic image showing markedly dilated loops of small intestine with visible intraluminal fluid and altered peristalsis, suggestive of mechanical obstruction.

Subsequent abdominal X-ray demonstrated small bowel obstruction with multiple air-fluid levels and no free air. A CT scan confirmed a distended small bowel and stomach (Fig. 3), pneumobilia (Fig. 4), and a 35 mm bilioenteric fistula ending in the ileum (Fig. 5), consistent with a diagnosis of gallstone ileus.

Axial CT scan demonstrating markedly dilated loops of small intestine consistent with mechanical bowel obstruction. Note the accumulation of intraluminal fluid and absence of colonic gas in the distal bowel.

Axial CT scan showing absence of a clearly defined gallbladder with fluid content. The presence of intraluminal air and loss of normal gallbladder wall architecture suggest a cholecystoenteric fistula with adjacent small bowel loops.

Axial CT scan confirming the presence of an intraluminal gallstone measuring 35 mm within a dilated ileal loop, consistent with gallstone ileus.

Management and outcome

Given the extent of small bowel distension observed on imaging and confirmed on clinical assessment, an emergency exploratory laparotomy was deemed necessary. The degree of dilatation precluded a safe laparoscopic approach, particularly in light of the patient's advanced age and overall frailty. Intraoperatively, a wide cholecysto-duodenal fistula was identified, accompanied by markedly dilated loops of small intestine proximal to the site of obstruction (Fig. 6). The fistula appeared fibrotic and sealed, with no active leakage or inflammation, and was therefore left in situ. The obstructing gallstone was located approximately 150 cm from the terminal ileum, lodged within an ileal loop.

Intraoperative image demonstrating markedly dilated small bowel loops proximal to the site of obstruction, consistent with gallstone ileus.

The involved segment appeared distended with loss of peristalsis and early signs of vascular compromise, including patchy dusky serosa and delayed capillary refill. Following retrograde decompression of the proximal bowel, a limited resection of the compromised segment was performed to facilitate safe extraction of the calculus and reduce the risk of anastomotic failure.

A single faceted gallstone measuring approximately 3.5 cm in diameter was retrieved (Fig. 7), consistent with preoperative imaging findings.

Resected segment of small intestine alongside the extracted gallstone, measuring approximately 3.5 cm in diameter, following enterolithotomy.

Restoration of bowel continuity was achieved through a side-to-side isoperistaltic anastomosis, using a linear Tri-stapler device (Fig. 8). The procedure was completed without intraoperative complications.

Intraoperative view of the completed side-to-side isoperistaltic small bowel anastomosis performed using a mechanical stapling device.

Intraoperatively, the decision to perform a limited ileal resection rather than a simple enterolithotomy was based on the assessment of bowel viability and local conditions surrounding the impacted gallstone. The involved segment demonstrated early signs of ischemia, including patchy serosal discoloration and diminished peristalsis, raising concerns about mucosal compromise. Moreover, the large size and impacted nature of the 3.5 cm gallstone had caused focal pressure necrosis of the bowel wall. Resection was therefore preferred to reduce the risk of postoperative leakage, stricture, or delayed perforation that may follow a primary closure under tension. The approach aimed to ensure a more durable outcome, particularly in this elderly and frail patient, by minimizing the likelihood of reoperation or anastomotic failure.

Postoperatively, the patient was monitored in a high-dependency surgical unit. He remained hemodynamically stable, with no signs of infection or leak. Early mobilization was initiated on postoperative day (POD) 2. Bowel sounds returned by POD 3, and oral intake was progressively resumed—starting with clear fluids and advancing to soft solids by POD 5. The patient progressed favourably, with resolution of ileus and good tolerance to diet. He remained clinically stable throughout the hospital stay and was discharged in good condition on POD 10 with outpatient follow-up arranged.

Discussion

Gallstone ileus represents a rare but severe complication of cholelithiasis, accounting for approximately 1–4 % of mechanical intestinal obstructions and occurring in 0.3–1.5 % of patients with gallstones [12]. Its pathophysiology is typically the result of chronic cholecystitis leading to the formation of a cholecystoenteric fistula, most frequently involving the duodenum. This inflammatory erosion allows one or more gallstones to migrate into the gastrointestinal tract, where they may become lodged, most often at anatomical narrowings such as the terminal ileum. The condition may evolve from a long-standing asymptomatic cholelithiasis and is more frequently observed in elderly, frail patients with multiple comorbidities, where diagnosis may be delayed and clinical signs atypical, thereby contributing to significant morbidity.

Definitive management is surgical, with multiple operative strategies described. These include enterolithotomy alone, enterolithotomy with concurrent cholecystectomy and fistula closure (single-stage procedure), enterolithotomy with delayed cholecystectomy (two-stage approach), and, in cases of compromised bowel, intestinal resection [13]. In elderly or high-risk individuals, a limited approach focusing on relief of the obstruction through enterolithotomy alone is generally favoured, particularly when the presence of inflammation or dense adhesions increases the risk of a more radical procedure.

Minimally invasive methods, such as laparoscopic-assisted enterolithotomy, have also been explored and appear to offer benefits in selected patients by reducing postoperative recovery time and surgical trauma [14]. Regardless of the operative plan, preoperative optimization remains essential, typically involving nasogastric decompression and fluid resuscitation to stabilise the patient prior to intervention [15].

In recent years, advances in diagnostic imaging have facilitated earlier recognition of this condition. In particular, PoCUS has emerged as a valuable frontline tool in emergency and geriatric settings. PoCUS enables the bedside identification of key sonographic signs such as pneumobilia, small bowel dilatation, and intraluminal hyperechoic calculi with posterior acoustic shadowing—all of which may raise suspicion for biliary ileus. When used early, PoCUS can expedite the diagnostic pathway by guiding further imaging or surgical decision-making, a benefit that is especially relevant in frail patients with equivocal clinical signs.

Compared to traditional imaging approaches such as plain abdominal X-rays—which may demonstrate non-specific findings like air-fluid levels—or even CT scans, which remain the gold standard for confirmation, PoCUS offers advantages in terms of accessibility, rapid deployment, and lack of ionizing radiation. Notably, PoCUS has demonstrated a sensitivity of up to 92 % for detecting gallstones and has been shown to correlate with the need for surgical management [16,17]. In a recent prospective study, the detection of gallstones via PoCUS was associated with a 13-fold increased likelihood of cholecystectomy [16].

In addition to imaging, supportive laboratory findings may aid in the diagnostic process. Although often non-specific, elevated liver enzymes such as alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and bilirubin may reflect biliary involvement and should be considered in the broader differential diagnosis.

In summary, PoCUS represents a valuable adjunct in the early diagnostic algorithm for biliary ileus, particularly when integrated with clinical findings and biochemical markers. Its incorporation into acute surgical assessment pathways may reduce diagnostic delays, minimize unnecessary imaging, and enhance patient outcomes, especially in vulnerable populations.

Conclusion

This case underscores the diagnostic challenges posed by biliary ileus, particularly when presenting with non-specific symptoms in elderly, comorbid patients. The initial clinical evaluation did not yield conclusive evidence of obstruction, illustrating the limitations of traditional physical examination in complex abdominal presentations. The use of point-of-care ultrasound at the bedside proved instrumental in identifying the obstructing gallstone, guiding the diagnostic workup, and expediting surgical referral.

PoCUS enabled rapid visualization of key features (gallstones, bowel distension, and pneumobilia) prior to radiologic confirmation, thereby significantly reducing diagnostic delay. Its integration into early assessment protocols may enhance clinical efficiency and improve patient outcomes, especially in frail populations where time-sensitive intervention is critical. As demonstrated in this case, PoCUS should be regarded not merely as a supplementary tool but as an essential component of the initial diagnostic approach to suspected intestinal obstruction of biliary origin.
