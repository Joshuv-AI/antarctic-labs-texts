Biological Performance of Modified Glass Ionomer Cement: Antibacterial Activity, Cytocompatibility, and Rabbit Pulpal Response
Authors: Krittee Dejyong, Saowakon Indoung, Peerapon Sornying, Sareepah Manmoo, Nattapon Rotpenpian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConventional glass ionomer cement (GIC) is widely used as a restorative base and liner owing to its chemical adhesion to dentine, fluoride release, and favourable biocompatibility. Nevertheless, its intrinsic antibacterial activity and regenerative capacity remain limited. To enhance these properties, a series of modified GIC formulations, referred to as BIOGIC, incorporating tricalcium phosphate (TCP), TCTP-derived peptides, and 10% chlorhexidine (CHX), were developed. This study evaluated their antibacterial efficacy, cytocompatibility, and pulpal responses in a rabbit-tooth model.MethodsSix formulations were examined: GIC, BIOGIC, BIOGIC+TCP, BIOGIC+TCP+3 µg TCTP, BIOGIC+TCP+10 µg TCTP, and BIOGIC+10% CHX. Antibacterial activity against Streptococcus mutans and Enterococcus faecalis was assessed using liquid culture assays and spread-plate analysis. Dental pulp cell viability was evaluated using a metabolic assay. In vivo pulpal responses were examined in rabbit incisors restored with each material and assessed histologically after 21 days, focusing on inflammation, collagen formation, and pulp–dentine integrity.ResultsBIOGIC+10% CHX demonstrated the strongest antibacterial activity, significantly reducing S. mutans and E. faecalis. Dental pulp cell viability decreased significantly in the GIC, BIOGIC+TCP+10 µg TCTP, and BIOGIC+10% CHX groups, with BIOGIC+10% CHX showing the greatest cytotoxicity. In contrast, BIOGIC, BIOGIC+TCP, and BIOGIC+TCP+3 µg TCTP significantly enhanced pulp cell proliferation. Histological analysis revealed no pulpal inflammation in any group. Mild collagen deposition was noted only in the BIOGIC group, whereas the remaining formulations exhibited normal pulp morphology without adverse reactions.ConclusionIncorporation of 10% CHX markedly enhanced the antibacterial properties of BIOGIC but increased cytotoxicity. BIOGIC formulations containing TCP and low-dose TCTP improved pulp cell viability and maintained favourable pulpal responses. These modified BIOGIC materials show promise as bioactive, pulp-compatible alternatives to conventional GIC, although CHX-containing formulations should be used with caution owing to their cytotoxic effects.Key words: Glass ionomer cement, Pulp viability, Antimicrobial, Rabbit teeth

Introduction

Glass ionomer cement (GIC) has been used in restorative dentistry for many years, most commonly as a base, liner, or temporary restorative material. Its continued clinical acceptance reflects a combination of useful properties, including fluoride release, chemical bonding to enamel and dentine, thermal compatibility with natural tooth structure, and a generally favourable biocompatibility profile.1 Conventional GIC consist of a fluoroaluminosilicate glass powder combined with polyalkenoic acids. When mixed, these components undergo an acid–base reaction that produces a hardened matrix able to form chemical bonds with hydroxyapatite.2 Despite these well-recognised advantages, traditional GICs continue to present several inherent limitations, such as modest mechanical strength, vulnerability to early moisture exposure, and limited antibacterial properties.3 These shortcomings can undermine long-term clinical success, especially in deep cavities where the likelihood of residual bacteria and subsequent pulpal irritation is greater.

Residual bacteria remaining in the cavity after caries removal – most commonly Streptococcus mutans4,5 and Enterococcus faecalis5,6 – are key contributors to postoperative sensitivity, recurrent caries, and the failure to maintain pulp vitality. Although the fluoride released from GIC helps to reduce cariogenic activity, it is not sufficient on its own to prevent bacterial regrowth.7 As a result, considerable research has been directed towards enhancing the antimicrobial performance of GIC by incorporating therapeutic additives such as chlorhexidine (CHX), calcium phosphate derivatives, and bioactive peptides. CHX, in particular, is recognised for its broad-spectrum and long-lasting antibacterial effects; however, its inclusion in restorative materials requires careful optimisation to limit any potential cytotoxic impact on pulp cells.8

In recent years, increasing attention has been given to improving the bioactivity of restorative materials in order to support pulp healing and dentine regeneration. Tricalcium phosphate (TCP) is often added to bioactive systems because it provides a source of calcium and phosphate ions, which encourage remineralisation, aid dentine bridge formation, and enhance the stability of the tooth–material interface.9 Meanwhile, biomimetic peptides derived from translationally controlled tumour protein (TCTP) have shown encouraging cytoprotective effects, supporting cell survival and moderating inflammatory responses.10 anti-apoptotic, and cell-proliferative effects in various tissue types, including dental pulp cells.11 These characteristics suggest that TCTP-derived peptides may enhance pulp biocompatibility and support regenerative processes when incorporated into restorative materials.12 While previous research has independently demonstrated the antimicrobial efficacy of CHX and the remineralising capacity of TCP, the incorporation of a TCTP-derived bioactive peptide into this matrix has not previously been explored.13

The aim of this study was to provide a comprehensive assessment of the biological and antibacterial behaviour of BIOGIC formulations modified with TCP, TCTP-derived peptides, and 10% CHX. The investigation focused on determining the antibacterial activity of each formulation against S. mutans and E. faecalis through liquid culture assays and spread-plate methods, as well as evaluating the cytotoxicity and viability of dental pulp cells in contact with the materials. In addition, the in vivo pulpal response in rabbit teeth restored with the experimental formulations was examined histologically, with particular emphasis on inflammation, collagen deposition, and the preservation of the pulp–dentine complex. Finally, the performance of the modified BIOGIC materials was compared with that of conventional GIC to establish whether the inclusion of TCP, TCTP, and/or CHX offered meaningful improvements in antibacterial efficacy and overall biocompatibility.

Material and methods

This study received ethical approval from the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry, Prince of Songkla University (EC6711-048), as well as from the Animal Welfare Committee, Prince of Songkla University (MHESI Approval Code EC6711-048). All procedures were carried out in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines.

In this research, a conventional glass ionomer cement was selected as the base material. 3M Ketac Molar Easymix (3M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA), a fluoroaluminosilicate glass system containing aluminium, calcium, and lanthanum, together with copolymers of acrylic and maleic acids (approximately 5%), served as the foundational GIC.

The materials used in this investigation were divided into two principal categories focusing on BIO-GIC formulations containing fortilin protein (Pmer-TCTP). Six experimental GIC-based compositions were prepared. Group 1 served as the negative control and consisted of 100% conventional GIC powder. Group 2 (BIO-GIC) comprised 80% GIC powder, 15% chitosan, and 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). Group 3 (BIO-GIC + TCP) contained 79.95% GIC powder, 15% chitosan, 5% BSA, and 0.05% tricalcium phosphate (TCP).

Groups 4 to 6 were formulated from the Group 3 composition, with increasing concentrations of TCTP-derived fortilin peptide (Pmer-TCTP): 1 μg in Group 4, 3 μg in Group 5, and 10 μg in Group 6. These formulations were designed to assess the combined influence of TCP and fortilin dosage on the biological behaviour of BIO-GIC. The powder-to-liquid ratio was strictly maintained according to the manufacturer’s instructions, at one level scoop of powder to one drop of liquid (1:1).

Microbial culture methods and determination of the lowest concentrations that can inhibit bacterial growth Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) Bacterial Strains and Culture Conditions

The bacterial strains used in this study were S. mutans (ATCC 25175) and E. faecalis (ATCC 29212), obtained from a −80 °C frozen microbial stock. After removal from storage, the samples were thawed and streaked onto Brain Heart Infusion agar (BHI agar; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) using the streak-plate technique. The plates were incubated at 37 °C in an atmosphere containing 5% carbon dioxide for 24 to 48 hours, depending on the growth characteristics of each organism.

Well-defined colonies were then selected: typically 4 to 5 colonies for S. mutans and 1 to 2 colonies for E. faecalis. These were transferred into 1 mL of Brain Heart Infusion broth (BHI broth) and incubated under the same atmospheric and temperature conditions. S. mutans required approximately 16 to 18 hours to reach the exponential phase in broth culture, while E. faecalis exhibited a shorter growth period of about 2 to 4 hours in broth and approximately 24 hours when grown on solid media.7

The turbidity of the resulting suspension was adjusted to the McFarland standard level of 0.5, which corresponds to a bacterial concentration of approximately 1 × 10⁸ colony forming units per milliliter (CFU/mL), and then the obtained S. mutans were diluted 100 times to prepare it as a substrate for the experiment. MIC with a final concentration of 1 × 10⁶ CFU/mL

The materials from each experimental group are molded by 3 × 3 mm2 and stored in microtubes with pure distilled water. One ml of deionized water was applied at 37 °C for 24 hours, and then the water obtained from the storage solution was used for the test. Bacterial precursor preparation is made by mixing a 30 μL concentration of 1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL with 2970 μL of BHI broth semen to achieve a final concentration of 1 × 10⁶ CFU/mL.

In a 96-well microtiter plate, 100 μL of the bacterial suspension was added to each well, followed by 100 μL of the material immersion solution, giving a total volume of 200 μL per well at a 1:1 ratio of bacterial substrate to test material. After curing, bacterial growth was first assessed visually by examining the turbidity of the suspension. A clear solution comparable to the positive control indicated effective inhibition of bacterial proliferation.

Subsequently, turbidity was quantified using a spectrophotometer at 600 nm. Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values were interpreted based on the optical density (OD₆₀₀); readings below 0.05 were considered to represent the lowest concentration at which bacterial growth was effectively inhibited.7

Cytotoxicity test in human pulp cells

To evaluate cell viability following exposure to the experimental materials, the corresponding material storage solutions were used as test extracts. Cells cultured in T-75 flasks were detached using 0.05% trypsin–EDTA and collected by centrifugation at 1500 × g for 5 minutes at 4 °C. After centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded and the cell pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of culture medium. Cell numbers were determined using an automated cell counter (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), after which the cells were seeded into 96-well culture plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well (n = 6 per group).

Following seeding, the plates were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% carbon dioxide for 24 hours to allow complete cell adhesion and spreading. After this period, the cells were gently washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4), and 100 μL of fresh culture medium supplemented with the appropriate material extract was added to each well. Control wells without cells received only culture medium to establish baseline readings.

The plates were then incubated for a further 72 hours before being subjected to MTT staining. Cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), following standard procedures commonly applied in the evaluation of cytotoxicity of modified glass ionomer materials.10

Study of modified GIC in rabbit teeth

New Zealand White rabbits were used in this study. Six male rabbits, each weighing approximately 1 kg, were randomly assigned to three experimental groups, with four teeth allocated per rabbit.

Group 1 (negative control): teeth received no restorative material (n = 4 teeth; rabbit 1).

Group 1 (negative control): teeth received no restorative material (n = 4 teeth; rabbit 1).

Group 2 (positive control): teeth were restored with conventional glass ionomer cement (GIC) (n = 20 teeth; rabbits 1-6).

Group 2 (positive control): teeth were restored with conventional glass ionomer cement (GIC) (n = 20 teeth; rabbits 1-6).

Group 3 (experimental): teeth were restored with the formulated BIOGIC materials (n = 20 teeth; rabbits 1-6).

Group 3 (experimental): teeth were restored with the formulated BIOGIC materials (n = 20 teeth; rabbits 1-6).

Random allocation of treated teeth was carried out using a computer-generated sequence to balance group distribution among animals and minimise potential confounding. All histological assessors were blinded to the experimental grouping.

Anaesthesia was induced via intramuscular administration of dexmedetomidine (0.2 mg/kg; Dextometor, Zoetis, USA) combined with tiletamine/zolazepam (20 mg/kg; Zoletil, Virbac, France). Tramadol hydrochloride (5 mg/kg; TRAMADOL, T.P. Corporation, Thailand) was given subcutaneously for analgesia. Anaesthetic depth was monitored at 5-minute intervals using the pedal and tail-pinch reflexes. When required, atipamezole (1 mg/kg; Atipam, Zoetis, USA) was administered to reverse the effects of the alpha-2 agonist component and facilitate smoother recovery.

Cavity preparation was performed using a low-speed handpiece under sterile saline irrigation to create a standardised 1-mm-diameter cavity on both maxillary and mandibular incisors, extending to the level of the pulp chamber. All cavity preparations were performed by a single experienced operator to ensure procedural consistency and minimise operator-related variability. Following restoration, postoperative discomfort was monitored by assessing feeding behaviour and facial expressions consistent with the Rabbit Grimace Scale. Tramadol hydrochloride (15 mg/kg, subcutaneous) was administered to alleviate postoperative pain when indicated.

Radiographic assessments were conducted on days 0, 3, 7, 14, and 21 under inhalation anaesthesia using isoflurane (1.5%-2%) delivered with oxygen (1-1.5 L/min) to monitor the longitudinal adaptation of the restorative material and the tooth’s structural response. While Day 0 established the baseline, the Day 3 and 7 intervals were selected to detect ‘early failure’ – specifically interfacial gaps or bond fractures resulting from immediate polymerization stress or early functional loading. Subsequent evaluations on Day 14 and 21 were designed to capture ‘secondary changes’, allowing for the observation of the tooth’s internal structure as it begins to react to the restorative material, such as the initiation of reparative dentin or changes in periapical density.

Histological analysis

The tissue specimen was carefully excised together with the surrounding maxillary and mandibular structures. Following isolation, the sample was rinsed under running water and fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 48 hours. Subsequently, the specimen underwent calcium decalcification, dehydration, and paraffin embedding in accordance with standard histological procedures.

Paraffin blocks were sectioned and mounted onto glass slides, which were then dried at 37 to 40 °C. Prior to staining, paraffin was removed using xylene, and the sections were rehydrated through a graded series of alcohol solutions (100%, 95%, 70%, and 50% v/v), each for 1 to 2 minutes, followed by rinsing in distilled water.

For routine histological evaluation, sections were stained with haematoxylin for 5 to 10 minutes and rinsed with tap water. Differentiation was performed using 1% acid alcohol (1% HCl in 70% ethanol), followed by rinsing and bluing in ammonia water for 1 to 2 minutes. Masson trichrome staining was then applied to assess collagen organisation. After staining, the sections were dehydrated in graded alcohols, cleared in xylene, and mounted with a resin-based medium under a coverslip.

Histological examination was performed using a bright-field polarisation microscope (Carl Zeiss Axioskop 40), with six slides evaluated per group. Haematoxylin and eosin staining was employed to assess general tissue morphology. Three histopathological parameters were evaluated: (1) the number of newly formed capillaries, (2) the number of fibroblasts, and (3) the number of inflammatory cell infiltration. Capillaries and fibroblasts/collagen were quantified per high-power field (HPF: 400X magnification). Six sections per sample were analysed by observers blinded to the experimental groups. Collagen fibre organisation was additionally assessed under polarised light following Masson’s trichrome staining.

Collagen and inflammatory responses were scored according to a five-level severity scale14:

Statistical analysis

The data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. A probability value of ≤ .05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

From the antibacterial assay in liquid medium, sample 6 (BIOGIC + 10% CHX) cultured in a 24-well plate exhibited the greatest inhibition of Streptococcus mutans growth compared with all other groups. Consistent with these findings, the spread-plate analysis showed a marked reduction in bacterial load in the BIOGIC + 10% CHX group relative to the negative control, decreasing from 5.19 × 10⁹ CFU/mL to 4.46 × 10² CFU/mL (Figure 1).

Similarly, for E. faecalis, the liquid-phase antibacterial assay demonstrated that sample 6 produced the strongest inhibitory effect. The spread-plate data confirmed a substantial reduction in viable bacterial counts compared with control group 7, decreasing from 1.24 × 10⁹ CFU/mL to 1.48 × 10⁵ CFU/mL (Figure 2).

The dental pulp cell viability assay indicated that three formulations – formulation 1 (GIC), formulation 5 (BIOGIC + TCP + 10 µg TCTP), and formulation 6 (BIOGIC + 10% CHX) – displayed reduced cell viability. Among these, BIOGIC + 10% CHX showed a statistically significant decrease in viability compared with the control, demonstrating greater cytotoxicity towards dental pulp cells than the other materials. In contrast, formulation 2 (BIOGIC), formulation 3 (BIOGIC + TCP), and formulation 4 (BIOGIC + TCP + 3 µg TCTP) produced a significant increase in cell viability (Figure 3).

Discussion

Our study investigated the biological and antibacterial characteristics of a series of modified GIC formulations – collectively termed BIOGIC – incorporating TCP, a TCTP-derived peptide at two concentrations (3 µg and 10 µg), and 10% CHX, and compared these with conventional GIC. The evaluation encompassed antibacterial activity against Streptococcus mutans and Enterococcus faecalis, cytotoxicity and cell viability in dental pulp cells, and the in vivo pulpal response in a rabbit model.

Accordingly, this study evaluates a novel triple-component system designed to achieve concurrent pathogen control and pulpal protection. In contrast to conventional modified glass ionomer cements, the present work specifically examines whether TCTP incorporation can attenuate the dose-dependent cytotoxicity associated with high-concentration (10%) CHX.13 By assessing this system through both in vitro assays and a 21-day in vivo model, we provide new evidence on the feasibility of combining potent disinfectants with bioactive signalling peptides to support the pulp-dentin complex.15,16

Overall, the findings revealed a notable trade-off between antibacterial efficacy and cytocompatibility. BIOGIC formulations containing TCP and low-dose TCTP demonstrated enhanced pulp cell viability while maintaining favourable biocompatibility. In contrast, the CHX-containing BIOGIC exhibited pronounced antibacterial activity, although this was accompanied by increased cytotoxicity.

One of the principal challenges in developing modified GICs for restorative and pulp-preserving applications lies in achieving robust antimicrobial efficacy while maintaining favourable pulp cell viability and supporting tissue healing. In our study, the BIOGIC formulation containing 10% CHX exhibited the strongest antibacterial effect, demonstrating a marked reduction in S. mutans. This finding indicates that CHX, when incorporated at a high concentration within a GIC matrix, can effectively inhibit the growth of clinically important cariogenic and endodontic pathogens. These results align with previous reports showing that CHX-modified GICs possess enhanced antimicrobial properties.7

However, this antibacterial advantage was accompanied by a notable drawback. The dental pulp cell viability assay demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in cell viability in the BIOGIC + 10% CHX group compared with the control, highlighting cytotoxic effects that are plausibly attributable to the high CHX concentration. This observation is consistent with concerns reported in previous literature17; although CHX is widely recognised for its broad-spectrum antimicrobial properties, its incorporation into GIC matrices – particularly at elevated concentrations – may compromise biological compatibility and limit its suitability for pulp-preserving applications.18

In contrast, formulations incorporating TCP and low-dose TCTP (3 µg) – namely BIOGIC + TCP and BIOGIC + TCP + 3 µg TCTP – demonstrated enhanced pulp cell viability compared with conventional GIC. This indicates that the inclusion of bioactive, non-antimicrobial components may promote cell survival and proliferation, presenting a potentially safer strategy for improving the biological performance of GIC without relying on inherently cytotoxic antimicrobial agents. These findings emphasise the importance of achieving an appropriate balance between antimicrobial efficacy and cytocompatibility when developing bioactive restorative materials intended for deep cavities or vital pulp therapy.19 An excessively potent antimicrobial additive may impair cell viability and hinder tissue healing, whereas formulations relying solely on bioactive components may be insufficient to suppress residual bacterial load.20

The incorporation of TCP into GIC-based materials has attracted considerable interest, as calcium and phosphate ions are fundamental to remineralisation and bioactivity. The favourable cell-viability outcomes observed for the TCP-containing BIOGIC formulations are consistent with the concept that calcium phosphate-modified GICs may provide a more biologically supportive environment for dental pulp cells, potentially through the release of Ca²⁺ and PO₄³⁻ ions that facilitate mineral deposition and maintain tissue homeostasis.21

A recent systematic review on calcium- and phosphate-modified GICs supports our findings, showing that calcium and phosphate release enhances hydroxyapatite formation, raises local pH, and inhibits cariogenic bacteria – features that improve caries control and restoration longevity.22 Although our study did not measure ion release or mineral deposition, the improved cytocompatibility of TCP-containing formulations suggests that TCP may help create a more supportive pulpal microenvironment, with potential to reduce cytotoxic stress and possibly promote reparative processes. Confirmation, however, will require direct assessment of ion-release behaviour, mineralisation, and dentine bridge formation in future work.

Incorporating bioactive peptides into GICs is an emerging strategy. In our formulations, low-dose TCTP (3 µg) enhanced pulp cell viability, whereas the higher dose (10 µg) reduced viability, though less markedly than CHX. This dose-dependent response suggests that small amounts of TCTP may provide cytoprotective or proliferative signals, while higher concentrations may disrupt cellular homeostasis. These observations emphasise the need for precise optimisation of peptide dosing when developing biologically active, pulp-preserving GICs.20

Notably, a recent study23 incorporating recombinant fortilin – a cytoprotective protein closely related to TCTP – into GIC demonstrated enhanced odontogenic differentiation and mineral deposition in human dental pulp stem cells, particularly when combined with TCP. In that work, the GIC + TCP + fortilin composite produced greater alkaline phosphatase activity and mineral formation than either GIC + TCP or GIC alone, indicating that bioactive peptides may support not only cell viability but also differentiation and mineralising capacity.23

These observations lend further support to the concept that peptide-modified GIC may contribute positively to pulp regeneration and restorative outcomes, provided that the concentration and release characteristics of the peptide are appropriately optimised. In our study, the low-dose TCTP formulation appears to offer a favourable balance between biological activity and cytocompatibility, whereas the high-dose formulation may exceed the threshold tolerated by pulp cells.

The selection of 3 μg and 10 μg of TCP was based on both biological relevance and preliminary feasibility considerations. Previous studies have demonstrated that low microgram-level additions of calcium phosphate phases are sufficient to enhance calcium and phosphate ion release and promote dentine remineralisation without adversely affecting the setting reaction or mechanical integrity of glass ionomer cements.24 The lower concentration (3 μg) was chosen to represent a minimal bioactive dose capable of providing supplemental ionic release, while the higher concentration (10 μg) was selected to evaluate a dose-dependent effect on antibacterial activity, cytocompatibility, and pulpal response. Importantly, these concentrations were identified through pilot mixing trials as being compatible with the Ketac GIC matrix, allowing homogeneous dispersion of TCP without compromising handling properties or setting behaviour, thereby ensuring clinical relevance and material stability.

The mechanistic basis for the beneficial effects of TCTP-derived peptides remains speculative, but several plausible pathways exist. These include stabilisation of mitochondrial function and inhibition of apoptosis, attenuation of oxidative stress, modulation of growth-factor-related signalling cascades, or enhancement of extracellular matrix synthesis. Nevertheless, these proposed mechanisms remain hypothetical; our data extend only to cellular viability and histological assessment and do not yet provide molecular insight. Further mechanistic studies will be required to delineate the pathways through which TCTP-based peptides influence pulp cell behaviour.25

The histological examination of rabbit incisors restored with the experimental materials for 21 days yielded favourable outcomes across all groups. No inflammatory infiltrate, necrosis, or disruption of the pulp–dentin complex was observed, including in the BIOGIC formulation containing 10% CHX. Interestingly, only the plain BIOGIC group (without TCP, TCTP, or CHX) exhibited mild collagen deposition, classified as grade I, whereas the BIOGIC + TCP, BIOGIC + TCP + TCTP, and BIOGIC + 10% CHX groups presented with normal pulp architecture and no appreciable collagen formation.

The 21-day endpoint was selected based on established protocols for evaluating pulp response. This timeframe is sufficient to capture the resolution of acute inflammation and the initiation of reparative processes. As demonstrated by Paranjpe et al,15 3 weeks is a critical window for observing the recruitment of odontoblast-like cells and the production of mineralized tissue markers, providing a clear histological distinction between persistent irritation and successful tissue repair.

These results suggest that, notwithstanding the in vitro cytotoxicity associated with CHX at high concentrations, the short-term in vivo pulp environment was able to tolerate the material without triggering overt inflammatory or degenerative changes. Several physiological factors may account for this discrepancy, including dilution and dispersion of potentially harmful constituents, the intrinsic buffering capacity of pulpal tissue, and the protective influence of vascular perfusion and immune modulation. Collectively, these mechanisms may attenuate cytotoxic effects that are readily detected under controlled in vitro conditions. Thus, transient reductions in cell viability observed in vitro do not necessarily equate to clinically significant tissue injury in vivo – at least within the 21-day observational period employed in this study.16

However, the absence of collagen deposition in all groups – aside from the mild grade-I response observed in the plain BIOGIC formulation – may also indicate that none of the modified materials effectively stimulated reparative dentinogenesis or extracellular matrix remodelling within the 3-week experimental period. From a regenerative standpoint, this represents an important limitation. An ideal bioactive liner or base should not merely be non-inflammatory; it should also actively promote tissue repair and matrix synthesis. The lack of detectable collagen formation in the peptide- or TCP-modified BIOGIC groups therefore suggests that their regenerative potential remains unconfirmed, or that the 21-day timeframe was insufficient to reveal early stages of matrix deposition or dentin bridge development.

Notably, a recently published in vivo investigation of an “enhanced GIC” incorporating 0.05% TCP, 1 µg TCTP, chitosan, and albumin reported increased collagen formation and superior pulp healing relative to conventional GIC at 21 days post-restoration. The discrepancy between those findings and ours may reflect differences in additive concentrations, base formulations, experimental species, cavity design, or histological assessment criteria. In particular, our higher TCTP dose (10 µg) may have exceeded the optimal biological window for regenerative signalling, whereas the lower dose (3 µg) may require a longer period before measurable extracellular matrix changes occur.26

Our findings are consistent with the wider trends and challenges reported in recent literature. A systematic review of CHX-modified GICs concluded that incorporating CHX (commonly 0.5%-2%) can reduce Streptococcus mutans and Lactobacillus acidophilus counts when compared with conventional GIC; however, there is no evidence that such modification improves the long-term clinical survival of restorations.27 The review noted that the certainty of evidence was very low, largely due to heterogeneity in CHX concentrations, material formulations, and study designs. Furthermore, a more recent in vitro investigation reported that applying 2% CHX as a cavity disinfectant before placement of a high-viscosity GIC enhanced the remineralisation of demineralised dentine,28 as shown by micro-CT and SEM, with thicker intertubular dentin and smaller dentinal tubule orifices compared with conventional GIC control.29 This suggests an additional beneficial effect of CHX beyond its antimicrobial activity: stabilisation of collagen fibrils, preservation of dentine architecture, and facilitation of remineralisation beneath the restoration. Although our study did not directly evaluate remineralisation or dentine microstructural changes, the notion that CHX-modified GIC may contribute to a more stable dentine–material interface is consistent with these earlier findings.

Regarding bioactive additives, previous reviews have shown that calcium–phosphate modified GICs enhance hydroxyapatite formation and overall bioactivity, while peptide-enriched GICs (eg, fortilin) can promote odontogenic differentiation in vitro. These findings support the rationale for combining TCP and TCTP in our formulations, and our study is among the first to integrate both components within a single GIC system.24

BIOGIC containing TCP and low-dose TCTP shows potential as a bioactive liner or base for deep cavities or vital pulp therapy, demonstrating acceptable biocompatibility and a more favourable pulpal environment than conventional GIC. By contrast, BIOGIC + 10% CHX may be useful when strong antibacterial action is needed – such as in high-caries-risk cases or atraumatic restorative treatment – although its cytotoxicity limits use near the pulp and warrants placement over sufficient remaining dentine or with a protective liner. Notably, none of the formulations induced appreciable collagen deposition or reparative changes within 21 days, indicating that their regenerative capacity remains undetermined and requires longer-term investigation.

The inclusion of both components represents a dual-action strategy: CHX provides effective antimicrobial clearance of pathogens such as S. mutans, whilst TCP offers bioactive support conducive to pulpal homeostasis. This integrated approach recognises that, although higher concentrations of CHX enhance disinfection efficacy, they may simultaneously impose dose-dependent biological stress on dental tissues. Accordingly, combining CHX with TCP seeks to balance antimicrobial potency with biological compatibility, promoting a more favourable environment for the pulp–dentine complex.13

This study has several limitations: the short observation period, absence of clinical trials, lack of optimisation of CHX concentration or controlled-release mechanisms, and no investigation of TCTP-associated molecular pathways. These constraints highlight the need for further research before definitive clinical recommendations can be made.

Conclusion

Our study indicates that modified GIC formulations incorporating TCP, TCTP-derived peptides, and/or CHX represent a promising pathway towards next-generation restorative materials designed to restore tooth structure while also supporting pulp health and suppressing residual bacteria. The findings suggest that BIOGIC containing TCP with low-dose TCTP enhances pulp cell viability and maintains favourable pulpal histology in vivo, whereas CHX-modified BIOGIC provides strong antibacterial activity but with a clear compromise in cytocompatibility. Given the clinical need for materials that protect pulp vitality and reduce secondary caries – particularly in deep lesions – these modified BIOGIC formulations present a credible and valuable direction for future restorative practice. With more extensive mechanistic and long-term evaluation, such materials may ultimately offer clinicians the ability not only to restore but also to promote biological repair.
