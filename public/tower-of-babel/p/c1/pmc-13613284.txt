Evaluating ChatGPT's Reliability in Analyzing Facial Asymmetry: A Comparative Study With Human Evaluators Using Cross Sectional Static Images
Authors: Ali B. Jafar, Malak Motair, Shahah Al Hajeri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveFacial asymmetry assessment is often subjective and time intensive, thus we aim to evaluate reliability of ChatGPT in analyzing facial asymmetry compared to human raters.MethodsThirty patients with unilateral facial paralysis who underwent facial reanimation surgery were included in this study. Sixty static 2D frontal images (pre and postoperative) were obtained from our database. Facial asymmetry was assessed using the Sunnybrook resting symmetry scale and a 0–4 global asymmetry rating scale. Two human raters evaluated all images independently. ChatGPT Pro 5.0 accessed from September 2025 to October 2025, was prompted through standardized instructions to evaluate the same set. Agreement was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), Cohen's kappa, the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, and Bland–Altman plots.ResultsChatGPT Pro 5.0 showed no statistically significant difference compared with human raters in preoperative facial assessment (p = 0.701), indicating high reliability in detecting pronounced asymmetry. However, a significant difference emerged in the postoperative assessment (p = 0.0001), where the ChatGPT was less sensitive to subtle facial asymmetry in postoperative stage. Cluster analysis confirmed agreement in high asymmetry cases and divergence in mild cases.ConclusionChatGPT reliably assesses pronounced facial asymmetry but is less accurate with subtle facial asymmetry features.

Introduction

Assessment of facial asymmetry plays a critical role in the diagnosis, monitoring, and evaluation of patients with facial paralysis. Accurate assessment is essential not only for grading the severity of facial dysfunction but also for tracking progress in surgical postintervention and guiding therapeutic decisions. Traditionally, these evaluations have been performed by experienced clinicians using visual inspection and various standardized grading systems. Although these clinical assessments offer valuable insights, they are inherently subjective, time‐consuming, and dependent on the evaluator's training and experience [1].

Over the past six decades, numerous facial palsy grading systems have been developed. The House‐Brackmann and Sunnybrook facial grading scales are among the most widely used scales in clinical practice [2, 3]. However, their use has been consistently criticized for its coarse nature, subjective evaluation, and time‐consuming implementation in clinical practice [4]. In response to such limitations, more sophisticated tools such as the eFACE grading system have been developed [5]. The eFACE tool evaluates static and dynamic facial symmetry, regional changes, and synkinesis, demonstrating strong intraobserver and interobserver reliabilities [6]. Nevertheless, its use in clinical and research settings is time‐intensive and requires complex software and specialized training.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI), particularly machine learning and computer vision, offer promising avenues for automating facial assessments [7]. AI‐based tools are capable of localizing facial landmarks, quantifying facial movements, and analyzing emotive expressions with increasing precision [8]. These developments hold promise for facial plastic and reconstructive surgery, in which objective and repeatable assessments are essential for evaluating outcomes and optimizing patient care.

ChatGPT 5.0, an AI model developed by OpenAI, has rapidly gained popularity owing to its natural language processing capabilities and recent expansion into multimodal functions with the release of Generative Pre‐Trained Transformer 4 with Visual Capabilities (GPT‐4V), which integrates textual and visual inputs. This transformation from a purely textual tool to a vision‐capable assistant opens new possibilities for applications in healthcare, including facial analysis. Despite its widespread use in various domains of medicine, no studies to date have assessed the reliability of ChatGPT or similar generative AI models in evaluating facial asymmetry compared with human experts.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the reliability of ChatGPT in assessing facial asymmetry in patients who underwent facial reanimation surgery using static frontal facial images. We hypothesized human raters given the extensive clinical training, would demonstrate superior evaluation of facial asymmetry compared to AI‐based tool. Thus, in this study, we compared AI‐generated assessments of preoperative and postoperative facial images with evaluations performed by experienced trained human raters to determine whether ChatGPT offers comparable accuracy in analyzing facial symmetry. Unlike existing automated tools such as eFACE software, which require technical expertise and specific training, ChatGPT offers a user‐friendly interface and wide accessibility, potentially democratizing access to high‐quality facial assessments [9].

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the application of ChatGPT in the context of facial asymmetry. Our findings could have important implications for the integration of AI in clinical practice, potentially streamlining facial assessment workflows and supporting clinicians in both routine evaluations and complex surgical outcome analyses.

Method

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Ethics Committee Board of the Faculty of Medicine, Health Science Center, Kuwait University (No. VDR/EC‐2025‐68).

Data Collection

We obtained data from our clinical database for 30 patients who had undergone facial reanimation surgery for unilateral facial paralysis. This study was designed as an exploratory pilot study; therefore, no formal a priori sample size or power calculation was performed. Our inclusion criteria included images of patients with obvious unilateral facial paralysis, adults or children, males or females, any ethnicity, frontal view only, and static high‐resolution images. All patients should have undergone facial reanimation surgery and have postoperative images that meet our image quality criteria. We excluded any low resolution, obscured, incomplete (e.g., not showing full face), or if only pre or postoperative images were available.

Facial Asymmetry Assessment

We retrieved 60 images from our cohort of 30 images. Given that the images were static, our group felt that the Sunnybrook facial grading system – resting facial assessment domain–was the closest validated facial symmetry assessment tool that could be used. This validated tool can assess three key facial regions: the eyes, cheek/nasolabial folds, and the mouth. The total score was multiplied by 5 to yield the overall score (Table 1). In addition, we formulated a re‐scaled visual analog scale into ordinal scale as a simplified global asymmetry score from 0 to 4, where 0 denotes a full facial symmetry and 4 denotes complete asymmetry was used to evaluate facial asymmetry (Table 2) [10].

Human Rater Evaluation

Two trained facial plastic surgeons judged the images separately. They did apply the Sunnybrook grading system and global score scale to all preoperative and postoperative images independently. Each patient's facial asymmetry was scored according to the Sunnybrook resting face scale based on three regional domains (eyes, cheek/nasolabial fold, and mouth) and subsequently on a simplified global rating scale.

AI Evaluation

The same set of images (n = 60) was then uploaded to the ChatGPT Pro 5.0 accessed from September 2025 to October 2025 in a sequential manner. All evaluations were conducted within the same platform version during the study period to reduce variability related to model updates. We uploaded the Sunnybrook facial evaluation form [3] to ChatGPT. The senior author then performed a pilot test on ChatGPT with a random selection of 10 images comprising pre and postoperative images and assessed the degree of facial asymmetry based on prompts given to score the degree of asymmetry according to the Sunnybrook and simplified global score scale. Then, the senior author manually evaluated the same images using the same criteria. For each image, standardized prompts were provided asking the ChatGPT to identify (a) evaluate facial asymmetry using the resting symmetry score, and (b) provide a global rating score from 0 to 4 for facial symmetry. After completion of the pilot test and confirmation that ChatGPT was able to conduct the prompts, we then proceeded by uploading the same images previously assessed by human raters and asking ChatGPT to evaluate every pre and postoperative image using the same criteria. We performed six image evaluations each time to prevent any chances of hallucination that ChatGPT could result in, as well as prevent the introduction of bias in case ChatGPT uses memory of previous images to formulate its outcomes [11]. The complete standardized prompt set used for image evaluation is provided in Supporting Information: Appendix S1.

Statistical Analysis

The primary outcome measure was the accuracy and success rate of the ChatGPT in evaluating facial asymmetry compared to human raters. Statistical analysis was performed using R (version 3.6.3) to evaluate the interrater reliability between human raters (rater 1 vs. rater 2) using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for continuous scores and Cohen's Kappa for categorical agreement. We then compared the mean human ratings and AI ratings using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test and agreement assessment using the Bland‐Altman analysis. All statistical tests were two‐tailed, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Visual representations in the form of data cluster analysis were created to illustrate the relationships between human and AI ratings across patients in both the pre and postoperative conditions. To further explore patterns of agreement between human and AI assessments, unsupervised clustering analysis was performed using the k‐means algorithm. Clustering was conducted using Euclidean distance, with the number of clusters (k = 2) selected a priori to represent high versus low facial asymmetry groups. The analysis was performed in R (version 3.6.3). Cluster assignments were then compared with pre and postoperative conditions to evaluate differences in distribution patterns between human and AI ratings.

Results

Patient Demographics

Thirty patients were included in the study. The cohort comprised 26 adults (87%) and 4 children (13%). The ethnic distribution was predominantly Caucasian (n = 21, 70%), followed by Hispanic (n = 6, 20%) and African American (n = 3, 10%). Facial paralysis affected the right side in 17 patients (57%) and left side in 13 patients (43%). Various surgical techniques were utilized, with the most common being the gracilis free flap (n = 9, 30%), nerve transfer (n = 5, 17%), and tensor fascia lata (n = 5, 17%). Other procedures performed included depressor anguli oris excision (n = 4, 13%) and Botox (n = 4, 13%), selective neurolysis (n = 4, 13%), facial nerve repair (n = 2, 7%), and temporal muscle transfer (n = 1, 3%).

Descriptive Analysis

Descriptive analysis of facial asymmetry scores demonstrated comparable mean preoperative values between human raters and AI. Using the Sunnybrook resting symmetry scale, the mean preoperative score was 18.10 ± 2.70 (mean ± SD) for human raters and 18.10 ± 2.45 for AI. Similarly, for the global asymmetry rating scale, the mean preoperative score was 3.69 ± 0.63 for human raters and 3.50 ± 0.73 for AI.

In the postoperative condition, human raters demonstrated lower mean asymmetry scores compared with AI. The mean postoperative Sunnybrook score was 5.66 ± 3.92 for human raters and 9.33 ± 4.30 for AI. For the global asymmetry scale, the mean postoperative score was 0.90 ± 0.71 for human raters compared with 1.53 ± 0.73 for AI.

Human Rater Evaluation

Interrater reliability evaluation between the two human raters showed moderate agreement in the evaluation of preoperative images (ICC: 0.558; 95% confidence interval: 0.254–0.762 and Kappa value of 0.426). This indicates acceptable consistency between human raters during the preoperative facial asymmetry evaluation process. However, for postoperative assessment, the reliability between human raters decreased substantially, with an ICC of 0.147 (95% confidence interval: –0.219 to 0.477) and Kappa value of 0.188). This suggests poor agreement between human raters when evaluating postoperative outcomes, highlighting the subjective nature and complexity of post op facial asymmetry assessments.

Comparison Human Versus AI

Comparative analysis between human raters for total facial asymmetry and global facial asymmetry scores showed no significant difference between AI (ChatGPT) and human evaluation during preoperative facial assessments (Wilcoxon test p = 0.701). This indicates that ChatGPT's evaluation of preoperative facial asymmetry was statistically similar to that of human specialists, suggesting that AI can reliably assess the more pronounced facial asymmetry typically seen in preoperative cases (Figure 1). However, postoperative facial asymmetry assessment revealed a significant difference between the AI and human mean ratings (Wilcoxon test p = 0.0001). This suggests that AI was less accurate in detecting the more subtle asymmetries present after surgical intervention, potentially overestimating or underestimating the degree of residual asymmetry compared with human assessment (Figure 2).

Data Clustering Analysis

Visualization analysis revealed several key findings regarding AI and human assessment patterns. Scatter plot analysis comparing preoperative and postoperative evaluations demonstrated clustering patterns that highlighted the differences between human and AI assessment capabilities.

K‐means clustering analysis identified two distinct clusters representing higher and lower degrees of facial asymmetry across the dataset.

Preoperative Data Clustering

Preoperative cases (red cluster) showed that both human and AI scores were concentrated in the higher asymmetry range (2–4 points), with data points distributed relatively close to the diagonal line of perfect agreement. This indicates that both evaluations can reliably identify pronounced facial asymmetries typical of severe facial asymmetry (Supporting Information: Figure S1).

Post Operative Data Clustering

In postoperative cases (blue cluster), a distinctly different pattern was observed, with human scores predominantly clustered in the lower ranges (0–2 points), while AI scores remained more distributed across a wider range (0–3 points). This systematic difference suggests that human evaluators are more sensitive to the improvements achieved through surgical intervention, whereas AI appears to maintain higher asymmetry scores even after successful treatment (Figure 3).

Discussion

Facial asymmetry is a common clinical presentation with a variable spectrum from subtle, hardly detectable, to severe, more pronounced asymmetry. Very mild or subtle asymmetry can be considered within acceptable normal limits, with no limited functional or aesthetic impairments. However, significant facial asymmetry can result in profound aesthetic, emotional, and functional implications for patients, affecting speech, facial expression, and psychosocial well‐being [12]. Therefore, there is a need for an accurate, convenient, and reliable tool for the disposal of patients. More recently, we have seen the integration of AI into the field of facial plastic surgery in providing consultation, postoperative instructions, and assisting patient counseling [13, 14].

However, there has not yet been any study that evaluated open access AI (ChatGPT) as a tool in evaluating facial asymmetry. Thus, we conducted this study to evaluate the reliability of the ChatGPT in assessing facial asymmetry from more pronounced to less pronounced asymmetry and compared it to human raters. Our findings demonstrated that the ChatGPT is capable of evaluating preoperative facial asymmetry with a higher level of accuracy and reliability compared to human specialists. This suggests the potential utility of the ChatGPT as a free and accessible tool for the evaluation of facial asymmetry in more overt situations. However, our study also revealed key limitations in the performance of the ChatGPT system, showcasing that AI tended to underestimate postoperative changes, consistently rating residual asymmetry more conservatively.

This discrepancy indicates that while AI may be reliable in assessing pronounced asymmetry, it currently lacks the sensitivity required to detect subtle improvements in patients with mild facial asymmetry. These findings align with those of previous studies on AI performance variability [1, 5, 6, 15].

Interestingly, the interrater reliability among human evaluators also declined in postoperative cases, showcasing the inherent difficulty of assessing subtle facial changes, even in experienced clinicians. These findings need to be interpreted with caution. First, AI‐human divergence increased after surgery, suggesting that ChatGPT was less sensitive to the subtler residual asymmetries present in postoperative images. Second, agreement between the two human raters also declined substantially in postoperative cases, indicating that the assessment of mild residual asymmetry was itself challenging and less reproducible. These findings should not be interpreted as evidence of AI failure alone; rather, they suggest that residual postoperative facial asymmetry represents a difficult evaluation domain for both AI and human raters, although the instability of the human benchmark further complicates interpretation.

Thus, we can interpret our findings that AI can serve as a triage or adjunct tool, particularly effective in severe forms of facial asymmetry or in preoperative counseling, to streamline clinical efficiency workflow, but would require human specialist validation in the postoperative context.

This study is limited by the modest sample size (n = 30) and the reliance on static, frontal view‐only images. Animated facial movements, dynamic asymmetry, and conditions such as synkinesis were not assessed, which is an important limitation of this study. The cohort was predominantly Caucasian, which further limits generalizability. This is particularly relevant in AI‐based vision assessment, where performance may vary across skin tones, facial morphology, and demographic groups depending on the composition of the training data. More ethnically diverse validation datasets are needed before broad clinical application can be considered.

Moreover, our findings are specific to the current iteration of ChatGPT and may not be generalizable to other AI platforms or newer versions of this platform. Thus, comparative studies evaluating multiple different AI platforms using the same image set, prompting framework, and scoring rubric would help determine reproducibility across other platforms and identify the most clinically reliable systems for facial analysis.

Conclusion

We conducted the first pilot study to evaluate the reliability of the ChatGPT in evaluating facial asymmetry. Our study demonstrated that AI (ChatGPT) is a widely accessible and reliable tool for initial screening of pronounced facial asymmetry, with results comparable to those of specialists. However, in the context of mild facial asymmetry, specialists remain essential because of AI's current limitations of AI in detecting subtle improvements. Future development will likely extend toward integration with automated facial landmarking systems and AI facial analysis platforms. Combining multimodal AI assessment with automated landmark detection may allow patient images to be processed before clinician review, with objective quantification of asymmetry across facial subunits. Such a workflow could reduce assessment time during consultation, improve efficiency in high‐volume practice, and allow physicians to focus more on clinical interpretation, operative planning, and patient counseling.
