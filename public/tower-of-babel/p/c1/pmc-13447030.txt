Descriptive Analysis of Surgically Resected Pure Radiation Necrosis After Radiation Therapy for Brain Metastasis: Clinical Features, MRI Findings, and Outcomes
Authors: Rae Hyung Kim, Soojin Jang, Ho-Shin Gwak, Yun-Sik Dho, Sang-Hoon Shin, Heon Yoo, Kyu Chang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDifferentiating radiation necrosis (RN) from tumor progression is challenging despite advanced neuroimaging techniques. We collected a consecutive series of surgically resected RN with presumptive diagnosis of recurrent/refractory brain metastasis (BM) after radiation therapy (RT) and performed a descriptive analysis of characteristics and clinical courses.MethodsFrom 107 patients who underwent surgery to remove previously irradiated BM, we retrieved 18 patients with pathologically confirmed pure RN.ResultsSixteen patients (89%) had preoperative MRI suggesting recurrent/refractory BM in accordance with clinical suspicion, and 14 (78%) patients showed radiological response after RT before progression. The median time from RT to surgery was 28.5 months (range 6–94 months). RT doses varied from 1,800 cGy/single fraction to 8,300 cGy/21 fractions. Lobulated/irregular shape, central necrosis, and substantial increase of perilesional edema were dominant MRI features. Neurological symptoms improved or stabilized in 13 patients (72%) after surgery. Five cases of distant recurrences occurred in patients with uncontrolled systemic cancer, except for one patient with controlled small cell lung cancer who recurred as a leptomeningeal metastasis. One patient had multiple enhancing lesions along the surgical cavity, suggesting local recurrence. Median overall survival after surgical removal of RN was 34 months (range 4.3–132 months), and eight out of eleven patients with controlled systemic cancer remained alive at last follow-up.ConclusionRN frequently mimics recurrent BM despite advanced imaging. Surgical resection provides both definitive diagnosis and symptomatic benefit in selected patients. However, distant recurrence of BM could happen in patients with uncontrolled systemic cancer.Keywords: Brain neoplasms, Metastasis, Necrosis, Radiation, Surgery

INTRODUCTION

The incidence of brain metastasis (BM) is increasing as the survival of cancer patients is prolonged by advanced therapy, and 10%–40% of solid tumor patients can be expected to develop BM over their clinical course [1,2]. New treatments for BM have emerged in recent decades, including stereotactic radiosurgery/radiotherapy (SRS/SRT), which delivers a relatively high biological equivalent dose to the target site with or without combination surgery or whole-brain radiotherapy (WBRT), and conventional WBRT alone is no longer a standard therapy even for multiple BM [3,4]. Furthermore, as SRS/SRT is believed to reduce the radiation dose applied to normal brain tissue in addition to providing prolonged control of BM, re-treatment of recurrent BM with SRS/SRT has been tried in recent decades [5,6,7]. However, a considerable incidence of radiation necrosis (RN), and of cognitive dysfunction due to brain atrophy, was reported to occur after re-irradiation [8,9,10,11].

RN of the brain manifests as necrosis of targeted white matter with perilesional edema from leaky blood vessels [12]. Clinical manifestations include various neurological deficits according to the location of the lesion, which sometimes progress to clinical deterioration. Diagnosis of RN has been problematic because it is hard to distinguish RN from recurrent tumors on routine gadolinium (Gd)-enhanced MRI [13,14,15]. Recently, dynamic susceptibility-weighted perfusion MRI has been used to differentiate RN from tumor recurrence, but the reliability of perfusion MRI seems low, and new MRI protocols are still on the verge of trial [16].

Treatment for RN is not yet fully standardized. Medical treatments such as glucocorticoids are the most commonly prescribed option for brain RN; however, their adverse effects in long-term use and lack of symptom relief in some cases limit the treatment of RN. The efficacy of other treatments, such as hyperbaric oxygen therapy, the anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) monoclonal antibody bevacizumab, nerve growth factor, and gangliosides, is still controversial [11,17,18]. Surgical removal might be a treatment option in case of progressive symptomatic RN and/or in cases where recurrent BM after radiation therapy (RT) is considered more likely than RN [17,18].

We retrospectively evaluated surgically removed RN from patients with previously irradiated BM at a single institute. The aim of this study was to investigate the presumptive diagnosis (RN vs. recurrent BM) before surgery, the goal of surgery (symptomatic relief vs. limited treatment option), radiological progression after RT, and follow-up results. The secondary objectives were to find common clinical characteristics and brain MRI features that differentiate RN from irradiated BM.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient selection and eligibility criteria

This retrospective study was in accordance with the precepts established by the Helsinki Declaration and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Cancer Center of Korea (NCCCTS-2020-0122-0001).

We retrospectively searched our electronic medical record (EMR) database for patients who underwent surgical removal of BM from January 2001 through December 2023. This search identified 631 patients (Fig. 1). After further EMR review, we selected 107 patients who received brain RT before the surgery to remove BM, for which the decision of surgical removal was based on physician’s discretion. After a review of serial brain MRI images, we excluded nine patients with either “out-of-field” recurrence or new lesion after WBRT, leaving 98 patients who underwent surgery for a presumed “in-field” recurrent/refractory BM after RT.

Schematic of the study flow. EMR, electronic medical record; RT, radiation therapy; WBRT, whole-brain radiotherapy; BM, brain metastasis.

The diagnosis of “pure RN” was solely based on the formal pathology report on EMR. We did not perform a supervision of pathologic finding by designated pathologist. Thus, the pathologic finding had been reported by several pathologist over a long-period of study. After careful review of the description of whole pathology report, we exclude any “necrotic tumor,” “necrotic portion,” or “a few viable tumor cells,” and include “necrosis only,” or “no viable tumor.” In final, we confirmed 18 patients with surgically removed pure RN.

Preoperative MRI interpretation

All patients underwent serial Gd-enhanced brain MRI from the time of RT to surgery, as well as postoperative follow-up imaging. Imaging protocols varied across decades and between institutions. The standard BM MRI protocol of our institution (“MRI Brain (meta with diffusion)”) comprised T1, T2/fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), and Gd-enhanced T1 and did not include susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI). Perfusion MRI was obtained in limited cases based on radiologist suggestion or neurosurgeon’s discretion, and SWI was acquired only on those same occasions; SWI was therefore not systematically available across the cohort. The evaluation of MRI findings was mostly based on formal reading of the MRI. In case of no specific comment for a variable of interest, two independent authors reviewed the image independently, and if there was debate, a third author decided. Thus, written radiology findings by radiologist were made blinded to the final pathological diagnosis. However, unmentioned radiological finding was interpreted by authors, who already knew the cases were included pure RN.

To characterize surgically resected RN lesions, we evaluated T1 and T2/FLAIR signal intensity, the degree of Gd enhancement (homogeneous, rim enhancing, or none), the shape of the lesion (lobulated, round, or irregular), and the existence of central necrosis. We evaluated the degree of perilesional edema on T2/FLAIR images using a tentative definition as follows: 1) mild, maximal edema diameter <1.2×tumor diameter; 2) moderate, maximal edema diameter 1.2–2.0×tumor diameter; and 3) severe, maximal edema diameter >2.0×tumor diameter.

Criteria for radiological response and evaluation period

To accurately evaluate the response to RT, we adopted the Response Assessment in Neuro-Oncology for Brain Metastasis (RANO-BM) criteria published in 2015 [19]. RANO-BM response assessment is based on the sum diameter of one-dimensional measurements, corticosteroid dosing, and clinical status. In brief, we measured the longest diameter of RN on T2-weighted FLAIR images and T1-weighted Gd-enhanced images. Measurable lesions were 10 mm or more in the longest diameter, and the sum of measurable lesions was used in the case of multiple RNs. Complete response (CR) was defined as the disappearance of measurable lesions. Partial response (PR) was defined as a ≥30% decrease in the sum of the longest diameters relative to baseline. Stable disease (SD) was defined as a <30% decrease in the sum of the longest diameters relative to baseline but <20% increase relative to the nadir. Progressive disease was defined as a ≥20% increase in the sum of the longest diameters relative to the nadir.

Statistical analysis

We estimated progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) using the Kaplan–Meier method in SPSS version 18 (SPSS Inc.). Because of the relatively small number of patients, we analyzed other variables and endpoints using a descriptive analysis rather than a statistical analysis.

RESULTS

Clinical characteristics of the patients

A total of 18 patients who underwent surgical resection for what was initially suspected as recurrent BM but ultimately diagnosed as pure RN on pathological examination were included.

The demographic data and clinical characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 1. The median age at surgery was 56 years (range, 44–72 years), and 9 patients (50%) were male. The most common primary malignancy was non-small cell lung cancer (44%), followed by breast cancer (33%). Other primary cancers were small cell lung cancer, renal cell carcinoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, and large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma. Thirteen patients (72%) had controlled systemic disease at the time of surgery based on CT or positron emission tomography (PET) within 3 months prior.

Ten patients (56%) had neurological symptoms attributable to the lesion, including headache, hemiparesis, facial palsy, dizziness, ataxia, dysmetria, and seizure, whereas eight patients remained asymptomatic. The patients’ performance was mostly good enough to maintain independent average daily living as measured by Karnofsky performance status (KPS) score (median 80, range 60–90), except for one patient with a KPS score of 60.

The RT given for BM was a single session of SRS/SRT in nine patients (50%), WBRT combined with SRS/SRT boost to the lesion in seven patients, and repeated SRS/SRT in the remaining two patients (Supplementary Table 1 in the online-only Data Supplement). No patient was given only WBRT for the initial BM. The nominally summated radiation dose ranged from 1,800 cGy/single fraction to 8,300 cGy (2,500 cGy/10 fractions+1,800 cGy/single fraction+4,000 cGy/10 fractions). The median interval from RT for BM to surgery was 28.5 months (range, 6–94 months). Only two patients had an interval from RT to surgery of less than 1 year (6 and 7 months, respectively). Before the decision was made to perform surgery, 14 of 18 patients (78%) had shown radiological response on follow-up MRI after RT. The best response for each patient evaluated by Response Assessment in Neuro-Oncology (RANO) criteria is described in Supplementary Table 2 (in the online-only Data Supplement). Briefly, the best response was CR in 2 patients, PR in 12 patients, and SD in the remaining 4 patients. All of the lesions that showed an initial radiological response increased in size after the best response, and the four SD lesions showed no change in tumor size. Thus, the clinical presumptive diagnosis of all surgically resected RNs was recurrent or refractory BM after RT.

Preoperative radiological findings

Eleven patients (61%) had a single lesion, and seven (39%) had multiple lesions. Among the latter, the number of lesions was two in five patients and three in the other two patients (Supplementary Table 1 in the online-only Data Supplement). Hence, all cases of multiple lesions were considered oligo-metastasis (≤3) at the time of surgical removal. The frontal lobe was the most frequent location of surgically resected lesions (61%), followed by the cerebellum (22%), occipital lobe (11%), and parietal lobe (6%). There was no surgically resected RN located in the basal ganglia and/or brainstem, which suggests that all the lesions were appropriate for surgical access.

We also evaluated the mass shape, signal intensity, central necrosis, enhancement pattern, and perilesional edema of each resected lesion as MRI characteristics (Table 2 and Supplementary Table 2 [in the online-only Data Supplement]). The mass shape was predominantly lobulated/irregular in 15 cases (83%), but it was round in the remaining three cases, mimicking recurrent BM. The median maximal diameter of the lesion was 2.5 cm (range 1.1–4.0 cm). All lesions showed a typical central necrosis and homogeneous enhancement except for one (Case 16), which was round, isointense on T1, dark on T2/FLAIR, and showed faint rim enhancement. Hemorrhagic component was found in 4 cases (22%). Perilesional edema was severe (edema diameter >2.0×tumor diameter) in 14 cases (78%), moderate in 3 cases, and mild (edema diameter <1.2×tumor diameter) in only 1 case (Case 14). Typically, most cases (16/18, 89%) showed increased perilesional edema compared with pre-RT images in terms of the edema diameter, with a median increase of 3.57-fold (range 0.86-fold to 460-fold). The two remaining cases (Cases 8 and 17) maintained almost the same edema diameter (fold change of 0.86 and 0.91, respectively). In terms of the degree of perilesional edema, 11 cases (61%) showed an escalated degree of perilesional edema compared with pre-RT images, and five of the remaining seven cases showed an increased extent of edema, although the degree was the same as in pre-RT images (Supplementary Table 2 in the online-only Data Supplement). Perfusion MRI imaging was available in six patients (Supplementary Table 2 in the online-only Data Supplement). Among these, five cases (83%) showed increased perfusion parameters of regional cerebral blood volume (rCBV) and/or regional cerebral blood flow (rCBF) suggestive of tumor recurrence in line with the formal reports of “residual metastasis with elevated perfusion,” although the degree of increased perfusion was described as “mild.” There was one discrepancy between rCBV and rCBF, with decreased rCBV and increased rCBF (Case 5). A single case showed hypoperfusion of both rCBV and rCBF (Case 1).

Based on MRI images, 16 patients (89%) were radiologically interpreted as having recurrent or residual metastatic tumors with descriptions such as “tumor progression rather than radiation-related change,” “interval increase of residual malignancy,” or “local recurrence,” whereas the remaining two patients were suggested to be more likely to have RN (Table 2). In the two cases in which imaging favored RN (Cases 6 and 15), the corresponding physician asked surgical treatment because of progressive symptomatic mass effect refractory to corticosteroids (Case 6: papilledema and raised intracranial pressure; Case 15: repeated seizures despite anti-epileptic drugs).

Clinical courses after surgical removal of lesions

Thirteen patients (72%) experienced symptomatic improvement or remained neurologically stable after surgery (Table 3). Postoperative KPS score at 3 months after surgery was improved in six patients (33%), remained stable in seven (39%), and worsened in the remaining five. The worsened status was due to involvement of eloquent areas, resulting in aggravated hemiparesis in four patients, one of which developed subdural hemorrhage at the craniotomy site (Case 16), and enlarged visual field defect in the other. All four patients who had preoperative hemiparesis worsen after surgery lost ambulatory function (KPS <70) and had not recovered it at the last follow-up in the medical records.

All patients had postoperative MRI follow-up at various intervals according to each physician’s decision until a median of 13.5 months (range 0.9–84 months). Five patients developed recurrence during follow-up; the median PFS was therefore not reached. All five recurrences occurred in patients with uncontrolled systemic cancer, except for one patient with small cell lung cancer whose systemic disease was controlled on preoperative whole-body PET but who developed leptomeningeal metastasis (Case 2, Supplementary Fig. 1 [in the online-only Data Supplement]). Among the four remaining patients who experienced recurrence, one had a strong suspicion of local recurrence based on the appearance of multiple enhancing nodules along the surgical defect (Case 15, Supplementary Fig. 2 [in the online-only Data Supplement]), and another developed spinal leptomeningeal metastasis (Case 18, Supplementary Fig. 3 [in the online-only Data Supplement]). The remaining two cases of recurrence presented as multiple BM (Case 14, Supplementary Fig. 4 [in the online-only Data Supplement]; Case 17, Supplementary Fig. 5 [in the online-only Data Supplement]), without any evidence of local recurrence.

In the context of pure RN with pathological confirmation, local recurrence is against the expected disease course except in cases of reseeding from systemic progression (Case 15) or leptomeningeal metastasis (Case 2). However, reappearance of enhancing necrotic lesions with substantial edema at the surgical site mimicking local recurrence occurred in two cases that received relatively high RT doses. In Case 18 (spinal leptomeningeal metastasis developed after surgery), the resected RN had received fractionated stereotactic radiotherapy (FSRT) 3,600 cGy/6 fractions+WBRT 3,000 cGy/10 fractions, and an interim MRI taken 12 months after surgery revealed an enlarging necrotic enhancing lesion at the surgical cavity, which later regressed without specific treatment (Supplementary Fig. 3 in the online-only Data Supplement). However, Case 15, a patient with controlled systemic cancer who had received repeated SRT of 4,500 cGy/10 fractions+3,000 cGy/3 fractions at a 14-month interval, showed both clinical and radiological progression after surgical removal of the RN (Supplementary Fig. 2 in the online-only Data Supplement). Although immediate postoperative status was favorable with a KPS score of 80, the same as the preoperative KPS score, gradual deterioration led to a KPS score of 60 at 3 months post-surgery, and the patient expired 19 months after surgery without evidence of systemic progression.

At the end of the follow-up period, nine patients (50%) were alive, and nine had died. The median OS after surgical resection of RN was 34 months (range, 4.3–132 months; 95% confidence interval, 7.4–60 months) (Fig. 2). Among the 13 patients who had achieved systemic cancer control at the time of surgery, two lost systemic control during follow-up. One of these (Case 15, Supplementary Fig. 2 [in the online-only Data Supplement]) had both local and distant recurrence of BM, and the other (Case 16) experienced worsening hemiparesis, was lost to follow-up, and expired 7.1 months after surgery. Eight of the 11 patients who maintained systemic control of their cancer were alive at a mean of 96 months (standard deviation, ±17.1 months) after surgery without evidence of either systemic or central nervous system recurrence.

An illustrative case of symptomatic RN with progressive mass effect successfully managed by surgical resection

A 58-year-old man with systemically controlled EGFR-mutant lung adenocarcinoma (exon 21 deletion) developed a solitary BM in the right frontal lobe and underwent FSRT in April 2018 (3,000 cGy in three fractions) during treatment with gefitinib (Case 6, Fig. 3). After an initial radiological response on 3-month follow-up MRI, 12 months follow-up MRI demonstrated a lobulated enhancing mass measuring approximately 3.9×4.0 cm with central necrosis and marked perilesional edema exerting mass effect, which resulted in progressively worsening headache and visual disturbance. As corticosteroids failed to provide durable symptom control, surgical removal was performed. Permanent histopathological examination confirmed pure RN with no residual tumor. Neurological symptoms improved markedly, and functional status improved from a KPS score of 90 to 100. A recent MRI follow-up at 84 months after surgery did not reveal any enhancing intracranial lesions. This case illustrates the role of surgical resection as an effective treatment for symptomatic RN associated with progressive edema and mass effect.

DISCUSSION

This study aimed to evaluate pathologically confirmed pure RN. The cohort was selected from among BMs surgically resected in our institution after RT. Despite the limits inherent to a retrospective study, several clinically meaningful observations emerged.

Incidence and risk factors for the development of RN from irradiated BM

The reported incidence of RN varies from 0% to 30% depending on the definition of RN (either radiological or histological), the follow-up period after RT, the RT dose prescribed, the quality of neuroimaging, and other factors [18]. Minniti et al. [20] reported a 24% incidence of RN among 310 BMs treated with SRS as primary treatment. Chin et al. [21] reported a 7% incidence of RN among 243 brain tumors treated by Gamma Knife SRS and suggested increased tumor volume, integral dose, repeated radiosurgery to the same lesion, and histological finding of glioma as risk factors. The Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) 90-05 SRS dose-escalation study of recurrent BM or gliomas revealed that the 1- and 2-year incidence of RN was 8% and 11%, respectively [22]. In our study, 18 of 98 (18%) recurrent BMs after RT were confirmed to be pure RN without viable tumors at a median interval from RT of 28.5 months, and half of these patients had received repeated RT either as local SRS/SRT alone or in combination with WBRT. Although we did not calculate the cumulative dose per tumor volume using a biological equivalent formulation [23], re-irradiation to considerable tumor volume (median 2.5 cm diameter) seemed to be a significant risk factor for developing RN.

Characteristics of RN mimicking recurrent BM

A major finding of our study was the frequent radiological resemblance between RN and recurrent BM. A majority (89%) of patients were preoperatively diagnosed as having recurrent/refractory BM, reflecting the substantial overlap in conventional MRI findings between the two entities, as both present enlarging, contrast-enhancing mass lesions accompanied by mass effect and extensive surrounding edema [11,15,18]. We specifically evaluated the increase of perilesional edema compared with pre-RT conditions. The edema diameter increased in 89% of cases (16/18) with a median fold change (calculated as the preoperative edema diameter divided by the pre-RT edema diameter) of 3.57. To our knowledge, our study is the first to report this fold change in perilesional edema. We introduced the edema-diameter fold change because progressive perilesional edema out of proportion to the treated lesion is a recognizable feature of evolving RN. As it can be measured on routinely available T2/FLAIR images without dedicated post-processing, this index could be useful to define evolving RN. However, the fold change of perilesional edema should remain exploratory at this point. To support our findings of increased peri-lesional edema of evolving RN, we should performed a case-control study with recurrent BMs after RT, which was confirmed by the pathology report. Furthermore, the figures of fold-change depended on the pre-RT baseline could be biased by small baseline values in case of “minimal” pre-RT edema (e.g., the outlier fold change of 460 in Case 10).

A viable tumor with intact vasculature from neovascularization is postulated to show higher perfusion and blood volume, resulting in increased rCBV, but this is not the case in the setting of RN. Therefore, hyperperfusion on perfusion MRI has been suggested as a radiological parameter that can differentiate RN from viable brain tumors. Despite the high sensitivity (91%–100%) and specificity (72%–95%) of increased rCBV in selected studies [24,25], the cutoff value differentiating RN from recurrent tumor and the protocol for calculating signal-intensity recovery have not been standardized; moreover, it is difficult to evaluate RN near blood vessels, air, or bone [18]. In this study, only four out of six rCBV measurements on perfusion MRI were elevated, suggesting lower diagnostic yield than expected. Perfusion MRI in our retrospective cohort was performed in limited cases without either specified indication to obtain or the standardized protocol (due to long study period of more than two decades). Thus, there could be modest inconsistent yield and interpretation from technical variability across scanners and vendors. Furthermore, the absence of a standardized rCBV/rCBF threshold for distinguishing RN from recurrence, susceptibility artifact near blood, air, or bone, and the variable timing of perfusion imaging relative to time from RT to the evolving necrotic process. These limitations reinforce that perfusion MRI alone was insufficient for diagnosis in our cohort and that tissue confirmation remained necessary. This conclusion is consistent with previous reports emphasizing that no imaging modality can reliably distinguish RN from recurrent BM. Thus, a multi-modal approach, such as MR spectroscopy, PET-CT, and pathological assessment, is often required [13,18].

Current treatment strategy for RN

It has been reported that some RN might regress or resolve without treatment [26]. Therefore, asymptomatic RN does not require medical intervention except for clinical and radiological follow-up. However, a considerable portion of RN is symptomatic. Kohutek et al. [27] evaluated 271 BMs treated with single-fraction SRS and reported a 26% incidence of RN at a median follow-up of 17 months, with two-thirds of these (47/70) being symptomatic. Minniti et al. [20] similarly reported a 24% (75/310 treated lesions) incidence of RN after SRS for BM less than 3.5 cm in diameter, of which 59% (44/72) were symptomatic. Our proportion of symptomatic RN (56%, 10/18; median tumor diameter of 2.5 cm) was consistent with previous reports.

Corticosteroids have been a preferred treatment option for brain RN, as steroids suppress inflammatory signals and dramatically reduce brain edema. However, corticosteroids do not affect RN per se, and long-term steroid use is frequently accompanied by serious adverse effects such as iatrogenic Cushing syndrome [13]. The efficacy of other treatment options, such as anti-coagulation, anti-platelet agents, and hyperbaric oxygen therapy, is still controversial. The anti-VEGF monoclonal antibody bevacizumab can reduce vascular permeability and brain edema, as the pathophysiology of RN involves endothelial cell dysfunction with concomitant release of vasoactive substances such as VEGF [28]. Since Gonzalez et al. [29] first demonstrated that bevacizumab dramatically reduced findings of RN on both Gd-enhanced and FLAIR MRI, several studies have supported the efficacy of bevacizumab alone or in combination with other chemotherapeutic agents for the treatment of RN or recurrent malignant brain tumors after RT [29,30,31,32]. In our previous study of bevacizumab monotherapy for RN due to BM RT, the objective radiological response rate was 60% (6/10), and the clinical response rate was 90% [33]. However, a standard protocol for bevacizumab treatment of RN, in terms of the appropriate dose and maintenance period, has not yet been established, and a rebound phenomenon after discontinuation of bevacizumab has been reported.

Surgery can be used to remove necrotic lesions in patients with symptomatic RN who show poor response to conservative medical treatment. It can also provide a tissue diagnosis of pure RN, as biopsy might miss scattered tumor cells [17]. In this context, piecemeal removal using a surgical aspirator, rather than en bloc removal, might miss focal or scattered tumor cells within the necrotic tissue. In our study, local and distant recurrence after total removal of pure RN (Case 2) might have been due to missed tumor cells, as systemic cancer was determined to be controlled. Relief of mass effect seemed to result in symptom improvement, but the focal neurological deficit, such as hemiparesis, was not substantially improved. The relief of mass effect from RN after surgery is supported by a reduced steroid requirement and a reduction of perilesional edema. Newman et al. [34] evaluated postoperative MRI after surgical removal of RN in irradiated BM and observed a mean 78% reduction of T2-FLAIR edema by 6 months after surgery, which was durable to last follow-up and correlated with the extent of resection. In our study, Case 13 (subtotal resection) (Supplementary Fig. 6 in the online-only Data Supplement) showed reappearance of a necrotic lesion on MRI 3 months after surgery with worsening symptoms. McPherson and Warnick [35] reported the outcomes of gross total removal of RN using frameless stereotaxis and intraoperative MRI. Although their definition of RN included scattered tumor cells and primary brain tumors, four out of five cases with non-localizing deficit (global aphasia and psychomotor slowing) improved, but two out of four cases of hemiparesis worsened after the removal. This pattern of improved mass effect and unchanged/worsened localizing deficit also occurred in our study. All four patients with preoperative hemiparesis in our study worsened after surgery and did not recover during follow-up. We speculate that compromised local blood flow could hinder postoperative recovery or rehabilitation, unlike surgery for non-irradiated BM, although one case was affected by subdural hemorrhage (Case 16).

A clinically important observation was that post-surgical recurrence was strongly associated with uncontrolled systemic disease: all five recurrences occurred in patients with uncontrolled systemic cancer, with the single exception of a patient with controlled small cell lung cancer who developed leptomeningeal metastasis (Case 2), whereas eight of eleven patients with controlled systemic disease remained alive without recurrence at last follow-up. Systemic disease control therefore appears to be a key determinant of outcome and should inform patient selection.

Limitations and future direction

This study is subject to selection bias because of its retrospective, single-center design. In addition, only surgically resected lesions were included, resulting in a highly selected cohort enriched for symptomatic or radiologically progressive lesions. Consequently, our findings may not be generalizable to all patients with RN. Furthermore, imaging protocols evolved during the extended study period, and advanced imaging modalities such as perfusion MRI were not uniformly available in all patients. Finally, the relatively small sample size limited our ability to perform statistical analyses to identify independent predictors of RN or evaluate the appropriateness of surgical removal according to clinical status. Further large-cohort studies are warranted, using standardized MRI protocols and conservatively managed RN as a control, to define RN, assess the diagnostic value of individual MRI and clinical findings, and determine indications for surgical removal.
