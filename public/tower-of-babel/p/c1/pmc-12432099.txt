Wernicke Encephalopathy Induced by Prolonged Total Parenteral Nutrition in a 16-Year-Old Girl with Acute Myeloid Leukemia
Authors: Eymen Pınar, Burcu Kılınç Oktay, Simge Çınar Özel, Ayşe Gonca Kaçar, Ayşe Kalyoncu Uçar, Osman Kızılkılıç, Alp Özkan, Hilmi Apak, Süheyla Ocak
Published: 2025

Wernicke encephalopathy (WE) is a severe neurological disorder caused by thiamine deficiency, associated with significant morbidity and mortality. Symptoms are often nonspecific, mimicking other neurological conditions, which makes early recognition and intervention critical for preventing irreversible outcomes. Here, we report a rare case of WE induced by prolonged total parenteral nutrition (TPN) in a 16-year-old girl undergoing treatment for acute myeloid leukemia (AML). Written informed consent was obtained from patient and the legal guardian of the patient who participated in this study.

The patient presented with fever, vomiting, fatigue, gingival bleeding, and a 4 kg weight loss over a month. Physical examination revealed gingival hypertrophy. Laboratory tests showed pancytopenia, and bone marrow aspiration confirmed AML-M2 with 55% blasts expressing CD13, CD33, CD117, and myeloperoxidase. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis was normal. The AML-BFM 2019 protocol was initiated, achieving remission (<5% blasts) after the first induction course.

Following the second course of high-dose cytarabine, the patient developed febrile neutropenia, severe mucositis, and intracranial hypotension, resulting in a subdural hematoma, which was surgically drained. Due to severe mucositis, her oral intake was restricted, and she was maintained on TPN for 77 days without ready-made solutions. Oral thiamine (2.5 mg) was not administered routinely during this period.

Before the third chemotherapy course, she developed confusion, dysarthria, limb weakness, and ataxia. Deep tendon reflexes were absent, but there were no meningeal signs. Craniospinal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed hyperintensities in the posteromedial thalami and periaqueductal area, findings consistent with WE (Figure 1). Cerebrospinal fluid analysis excluded central nervous system (CNS) infection or leukemic involvement.

Thiamine therapy was initiated immediately. Neurological improvement was rapid following high-dose thiamine therapy (500 mg intravenous (IV) daily for 5 days, tapered to 200 mg orally over 3 months, and discontinued at the end of the third month). Given her remission status and an HLA-matched sibling donor, allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation was performed successfully. At her last follow-up, the patient exhibited no neurological sequelae.

Thiamine is essential for glucose metabolism and neurotransmitter synthesis. Critically ill patients, especially those undergoing chemotherapy or prolonged TPN, are at increased risk of thiamine deficiency due to increased metabolic demands, oxidative stress, and energy utilization, which can rapidly deplete stores.1-4 These patients require doses exceeding 100 mg daily to maintain adequate plasma and CNS thiamine levels due to increased utilization. Since oral absorption is limited to 4.5 mg/day, intravenous administration is necessary to achieve therapeutic levels.3,5,6 Ready-made TPN solutions typically provide 2.5 mg/L of thiamine, which is insufficient for critically ill patients, and in our case, these solutions were not used.

Historically, 100 mg IV daily was recommended for prophylaxis; however, this dose is now considered insufficient to meet the metabolic demands of critically ill patients.3 For prophylaxis, the Royal College of Physicians recommends 250 mg IV daily for 3-5 days,7 while the European Federation of Neurological Societies suggests 200 mg IV daily, far exceeding the Recommended Dietary Allowance.4,6,7 For the treatment of WE, the literature supports 500 mg IV/IM 3times daily for 2-3 days, followed by 250 mg daily for 3-5 days or until symptom resolution.6,7

Wernicke encephalopathy symptoms, including confusion, ataxia, ophthalmoplegia, and seizures in nonalcoholic patients, require high clinical suspicion for timely diagnosis. Craniospinal MRI findings, such as symmetric hyperintensities in the posteromedial thalami and periaqueductal gray matter, are characteristic but have limited sensitivity (~50%) in early stages.6 Thus, diagnostic confirmation should integrate clinical criteria with imaging findings. In our case, the patient met 3 of the 4 Caine criteria—nutritional deficiency, confusion, and cerebellar signs—and demonstrated a rapid response to thiamine administration, which strongly supports the diagnosis.8 Delayed diagnosis of WE can lead to persistent cognitive impairment, vision problems, and ataxia.9,10

This case underscores the importance of early recognition and prophylactic high-dose intravenous thiamine in patients receiving prolonged TPN or restricted oral intake during intensive chemotherapy. Proactive management with appropriate supplementation can prevent WE, improve outcomes, and reduce long-term sequelae. Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for WE in at-risk patients with nonspecific neurological symptoms, ensuring timely intervention.
