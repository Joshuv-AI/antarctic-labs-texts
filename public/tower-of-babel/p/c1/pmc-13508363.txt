Performance and pilot clinical validation of MALDITEC-CTC: a circulating tumor cell detection platform using whole-cell MALDI-TOF MS fingerprinting in osteosarcoma

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteosarcoma is a highly metastatic bone malignancy, with hematogenous spread as the leading cause of mortality. Circulating tumor cells (CTCs) offer a minimally invasive window to detect metastatic potential and real-time tumor dynamics. However, detecting osteosarcoma CTCs is challenging due to their rarity in the bloodstream and eligibility for positive-enrichment methods targeting epithelial markers.MethodsWe developed MALDI Technology Enabling Classification of Circulating Tumor Cell (MALDITEC-CTC), a novel platform that combines negative-selection CTC enrichment with MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry for osteosarcoma CTC detection. A custom main spectrum profile (MSP) database was generated from normal cells, carcinoma and sarcoma cell lines, patient-derived osteosarcoma cells (PDCs), and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). Using the MALDI Biotyper, CTCs in blood samples were identified by log(score) matching against the MSP database. Diagnostic performance was evaluated in 12 osteosarcoma patients and 11 healthy donors.ResultsCTC-enriched samples from patients showed high log(score) matches to their corresponding PDCs, supporting accurate identification. At the cut-off score of 1.625, MALDITEC-CTC achieved 67% sensitivity and 90.9% specificity with an area under the curve of 0.871 for distinguishing patients from healthy donors. Importantly, CTC-positive patients showed a higher tendency to develop metastasis than CTC-negative patients, indicating potential prognostic value.ConclusionsMALDITEC-CTC demonstrates the clinical feasibility of a rapid, label-free, proteomics-based approach for detecting osteosarcoma CTCs. This platform may enable early risk stratification for metastasis and non-invasive monitoring of tumor progression in osteosarcoma patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-026-04386-0.Keywords: Circulating tumor cells, MALDI-TOF MS, Main spectrum profile, Metastasis, Osteosarcoma

Background

Osteosarcoma is the most common primary bone tumor affecting adolescents and young adults. Although relatively rare, osteosarcoma exhibits complex genetic and chromosomal abnormalities, making it highly aggressive and prone to metastasizing to the lungs and other parts of the body [1]. Patients with osteosarcoma who respond poorly to chemotherapy, even in the absence of metastasis at initial diagnosis, may still develop metastatic disease after completing intensive treatment [2]. The presence of metastasis is a significant predictor of poor prognosis [3, 4], with metastatic osteosarcoma patients often having a worse prognosis and a long-term survival rate of only 30%. In clinical practice, the ability to monitor therapeutic efficacy and detect metastatic disease is critical for improving patient outcomes. Despite advancements in radiological investigations that enable repeated detection of tumor metastases, there is still a need for reliable methods that are highly accurate, easily repeatable, and minimally invasive [5].

Circulating tumor cells (CTCs) play a central role in the hematogenous spread of cancer; they are shed from the primary tumor, enter the bloodstream as single cells or clusters, and migrate to distant sites to form metastases [6]. The detection of CTCs in peripheral blood reflects a key malignant trait, which is the ability to metastasize, even at early stages of disease [7, 8]. CTCs can also serve as indirect biomarkers of tumor progression and response to chemotherapy across cancer types [9, 10]. Due to their strong association with metastasis, CTC detection and enumeration can predict metastatic progression before clinical or radiological evidence emerges and may provide real-time insight into therapeutic response. Accordingly, a reliable CTC detection method would be highly valuable for assessing metastatic risk in osteosarcoma patients.

The most widely used CTC detection platform, CellSearch, relies on epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM)-dependent capture, which is effective for isolating epithelial CTCs but not for CTCs of mesenchymal origin, such as sarcoma [11, 12]. Most CTC detection methods use immunoaffinity-based approaches, employing antibodies against specific antigens expressed on target cancer cells [13]. However, the lack of sarcoma-specific markers limits the effectiveness of marker-based CTC detection in osteosarcoma. In this regard, marker-independent isolation and detection techniques offer a promising alternative by enabling both the enrichment and detection of CTCs from mesenchymal cancers.

The RosetteSep™ human CD45 depletion approach relies on hematopoietic surface antigen expression to negatively enrich intact CTCs directly from whole blood. Unwanted cells are targeted by a tetrameric antibody complex recognizing CD45, CD66b and glycophorin A on hematopoietic cells and removed following separation over a buoyant density medium, resulting in label-free enrichment of CTCs. Reported capture efficiencies for this approach vary by tumor type and assay; approximately 70% in spike-in experiments using head and neck squamous cell carcinoma cell lines enumerated by immunocytochemistry [14] but less than 40% in blood spiked with breast cancer, non-small cell lung cancer, and renal carcinoma cell lines [15–17]. Beyond inter-laboratory differences, CTC recovery is influenced by factors such as cell morphology, size, and the enumeration method used [11, 18]. As with many immunoaffinity-based negative enrichment strategies, residual WBC contamination may be substantial, making it essential to use a highly sensitive downstream method for CTC detection.

Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) has gained widespread clinical adoption and driven substantial advances in disease diagnosis [19]. MALDI-TOF MS directly measures constitutive cellular proteins to generate a characterisic proteomic fingerprint. Identification is performed by matching spectra from unknown samples to reference main spectral profiles (MSPs) curated in the proteomic database. MALDI-TOF MS is now routinely used for microbial identification in clinical laboratories [20]. The MALDI-TOF MS system has been used for pathogen identification in human infectious disease diagnosis, and has become a reference method due to its sensitivity, simplicity, speed, and reproducibility [21, 22]. Beyond routine microbiology, MALDI-TOF-MS has also advanced cancer diagnostics and biomarker discovery by enabling proteins/peptide profiling to support detection, diagnosis, and monitoring of tumor progression across various clinical sample types, including urine and serum [23, 24]. Characteristic MSP patterns allow accurate identification and classification of intact organisms and cell types. The technique requires minimal sample preparation, even for complex specimens, while maintaining high analytical performance in terms of sensitivity, specificity and reproducibility. Notably, Chiangjong et al. [25]. developed a MALDI-TOF MS-based approach to detect CTCs of multiple cancer types in simulated samples. This MALDI-TOF MS–based CTC platform demonstrated a detection limit of 10 cells for non-small cell lung cancer cells; however, it has neither been applied to osteosarcoma nor validated using clinical specimens.

In this study, we established a novel approach that integrates immunodensity-based, label-free CTC enrichment with MSP detection using a MALDI-TOF MS platform to identify osteosarcoma CTCs in clinical blood samples. We developed and validated a custom MSP database comprising various cancer types, including sarcoma cell lines, carcinoma cell lines, and patient-derived osteosarcoma cells (PDCs). After validating methodological feasibility, we evaluated whether this label-free enrichment combined with tumor marker-independent detection could reliably identify CTCs enriched from the peripheral blood of osteosarcoma patients and assess prognostic value of CTC detection for metastasis.

Methods

Patient and healthy participant recruitment protocol

Blood samples were collected from healthy donors (n = 11) with no known illness at the time of collection and no history of malignant disease. Additionally, tissue and blood specimens were obtained from osteosarcoma patients (n = 12) diagnosed at Maharaj Nakorn Chiang Mai Hospital. Ten-milliliter whole blood samples were collected into EDTA anticoagulant tubes at various time points, including before neoadjuvant chemotherapy (pre-chemotherapy) and after neoadjuvant chemotherapy (post-chemotherapy). Tissue samples were obtained from surgical resections or core needle biopsies. The specimens were collected by orthopedic surgeons during routine clinical procedures. All collected tissues were subsequently submitted for histopathological examination, and the diagnosis of osteosarcoma was confirmed by experienced pathologists prior to inclusion in this study. All specimens were collected as part of the osteosarcoma repository program (Suandok Repository Unit (SRU), CMU, EC-No: ID2717, Study code: ORT-2557-02717). The research study was approved by the Committee on Biomedical Research Ethics, Faculty of Medicine, Chiang Mai University (ORT-2563-07284) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided informed consent for study inclusion and were enrolled using institutional review board–approved protocols.

Cell cultures

Following manufacturer’s instructions, cell lines were cultured in the appropriate medium optimized for a given cell type to maintain optimal viability, phenotype, and growth characteristics. For adenocarcinoma cell culture, the hepatocellular carcinoma cell line (HepG2), colorectal adenocarcinoma cell lines (HT-29, SW480, and SW620), and mammary adenocarcinoma cells (MDA-MB-231) were cultured in DMEM, while lung adenocarcinoma cell lines (H1975, HCC827, and H460) were maintained in RPMI-1640. All media were supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 1% L-glutamine.

For osteosarcoma cell culture, U2OS, MNNG/HOS, and SaOS-2 cells were maintained in DMEM-F12 supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 1% L-glutamine, while 143B cells were cultured in DMEM with the same supplements and an additional 100 µg/mL BrdU (Sigma, St. Louis, MO). All cell lines were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Patient-derived primary osteosarcoma cells (PDCs) are established from freshly resected tumor tissues or needle biopsies collected by orthopedic oncology surgeons. Tumor samples (biopsies and resection specimens) were first minced into small pieces (1–3 mm3) and incubated with 5 mg/mL of collagenase type I in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C for 18 h without aggregation. The cell suspension was filtered through 100 μm filters to remove undissociated clumps and debris and was then centrifuged at 2,500 g for 5 min at room temperature. The isolated osteosarcoma cells (Passage 0; P0) were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 20% FBS and 1% L-glutamine and incubated at 37 °C incubator in a humidified atmosphere at 5% CO2.

PDCs were sub-cultured at 90% confluent. Cells (passage 2–4) were harvested and centrifuged at 300 ⋅g for 3 min before resuspension in PBS and cell densities were measured using a hemocytometer. The cells were then aliquoted at the desired concentration into 1.5 mL microtubes and centrifuged at 300 ⋅g for 10 min.

Genome-wide copy number variation (CNV) analysis

Genomic DNA of PDCs was isolated by the QIAamp DNA Micro Kit, Cat. No. / ID: 56,304 (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). CNV was performed using low-pass whole-genome sequencing (1× WGS) data obtained from patient-derived cells. Genomic DNA was prepared using the TruSeq Nano DNA Kit. Paired-end sequencing adapters were trimmed using Cutadapt (v4.9) and Fastp (v1.0.0). The processed reads were aligned to the human reference genome (Genome Reference Consortium Human Build 38, GRCh38.p14) using BWA-MEM. Duplicate reads were removed using GATK as implemented in the fq2bam pipeline (NVIDIA Parabricks v4.7.0). Genome-wide CNVs were inferred using IchorCNA (v0.2.0). Deduplicated BAM files were analyzed to estimate corrected read depth, corrected copy number, and log2 ratios across the genome using 1 Mb windows. For statistical evaluation, linear regression analysis including R² and slope estimation was performed using SciPy (v1.15.2). CNV profiles and regression plots were visualized using Matplotlib (v3.10.1) and Seaborn (v0.13.2).

Von Kossa staining

PDCs were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in 24-well plates and cultured for 14 days in osteogenic media (OSM). OSM consisting of basal medium supplemented with ascorbic acid (50 µg/mL), β-glycerophosphate (10 mM), and dexamethasone (100 nM). Cells were maintained under osteogenic conditions for at least 14 days to induce extracellular matrix mineralization prior to Von Kossa staining [26]. Cells were washed with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature, and then washed three times with deionized (DI) water. For mineralization detection, cells were incubated with 1% silver nitrate solution for 2 min at room temperature. Subsequently, a solution containing 5% sodium carbonate and 10% formaldehyde was added for 2 min, followed by gentle rinsing with DI water. To remove unreacted silver, cells were incubated with 5% sodium thiosulfate solution for 2 min, then rinsed again with DI water and air-dried at room temperature. The assay was conducted in duplicate within a single independent experiment. Simultaneously, the test was performed on hFOB and osteosarcoma 143B cell lines as control groups, and the same basal medium (DMEM) was used as uninduced control to provide a baseline for comparison.

PBMCs isolation and CTCs enrichment

The 10 mL of peripheral blood was collected from each volunteer. An aliquot of 1 mL whole blood was separated for PBMC isolation by density gradient centrifugation method using Lymphoprep™. Subsequently, the remaining 9 mL of whole blood from the same sample was subjected to the CTC enrichment procedure. CTCs were isolated and enriched by an immunodensity method using the RosetteSep™ human CD45 depletion cocktail (StemCell™ Technologies, Canada) combined with the Lymphoprep™ density gradient medium. Briefly, 500 µL (50 µL/ mL) of the RosetteSep™ cocktail was added to the 10-mL whole blood and incubated for 20 min at RT. Blood samples were then diluted with equal volumes of PBS and layered into Sepmate-50™ tube containing Lymphoprep and centrifuged at 1,200 ⋅g at RT for 10 min. Then, the lymphoprep-plasma interface was collected into the new Falcon™ 15-mL conical centrifuge tubes (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). PBMCs pellet and CTC enrichment samples were washed twice by adding the Dulbecco’s Phosphate Buffered Saline (DPBS) and centrifuged at 300 ⋅g for 10 min. After the final wash, 1 mL of 70% methanol was added into pellet and centrifuged at 10,000 ⋅g, 4 °C for 5 min. The pellet was gently resuspended with 10 µL of 70% methanol and dotted on an ITO coated slide (1 µL/dot). Subsequently, the samples were overlaid with a double-layered HCCA matrix. The information will be described in the following section.

CTCs recovery rate assay

To evaluate the recovery rate of RosetteSep™ human CD45 depletion cocktail, 143B were incubated with Hoechst 33,342 in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2, 37 °C, 15 min. A total of 10,000 cells of pre-stained 143B were then spiked into 10 mL of blood samples of healthy donors. Spiked CTCs were enriched as explained previously. Subsequently, fluorescently labeled nuclear cells were resuspended and subjected to fluorescence microscopy. The enumeration of CTC was performed using a Lionheart™ LX automated microscope.

Cell dotting on indium tin oxide (ITO) coated slide

Cells were dotted on ITO coated slides following the protocol described in our previous study [25]. Briefly, cells were washed twice by adding DPBS and centrifuged at 300 ⋅g for 10 min. After the final wash, 106 cells were centrifuged at 10,000 g for 5 min to discard the supernatant and were gently re-suspended in 1 mL of 70% methanol. Following centrifugation, cell pellet was collected, and 10 µL of 70% methanol were added to obtain a cell concentration of 105 cells/µL. The cell suspension was 2-fold serially diluted to achieve the final concentration ranging from 2 to 105 cells/µL. Subsequently, 1 µL of each dilution were spotted on the ITO coated slide and dried at room temperature. The samples were overlaid with 1 µL of alpha-cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid (HCCA) matrix solution (10 mg/mL HCCA dissolved in 50%ACN/0.1%TFA and mixed 1:3 with ACN) and allowed to air dry at room temperature. This step was repeated to acquire a double-layer HCCA matrix covering each sample dot. Cell lines were processed by biological and technical triplicates.

MALDI-TOF-MS

MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry was performed using a Ultraflex TOF/TOF mass spectrometer with flexSeries 1.4 and FlexControl version 3.4 software (Bruker Daltoniks) as previously described with minor modifications [25]. The nitrogen laser was fired onto HCCA coated sample spots on ITO coated slides with pulse ion extraction set to 250 ns. Extraction of released ions was accelerated with a voltage of 25 kV in linear positive mode for the mass range (m/z) of 4000–20,000 Da. Protein I calibration standard containing insulin, ubiquitin I, cytochrome c and myoglobin (Bruker Daltoniks) was used to calibrate with 500 ppm peak assignment tolerance before sample analysis. Each spectrum is derived from the sum of 5,000 laser shots of random walk partial-sample mode with 2000 Hz frequency and 70% laser power performed in five different regions, totally 25,000 laser shots. To obtain high quality spectrum, we accumulated multiple individual mass spectra by summation of 25,000 laser shot from each spot. Briefly, the averaged spectrum was acquired by 5,000 laser shots of random walk partial-sample mode in five different regions within sample spots. Signal accumulation was applied to improve signal-to-noise ratio and minimize shot-to-shot variability to MALDI measurements. Cell spots with varying cell concentrations, including spots containing as few as two cells, were all measured using the same acquisition protocol. Flex Control® (version 3.4) software was used to acquire the data and set the method parameters to adjust baseline subtraction and smoothen background noise. A signal-to-noise ratio of more than 3 was selected to define peaks with a maximum of 100 peaks per spectrum.

MSP database creation by MALDI Biotyper software

A minimum of 25 spectra (Table S1) for each individual cancer cell type derived from different cell concentration (2 to 105 cells per spot) and five spectra per cell concentration were accumulated to create a MSP database by MALDI Biotyper OC version 3.1 software (Bruker Daltonics). The following parameters were used: maximum mass errors of each single spectrum, 2,000; desired mass error for the MSP, 200; desired peak frequency minimum, 25%; maximum desired peak number for the MSP, 70. The hierarchical clustering dendrogram was used as follows: distance measure, correlation; linkage, average; score threshold value for a single organism, 300; and score threshold value for a related organism, 0. For the identification method, the following parameters were set: frequency threshold for spectra adjusting, 50; frequency threshold for score calculation, 5; maximum mass error of raw spectrum, 2,000; desired mass tolerance of the adjusted spectrum, 325; furthermore accepted mass tolerance of a peak 800, parameter of the intensity correction function, 0.25. The spectra derived from 2 to 105 cells of each cell type were predicted for cell type identification. Resulting identification scores were generated using the Bruker Biotyper algorithm ranging from 0.00 to 3.00. The scores reflect composite similarity between sample and the reference MSP based on both peak positions and relative intensities. Higher scores indicate greater similarity; a score of 3.0 represents an identical match to the reference MSP, while a score of 0 indicates complete dissimilarity.

Repeatability and Reproducibility test

Repeatability and reproducibility of MALD-TOF MS was evaluated by using 143B cell lines with different cell concentrations ranging from 100, 103 and 104 cells per spot, and five spectra per cell concentration were taken. Intra-run precision was assessed by having the same samples spotted three times onto ITO coated Slide (three spots per cell concentration) which was then analyzed within the same run. For inter-run assay, the replicates of the same samples were analyzed across three separate days.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed with GraphPad Prism 9 software. Receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was calculated using the Wilson/Brown methods and was applied to generate area under the curve (AUC) with a confidence interval (CI) of 95%. Chi-square test was used to analyze the differences in the incidence of positive CTC detection rates between groups. Survival analysis was performed by the Kaplan-Meier method and a log-rank test.

Results

Establishment of the cancer profiler database

The proposed MALDITEC-CTC platform (Fig. 1) combines WBC (white blood cells) depletion for CTC enrichment with MALDI-TOF MS-based detection in osteosarcoma. This approach relies on matching the MSP of the sample to pre-defined reference MSPs in curated database. A custom MSP database, termed Cancer Profiler, was established from MSPs generated from a diverse set of cancer cell lines and patient-derived osteosarcoma cells, including both carcinoma and sarcoma cell lines, and was integrated into the MALDI Biotyper software (Fig. 2A-C and S1A). PCA showed close clustering of the representative spectra from the biological replicates used to construct the database, indicating a high level of spectral consistency (Figure S1B). Hierarchical cluster analysis of the MSPs showed a close relationship of mass spectral fingerprints within the same cell types (Fig. 3), suggesting internal consistency of the database. Human PBMCs isolated from normal healthy donor and osteosarcoma patients formed a distinct cluster (cluster I), clearly separated from all cancer cell lines, supporting the discrimination power of the Cancer Profiler database.

Within the cancer clade, the largest grouping comprised carcinoma cell lines (clusters II and III), which exhibited an interrelated similarity; these epithelial-derived cancers were primarily organized by tissue of origin. In contrast, sarcoma cell lines (cluster IV) were distinctly separated from the carcinoma group, highlighting lineage-specific spectral differences. Among the osteosarcoma cell lines, 143B was classified independently, while the other osteosarcoma cell lines clustered closely within the same clade. Notably, the osteoblast cell line hFOB nested within the human osteosarcoma clade, indicating an MSP closely aligned with osteosarcoma lines, consistent with its physiological osteoblastic phenotype.

To better capture phenotypic complexity and heterogeneity, we incorporated osteosarcoma PDCs collected from 9 cases, including samples obtained before chemotherapy (n = 7) and after chemotherapy (n = 5), into the Cancer Profiler database (Figs. 2D and 3). The tumor origin of PDCs was further evaluated by genomic characterization in three representative cases (OS7, OS8, and OS10) using copy number variation (CNV) analysis (Fig. 4A.). The results revealed multiple chromosomal gains and losses in paired pre- and post-chemotherapy PDCs, consistent with the genomic instability characteristic of osteosarcoma. However, an exception was observed in patient OS10, in which PDCs obtained prior to chemotherapy did not exhibit detectable CNV alterations. To further evaluate the osteoblastic phenotype of the PDCs, cells were cultured in osteogenic medium to induce osteoblastic differentiation and their ability to mineralize. Subsequently, Von Kossa staining was performed to detect calcium-phosphate deposits associated with matrix mineralization to reflect functional characteristic of osteosarcoma cells. The microscopic examination of mineralized nodules and mineralized formation was observed in PDCs obtained at the time of pre-chemotherapy (naive) and post-chemotherapy after 14 days and up to 21 days when compared with hFOB and 143B cells (Fig. 4B and Figure S2). In addition, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed and examined by a pathologist to confirm that the tumor tissues used for establishing PDC cultures contained viable tumor cells (Fig. 4C). In total, 12 spectral sets were added to the Cancer Profiler database, and the spectra from pre- and post-chemotherapy PDCs were served as tissue-specific MSPs.

Validation of the cancer profiler database and MALDI-TOF MS reproducibility

To evaluate classification performance, the MSPs from all reference cells were matched against the Cancer Profiler database (Fig. 5). Identification scores, which reflected spectral similarity, were consistent with the clustering observed in the MSP dendrogram. The Cancer Profiler database correctly identified the tested MSPs with high confidence scores (up to 3.0), and second-best matches typically corresponded to the same cancer type based on nearest-neighbor relationships. Notably, PDCs achieved the highest identification scores when matched against osteosarcoma PDCs and sarcoma reference MSPs. These results indicate that each cancer cell type and PBMCs possesses a distinct MSP, enabling reliable discrimination between PBMCs and cancer cells, as well as between different cancer types. No significant batch effect was observed across cell batches in the prediction results.

To assess robustness and reproducibility, we evaluated spectral quality using the osteosarcoma cell line 143B. Mass spectra acquired across different batches over time and passages were highly consistent (Figure S3A), demonstrating strong biological reproducibility. The reproducibility of MALDI-TOF MS profiles was confirmed across inter-spot and inter-day conditions. A heat map and quantitative analysis were used to compare three replicate spectra per spot across three consecutive days, showing no significant within-run or between-run variation (Figure S3B, and Table S2).

Together, these results confirm the reliability and reproducibility of the MALDI-TOF platform for cancer cell identification. Our initial Cancer Profiler database demonstrated strong classification performance, accurately distinguishing cancer cell types and effectively discriminating cancer cells from human PBMCs.

Evaluation of CTC recovery and enrichment efficiency

MALDITEC-CTC employed an immuno-density negative depletion strategy targeting multiple blood cell markers, including CD45 (pan-leukocyte), CD66b, and glycophorin A on red blood cells. To evaluate the feasibility and performance of this approach, we assessed both the recovery of 143B cells and the enrichment efficiency. A total of 10,000 spiked 143B cells were spiked into 10 mL of whole blood and subjected to enrichment. The recovery rate of the spiked tumor cells was 65.1% ± 1.7233(mean ± SD) as shown in Figure S4A. WBC depletion ranged from 91 to 98% (median 98%), corresponding to an average ~ 2-log reduction of pre-enriched PBMCs. Variability in % depletion was primarily influenced by differences among blood donors (Figure S4, B-C).

Diagnostic performance of MALDITEC-CTC for osteosarcoma at diagnosis

We evaluated the diagnostic performance of MALDITEC-CTC in a cohort of patients with osteosarcoma (n = 12) and healthy donors (n = 11) (Figure S4). CTCs were enriched from 12 peripheral blood samples of osteosarcoma collected at the time of diagnosis before receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis identified an optimal cutoff of 1.625 for CTC detection. At this threshold, MALDITEC-CTC achieved a sensitivity of 67.0%, specificity of 90.9%, positive predictive value (PPV) of 89%, and negative predictive (NPV) of 71% for distinguishing osteosarcoma patients from healthy controls. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was 0.871 (95% CI 0.724–1.000; P = 0.0026) (Fig. 6A). In CTC-enriched samples from patients, most MSPs matched their corresponding PDC MSPs, yielding higher log(scores) compared to matches with osteosarcoma cell lines or other cancer types (Fig. 6B). An unexpected single false-positive result was observed among healthy donors (1/11) (Fig. 6C).

To further validate the accuracy and feasibility of MALDITEC, we analyzed longitudinal blood samples collected from patients after receiving chemotherapy (n = 8). The assay was able to detect CTCs in 75% of samples (6 of 8) as shown in Fig. 6C.

Prognostic value of CTC detection for metastasis

We assessed the association between CTC positivity and clinicopathological features (Table 1). No significant associations were observed with sex, age, stage, tumor size, serum alkaline phosphatase (ALP), or chemotherapy response. Although not statistically significant, most CTC-positive patients had high-grade tumors (73%; n = 8).

Among 12 patients initially enrolled, follow-up data were unavailable for three due to loss to follow-up. The prognostic value of baseline CTCs was therefore assessed in the remaining nine patients with osteosarcoma, with a median follow-up of 28.4 months (range: 5.3 to 36.7 months). For prognostic performance analysis, we further evaluated different cut-off values and found that a cut-off of 1.700 provided superior predictive performance for metastasis-free survival (MFS), as the higher threshold improved specificity for prognostic stratification of patients at risk of metastasis. CTCs were detected in the peripheral blood samples of 4 out of 9 patients (44%). Among these, two CTC-positive patients (OS2 and OS10) developed distant metastases postoperatively with MFS of 11.6 months and 5.3 months, respectively. Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed that CTC-positive patients had a shorter metastasis-free survival compared to CTC-negative patients (Fig. 5D), although this difference showed a trend toward statistical significance (log-rank p = 0.0888, HR; 11.62, 95% CI; 0.6891 to 196.0). Overall, CTC positivity at diagnosis was associated with a higher likelihood of subsequent metastasis, suggesting that preoperative CTC detection may serve as an early indicator of metastasis risk in osteosarcoma.

Discussion

CTC analysis remains challenging due to their low frequency in the bloodstream. Many studies have been constrained by technical difficulties in isolating CTCs from a large background of normal blood cells, which vastly outnumber CTCs. In addition, the phenotypic heterogeneity of CTCs makes high-precision detection difficult. Current EpCAM-based enrichment assays, such as CellSearch, do not efficiently detect cells with non- or low-EpCAM expression [27–29]. This limitation particularly affects tumor cells undergoing epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) [30] and non-epithelial cancers such as sarcomas [31, 32]. Likewise, identifying CTCs originating from mesenchymal tumor types is challenging due to the paucity of tumor-specific surface markers. There is an urgent need for efficient CTC detection methods capable of capturing all CTC subtypes and robustly distinguish them from surrounding blood cells. To address this gap, we developed MALDITEC-CTC, which integrates CD45-based negative enrichment, a marker-independent approach that minimizes selection bias and enables capture of heterogeneous CTC populations. This enrichment strategy is coupled with intact-cell MALDI-TOF MS, a proteomics-based technique that can discriminates cell types based on protein expression patterns with higher-resolution of cellular characterization. In contrast to antibody-based CellSearch® assay, that relies on epithelial markers (e.g., EpCAM and cytokeratins) and therefore detect only EpCAM-positive populations. Our platform does not rely on predefined surface markers. As a result, it enhances the detection of EpCAM-low or EpCAM-negative cells, that are often missed by conventional methods.

Using the MALDITEC-CTC, we successfully identified CTCs from 10 mL blood samples obtained from osteosarcoma patients. MSPs, which serve as reference spectra in the MALDI Biotyper, are composed of characteristic peak sets assigned to specific cell types or species. Identification is achieved by comparing the mass spectra of an unknown sample against the MSP database. While the MALDI Biotyper provides established score thresholds for bacterial identification, which indicate high to low confidence at the species and genus levels [33], no standardized score cut-offs currently exist for mammalian cell lines. In this study, a cut-off score of 1.625 was established by ROC analysis for the identification of CTCs in osteosarcoma patients and was used for diagnostic classification. This score enabled reliable and unambiguous identification of unknown circulating cells by matching their mass spectra to the reference MSPs in the database. Notably, most CTC-positive samples showed spectral concordance with PDCs, which were established from primary tumor tissues and maintained key genomic alterations and osteogenic phenotype such as mineralization. These findings support that the MSP database represents authentic osteosarcoma tumor signatures and reinforces the reliability of the spectral matching approach for CTC identification.

MALDITEC-CTC is a platform specifically designed for seamless integration into routine clinical laboratory workflows. Our enrichment protocol utilizes blood cell depletion, including WBCs and RBCs, combined with immunodensity separation, employing fundamental techniques that are easily implemented in hospital laboratories. The enriched CTCs can then be deposited as spots on MALDI target slides, allowing samples to be collected from multiple sites and shipped to a centralized laboratory for batch analysis. This workflow facilitates broader adoption in clinical practice and is especially well-suited for wider hospital networks and CTC identification in resource-limited settings.

CTCs represent an attractive, truly cellular component for noninvasive analyses and have demonstrated prognostic value in several solid cancers [34]. However, for osteosarcoma, there are no clinically standardized, widely adopted circulating biomarkers for early detection or robust prognostication; conventional blood tests such as ALP and LDH are nonspecific and variably informative. Using a 12-patient validation cohort, we defined an optimal diagnostic threshold for MALDITEC-CTC. The results support the diagnostic utility of CTC detection in osteosarcoma, though the sensitivity remains moderate (< 90%). Notably, among the CTC-positive patients, one had dedifferentiated parosteal osteosarcoma (DPOS), a rare low-grade osteosarcoma subtype that can transform into high-grade sarcoma. This indicated that CTC detection may reflect the underlying biological aggressiveness and potential for malignant transformation in such cases. Tumor differentiation has been associated with CTC burden; studies in colorectal cancer reported higher CTC shedding in poorly differentiated tumors compared with well-differentiated tumors [35], and in thyroid cancer, CTC levels have been linked to differentiation status [36]. Elevated CTC levels in poorly differentiated tumors may reflect aggressive biology and increased metastatic potential.

Our data suggest that CTC-positive tumors are linked to the aggressiveness and progression of osteosarcoma. Given the aggressive nature and typically large tumor burden in osteosarcoma, one might expect a higher proportion of CTC-positive cases. However, in this study, CTCs were detected in only 67.7% of cases. This observation indicates that specific pathological features might affect the dissemination of CTCs. Future studies with a larger cohort will help clarify the relation of CTC detection and clinical factors, including tumor size, volume, and location or other biological characteristics of CTCs.

Tumor size/volume is a key component of staging and prognosis, but its relationship with CTCs is inconsistent (Table 1). Multiple studies have reported discordance between tumor burden and CTC counts; some patients with small tumors (e.g., hepatocellular, cervical, lung, or prostate cancers) exhibit high CTC levels, whereas others with larger tumors have low or undetectable [37–40]. CTCs can also be detected in early-stage disease [41]. Moreover, CTCs may not correlate with serum protein markers commonly used for detection or monitoring (e.g., PSA, AFP), suggesting that CTCs can be present before tumors reach substantial size and may provide information about tumor biology and metastatic potential that is independent and complementary to size-based metrics.

Metastases are associated with poor prognosis in osteosarcoma, and there are no clinically established circulating biomarkers that reliably predict metastatic progression. In our cohort, baseline CTC positivity showed a trend toward predicting subsequent metastasis. It is noteworthy that many patients had intermediate-stage disease, which may shed low CTC levels near or below the assay’s detection limit. In practice, CTC analysis will likely be most informative when combined with other biomarkers to create a more comprehensive picture of disease status. Integrating CTCs with tumor features or blood-based markers; such as ALP, LDH, or circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), may improve diagnostic and prognostic performance in osteosarcoma [42–44].

There are some limitations in this study as follows; (1) The relatively small cohort size limited the statistical power to detect significant differences in metastasis-free, recurrence-free, or overall survival. Future studies involving larger patient cohorts, particularly through multi-center study, would increase the statistical power and improve the robustness of the findings. Expanding the cohort size may also enhance the sensitivity of CTC detection and allow a more accurate assessment of the clinical performance of CTC-based assays in osteosarcoma. In addition, longer follow-up periods are required to further substantiate the prognostic value of CTCs in predicting disease progression and long-term clinical outcomes. (2) The conventional CTC identification methods such as immunofluorescence (IF) or fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) was not feasible in the current study. IF-based identification is particularly challenged by the high WBCs contamination, as certain leukocyte subpopulations may exhibit overlapping marker expression or autofluorescence [45]. Furthermore, CD45, the negative staining marker to exclude leukocyte may exhibit low or absent CD45 expression in some population, leading to false-positive or ambiguous classification.

In conclusion, we establish an unbiased, marker-independent platform for CTC detection and evaluate its clinical utility in osteosarcoma. This study provides a conceptual framework and proof-of-principle for diagnostic and prognostic CTC analysis using a simple and rapid MALDI-TOF MS–based approach. The MALDITEC-CTC platform is sensitive and scalable for use with clinical samples, showing strong potential as a tool for cancer diagnosis and risk stratification.
