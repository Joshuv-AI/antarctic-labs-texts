Long-term trends in HIV-related stigma among older adults with and without HIV: a cohort study from Mbarara, Uganda
Authors: Chase Mandell, Janet Seeley, Samuel Maling, Noeline Nakasujja, Robert Paul, Zahra Reynolds, Eliza Passell, Christine S Ritchie, Susanne S Hoeppner, Flavia Atwiine, Deanna Saylor, Meredith Greene, Edna Tindimwebwa, Jeremy A Tanner, Brianne Olivieri-Mui, Stephen Asiimwe, Mark J Siedner, Alexander C Tsai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesPerceived HIV stigma is a persistent challenge, contributing to reduced antiretroviral therapy adherence among people with HIV. Although perceived stigma declined with treatment rollout, it is unknown if that trend has continued, particularly among the growing population of older adults with HIV in sub-Saharan Africa.Research Design and MethodsWe analyzed data from 6 waves of data collection over 9 years in a cohort of older adults with and without HIV in Uganda. Participants completed a standardized 15-item perceived stigma questionnaire at each visit. We fitted generalized estimating equations to characterize trends in stigma over time, stratified by HIV serostatus.ResultsWe analyzed follow-up data on 271 individuals with a median duration of 9 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 8–9). Approximately 51% were people with HIV, 47% were women, and the median age was 51 years (IQR: 48–55) at baseline. Compared to 2015, perceived stigma decreased among people with HIV through 2018 with further declines up to 2024 (p < .05 for all comparisons). By contrast, among people without HIV, after a decrease from 2015 to 2018 (p < .05), perceived stigma increased substantially, such that levels in 2022 were similar to 2015 (p = .92).Discussion and ImplicationsPerceived stigma has decreased over the past decade among older people with HIV but remains at a similar level in the early 2020s as in 2015 among those without HIV in Uganda. These findings highlight the importance of maintaining HIV sensitization efforts in the general population, even in an era with decreasing HIV-related deaths.

Background and objectives

Since the beginning of the HIV epidemic, the stigma associated with HIV has remained an intractable public health problem in sub-Saharan Africa because it poses multiple barriers to ending the epidemic (Nyblade et al., 2021). People with HIV in sub-Saharan Africa often perceive negative attitudes from community members about their HIV serostatus or anticipate that community members would have negative attitudes if their HIV serostatus were to become known (Meyer et al., 2023; Visser, 2018; Wolfe et al., 2008). Among people without HIV, this perceived stigma is associated with lower rates of HIV testing (Akatukwasa et al., 2021; Ayiga et al., 2013; Chesney & Smith, 1999; Kalichman et al., 2020; Kelly et al., 2016). Among people with HIV, stigma is associated with poorer antiretroviral therapy (ART) adherence (Akatukwasa et al., 2021; Katz et al., 2013; Rao et al., 2007; Rintamaki et al., 2006) and more severe symptoms of depression and anxiety (Meyer et al., 2023; Parcesepe et al., 2023; Tsai et al., 2012). Thus, ending the stigma attached to HIV has been described as a key milestone in ending the HIV epidemic (Nyblade et al., 2021).

Evidence suggests that the scale-up of ART corresponds to a significant reduction in HIV-related stigma (Chan et al., 2015a; Wolfe et al., 2008). However, since access to ART rapidly expanded in sub-Saharan Africa, little empirical study of long-term trends in perceived HIV stigma has been conducted. One analysis of data from rural Uganda in 2006–2011, a period of rapid ART expansion, showed that perceived stigma increased in the general population, and internalized stigma increased among people with HIV presenting for HIV care, despite an apparent decline in self-reported interpersonal distancing in the population (Chan et al., 2015b). Interpersonal distancing, also referred to as social distancing, is defined as a desire or tendency to avoid contact, closeness, or association with an individual or group because they have a socially devalued characteristic (Albrecht et al., 1982; Bogardus, 1925; Link et al., 1987). These findings are consistent with data from 31 African countries during 2003–2013 (Chan & Tsai, 2016), demonstrating that perceived stigma increased despite declining self-reported interpersonal distancing.

We sought to characterize changes in perceived stigma using data collected during the current treat all era with ART regimens, during which time HIV mortality has decreased even further. To accomplish this goal, we leveraged a well-established cohort of older adults aged over 40 years in Uganda between the years of 2015, when ART became available to all irrespective of CD4+ T-cell count (World Health Organization, 2015), and 2024 to assess the extent to which levels of perceived HIV stigma among older adults with and without HIV have changed over time. We also aimed to identify sociodemographic and clinical correlates of perceived stigma. We hypothesized that perceived stigma decreased over calendar time, but that the decrease was more pronounced among people with HIV than among people without HIV.

Research design and methods

Conceptual framework

Multiple theoretical frameworks have been used to explain socio-ecological determinants of stigmatization. Our analysis draws on the Health Stigma and Discrimination Framework (Stangl et al., 2019), which conceptualizes HIV-related stigma as being driven by a confluence of factors. Following this conceptual model, we conceptualize HIV stigma as a time- and context-sensitive social process in which drivers (e.g., fear of infection, moral judgment/blame) and facilitators (e.g., laws/policies and institutional practices) shape the extent to which individuals with stigmatized conditions are “marked” (i.e., socially identified as having a devalued characteristic) as well as the extent to which stigma is manifested through experiences and practices. With this framing acknowledged, our primary interest is in describing sample-wide trends in HIV stigma, with calendar-year indicators serving as proxies for historically situated shifts in the stigma environment (e.g., changes in HIV-related discourse, policy, and service systems) that can alter drivers and facilitators of stigma and, therefore, levels of reported stigmatizing beliefs observed in the sample over the decade.

Study design and population

We analyzed data from four waves of study visits (2015–2018) from the Ugandan Non-Communicable Diseases & Aging Cohort Study (UGANDAC) and two waves of study visits (2022 and 2024) from the Quality of Life and Aging with HIV in Rural Uganda Study. We did not have study visits that measured stigma in 2019, 2020, 2021, and 2023. The former was a parent cohort of the latter, which enrolled the same participants. Both studies were observational cohorts of older adults with and without HIV in southwestern Uganda and have been described in detail previously (Siedner, 2019; Siedner et al., 2016a, 2016b). In short, we recruited participants over 40 years of age who were living with HIV, had been on stable ART for a minimum of 3 years, and were involved in ambulatory HIV care at either the Mbarara Regional Referral Hospital or the Kabwohe Clinical Research Centre (two public healthcare facilities in Uganda). The age threshold of 40 years was selected because at the time this study was initiated, median life expectancy for people with HIV in Uganda at age 20 years was approximately 20 years for men and 30 years for women (Mills et al., 2011; Tsai & Siedner, 2015), so the age threshold of 65 years that is typically used to define older adults in many studies in better-resourced countries would have been inappropriate (Ferreira & Kowal, 2006; Gómez-Olivé et al., 2018). We then leveraged household census data to recruit community-based, HIV-negative comparators from the clinic catchment areas who were age- (within quartiles) and sex-matched to people with HIV (Takada et al., 2019). We restricted our analytic sample to participants who completed a minimum of three study visits to enable us to characterize both within-participant change as well as population-level change over calendar time.

Outcome measures

Our primary outcome of interest was perceived HIV stigma. To estimate this, all participants completed a previously validated, 15-item questionnaire to elicit multiple dimensions of perceived HIV stigma, including desires for social distance, negative attributions, and concerns about reciprocity (Tsai et al., 2021). Assessment of its construct validity was determined from qualitative work conducted in Mbarara and other settings in Eastern and Southern Africa (Tsai et al., 2013, 2017, 2021). Based on the scale development study (Tsai et al., 2021), it has a 3-factor structure encompassing three dimensions of negative attitudes toward people with HIV. The first two dimensions assess (a) fear of acquiring HIV through routine social interaction, and (b) the tendency to assign blame, make moral judgments, and hold people with HIV personally responsible. These are established drivers of negative attitudes toward people with HIV and align with Pryor et al. (1989)’s “instrumental” versus “symbolic” framework. The third dimension assesses doubts about the capacity of people with HIV to participate reciprocally in local systems of mutual support.

Of the 15 questions asked, five were “yes” or “no” questions (e.g., “In your personal opinion, would you be willing to care for a relative who is sick in bed because of HIV/AIDS?”), with the additional options of “it depends on knowing more details” or “do not know.” We considered a response of “no” to be an unambiguously stigmatizing response and a response of “yes,” “it depends on knowing more details,” or “do not know” as otherwise. The remaining 10 items were “agree” or “disagree” questions (e.g., “In your personal opinion, do you agree or disagree with the following statement? HIV/AIDS is a divine punishment for doing immoral things”), with an additional response option of “do not know.” We considered a response of “agree” to be an unambiguously stigmatizing response and a response of “disagree” or “do not know” as otherwise. Participants who refused to answer a question were also grouped as otherwise. We then defined perceived stigma in two ways: (a) as a continuous variable, which we specified by calculating the total number of stigmatizing responses, or (b) as a dichotomous variable, which we defined as being in the highest quartile of the total stigma score. We estimated the scale’s internal consistency reliability in this sample using Cronbach’s alpha (α = 0.75).

Exposure measures

Our primary exposures of interest were calendar year and HIV serostatus. Calendar year indicators function as the exposures, capturing secular shifts in the social/structural context of stigma. HIV serostatus demarcates our sample into the “stigmatized” and the “stigmatizers” (Pescosolido et al., 2008). Following VanderWeele (2019)’s principles of confounder selection, we selected additional covariates for adjustment based on substantive knowledge that they are plausible causes of HIV-stigma reporting and/or proxies for common causes (Bebell et al., 2021; Chan & Tsai, 2016; Chan et al., 2015b). We accordingly adjusted for age and sex, and for core socioeconomic and social-position measures—educational attainment (Tsai & Venkataramani, 2015), household asset wealth, marital status, and employment status—because these characteristics plausibly shape stigma vulnerability and are also empirically liable to vary across years. To operationalize household asset wealth, we applied principal component analysis to a series of variables capturing household-owned assets and housing characteristics (Filmer & Pritchett, 2001; Smith et al., 2020). From this continuous variable, we created a categorical variable based on quartiles of household asset wealth. We additionally adjusted for comorbidity burden, specified as the total number of self-reported health worker-diagnosed comorbidities, inclusive of hypertension, diabetes, high cholesterol, heart attack/heart failure, kidney disease, stroke, and cancer. This enables us to account for underlying health status and healthcare contact that may influence stigma experiences and reporting, thereby improving precision of year-to-year comparisons conditional on measured covariates. We aimed to avoid over-adjustment so that covariate control supports interpretation of year coefficients as differences in mean stigma over time, standardized to a common distribution of measured sociodemographic and health characteristics.

Statistical analysis

We compared sociodemographic characteristics of study participants at baseline by HIV serostatus, using the Wilcoxon rank sum test for continuous variables and the chi-squared test for categorical variables. We also summarized HIV-specific clinical characteristics (i.e., CD4+ T-cell count and HIV-1 RNA viral load suppression). To assess within-participant changes in stigma over time, we calculated a crude change in stigma score by subtracting the first stigma score from the last for each participant and dividing it by the number of years between the two estimates. To estimate associations between perceived stigma and both year and HIV serostatus, we used linear and logistic generalized estimating equations (GEE), specifying the continuous stigma outcome as the dependent variable in the linear GEE and the dichotomous stigma outcome as the dependent variable in the logistic GEE. We fitted models with and without the confounders described above and included a time × HIV serostatus product term to assess for a potential interaction. In a subgroup analysis restricting estimation to people with HIV, we additionally adjusted for ART regimen at baseline study visit, years on ART at baseline study visit, nadir CD4+ T-cell count, and HIV-1 RNA viral load undetectability (defined as < 200 copies/ml) at any study visit. All analyses were conducted in Stata version 18.0.

Ethical considerations

Study participants gave written informed consent. Study procedures were reviewed and approved by the Massachusetts General Brigham and Mbarara University of Science and Technology human subjects review boards. We also obtained clearance to conduct the study from the Uganda National Council for Science and Technology.

Results

Cohort characteristics

Of the 296 study participants enrolled in the parent cohort, 271 (91.6%) completed a minimum of three study visits across the UGANDAC Study and the Quality of Life and Aging with HIV in Rural Uganda Study from 2015 to 2024 (Table 1). Participants completed a median of five (interquartile range [IQR]: 5, 5) study visits, with 34 (12.6%) participants completing six visits, 170 (62.7%) participants completing five visits, 39 (14.4%) participants completing four visits, and 28 (10.3%) participants completing three visits. The median number of observation years was 9 [IQR: 8, 9]. At the baseline visit, 51.3% (n = 139) were people with HIV, 47.2% (n = 128) were women, and the median age was 51 years [IQR: 48, 55]. Compared to people without HIV, people with HIV were less likely to be farmers (n = 79 [56.8%] vs. n = 111 [84.1%], p < .001), less likely to be married or cohabitating (n = 53 [38.1%] vs. n = 87 [65.9%], p < .001), and more likely to belong to the wealthiest asset index quartile (n = 49 [35.3%] vs. n = 17 [12.9%], p < .001). Most (n = 239 [88.2%]) participants had attained a primary school level of education or had received no formal education, and only a quarter (n = 72 [26.6%]) of participants had at least one non-HIV comorbidity. Among people with HIV at baseline, the median duration of ART use was 8.1 years [IQR: 7.4, 9.0], and the median nadir CD4+ T-cell count was 121 cells/mm3 [IQR: 74, 186]. Most (n = 67 [62.0%]) were taking the ART regimen 3TC/AZT/NVP, yet the majority (n = 88 [81.5%]) had switched to 3TC/TDF/DTG by their most recent visit. Of the 134 [96.4%] people with HIV who had their viral load measured within 12 months of their baseline visit, nearly all (n = 129 [96.3%]) had an undetectable viral load.

Perceived stigma at baseline by HIV serostatus

Among the 224 participants who completed their initial study visit in 2015, nearly all (n = 195 [87.1%]) reported at least one stigmatizing belief, and the median level of perceived stigma was 3.0 [IQR: 1.0, 5.0], which did not differ significantly by HIV serostatus (people with HIV: 2.5 [IQR: 1.0, 5.0] vs. people without HIV: 3.0 [IQR: 1.0, 4.0], p = .53). A quarter (n = 58 [25.9%]) had high levels of perceived stigma in 2015, which also did not differ significantly by HIV serostatus (people with HIV: n = 37 [26.8%] vs. people without HIV: n = 21 [24.4%], p = .69).

Changes in perceived stigma over time

Among people with HIV, perceived stigma continuously declined during the study period, whereas among people without HIV, perceived stigma continuously declined until 2018, rebounded in 2022, and then declined again in 2024 (Figure 1). Looking at within-participant changes over time, the average change in the level of perceived stigma between the last study visit and first study visit was -0.18 points/year (standard deviation [SD]: 0.55), which was larger among people with HIV compared to people without HIV (people with HIV: -0.25 points/year [SD: 0.64] vs. people without HIV: -0.10 points/year [SD: 0.43], p = .02). Viewed differently, 162 [59.8%] study participants had a lower level of perceived stigma at their last study visit compared with their first, and 57 [21.0%] had no change in their level of stigma, while only 52 [19.2%] had a higher level of stigma at their last study visit compared with their first.

In the adjusted multivariable linear GEE model, the level of perceived stigma was lower in 2017 (b=-0.66, 95% CI, -1.14 to -0.18), 2018 (b=-1.28, 95% CI, -1.93 to -0.63), and 2024 (b=-0.94, 95% CI, -1.51 to -0.36), compared to baseline (Table 2). The interaction term between time × HIV serostatus was negative in 2022 (b=-1.06, 95% CI, -1.70 to -0.41) and 2024 (b=-0.88, 95% CI, -1.53 to -0.23), indicating that the estimated association between year and stigma differed by HIV serostatus: people with HIV experienced larger decreases in stigma compared to people without HIV. Using a Wald-type F test, we rejected the null hypothesis that all of the regression coefficients on the time × HIV serostatus product terms were jointly equal to zero (p = .006). This finding suggests that there was a divergence in stigma trends by HIV serostatus. There were no other statistically significant correlates of perceived stigma. Results were similar in models that considered stigma as a dichotomous variable (Table 3; Supplementary Figure 1). Discussion and implications

In this mixed HIV cohort study of older adults from Uganda spanning nearly a decade from 2015 to 2024, we found evidence of persistently declining perceived HIV stigma. These data provide reassurance that the expansion of HIV treatment programs has potentially reduced perceived stigma from the perspective of people with HIV in this setting. By sharp contrast, among people without HIV, after an initial decline during 2015–2018, perceived HIV stigma appeared to rise in the early 2020s to its highest level in the past decade. This second finding illustrates the fragility of improved perceptions of HIV (and of people with HIV) in the general population and—consistent with other research findings from South Africa demonstrating that ART expansion alone is an insufficient anti-stigma intervention (Treves-Kagan et al., 2016)—reinforces the need for persistent sensitization programs to ensure HIV-related stigma and discrimination do not re-emerge.

Existing reports on trends in HIV-related stigma present contrasting findings depending on the population surveyed (people with HIV vs. people without HIV) and depending on the time period under study. Most data have been collected among people with HIV and focused on documenting temporal changes in internalized stigma and experiences of enacted stigma. Prior to the test and treat era, when HIV mortality remained quite high, studies from Uganda found evidence of increasing trends in internalized stigma among people with HIV (Chan et al., 2015b). A more recent study of people with HIV in Uganda, Kenya, Tanzania, and Nigeria (Esber et al., 2022) found a declining trend in enacted stigma experiences from 2013 to 2018, which is also consistent with findings from people with HIV enrolled in the 2013–2018 HPTN 071 (PopART) trial in Zambia and South Africa (Stangl et al., 2020).

In contrast, studies of HIV-related stigma in general population samples have yielded mixed findings. Maughan-Brown (2010) analyzed data from the Cape Area Panel Survey and found that both interpersonal distancing and perceived stigma increased from 2003 to 2006. Visser (2018) conducted two cross-sectional surveys in Pretoria, South Africa in 2004 and 2016 and found that interpersonal distancing and negative attitudes toward people with HIV declined while perceived stigma did not (and perhaps slightly increased)—a finding that was not consistent with data from the general population sample of the 2013–2018 HPTN 071 (PopART) trial in Zambia and South Africa, in which levels of perceived stigma declined while levels of self-reported interpersonal distancing did not (Stangl et al., 2020).

Our study combined long-term data collection on stigma among both people with HIV and people without HIV in a cohort of older adults, a population of increasing public health relevance, given ART-related advances in life expectancy in the region (Hontelez et al., 2012; Siedner, 2019). There are no other longitudinal studies of older people with HIV, much less mixed-serostatus cohorts, that provide insights into the changing dynamics of stigma in this population over such an extended time frame. One analysis of 2014–2019 data from the Health and Aging in Africa: A Longitudinal Study of an INDEPTH Community in South Africa found that higher levels of stigma were associated with reduced HIV testing uptake (Mahlalela et al., 2024). In that article, however, changes in the degree of stigma over time (i.e., between 2014 vs. 2019) were not presented.

Although the cause of increasing stigma identified among people without HIV is not clear, several events that occurred in Uganda between 2018 and 2022 may have contributed to the increase in perceived stigma among people without HIV during this period. Using socio-ecological perspectives (Stangl et al., 2019), we considered how various structural and social forces may have led to upticks in stigma in this population. First, the Ugandan parliament passed the Sexual Offences Bill, 2019, in May 2021, which criminalized same-sex sexual relations and imposed stricter criminal sentences for people with HIV compared to people without HIV (Nakkazi, 2021). While ultimately vetoed by President Yoweri Museveni, civic discussion about and parliamentary passage of the Bill could have been associated with increased prejudicial views against the LGBTQ community, which is a population that bears a disproportionate share of the HIV burden in sub-Saharan Africa (Hladik et al., 2017; Jobson et al., 2018; Keshinro et al., 2016). Second, the global mpox outbreak in 2022 may have also heightened prejudicial views toward men who have sex with men in Uganda—a particularly vulnerable group to HIV infection (Acharya et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025). Third, the COVID-19 pandemic may have had implications for HIV-related stigma by contributing to medical misinformation. In Uganda, misinformation about the COVID-19 vaccines was pervasive on social media platforms and the Internet more generally, promoting some vaccine hesitancy in the region (Amodan et al., 2025). These COVID-19 vaccines, developed early in the pandemic, utilized mRNA technology, which is also the technology being used to create HIV vaccines. In 2022, an analysis found that conspiracy theories about mRNA HIV vaccines were conspicuously circulated on the social media platform “X” (Zhang et al., 2024). In addition, a United States study of 247 adults found that those who harbored negative attitudes toward people with COVID-19 also tended to have negative attitudes toward people with HIV (Wong & Paat, 2023). This result is significant because one study conducted in rural South Africa, a location that is comparable to rural Uganda, found that COVID-19 stigma was highly common, especially among older populations (Mashinini et al., 2024).

Strengths of this analysis include the large sample size with nearly 10 years of observation and a high retention rate. The sample was also evenly divided by HIV serostatus and gender, allowing for comparisons between these groups. There are also several limitations to this analysis.

First, participants did not complete stigma questionnaires in 2019, 2020, and 2021, so we had a 3-year gap in our data, which restricts our ability to more carefully assess the impacts of COVID-19 on our findings. Second, our study is observational, so we cannot establish causal relationships between HIV serostatus, sociodemographic and clinical measures, and perceived stigma.

In summary, we found perceived HIV stigma has decreased over the past decade among older people with HIV but increased to levels seen 7 years ago among people without HIV in Uganda. Our findings emphasize the need to more sustainably address the causes of persistent stigma among people without HIV. Future work should also identify temporal trends in other dimensions of stigma, including internalized stigma and enacted stigma, and evaluate how interruptions in HIV treatment programs that are likely under the current administration may affect the future of HIV-related stigma in the region.
