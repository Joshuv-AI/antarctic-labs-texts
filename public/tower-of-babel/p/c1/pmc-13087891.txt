Antibiotic‐Loaded Calcium Sulphate Beads in Wound Management: A Scoping Review of Emerging Applications in Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery
Authors: M. Marcasciano, M. Astolfi, J. Nanni, C. Chang, A. Russo, L. Losco, F. Lo Torto, B. Fanelli, M. Greco, D. Casella
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCalcium sulphate (CS) is a fully synthetic, sterile, bioabsorbable biomaterial extensively applied for the management of infected tissues and postoperative dead spaces resulting from surgical interventions. Residual DS may facilitate hematoma accumulation and bacterial colonisation, thereby heightening the risk of surgical‐site infections. Within orthopaedic surgery, CS has been predominantly evaluated as a bone‐void filler and an off‐label antibiotic delivery vehicle—particularly in arthroplasty revisions, chronic osteomyelitis, and open fractures—yielding high rates of infection prophylaxis, bone regeneration, and low complication profiles. Commercially available as injectable ‘pearls’ or beads, CS permits local, sustained antibiotic elution while undergoing gradual biodegradation, thus obviating the need for secondary removal procedures. Over the last decade, Calcium Sulphate beads (CSBs) have transcended orthopaedics, gaining traction across general, vascular, and endocrine surgery disciplines for the prevention and treatment of complex wound infections. However, their application in plastic and reconstructive surgery remains underreported, despite the specialty's frequent engagement with complex soft‐tissue defects, bone exposure, suture dehiscence, and trauma‐related wounds vulnerable to infection. To our knowledge, this represents the first scoping review synthesising current evidence, clinical indications, and emerging roles of CSBs within plastic and reconstructive surgery.

Introduction

Calcium sulphate (CS) is a bioabsorbable, high‐purity, versatile, completely synthetic and sterile material [1, 2]. It largely owes its reputation in the orthopaedic field as a bone filler, and more recently it is gaining renown as a tool in the management of dead spaces and infected tissues when mixed with lyophilised antibiotics [3]. First reported in 1892 by Dreesmann et al., who described a blend of calcium sulphate and ephenol as a filler in the treatment of eight patients with bone voids [4], it took over half a century later for its safety to be proven [5]. As a matter of fact, in 1961 Leonard Peltier et al. were the first to conclude that implanting calcium sulphate into bone or soft tissue did not cause a foreign body reaction [6]. Moreover, he demonstrated that CS promoted new bone formation when periosteum or bone was present, finally proving the biodegradable nature of the materials. In fact, no substrate remains ‘in vivo’, thus avoiding potential bacterial colonisation or biofilm formation. Therefore, no additional procedures are required for removal since the inserted material completely dissolves over the course of 3–6 weeks in soft tissue, or 6–12 weeks into the bone [7, 8].

Calcium sulphate powder can be mixed with freeze‐dried antibiotics to make beads of varying diameters (3–6 mm), depending on the size of the dead space due to the surgical procedure. Drug combinations most frequently involve the use of gentamicin, tobramycin, and vancomycin according to the underlying infection [8]. Once the mixture is obtained, it is moulded on the beads mat during the operating session and then directly inserted into tissues in the form of pearls.

There are multiple studies in the literature reporting on the safety and advantages of using CS, and it recently gained a role in the modern orthopaedic practice and wound management as an alternative to Polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA), that has been shown to reduce infection rates when filled with antibiotics in the form of spacers or pellets as well, in the presence of infection, non‐union or bone loss [9].

However, PMMA is non‐absorbable and requires re‐intervention to remove it; thus, it acts as a potential foreign body for bacterial colonisation. For this reason, the necessity of finding an absorbable cement paved the way for the use of calcium sulphate, receiving increasing acceptance for its exploitation [10].

Although there is increasing evidence supporting the safety and efficacy of calcium sulphate beads in the prophylaxis and treatment of infected wounds, reports of their application beyond orthopaedic surgery remain limited [11].

To date, their use has been most commonly documented in general, vascular, and endocrine surgery, while data assessing their role in plastic and reconstructive surgery are still scarce [12, 13]. As reconstructive surgeons we are often called upon to deal with complex wounds secondary to bone and soft tissue exposure, suture dehiscence, trauma, and surgical site infections [14, 15]. Standard practice includes debridement of infected or dead tissue, while the coverage is achieved with vascularised muscle or fascio‐cutaneous flaps whenever vital structures are exposed [16, 17].

Although various promising protocols have been proposed to address complex infections, their management and eradication often remain challenging [18]. This is particularly true when the presence of critical anatomical structures or prosthetic devices limits the extent of debridement and hampers effective bioburden reduction. In such scenarios, the use of absorbable calcium sulphate antibiotic beads has shown encouraging outcomes, similar to those reported in other surgical specialties.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first scoping review to systematically examine the available evidence on the use of calcium sulphate beads for wound and infection management in complex plastic and reconstructive surgical settings.

Material and Methods

Eligibility Criteria

Following an extensive review of the literature, and given the highly interdisciplinary nature of the topic, we expanded our selection criteria beyond articles directly related to antibiotic‐loaded calcium sulphate beads. In particular, we identified a set of reference journals that, while not exclusively focused on the specific subject, are highly relevant due to their prominence in related fields. Consequently, the five journals with the highest publication index in the area of Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery, as reported by Google Scholar Metrics, were included in our analysis: Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery, Journal of Cosmetic Dermatology, Aesthetic Surgery Journal, Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery—Global Open, and JPRAS Open. To broaden the search, the following journals were considered relevant due to similarity of scenarios shared by plastic surgery and other surgical specialties: ‘International Wound Journal’; ‘Wounds’; ‘Wound repair and regeneration’; ‘The Journal of Foot & Ankle Surgery’; ‘The Diabetic Foot Journal’, ‘Annals of Translational Medicine’. To facilitate the eligibility of data, the PRISMA guidelines were adhered to throughout this study [19]. Eventually, only articles written in English and in vivo human studies were included.

Research Strategy

From 2002 until 2025, collections of case studies and scientific reviews were searched using the following Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) keywords: ‘Calcium sulphate beads’, ‘Stimulan’, ‘Antibiotic beads’, ‘Calcium sulphate beads and Plastic Surgery’, ‘Calcium sulphate beads and Reconstructive Surgery’. The terms were searched in various combinations to collect as many items as possible that might be eligible for the study. The websites where the research was carried out were PubMed, Cochrane Library, Medline Plus, OpenAIRE and Medscape. Each abstract from the search results was reviewed by two authors (A.M. and M.M.) for relevant papers independently. All references cited in relevant articles were also checked for other studies not found during the initial search. In duplicate cases, only one record was considered.

Data Selection

The following variables were collected from the selected scientific literature, both open‐access and subscription‐based articles: author, year of publication, study setting, journal, nationality, level of impact, bead characteristics, outcomes measured (infection control, healing, healing time, revision surgery, wound leakage, other complications), anatomical sites, minimum follow‐up, age, sex, underlying pathology, microorganisms detected, antibiotic regimen used.

Summary of Studies

Eleven articles met the inclusion criteria (Table 2). The 11 studies were published between 2017 and 2025. These studies included a total of 1202 patients. The mean follow‐up varied from 1 week up to 13 months. Regarding the body districts considered, the following were involved: the hypopharynx, breasts, genital area, lower limbs and feet. The male:female ratio has been reported in five papers. The most used antibiotic mixes were vancomycin—tobramycin and vancomycin—gentamicin. Only three studies reported the size of the involved CS beads: Khansa et al. [24], 4.8 mm of diameter; Morley et al. [26], 5 cm3 of volume and Ahmed et al. [28, 29, 31], 3–6 mm of diameter. Four of eleven studies were considered Level III of evidence, or therapeutic level. A wide spectrum of bacteria was found in the several samplings carried out in the studies, the most common were Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus, Acinetobacter, Pseudomonas and Escherichia coli . Using the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Quality Assessment Tool for case series studies, seven studies (Kenna et al. [22], Sherif et al. [23], Khansa et al. [24], Morley et al. [26], Chadwick et al. [27] and Ahmed et al. [29, 31]) were rated as a ‘Good’ quality and four studies (Trujillo et al. [21], Nealon et al. [25], Ahmed et al. [28] and Chew et al. [30]) as a ‘Fair’ quality rating (Table 1). Trujillo et al. [21] used CS beads in the management of a skin fistula after laryngopharyngectomy due to squamous cell carcinoma, Kenna et al. [22] and Sherif et al. [23] both involved CS beads in breast reconstruction for the first time, the former with an expander, the latter with a prosthesis. Recently, Ahmed et al. [28] proposed calcium sulphate beads as prophylactic practice in implant based prepectoral breast reconstruction. Khansa et al. [24] involved calcium sulphate beads in the treatment of stage IV pressure ulcers and Nealon et al. [25] used them in two cases of recurrent scrotal pocket infection after inflatable penile prosthesis implant. Lastly, Morley et al. [26] and Chadwick et al. [27] applied CS beads in the context of complex foot wound management in patients with diabetes and/or peripheral arterial diseases and Chew et al. [30] in the treatment of recalcitrant infected right lower limb fasciotomy wounds in conjunction with a type of dermal matrix. Discussion

Calcium sulphate beads (CSBs), owing to their ability to release antibiotics at concentrations exceeding the minimum inhibitory level, can significantly reduce infection rates in procedures such as tissue expander‐based breast reconstruction and chronic wound management [32, 33]. Their natural resorbability eliminates the need for surgical removal, thereby lowering the risk of bacterial colonisation and related complications. This benefit, together with the possibility of tailoring the antibiotic formulation to microbiological findings, positions CSBs as a valuable adjunct when conventional systemic therapies are ineffective or poorly tolerated [34, 35].

Beyond plastic and reconstructive surgery, local antibiotic delivery systems have been widely investigated in diabetic foot care, limb salvage, and complex musculoskeletal infections [36] In these settings, a variety of carriers—including calcium sulphate, PMMA, and other biomaterials—have been used to achieve high local antibiotic concentrations while limiting systemic exposure, providing a robust biological and antimicrobial rationale for the use of antibiotic‐loaded CSBs in complex soft tissue reconstruction [1, 2, 7, 36].

Although the current body of evidence for CSBs in plastic and reconstructive surgery is still emerging and largely based on retrospective series and case reports, their rationale is underpinned by more extensive experience in diabetic foot and musculoskeletal infection literature [2, 4, 37]. This broader interdisciplinary background helps contextualise our findings and supports the cautious extrapolation of local antibiotic strategies to high‐risk reconstructive scenarios [2, 37].

McConoughey et al. (2014) were the first to demonstrate that CSBs are both safe and comparable to PMMA in the local delivery of antibiotics to infected surgical sites. Since then, increased clinical use has led to a broader understanding of their therapeutic potential, as well as their associated risks. Main side effects include transient hypercalcemia, wound drainage, seroma and heterotopic ossification (HO) [9].

Despite reported complications, this review highlights encouraging clinical outcomes associated with calcium sulphate beads in the management of infected and complex wounds, underscoring their expanding role in plastic and reconstructive surgery.

Soft Tissue Infection—Complex Surgical Wounds

Trujillo et al. [21] reported on the addition of antibiotic‐loaded CS beads in a prophylaxis regimen to improve the outcomes of soft tissue surgical site infections in the management of complex wounds, with a 10.56% re‐operation rate. In their study, each patient received CSBs during orthopaedic, breast, thoracic, and vascular graft surgeries depending on their respective categories. Although the average age, gender, comorbidities, and characteristics of the beads used were not mentioned, of a total of 142 patients treated at two different hospital facilities with the same prophylaxis protocol, only 15 needed re‐intervention at 30 days after CSBs implantation, demonstrating that local antibiotic delivery can markedly reduce the persistence of surgical site infections. The objective of promoting further protocols involving CSBs in the management of complex surgical wounds was shared by Morley et al. [26] and Chadwick et al. [27]. Their purpose was to investigate whether surgical debridement and implantation of antibiotic‐loaded calcium sulphate was effective in the resolution of foot infection and wound healing. Morley et al. [26] in a double‐centre cohort study retrospectively reviewed the use of calcium sulphate beads impregnated with gentamicin and vancomycin on the foot ulcers of 137 individuals with osteomyelitis (n = 127) or significant soft tissue infection (n = 10) following surgical debridement. In 137 cases, 88.3% of infections were resolved, with 22 patients not requiring postoperative systemic antibiotics. About 82.5% of wounds healed, with a mean healing time of 11.3 weeks. Recovery time was significantly prolonged in patients with diabetes and peripheral artery disease (p < 0.05) and long‐term postoperative systemic antibiotics were required. The only adverse effect observed was skin maceration of varying severity during CSB resorption, which may have prolonged healing times in some cases. However, Khansa et al. [24] found that overall complication rates between patients packed with CS and those that were not appeared similar (43.8% vs. 51.9%, p = 0.54) in 120 stage IV pressure ulcers involving various districts of the lower limbs.

Although overall complication rates were comparable, the benefits of using CSBs in sacral, trochanteric, ischial, and femoral head ulcers were clinically meaningful. They drastically reduced the wound re‐occurrence rate at 1 year after excision, CS beads application, and flap coverage. In the CSBs group, the rates were 12.5%, compared to 39.4% in the non‐CSBs group. The rates of dehiscence were 18.8% versus 10.6%, seroma 12.5% versus 0%, and cellulitis 0% versus 1.9%.

The use of scanning electron microscopy (SEM) in this study provided critical evidence that even extensive surgical debridement may be insufficient to eradicate biofilm. Biofilms—structured communities of bacteria embedded in a self‐produced extracellular matrix—are highly resistant to conventional antimicrobial therapies, as the matrix impedes antibiotic penetration and shields pathogens from host immune responses [38]. Conventional bacterial culture techniques were found to be inadequate for diagnosing bacterial biofilm infections, as scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed biofilm presence in six patients, while only one of them had a positive wound culture. Based on these findings, the authors concluded that the use of antibiotic beads in combination with surgical debridement may be effective in promoting the eradication of biofilms and the healing of ulcerated wounds. Chadwick et al. [27] similarly supported the use of antibiotic‐loaded calcium sulphate beads for treating infected foot ulcers in people with diabetes, and proposed a clear clinical pathway to guide safe and effective use in routine practice. Their guideline comprises four steps: assess suitability for CSBs with attention to vascular status and wound characteristics; apply beads, preferably postdebridement; monitor wound healing and infection; and implement measures to reduce exudate, the most frequently reported side effect. Notably, an increasing incidence of seroma formation is registered when higher volumes of CS beads are used. This is especially evident in subcutaneous tissues and among patients with comorbidities such as diabetes or long‐term corticosteroid use, with reported rates ranging from 4% to 51% [11, 12].

In response to this, Patel et al. [39] proposed reducing the quantity of beads applied to minimise fluid accumulation; however, Chadwick et al. argued that such reduction may compromise treatment efficacy, as fewer beads are more likely to detach from the wound bed. Although the risk of maceration at the wound perimeter is a concern, it should not preclude the use of CSBs, as several products, such as non‐adhesive silicone transparent dressings, are available to effectively manage exudate.

Interdisciplinary Infection Management

The infection management principles endorsed by the International Working Group on the Diabetic Foot (IWGDF/IDSA) offer a useful interdisciplinary framework for interpreting the role of CSBs [40, 41]. These guidelines emphasise accurate classification of infection severity, prompt and adequate surgical debridement when indicated, and systemic antibiotic therapy for 1–2 weeks for most soft‐tissue infections, with extension up to 3–4 weeks only in more extensive or slowly resolving cases. Importantly, the IWGDF advises against the use of either systemic or local antibiotics in clinically uninfected ulcers, underscoring that both systemic and locally delivered agents should be reserved for confirmed infection rather than prophylaxis in clean wounds [37]. Within this framework, antibiotic‐loaded CSBs may be viewed as a potential adjunct to guideline‐driven debridement and systemic therapy in carefully selected high‐risk, infected wounds, rather than as a replacement for established systemic regimens [37, 41].

Systemic Antibiotic Burden and the SOLARIO Trial

The interaction between local antibiotic delivery and systemic antibiotic duration is central to contemporary antimicrobial stewardship. The Short or Long Antibiotic Regimens in Orthopaedics (SOLARIO) trial, a multicentre randomised non‐inferiority study of adults with operatively treated orthopaedic infections receiving local antibiotics, demonstrated that a short systemic antibiotic course (≤ 7 days) was non‐inferior to prolonged therapy (≥ 4 weeks) in terms of treatment failure while significantly reducing antibiotic‐related adverse events [42]. Although SOLARIO did not specifically address plastic surgery or soft‐tissue reconstruction, its findings support the concept that high‐concentration local therapy can enable rational reduction of systemic antibiotic exposure in selected patients [42]. When extrapolated cautiously, these data reinforce the need to consider CSBs not only as a means of achieving infection control, but also as part of broader strategies aimed at minimising systemic antibiotic burden and resistance selection.

Urological Reconstructive Surgery

The use of CSBs has been described in the urological‐reconstructive field for the first time by Nealon et al. [25], presenting two cases in which the use of antibiotic beads in the scrotum allowed prosthesis salvage. In one case, an inflatable penile prosthesis (IPP) was explanted and replaced with a semirigid penile prosthesis (SRPP) and calcium sulphate beads were inserted to prevent recurrence of infection. In the second case, a patient with complications who had previously undergone SRPP due to infection had recurrent scrotal infections and recovered with beads placement in the scrotal pocket. Although this brief report lacks statistical data and provides limited detail on bead characteristics and patient comorbidities, both patients recovered postoperatively without recurrent infection.

Breast Reconstructive Surgery

Postoperative infections in breast surgery remain a significant concern, with clinical presentations ranging from mild cases managed with short‐term antibiotics to severe infections requiring implant/expander removal. Despite growing experience, there is no universal consensus on the optimal management strategy, particularly in complex or high‐risk cases [43, 44].

In this regard, Sherif et al. [23], Kenna et al. [22] and Ahmed et al. [28, 29, 31] evaluated the effectiveness of antibiotic‐loaded calcium sulphate beads in the setting of implant‐based breast reconstruction, tissue expander‐based reconstruction, and both approaches, respectively.

Sherif et al. [23] were the first to present a single‐centre case series demonstrating the use of CSBs infused with vancomycin and tobramycin. Management of breast implant infections involved prosthesis removal, debridement, partial capsulectomy, implantation of antibiotic‐loaded CSBs, and immediate implant replacement. This strategy aimed to maximise implant salvage while limiting reliance on prolonged intravenous antibiotics and their associated morbidity. One‐step immediate salvage was successful in 9 out of 12 patients, yielding a 75% salvage rate—surpassing outcomes typically reported with conservative implant preservation approaches. The remaining 25% of failures occurred in particularly challenging scenarios, including loss to follow‐up, inadequate soft tissue coverage following radiotherapy, and infection with antibiotic‐resistant Rhodococcus.

These findings underscore the limitations of non‐targeted antibiotic regimens in achieving reliable infection control in complex scenarios. Consequently, customising CSB‐based antimicrobial strategies according to antibiogram results may significantly improve clinical outcomes, bolster salvage efforts in heterologous reconstruction, and reduce the risk of reconstructive failure.

Comparable promising outcomes were reported by Kenna et al. [22] In a retrospective study, they demonstrated that incorporating vancomycin‐ and gentamicin‐loaded calcium sulphate beads into the submuscular pocket during tissue expander (TE) breast reconstruction was associated with a reduced risk of periprosthetic implant infection requiring implant removal.

Out of 127 breast reconstructions with TE covered by an acellular dermal matrix (ADM), 68 adhered to such an ‘off‐label’ prophylactic protocol, while the remaining 59 only washed with chlorhexidine several days before surgery and received intravenous antibiotics in the post‐surgery time. Infection was defined by at least one of the following criteria: purulent drainage, spontaneous dehiscence of the surgical incision with positive bacterial swabs, evidence of abscess, or a surgeon's diagnosis of a deep infection occurring up to 1 year postoperatively. The rate of tissue expander (TE) loss due to infection was 1.5% in the group using CSBs (p = 0.02), compared to 11.9% in the group not using them [45].

The efficacy of CSBs in reducing implant removal rates is often attributed to their ability to target biofilm, a key contributor to resistant infections, capsular contracture, and potentially, implant‐associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma [46] Recent studies, including Howlin et al. [47], have demonstrated that high concentrations of antibiotics released from calcium sulphate significantly reduce the process of biofilm development over several days.

In 2023, Ahmed et al. [28] proposed the use of calcium sulphate beads as a prophylactic measure in high‐risk, implant‐based pre‐pectoral breast reconstruction. In this study, CSBs were placed behind the implant over the pectoralis major muscle and anteriorly between the subcutaneous adipose layer and the implant. Surgical site infections or implant loss occurred in 3.2% of high‐risk patients receiving prophylactic CSBs, compared to 7.6% in the control group, although this difference did not achieve statistical significance.

In 2024, Ahmed et al. published a promising study examining the impact of biodegradable calcium sulphate antibiotic beads in immediate tissue expander (TE) reconstruction following mastectomy [29] The study compared two groups: 33 patients received antibiotic beads (Group 1), and 330 did not (Group 2). TE loss occurred in only 1.5% of Group 1, compared to 9.4% in Group 2, a statistically significant difference, with only one infection observed in the case group. These findings suggest that the routine use of absorbable antibiotic beads in pre‐pectoral TE reconstruction may reduce complications and implant loss, supporting their prophylactic application to lower the risk of postoperative infections. In 2025, the same research group addressed a key concern regarding the use of absorbable antibiotic beads in immediate postmastectomy tissue expander (TE) reconstruction: whether the beads increase drain duration due to calcium sulphate absorption [31] The study analysed 325 patients (548 TEs), with 36 patients (Group 1) receiving antibiotic beads and 289 (Group 2) not. No significant differences were found in demographics, surgical technique, or drain duration, which averaged 17.2 days in Group 1 versus 17.5 days in Group 2 (p = 0.7933). These findings suggest that absorbable antibiotic beads do not prolong drain use, despite concerns regarding local seroma occurrence. However, seroma management in pre‐pectoral breast reconstruction in the setting of CSB use remains a matter of debate and warrants further investigation [48, 49].

Dermal Matrices

The clinical landscape of plastic and reconstructive surgery is increasingly shaped by biomaterials and innovative adjunctive therapies designed to optimise outcomes in complex wound management. Among these, dermal substitutes—bioengineered matrices facilitating dermal regeneration—have established their role in treating extensive soft tissue defects. In particular, they are widely used to enhance neodermis formation, support autologous skin grafting, and reduce scar contracture in severe burn injuries, trauma, and oncologic resections. Their efficacy lies in their biomimetic architecture, promoting angiogenesis, fibroblast infiltration, and extracellular matrix remodelling [50].

Despite their individually promising performance, the combined use of dermal matrices and local antibiotic carriers remains a scarcely explored area that deserves systematic scientific attention.

In this perspective, in 2024 Chew et al. [30] published the first case report describing the successful management of infected fasciotomy wounds in a male patient using a 1 mm thick‐layered dermal matrix in conjunction with calcium absorbable antibiotic beads. One week after applying the dermal matrix and CSBs, wound revascularisation and granulation enabled split‐thickness skin grafting. The patient recovered without complications, with 97% graft uptake and well‐healed donor site. This case suggests that combining dermal matrices with antibiotic beads may accelerate wound readiness for definitive coverage and improve outcomes in complex, infected soft tissue injuries. As surgical innovation increasingly prioritises personalisation and infection resilience, this hybrid strategy may hold substantial transformative potential for both elective and emergency reconstructive procedures.

Limitations

This scoping review has several limitations. Most included studies were retrospective case series with heterogeneous indications, variable follow‐up, and inconsistent reporting of antibiotic regimens, limiting conclusions on comparative effectiveness and optimal dosing. Additionally, data largely derive from small single‐centre cohorts, reducing generalisability. Nevertheless, the consistent reports of infection control and wound healing across different anatomical sites support further prospective evaluation of CSBs within standardised reconstructive protocols.

Conclusion

Scientific evidence on the use of calcium sulphate beads (CSBs) is highly promising, offering new perspectives for infection management in complex surgical scenarios. To the best of our knowledge, this review is the first comprehensive analysis to document the emerging use of antibiotic‐loaded calcium sulphate beads across all reported cases in the field of plastic and reconstructive surgery. Current data highlight the need for comparative prospective studies and randomised controlled trials to validate these findings, clarify indications, optimise dosing strategies, and further assess their impact on postoperative infection–related morbidity and the potential reduction of systemic antibiotic exposure in complex wound management.

Data S1: 1 video (0:48 min), Table 2 horizontal layout.

A 50‐year‐old woman developed a left hip soft‐tissue infection following bladder stimulator implantation. Despite targeted antibiotic therapy, surgical debridement was required. Antibiotic‐loaded calcium sulphate beads, prepared with daptomycin based on antibiogram results, were applied intraoperatively, enabling local targeted antibiotic delivery. The video shows the preparation of the beads and their application in this setting.
