Tele Tai Chi for people aging with mobility disabilities: Novel methodology and structured adaptation approach
Authors: Elena T. Remillard, Tracy L. Mitzner, Kara T. Mumma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Many people aging with mobility disabilities experience barriers engaging in exercise programs and social events in-person and could benefit from virtual programs that make participation more accessible. The Tele Tai Chi clinical trial is assessing the acceptability and effectiveness of an evidence-based in-person Tai Chi program for older adults, Tai Chi for Arthritis and Fall Prevention (seated version), when adapted to be an online group intervention (via videoconferencing) with moderated social time for individuals aging with mobility disabilities. Specifically, we are examining the intervention efficacy for the target population for increasing physical activity and social connectedness, which are the primary outcome measures. Secondary outcome measures include exercise self-efficacy, falls efficacy, depression, quality of life, and pain. The participant sample (N = 60) includes community-dwelling adults (60–77 years of age) with a self-identified mobility disability (i.e., using a mobility aid or having serious difficulty walking or climbing stairs) for at least 10 years. Follow-up assessments occurred at the end of the 8-week intervention and 1 month thereafter. This methods-focused paper highlights our novel, user-centered, technology-mediated approach to adapting an in-person intervention for individuals aging with mobility disabilities, which can be used as a roadmap for researchers and practitioners launching similar trials or programs.ClinicalTrials.gov noNCT04696887.

Introduction

Telewellness programs are wellness programs delivered virtually using technology, such as videoconferencing software (e.g. Zoom). These programs offer the convenience of participating from anywhere using a personal device and can facilitate access to specialized content. Telewellness programs could be especially beneficial for older adults with mobility disabilities who experience significant barriers to participating in traditional exercise programs in-person, such as limited transportation, lack of appropriate modifications, inaccessible facilities, and feeling stigmatized [1]. Many older adults with mobility disabilities have functional limitations and complex support needs that require specialized instruction and adaptable programs [2]. For people aging with mobility disability, exercise has been associated with less pain, fatigue, and depression, as well as greater self-efficacy in managing health and social participation [3]. Unfortunately, less than one third of this population engages in the recommended amounts of physical exercise to receive health benefits, including management of chronic conditions [4].

People aging with mobility disabilities are also at risk of social isolation and loneliness [5] and could benefit from group exercise programs that have been shown to be an effective strategy to reduce isolation and enhance social connection for older adults [6]. Moreover, the social component of group physical activity programs can serve as a motivator for class participation and promote greater adherence [7]. Group telewellness classes hold great potential to expand access to a range of physical and social benefits for people aging with mobility disabilities. However, there are important considerations to ensure participants are safe, supported, and engaged in a virtual exercise environment [8]. Technology training and support is essential to accommodate older adults who may have lower levels of technology adoption or familiarity [9,10]. Additionally, selecting an appropriate exercise program that is both efficacious and adaptable can help ensure participant safety given the absence of an in-person instructor.

The current research is evaluating Tele Tai Chi - a telewellness intervention designed to be both inclusive of older adults with mobility disabilities and socially engaging. Tele Tai Chi was developed by the Rehabilitation Engineering Research Center on Technologies to Support Aging among People with Long-Term Disabilities (TechSAge RERC). We adapted an in-person, evidence-based tai chi exercise program to be a virtual small group class (via Zoom videoconferencing) that features a seated version of the exercise program and moderated social time. The social component involves guided discussion with classmates before and after each lesson. The intervention includes Tai Chi for Arthritis and Fall Prevention (TCAFP) - a low-impact mind-body group exercise program that incorporates slow, continuous movements and intentional breathing. Research on TCAFP has demonstrated a variety of health benefits (e.g., improved balance, pain reduction, increased flexibility, fall prevention; [[11], [12], [13]]. The seated version of this program was designed to accommodate older adults with a range of disabilities and health conditions. The principal aim of this clinical trial is to evaluate the intervention efficacy for the target population for increasing physical activity and social connectedness (primary outcomes), as well as improvements in secondary outcomes including exercise self-efficacy, falls efficacy, depression, quality of life, and pain. We are also exploring participants’ perceptions about the intervention, including acceptability (i.e., usefulness, usability, enjoyment, satisfaction) and willingness to adopt (i.e., continue using in the future).

Tele-exercise and mind-body interventions for older adults with disabilities are emerging and under-documented. We used a structured adaptation process to guide and detail the decisions we made to adapt the original intervention to be accessible by our population and to be delivered virtually in a socially engaging manner. Key adaptations included selecting a technology platform, managing participant onboarding and technology support, modifying exercises for accessibility, and ensuring safety. This paper will provide an overview of the study materials and methods, including intervention development and components, as well as study procedures, sample characteristics, and structured adaptation approach.

Materials and methods

Study design overview

The Tele Tai Chi study is a single-arm clinical trial that was conducted at the Georgia Institute of Technology, where Institutional Review Board approval was received. This study used videoconferencing (i.e., Zoom) to deliver an evidence-based tai chi exercise program to adults aging with long-term mobility disabilities. For the 8-week Tele Tai Chi intervention, participants joined the online classes from home using a computer or tablet; during each session, participants exercised along with pre-recorded video lessons and engaged in guided social discussion before and after the exercise. The classes were facilitated by a trained TCAFP instructor and were attended by a technical moderator to support participants’ use of Zoom software. Participant enrollment began in February 2021 and data collection ended in August 2023.

Intervention development

We employed a human factors approach to develop the Tele Tai Chi intervention [8]. There were several foundational research efforts that informed the intervention design including: a review of relevant literature on user needs [2]; subject matter expert interviews with exercise instructors (n = 12) who had experience working with older adults with mobility disabilities [14]; a needs assessment among older adults aging with mobility disabilities (n = 14) about their attitudes towards telehealth technologies [15]; a mixed method feasibility study among the target population (n = 19) to assess attitudes and implementation requirements for a tele tai chi intervention [16]; and iterative usability testing with older adults (n = 9) to identify the optimal videoconferencing platform and settings. For detailed information on intervention development, including research methods and protocol, and research-driven guidelines for delivering telewellness classes to older adults, see Refs. [8,17], respectively.

We utilized a structured adaptation process [18] to translate the evidence-based exercise program from in-person to videoconference delivery. To ensure relevant expertise for the intervention adaptations, we established an adaptation committee that included members of the target population, a representative from the Tai Chi for Health Institute, a researcher with expertise in exercise among people aging with mobility disabilities (PhD, MPT, ATP), and a researcher with expertise in technology design for older adults (PhD). All adaptations were decided by the research team with consultation of the adaptation committee. All iterative study design changes were documented.

Tele Tai Chi intervention components

Tai Chi for Arthritis and Fall Prevention (TCAFP) is a low-impact mind-body exercise developed by the Tai Chi for Health Institute that involves gentle movements and controlled breathing. TCAFP is a Title III-D Highest Tier Evidence-Based Health Promotion/Disease Prevention Program recommended by the Administration on Community Living and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the program has been adopted by a wide range of Area Agencies of Aging across the country. The program was designed for people with and without arthritis and is especially appropriate for adults at high risk of falling. We utilized the seated version of this program, which was designed to accommodate people with a range of abilities, including those who are unable to stand.

We added a social component to the intervention, designed to provide an opportunity for participants to interact, make connections, and build a sense of community – factors that are associated with adherence for in-person group exercise programs [7,19]. The social component includes moderated social time before and after the exercise. For the pre-exercise discussion, the class facilitator asks a discussion question allowing each participant an opportunity to respond, if desired. Questions were designed to be productive, positive, and enable participants to get to know one another. As the program progresses, questions shift from general questions (e.g., hobbies, where you are from) to be more focused on physical activity (e.g., goals, challenges) and tai chi (e.g., favorite movements, accomplishments). Post-exercise discussion time is intentionally less structured and formal to allow participants to drive the discussion based on their own practice needs and have ample time to ask questions about the movements. Discussion centers on how participants are feeling and tai chi principles (e.g., slow, continuous movement, gentle resistance).

For the 8-week intervention, participants engage in small group, online classes (1 h each) that convene twice weekly on Zoom. Each class begins with a technology check period (up to 10 min) to assist participants with technology troubleshooting, as needed. Prior to the lesson, participants engage in moderated social time (10 min) answering a discussion question. Classes include pre-recorded tai chi lessons (approximately 30 min) that are broadcast directly from the Tai Chi for Health Institute online portal. Each lesson consists of warm-up exercises, learning a new form (i.e., movement), performing a sequence of previously learned movements to music, and cool down exercises. During the lesson, participants exercise along with the video instruction and their classmates on Zoom in real time. After the lesson, the class facilitator leads another 10 min of moderated discussion that focuses on tai chi. Participants can ask questions about movements, continue practicing with the facilitator, and discuss different principles of tai chi (e.g., slow, continuous movement).

Classes are led by a facilitator who guides the participants through each phase of the session using a semi-structured script. Facilitator responsibilities include: welcoming and introducing participants to the session; moderating the social time discussion, transitioning participants between social time and exercise; demonstrating movements; and attending to any questions from the participants during the session. Facilitators are either research team members (i.e., research scientist, student research assistant) or a tai chi instructor consulting on the project. All facilitators are certified to teach the TCAFP program; even though the facilitators do not teach the lessons (i.e., pre-recorded and broadcasted), they are required to be certified so they can adequately address participant questions about the program and provide supplemental support (e.g., modifications, demonstrations). A member of the research team also serves as a technical moderator for the class and is responsible for: broadcasting the pre-recorded exercise lessons on Zoom; monitoring participant attendance and sending a reminder email to those not present at the beginning of class; addressing any technical issues that occurred during the session; answering any technical questions initiated by participants on the Zoom chat feature, phone, or email; and documenting notes about the session (e.g., start and end times, technical issues, discussion topics).

Eligibility criteria

Eligible participants were between the ages of 60–80 who self-identified as having a mobility disability for at least 10 years (i.e., use a mobility aid or have serious difficulty walking or climbing stairs). Participants were required to: pass the Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire (PAR-Q; Adams [20] or provide a letter from a healthcare provider stating that he or she is approved to participate in the seated exercise program; have access to a computer or tablet with a webcam; have internet access for the next six months; have basic computer skills (e.g., comfortable checking email, watching videos online); live in the United States; and be conversational in English. Participants were ineligible if they were blind or deaf; were unavailable for the duration of the study; practiced Tai Chi during the past 6 months; or did not pass the Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (TICS; [21], by scoring lower than 26.

Recruitment and protocol

Participants were recruited by word-of-mouth, participant registries (i.e., TechSAge), social media, community flyers, and through outreach to local and national organizations for people with mobility disabilities. The most effective recruitment strategy for our target population was outreach to condition-specific organizations (e.g., multiple sclerosis organizations, post-polio support groups). Interested individuals completed an initial telephone screening to determine eligibility. Eligible participants then completed a one-on-one orientation with a researcher on Zoom. First, participants were sent step-by-step instructions on how to join a meeting. The researcher initiated the orientation by calling the participant on the phone and talking them through the steps of joining the meeting. During the orientation, participants were taught to use some of Zoom's features, including muting and unmuting the video, stopping and starting the video, and using the chat feature. The researcher also administered an environmental checklist to assess the participant's immediate environment, provide recommendations (e.g., flat surface for device, space to extend arms, phone within reach), address any potential safety concerns (e.g., removing clutter, sturdy chair), and optimize the participant's audio and video quality, if necessary (e.g., lighting, reducing background noise). In the event a participant experienced technical issues during the orientation (e.g., poor Wi-Fi connection, inability to turn on microphone or camera, home environment concerns), the researcher helped them troubleshoot to find a solution. If the problem could not be resolved after significant troubleshooting, the individual was deemed ineligible.

Eligible participants provided informed consent and were recruited into cohorts, that varied in size from 6 to 9 participants, for the 8-week intervention. Once a complete cohort was recruited, the research team set a class schedule that considered the availability of the participants and staff. Classes were always offered at the same time two days a week with one rest day in between (e.g., Mondays and Wednesdays at 2pm). There were 8 total cohorts who completed the intervention over a 2.5-year period. Each cohort was a convenience sample, although there was some effort to include a mix of gender and racial representation in each cohort (e.g., goal of at least two males in each class).

For the 8-week Tele Tai Chi intervention, participants joined the small group, online class (1 h, twice weekly) from home using a computer or tablet. During each class, participants exercised along with broadcasted pre-recorded video lessons and engaged in guided social discussion before and after the exercise. At the end of each class, participants were sent a link to complete a brief post-class survey assessing their perceptions about the class (e.g., enjoyment, technical issues). Assessments were completed at three timepoints: baseline, post-intervention, and 1-month follow-up. Participants received up to $60 total in compensation: $20 for completing the pre-intervention assessment, $30 for completing the post-intervention assessment and interview, and $10 for completing the follow-up questionnaire. Participants were required to attend a minimum of two Tele Tai Chi classes to be eligible to complete the post-intervention and follow-up assessments.

Participants were asked if they use 13 common mobility aids. The most commonly used mobility aids included grab bars (used by 57 % of participants), cane or walking stick (50 %), grabber or reacher (43 %), walker or rollator (42 %), manual wheelchair (35 %), and power wheelchair (33 %). During the screening, 62 % of the sample said they have serious difficulty walking or climbing stairs, 10 % said they did not have difficulty, and 28 % said they were unable to walk. Participants were asked if they had certain health conditions from a checklist of 19 common conditions. The most reported health condition was arthritis, affecting 60 % of the sample. Other common conditions included multiple sclerosis (42 % of participants), hypertension (30 %), post-polio syndrome (28 %), cataracts (25 %), and diabetes (18 %). In addition, 23 % of the sample reported ‘other’ health conditions (e.g., sleep apnea, osteoporosis, muscular dystrophy, hereditary spastic paraplegia).

Structured adaptation approach

The adaptation of TCAFP to a virtual format that was socially engaging and inclusive of older adults with mobility disabilities required several adaptations. A key challenge was ensuring participant safety in the home environment without an in-person instructor. In addition to selecting a seated exercise program, we added a variety of safety procedures including requiring emergency contact information, a virtual environmental check to ensure participants were in a safe space to practice tai chi (e.g., sufficient space, adequate lighting to be seen, phone nearby, sturdy chair), and requiring a letter from a healthcare provider if participants did not pass the Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire (PAR-Q; Adams [20]). We quickly discovered that obtaining a letter from a physician was a barrier (e.g., difficult to reach, busy) and transitioned to accept letters from a wider range of healthcare providers, including physician assistants, nurses, and physical therapists.

Some adaptations were made to the exercise intervention itself. In response to participant feedback, we provided handouts with photos and descriptions of tai chi movements from the Tai Chi for Health Institute as a supplemental resource. It was essential to emphasize modification and visualization techniques as participants represented a wide range of mobility limitations. Several participants were unable to do movements without modification, and some were unable to do a physical movement (e.g., paralyzed limb) and relied on visualizing and imagining movements. Although facilitators were trained in TCAFP, we sought additional guidance from a master trainer consulting on the project and developed a training document with tips for teaching modification and visualization (e.g., working within comfort zone; cues and imagery to support imagining movements). Another study adaptation was training existing tai chi instructors to be facilitators (vs. members of the research team). We found this set up reduced training time significantly as it was more efficient to train tai chi instructors on a research protocol vs. training a researcher to become a certified tai chi instructor. Moreover, this adaptation demonstrated scalability and a real-world application.

Regarding class format, there were only 6 online lessons for the seated TCAFP program. As such, each lesson was repeated twice each week to create the 8-week program. In the final 2 weeks, participants had the option to choose which lessons to revisit. During the final class, participants were invited to take part in a showcase where everyone performed the entire sequence of movements together without the pre-recorded lesson playing. In the showcase, participants followed along with the facilitator, who broadcasted the instrumental tai chi music from their computer. Participation in the showcase was optional but was overall well-received. Some commented that this gave them sense of accomplishment and closure to the program.

Adaptations were also made for the social component. To ensure conversation was respectful and the group was a safe space for discussion, we developed a class etiquette guide with rules such as trying not to interrupt one another and avoiding potentially controversial or inappropriate topics (e.g., politics, religion, medical advice). We limited the class size to 9 people as facilitators shared that a larger group would make it difficult for individual participants to have sufficient time to talk. Constraining class size to 9 students also ensured that facilitators could monitor all participants while exercising. In the first cohort, we discovered that many participants made friends in the group and wanted to stay connected with their peers outside of class. Participants also wanted to share resources with each other (e.g., pool therapy, tai chi videos). Based on participant feedback, we encouraged participants to utilize the chat feature to share contact information or give us their permission to share their email with the class participants.

Technology adaptations were made throughout the intervention design and execution. We evaluated a variety of video conferencing platforms (e.g. OneClick, BlueJeans) and identified Zoom as the best option for broadcasting video. Through trial and error, we established optimal settings for broadcasting online videos via Zoom to reduce audio lag and video choppiness (e.g., reducing video resolution quality, screen sharing, and sound on Zoom). Additionally, we determined Zoom meeting security settings to ensure privacy for the group (e.g., passcode, only facilitator able to share screen). To ensure participants were successful in using Zoom, we developed comprehensive technology support materials (e.g., How to Join a Meeting on Zoom, Zoom Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)); and training opportunities (e.g., orientation). These materials needed to be regularly updated to reflect the latest software updates and features (e.g., location and labels of buttons). Similarly, we modified our research team technology protocols to assist with troubleshooting on different types of devices and operating systems (e.g., PC, Mac). We also created the tech moderator role designating a point person for broadcasting the video, documenting challenges, and assisting participants in real-time.

Discussion and lessons learned

We adapted the seated version of an evidence-based tai chi program to be delivered virtually via videoconferencing with a social component. While there are numerous benefits demonstrated for Tai Chi for Arthritis and Fall Prevention (TCAFP), the efficacy of tele-delivered seated version has not been established. The primary goal of this clinical trial is to evaluate the effectiveness of the intervention in increasing physical activity and social connectedness for older adults with long-term mobility disabilities. We are also exploring secondary outcomes (e.g., exercise self-efficacy, falls efficacy, depression, quality of life, and pain) and gathering data on acceptability of the intervention.

The baseline sample characteristics for the clinical trial suggest we are targeting a population who could benefit from access to this evidence-based exercise program. Most participants rely on mobility aids and approximately a third of the sample is unable to walk and relies on wheelchairs. TCAFP was designed to be adaptable for people with a wide range of abilities and limitations. Moreover, over half the sample has arthritis, which is the health condition this tai chi program was designed to support and has demonstrated benefits in symptom improvement (e.g., improved joint pain). The trial results will demonstrate to what extent other established benefits of TCAFP, such as reduced pain, fatigue, fear of falling, depression, as well as improvements in self-efficacy are seen for this population from a virtual delivery model.

In addition to fostering physical activity participation, the intervention was also designed to provide opportunities for social connection and peer support for a target population at risk of social isolation and loneliness. This study overlapped with the COVID-19 pandemic, where potential negative impacts of social isolation was increasingly recognized [24]. During this time, people around the world were trying to figure out how to deliver and participate in wellness programming virtually. Several participants made new friends during the intervention and requested a way to stay connected beyond the class. For social programs like this, it is important to consider how study design could support peer connection while respecting the preferences and privacy of individuals.

Our methodological approach was novel in that we adapted the TCAFP program in two ways: to a new population and a new delivery modality. The complexity and relevance of our methodology merit independent dissemination from our results (manuscript in preparation). We employed a user centered approach to adapt an evidence-based intervention to a specific target population (adults aging with mobility disabilities) and to a technology-mediated delivery. This approach included literature reviews, subject matter expert interviews, as well as feasibility and usability studies. Building on this background research, our research team worked with an adaptation committee of stakeholders to determine the adaptations required with the goal of maintaining the fidelity of the original program. Many of the adaptations were innovative and not commonly reported in the literature, including developing safety protocols for exercising remotely, providing exercise modifications for a wide range of abilities (including those with paralysis), and including a social component to parallel the natural socialization that often occurs before and after an in-person exercise class. Given that older adults can be less technologically experienced than younger adults we provided comprehensive ongoing technical support and training to ensure they would be able to engage in the virtual class successfully.

Telewellness has become increasingly popular and in-demand, as it has the potential to reduce costs and logistical barriers as well as expand access to content. This creates an opportunity to expand the evidence-base for Telewellness programs that are accessible to older adults, especially those with mobility disabilities, who experience significant barriers to participating in in-person wellness programs. Other wellness interventions may be able to be scaled to a wider population by using technology as well. The virtual delivery of this program also has the potential to expand opportunity for exercise and social connectedness for people aging with mobility disabilities.

Limitations

We employed a single-arm clinical trial design to evaluate this novel delivery model in-depth, including certain aspects of feasibility (e.g., technology acceptance, class satisfaction). While the absence of a control group limits our ability to demonstrate the effectiveness of the intervention without bias, we chose a single-arm clinical trial for several reasons. For one, the TCAFP program has previously demonstrated efficacy in other populations and formats (e.g., ambulatory older adults, in-person). In addition, older adults with mobility disabilities have known barriers to attending in-person programs. Randomizing them to a no-intervention or waitlist control raises ethical concerns given that the original TCAFP program is known to be beneficial. An important area for future research is exploring the comparative effectiveness of in-person vs tele-delivery of the tai chi program. The Tele Tai Chi intervention was designed and conducted by a research team in a laboratory setting yet has the potential to be scaled to community settings. Future research is needed to assess the feasibility of implementing telewellness programs in real-world, community-based settings, such as senior centers where there are a variety of other important factors such as staffing, facilities, equipment, and member preferences that must be considered. An additional limitation to this study is inequitable technology access. All participants were required to have access to the internet and a device to participate in the study. We recognize technology access is not equitable and remains a barrier for many people. Lastly, the sample is predominately White Caucasian and highly educated. Individuals with disabilities from underrepresented racial/ethnic groups are likely to experience health disparities such as higher rates of health conditions and less access to health and wellness services [25].
