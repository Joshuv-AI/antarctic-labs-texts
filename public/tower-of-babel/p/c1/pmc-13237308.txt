Intraocular pressure induced blood retinal barrier compromise in mouse models and human glaucoma
Authors: Chi Zhang, Haeyn Lim, Jennifer D Ballheim, Marina Simón, Rui Fu, Nicholas G Tolman, Logan J Horbal, Felicia A Juarez, Qing Wang, Aakriti Bhandari, Christa Montgomery, Ling Zhu, Jonathan G Crowston, Nicolas Robine, Michael P Fautsch, Michael H Elliott, Simon W M John
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe integrity of the blood-retinal barrier remains largely unexplored in glaucoma. Here we show that elevated intraocular pressure partially compromises the blood-retinal barrier in three distinct mouse models of glaucoma. This compromise induces subtle leakage without bleeding or detectable disruption of endothelial cell junctions and precedes neurodegeneration. Leakage occurs from peripheral veins in the retinal ganglion cell layer with a concomitant loss of the transcytosis inhibitor MFSD2A. RNA-sequencing, protein analyses and electron microscopy support enhanced transcytosis as the mechanism for leakage, although a subtle and partial junctional component cannot be ruled out. Importantly, stabilizing β-catenin in retinal endothelial cells prevents both vascular leakage and neurodegeneration. We further detect ganglion cell layer-specific leakage in 14/17 human primary open-angle glaucoma eyes but not in age-matched controls. The high incidence of leakage in mouse models and the human glaucoma eyes suggests that blood-retinal barrier compromise may be a common mechanism of glaucoma.Subject terms: Neurodegeneration, Retina, Neuro-vascular interactions

Introduction

Glaucoma is a substantial public health burden accounting for 8.4% of all blindness worldwide1. It currently affects ≈80 million people2,3, with an estimated 10% being bilaterally blind4. It is projected to affect > 111 million people by 20403. In most types of glaucoma, a harmfully high intraocular pressure (IOP) results in retinal ganglion cell (RGC) death and ultimately vision loss5. Multiple insults result in damage to distinct parts of RGCs in glaucoma6,7. In addition to direct damage to optic nerve head (ONH) axons7–10, there is evidence for various damaging processes within the retina, including perturbed metabolism, dendrite/synapse changes, and altered blood flow6–8,11–22. Understanding and targeting distinct pathogenic events in the retina, ONH, and their vasculature is crucial to developing improved treatments.

The blood-retinal barrier (BRB), like the blood-brain barrier (BBB), controls and restricts the transit of molecules from blood into the surrounding neural tissue23–25. This maintains retinal function and prevents neural damage by immune and other harmful molecules. The BRB restricts the entry of molecules into neural tissues by both maintaining paracellular endothelial cell junctions and actively suppressing transcytosis (transcellular vesicular transport)26. Transcytosis includes receptor-mediated and absorptive transport mechanisms as well as non-specific bulk transport of fluids27–29. The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway maintains the BBB/BRB by inducing expression of tight junction proteins (e.g., claudin-5 and zonula occludens-1, ZO-1) and inhibiting transcytosis in endothelial cells (ECs) to a nearly undetectable level30–33. Transcytosis is inhibited by inducing the expression of the major facilitator superfamily domain-containing protein 2 A (MFSD2A, a key inhibitor of transcytosis). MFSD2A helps create a lipid-enriched environment that suppresses caveolar vesicle formation and transcytotic transport32,34. The loss of BRB or BBB integrity, either due to the loosening of tight junctions, increased transcytosis, or both, triggers vascular leakage and is damaging in several central nervous system diseases26,35,36. Despite a large volume of literature on perturbed blood flow in glaucoma, few have considered the role of the BRB in glaucoma pathogenesis37–39. In this study, we tested the effects of high IOP on the BRB. We investigated the BRB in three models with distinct IOP-elevating mechanisms. We used two inherited, human-relevant mouse models of high IOP and glaucoma (DBA/2 J and B6.Lmx1bV265D/+ mice). DBA/2 J mice develop a chronic form of pigmentary glaucoma caused by genes that affect melanosomal biology and are widely used for glaucoma research40–44. Melanosomal biology also contributes to human pigmentary glaucoma, as some patients have mutations in the melanosomal gene PMEL45. Findings using DBA/2 J mice have been translated to clinical trials for primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG, a common form of glaucoma), with promising initial results13,14,46,47. The Lmx1b model is directly relevant to human glaucoma as LMX1B is an important human glaucoma gene, contributing to both childhood glaucoma and later-onset POAG48–51. Here, we used mice with an Lmx1b mutation that causes early-onset glaucoma on the C57BL/6 J mouse strain background (B6.Lmx1bV265D/+)51. As our third experimental system, we chose an experimentally induced model of high IOP (HAMA model) to precisely control the timing of IOP elevation and allow clear determination of the role of IOP. In this model, IOP elevation is induced via injection of a photopolymerizable, hyaluronic acid-based hydrogel into the drainage angle in front of the ocular drainage tissues52. This stable, biocompatible hydrogel53 acts as a fluid-flow resistor that elevates IOP without a need to induce tissue damage, contrary to most induced models that use lasers, saline, or beads to block and damage the drainage tissues52,54,55. In all three mouse models, we show that high IOP induces BRB breakdown and vascular leakage that is detected by low molecular weight tracers, but is subtle and does not involve hemorrhage. Using DBA/2 J mice, we experimentally rescue BRB function by stabilizing β-catenin in ECs and show that suppressing vascular leakage reduces RGC damage. Finally, we detected a similar pattern of leakage in human POAG donor retinas. Together, our findings support a frequent and important contribution of abnormal vascular permeability to neurodegeneration in glaucoma.

Results

Increased IOP induces BRB compromise that precedes neurodegeneration

We first studied the BRB in the DBA/2 J (D2) model. We used Hoechst tracer (MW 561.93) as it is a highly sensitive marker of leakage that binds to DNA in cells that it encounters56. BRB compromise detected by leakage of Hoechst tracer was present in D2 eyes that had developed high IOP. In contrast, it did not occur in any age- and strain-matched, normotensive control eyes (D2-Gpnmb+/+, hereafter called D2-Gpnmb+, which do not develop high IOP57) (Fig. 1a, b and Supplementary Fig. S1). IOP first becomes elevated in D2 eyes at 6 months of age, with almost all eyes having high IOP by 8.5–9.5 months of age42. High IOP is well-established to induce corneal stretching and anterior chamber (AC) deepening in mice41,43,51,58. This deep-chamber phenotype confirmed exposure to high IOP in each eye with detected BRB compromise (“Methods”). The percentage of retinas demonstrating leakage of Hoechst tracer from retinal blood vessels in D2 eyes increased in a manner consistent with a pressure-induced etiology. Leakage occurred in 0% of D2 eyes at 6.5 months (despite some having been exposed to high IOP as evidenced by deep ACs), 30% at 7.5 months, 60% at 8.5 months, and 90% of eyes at 9.5 months of age (Fig. 1e). The leakage was always subtle in that it was only evident by tracer monitoring with no detectable hemorrhage. Although 30% of eyes had tracer leakage at 7.5 months of age, neurodegeneration is not present at this age in D2 eyes42. This lack of neurodegeneration in eyes with leakage was confirmed by sensitive optic nerve analysis of > 20 eyes (Supplementary Fig. S2). Thus, BRB compromise occurs prior to neurodegeneration, raising the possibility that it contributes to glaucomatous neurodegeneration.

In D2 mice, detected leakage occurs from veins and is restricted to the ganglion cell layer

Hoechst tracer that leaks from blood vessels initially stains nuclei adjacent to the leak. This allowed us to precisely locate sites of BRB compromise. Hoechst-stained neurons are first detected next to the peripheral segments of veins, where they branch in a Y shape as they travel around the far periphery of the retina (Fig. 1a). With longer exposure to high IOP and increased leakage, tracer spreads further across the tissue labeling neurons distant from vessels and throughout the ganglion cell layer (GCL) (in some eyes by 9 months and all by 9.5 months of age). No leakage was ever observed arising from arteries or capillaries. Importantly, leaked tracer was restricted to the GCL, consistent with leakage from the major retinal veins of the inner retina and not capillaries in other retinal layers (Fig. 1c, d). No leaked tracer was present in the retinal tissue extending from the inner to outer nuclear layers, even with more extensive aging to 14 months of age (Supplementary Fig. S3).

IOP-induced BRB compromise appears to be a general feature of mouse glaucoma models

IOP elevation in D2 mice is caused by a pigment-dispersing iris disease that induces a pigmentary form of glaucoma41,42,59. To determine if IOP-induced BRB compromise occurs in eyes with a very different IOP-elevating etiology and to initially assess its relevance to more common human glaucoma, we next tested the BRB integrity in mice with a mutation in a human glaucoma gene, Lmx1b, which contributes to a spectrum of human glaucoma, including POAG48–51. Leakage of Hoechst tracer again indicated BRB compromise in ocular hypertensive Lmx1bV265D/+ mice but not in normotensive Lmx1b+/+ control littermates (Supplementary Figs. S4A, B, S5). B6.Lmx1bV265D/+ mice develop high IOP by 2 months of age (Supplementary Fig. S6) and have significant incidence of glaucomatous degeneration starting at 6.5 months of age51. Tracer leakage was evident in 5.5-month-old eyes prior to neurodegeneration. As with D2 mice, BRB compromise appeared venous-specific, with tracer leakage restricted to the GCL (Supplementary Fig. S4E, F).

As a final model with another distinct IOP-elevating etiology, we induced IOP elevation using a photo-polymerizable HAMA hydrogel (HAMA)52. Although not a genetic model with a human counterpart, this model allowed us to definitively control the timing of IOP elevation and assess the effects of high IOP without the confounding effects of other ongoing disease processes. Placing this hydrogel, fluid-flow resistor in front of the ocular drainage tissues reproducibly elevates IOP without requiring tissue-damaging/blocking processes that are typically more variable. This model reproducibly induces a sustained elevation of IOP (Supplementary Fig. S7). To determine the effects of experimentally elevating IOP on the BRB, we compared ocular hypertensive C57BL/6 J eyes that had photopolymerized HAMA (pHAMA) to normotensive strain-matched control littermates that were administered HAMA but without photopolymerization (npHAMA. Supplementary Figs. S4C, D, S5). BRB leakage was detected in ocular hypertensive but not normotensive eyes 1 month after polymerization of HAMA. Again, the BRB compromise preceded neurodegeneration, was venous-specific, and was restricted to the GCL (Supplementary Figs. S2D, S4G, H). The documented presence of BRB compromise in ocular hypertensive eyes of mice with two distinct types of inherited glaucoma on two genetically distinct strain backgrounds, as well as following experimentally elevated IOP, suggests that high IOP may generally induce BRB compromise.

Tight junctions and mural cells appear normal

Since they are a widely used model of chronic glaucoma with findings that have translated to human glaucoma13,14,46,47, we continued to use ocular hypertensive D2 mice to investigate the mechanisms of BRB compromise. The BRB is maintained by the neurovascular unit, including pericytes and ECs, with a key component being tight junctions between ECs60. Mural cells, including pericytes are key regulators of the BRB by acting to limit transcytosis and modulate tight junction formation61. BRB breakdown can result from compromised tight junctions or loss/dysfunction of mural cells62. Thus, we investigated the integrity of tight junctions as well as the distribution and morphology of mural cells/pericytes by immunofluorescence in retinal vessels of D2 and D2-Gpnmb+ mice. No differences in fluorescence intensity of tight junction components or mural cells/pericytes were detected between ocular hypertensive D2 and normotensive control mice (Fig. 2a–dand Supplementary Fig. S8). The expression levels of tight junction proteins claudin-5, ZO-1, and occludin were also unchanged when assessing whole retinal lysates by Western blotting in D2 mice (Fig. 2e, fand Supplementary Fig. S13). Finally, we analyzed claudin-5 staining in retinal flat mounts of pHAMA and B6.Lmx1bV265D/+ mice via immunofluorescence and confirmed that there was no difference in fluorescence intensity or distribution pattern between the retinas of ocular hypertensive mice and their controls (Supplementary Fig. S9). These findings suggest that neither tight junction composition nor mural cell/pericyte loss are the main driver of BRB disruption after IOP elevation.

Bulk RNA-Seq suggests enhanced transcytosis with decreased Wnt signaling in DBA/2 J retinal veins

To achieve a deep molecular understanding of BRB leakage, we performed bulk-RNAseq on dissected retinal veins (Supplementary Fig. S10A). We compared 9 samples from 9-month-old D2 vs. 5 samples from normotensive D2-Gpnmb+ mice to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (false discovery rate; FDR < 0.05) in retinal ECs (Supplementary Fig. S10B, full list of genes in Supplementary Data 1). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) suggested that vesicular transport pathways are activated in hypertensive vs. control veins, supporting increased transcytosis. Wnt signaling and metabolism related signaling pathways appeared downregulated in hypertensive D2 veins (Supplementary Fig. S10C, full list of significantly changed pathways-Biological Process in Supplementary Data 2).

Abnormally active transcytosis may underlie IOP-induced BRB compromise

The lack of detected changes in tight junction composition and abundance led us to investigate transcytosis as the mechanism for BRB disruption. Albumin transport is generally considered a marker of transcytosis63,64. Thus, we compared albumin transport across the vascular endothelium in D2 and normotensive control mice (Fig. 3). Albumin remained confined within retinal vessels in age- and sex-matched normotensive D2-Gpnmb+ mice, reflecting an intact BRB with strongly repressed transcytosis. In contrast, albumin leakage was readily evident from peripheral retinal veins (but not arteries or capillaries) in ocular hypertensive D2 mice of both sexes, consistent with excessive transcytosis due to deficient repression of vesicular transport. Importantly, albumin extravasation was also confined to the GCL with no leakage detected from deeper retinal vascular plexuses (Fig. 3c). This suggests that abnormally active transcytosis contributes to IOP-induced BRB compromise, but a junctional component is not completely ruled out.

Given the role of MFSD2A as a key transcytosis inhibitor in CNS veins and capillaries (but not arteries65,66), we next investigated if it is affected by IOP (Fig. 4). Further supporting a transcytotic mechanism of barrier compromise, MFSD2A immunofluorescence was decreased in ECs of peripheral retinal veins of ocular hypertensive mice. In contrast, MFSD2A immunofluorescence was not altered in capillaries of the same hypertensive eyes or in any vessels of normotensive control eyes (Fig. 4a). Furthermore, the regional loss of MFSD2A matched the locations of BRB compromise (Fig. 4b). For example, albumin leaked at sites with no detectable MFSD2A but was constrained to the vascular lumen in regions where ECs still expressed MFSD2A, even if that MFSD2A expression was at lower-than-normal levels. These data argue that excessive transcytosis due to loss of MFSD2A may be the cause of BRB dysfunction following IOP elevation in D2 mice.

To further assess whether BRB breakdown in D2 glaucoma occurs through enhanced transcytosis or impaired tight junctions, we examined retinal veins of D2 and normotensive D2-Gpnmb+ mice by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). TEM analysis revealed no tight junction abnormalities in either glaucoma or normotensive control eyes (Fig. 5d–h). Tight junctions were observed as electron-dense regions between closely apposed EC membranes, where horseradish peroxidase (HRP) activity was effectively blocked. In contrast, ECs of D2 retinal veins had significantly increased numbers of vesicles compared to normotensive controls, indicating increased transcytosis. As positive controls for junctional disruption, mice were injected with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to induce inflammation and BRB breakdown67. As expected, LPS-injected mice had defective tight junctions with HRP leakage evident beyond endothelial junctions (Fig. 5b, c). Together, our data argue that BRB breakdown in D2 glaucoma is primarily driven by excessive transcytosis rather than tight junction failure, but partial junctional compromise remains possible.

Stabilization of β-catenin prevents BRB breakdown and ameliorates glaucoma

WNT β-catenin signaling stabilizes the BRB. LEF1 is a member of the TCF/LEF transcription factor family that binds to β-catenin to mediate the canonical Wnt response68. Nuclear LEF1 levels in venous ECs in the retina are established to reflect β-catenin signaling levels, as this signaling induces its nuclear translocation69–71. Therefore, we assessed nuclear LEF1. We found that its level is decreased in ECs of peripheral retinal veins in ocular hypertensive compared to normotensive control eyes (IF, Supplementary Fig. S11), indicating decreased β-catenin signaling. To test if the BRB leakage damages RGCs in glaucoma, we asked if specifically stabilizing β-catenin in ECs rescues the BRB and modulates glaucoma development. We used an EC-specific Cdh5-Cre/ERT272 to conditionally activate the expression of a stabilized allele of β-catenin (Ctnnb1flex3) (Fig. 6). This allele encodes a form of β-catenin that cannot undergo normal phosphorylation and so is degraded at a decreased rate73. Tamoxifen was administered to 6.5-month-old D2 mice to activate the Cre, which in turn activated the stabilized β-catenin allele (D2.Ctnnb1flex3/+; Cdh5-Cre/ERT2 mice). Vascular endothelial β-catenin stabilization did not alter the IOP-elevating iris disease or IOP itself (Supplementary Fig. S12). However, the stabilized β-catenin ameliorated vascular leakage (Fig. 6a, b) and decreased glaucomatous optic nerve and retinal damage (Fig. 6c–e), compared to control mice (tamoxifen-injected D2.Ctnnb1flex3/+ mice). Thus, β-catenin stabilization restored BRB function and significantly lessened glaucoma in D2 mice.

BRB compromise is common in human eyes with glaucoma

To determine whether BRB breakdown also occurs in human glaucoma, we used albumin immunostaining to detect vascular leakage in flat mounts of POAG and reasonably age-matched control human donor retinas (Fig. 7and Supplementary Data 3). Review of clinical history confirmed that all donors had no known history of retinal vascular disease or other potential causes of BRB breakdown, such as diabetes, uveitis, and sepsis. In agreement with our findings in mouse glaucoma models, we observed albumin leakage in human glaucoma eyes within the GCL but not in the deeper retinal layers (Fig. 7a–f, h, i). 14 out of 17 glaucoma eyes had leakage while the BRB was intact in all 12 control eyes (P = 1.052e-05). The extent of leakage varied between individual eyes (Fig. 7c, e, hand Supplementary Data 3). These data support the BRB compromise as a common feature of human glaucoma.

Discussion

Here, we document an IOP-induced compromise of the BRB in retinal veins that colocalizes with loss of MFSD2A. This compromise allows low molecular weight molecules and proteins, but not cells, to leak from the veins into the retinal parenchyma. Further, we experimentally determine that this subtle compromise precedes RGC loss and contributes to glaucoma progression. By specifically stabilizing β-catenin within vascular ECs, we prevent leakage and ameliorate RGC death. Vascular leakage occurred following IOP elevation of three distinct etiologies and on different genetic backgrounds. This suggests that BRB compromise may be a general feature driving glaucoma. Recently, elevated blood pressure was shown to cause focal vascular leakage with a similar decrease of MFSD2A in the brains of spontaneously hypertensive rats74. Together with our data, this supports pressure-based modulation of MFSD2A in different species. Interestingly, mutations in some genes that contribute to POAG (CAV1, FOXC1) have been shown to disturb the BRB/BBB in animal studies26,37,65,75–77.

We show leakage in three ocular hypertensive mouse models and in human POAG patients. In all cases, BRB leakage was only detected in the GCL but not in the deeper layers of the retina that are unaffected by glaucoma. In other studies of vascular leakage caused by β-catenin signaling pathway mutations (e.g., LRP-5, Frizzled-4), leakage occurs from both veins and capillaries of all retinal layers78,79. This difference is likely related to those mutations profoundly affecting signaling in all of these vascular beds. For unknown reasons (possibly mechanical), IOP elevation in our models was only detected to affect the peripheral segments of major veins at the inner retinal surface. Even in 9.5-month-old D2 eyes, when all GCL neurons are labeled by Hoechst, albumin leakage was only detected from peripheral veins abutting the GCL (most albumin detected on and around veins). This GCL-specific pattern of leakage in the ocular hypertensive models may be another factor contributing to the unique vulnerability of RGCs versus other retinal neurons to glaucoma. Molecules that may leak when the BRB is compromised and can be neurotoxic include albumin (which injures neurons at least indirectly through astrocytes)80, hemoglobin81, thrombin82, transferrin83, fibrinogen (which activates microglia)84, immunoglobulins83, and complement system components (already implicated in glaucoma)16,85–87. All are abundant in plasma and could result in neuronal damage when the BRB breaks down. In addition to albumin, there is good evidence for transcytosis of transferrin88, immunoglobulins89, and fibrinogen90. Different insults damage different parts of RGCs, with a key insult damaging RGC axons in the ONH7–10. This axonal insult is believed to be a critical insult for RGC demise, as the topography of RGC dysfunction/loss and the pattern of visual field deficits are consistent with the bundling of axons in the ONH6,7,9,91,92. The generally accepted necessity of the ONH insult and sparing of amacrine cells (another major neuronal population in the GCL) may suggest that multiple hits are necessary to kill RGCs, with the partial BRB compromise alone being insufficient. In addition, amacrine cells may be more resilient than RGCs to the local environmental changes following IOP elevation and BRB compromise. This is supported by the greater vulnerability of RGCs in both diabetes models and Alzheimer’s disease, conditions with vascular leakage in the retina93–95.

Our data suggest that increased transcytosis due to loss of MFSD2A, but not tight junction defects, is the main driver of BRB compromise in D2 mice. Our existing data support the same mechanism in the other tested models of IOP elevation. Previous studies have convincingly demonstrated that experimental ablation of Mfsd2a in mice results in BBB/BRB leakiness due to increased vesicular trafficking across ECs30,65,96. However, other studies have not observed an effect of MFSD2A mutation on vascular leakage97–99. Overall, this reveals complexity that is not yet understood and may be related to genetic, experimental and environmental differences. Our data further support a role of MFSD2A in regulating the BRB and transcytosis. MFSD2A loss is spatially associated with leakage, and an increase in transcytotic vesicles is found by EM, while tight junctions appear to remain functional in D2 mice (though very mild junctional leakage cannot be ruled out). Experimentally stabilizing β-catenin/Wnt signaling rescues vascular barrier defects in ocular hypertensive D2 mice, consistent with previous studies in Alzheimer’s disease and diabetic retinopathy93,94.

The exact mechanisms by which high IOP induces EC changes and BRB breakdown remain to be elucidated. Studies have shown that high IOP leads to oxidative stress and other cellular stresses (that mimic or exacerbate age-dependent changes), causing oxidative damage and metabolic disturbances to cells100. Stress and metabolic alterations in ECs, mural cells, or other neurovascular unit cells would likely result in BRB deterioration over time. Alternatively, signaling by mechanosensing molecules in the endothelium may be modulated by IOP in a way that affects barrier integrity101. Our data show that high IOP results in decreased MFSD2A, suggesting that releasing its suppression of vesicular trafficking increases transcytosis. A putative connection could be that pressure-induced metabolic dysregulation and oxidative stress decreases β-catenin signaling, as oxidative stress promotes GSK3β activation102, which in turn enhances β-catenin degradation. Mechanosensing molecules include VE-Cadherin, integrins101 and Piezo 1103, but they are not known to modulate MFSD2A. Other possible mediators include pressure-induced cytoskeletal remodeling104 or altered lipid metabolism105, each of which could potentially converge on MFSD2A regulation. Our detection of decreased MFSD2A due to increased pressure is consistent with the pressure-based modulation of MFSD2A observed in the brain vasculature of hypertensive rats74 and offers a plausible link between mechanical forces/mechano-signaling and the molecular alterations we observe in ocular hypertensive models. Changing either intravascular pressure or tissue pressure (an effect of IOP) alters the transmural pressure across vessel walls; this induces shifts in vessel caliber and flow, which changes the wall shear experienced by ECs. These changes translate into gene-expression changes. For example, higher steady shear is linked with increased KLF2/KLF4 and NOS3106; while reduced or disturbed shear and increased stretch favor NF-κB/AP-1107 and YAP/TAZ activity108 that alter EC phenotypes. More directly, shear and stretch status are well known to alter endothelial β-catenin localization and signaling109, while β-catenin drives Mfsd2a expression. Raising intraocular pressure may lower ocular perfusion pressure, dampen the ocular pulse, and alter shear/flow in retinal veins, resulting in reduced nuclear localization of β-catenin109,110 and transcriptionally reduced MFSD2A expression. In addition, the altered EC phenotype may result in increased MFSD2A ubiquitination and degradation111. Understanding these mechanisms will identify new therapeutic targets, potentially transforming the treatment of glaucoma and other diseases characterized by BRB dysfunction.

BRB compromise, similar to that reported here, may occur commonly in POAG and other forms of human glaucoma. It could have been previously missed as tracers need to be used for detection, and the leakage could be restricted to the far peripheral regions of retinal veins. In the clinic, glaucoma patients are not normally evaluated using vascular tracers, and the vasculature in the far retinal periphery is rarely assessed, even in research studies. Inflammation is known to compromise vascular barriers and is clinically known to cause more generalized vascular leakage in a subset of eyes with inflammatory conditions and glaucoma112,113. We previously reported leakage in D2 mice but these initial experiments neither determined the peripheral venous or molecular nature of leakage nor tested the causative role of IOP114. In the Lmx1b mouse model, some leakage also occurred from vessels in the ONH, but further experiments are needed to determine both how common this is and the timing of its onset in relation to retinal vein leakage. Importantly, studies in humans have reported subtle vascular leakage (tracer leakage without hemorrhage) in the ONH of patients with POAG115–128. The leakage was present in 30% or more of POAG patients120 and was significantly correlated to IOP128. Despite this and an extensive literature on the vasculature and blood flow129–131, BRB compromise has hardly been considered in glaucoma. Neither IOP-induced BRB compromise nor vascular leakage were previously shown to contribute to glaucoma progression. Our finding of albumin leakage in human POAG retinas in a pattern similar to that seen in our mouse glaucoma models supports a role for BRB leakage in human glaucoma pathogenesis.

BRB disruption occurs in Alzheimer’s disease and other neurodegenerative diseases132–136, but is less specific and more severe than we document here in glaucoma. In Alzheimer’s disease, it disrupts tight junctions and affects the extensive capillary network in the retina137–139. We are unaware of studies other than our current one that document partial and focal BRB compromise specifically in retinal veins. Our results suggest that venous leakage of tracers may become a useful diagnostic test in glaucoma and a tool in guiding treatment choices. On the other hand, it is possible that different patterns of leakage may occur depending on the magnitude of IOP, type of glaucoma, and/or genetic and environmental background. Although further experiments are needed, it is conceivable that the presence, absence, severity and/or location of vascular leakage may be able to distinguish those whose glaucoma is actively progressing from those in a currently stable state. Another possibility is the use of such tests to differentiate those with ocular hypertension who need treatment because their glaucoma is likely to progress from those with ocular hypertension who are unlikely to progress and do not need treatment. Currently, it is not possible to predictively distinguish these groups. This results in withholding of necessary treatment from some individuals with high IOP but overtreating of others with unnecessary exposure to the potential side effects of these treatments5,140,141. An important study recently documented metabolism-related decreases in oxygen consumption and NAD levels in blood cells of glaucoma patients, with lower levels of each associating with a greater rate of glaucoma progression142. Along with our current study, this offers promise for valuable, clinically achievable tests to predict glaucoma progression by assessing distinct disease features/mechanisms. We propose that assessing vascular leakage, both with and without assessing NAD and/or metabolism in blood cells, eyes, or other tissues, may provide valuable and rapid tests of treatment efficacy (or of patient compliance). Such tests would be important when refining treatment options for individual patients with IOP-lowering or other medications, or even when developing new therapeutic molecules using animal models. BRB leakage could conceivably occur in individuals with normal-tension glaucoma, either for reasons other than IOP, due to a unique sensitivity of their BRB to IOP levels that are not considered elevated, or because higher IOP was missed due to inadequate sampling.

In summary, our study highlights IOP-induced BRB compromise as a significant component of glaucoma pathogenesis and warrants further research.

This study has several limitations. First, further RNA and protein-level validation of the transcriptomic findings would strengthen the molecular interpretations. Second, although it is expected that β-catenin stabilization increased MFSD2A and lessens transcytosis, this was not assessed by EM and is a future goal. Third, very subtle leakage or mild tight junction defects cannot be completely excluded by EM, and so further, more specific experiments targeting transcytosis, but not tight junctions, are underway. Finally, in addition to classic vascular barriers that regulate the entry of molecules into neural tissue, ECs also contribute to immune privilege by controling immune cell extravasation. Our previous studies identified changes in myeloid cell extravasation that occur very early in glaucoma and modulate damage114,143, while others suggest T cell involvement144,145. Further work is needed to determine how extravasation of specific classes of immune cells and barrier compromise interact to modulate glaucoma.

Methods

Mouse strains, breeding, and husbandry

All mice were treated in accordance with the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology’s statement on the use of animals in ophthalmic research. All animal procedures were performed according to the protocols approved by Columbia University’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee under protocol number AABU0654 and AABE9554, and University of Oklahoma’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee under protocol number 24-039. DBA/2 J mice (strain #000671), DBA/2J-Gpnmb+/SjJ (strain #007048) and C57BL/6 J mice (strain #000664) were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory. Tg(Cdh5-cre/ERT2)1Rha mice72 were imported from Dr. Carol Troy’s lab at Columbia University. Ctnnb1tm1Mmt mice73 were imported from Dr. Xin Zhang’s lab at Columbia University. Both alleles were backcrossed to DBA/2 J for > 10 generations to produce congenic mice on the DBA/2 J background (all experimental mice were > N10). B6.Lmx1bV265D/+ mice51 were from our lab stock. All mice were housed in a 21 °C environment with a 14 h light and 10 h dark cycle and fed with a 6% fat diet (PicoLab Rodent Diet 20). Both female and male mice were used for analysis.

Tamoxifen injection

Tamoxifen (Sigma T5648) solution was prepared at 20 mg/mL in sterile corn oil (Sigma C8267) using a rotator to facilitate dissolution overnight at room temperature, then aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C. For each mouse, 100 µL of 20 mg/mL tamoxifen in corn oil was administered in 4 intraperitoneal injections spaced 2 days apart.

Assessment of retinal vascular permeability with tracer

Mice were restrained and intravenously injected with 200 μL 0.02% Hoechst 33342 (Invitrogen, H1399) in 0.9% saline. In some cases, mice were intravenously injected with 100 μg Isolectin GS-IB4 From Griffonia simplicifolia, Alexa Fluor 647 conjugate (Thermo Fisher Scientific I32450) in 100 μL 0.9% Saline one-hour prior to Hoechst injection to label the retinal vasculature. Fifteen minutes after Hoechst injection, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, eyes enucleated, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M Phosphate Buffer, pH 7.4 for 1 h at room temperature. Retinas were subsequently dissected, flat-mounted onto slides, and imaged with a Leica SP8 laser scanning confocal microscope.

Immunohistochemistry

Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, eyes were enucleated and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M Phosphate Buffer pH 7.4 at room temperature for 1 h. Retinas were dissected and blocked for 4 h at room temperature in blocking solution (Phosphate-buffered saline, 0.5% Triton-X100, 5% donkey serum), and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody in blocking solution. After washing 5 times for 40 min at room temperature with washing solution (Phosphate-buffered saline, 0.5% Triton-X100), retinas were incubated with secondary antibody in blocking solution overnight at 4 °C. Retinas were washed for another 5 times for 40 min at room temperature with washing solution, and flat-mounted. The following primary antibodies and lectins were used for this study: Mouse anti-claudin-5, Alexa Fluor 488 conjugate (Thermo Fisher Scientific 352588, 1:500); Rabbit anti-ZO1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific 40-2200, 1:200); Rabbit anti-desmin (Cell Signaling 5332, 1:200); Rabbit anti-NG2 (Sigma AB5320, 1:200); Goat anti-Albumin (mouse), FITC conjugate (Thermo Fisher Scientific A90-234F, 1:200); Goat anti-Albumin (human), FITC conjugate (Thermo Fisher Scientific A80-229F, 1:200); Rabbit anti-Mfsd2a (Cell Signaling 80302, 1:200); Rat anti-CD31 (BD Pharmingen 550274, 1:50); Rabbit anti-RBPMS (Novus NBP2-20112, 1:200); Goat anti-VE-Cadherin (R&D Systems AF1002, 1:200); Rabbit-anti-LEF1 (Cell Signaling 2230, 1:200) and Isolectin GS-IB4 From Griffonia simplicifolia, Alexa Fluor 647 conjugate (Thermo Fisher Scientific I32450, 1:50). For LEF1, eyes were fixed in 1% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M Phosphate Buffer pH 7.4 at room temperature for 1 h. Retinas were dissected and blocked for overnight at 4 °C in blocking solution (Phosphate-buffered saline, 1% Triton-X100, 5% donkey serum) before adding primary antibodies. For each genotype and each antibody, at least 16 eyes were stained for DBA/2 J and controls, 8 eyes for Lmx1bV265D/+, 8 for HAMA and their controls. Peripheral human retinas were dissected into 5 mm × 5 mm squares, with three squares from each eye examined. After staining, retinas were flat-mounted and examined by a Keyence BZ-X810 microscope. All stained eyes were visually analyzed, while a subset of them (subset numbers indicated in figure legends) were imaged by a Leica SP8 laser scanning confocal microscope with a 63X objective lens for further analysis. Findings agreed between the Keyence and Confocal analyses. Images were stitched with Imaris stitcher 10.2.

Nerve staining and evaluation of damage

The intracranial segments of the optic nerves were fixed in 0.8% paraformaldehyde; 1.22% glutaraldehyde; and 0.08 M Phosphate Buffer pH 7.4 at 4 °C for 12 h, followed by processing and embedding in plastic. The retro-orbital ends of the nerves were cut into 1 μm thick sections and stained with paraphenylenediamine (PPD). PPD stains the myelin sheath of all axons, while specifically darkly staining the axoplasm of damaged axons146. At least 3 sections were examined to determine the level of damage for each nerve. The optic nerves were determined to have 3 levels of damage as previously reported and validated against axon counting12,14: 1. No or early damage (NOE) - less than 5% of axons were damaged, and there was no gliosis. These nerves do not have glaucoma (no damage by conventional criteria) but are called no or early, as some of them have early molecular changes that precede neurodegeneration. 2. Moderate damage (MOD) - had an average of 30% axonal loss and early gliosis. 3. Severe damage (SEV) - had more than 50% axonal loss and damage with prominent gliosis. All nerves were evaluated by at least two masked investigators. In cases where the two investigators did not agree on the damage level, a third investigator (also masked) analyzed the nerve and the most assigned damage level was used147.

IOP assessment and induction of high IOP

IOP was assessed using the microneedle method as previously outlined in detail148,149. This prevents overestimation of IOP by noninvasive IOP-measuring instruments due to corneal property changes that can occur in the models used. In brief, mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (99 mg/kg; Ketlar, Parke-Davis, Paramus, NJ, USA) and xylazine (9 mg/kg; Rompun, Phoenix Pharmaceutical, St Joseph, MO, USA) immediately before the IOP measurement. All IOP measurements were taken during the same time period each day. We also assessed anterior chamber (AC) depth in all studied eyes as an indicator of exposure to high IOP. All studied ocular hypertensive animals had an obviously enlarged AC, while the space in the AC is tiny in normotensive mice (for representative examples see Supplementary Fig. S7). This ensured that all eyes with genotypes or experimental treatment that increase IOP had experienced high IOP, as increased AC depth is a reliable measure of even modest exposure to high IOP in mice43,51,58. When IOP becomes elevated, the measured IOP values of a mouse population spread in each direction as homeostatic regulation and diurnal cycles are perturbed42. This is well established in various mouse models, with some mice having higher IOP during the light-period of the day and others during the dark cycle149–151. Thus, among eyes with abnormal IOP exposure, high IOP is not detected in every eye at every measurement time, and AC depth serves as a reliable surrogate43,51,58. Increased AC depth was never observed in control normotensive mice. For the experimentally induced model, IOP was elevated by polymerizing a hydrogel resistor in the path of aqueous humor drainage, as published52 with some modifications in C57BL/6 J mice at 3 months of age. Mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (99 mg/kg; Ketlar, Parke-Davis, Paramus, NJ, USA) and xylazine (9 mg/kg; Rompun, Phoenix Pharmaceutical, St Joseph, MO, USA). Instead of penetrating the cornea with a 32 G needle, A small mid-peripheral corneal incision was made with a very sharp sapphire knife (7900-P, Katalyst Surgical) to prevent corneal damage from the surgery. One μL of HAMA was delivered into the angle using a 100-micron OD glass microneedle. Using this method, IOP becomes promptly elevated, and the higher values are sustained for about 4 weeks.

Western blot

Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, eyes were enucleated and retinas were dissected in ice cold phosphate-buffered saline, homogenized in lysis buffer (150 mmol/L NaCl, 50 mmol/L Tris HCl pH 8.0, 1 mmol/L CaCl2, 1% NP40, 0.1% N-dodecyl-β-D-maltoside, 3 mmol/L MgCl2, and DNAseI 80 units/mL) with protease inhibitor (Roche, cOmplete 4693116001) and incubated on ice for 30 min. Retinal lysates were then centrifuged at 20000 x g for 5 min. 160 μL supernatants were mixed with 40 μL 1 M DTT and 200 μL Laemmli sample buffer (Bio-Rad, 1610737), incubated at 40 °C for 10 min, run on 4%–15% polyacrylamide gel (Bio-Rad, 4561084), and transferred to PVDF membranes. The following primary antibodies were used for this study: Rabbit anti-claudin-5 (Cell Signaling 49564S, 1:1000); Rabbit anti-ZO1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific 40-2200, 1:4000); Rabbit anti-occludin (Thermo Fisher Scientific 40-6100, 1:5000); Mouse anti-β-actin, HRP conjugate (Thermo Fisher Scientific MA5-15739-HRP, 1:20000). Chemical signals were imaged by iBright system (Invitrogen). Signals were quantified with the ImageJ gel plugin and normalized by β-actin.

Mouse retinal vein dissection and RNA sequencing

9-month-old DBA/2 J and DBA/2J-Gpnmb + /SjJ were intraperitoneally injected with 0.33 mg/g body weight sodium fluorescein (AK-FLUOR, Akorn). Five minutes after injection, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, eyes enucleated and retinal veins were dissected using super fine forceps (Dumont, NC9497215) under the fluorescent dissection scope. 4 major veins from the same mouse, including main branches, were combined as one sample. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Micro kit (Qiagen, 74004). RNA concentration and quality (RIN) was measured by Bioanalyzer. About 100 pg total RNA (RIN > 9) were reverse transcribed to cDNA using SMART-Seq v4 Ultra Low Input RNA Kit for Sequencing (Takara). The library was prepared using Nextera XT (Illumina) and sequenced by Aviti Element. Bulk RNA-seq data was aligned to GRCm38 and gencode M23 and quantified by Kallisto. Transcript quantifications were imported into the R framework for downstream analysis. Differential expression analysis between the DBA/2 J and DBA/2J-Gpnmb + /SjJ eyes was conducted using Sleuth, accounting for batch and other experimental covariates in the design model. Next, significant differentially expressed genes were passed to Gene Set Enrichment Analysis against Gene Ontology gene lists. The annotated datasets can also be viewed at: https://glaucomaveinrnaseq.nygenome.org/

LPS induced tight junction breakdown in mice

Six months old C57BL/6 J mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (100 mg/kg)/xylazine (10 mg/kg). Eyes were intravitreally injected with 1 µg LPS (L2262, Sigma) diluted in 1 µL sterile Phosphate-buffered saline using a 10 µL glass syringe (Hamilton Company, Reno, NV, USA). Eyes were harvested 24 h after LPS injection for further analysis.

Transmission electron microscopy

Ultrastructural analysis of primary retinal venules was done by the method of Chow and Gu65 with some modifications. Mice were restrained and intravenously injected with 0.5 mg/g body weight Peroxidase from horseradish (HRP, P8250, Sigma) dissolved in 200 μL 0.9% saline. Thirty minutes after injection, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, eyes enucleated, retinas were dissected in 4% PFA and fixed in 5% glutaraldehyde, 4% PFA, 0.1 M sodium cacodylate at room temperature for 1 h. Followed by washing with 0.1 M sodium cacodylate 3 times and overnight fixation in 4% PFA at 4 °C. Fixed retinas were washed 3 times with 0.05 M Tris-HCl pH 7.4 buffer before reaction with 3-3’ diaminobenzidine (DAB). Retinas were incubated for 45 min at room temperature on a shaker in filtered DAB solution (0.5 mg/mL 3-3’ DAB in 0.05 M Tris-HCl pH 7.53 buffer, 0.01% hydrogen peroxide). The retinal vasculature became visible during the DAB reaction. Following the DAB reaction, the retinas were washed 3 times with 0.1 M sodium-cacodylate-buffer pH 7.37 before being placed in TEM fixative (5% glutaraldehyde, 4% PFA, 0.1 M sodium-cacodylate-buffer pH 7.37) and incubated at 4 °C overnight. After fixation, retinas were washed 3 times with 0.1 M sodium-cacodylate-buffer pH 7.37. Peripheral retinal regions containing the distal branch points of primary retinal venules were dissected into ~ 1 cm-sided triangles. A small nick on the peripheral retina side provided orientation during processing. The dissections were postfixed in 1% osmium tetroxide and 1.5% potassium ferrocyanide, then dehydrated and embedded in Eponate/Araldite epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections (100 nm) containing the venule regions of interest were cut from the resin block, placed onto 200-mesh copper grids, and stained with Reynold’s lead citrate for approximately 8 min. The venule endothelial lining along the entire lumen was imaged at 7000 × magnification using a Hitachi H7600 TEM operating at 100 kV, equipped with an AMT Nanosprint 1200 camera.

Image analysis and quantification

Vascular leakage

Hoechst tracer-labeled retinal images were processed with Imaris 10.2 software to detect and quantify vascular leakage. Smoothed surfaces were created based on Hoechst signal (blue wavelength, pseudo colored to red for enhanced visualization in figure) with background subtraction and splitting of touching objects. All created surfaces were then filtered in Imaris to separate ECs from labeled neurons using its inbuilt AI machine-learning tools. In the first round of AI training, several examples of each cell type category were manually assigned. After the next round of AI prediction, several incorrect results were manually corrected and used for the following training round. After 5 rounds of training, the trained model was saved and applied to other images for automated cell-type discrimination. For each new image, an additional 1-2 rounds of training were applied. The amount of leakage was quantified by counting Hoechst-positive neuronal nuclei in 0.81 mm2 regions of the compressed z-stack that were centered over the Y branches of both peripheral veins for each flat-mounted retina. Maximum intensity projections were used to obtain the following stacks: 14 μm z-stacks for GCL, starting from the peripheral veins toward the IPL; 21 μm z-stacks for INL/IPL, starting from the IPL vessels toward the GCL layer; and 21 μm z-stacks for OPL/ONL, starting from the OPL vessels toward the ONL.

RGC quantification

RBPMS-stained RGCs in high-resolution images of whole retinal flat mount images were counted by Imaris 10.2 software Spots function. Each individual RGC was marked by a spot according to the RBPMS signal and total number of spots were counted.

Tight-junction and mural cell protein

Fluorescence signals were quantified in high-resolution retinal flat mounts. The “freeform” selection tool in Image J was used to obtain the mean fluorescence value exclusively for the major veins in each retina.

EM quantification

Transcytotic vesicles were manually counted based on their characteristic shape and size (30–100 nm). EC boundaries were determined by morphology, and areas were measured using ImageJ. For each vein section, all vesicles lining the entire lumen were counted and normalized to the total EC area.

Box Plot

Box plots display the median (center line), with the box representing the interquartile range (25th–75th percentiles). Whiskers extend to the minimum and maximum values within 1.5 × the interquartile range. Individual data points are shown as overlaid dots.

Statistical analysis

Fluorescence intensity for immunofluorescence and Western blot signals (Fig. 2) were compared using Welch’s t-test for each marker. Vesicular density (Fig. 5) was compared using Welch’s t-test. In the β-catenin stabilization experiments (Fig. 6), numbers of leakage-labeled RGCs (Hoechst-positive neurons per mm2), total RGC counts (RPBMS), as well as control vs. stabilized β-catenin-expressing IOPs were compared using Welch’s t test. Fisher’s Exact test was used to compare the degree of nerve damage between control and stabilized β-catenin-expressing groups.
