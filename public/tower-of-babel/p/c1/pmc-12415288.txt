Communication increases cooperation among students in a coordination game
Authors: Elizabeth T. Hallers-Haalboom, Kris J. M. De Jaegher, Elisabeth H. M. Sterck, Sarah F. Brosnan, Marjolijn M. Vermande
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cooperation often requires individuals to balance personal risk with mutual gain. The Stag Hunt game provides a well-established paradigm for studying such decision-making. Prior research suggested that verbal communication about the game correlated with participants finding the optimal coordinated solution, but these studies either did not manipulate communication directly or informed participants of the payoff structure in advance. This study examined whether communication improves cooperative decision-making among college students under conditions in which the payoff structure had to be inferred through repeated play. A total of 127 same-sex dyads (Mage = 22.8 y, 51.2% female) played 40 rounds of an online Stag Hunt game, with dyads randomly assigned to either a no communication or communication condition. Participants were not informed about the game’s payoff structure in advance and had to infer it during play. Results showed that coordination on the payoff-dominant outcome (Stag–Stag) increased across trials, but only when communication was possible. No significant sex differences were observed. These findings highlight the central role of communication in fostering cooperation, particularly in environments in which information must be jointly discovered. This is an important consideration for developing and interpreting future research. Moreover, subsequent research should explore how the content, timing, and relevance of communication shape cooperative outcomes over time.

Cooperation is fundamental to social behavior and society (1, 2). Many cooperative interactions are mutualistic, benefitting all parties involved (3). The Stag Hunt game, also known as the Assurance game, is commonly used to study this type of cooperation (4). In this game, two players choose each round between Stag or Hare. Coordinating on Stag yields the highest payoff for both (payoff-dominant equilibrium), while choosing Hare provides a smaller but guaranteed reward (risk-dominant equilibrium). However, if one player chooses Stag but the other Hare, only the Hare player receives the small payoff and the Stag hunter receives nothing. The Stag Hunt game thus represents a conflict between risk-averse behavior and cooperation (4).

Previous studies have yielded mixed findings regarding students’ cooperative abilities. In one study (5)—using physical tokens—only 19% of the student pairs coordinated on Stag–Stag in most rounds. Although communication was allowed, only 8% of the pairs actually spoke during the game—possibly due to the presence of a barrier between players. In contrast, a computerized version of the game without such a barrier (6) found that 81% of the student pairs eventually converged on Stag–Stag. In this study, all pairs spoke with each other, and successful coordination was closely tied to game-related communication. However, the design of the study meant that this was a correlational finding, and the small number of players who did not communicate made it difficult to draw firm conclusions. Other research also finds that (pre-play) communication increases mutual cooperation (7), especially when two-way communication is possible (8, 9). One challenge is that in most human studies, the payoff structure is presented before the game (7–9), which may decrease the need for communication compared to situations in which players must infer the structure by interaction (5, 6).

Here, we examined whether allowing verbal communication increases the likelihood of coordinating on the payoff-dominant outcome (Stag–Stag). As in ref. 5, 6, students were not informed about the reward structure in advance, but had to learn it through interacting with one another, which may more realistically reflect day-to-day interactions and increase the benefit of cooperating. We hypothesized that communication would enhance coordination on Stag–Stag. In addition, given some prior suggestions that females may show stronger cooperative tendencies than males (10), we also included an exploratory analysis of potential sex differences. The hypotheses, study design, and planned analyses were preregistered at the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/kp6e2).

Results

Overall, students chose the optimal Stag–Stag option in 39.25% of the trials (26.95% in the no communication condition and 51.75% in the communication condition). A significant difference emerged between the two conditions, Fisher’s Exact Test P = 0.029, indicating that dyads who were able to communicate chose the Stag–Stag option more frequently than those who could not communicate. Further inspection of the communication sessions confirmed that nearly all dyads used the communication option and that their discussions were task-relevant (SI Appendix). No significant difference was found between female (40.44%) and male (38.09%) pairs, Fisher’s Exact Test P = 0.437.

Table 1 shows the results of the model effects of the Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) analyses. A significant main effect of trial number was observed (P < 0.001), indicating that cooperative success increased across the trials. This effect was qualified by a significant interaction with condition (P < 0.001). Students in the communication condition showed a marked increase in cooperative success (i.e., playing Stag–Stag) over the course of trials, whereas students in the no-communication condition demonstrated little to no improvement over time (Fig. 1). These findings were consistent across analyses conducted for the first 10, 20, 30, and all 40 trials.

Discussion

This study examined whether verbal communication enhanced cooperative success in an online Stag Hunt game. Consistent with prior findings (7–9), we found that communication substantially increased the likelihood of students coordinating on the payoff-dominant outcome. Unlike many earlier studies (but see 5, 6), participants in our study were not informed about the reward structure and had to discover it through repeated play. This highlights the key role of communication, not just in signaling intentions, but also in coconstructing an understanding of the task.

These findings support earlier evidence that communication reduces uncertainty and helps players to align expectations (8). In the Stag Hunt game, where mutual trust is necessary to choose the higher-risk option, communication likely enabled participants to express intent and build a shared commitment to cooperation. Without such interaction, players may default to the safer but suboptimal Hare choice. Moreover, when the payoff structure is unknown, as in our study, communication may play a unique role by primarily enabling participants to coconstruct a shared interpretation of the game—a process that may be less necessary when players already know the game’s structure, potentially reducing the need for strategic dialog. Our inspection of the communication sessions supports this interpretation, showing that nearly all dyads discussed their intended choices and most dyads explicitly reasoned about the payoff structure.

Importantly, the content and quality of communication may shape its effectiveness. Recent work shows that task-relevant discussion fosters coordination (11), while competitive expressions and off-topic talk can undermine it (11, 12). Future work should therefore examine how communication unfolds over time, including its content, timing, and evolution.

In contrast to our expectations (10), no significant sex differences in cooperative success were observed. While this aligns with other findings (11), further research with larger and more diverse samples is needed to clarify the role of sex and communication style in cooperative contexts.

In sum, our findings highlight the central role of communication in facilitating coordination—especially when the reward structure must be jointly discovered—and deepens our understanding of how human cooperation is shaped by the interplay between social interaction, shared reasoning, and strategic uncertainty.

Materials and Methods

Participants were Dutch (under)graduate students with no prior knowledge of the Stag Hunt game. Participants provided informed consent. Each dyad participated in a 30 to 45-min online session via MS Teams, led by a trained game leader. Participants were informed that they were paired with another student of the same sex and were randomly assigned to either the communication or no communication condition. In both conditions, players could see each other via video in MS Teams. In the communication condition, they could also talk to each other through audio, whereas in the no communication, microphones were muted to prevent communication. At the start of each session, the game leader provided general information about the game, without mentioning the study’s aims and payoff structure (5, 6). The game sessions were recorded in MS Teams and the participants’ choices for Stag or Hare were saved in “Stag Hunt Score Calculator” files. The study was approved by the Ethics Review Board of the Faculty of Social and Behavioral Sciences, Utrecht University (FETC approval code: 20-287). For more details, see SI Appendix.
