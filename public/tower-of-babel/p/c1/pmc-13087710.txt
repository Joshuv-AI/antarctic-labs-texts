The TcaR-CtsR regulatory cascade governs multi-stress tolerance and biofilm formation in foodborne Staphylococcusaureus
Authors: Hao Pei, Li Xu, Xiaoxiao Chen, Fanwenqing Kong, Meng Wu, Fei Shang, Ting Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStaphylococcus aureus exhibits remarkable resilience to environmental stresses, largely governed by complex transcriptional regulatory networks. While CtsR is a well-established repressor of the heat shock response in Gram-positive bacteria, its role in S. aureus extends beyond proteotoxic stress management. In this study, using a foodborne S. aureus strain RMSA49, we demonstrated that CtsR is critical for biofilm formation, glycopeptide antibiotic susceptibility, and multi-stress tolerance to heat, desiccation, oxidative, and salt stresses. Genetic disruption of ctsR significantly impaired biofilm development and increased bacterial sensitivity to vancomycin and teicoplanin. Furthermore, we identified TcaR as a direct upstream regulator of ctsR, binding specifically to its promoter region, as confirmed by EMSA. This TcaR-CtsR regulatory cascade represents a novel layer of control in the stress adaptation mechanism of S. aureus. Our findings expand the functional scope of CtsR and provide new insights into the molecular basis of stress resilience and antibiotic susceptibility in this pathogen, highlighting potential targets for controlling S. aureus in both food safety and clinical contexts.Keywords: Foodborne, Staphylococcus aureus, CtsR, Cascade regulation, Stress resistance

Introduction

Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), a Gram-positive bacterium prevalent in environmental reservoirs, poses dual threats as a significant zoonotic pathogen and a critical food safety concern (Bianchi et al., 2022). The pathogenicity of S. aureus primarily stems from heat-stable enterotoxins (SEs), particularly SEA and SEB, which induce food poisoning and resist thermal inactivation during standard food preparation (Umeda et al., 2021). Epidemiological evidence highlights dairy products as high-risk transmission vectors, with mastitic bovine secretions contaminating raw milk through milking equipment and subsequently infiltrating processed foods like cheese (Pineda et al., 2025; Wald et al., 2019). Notably, even frozen commodities such as ice cream demonstrate vulnerability (Gücükoğlu et al., 2013). The thermostable nature of SEs, coupled with S. aureus rapid food matrix proliferation capacity, necessitates enhanced processing protocols across dairy production chains to mitigate contamination risks. Understanding the environmental adaptation mechanisms of Staphylococcus aureus is of critical importance for advancing the development of food processing technologies and optimizing pathogenic control strategies.

Critical environmental factors including temperature, humidity, oxygen availability, osmotic pressure and pH levels exert significant impacts on the growth and survival of bacteria. S. aureus exhibits remarkable environmental adaptability, enabling it to survive under diverse adverse conditions, particularly in foodborne strains. Temperature is one of the key factors influencing the growth and survival of S. aureus. Studies have shown that S. aureus can grow within the temperature range of 7 °C to 48.5 °C (Schmitt et al., 1990). After being subjected to high-temperature acclimation, the S. aureus will significantly enhance its adaptability to high temperatures (Cebrián et al., 2009). Drying stress can be regarded as an extreme form of osmotic stress, which causes the denaturation of cell membranes and intracellular proteins, leading to cell dehydration until death (Zeidler et al., 2017). S. aureus has an extremely strong tolerance to drying, enabling it to survive for a long time in dried milk powder. Studies have shown that S. aureus can survive on dry non-biological surfaces for more than 318 days (Chaibenjawong and Foster, 2011). Environments characterized by elevated sodium chloride concentrations induce high osmotic pressure, leading to cellular water loss, deformation, and potential cell death. S. aureus exhibits an adaptive response to this osmotic stress by modulating the cytoplasmic concentrations of proteins, amino acids, and compatible solutes (Proctor et al., 1998; Sreedharan et al., 1990). This physiological adaptation confers tolerance, enabling the bacterium to maintain viability even under the high-salt conditions typical of pickled food preservation (Feng et al., 2022; LupÍN et al., 1981). Furthermore, S. aureus counters the oxidative burst from phagocytic cells by expressing a constellation of antioxidant enzymes, notably superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase, and alkyl hydroperoxide reductase (AhpR). These enzymes orchestrate the neutralization of host-derived reactive oxygen species, a critical defense mechanism that prevents oxidative injury to indispensable intracellular macromolecules (Chatham et al., 1994; Cosgrove et al., 2007; Das et al., 2008). Bacteria form a dense membranous substance, biofilm, by secreting a self-generated extracellular matrix composed of proteins, carbohydrates and extracellular DNA (eDNA) (Moormeier and Bayles, 2017). This structure encapsulates the bacteria, enabling them to survive in harsh or extreme environments.

The strong adaptability of S. aureus to the environment is attributed to a complex regulatory network composed of multiple transcriptional regulatory factors. The SigB protein plays a pivotal role in S. aureus by coordinating its global defense against a plethora of environmental insults. It integrates stress signals induced by heat, reactive oxygen species, and high osmotic conditions to mount a protective response (Cebrián et al., 2009; Miyazaki et al., 1999b; Wang et al., 2022). The SarA transcription factor family plays an important role in the virulence and stress response of S. aureus. Report has been shown that SarA directly regulate the expression of trxB encoded the thioredoxin reductase to resist oxidative stress (Ballal and Manna Adhar, 2010). In our previous study, we found that inactivation of MarR family transcriptional regulator TcaR resulted in a significant decrease in the tolerance of mild-derived S. aureus RMSA49 to environmental stresses (Pei et al., 2024). Furthermore, the expression of ctsR was significantly up-regulated in the tcaR deletion mutant. CtsR, a globally conserved transcriptional regulator found in diverse bacterial species, governs the proper functioning of the protein quality control network. Upon encountering environmental stress, CtsR facilitates a rapid cellular response through its own inactivation and activation (Elsholz et al., 2010). This regulatory mechanism is crucial for the survival and virulence of several pathogenic bacteria. However, little is known about the function of CtsR in foodborne S. aureus.

In this study, we constructed the ctsR-deletion strain and complementary strain in foodborne S. aureus and compared their tolerance of environmental stress and biofilm. The results demonstrated CtsR plays a significant role in the environmental tolerance and biofilm formation. Furthermore, electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) demonstrated the direct regulatory effect of TcaR on ctsR, suggesting that TcaR may regulate the environmental tolerance and biofilm formation of S. aureus through the CtsR cascade. Based on the above results, we proposed a new regulatory pathway for S. aureus stress resistance, which provided experimental basis for revealing the molecular mechanism of S. aureus stress resistance and contributing to food processing technology.

Material and method

Strains and growth conditions

The initial strain, S. aureus RMSA49, was acquired from a raw milk specimen and its identity was verified through 16S rRNA gene sequencing (Shen et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022). Routine cultivation involved tryptic soy broth (TSB) or TSB-based agar (1.5%) from Oxoid (Basingstoke, UK). Antibiotic selection was applied using compounds supplied by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), with media being amended to final concentrations of 100 μg/mL for ampicillin and 15 μg/mL for chloramphenicol. Mutants construction

Homologous recombination served as the basis for the knockout of gene ctsR, with the shuttle plasmid pBTs being utilized. PCR was employed to amplify upstream (1226 bp) and downstream (1087 bp) fragments from the RMSA49 genome, and the primers used were ctsR-up-KpnⅠ-F/ctsR-up-R and ctsR-down-F/ctsR-down-SalⅠ-R. Recombinant PCR was applied to ligate the upstream and downstream fragments. The ligated fragments and plasmid pBTs were both digested with KpnI/SalI, and then ligation was performed using T4 ligase (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to obtain pBTs-ctsR. The pBTs-ctsR was first transferred into S. aureus RN4220 for modification, and after that, it was further transferred into S. aureus RMSA49. Homologous recombination between the vector and the genome was induced to delete the ctsR gene at 42 °C. The ctsR-deletion strains (ΔctsR) were selected via subculture and antibiotic treatments, and their confirmation was done by polymerase chain reaction and sequencing with primers check-out-F/check-out-R. From the RMSA49 genome, primers pLI50-ctsR-F and pLI50-ctsR-R were used to amplify the fragment contained ctsR gene and its promoter region. After that, the pLI50 plasmid was digested with HindIII, and ligation with the fragment was carried out by One Step Cloning Kit (ATG, Nanjing, China) to get pLI50-ctsR. The pLI50-ctsR underwent modification within strain RN4220 before being introduced into ΔctsR to create the complemented strain ΔctsR-com (Table 1). To account for possible plasmid-related influences on bacterial phenotypes, RMSA49 was transformed with the empty pLI50 vector. Verification of all mutant strains was achieved through PCR amplification followed by DNA sequencing, utilizing primers pLI50-check-F and pLI50-check-R (all primer sequences are provided in Table 2).

Groth curve measurement

Overnight bacterial cultures were adjusted to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.03. A 1:100 dilution of this culture was inoculated into 100 mL of Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) in conical flasks. Cultures were incubated with shaking (200 rpm) at 37 °C, with OD600 measurements recorded at 2-h intervals. All experiments involved three independent biological replicates to ensure reproducibility.

Analysis of biofilm formation ability

Biofilm formation was quantified using a modified crystal violet assay, adapted from our previous methodology (H. Pei et al., 2023). Briefly, late-exponential-phase bacterial suspensions were diluted 50-fold in fresh Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) supplemented with 1% (w/v) glucose. Aliquots (200 μL) of these diluted suspensions were transferred into sterile 96-well microtiter plates and incubated statically for 24 h at 37 °C. Following incubation, planktonic cells and culture medium were carefully removed. Adherent biofilms were fixed and stained with 0.1% (w/v) crystal violet solution for 15 min. Subsequently, unbound dye and non-adherent cells were thoroughly rinsed away using phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). To solubilize the biofilm-associated crystal violet, 200 μL of 33% (v/v) glacial acetic acid was added to each well. The absorbance of the solubilized dye solution was then measured at 492 nm using a Micro ELISA auto-reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Absorbance values from three technical replicate wells were averaged for each experimental condition. The entire experiment was performed in triplicate, representing three independent biological repetitions.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis

Following 24 h of biofilm growth, samples were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and subsequently fixed overnight (12 h) at 4 °C using 2.5% (v/v) glutaraldehyde in PBS. Fixed specimens were then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, 100%). Dehydration was followed by critical point drying using liquid CO2. Finally, dried samples were sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold-palladium prior to microscopic examination.

Survival under environmental stress

Staphylococcus aureus strains were initially cultured in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) for 16 h at 37 °C. Primary cultures were then diluted in fresh TSB to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.03. These diluted cultures were incubated statically at 37 °C until reaching late-exponential phase prior to stress exposure.

Heat stress: Aliquots (200 μL) of late-exponential phase cell suspensions were dispensed into a 48-well plate and exposed to 63 °C for 30 min or 60 min.

Drying Stress: Cell suspensions (200 μL) were transferred to a 48-well plate and subjected to controlled desiccation (25% ± 5% relative humidity; water activity: 0.38075 ± 0.01176) within a drying oven at 37 °C. Samples were collected after 5 and 10 days.

Oxidative Stress (H2O2): Cell pellets were harvested by centrifugation (details implied, e.g., 10,000×g, 5 min), washed once with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and resuspended in 5% (v/v) hydrogen peroxide. Exposure durations were 30 and 60 min at ambient temperature.

For salt stress, late-exponential phase cell suspensions (1 mL) were inoculated into 100 mL of TSB supplemented with 10% (w/v) NaCl or standard TSB (control). Cultures were incubated in shaker (200 rpm) at 37 °C. OD600 measurements were recorded hourly using a spectrophotometer. Growth curves were generated using GraphPad Prism 9.

Glycopeptide antibiotics resistence

These experiments followed the standards of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute. The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of antibiotics were determined via the microbroth dilution method as described previously (Yu et al., 2020). Overnight bacterial cultures were inoculated into Müller-Hinton broth (MH) supplemented with 24 μg/mL teicoplanin or vancomycin to an OD600 of 0.03, followed by serial two-fold dilution in antibiotic-free MH using Eppendorf tubes. All suspensions were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and the MIC was defined as the lowest antibiotic concentration that completely inhibited bacterial growth.

Vancomycin tolerance was assessed using both solid and liquid media supplemented with varying vancomycin or teicoplanin concentrations. Single colonies were inoculated into MH and incubated with shaking (200 rpm) at 37 °C for 12 h to reach mid-exponential phase (OD600 ≈ 1.0). Bacterial suspensions were serially diluted (10-fold increments) in sterile physiological saline. Aliquots (2 μL) of each dilution were spotted onto MH agar plates containing vancomycin (1.0 μg/mL or 2.0 μg/mL) or teicoplanin (4.0 μg/mL or 6.0 μg/mL). Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 16 h to evaluate growth inhibition. All assays were performed in triplicate and independently repeated in three separate experimental runs.

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) assays were performed according to established methods (Hao Pei et al., 2024). Bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation. Cell pellets were resuspended in RNase-free water supplemented with lysozyme (10 mg/mL) and lysostaphin (40 μg/mL) (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h to facilitate lysis. Total RNA was extracted from lysed cells using the Spin Column Bacteria Total RNA Purification Kit (Sangon Biotech). Genomic DNA removal and cDNA synthesis were performed in one step using the EasyScript One-Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). qPCR amplification was conducted using the TransStart Tip Green qPCR SuperMix (TransGen Biotech). The hu gene served as the endogenous reference (Valihrach and Demnerova, 2012). Primer sequences are provided in Table 2. All reactions included technical triplicates and appropriate negative controls.

Cloning, expression and purification of His6-TcaR

The DNA fregment of tcaR was amplified from RMSA49 genomic using the primers NcoⅠ-tcaR-f/XhoⅠ-tcaR-r. The plasmid pET28a and tcaR DNA fragment were digested with NcoⅠ and XhoⅠ. Then the products were ligated with T4 ligase (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to obtain pET28a-tcaR. Primers T7-f/T7-r were used to verify the plasmid pET28a-tcaR by PCR and sequencing analysis. Finally, the constructed plasmid was transformed into E. coli BL21 cells. E. coli BL21 containing the plasmid pET28a-tcaR was cultured for 12 h. Then 1 ml of the bacterial suspension was added to a fresh 100 ml of TSB and incubated at 37 °C for 3 h to make the OD600 become 0.5. After that 0.5 mM IPTG was added and incubated at 16 °C for 16 h in a shaker. The cells were disrupted using ultrasonic homogenizer, and the TcaR protein with His6 tag was adsorbed onto an affinity chromatography column. Then, different concentrations of imidazole buffer were used to elute the TcaR protein.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)

The concentration of purified TcaR proteins used in this assay were 20 μM. Primers pctsR-f/pctsR-r were used to amplify from the genomic DNA of the RMSA49 to get the probe. Binding assays were conducted by incubating fluorescently labeled DNA fragments with increasing concentrations of purified TcaR protein at 25 °C for 25 min in a 20 μL reaction mixture containing assay buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 3 mM magnesium acetate, 0.1 mM EDTA, and 0.1 mM DTT). Following incubation, 5 μl of loading dye (0.25 × TBE, 60% glycerol, 0.2% bromophenol blue) was added to each sample. The complexes were resolved by electrophoresis on a 6% non-denaturing polyacrylamide gel using 0.5 × TBE running buffer (45 mM Tris-borate, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0). Then the polyacrylamide gel was immersed in a 0.5 × TBE TBE buffer containing nucleic acid dye for 15 min. Finally, shift bands were detected with BIO RAD ChemiDoc All-in-One Imaging System (Bio-Rad, California, America). Previous studies have reported that TcaR can directly bind to the promoter of icaA, so the promoter of icaA was used as a positive control (Jefferson et al., 2004). A non-specific DNA fragment from the hu coding region was used as a negative control to confirm the specificity of TcaR binding to the target ctsR promoter. Software ImageJ was used to analyze the binding ability of TcaR to the probe.

Statistical analysis

The data analysis was conducted utilizing GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0.1.244, GraphPad Software Inc., USA) in conjunction with R Studio. To compare the experimental groups, we applied the Student's t-test, and the threshold for statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Deletion of ctsR did not affect the growth of S. aureus RMSA49

To rule out growth-related confounding effects, we compared the growth curves of WT, ΔctsR-pLI50, and ΔctsR-com in TSB at 37 °C. All strains exhibited nearly identical growth kinetics over 24 h (Fig. 2), indicating that ctsR deletion does not impact S. aureus growth under standard culture conditions.

The growth curves of WT, ΔctsR-pLI50 and ΔctsR-com in TSB at 37 °C.

Mutation of ctsR lead to obvious decrease in the environmental stress resistance of S. aureus RMSA49

As a global regulator of protein quality network in Gram-positive bacteria, CtsR plays an important role in the resistance of bacteria to the external environment (A. K. Elsholz et al., 2010). To explore the role of ctsR in the protection against environmental stress in S. aureus, we simulated various environmental stress treatments, including heat, desiccation, oxidative and salt stress. Under oxidative stress (5% H2O2), ΔctsR-pLI50 showed significantly lower survival rates than WT and ΔctsR-com at 30 and 60 min (Fig. 3A and B). Heat stress (63 °C) also impaired ΔctsR-pLI50 survival: after 30 min and 60 min, its survival rate was 10-fold lower than WT and ΔctsR-com (Fig. 3C and D). Similarly, desiccation stress reduced ΔctsR-pLI50 survival by 30% compared to WT after 5 d, with further reduction at 10 d (Fig. 3E and F). Under high-salt stress (10% NaCl), ΔctsR-pLI50 exhibited delayed growth and lower final OD600 compared to the other two strains (Fig. 3G). Collectively, these results demonstrate that CtsR is essential for S. aureus tolerance to multiple environmental stresses.

Deletion of ctsR resulted in a significant reduction in the biofilm formation ability of S. aureus RMSA49

Biofilm formation is a crucial survival strategy for S. aureus, providing multifaceted protection against adverse environmental conditions. We employed the crystal violet staining method to analyze and compare the biofilm-forming capabilities of the knockout strain ΔctsR-pLI50, the wild-type strain (WT), and the complementary strain ΔctsR-com. The results revealed that biofilm formation of ΔctsR-pLI50 was significantly reduced compared to that wild-type and complementary strains (Fig. 4A and B). To further investigate the impact of ctsR deletion on biofilm formation, we performed observations using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The SEM results demonstrated that, unlike the WT, ΔctsR-pLI50 was unable to form dense biofilms and exhibited a markedly thinner and more dispersed biofilm structure (Fig. 4C). These results demonstrated that the deletion of the ctsR gene leads to the inability of S. aureus to form biofilm normally.

CtsR affects the resistance of S. aureus to glycopeptide antibiotics

Glycopeptide antibiotics (e.g., vancomycin and teicoplanin) play a pivotal role in the treatment of severe infections caused by methicillin-resistant S. aureus, such as bloodstream infections and endocarditis, by inhibiting cell wall synthesis (Martin et al., 2010). In order to investigate the effect of ctsR on the tolerance to vancomycin or teicoplanin, we analyzed and compared the MIC of WT, ΔctsR-pLI50 and ΔctsR-com. As show in Table 3, the MIC of ctsR deletion mutant ΔctsR-pLI50 was lower than WT and ΔctsR-com. Furthermore, we transfer the 2 μL bacterial cultures to the similar OD600 onto the MH agar plates containing vancomycin or teicoplanin to test the sensitivity of S. aureus to glycopeptide antibiotics. The sensitivity of ΔctsR-pLI50 was obviously higher compared to WT and ΔctsR-com (Fig. 5). These results indicated that the mutation of ctsR significantly reduce the tolerance of S. aureus to glycopeptide antibiotics.

The gene ctsR expression is directly regulated by transcriptional regulator TcaR

In our previous study, the MarR family transcriptional regulator TcaR was found to play a critical role in modulating environmental tolerance and vancomycin susceptibility in S. aureus RMSA49. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that the deletion of tcaR significantly upregulates the expression of ctsR (Hao Pei et al., 2024). This observation was further confirmed by independent qPCR assays, suggesting that TcaR regulates the transcription of ctsR (Fig. 6A). To investigate whether this regulation is direct, we purified TcaR protein using a histidine tag and performed a gel shift assay with a DNA probe covering the promoter region of ctsR. To assess binding specificity, a DNA fragment derived from the coding region of the housekeeping gene hu was employed as a non-specific DNA to TcaR in the EMSA assays (Fig. 6B).The promoter region of icaA, a known target of TcaR (Jefferson et al., 2004), was used as a positive control to confirm the DNA-binding activity of the purified TcaR protein (Fig. 6C). As shown in Fig. 6D, incubation of the ctsR promoter probe with increasing concentrations of purified TcaR protein resulted in the appearance of shifted bands with reduced electrophoretic mobility. Moreover, the binding affinity of TcaR protein to ctsR is higher than that of icaA protein (Fig. 6E and F).The intensity of these shifted bands increased progressively with higher protein concentrations. Collectively, these results demonstrate that TcaR binds directly and specifically to the promoter region of ctsR, establishing a direct TcaR-CtsR regulatory cascade in S. aureus.

Discussion

CtsR is established as a critical transcriptional repressor governing the heat shock response in Gram-positive bacteria, primarily through its regulation of genes involved in intracellular protein quality control, such as ATP-dependent proteases (e.g., ClpP, ClpC) and molecular chaperones (Chastanet et al., 2003). However, its functional role in Staphylococcus aureus, particularly concerning multi-stress adaptation and virulence-related processes, has remained incompletely understood. In this study, using a foodborne S. aureus strain RMSA49, we identified novel functions of CtsR in biofilm formation, glycopeptide antibiotic susceptibility, and environmental stress tolerance. Furthermore, we elucidated a previously uncharacterized regulatory axis wherein the transcriptional regulator TcaR directly controls ctsR expression, revealing an additional layer of complexity in the stress response network of this pathogen.

The remarkable resistence of S. aureus to environmental stresses, such as those encountered during dairy processing (e.g., high temperature, desiccation, oxidative and salt stress), underscores its sophisticated adaptive mechanisms. This adaptability is largely orchestrated by a network of transcriptional regulators, including the general stress sigma factor SigB, the redox-sensing regulator MgrA, and the heat shock repressor CtsR. SigB coordinates a broad regulon in response to diverse stimuli such as energy depletion, osmotic shock, and pH shifts, redirecting cellular transcription to promote survival (Eishi Miyazaki, Chen, Ko and Bishai William, 1999; Sihto et al., 2017). MgrA, modulated by post-translational modifications like oxidation and S-bacillithiolation, integrates oxidative stress signals with virulence and antibiotic resistance gene expression (Chen et al., 2006). Within this network, CtsR specifically responds to proteotoxic stress by derepressing genes essential for protein refolding and degradation (A. K. W. Elsholz et al., 2010). Our findings demonstrate that CtsR is indispensable for tolerance to heat, desiccation, oxidative, and salt stresses, positioning it as a central role in the environmental adaptability of S. aureus. In addition, a significant and previously unreported finding is the function of CtsR in biofilm formation. Biofilms are critical for bacterial persistence in food-processing environments, enhancing resistance to desiccation and sanitizers. Our results showed that ctsR deletion severely impairs biofilm development through crystal violet staining and scanning electron microscopy. This suggests that proper protein homeostasis, governed by CtsR, is essential for the structural integrity or developmental regulation of biofilms. This finding expands the functional scope of CtsR beyond cytoplasmic protein quality control to include a role in extracellular community behavior.

Glycopeptide antibiotics, such as vancomycin and teicoplanin, inhibit cell wall biosynthesis in S. aureus by binding to the D-alanyl-D-alanine (D-Ala-D-Ala) terminus of lipid II, a key peptidoglycan precursor (Binda et al., 2014). This binding prevents the transglycosylation and transpeptidation reactions essential for cross-linking the peptidoglycan layer, ultimately leading to cell lysis and death (Li et al., 2023). These agents remain crucial for treating methicillin-resistant MRSA infections. Equally notable is our discovery that CtsR influences susceptibility to glycopeptide antibiotics. While CtsR's role in heat shock is well-documented, no prior evidence linked it to antibiotic resistance. We found that ctsR inactivation significantly increases susceptibility to vancomycin and teicoplanin. This suggests a intriguing connection might exist between the protein-folding stress response and cell wall biosynthesis or integrity, possibly through indirect effects on the cell wall stress response or autolytic activity. This newly uncovered role provides a fresh perspective on the interplay between core cellular homeostasis systems and antibiotic efficacy. However, further experimental results are needed to confirm the molecular regulatory mechanism.

In our previous study, we found that TcaR functions as a critical negative regulator of environmental stress tolerance in S. aureus. Deletion of tcaR significantly enhances bacterial survival under heat, desiccation, and oxidative stress. Moreover, transcriptome sequencing analysis showed that the transcription level of ctsR was significantly increased under the condition of TcaR inactivation (Hao Pei et al., 2024). These results suggested that TcaR may regulate the transcription of ctsR in response to the environmental stress. In this study, we confirmed that TcaR binds directly and specifically to the ctsR promoter through EMSA, and genetic complementation experiments further demonstrated that TcaR represses ctsR transcription. These combined genetic and biochemical data establish a direct TcaR-CtsR regulatory cascade, wherein TcaR functions as an upstream repressor of the central stress regulator CtsR. We propose a model wherein external stress signals are perceived and transduced through TcaR, which then fine-tunes the expression of the central proteotoxic stress regulator CtsR (Fig. 7). This regulatory cascade allows S. aureus to mount a rapid and tailored adaptive response to environmental challenges. However, the specific signal sensed by TcaR remains to be identified. As a MarR family regulator, TcaR likely responds to environmental or metabolic ligands to modulate its DNA-binding affinity. Given that TcaR inactivation enhances survival under multiple stress conditions, we hypothesize that TcaR may function as a multi-signal sensor that integrates diverse environmental cues to fine-tune CtsR-mediated stress response (Hao Pei et al., 2024). Identifying the TcaR ligand represents an important direction for future biochemical studies.

In conclusion, our study significantly expands the known functional landscape of CtsR in S. aureus, revealing its critical involvement in biofilm formation, glycopeptide antibiotic susceptibility, and multi-stress tolerance. The discovery of its direct regulation by TcaR unveils a novel regulatory axis within the stress response network. These findings enhance our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying the persistence and resistance of S. aureus in challenging environments, offering potential new targets for strategies aimed at controlling this pathogen in both food safety and clinical contexts.

Conclusion

This study elucidates the multifaceted role of CtsR in foodborne S. aureus RMSA49, extending its known function beyond heat shock response to include biofilm formation, glycopeptide antibiotic susceptibility, and tolerance to heat, desiccation, oxidative, and salt stresses. CtsR deletion impairs biofilm structure, increases vancomycin/teicoplanin sensitivity, and reduces stress survival—highlighting its central role in environmental adaptation. Critically, through qPCR analysis in tcaR deletion and complementation strains combined with EMSA demonstrating direct and specific binding of TcaR to the ctsR promoter, we establish TcaR as a direct transcriptional repressor of ctsR. These findings reveal a novel TcaR-CtsR regulatory cascade that modulates stress response and biofilm formation in S. aureus.
