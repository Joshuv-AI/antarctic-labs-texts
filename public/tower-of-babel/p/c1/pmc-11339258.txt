Invalidation by medical professionals in post-treatment Lyme disease
Authors: Alison W Rebman, Ting Yang, John N Aucott
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients with post-treatment Lyme disease (PTLD) report negative perceptions of care and significant invalidation from medical professionals. However, the relationship of invalidation to illness severity has not been examined, nor have risk factors for invalidation been identified. This cross-sectional study enrolled 80 patients who met stringent criteria for PTLD. We examined correlations between the Illness Invalidation Inventory and measures of symptom severity, quality of life, and trust in physicians. To study the relationship between invalidation and potential demographic and clinical factors, we generated simple unadjusted and multivariate adjusted linear regression models. We found that higher ‘lack of understanding’ and ‘discounting’ subscale scores of the Illness Invalidation Inventory were significantly positively correlated with higher symptom severity, lower quality of life, and lower trust in physicians. In adjusted linear regression models, older age (lack of understanding: β = − 0.17, p = 0.008, discounting: β = − 0.19, p = 0.001, every 10 years) and male gender (lack of understanding: β = − 0.49, p = 0.016, discounting: β = − 0.51, p = 0.006) were associated with less invalidation. We also identified receiving an alternative diagnosis for PTLD as a mediator in the relationship between gender and invalidation. Based on our findings, we hypothesize that reducing invalidation within the clinical encounter could positively affect illness burden and quality of life for patients with PTLD.Keywords: Lyme disease, Post-treatment Lyme disease, Invalidation, Gender, Quality-of-lifeSubject terms: Quality of life, Risk factors, Chronic pain

Introduction

Lyme disease is a common tick-borne disease in many geographic regions of the United States and Eurasia1. If not recognized and appropriately treated in the early stages of infection when a rash at the site of the tick bite and/or flu-like symptoms may appear, it can progress to later stages and present with cardiac, neurologic, and/or joint system abnormalities1. Additionally, a subset of untreated patients, as well as those appropriately treated with antibiotics, may continue to experience symptoms such as fatigue, musculoskeletal pain, cognitive difficulties, disrupted sleep, paresthesia, and/or mood disturbance2–4. These symptoms can be chronic in nature and may be sufficiently severe to disrupt daily function and quality of life2.

It is estimated that as many as 2 million people are living with persistent symptoms due to Lyme disease in the United States, a condition sometimes called chronic Lyme disease5. This patient population is clinically very heterogenous, and only some patients with such symptoms meet criteria for the specific research case definition of post-treatment Lyme disease (PTLD)4. There is significant overlap in the symptom profile and social context of PTLD and other infection-associated or otherwise unexplained chronic illnesses, including fibromyalgia, myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS), and post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 or long COVID6. An uncertain underlying etiology and ill-defined treatment paradigms have contributed to a long-standing and polarizing medical debate regarding many aspects of PTLD7, and led to a contested illness experience for many patients8–10.

Furthermore, the most common symptoms of PTLD are invisible to others, which can contribute to invalidation from those in patients’ social environment, including spouses, family, friends, colleagues, the health care system, and society in general10,11. The overall construct of invalidation can be defined as the process through which the legitimacy of another’s experience is called into question.11,12Indeed, although not necessarily described as invalidation, prior qualitative studies have found that patients with PTLD frequently report negative experiences within the healthcare system, with many describing dismissive or condescending encounters13–15.

Despite the seemingly high prevalence of the negative perceptions of care expressed by patients with PTLD, few studies have sought to quantify the frequency or impact of these experiences. More broadly, a recent scoping review found no studies with the primary objective of addressing patient-provider interactions in the setting of Lyme disease or PTLD16. There is a notable lack of knowledge in the literature regarding the nature of patient-provider interactions in this setting and the impact of these interactions on patient outcomes and subsequent quality of life.

Given this gap, we sought to quantitatively examine perceived invalidation from medical professionals among a sample of patients with well-documented PTLD. First, we assessed the validity of the Illness Invalidation Inventory (3*I)12 in our sample population. This measure has been administered to individuals with fibromyalgia and other rheumatic diseases17, ME/CFS18, and chronic low back pain19. We then explored associations of invalidation with other measures of symptom severity, quality of life, and trust in physicians. Finally, in adjusted analyses we sought to identify clinical and demographic factors associated with a higher risk of invalidation. In all analyses, we were particularly interested in the potential effect of gender on these relationships, based on well-described implicit gender biases and historical tendencies to minimize women’s symptoms, such as pain20–22.

Methods

Patient sample

In the current study, we recruited from those who had participated in a prior study of PTLD and gave written informed consent for future research contact. This sampling frame included patients initially drawn largely from an academic clinic-based population who met criteria for PTLD based on extensive screening2. Briefly, this included medical record review to confirm prior Lyme disease, as well as self-report of current PTLD-defining symptoms impacting daily functioning, and no prior diagnoses named in the IDSA’s proposed case definition23 as exclusionary. These former participants were sent a mailing with study contact information, and those who expressed interest were screened to ensure they still met criteria for PTLD. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine, and have therefore been performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. Participants provided verbal informed consent prior to any study activities being performed.

Self-administered questionnaires

Eligible participants were emailed a link to complete a series of self-administered questionnaires (described below), as well as demographic information, and self-report of alternative diagnoses both prior to and following initial diagnosis and treatment of their Lyme disease. Specific alternative diagnoses were elicited via text field and were later grouped by a physician (JA) into the following: another contested illness, a psychological illness, and an uncontested condition. Participants were also asked to respond to four stand-alone questions to assess the frequency of specific types of negative health care experiences. These questions were developed by the authors based on experiences commonly described in prior qualitative studies13,14. Finally, information on participants’ initial Lyme disease presentation and duration of their illness was abstracted from their medical record as part of their prior study participation.

Our primary measure of interest was the Illness Invalidation Inventory (3*I) developed by Kool et al., who defined invalidation as both actively negative responses from others (such as denying or patronizing) as well as the absence of positive social responses, such as support or acknowledgement.11,12 The 3*I is an 8-item measure of perceived invalidation in which participants indicate on a 5-point Likert scale how frequently (from ‘never’ = 1 to ‘very often’ = 5) specific individuals have responded to them in the described way for each item12. We chose to elicit responses regarding experiences with medical professionals, although the 3*I can be administered to understand invalidation from other sources as well. It has shown strong measurement invariance across rheumatic diseases and various language versions, including English17. A two-factor model (‘discounting’ and ‘lack of understanding’) has been previously identified and confirmed in other sample populations17,19. Discounting experiences are described as denying, disbelieving, rejecting, or patronizing as a result of a patient’s illness, whereas lack of understanding is the absence of acknowledgement, comprehension, or emotional support surrounding a patient’s illness12,24.

Additional questionnaires included the Short-Form Health Survey (SF-36) version 225, a 36-item measure of health-related quality of life with physical (PCS) and mental (MCS) component scores, which are calculated as weighted sums of eight standardized scale scores. Cronbach’s alpha on all eight scales of the SF-36 were good to excellent, exceeding 0.81 for each. The Post-Lyme Questionnaire of Symptoms (PLQS) is a 36-symptom list developed based on prior clinical and research experience among patients with PTLD and the Cronbach’s alpha was 0.898. A total score indicative of current symptom severity over the past 2 weeks was created by summing responses to each of the 36 self-reported symptoms (‘none’ = 0, ‘mild’ = 1, ‘moderate’ = 2, ‘severe’ = 3) for a range of 0 to 108. Participants also completed an 11-item scale developed by Hall et al. which measures general trust in physicians (TIP) wherein a higher score indicates more trust26. Participants indicated on a 5-point Likert scale their level of agreement (from 1 = ‘totally disagree’ to 5 = ‘totally agree’). The Cronbach’s alpha for the TIP was 0.943.

Statistical methods

To examine the factor structure of the 3*I in our sample, we first used the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s test of sphericity to assess the data’s suitability for factor analysis. We performed exploratory factor analysis (EFA) with both Polychoric and Pearson’s correlation coefficients and oblique rotation to allow for correlations between extracted factors. The number of retained factors was informed by the visual scree test and parallel analysis. We used a factor loading cut-off value of 0.327.

We calculated spearman correlation coefficients to evaluate the relationships between 3*I scores and the other measures described, including the SF-36 PCS, MCS, PLQS, and TIP. We then calculated correlation coefficients between 3*I scores and those symptoms listed on the PLQS which were present for ≥ 10% of our sample at the moderate or severe level. As symptom scores on the PLQS are ordinal, Kendall’s tau correlation was used for this analysis. Sub-scale scores following EFA were calculated as unweighted mean scores for a more straight-forward interpretation.

Finally, to study the relationship between invalidation and potential demographic and clinical factors, we generated simple unadjusted and multivariate adjusted linear regression models with 3*I scores as the outcomes, and various demographic and clinical variables as predictors. Robust standard errors were used in all models. Predictors with p < 0.15 in the unadjusted analysis were included in final multiple regression models. To explore how the experience of invalidation may change over time, we examined the relationship between 3*I scores and illness duration using linear and simple linear spline models. In addition, as our regression analyses highlighted the collinearity of gender and receiving an alternative diagnosis during the course of PTLD, we conducted a mediation analysis of this factor as a mediator in the relationship between 3*I and gender using the R mediation package28.

All statistical analyses were performed in SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) or R version 4.2.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

We achieved a response rate of 99/280 (35.4%) to our recruitment mailings and found that 83.8% of those who responded still met criteria for PTLD a median of 2.6 years after their initial participation in our studies. A total of 80 participants with complete data were included in the final sample. Their demographic and clinical Lyme disease characteristics are shown in Table 1. Our sample was a slight majority men (55.0%), and had high reported income and education levels. Before their initial diagnosis of Lyme disease, approximately half (51.3%) had first been told that their symptoms represented another illness or condition. This high rate is consistent with the hypothesis that diagnosis and treatment delays, and possibly exposure to inappropriate treatment, may be risk factors for PTLD4. There were no statistically significant differences in the demographic characteristics shown in Table 1 between men and women.

A two-factor model structure with Pearson’s correlation was identified by parallel analysis and provided the best trade-off between model fit and model complexity according to the Bayesian information criterion (BIC), with satisfactory fit estimates. The root mean square of the residuals was 0.04, the root mean square error of approximation index was 0.096, and the Tucker Lewis Index of factoring reliability was 0.939.

Invalidation and symptom severity

﻿Fig. 2 shows the average 3*I-LOU, 3*I-DIS, PLQS, SF-36 PCS, SF-36 MCS, and TIP scores in the sample as a whole, as well as the magnitude and statistical significance of the correlations between them. Thirty-nine/80 (48.75%) experienced lack of understanding ‘sometimes’, ‘often’ or ‘very often’ from medical professionals, whereas 23/80 (28.75%) experienced discounting at the same frequency. Both 3*I subscales were significantly correlated with all measures, with the exception of the SF-36 PCS and the 3*I LOU (Fig. 2). Across most measures, ‘discounting’ had similar or stronger correlations and greater statistical significance than ‘lack of understanding’. Participants were also asked the following four questions: if, from when they were diagnosed and treated for Lyme disease to now, they felt that a physician or other health care provider: rejected them (43/80, 53.75%), doubted the severity of their illness (48/80, 60.00%), doubted their illness was real (41/80, 51.25%), and told them their illness was due only or in large part to psychological causes, such as anxiety or depression (26/80, 32.5%). For all but one of these statements, they were endorsed at significantly higher rates among women compared to men (rejected: 69.4% vs. 40.9%, p = 0.011; doubted severity: 72.2% vs. 50.0%, p = 0.044; doubted real: 61.1% vs. 43.2%, p = 0.111; psychological causes: 47.2% vs. 20.5%, p = 0.011).

Factors related to invalidation

Table 3 shows the results of unadjusted and adjusted linear regression analyses with 3*I scores as the outcomes. Unadjusted analyses identified age, gender, race/ethnicity, and illness duration as important variables associated with 3*I lack of understanding and/or discounting. In adjusted models, older age and male gender were associated with significantly lower 3*I lack of understanding and discounting scores. In addition, patients who self-identified as non-Hispanic white had higher lack of understanding scores. For discounting, a simple linear spline model, with an optimal knot value of 2 years selected based on Akaike information criterion (AIC), outperformed the linear model for illness duration. Discounting scores increased along with increasing illness duration to the peak of 2 years, after which point, we found no significant association between these two variables. No such non-linear relationship was found for lack of understanding.

We found that women were significantly more likely to report receiving an alternative diagnosis for their symptoms over the course of their PTLD (14/36 [38.9%] vs. 5/44 [11.4%], odds ratio = 4.86, p = 0.007) despite no statistically significant difference in misdiagnosis of their initial Lyme disease prior to first treatment (21/36 [58.3%] vs. 20/44 [45.5%], odds ratio = 1.67, p = 0.271). Among the 19 participants who received an alternative diagnosis for PTLD, women were more likely to report another contested illness (20.0% men vs. 57.1% women, p = 0.153) and/or a psychological illness (20.0% men vs. 28.5% women, p = 0.709) diagnosis, whereas men were more likely to report only an uncontested diagnosis (60.0% men vs. 28.6% women, p = 0.211), however these were not statistically significant.

We then tested whether receiving an alternative diagnosis for PTLD serves as a mediator in the pathway between gender and 3*I subscale scores. The data were supportive that women were more likely to receive an alternative diagnosis during the course of their PTLD, which in turn, was associated with more lack of understanding (indirect effect [regression coefficient]: -0.17, p = 0.010; direct effect: -0.32, p = 0.144), and discounting (indirect effect: -0.18, p = 0.008; direct effect: -0.37, p = 0.038). The proportion of the gender difference in invalidation which was mediated by an alternative diagnosis was (-0.17/-0.49) = 0.35 (p = 0.036) for lack of understanding and (-0.18/-0.55) = 0.33 (p = 0.014) for discounting. However, additional factors likely play a role as well, as an alternative diagnosis for PTLD did not fully explain this relationship. There were no mediating effects found when the same analysis was performed with the early initial misdiagnosis variable instead (data not shown).

Discussion

In this study of patients with well-characterized and established PTLD, we found that discounting and lack of understanding by medical professionals is commonly reported. The median 3*I lack of understanding and discounting scores in our sample were somewhat lower (indicating more understanding and less discounting) than an online sample of patients with ME/CFS18, but similar to or higher than those described in recent samples of patients with fibromyalgia, which were in turn higher than those with rheumatoid arthritis12,29. Although common, the experience of discounting and lack of understanding was not universal, with 48.8% of patients reporting mean scores in the ‘never’ or ‘seldom’ range (≤ 2.5 for both).12 This suggests a heterogeneity of experience useful to future qualitative and quantitative studies seeking to understand the nature and impact of perceived invalidation.

Notably, we also found weak-to-moderate correlations between invalidation sub-scale scores and measures of physical and mental quality of life as well as current symptom severity. This relationship was generally stronger for the ‘discounting’ sub-scale. This is consistent with prior studies among patients with fibromyalgia12,24,29, however the reasons for the reproducible association between discounting experiences and worse physical health are unknown. Other authors have detailed how significant invalidating experiences may trigger emergent physiological effects24,30. Support for this explanation comes from neuroimaging and other studies suggesting that physical and social pain (e.g. invalidation) occupy overlapping neural regions, with subsequent common neurocognitive substrates and shared sensitivity to both kinds of pain31. Adverse social interactions have also been shown to affect immune processes32,33, which may contribute to the development and maintenance of PTLD4.

Perhaps the most intuitive consequence of significant or recurrent invalidation is the potential effect on mental health, particularly for those patients who may already be at risk. A prior diagnosis of major depression or another significant psychiatric condition was exclusionary in our study. Nevertheless, we found a weak but significant association between both 3*I sub-scale scores and the SF-36 MCS. Furthermore, while anxiety, irritability, and depression are not among the most common symptoms reported in our sample (reported by 33%, 23%, and 15% respectively, whereas fatigue and pain are reported by 60–70%) they had some of the strongest, most statistically significant correlations with discounting.

Several prior qualitative and quantitative studies support an association between invalidation and various aspects of mental health34–37. In a recent study by Boring et al., the relationship between pain invalidation and depression was found to be fully mediated by a measure of internalized shame37. The authors hypothesize that invalidation communicates a lack of worthiness, which if internalized, may contribute to or worsen depression. This is similar to another recent study by Bontempo, in which invalidation, particularly when personalized, was predictive of both depression and self-esteem, which was found to fully mediate the effect of personalized invalidation on depression36. Likewise, invalidation may also heighten feelings of loneliness, an association found among patients with rheumatic diseases, particularly fibromyalgia38. Aside from the clear relationship between mental health and quality of life, conditions such as depression have been found to negatively affect the chronicity of symptoms (specifically, pain39,40).

As has been noted by other authors, invalidation may have a significant role to play in complicating illness trajectories and prolonging paths to healing.30,37 Fricker’s concept of epistemic injustice argues that a specific type of harm is done to individuals when their capacity as a possessor of knowledge is questioned, treated unfairly, or given less credence than deserved41. This framework has been applied to the healthcare setting as well, and in particular, discussed in the context of ME/CFS42. The authors argue that patients with contested illnesses may be particularly vulnerable to this type of harm, with significant ethical and practical consequences for both patients and medical professionals.

It should be noted that our cross-sectional study design could not address the directionality of the associations we observed between invalidation and higher symptom severity. While we have hypothesized that invalidation likely impacts mood and physical functioning, it is also possible that patients who report a higher number of symptoms may subsequently be at higher risk for invalidation as a result of their disease severity. Similarly, having diminished pre-morbid mental health may make an individual more likely to perceive and internalize interactions as invalidating. This process may also be affected by patients’ relationship to medical professionals and interactions with the health care system prior to developing Lyme disease. It will be difficult for future studies to assess the directionality of these relationships without pre-morbid and/or serial measurements to assess change over time.

In our analyses, we also aimed to identify factors that may increase risk for discounting and lack of understanding. We were surprised that demographic variables such as gender, age, and race/ethnicity were independently significant in adjusted models but that clinical factors related to initial Lyme disease presentation were not. Specifically, after controlling for other factors, presenting with the erythema migrans rash of early Lyme disease (the most distinctive and easily diagnosed presentation of acute infection) was not significantly associated with lower discounting or lack of understanding scores. Similarly, presenting only with non-specific symptoms and a positive test for Lyme disease was not significantly associated with higher discounting or lack of understanding scores. We would have predicted that patients presenting without outwardly visible, objective signs of infection would have experienced greater invalidation, as they are often more challenging to diagnose. Our findings imply that common, implicit, demographic biases are prevalent in this disease setting as well. It also suggests that medical professionals are not seeking out or relying upon objective findings from patients’ Lyme disease onset in order to validate the PTLD diagnosis itself.

Among demographic factors, we found a strongly significant relationship between younger age and higher discounting and lack of understanding. We hypothesize that this could result from medical professionals’ assumptions that illness should be less severe among younger patients. Additionally, how invalidation is experienced and internalized may be more significant for younger patients, especially if this represents their first substantial or prolonged interaction with the health care system. Interestingly, race/ethnicity was highly significantly associated with lack of understanding but not discounting scores. This is an important area of research for future, larger studies to elucidate, as our findings in this area are limited by the diversity of our sample. Finally, we hypothesized that women would report more invalidation than men, and this association was found in our adjusted models for both lack of understanding and discounting.

We also found that receiving an alternative diagnosis for symptoms (which was reported more frequently by women than men in our sample) was a significant mediator in the pathway between gender and invalidation. However, these findings only related to alternative diagnoses given in the post-treatment PTLD period, not during initial Lyme disease diagnosis. This suggests that one potential basis for the degree of invalidation reported by women is the more frequent attribution of their post-treatment symptoms, such as fatigue, pain, and cognitive dysfunction, to other conditions, particularly if those other conditions are also contested or psychological in nature. Future qualitative studies are needed to provide additional insight into this relationship. It also highlights that physician’s responses to persistent symptoms, rather than those in the initial Lyme disease diagnosis encounter, are likely the most consequential in driving perceived invalidation. Interestingly, we also found that discounting scores worsened every year for the first two years then afterwards remained stable.

Perceived invalidation has been qualitatively identified as contributing to the often-fraught patient-provider relationship in PTLD10,13,15,16. In our study, both discounting and lack of understanding were moderate-to-strongly negatively correlated with a measure of overall trust in physicians, suggesting that greater invalidation is associated with lower levels of trust. It is important to note that while the 3*I measures invalidation among medical professionals, the TIP measures trust in physicians, a more specific subset with high perceived power, and this may have affected our results. Regardless, this trend is consistent with a previous study by Mechanic and Meyer comparing trust across patients with different significant illnesses14. In that study, physician rejection was mentioned almost exclusively by patients with chronic Lyme disease and they were twice as likely to report a loss of trust in their physician compared to patients with breast cancer or mental illness14. This loss of trust has implications for patient-provider care relationships at the individual level. Moreover, on a broader scale it likely contributes to the frequency with which patients with PTLD seek out alternative forms of healthcare16.

In order to sustainably improve the nature of the patient-provider relationship in PTLD, future studies that seek to better understand the perspective of medical professionals will also be needed. It is clear that without an established biomedical model for conceptualizing PTLD, the uncertainty surrounding diagnosis and treatment approaches presents a challenge for medical professionals as well16. As posited in the ME/CFS context, it is this uncertainty which implicitly affects the validity assigned by medical professionals to patient testimonies42. Consequently, barriers to empathic care will continue to arise from the lack of a shared understanding, or even an applied clinical case definition, for PTLD. In the meantime, limiting the degree to which patients with PTLD are subject to invalidating experiences in the healthcare encounter could potentially affect quality of life and coping skills. We suggest that this could be accomplished by first, recognizing that invalidation commonly exists for patients with this condition, and secondly, consciously de-coupling the genuine uncertainty that medical professionals feel regarding PTLD from the instinct to invalidate patient concerns. As suggested by Blease et al., medical education can contribute to this shift by naming and clearly addressing infection-associated or otherwise unexplained chronic illnesses42.

Our study has several limitations and leaves additional questions for future research. First, we had a relatively small sample size, which was a large proportion non-Hispanic, white, and had high income and education levels. Therefore, more diverse samples are needed to confirm and clarify our findings. It is also unknown if our final sample was biased as a result of who chose to respond to our recruitment mailing (those with more strikingly positive or negative interactions with the health care system). Additionally, it is also unknown whether or how more invalidation may alter symptom reporting patterns for some participants. This could result in patient over-reporting as a way of recognition seeking or under-reporting due to internalized shame regarding their illness37. Finally, participants were recruited from those seen at some point in their care for PTLD at an academic specialty referral clinic generally attune to issues of perceived invalidation and sensitive to their impact on patient care. Thus, our findings may underestimate the invalidation experienced by the broader population of patients with PTLD.

Conclusion

The pervasiveness of invalidation and its association with symptoms (in particular, mental health symptoms) in our sample of patients with well-characterized PTLD suggest that invalidation within the patient-provider relationship has the potential to exacerbate and complicate patients’ illness burden. This may be especially true for women and for younger patients who reported experiencing higher degrees of invalidation from medical professionals in our sample. It is known that improvements in patient-centered care and communication can lead to better health outcomes43. We would therefore argue for a reduction in invalidation and a model for understanding and alleviating PTLD that acknowledges and incorporates the patient illness experience and the effects of that experience on the healing process.
