Correlation between human expert macular fluid height assessment and fluid volume quantification in neovascular age-related macular degeneration
Authors: Stefan Steiner, Bianca S. Gerendas, Gabor Deak, Oliver Leingang, Ariadne Whitby, Hrvoje Bogunovic, Gregor S. Reiter, Ursula Schmidt-Erfurth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To investigate the association between manually measured retinal fluid heights and AI-quantified retinal fluid volumes and to explore disease activity indicated by fluid volume distributions in neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD) using an approved AI-based algorithm. This retrospective study analyzed baseline OCT data from a multicenter nAMD cohort. Manually measured maximum macular fluid heights on B-scans in the central millimeter (CMM) for intraretinal fluid (IRF), subretinal fluid (SRF), and pigment epithelial detachment (PED) were compared with vertical fluid heights and three-dimensional volumes obtained by automated quantification using an AI-based tool (RetInSight Fluid Monitor Version 2). Out of 890 eyes, IRF was identified in the CMM in 328 eyes, SRF in 502 eyes, and PED in 705 eyes. The correlation between manual and AI-based height measurements was strong for IRF (r = 0.87) and PED (r = 0.91), and moderate for SRF (r = 0.67). Manual height–AI volume correlations within the CMM were strong for IRF (r = 0.76) and PED (r = 0.87), and moderate for SRF (r = 0.55). These correlations decreased when total fluid volumes within the central 6 mm were compared with manual CMM height, indicating that CMM height does not represent total nAMD disease activity. In conclusion, AI-derived and manual height measurements showed strong agreement for IRF and PED and moderate agreement for SRF. Maximum fluid height did not reliably reflect overall fluid volume or spatial distribution. AI-based retinal fluid assessment enables quantitative, whole-volume evaluation of retinal fluid and provides a measure of nAMD disease activity.

Introduction

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a widespread and debilitating ocular condition primarily affecting the elderly, with a growing impact not only on patients but also on ophthalmologists, insurance companies, and healthcare providers because of increased life expectancy.1–3 Neovascular AMD (nAMD) is characterized by an increased release of pro-angiogenic factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), leading to macular neovascularization (MNV) and resulting in the accumulation of fluid in different retinal layers which causes vision impairment and distortion. The gold-standard for treating nAMD is the application of intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy, with the aim of reducing the amount and recurrence of retinal fluid and preventing irreversible scarring of the macula. The accumulation of intraretinal (IRF) and subretinal fluid (SRF) is the main driver in clinical decision-making regarding whether a patient needs anti-VEGF administration.4–6 Optical coherence tomography (OCT) enables high-resolution visualization of macular fluid compartments, including IRF, SRF, and pigment epithelial detachment (PED). To date, it has been time-consuming and challenging to quantify the amount of three-dimensional retinal fluid present in entire volumes rather than in individual B-scans. Manual reading center measurements (e.g., central retinal thickness, central subfield thickness, and center point thickness) have historically been used as surrogate markers for the accumulation of retinal fluid, disease activity and were the basis for dosing regimens in clinical trials.7–9 These quantitative indicators were introduced because time-domain OCT lacked the raster scanning capability of modern OCT technology and was therefore not able to perform fluid quantification throughout the entire volume. In addition, available evidence suggests that these semi-quantitative measurements are poor indicators of total disease activity.10,11

Artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms can precisely identify and segment individual fluid compartments in nAMD from entire OCT volumes in a fraction of time compared to a human manually counting fluid associated pixels across a large number of B-scans, allowing for the detection and quantification of nAMD-related exudation.12 Visualization and quantification of AI-based features support ophthalmologists in detecting and monitoring retinal fluid over time, enabling personalized treatment and efficient therapeutic interventions. By automating retinal segmentation, AI reduces the burden on healthcare professionals, enhances diagnostic precision, and improves workflows and potentially enhancing patient care and visual outcomes in patients with nAMD.

The purpose of this study is to investigate the correlation between manually measured retinal fluid via conventional assessment of B-scan-related heights by certified reading center experts and fully automated AI-based quantification of retinal fluid volumes, as well as to identify the distribution of macular fluid pooling in active nAMD and disease activity using an approved and validated AI-based deep-learning algorithm.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study adhered to the ethical guidelines outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Conference of Harmonization of Good Clinical Practice Guidelines The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board, with the most recent renewal dated 5 July 2024 (Approval No. 1246/2016). The study included baseline visits of nAMD patients from multicenter study data obtained from the Vienna Reading Center (VRC). The inclusion and exclusion criteria were similar across the studies, meaning that only treatment-naive patients with MNV secondary to AMD, without any other vision-impairing ocular conditions, were included. Heidelberg Spectralis OCT-Volume scans with either 49 or 97 B-scans were segmented using the automated fluid segmentation method described below, and manually graded by expert readers.

Human expert grading

Manually measured parameters were obtained by certified readers at the VRC under the supervision of a senior reader. Data were extracted from trial datasets of large randomized controlled trials investigating anti-VEGF agents. An ETDRS grid centered on the fovea was applied, and the presence of SRF, IRF, and PED was assessed within the CMM. For each parameter, manual measurements were performed on the B-scan showing the maximum vertical extension within the CMM using an in-house–developed reading tool (OCTAVO)13,14.

PED was defined as the space between Bruch’s membrane and the outer boundary of the RPE)14, including all lesion sizes and excluding subretinal hyperreflective material or other hyperreflective tissue on OCT. IRF was defined as hyporeflective, cystoid-filled spaces within the retinal layers. SRF was defined as hyporeflective fluid accumulation between the photoreceptor outer segments and the inner boundary of the RPE.

Deep learning-based automated fluid segmentation

A validated deep learning–based algorithm (RetInSight Fluid Monitor, Version 2; Vienna, Austria), CE-marked under MDR EU 2017/745, was used for automated retinal fluid segmentation7,12. The algorithm is based on a semantic segmentation approach using convolutional neural networks and was trained on OCT volumes acquired with Heidelberg Spectralis systems. The analyses were performed independently by the study investigators, and no company support was provided for running the analyses.

The network follows an encoder–decoder architecture, in which the encoder learns abstract feature representations directly from annotated OCT images and the decoder maps these representations to pixel-wise class labels in an end-to-end manner. Each pixel is classified as normal tissue or as belonging to one of the retinal fluid compartments (IRF, SRF, or PED). Network parameters were learned exclusively from manually annotated training data, without the use of handcrafted features.

Pixel-wise three-dimensional segmentation outputs were used to compute fluid volumes (in nanoliters), maximum vertical fluid extent, and spatial localization within the central 6-mm ETDRS subfields. The automated parameters derived in this manner were subsequently correlated with the corresponding measurements obtained from human expert graders.7,12

Code availability

The custom code developed and used to generate the results reported in this study is not publicly available due to proprietary restrictions and the inclusion of licensed or institution-specific components. However, the underlying algorithm itself forms part of a commercially available AI toolset developed by RetInSight GmbH, and interested parties may contact the company for potential access or collaboration.

The data are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. The R scripts used for the analyses and plotting are publicly available on Zenodo at 10.5281/zenodo.18965531. The repository includes a README file with session information and package versions.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using the R Statistics Version 4.2.3. For fluid level heights and volumes, the median and the interquartile range (IQR) are reported. To explore the correlation between manually measured heights and those derived from the automated Fluid Monitor, Bland–Altman analysis and Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated, including correlations between height- and volume-based measurements. The Chi-square test was used to compare proportions of maximum fluid locations.

Results

Our study cohort included 890 baseline visits. In this treatment-naïve nAMD population, IRF was detected within the CMM in 328 eyes by both manual grading and the AI algorithm; overall, IRF was identified in 348 eyes by manual assessment and in 536 eyes by the AI. Similarly, SRF was jointly identified in 502 eyes, with total detections of 560 eyes manually and 649 eyes by the AI, while PED was detected by both approaches in 705 eyes, with overall detections of 731 eyes manually and 793 eyes by the AI.

Discussion

The aim of our analysis was to identify the correlation and consistency between human expert-based retinal fluid assessment and automated AI-based quantification. The parameters included the vertical maximum height observedwithin the central 1 mm subfield, which is typically used in standardized reading center settings with manual reading protocols. Additionally, we considered automated quantification of vertical B-scan extension using a validated tool, as well as three-dimensional fluid volume determination, a feature of automated segmentation. The results of this head-to-head comparison will provide insights into the efficiency and accuracy of quantifying pathological retinal fluid activity in nAMD.

The relationship between human and AI-derived maximum fluid height measurements in the central millimeter was particularly strong for IRF and PED, whereas the correlation for SRF was moderate. The weaker correlation for SRF may be explained by its typically broader horizontal extent, which makes manual identification of the true maximum height more challenging, particularly in the presence of mildly hyperreflective material within the subretinal space. This introduces subjectivity in defining the fluid boundaries.

These findings likely reflect methodological differences between manual height-based grading and the AI-based approach. Human graders tended to include mildly hyperreflective material when delineating SRF, whereas the AI algorithm conservatively excluded such regions. Notably, the RetInSight model was trained using pixel-wise segmentation labels on individual B-scans. Although these annotations inherently encode local SRF heights along individual A-scans, training on segmentation areas across B-scans may yield different outcomes compared with training directly on height-based metrics, as differences may arise in the detection and delineation of fluid boundaries. This discrepancy has not been observed in prior validation studies and should therefore be interpreted as a difference in measurement paradigms rather than a systematic model bias. Future analyses may benefit from harmonized grading criteria or model extensions that explicitly incorporate SRF interface characteristics to improve alignment between manual and AI-based measurements.

First, we compared the identification of B-scan-related fluid height between human expert assessment and automated analysis. Traditionally, large clinical trials have used central retinal thickness (CRT) as a key measure to assess morphological changes after anti-VEGF treatment.7,8 CRT may be considered a surrogate measure for IRF and SRF, the main indicators for anti-VEGF treatment decisions. The dilemma with CRT is that it poorly reflects the actual total disease activity of IRF and SRF due to various influencing factors in nAMD patients,10,11 such as pigment epithelial detachments (often included in CRT measurements), fibrosis, neovascular membranes, SHRM, and other morphological pathologies. Each of these factors exhibits distinct dynamics during disease treatment, and in addition, CRT only addresses the central millimeter of the macular. Therefore, CRT was found to correlate only poorly with objective fluid pooling in nAMD with an R < 0.5. 10 However, although the majority of MNV appears subfoveally15,16, maximum vertical extensions of macular fluid compartments frequently occur outside the central millimeter. For example, it is useful to quantify fluid compartments beyond the central millimeter in type 3 MNV lesions which are mainly located temporally parafoveally, with their maximum IRF accumulation in that area.17 Recent studies have further highlighted that the spatial distribution and volume of retinal fluid outside the foveal center are associated with treatment response and visual outcomes, underscoring the clinical relevance of assessing fluid location beyond the central subfield.18–21 Furthermore, fluid estimation over the entire scan volumes is required in central geographic atrophies (GA), which is typically associated with thinning of the central retina, and MNV may develop adjacent to the border of the GA despite the advanced stage of disease.22 This fact may become particularly relevant when novel treatments potentially lead to an increased rate of MNV in patients with GA.23,24

Manually assessing maxima of fluids over whole OCT volumes, often comprising 47–92 individual B-scans, is highly time-consuming, difficult, and inevitably involves the subjective nature of human decision-making.

In our analysis we highlight the consistency of the algorithmic measurement for IRF and PED, but also the relative inconsistency observed for SRF. Pixel-wise, raster-based segmentation performed in a fast automated manner can objectively and precisely identify relevant peaks in each B-scan at any location.

In contrast, automated AI-based fluid segmentation can provide rapid and precise quantification of fluid volumes as well as the spatial distribution of the respective fluid types across the retinal layers. As we showed in our study, only IRF most frequently had its maximum height in the central millimeter but not even statistically significantly more frequenmtly than the 1–3 mm region while the distribution of SRF and PED showed their maxima in the parafoveal region (1–3 mm). Similar results were shown for nAMD in the study of Michl et al. where the maximum for IRF was in the central millimeter, and the maximum for SRF was parafoveally, indicating the need to quantify disease activity beyond the CMM.25

In a second step, we compared the consistency of vertical height and three-dimensional volume measurements to evaluate whether vertical height is a robust representation of overall fluid amount, as implied by the use of CRT suggests. The correlation between AI-based fluid monitor height measurements and AI-based fluid volumes in the central millimeter was strong for all fluid compartments, with Pearson correlation coefficients ranging from r = 0.84 to r = 0.93, but was weaker for the manual measurements where correlation coefficients were r = 0.55 for SRF, r = 0.76 for IRF, and r = 0.87 for PED. Moreover, this correlation was markedly worse when comparing maximum heights in the central millimeter with total fluid volumes of the central 6 mm. This suggests that both manual and AI-based height measurements can be used to estimate AI fluid volumes within the central millimeter only but are not sufficient for estimating the actual fluid volume of the entire macular area. Consequently, clinically relevant signs of disease activity may be overlooked when morphological assessment is restricted to the central millimeter alone. This is particularly relevant, as IRF, SRF and PED are not distributed evenly, but rather spatially separated and counter-located.14

Using AI to assess macular fluid compartments by volume and location has the potential to significantly accelerate and standardize the assessment of fluid accumulation and dynamics in nAMD. Moreover, since this technology has the potential to accurately measure fluid activity it may serve as a VEGF-meter as proposed by Rosenfeld26, paving the way for optimized anti-VEGF treatment regimens tailored to individual nAMD patient. Chakravarthy et al. have already shown, that fluctuation of retinal fluid volumes which reflects disease activity and causes long-term progressive vision loss.27 Capturing this information comprehensiveley requires automated assessment. As AI advances, it is anticipated to integrate novel imaging modalities, such as wide-field high resolution OCT angiography, enabling even more precise measurements and the identification of further pathological markers within the retina, thereby further improving our understanding and treatment of nAMD. Our study has some limitations. Baseline data on demographics, visual function, scan quality, and MNV type and location were not uniformly available across all cases and were therefore not included in the analysis. As a result, correlations between these factors and imaging-derived parameters could not be assessed, and the dataset may not fully represent the heterogeneity of nAMD encountered in clinical routine. The automated Fluid Monitor has been extensively validated and is capable of benchmarking against human-derived fluid height measurements, although these may be partly subjective and agreement on SHRM or other coexisting alterations is not always achieved. A further limitation of this study is the absence of a direct comparison with other AI models employing similar approaches. Such comparisons would require access to multiple algorithms applied to the same patient cohort using identical imaging data and reference standards, which was not available beyond the version used in the present study. A key strength of this study is the large dataset and the use of an unbiased comparative framework to evaluate expert-derived retinal fluid measurements against automated, volume-based fluid quantification obtained with an approved measurement tool. Building on this, the relevance of the study is underscored by the direct comparison of conventional expert-derived retinal fluid height measurements with automated assessment, demonstrating strong agreement with manual measurements for IRF and PED and moderate agreement for SRF, thereby supporting the robustness of the approach. This level of agreement indicates that automated analysis can provide consistently high-quality evaluation across patients, with performance for IRF and PED comparable to expert reading center level grading. In addition, whole-volume fluid quantification may be particularly valuable for rapid evaluation, as manual grading is impractical in routine clinical practice. For SRF, which often presents as a widespread fluid compartment and is known, together with PED, to substantially influence injection frequency, volumetric quantification offers a clear advantage over height-based metrics, as fluid height alone does not fully capture the spatial extent and complexity of SRF. Such quantitative measures may therefore provide additional parameters for assessing nAMD disease activity and support the development of more precise, individualized treatment strategies.

Conclusion

AI-derived and manual height measurements showed strong agreement for IRF and PED and moderate agreement for SRF. The correlation between maximum fluid height and fluid volume did not reliably reflect overall fluid volumes or spatial distribution within macular OCT scans. Automated AI-based retinal fluid assessment therefore extends conventional manual height measurements by enabling quantitative, three-dimensional evaluation of IRF, SRF, and PED across the entire OCT volume, offering a practical approach to retinal fluid assessment that supports improved interpretation of nAMD disease activity and has the potential to enable more personalized treatment strategies.
