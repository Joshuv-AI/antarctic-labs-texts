Targeting Temozolomide‐Resistant Glioblastoma: Therapeutic Potential of Neuronal Nitric Oxide Synthase Inhibitor
Authors: Mallikarjuna Nimgampalle, Shashank Kumar Ojha, Maryam Kartawy, Michelle Feivelson, Haitham Amal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and PurposeThe treatment of glioblastoma remains a major clinical challenge, largely due to intrinsic and acquired resistance to Temozolomide (TMZ). We aim to advance a novel therapeutic approach to overcome TMZ resistance in human glioblastoma by using the neuronal nitric oxide synthase inhibitor BA‐101 as both a monotherapy and an adjuvant therapy. This approach leverages the additive anti‐cancer activity of BA‐101 to enhance the efficacy of TMZ and thereby improve treatment outcomes for glioblastoma patients.Experimental ApproachWe used TMZ‐resistant human glioblastoma LN‐18 and LN‐229 cells to study the therapeutic potential and additive anticancer activity of nNOS inhibitor BA‐101 using in vitro cancer functional assays, including cell proliferation, cell invasion, and migration assays, and biochemical assessment of biomarkers for nitrosative stress, and apoptosis using western blot analysis, flow cytometry and AnnexinV/PI staining. We further performed an in vivo preclinical evaluation using an LN‐229 xenograft tumor model in SCID mice.Key ResultsOur findings demonstrate that BA‐101 displays significant anti‐cancer activity and sensitizes resistant glioblastoma cells to TMZ, reducing nitrosative stress, inhibiting clonogenic growth, invasion and migration, and promoting apoptotic cell death. It also demonstrates a significant decrease in tumor volume, suggesting that BA‐101 exerts additive anti‐tumor activity when used as an adjuvant to TMZ in SCID mice bearing LN 229 xenografts.Conclusions and ImplicationsCollectively, our findings strongly suggest that BA‐101 has potential for further preclinical and translational development as an anticancer agent and a novel TMZ chemosensitizer for GBM therapy.Keywords: glioblastoma, LN‐18 cells, LN‐229 cells, nitrosative stress, nNOS inhibitor, temozolomide

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is an aggressive and deadly brain cancer, causing recurrent malignant primary tumors in adults. Worldwide, GBM has an incidence of 3–5 cases per lakh adults every year; it comprises 12%–16% of all primary intracranial brain tumors and almost 45%–55% of gliomas [1, 2]. GBM arises from astroglial cells and is characterized by rapid proliferation, infiltrative growth, and a poor prognosis, with a median patient survival of 10–15 months [2]. Still, GBM treatment remains complex, and there has been little improvement in survival rates due to its aggressiveness, recurrence, malignancy, genetic heterogeneity, and, most importantly, resistance to Temozolomide (TMZ) [3]. Surgical ablation, combined with radiotherapy and TMZ chemotherapy, has become the standard of care for GBM [4, 5]. However, during the course of TMZ treatment, GBM patients have shown severe adverse effects and developed resistance against TMZ due to the presence of O6‐methylguanine‐DNA methyltransferase (MGMT), glioblastoma stem cells, tumor heterogeneity, defective DNA repair, adaptive resistance and tumor microenvironment [4, 6, 7, 8, 9].

The nitrosative stress is caused by excessive nitric oxide (NO) generated by three isoforms of nitric oxide synthases (NOS), including nNOS (neuronal), iNOS (inducible), and eNOS (endothelial) [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. We previously investigated the role of NO in glioblastoma, and in physiological, neurodevelopmental, and neurological disorders ([10, 13, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21]). Specifically, GBM cells exhibit overexpression of nNOS and iNOS, resulting in the excessive production of NO and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) that induce DNA damage, promote tumorigenesis, and confer TMZ resistance [11, 22, 23, 24]. Consequently, treatment for GBM has become a big challenge due to TMZ resistance and recurrence. Hence, nitrosative stress has been considered an emerging therapeutic target for GBM [25, 26]. Recently, we also reported that GBM cells overexpress nNOS and iNOS, leading to increased nitrosative stress, and that their normalization with NOS inhibitors has shown potential anticancer activity against TMZ‐sensitive GBM cells [27, 28]. Researchers have further validated that NOS inhibitors exhibit pronounced anticancer potential, accompanied by reduced nitrosative stress in GBM [26, 29, 30]. Our research aims to explore emerging insights into the nNOS inhibitor (BA‐101) against nitrosative stress in GBM, focusing on its additive anticancer therapeutic efficacy and its promising role in sensitizing TMZ‐resistant GBM LN‐18 and LN‐229 cells using both in vitro and in vivo strategies.

Materials and Methods

TMZ‐Resistant Glioblastoma Cell Lines

All in vitro experiments were conducted using two human TMZ‐resistant glioblastoma cell lines, including LN‐18 cells (ATCC CRL‐2610) and TMZ‐resistant LN‐229 cells (ATCC CRL‐2611). The parental LN‐229 cells are intrinsically resistant to TMZ exposure at 100–1000 μM during short‐term (24 h) treatment (Figure 2C). But the cells became sensitive to TMZ at 100 μM and 200 μM after long‐term treatment (12 days) in the colony formation assay (Figure S1). Hence, we established a stable TMZ‐resistant model: LN‐229 cells were continuously treated with TMZ (200 μM) for 3 cycles (each lasting 3 days, with damaged cells washed out) over 9 consecutive days, yielding TMZ‐resistant sublines that were resistant to 100 μM TMZ. These TMZ‐resistant sublines maintained a stable resistant phenotype up to 15 passages in culture [31]. Additionally, we maintained TMZ resistance in sublines by culturing them in medium containing a clinically relevant TMZ concentration (33 μM) for other experiments. And in vivo studies were performed using the TMZ‐sensitive LN‐229 subline (Figure 1). Both cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco 41965‐039) with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS; Gibco10270‐106) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco15140‐122). Cultures were incubated at 37°C in a humidified environment with 5% CO2. All experiments were conducted in LN‐18 cells between 5 and 15 passages, and the TMZ‐resistant LN‐229 subline was used between passages p2 and p6. Both cell lines were used in this study, authenticated by the suppliers (ATCC). They were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination using a Mycolor One‐Step mycoplasma detector (Vazyme: D101‐02), and only mycoplasma‐negative cultures were used for the experiments.

In vivo experiments were performed in 9‐week‐old NOD.CB17‐Prkdc‐scid/NCrHsd mice. The protocol was conducted in accordance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Hebrew University of Jerusalem and the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care International guidelines. Small Molecules

Two molecules were used in our study: one was a test agent, BA‐101, a neuronal nitric oxide synthase inhibitor, and the other was the TMZ drug, an established standard/reference drug (chemotherapeutic/anticancer) for GBM. MTT Assay

For cytotoxicity testing and for the determination of inhibitor doses to be used, both cells (LN‐18 and LN‐229) were plated at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well in 96‐well plates and allowed to attach for 24 h before receiving increasing concentrations of BA‐101 (50–250 μM) or TMZ (100–1000 μM) treatments for another 24 h. MTT (100 μL; 0.5 mg/mL) was added to the wells, and the plates were incubated for 4 h. The resulting formazan crystals were then dissolved in 100 μL of DMSO. Absorbance values were measured at 570 nm by a BioTek Cytation 3 microplate reader.

Colony Formation Assay

The antiproliferative effects of BA‐101, TMZ, and the combination against both LN‐18 and LN‐229 cells were detected using a clonogenic assay [27]. Five hundred cells were seeded into 6‐well plates in complete DMEM medium (n = 6). After 24 h, the LN‐18 cells were incubated with 200 μM of BA‐101, 200 μM of TMZ, the combination group, and the control group, which was maintained without treatment. LN‐229 cells were treated with 200 μM of BA‐101, 100 μM of TMZ, a combination, and a control. The full media, including BA‐101, TMZ, or a combination of both, was replaced every third day. On day 12, the colonies were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 1 h, and rinsed in water, then air‐dried for 24 h. Images were captured, and colonies containing > 50 cells were scored using ImageJ.

Transwell Matrigel Cell Invasion Assay

The assay evaluated the inhibitory effects of BA‐101, TMZ, and the combo on capillary cell invasion in LN‐18 and LN‐229 cells [32]. Both cells (2 × 10^4 cells) were placed in the upper compartment of Transwell inserts, which were precoated with Matrigel using 500 μL of serum‐free DMEM. After overnight incubation, the LN‐18 cells were treated with 200 μM BA‐101, 200 μM TMZ, a combination of both, or left as maintenance control cells (n = 3). While LN‐229 cells were treated with 200 μM BA‐101, 100 μM TMZ, a combination of both, and untreated cells as a control, the experiment continued (n = 3). The lower chamber was supplemented with 700 μL of DMEM complete medium (10% FBS, chemoattractant). After 24 h of incubation, the noninvasive cells were removed with Q‐tips, and the invasive cells that had entered through the membrane were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. They were then stained with 0.1% crystal violet in PBS solution for an additional 30 min. Subsequently, the inserts were rinsed three times by submerging in PBS. The inserts were used after drying overnight at room temperature. The stained cells were observed and captured using a light microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ts2), and ImageJ was used to count the invaded cells.

Cell Migration (Wound Healing Assay)

A wound‐healing assay was used to evaluate the effects of BA‐101 and TMZ treatments, as well as their combined effect on the migration of GBM cells, specifically LN‐18 and LN‐229 [32]. Both cells were disseminated at a density of 0.3 × 106 cells in six‐well plates (n = 6). A wound was created in the 80% confluent cell monolayer by scratching with a 200‐μL pipette tip, and the monolayer was washed with PBS. Immediately, wound images were obtained under a light microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ts2) at 0 h using 10× magnification before treating the cells. LN‐18 cells were treated with 200 μM BA‐101, 200 μM TMZ, a combination of both, and maintained control cells without any treatment (n = 6). Although LN‐229 cells were treated with 200 μM BA‐101, 100 μM TMZ, a combination of both, and untreated cells as a control, the experiment continued (n = 6). The cells were treated in complete DMEM media to observe cell migration for up to 48 h, and wound healing was photographed using a light microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ts2). The extent of wound healing was quantified as the area or percentage of the gap area, visualized using ImageJ.

Determination of Total Nitrate and Nitrite

Total nitrate/nitrite concentrations were determined using a commercial kit (Cayman cat. no.: 780001). We performed the assay per the manufacturer's instructions. In short, as described above, the treated TMZ‐resistant GBM cells (LN‐18 and LN‐229 cells) with BA‐101 and TMZ for 24 h, and protein lysate range (200–300 μg) of samples are diluted in nitrate/nitrite assay buffer and added to two wells of a 96 plate with standard range (0–10 μM) as substrate to obtain consistent results. Next, 5 μL of nitrate reductase cofactors and enzyme were added to both samples and standards, but not the blank (100 μL of NO Assay buffer only). We then incubated the sample plate at ambient temperature for 2 h. We then added 25 μL of both Griess reagents 1 & 2 per sample and standard, incubated for 10 min at room temperature (RT) & read the absorbance at wavelengths between 540 and 550 nm. The OD values were entered into a linear regression with the standard curve to determine nitrate/nitrite levels in the samples.

Western Blot Analysis

After treating TMZ‐resistant GBM LN‐18 and LN‐229 cells with BA‐101, TMZ, Combo, and untreated control cells for 48 h. The cells were lysed in freshly prepared RIPA buffer (30 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1% Nonidet P‐40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate, 5 mM EDTA, 1 mM Na3VO4, 50 mM NaF, 1 mM PMSF, and 1% protease/phosphatase inhibitors cocktail (pH 7.7)). Then, the cells were homogenized using a sonicator and incubated on ice for 30 min. The cell lysates were centrifuged (17,000 × g, 20 min at 4°C); a supernatant sample was collected, and protein concentration was calculated employing the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit by Sigma Aldrich. The gels were electrophoresed on polyacrylamide gels, and the sample proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane using a semi‐dry transfer method (Bio‐Rad Laboratories). Nonspecific binding sites were blocked with 5% dried skimmed milk in TBST (135 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris, and 0.1% Tween 20), pH 7.4. The blocking was performed for 2 h at room temperature. The PVDF membranes bearing the transferred proteins were incubated overnight at 4°C on a shaker with the primary antibody. Primary antibodies used are anti‐3‐nitrotyrosine (Abcam, AB110282; 1:5000), anti‐cleaved caspase 3 (Cell Signaling Technologies, 9661; 1:5000) and anti‐β‐actin (Abcam, AB8226; 1:1000), anti‐MGMT (Cell Signaling Technologies, 2739S; 1:1000), and anti‐IDH1 (Isocitrate dehydrogenase1) (Cell Signaling Technologies, 3997S; 1:1000). The membranes were washed with TBST for 3 × 10 min after incubation with the primary antibodies, and then added to anti‐mouse/rabbit HRP‐conjugated secondary antibody (anti‐rabbit (7074S; 1:10000) and anti‐mouse (7076S; 1:10000) from Cell Signaling Technologies) at room temperature for 1 h. The protein's binding activity was detected using an ECL substrate (Bio‐Rad Laboratories). A Bio‐Rad Chemidoc imaging system (Bio‐Rad Laboratories) was used to develop the bands' images. The intensity of protein band expression is quantified using ImageJ software.

Characterization of the TMZ‐Resistant Model

The parental LN‐229‐p2, the TMZ‐resistant LN‐229 subline‐p2, and the TMZ‐resistant subline grown for multiple passages up to p‐6 without TMZ (33 μM) supplementation were treated with various concentrations of TMZ (25–200 μM) for 48 h. Following treatment, an MTT assay was performed. Similarly, MGMT expression in the TMZ‐resistant sublines p2 and p6 was assessed by western blot analysis to determine TMZ resistance stability. The data were provided as Supporting Information.

Flow Cytometry Analysis Using FITC Annexin V/PI Apoptosis Detection for DNA Fragmentation and Cell Death

Apoptosis and DNA fragmentation were assessed after treatment of LN‐18 cells with BA‐101, TMZ, Combo, or control for 48 h. Flow cytometry was conducted using an FITC Annexin V/Propidium Iodide (PI)‐based apoptosis detection kit (ABP Biosciences, catalog No. A026) as described in the manufacturer's instructions. After 48 h of treatment, ~1 × 106 TMZ‐resistant human GBM LN‐18 cells were collected in cold PBS and resuspended in 1× annexin‐binding buffer. 1 μL of PI was added to the cells, mixed, incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark, then diluted with 400 μL of binding buffer and analyzed immediately by flow cytometry (λ > 575 nm for PI). For the detection of apoptosis by fluorescence microscopy, in parallel, control and 48‐h‐treated GBM cells grown on cover slips were stained with 5 μL Annexin V‐FITC and 1 μL PI, incubated for 15 min, washed once in binding buffer, and then mounted on a glass slide to be observed under a fluorescence microscope using FITC and Texas Red/PI filters. Additionally, the control and treated LN‐18 cell groups were also used to quantify apoptotic cell death by flow cytometry (CytoFLEX, Beckman Coulter). A minimum of 10,000 events per sample were acquired in the FITC Channel (488 nm laser, 525/40 BP filter) and the PI channel (561 nm laser, 585/42). The data were analyzed using FlowJo software v11, and quadrants were set based on unstained and single‐stained controls. The total apoptosis was calculated as the sum of both early and late apoptotic populations. The cells were categorized as viable (Annexin V‐/PI‐), early apoptotic (Annexin V+/PI–), or late apoptotic/necrotic (Annexin V+/PI+).

Xenograft Model and Drug Treatment

Subcutaneous GBM‐bearing mice were established by inoculating 4 × 10^6 parental LN‐229 cells (in 100 μL of a mixture of 50% Matrigel and 50% DMEM) subcutaneously into the flanks of 9‐week‐old male NOD. CB17‐Prkdc‐scid/NCrHsd (NOD‐SCID) mice [28]. Subsequently, mice were randomized into four groups, each containing seven animals, at 4 weeks after tumor cell injection, when the average tumor volume was approximately 200 mm3. Animals were administered an intraperitoneal DMSO vehicle (100 μL), TMZ (10 mg/kg), BA‐101 (80 mg/kg) in 100 μL of PBS/5% DMSO, or a Combo treatment (BA‐101 and TMZ). All mice were euthanized on 12 days.

Measurement of Tumor Size and Body Weight in the Tumor‐Bearing SCID Mice

The mouse body weight and tumor volumes were recorded every 2 days during the experiments. Tumor volume was measured using a digital caliper and calculated as follows: tumor volume = (length × width2)/2.

Statistics

Data are shown as Mean and SEM (M ± SEM). Statistical differences were assessed using one‐way ANOVA and the Tukey multiple‐comparison test across all experiments. Bar graphs and statistical analysis were conducted using Prism 9.3 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001 and ns, nonsignificant.

Results

Glioblastoma LN‐18 and LN‐229 Cells Are Highly Resistant to TMZ

We chose to use TMZ‐resistant glioblastoma cell lines, LN‐18 and LN‐229, for our study to evaluate the therapeutic effect of the nNOS inhibitor BA‐101. To corroborate their resistance to TMZ, we performed an MTT assay. The results indicated that TMZ (100–1000 μM for 24 h) could induce significant drug resistance in both LN‐18 and LN‐229 cells (Figure 2A,C). These results suggest that both cell lines are resistant to TMZ. Accordingly, we continued the two cell lines for later experiments to evaluate the therapeutic applications of the nNOS inhibitor (BA‐101) on TMZ‐resistant glioblastoma. The DMSO is non‐toxic (0.001%–0.003%) and slightly toxic to both the LN‐18 and LN‐229 cells at concentrations above this level. Therefore, based on these latter data, the 200 μM and 100 μM TMZ concentrations were selected for the following in vitro assays on LN‐18 and LN‐229 cells, respectively.

BA‐101 Is Cytotoxic to Both LN‐18 and LN‐229 Cells

BA‐101, a selective nNOS inhibitor, was utilized in our study to assess its anticancer properties through in vitro and in vivo experimental models. First, we evaluated the cytotoxicity of BA‐101 in LN‐18 and LN‐229 GBM cells using the MTT assay. Cells were exposed to BA‐101 at doses of 50–250 μM for 24 h. The data indicated concentration‐dependent cytotoxicity for both cell lines. Compared with the untreated control (100% viability), BA‐101 resulted in 83%–41% and 85%–58% reductions in cell viability in LN‐18 and LN‐229 cells, respectively (Figure 2B,D). DMSO is nontoxic (0.01%–0.04%) to both cell types; however, it reduces cell viability at 0.05%. Based on these and further experimental results (see next sections), 200 μM BA‐101 was chosen for further in vitro examinations with LN‐18 and LN‐229 cells (Figure 2E,F).

BA‐101 Alone and Combined With TMZ Suppress the Colony Formation of TMZ‐Resistant LN‐18 and LN‐229 Cells

The colony formation assay shows that 200 μM and 100 μM TMZ‐treated LN‐18 and LN‐229 cells, respectively, form similar, nonsignificant numbers of colonies compared with the respective untreated controls (p > 0.05). In contrast, BA‐101 alone (200 μM) significantly decreases colony formation in both cell lines compared with controls and TMZ (p < 0.001, p < 0.0001). In addition, the combination of BA‐101 and TMZ induces a substantially stronger reduction in colony‐forming ability compared to controls, TMZ alone, and BA‐101 alone, indicating that BA‐101 and TMZ exhibit potential additive growth inhibition (p < 0.05, p < 0.0001) (Figure 2E–H). Altogether, these results consistently demonstrate that BA‐101 monotherapy and combined therapy achieve similar inhibition of colony formation in TMZ‐resistant LN‐18 and LN‐229 cells, suggesting a strong inhibition of cell proliferation in TMZ‐resistant human glioblastoma cells. There is no significant difference in colony formation between control and DMSO vehicle (0.04%).

BA‐101 or in Combination With TMZ Inhibited the Cell Invasion of Both TMZ‐Resistant LN‐18 and LN‐229 Cells

The results of the cell invasion assay show that the 200 μM and 100 μM TMZ treatments of LN‐18 and LN‐229 cells have no significant effect on cell invasion compared to their respective controls (p > 0.05). In contrast, both cell lines treated with BA‐101 (200 μM) alone exhibit a significant decrease in invasion compared to the control and TMZ‐treated cells (p < 0.001, p < 0.001, p < 0.001, p < 0.01). Additionally, the combined treatment with TMZ and BA‐101 in both LN‐18 cells and TMZ‐resistant‐LN‐229 cells results in a remarkable reduction in cell invasion compared to control or TMZ alone (p < 0.001, p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001); However, the combo treatment is not significantly different from the BA‐101 alone treatment (p > 0.05) (Figure 3A–D). These data indicate that BA‐101 alone and in combination with TMZ significantly suppress cell invasion in both LN‐18 cells and the TMZ‐resistant LN‐229 subline, and that BA‐101 may suppress the invasive behavior in TMZ‐resistant glioblastoma tumors.

BA‐101 Blocks the Cell Migration of TMZ‐Resistant LN‐18 and LN‐229 Cells With an Additive Phenomenon in Combo Treatment

Wound healing assay indicates that treatment of LN‐18 and LN‐229 cells with 200 μM and 100 μM TMZ shows no significant wound closure (% of wound healing or inhibition of cell migration) compared to the untreated groups of these cells (p > 0.05). In contrast, treatment with BA‐101 (200 μM) alone significantly prevents cell migration by blocking wound healing in both cell lines, compared with control and TMZ‐treated cells (p < 0.05, p < 0.01). However, the combo treatment in LN‐18 cells shows a significant inhibition of wound closure compared to TMZ alone (p < 0.05) but no significant effect on wound closure compared to the control or BA‐101 (p > 0.05). Interestingly, the combination treatment in the TMZ‐resistant LN‐229 subline shows a significant prevention of wound closure compared to the control (p < 0.001) and to TMZ (100 μM) alone (p < 0.01), but not significantly different from BA‐101 alone (p > 0.05) (Figure 3E–H). These results agree with the cell invasion assay and indicate that combining treatments potently inhibited cell migration in both TMZ‐resistant glioblastoma cells.

BA‐101 Enhances TMZ Efficacy by Reducing Nitrosative Stress in TMZ‐Resistant Glioblastoma Cells

BA‐101 (200 μM), TMZ (200 μM), or their combination treatments significantly decrease nitrosative stress by reducing nitrate/nitrite levels in both LN‐18 and LN‐229 glioblastoma cell lines compared to the control (p < 0.0001). In contrast, the combination treatment causes a significantly greater decrease in nitrosative stress than the TMZ (200 μM) and BA‐101 (200 μM) alone treatments (p < 0.05) and the BA‐101 and TMZ alone groups (p < 0.05) (Figure 4A). The NO assay results also show that BA‐101 significantly reduces nitrosative stress by scavenging free radicals produced by NO.

TMZ and BA‐101 Reduce 3‐Nitrotyrosine Levels in LN‐18 Cells, Indicating Attenuation of Nitrosative Stress

Treatment with TMZ (200 μM), BA‐101 (200 μM), and their combination shows a reduction of the levels of 3‐nitrotyrosine in LN‐18 cells compared to untreated controls, as demonstrated by both Western blot and densitometric analysis (p < 0.01, p < 0.05). The control LN‐18 cells exhibit the highest levels of 3‐nitrotyrosine, whereas DMSO (0.04%) treatment does not significantly alter 3‐nitrotyrosine levels. The combination treatment shows a significant reduction in 3‐nitrotyrosine compared to the control (p < 0.01) and a nonsignificant difference in 3‐nitrotyrosine levels relative to individual treatments with TMZ or BA‐101 alone (p > 0.05) (Figure 4B,C). The DMSO group shows no significant change in 3‐nitrotyrosine levels compared with the untreated control, confirming that the vehicle had no effect. These results indicate that both TMZ and BA‐101 reduce protein nitration in LN‐18 cells, suggesting a potential role in alleviating nitrosative stress in TMZ‐resistant human glioblastoma LN‐18 cells.

TMZ and BA‐101 Have Opposite Effects on MGMT Rather Than on IDH1 Expression in LN‐18 Glioblastoma Cells

TMZ (200 μM) treatment alone in LN‐18 cells caused significant downregulation of MGMT expression compared with the control (p < 0.05). Whereas BA‐101 (200 μM) treatment alone didn't affect MGMT expression compared to the control (p > 0.05). Interestingly, the combination treatment showed the lowest MGMT expression compared with the control, TMZ, or BA‐101 alone, but this difference was not statistically significant (p > 0.05) (Figure 4D,E). In contrast, IDH1 expression remains unchanged under all treatment conditions compared to the control. This suggests that there is no modulation of IDH1 levels in response to TMZ, BA‐101, or their combination in this in vitro setting (Figure 4F,G).

BA‐101 Potentiate TMZ‐Induced Apoptosis in TMZ‐Resistant Glioblastoma Cells

Treatment with TMZ and BA‐101 alone shows no significant change in cleaved caspase‐3 levels in LN‐18 cells compared with the control (p > 0.05). On the contrary, the combo treatment of TMZ and BA‐101 exhibits a significant upregulation of cleaved caspase‐3 compared to the control (p < 0.01) and no significant changes with TMZ and BA‐101 treatments alone (p > 0.05) (Figure 4H,I). The results obtained suggest further evidence of an additive effect of BA‐101 with TMZ in TMZ‐resistant glioblastoma cells.

BA‐101 Greatly Intensifies the TMZ‐Induced DNA Fragmentation and Apoptotic Cell Death in TMZ‐Resistant Glioblastoma Cells

DNA fragmentation and cell death are assayed by staining with the vital dye propidium iodide (PI) and analyzed by FACS in LN‐18 glioblastoma cells 48 h posttreatment with BA‐101 (200 μM), TMZ (200 μM), or BA‐101 combined with TMZ. Exposure to BA‐101 results in a significant rightward shift in PI fluorescence compared with the control, indicating enhanced DNA fragmentation and an increased probability of cell death. In contrast, treatment with TMZ alone shifts PI staining only marginally compared to control, reflecting a lower extent of DNA damage induction under these conditions. Significantly, the coadministration of BA‐101 and TMZ exhibits a more significant rightward shift in PI fluorescence intensity than either agent alone (Figure 5A). Demonstrates the promotion of DNA fragmentation and of cell death. Comparative overlay analysis of all groups also proved that the combination treatment is the most effective and has advantages over BA‐101 alone and TMZ alone in inducing DNA damage. These data indicate that BA‐101 markedly sensitizes the TMZ‐induced DNA fragmentation and death in TMZ‐resistant LN‐18 cells.

BA‐101 Enhances TMZ‐Stimulated Apoptosis and Necrosis in LN‐18 Glioblastoma Cells as Detected by Annexin V/PI

Apoptosis and necrosis are evaluated by FITC Annexin V and propidium iodide (PI) staining on LN‐18 glioblastoma cells after 48 h of treatment with BA‐101 (200 μM), TMZ (200 μM), or the combination. The untreated control cells exhibit little Annexin V and PI fluorescence, indicating that they are mostly viable and intact in morphology. Exposure to TMZ (200 μM) alone results in a slight enhancement of green fluorescence, indicative of early apoptosis, with subtle changes in cell morphology, but no significant PI uptake, suggesting little late apoptosis or necrosis. By contrast, cells treated with BA‐101 (200 μM) exhibit high levels of Annexin V staining and moderate PI fluorescence, indicating that a substantial fraction of cells are early apoptotic, with membrane compromise and damage. The BA‐101 and TMZ combination induces a marked increase in Annexin V and PI staining, with intense red staining representing extensive DNA damage and membrane permeabilization (induced apoptosis), and green fluorescence indicating early apoptosis (Figure 5B). This was consistent with adjuvant cytotoxicity, as increased apoptosis and necrosis were observed in the combination‐treated group compared to either agent alone. These findings indicate that BA‐101 sensitizes TMZ‐resistant LN‐18 cells to TMZ by promoting cytotoxicity induced by TMZ through both apoptotic and necrotic cell death.

TMZ and BA‐101 Combo Treatment Enhances the Apoptosis in LN‐18 Cells

To evaluate the impact of TMZ, BA‐101, and their combination on cell apoptosis, human GBM LN‐18 cells were examined by Annexin V–FITC/PI staining, and apoptotic cell death was quantified by flow cytometry. Representative dot plots and quantification are presented in Figure 6. In untreated control cells, the majority of the population maintained viability, with ~84% Annexin V−/PI− and minimal early and late apoptotic populations observed. Vehicle control (0.04% DMSO) treatment did not significantly affect cell viability or induce apoptotic changes compared with untreated controls, indicating no solvent‐elicited cytotoxicity. TMZ 200 μM treatment resulted in a slight increase in apoptotic cells (a small increase in the Annexin V+ population compared to both control and DMSO‐treated cells), but this increase was not statistically significant. Also, treatment with BA‐101 (200 μM) alone resulted in a minor, nonsignificant increase in apoptotic death, and most cells remained viable. Interestingly, the combo treatment resulted in a higher percentage of Annexin V‐positive cells than either treatment alone, suggesting an enhancement of apoptosis (Figure 6). Quantitative analysis showed a significantly higher rate of apoptotic cells in the combo treatment compared with the control (p < 0.05). It confirmed that the combination was more effective than either TMZ or BA‐101 alone, although the comparisons did not reach statistical significance (Figure 6). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that either TMZ or BA‐101 alone could induce a relatively small amount of apoptosis in LN‐18 cells, whereas combined treatment led to a marked induction of apoptosis, providing evidence for adjuvant interaction between the two agents. The quantitative flow cytometry results are consistent with the upregulation of cleaved caspase‐3 detected by western blot analysis (Figure 4H,I) and with the microscopic detection of apoptosis (Figure 5B).

BA‐101 and Combination With TMZ Effectively Suppresses Tumor Progression in LN‐229 Tumor‐Bearing SCID Mice

The therapeutic efficacy of BA‐101, TMZ, and Combo on glioblastoma tumors was assessed on parental LN‐229 subcutaneous xenograft‐bearing SCID mice by vehicle control, TMZ (10 mg/kg b.w.), BA‐101 (80 mg/kg b.w.), and Combo. Representative tumor images at the end of the 12‐day treatment showed that a quantitative analysis of final tumor volumes revealed that both TMZ and BA‐101 significantly reduced tumor volume compared to the control (p < 0.05, p < 0.01) (Figure 7A,B). Interestingly, the combined treatment produced significant tumor inhibition compared to the control and TMZ groups (p < 0.0001, p < 0.01). Although the difference between BA‐101 and Combo was insignificant (p > 0.05), BA‐101 alone likely plays a major role in the therapeutic effect. Following tumor growth over time, tumors continued to grow in the control group, whereas all treatment groups exhibited marked tumor growth inhibition by 12 days. Combo had the strongest tumor growth inhibition (p < 0.0001 vs. control on day 12), followed by BA‐101 and TMZ (Figure 7A–C).

Notably, the animals' weights did not show a significant change in any treatment group during the observation period, suggesting that none of the treatments induced systemic toxicity (Figure 7D). These data indicate that parental LN‐229 tumors are TMZ‐sensitive and extremely responsive to BA‐101 treatment. Combined chemotherapy results in better tumor growth inhibition than chemotherapy alone without causing significant body weight loss in the SCID mice.

Discussion and Conclusions

Temozolomide is the standard‐of‐care chemotherapeutic agent for glioblastoma (GBM); however, its clinical effectiveness is limited by intrinsic and acquired resistance mechanisms, which largely involve DNA repair pathways and adaptive cellular signaling [6, 7]. In our results, the nNOS inhibitor BA‐101 additively enhanced the anticancer activity of TMZ in TMZ‐resistant GBM cells, establishing an effective adjuvant therapy for standard treatment of human GBM cell lines LN‐18 and LN‐229, diminishing clonogenic proliferation, reducing invasion and migration, and decreasing nitrosative stress with increased apoptosis in both TMZ‐resistant GBM cells in vitro and reducing tumor volume in the TMZ‐sensitive LN‐229 xenograft model. This combination represents a novel therapeutic approach to overcoming TMZ resistance in human GBM cells, a pressing clinical problem in GBM therapy [26, 33].

According to the ethical rules for animal experimentation, our work primarily followed the 3Rs principles (replacement, reduction, and refinement), as it is not possible to completely replace in vivo models due to their complexity, which is similar to that of biological systems. However, we have followed guidelines as closely as possible for Refinement and Reduction, using the minimum number of animals necessary to achieve statistical power, thereby enabling tumor volume and body weight measurements in the xenograft model. Methods were also implemented to minimize pain, suffering, and distress.

Both TMZ and BA‐101 exhibited cytotoxicity in a dose‐dependent manner; furthermore, their combination showed an additive effect, as confirmed by a colony formation assay, in which BA‐101 enhanced the TMZ‐induced inhibition of clonogenic survival in both TMZ‐resistant LN‐18 and LN‐229 cells. These observations are promising because clonogenicity is strongly correlated with recurrence, invasion, and treatment failure in GBM patients [34, 35]. This combo‐treatment suggests that BA‐101 not only acts additively with TMZ to enhance cytotoxicity but also disrupts the long‐term self‐renewal abilities of GBM cells, one of the major factors underlying tumor aggressiveness [34]. In addition, we also found that BA‐101 could potentiate TMZ‐mediated inhibition of GBM invasiveness in the transwell invasion assay, as well as migration in the wound‐healing assay, in both TMZ‐resistant LN‐18 and LN‐229 cells, indicating that BA‐101 can reverse TMZ‐induced mesenchymal‐like phenotypes, including aggressiveness, invasiveness, and resistance to chemotherapy after exposure to TMZ [36, 37]. Because GBM lethality is largely due to diffusive infiltration into adjacent brain tissue, therapies that impede cell motility have the potential to prolong survival and prevent tumor recurrence [38].

Mechanistic information obtained from nitric oxide (NO) and 3‐nitrotyrosine measurements demonstrates that nNOS inhibitor BA‐101 potentiates TMZ to reduce nitrosative stress in both TMZ‐resistant GBM LN‐18 and LN‐229 cells (Figure 8). The nNOS inhibitor BA‐101 enhances the complementary effect of TMZ treatment by decreasing the prosurvival action of endogenous NO, thereby indirectly activating an apoptosis‐upregulating mechanism and sensitizing GBM cells to TMZ, mimicking the induction of apoptosis [39]. Concordantly, we also found that cleaved caspase‐3 expression and Annexin V/PI staining were significantly higher in the comb treatment, suggesting that BA‐101 enhances GBM cell apoptosis in response to TMZ. Our previous research reports have also shown that NOS inhibitors, both alone and in combination with TMZ, reduce nitrosative stress and the elevation of apoptosis signaling in TMZ‐sensitive human glioblastoma cells in vitro and in vivo [27, 28]. The reactive nitrogen species, such as nitric oxide (NO) and NO‐derived products, can have context‐dependent effects in GBM; higher levels of NO may promote survival/angiogenesis, while the use of NOS inhibitors causes lower concentrations of NO/peroxynitrite, which can result in apoptosis in GBM [30, 40, 41]. Crucially, these effects were also present in TMZ‐resistant cell lines, indicating that BA‐101 could circumvent or counteract known patterns of resistance. Our data support a mechanism of action in which BA‐101 dislocates the redox/nitrosative equilibrium toward proapoptotic signaling, which renders GBM cells sensitized to TMZ.

BA‐101 and TMZ exert adjuvant anticancer activity in glioblastoma (GBM) cells. BA‐101 reduces nitrosative stress in GBM cells and exhibits anticancer properties. When combined with TMZ (temozolomide), these properties are further enhanced, leading to DNA damage associated with apoptosis. The additive anticancer effect of BA‐101 and TMZ is attributed to increased DNA damage and apoptosis, thereby enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of TMZ.

In general, MGMT expression is higher in TMZ‐resistant glioblastoma cells, consistent with its known involvement in chemoresistance. However, treatment of the TMZ‐resistant LN‐18 cells with 200 μM TMZ results in significant downregulation of MGMT expression, as detected by Western blot analysis, because it is consumed in repairing TMZ‐induced DNA lesions (i.e., suicide inactivation and degradation of the enzyme). This is independent of alterations to MGMT promoter methylation and is attributed to posttranslational loss, rather than reduced transcription. Consequently, MGMT protein is consumed and depleted upon exposure to TMZ, rather than showing no change compared to control cells [42, 43]. In our studies, cotreatment with BA‐101 attenuated the elevation of MGMT protein levels in LN‐18 cells, suggesting that it targets resistance nodes, which MGMT incompletely inhibits. Furthermore, the combination treatment with BA‐101 appeared to counteract this effect and maintain TMZ sensitivity, indicating that this observation may be one aspect of the sensitization mechanism via interference with DNA repair or MGMT‐mediated safety. Other, more comprehensive resistance programs, including mismatch repair, base excision repair (BER), adaptive signaling and tumor cell plasticity, are also involved in chemo‐refractoriness [44, 45]. IDH1 expression was relatively unchanged, suggesting that the mechanism of BA‐101 does not depend on the mutational status of IDH1. In aggregate, these findings indicate that BA‐101 can enhance TMZ's cytotoxic effects through diverse cooperative functions, including overcoming DNA repair barriers, augmenting nitrosative stress, and inducing apoptotic cascades.

In SCID mouse‐bearing LN‐229 xenografts, the combination treatment resulted in a significant inhibition of tumor volume, with no corresponding decrease in body weight, suggesting no systemic toxicity. These preclinical results thus strongly suggest that BA‐101 is a suitable adjuvant to TMZ in the treatment of GBM. Most significantly, the antitumor effect persisted over time, indicating a potential for durable responses when BA‐101 is combined with standard drug therapies, which encourages the translation of BA‐101 as an adjunct to TMZ. The current research is further supported by our recent report, which shows that NOS inhibitors, alone and in combination with TMZ, have therapeutic potential in reducing tumor volume in SCID mice bearing TMZ‐sensitive U‐87 MG cells [28].

The primary limitation of the study is that the TMZ‐resistant results and the conclusions drawn from our data are mainly supported by validation in established in vitro resistance models; however, we did not directly address an in vivo model of TMZ resistance. The in vivo confirmation in resistant models is still required and represents a major limitation of this study. The resistance to TMZ is primarily mediated by cell‐intrinsic mechanisms that enable tumor cells to withstand DNA damage and by adaptive (prosurvival) signaling, which can be most modeled and studied in vitro [8, 46]. In this manner, the in vitro TMZ‐resistant model used in the current study is relevant and mechanistically driven, enabling the evaluation of BA‐101 activity in a TMZ‐resistant LN‐229 cell line. In contrast, inhibition of tumor growth in vivo, even if biologically relevant, does not, by itself, discriminate whether a given effect is due to the reversal of TMZ resistance or simply to general antitumor activity. Therefore, the in vivo xenograft experiments with the parental LN‐229 cells were not intended as a direct test of TMZ resistance, but rather to demonstrate baseline antitumor activity and proof of concept in the animals after treatment with BA‐101. An additional limitation is that the chemical structure of BA‐101 is not disclosed due to patent restrictions. If this restricts the immediate structural reproducibility, the biological results reported here are supported by clearly defined in vitro experimental parameters and quantifiable functional readouts that can be compared independently. For verification purposes, BA‐101 requests will be considered from qualified researchers upon reasonable request, subject to a material transfer agreement. Full structural information will be provided after intellectual property protections are in place. Moreover, the limitations and prospects of our research include our molecular studies, which indicate a correlation between the downregulation of nitrosative stress and apoptosis, but do not demonstrate a direct cause‐and‐effect relationship between NO/3‐NT formation and cell death. Targeted pharmacologic or genetic manipulation of NO synthases and peroxynitrite signaling would provide support for causation. Although LN‐18 and LN‐229 cell lines, as well as their TMZ‐resistant derivatives, mimic critical features of GBM biology, patient‐derived and orthotopic models better represent tumor heterogeneity and microenvironmental cross‐talk. Resolution of these limitations will likely be necessary before initiating clinical studies. Given the multifactorial basis of TMZ resistance, combining TMZ with BA‐101 or other agents that simultaneously inhibit repair and stimulate apoptotic/nitrosative stress could yield clinically significant benefits in terms of progression‐free and overall survival.

In conclusion, our research demonstrates that BA‐101 exhibits strong anticancer activity by potentiating and sensitizing TMZ in TMZ‐resistant human GBM LN‐18 and LN‐229 cells in vitro, reducing nitrosative stress, inhibiting clonogenic proliferation, cell invasion, and migration, and enhancing apoptotic signaling. Furthermore, our in vivo preclinical results provide strong evidence that BA‐101 decreases tumor volume and maintains consistent body weight in mice, supporting its additive activity as an adjuvant therapy to overcome TMZ resistance in SCID mouse‐bearing LN‐229 xenografts. Together, these results strongly suggest the potential for continued preclinical and translational development of BA‐101 as an anti‐cancer agent and a novel TMZ adjuvant drug for treating GBM.
