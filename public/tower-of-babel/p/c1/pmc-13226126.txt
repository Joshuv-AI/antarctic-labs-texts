Directed evolution of heme biosynthetic enzymes enabled by a growth-coupled heme biosensor in Corynebacterium glutamicum
Authors: Fangming Mou, Dong Li, Hong Wang, Jianbo Li, Tao Chen, Zhiwen Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heme is an essential cofactor involved in diverse cellular processes and is an important target for microbial biosynthesis. However, engineering microbial heme production remains challenging due to the requirement for coordinated activity of multiple pathway enzymes and the lack of scalable strategies for enzyme-level screening. In this study, we developed a growth-coupled screening system in Corynebacterium glutamicum by establishing a ChrSA-based heme biosensor derived from the native heme-responsive two-component system, which directly converts intracellular heme levels into a selectable growth phenotype. Using this biosensor-enabled system, random mutagenesis libraries were constructed for four key enzymes of the coproporphyrin-dependent (CPD) heme biosynthesis pathway, and improved variants were identified through biosensor-guided selection. The selected variants were subsequently validated by chromosomal allelic replacement, resulting in an overall increase in heme production of approximately 38.9% and a final titer of 308.7 mg/L under native, single-copy regulation. Sequence and structural analyses indicated that the beneficial substitutions were predominantly located outside catalytic residues, suggesting that changes in enzyme stability and conformational properties contributed to the enhanced heme biosynthesis. This work established a heme biosensor-guided strategy for enzyme variant screening in tightly coupled metabolic pathways and provided a practical systems-level approach for microbial heme pathway engineering.

Introduction

Heme is an iron-porphyrin cofactor that is widely distributed in archaea, bacteria, and eukaryotes. It consists of a tetrapyrrole porphyrin ring coordinated with a central divalent iron ion, forming a hexacoordinate complex. As a key member of the tetrapyrrole family, heme participates in various biological processes, including signal transduction [1], oxygen transport [2], and electron transfer [3]. It functions as an essential prosthetic group in proteins such as hemoglobin, myoglobin, and cytochromes, where it supports oxygen transport and cellular respiration [4], and also serves as a catalytic center for enzymes involved in diverse metabolic reactions [5]. In addition to its physiological roles, heme has found applications in the pharmaceutical and food industries, for example, as a treatment for porphyria, a component of artificial meat [6], and a precursor for fine chemical synthesis. These diverse biological functions have driven increasing interest in the efficient microbial production of heme.

In microorganisms, heme biosynthesis is a highly conserved, multi-step metabolic pathway that uses 5-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA) as the universal precursor [7]. 5-ALA is synthesized via either the C4 pathway, which occurs in eukaryotes and some bacteria and uses succinyl-CoA and glycine as precursors [8], or the C5 pathway, in which glutamate is converted to 5-ALA in most bacteria and archaea [9]. Subsequently, 5-ALA is converted to uroporphyrinogen III (UPG III), which is further processed into heme through the protoporphyrin-dependent (PPD) [10], coproporphyrin-dependent (CPD) [11,12], or siroheme-dependent (SHD) [13] pathways (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Schematic overview of heme biosynthesis pathways. Downstream of UPG III, heme can be synthesized through three alternative routes: the CPD, PPD, and SHD pathways. Among heme biosynthetic routes, the CPD pathway has emerged as a promising route for microbial heme production. In Corynebacterium glutamicum, Wang et al. [14] demonstrated that the CPD pathway (hemY-hemH-hemQ) outperformed the PPD pathway (hemF-hemG-hemH) in both heme production and thermodynamic driving force. This comparison highlighted the CPD pathway as a more advantageous route for microbial heme biosynthesis. Despite this overall advantage, optimization of the CPD pathway remains challenging. Ko et al. [15] showed that CPD-based pathway improvement primarily depended on HemQ, achieving a heme titer of 28.66 mg/L, whereas overexpression of HemY or HemH alone was ineffective. This limited response was attributed to additional constraints within the CPD pathway, including CgoX-mediated H2O2 generation, which imposes oxidative stress, and iron-dependent regulation of the ferrochelatase CpfC, which restricts efficient heme formation. More broadly, Dailey et al. [12] summarized that prokaryotic heme biosynthesis, including CPD-dependent routes, is subject to complex and tightly regulated control mechanisms. Collectively, these studies suggest that CPD pathway performance is governed by complex regulatory constraints, underscoring the difficulty of rational pathway optimization. Such complexity highlights the need for systematic strategies to evaluate and optimize CPD pathway designs, particularly approaches that enable pathway-level heme output to be monitored in vivo [16].

Heme-responsive biosensors have been developed to report changes in intracellular heme levels in microbial systems [17]. These biosensors provide a means to monitor pathway-level heme output in vivo, and reported systems include transcription factor-based regulators (HrtR, FhtR, and ChuP) [18,19] and two-component systems (ChrSA and HssRS) [20,21]. However, most transcription factor-based biosensors rely on indirect fluorescent or transcriptional readouts, which can constrain their effectiveness in large-scale, high-throughput screening [22]. By contrast, the ChrSA two-component system from C. glutamicum has been characterized as a native heme-responsive module. Keppel et al. [23] showed that the sensor kinase ChrS directly responds to heme and transduces this signal into transcriptional regulation, establishing the molecular basis of ChrSA-mediated heme sensing. Building on this mechanistic insight, Frunzke et al. [24] demonstrated that ChrSA plays a central role in heme homeostasis by mediating cellular responses to elevated heme levels through regulation of heme detoxification and export-related genes. Taken together, the direct heme sensing, well-defined signal transduction, and native regulatory role of ChrSA make it a promising platform for the development of a growth-coupled screening strategy [25,26]. Growth-coupled evolutionary strategies have recently emerged as effective approaches for improving complex metabolic pathways. For example, Bang et al. [27] reported a growth-coupled evolutionary strategy in Saccharomyces cerevisiae that enabled the enrichment of mutants with enhanced heme production, highlighting the effectiveness of growth-coupled selection strategies for optimizing complex metabolic pathways. In C. glutamicum, the heme-responsive two-component system ChrSA has been extensively characterized as a regulatory system involved in heme homeostasis. However, its applications have mainly focused on dynamic gene regulation related to heme homeostasis and toxicity control [20]. In this context, the potential of ChrSA as a selection-based platform for probing functional states of heme biosynthetic systems has not been systematically explored.

In this study, the CPD heme biosynthetic pathway was investigated through a ChrSA-based heme biosensing approach. A heme-responsive biosensor was constructed and optimized based on the native ChrSA two-component system in C. glutamicum, enabling stable coupling of intracellular heme levels to cell growth. Using this ChrSA-based biosensor platform, random mutagenesis libraries generated by directed evolution were subjected to high-throughput, growth-coupled screening for key enzymes in the CPD pathway, resulting in enrichment of variants with improved functional performance within the native heme biosynthetic pathway context and providing initial insights into their potential structural basis. Overall, this work established ChrSA-based biosensing as a scalable growth-coupled screening platform for the identification of enzyme variants improving pathway-level heme biosynthesis performance.

Materials and methods

Media and culture conditions

Escherichia coli was cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium for molecular cloning. The liquid LB medium consisted of 10 g/L tryptone, 5 g/L yeast extract, and 10 g/L NaCl, and solid LB medium was prepared by supplementation with 20 g/L agar. C. glutamicum was cultured in brain heart infusion (BHI) medium for strain construction and preculture. Fermentations were carried out in CGIII medium containing tryptone (10 g/L), yeast extract (10 g/L), MOPS buffer (21 g/L), and NaCl (2.5 g/L), adjusted to pH 7.0. Glucose was supplied from a 50% (w/v) stock solution at inoculation, while glycine and ferrous sulfate heptahydrate were added at 8 h and 12 h after inoculation, respectively. The medium was supplemented with kanamycin at 40 mg/L for E. coli and 25 mg/L for C. glutamicum, and chloramphenicol at 10 mg/L for E. coli and 5 mg/L for C. glutamicum.

Shake-flask fermentations for heme production were carried out using the following procedure. Strains were stored at −80 °C and streaked onto BHI agar plates prior to use, followed by incubation at 30 °C. A single colony was inoculated into 5 mL of BHI liquid medium in a test tube and cultivated at 30 °C and 220 rpm for 24 h. Subsequently, 1 mL of the seed culture was transferred into a 500 mL shake flask containing 50 mL of CGIII medium supplemented with 10 g/L glucose as the initial carbon source and cultivated under the same temperature and shaking conditions. After 12 h, the culture was transferred into a fresh 500 mL shake flask containing 50 mL of CGIII medium with 20 g/L glucose, with the initial cell density adjusted to an OD600 of 0.5. Throughout cultivation, kanamycin was maintained at 25 mg/L to ensure plasmid stability.

Construction of plasmids and strains

The strains and plasmids used in this study were listed in Tables S1 and S2, and the primers used were provided in Table S3. All primers were synthesized by GENEWIZ. The plasmid pEC-XK99E was used as the backbone for biosensor construction, with gene expression driven by the constitutive promoter Ptuf. The plasmid pCDF-hrtR-tcR and the genome of C. glutamicum were used as templates for PCR amplification. DNA fragments corresponding to Ptuf, Cgl2010-Cgl2009, PhrtBA, and tcR were amplified using specific primers and assembled by overlap PCR. The resulting fusion fragments were assembled into the pEC vector via circular polymerase extension cloning (CPEC) or seamless cloning (ABclonal), yielding the plasmid pEC-ΔlacIq-Ptuf-ChrS-ChrA.

To fine-tune the expression levels of ChrS and ChrA, a combinatorial ribosome binding site (RBS) engineering strategy was employed. Three synthetic RBSs with defined translation initiation strengths (high, medium, and low) were selected from a previously characterized RBS library and combinatorially placed upstream of chrS and chrA, generating nine distinct RBS combinations. All gene pairs were driven by the strong constitutive promoter Ptuf. The sequences of RBSs and promoters were listed in Table S4, and the RBS elements were incorporated into the primers during primer design. The target gene fragments were amplified by PCR and assembled by overlap PCR. The assembled fragments were inserted into a PCR-amplified and purified plasmid backbone using pEC-ΔlacIq-Ptuf-ChrS-ChrA as the template via circular polymerase extension cloning (CPEC) or a seamless cloning kit, yielding plasmids pEC-ΔlacIq-Ptuf-H-H, pEC-ΔlacIq-Ptuf-H-M, pEC-ΔlacIq-Ptuf-H-L, pEC-ΔlacIq-Ptuf-M-H, pEC-ΔlacIq-Ptuf-M-M, pEC-ΔlacIq-Ptuf-M-L, pEC-ΔlacIq-Ptuf-L-H, pEC-ΔlacIq-Ptuf-L-M, and pEC-ΔlacIq-Ptuf-L-L. Promoter engineering was performed to further optimize biosensor performance. Using pEC-ΔlacIq-Ptuf-H-H as the template, alternative promoter variants were introduced by PCR amplification and seamless assembly with a 2 × MultiF Seamless Assembly Mix, yielding plasmids pEC-ΔlacIq-P69-H-H, pEC-ΔlacIq-P69-H-M, pEC-ΔlacIq-Ptrc-H-H, and pEC-ΔlacIq-Ptrc-H-M. The primer sequences used for plasmid construction were listed in Table S3.

Construction of mutant libraries for key enzymes in the CPD heme biosynthetic pathway

Random mutagenesis PCR was performed using the QuickMutation™ Gene Random Mutagenesis Kit. The plasmids pEC-ΔlacIq-Ptuf-HChrS-HChrA and pX-CPD were used as templates for PCR amplification. The genes hemE, hemY, hemH, and hemQ were amplified under mutagenic conditions using the QuickMutation™ kit, whereas the pEC vector backbone was amplified using Phanta SE DNA polymerase. The mutagenized gene fragments were purified and assembled with the pEC vector via circular polymerase extension cloning (CPEC) or seamless cloning (ABclonal), yielding plasmids pEC-ΔlacIq-Ptuf-HChrS-HChrA-hemE, pEC-ΔlacIq-Ptuf-HChrS-HChrA-hemY, pEC-ΔlacIq-Ptuf-HChrS-HChrA-hemH, and pEC-ΔlacIq-Ptuf-HChrS-HChrA-hemQ. A total of four independent mutant libraries were constructed for the four target genes. Recombinant plasmids were verified by sequencing (GENEWIZ) and transformed into C. glutamicum A23.

Screening of CPD pathway enzyme mutant libraries by growth-coupled selection

Mutant libraries of hemE, hemY, hemH, and hemQ were screened using a growth-coupled selection strategy based on tetracycline resistance. Transformants were plated onto solid agar medium supplemented with 30 μg/mL tetracycline and incubated at 30 °C for 48-72 h. Under these conditions, differential growth of mutants reflected differences in intracellular heme levels. After incubation, during the early stage of colony appearance, 96 well-isolated colonies with normal morphology were selected from each mutant library for subsequent screening.

Analytical methods

Cell biomass was determined by measuring the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) using a UV-visible spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). The tetracycline concentration required for activation of the heme biosensor and the corresponding heme response range were determined by plate counting. Cell cultures were diluted with sterile ddH2O, adjusted to an OD600 of 1.0, and subjected to tenfold serial dilutions (10−1 to 10−6) for colony enumeration. For intracellular heme extraction, 1 mL of fermentation broth was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of acidic acetone buffer (99% acetone and 1.6 M HCl mixed at a ratio of 95:5, v/v), vortexed for approximately 30 s, and centrifuged again under the same conditions. Iron-containing porphyrin derivatives in the resulting extracts were quantified using a fluorescence assay as described previously [28]. Heme content was further verified by HPLC analysis according to a previously established method [29], using a reversed-phase C18 column with UV detection at 400 nm.

Structural modeling, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulations

The three-dimensional structures of HemE, HemY, HemH, and HemQ were predicted using AlphaFold3 [30] with default parameters. Molecular docking between each enzyme and its corresponding substrate was then performed using the CDOCKER module. Docking poses were selected based on the following criteria: satisfaction of spatial distance constraints between the enzyme and substrate, relatively high CDOCKER [31] scores, and the absence of significant steric clashes. PyMOL was used to visualize and analyze the binding conformations of the wild-type enzyme–substrate complexes, including substrate positioning and the spatial distribution of mutation sites within the overall structures. To evaluate the potential effects of mutations on enzyme structure and enzyme-substrate interaction patterns, homology modeling was performed to construct mutant structural models using the wild-type enzyme-substrate complex as the template, with the substrate transferred from the template to the corresponding mutant models.

Molecular dynamics simulations of eight docking-derived complexes were performed with GROMACS 2025.2. Proteins were protonated and inspected in PyMOL 3.1. The protein used the Amber FF14SB force field, and ligands used GAFF2 parameters generated by Sobtop (Tian Lu, v2026.1.16). Each complex was placed in a cubic TIP3P water box with a minimum 1.0 nm solute–box edge distance, and Na+/Cl− ions were added to neutralize the system and reach 0.15 M ionic strength. The workflow was as follows: (i) 5000 steps of steepest-descent energy minimization; (ii) with 1000 kJ mol−1 nm−2 positional restraints on protein and ligand heavy atoms, a second minimization of 2500 steps steepest descent plus 2500 steps conjugate gradient to relax solvent; (iii) 100 ps NPT equilibration with restraints, using the v-rescale thermostat (298 K) and C-rescale isotropic barostat at 1 bar; LINCS for bonds, PME for electrostatics, and 1.2 nm cutoffs for van der Waals and electrostatics; (iv) 100 ns unrestrained NPT production with the same settings, except pressure coupling switched to Parrinello–Rahman, and trajectories saved every 10 ps.

Dose-response analysis of the heme biosensor

Strains were cultured in media supplemented with different concentrations of heme (0, 1, 2.5, 5, 7.5, 10, 12.5, 15, 17.5, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50, 55, 60, 70, and 80 μM). Biosensor responses were quantified by measuring cell growth (OD600). GraphPad Prism software was used to generate dose-response curves and calculate the corresponding parameters, including EC50 values, Hill coefficients, and dynamic ranges. The curves were fitted using a four-parameter logistic model.

Results and discussion

Development and functional characterization of a ChrSA-based heme-responsive biosensor

To enable phenotypic screening of heme biosynthesis, a growth-coupled heme biosensor was constructed in C. glutamicum based on the ChrSA two-component system. Because the strong optical absorption of porphyrins limits fluorescence-based readouts, a tetracycline-dependent growth phenotype was employed as the selectable output. In this design, intracellular heme levels were translated into differential growth on tetracycline-containing media (Fig. 2A). The biosensor exhibited a heme-dependent growth response, allowing discrimination between strains with different heme production capacities. The sensor plasmid pEC-ΔlacIq-Ptuf-ChrS-ChrA was introduced into C. glutamicum A23 to generate strain C1. This strain was subsequently used to determine a stringent screening condition by evaluating growth behavior under different tetracycline concentrations. At a tetracycline concentration of 10 μg/mL, no growth was observed in the absence of heme, whereas residual growth occurred at lower antibiotic concentrations. This concentration was selected as the screening threshold. Under this condition, the biosensor exhibited a clear heme-dependent growth response (Fig. 2B). No colonies were detected at heme concentrations below 10 μM, while colony numbers increased markedly between 10 and 20 μM. Further increases in heme concentration led to a pronounced decline in colony formation, likely reflecting that once the sensor activation threshold was reached, additional heme no longer enhanced tetracycline efflux protein expression, whereas excess heme exerted inhibitory effects on cell growth.Fig. 2Design and functional characterization of a ChrSA-based heme-responsive biosensor for growth-coupled screening.A: Schematic illustration of the ChrSA two-component system linking intracellular heme sensing to tetracycline resistance-dependent growth. In contrast to transcription factor-based heme sensors, which reflect intracellular heme levels indirectly through transcriptional or fluorescent outputs [18,19], the ChrSA-based system establishes a direct phenotypic coupling between heme sensing and cell survival. In this design, intracellular heme levels are translated into tetracycline-dependent growth, enabling direct plate-based selection. Accordingly, the ChrSA-tcR system functions as a growth-coupled heme-responsive biosensor. However, the relatively low activation threshold (∼10 μM) limits the ability of the sensor to resolve differences across higher heme concentration ranges, indicating that further elevation of the activation threshold is required for broader dynamic discrimination.

Distinct RBS combinations resulted in markedly different heme-dependent growth profiles. Sensor strains C2 (H-H) and C3 (H-M) exhibited a continuous, dose-dependent increase in colony formation across a heme concentration range of 20-60 μM, with no apparent saturation observed within this range and maximal colony numbers reaching approximately 1.9 × 108 at 60 μM. In contrast, sensor strains C4 (H-L) and C5–C7 (M-H, M-M, and M-L) displayed a bell-shaped response, with colony numbers increasing up to a peak at approximately 40 μM heme, followed by a decline at higher concentrations. Sensor strains C8–C10 (L-H, L-M, and L-L) reached maximal growth at 10 μM heme and were strongly inhibited at elevated heme concentrations, with peak colony numbers of approximately 1.0 × 108, nearly two-fold lower than those observed for high-strength RBS variants (Fig. 3B). These results indicate that RBS-mediated modulation of ChrS and ChrA expression shifts the biosensor activation threshold from 10 μM to ≥20 μM heme and expands the effective response window to concentrations as high as 60 μM, leading to the selection of the HChrS-HChrA and HChrS-MChrA RBS combinations for subsequent experiments.

Alternative promoters were examined to assess their impact on biosensor performance. Using the Ptuf-driven sensor strains C2 (Ptuf-H-H) and C3 (Ptuf-H-M) as references, Ptuf was replaced with P69 or Ptrc to generate sensor strains C11–C14 (Fig. 3C). Sensor strains C11 (P69-H-H) and C12 (P69-H-M) exhibited strong background activation, as substantial colony formation occurred on tetracycline-containing plates even in the absence of heme. Colony numbers peaked at approximately 8 × 108 at 40 μM heme and declined at higher concentrations, indicating a loss of heme-dependent growth control. In contrast, Ptrc-driven strains C13 (Ptrc-H-H) and C14 (Ptrc-H-M) showed growth activation only at high heme concentrations (40-60 μM), reflecting an excessively elevated activation threshold (Fig. 3D). By comparison, the Ptuf-driven strain C2 (Ptuf-H-H) displayed a well-defined heme-dependent growth response over the 20–60 μM range, with effective background suppression and a compatible activation threshold, and was therefore selected as the final biosensor strain for subsequent plate-based screening.

Notably, the optimized ChrSA biosensor exhibited an expanded effective response range of 20-60 μM heme, enabling growth-based discrimination at relatively high intracellular heme levels. This observation indicates that, in addition to its native regulatory role, the previously characterized ChrSA module can be adapted for growth-coupled screening applications through optimization of the signaling architecture. In the present study, tuning the expression levels of ChrS and ChrA improved the response characteristics of the system and broadened its effective sensing range, thereby increasing its suitability for screening strains with enhanced heme biosynthesis.

To quantitatively evaluate biosensor performance, dose-response analysis was performed for the control biosensor C1 and the optimized biosensor C2 across a gradient of heme concentrations. The dose-response curves were fitted using a four-parameter logistic model, enabling determination of key parameters including EC50, Hill coefficient, and dynamic range (Fig. S1). Compared with the initial sensor configuration, the optimized biosensor exhibited an increase in dynamic range from 6.59-fold to 12.27-fold, a shift of the EC50 from 13.36 μM to 38.28 μM, and a decrease in the Hill coefficient from 5.635 to 3.887. These changes indicate that the optimized biosensor produces a larger signal difference across heme concentrations, shifts the response toward higher heme levels, and displays a more gradual response transition, thereby improving discrimination of intracellular heme levels under elevated heme conditions. By comparison, Hanna et al. [33] reported a genetically encoded ratiometric heme sensor with a heme binding constant of approximately 3–10 nM and the ability to detect cytosolic free heme levels around 20–40 nM. In contrast, the optimized ChrSA biosensor developed in this study supports stable growth-based selection in the 20–60 μM heme range. The expanded response range achieved in this study enables stable growth-coupled selection under elevated heme conditions, making the system particularly suitable for screening strains with enhanced heme production.

Identification of functionally improved heme biosynthetic enzyme variants using the ChrSA-based growth-coupled heme biosensor

The CPD pathway comprises tightly coupled enzymatic steps, such that improvement of a single enzyme may merely shift the bottleneck rather than increase overall flux. To address this constraint, directed evolution was applied to four key CPD pathway enzymes (HemE, HemY, HemH, and HemQ), using the ChrSA-based growth-coupled heme biosensor as a selection platform. For each enzyme, independent plasmid-based random mutagenesis libraries (M1–M4) were constructed and transformed into the host strain A23 to generate the corresponding strain libraries (F1–F4). Biosensor-guided selection on tetracycline-containing plates enriched variants exhibiting improved growth phenotypes associated with enhanced heme production. From each strain library, 96 colonies were selected based on growth performance and subjected to an initial round of shake-flask fermentation for heme production (Fig. S2A–D). Based on the primary fermentation results, the top six strains ranked by heme production from each library were advanced to secondary evaluation. This rescreening identified the top-performing variants E96, Y96, H96, and Q96, which showed increases in heme production of 47.4%, 38.6%, 45.4%, and 56.9%, respectively, relative to their parental controls (Fig. S3A–D).

To assess the effects of the selected mutations under chromosomal expression, the native CPD pathway genes were sequentially replaced with their evolved alleles in C. glutamicum, generating strains L1–L4. Specifically, L1 carries hemEL42F/S144P/V229A, L2 carries hemEL42F/S144P/V229A and hemYI28V, L3 carries hemEL42F/S144P/V229A, hemYI28V, and hemHH23L/R89G/D169G/E181V/S272T, and L4 carries hemEL42F/S144P/V229A, hemYI28V, hemHH23L/R89G/D169G/E181V/S272T, and hemQK51E/K115R, with strain L0 carrying the upstream hemBCD module serving as the reference. Stepwise allelic replacement resulted in a progressive increase in heme production from L1 to L4. Compared with L0, strain L4 showed a 38.9% increase in heme production, reaching a titer of 308.7 mg/L as quantified by fluorescence analysis (Fig. 4A–B). Although chromosomal allelic replacement resulted in a clear improvement in heme production relative to the reference strain, the magnitude of enhancement was lower than that observed during plasmid-based screening. This outcome was expected, as the evolved alleles were evaluated under a single-copy, endogenously regulated context without additional promoters or gene dosage amplification, thereby preserving the native regulatory architecture of the CPD pathway. Nevertheless, chromosomal implementation provides superior genetic stability [34] and therefore represents a more practical foundation for further strain optimization and engineering of complex multi-enzyme pathways.

Future improvements in heme production will be pursued through further pathway-level engineering. In particular, coordinated expression of CPD pathway genes through promoter and RBS engineering may help achieve balanced expression among pathway enzymes. In addition, increasing precursor supply, especially enhancing intracellular 5-ALA availability, may further strengthen carbon flux toward heme biosynthesis. Strategies aimed at reducing intracellular tetrapyrrole accumulation, such as improving heme export or detoxification systems, may also help alleviate potential toxicity associated with heme overproduction. Finally, relieving potential feedback regulation within the tetrapyrrole pathway may further increase pathway flux and heme accumulation. Taken together, these pathway-level strategies may provide practical directions for further improving heme production.

Computer simulations reveal potential effects of the beneficial mutants

To investigate the structural basis of the beneficial mutations, we carried out a systematic computational analysis combining protein structure modeling and molecular dynamics simulations. RMSD profiles provided dynamic information for comparing the structural fluctuation behaviors of the wild-type and mutant proteins during the simulation process (Fig. S5). In HemEL42F/S144P/V229A, the L42F mutation is adjacent to the substrate-binding pocket (without participating in catalysis or substrate interaction) and may influence the local pocket environment through enhanced hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 5Ab). The S144P mutation introduces a proline residue, which may restrict the flexibility of the polypeptide backbone and enhance the rigidity of local conformation (Fig. 5Ac). The V229A mutation reduces the side-chain volume, which may alleviate local steric hindrance and affect local packing behavior (Fig. 5Ad).

In HemYI28V, the I28V mutation is located at the structural interface region of the protein. It may enhance local hydrophobic interactions to improve subunit assembly, thereby contributing to the overall structural stability of the enzyme (Fig. 5Bb).

In HemHH23L/R89G/D169G/E181V/S272T, the H23L, D169G, and E181V mutations are distributed on the protein surface, corresponding to the replacement of large polar amino acid residues with small nonpolar ones. These mutations may enhance the conformational stability of local regions by relieving steric hindrance and promoting hydrophobic packing, thereby providing a stable microenvironment for the catalytic center (Fig. 5Cb, d and e). Notably, although the E181V substitution abolished the hydrogen bond with K261, it appeared to alter local interactions with the surrounding secondary-structure elements (Fig. 5Ce). Similarly, although the R89G substitution abolished the hydrogen-bond interaction with D130, it appeared to enlarge the local space near the substrate-binding pocket and indirectly affect the microenvironment for substrate accommodation (Fig. 5Cc). The hydroxyl group of T272 may form weak hydrogen-bonding interactions with the substrate, which indirectly stabilizes the substrate conformation. In addition, the methyl branch of threonine can fill the local pocket space and further enhance the structural stability of the pocket (Fig. 5Cf).

In HemQK51E/K115R, the K51E mutation is located in the surface-exposed region of the protein. The charge reversal from lysine to glutamate may induce slight rotation or displacement of the local α-helix, thereby affecting the catalytic activity through long-range conformational effects (Fig. 5Db). In contrast, the K115R mutation adjacent to the substrate-binding pocket can strengthen local hydrogen-bond interactions to stabilize the pocket architecture, thereby influencing the enzyme-substrate microenvironment (Fig. 5Dc).

Together, the combined structural modeling and molecular dynamics analyses suggest that these beneficial non-catalytic mutations may contribute to improved pathway performance by altering local interaction patterns, conformational behavior, and structural dynamics, rather than through direct modification of catalytic residues. Notably, the beneficial substitutions identified in HemE, HemY, HemH, and HemQ have not been described in prior CPD pathway engineering studies and provide useful sequence-level targets for subsequent rational refinement and combinatorial pathway optimization to further improve heme production.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a growth-coupled screening strategy in C. glutamicum based on the native ChrSA heme-responsive system. This strategy enabled intracellular heme accumulation to be translated into a selectable growth phenotype, thereby allowing selection-based screening of CPD pathway enzyme variants. Using this approach, improved variants of multiple CPD pathway enzymes were identified and validated after chromosomal allelic replacement, confirming that their improved pathway performance was retained under native single-copy regulatory control. Notably, this work also revealed previously underappreciated functionally beneficial non-catalytic mutations across CPD pathway enzymes, suggesting that enzyme stability and conformational modulation may contribute to heme biosynthesis. In addition, by coupling intracellular heme levels to tetracycline-dependent growth and tuning the expression levels of ChrS and ChrA, this study further improved the applicability of the ChrSA module for growth-coupled pathway screening under elevated heme conditions. Together, these findings expand the application of ChrSA-based heme sensing and provide a practical systems-level framework for discovering and validating enzyme variants toward improved microbial heme production.
