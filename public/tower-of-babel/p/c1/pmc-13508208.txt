It depends on where you stand on screening: health professionals’ awareness, perspectives, and approaches to overdiagnosis in cancer screening - a qualitative interview study
Authors: Veerle Piessens, Emelien Lauwerier, John Brandt Brodersen, Melissa Ceuterick, Alexandra Brandt Ryborg Jønsson, Ann Van den Bruel, Stefan Heytens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOverdiagnosis is a major challenge in screening, leading to healthy individuals being diagnosed and treated for conditions that would never have caused harm. While evidence on overdiagnosis is growing, little is known about how health professionals (HPs) understand and engage with this phenomenon in practice. This study examines health professionals’ awareness, understanding and approaches to overdiagnosis in breast, lung, and prostate cancer screening.MethodsThis is a qualitative study with semi-structured interviews, using Positioning Theory as an analytical lens, among HPs involved in breast, prostate, or lung cancer screening in Flanders, Belgium, both in public health settings and in individual clinical care. 34 HPs, of whom 21 were purposively invited, because of their professional role, research background or public advocacy related to screening. In addition, 13 clinicians were randomly recruited from a list of registered physicians (GPs, gynaecologists, urologists and pneumologists).ResultsFew participants spontaneously mentioned overdiagnosis as a harm of screening, although most recognised it, primarily in relation to prostate cancer screening. Some HPs demonstrated a thorough understanding of overdiagnosis, but did not necessarily perceive it as a relevant harm. HPs’ perceptions and corresponding approaches depended primarily on their overall position regarding screening. The participating HPs articulated three different storylines to motivate their positions: (1) routine screening, where HPs accept screening as self-evident and are largely unaware of overdiagnosis, (2) determined screening, where HPs emphasise the evidence-based benefits of screening and acknowledge overdiagnosis, which they try to mitigate, but also de-problematise, and (3) cautious screening, where HPs question the evidence base and benefits of screening and consider overdiagnosis one of the reasons for adopting a reluctant position towards screening.ConclusionsParticipants rarely identify overdiagnosis as a harm of screening, despite being (passively) aware of it. HPs’ broader position on screening shapes how they understand, interpret, and respond to overdiagnosis and is primarily based on their knowledge of screening benefits, and influenced by professional contexts and personal characteristics. Regardless of their level of enthusiasm for screening, HPs with a more comprehensive understanding of its benefits and harms tend to adopt more deliberate and carefully considered - albeit divergent- approaches to overdiagnosis.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-15194-7.Keywords: Overdiagnosis, Screening, Harm, Health professionals, Public health, Professional responsibility

Background

Overdiagnosis is an increasing challenge in contemporary healthcare and society. It is defined as “turning people into patients unnecessarily, by identifying problems that were never going to cause harm or by medicalising ordinary life experiences through expanded definitions of diseases“ [1, 2]. Screening is a major driver of overdiagnosis, but it also occurs in routine clinical care [3–5], through commercial drivers [6], and in response to societal challenges [7, 8]. Overdiagnosis in screening is defined as detecting abnormalities that would never have caused symptoms, suffering or death if left undetected. It warrants particular attention because screening targets healthy people with the intention of improving their future health. Screening can have life-altering effects, for better or for worse. It can enable early detection and potentially life-saving or less invasive treatment [9, 10]. However, it may also detect conditions that would never have affected a person’s health, resulting in distress, overtreatment, financial repercussions, and opportunity costs without corresponding benefit [11]. Overdiagnosis, therefore, undermines the safety of screening and represents one of its most serious downsides for individuals, society, equity, and the environment [12, 13].

Although overdiagnosis affects individuals, it can only be estimated at the population level. Neither individuals nor their treating physicians can know whether the screen-detected condition would have become symptomatic or instead represents overdiagnosis [14]. Due to this lack of lived experience, overdiagnosis has long been underrecognised, compared with more immediate harms, such as false positives, anxiety, and invasive diagnostic procedures.

Overdiagnosis likely occurs across all forms of screening, but is best documented, based on evidence from randomised trials (RCTs), for breast, prostate and lung cancer screening [15–20]. Quantifying overdiagnosis is challenging, requires a rigorous methodological approach [15, 21, 22], and yields estimates that vary widely and are often graded with a low level of certainty [19, 23]. Moreover, estimates from RCTs reflect the screening practices of the era in which the study was conducted and may no longer be valid in contemporary health systems, where changes in screening and diagnostic practices may have reduced [24] or increased [25] the risk of overdiagnosis. Based on the best available evidence, recent systematic reviews estimate that mammography screening leads to approximately 4 women with overdiagnosed breast cancer per 1000 women screened [23], lung cancer screening to about 7 overdiagnosed lung cancers per 1000 individuals screened [18], and prostate cancer screening to around 18 men with an overdiagnosed prostate cancer per 1000 men screened [26]. Most high-income countries organise breast cancer screening, and some are exploring organised CT screening for lung cancer. Prostate screening occurs mainly opportunistically, with strong advocacy to organise it programmatically. In 2023, the European Commission recommended that member states explore programmatic screening for lung and prostate cancer [27].

Health professionals play a critical role in addressing overdiagnosis. They often initiate or promote screening and have a professional obligation to balance benefits and harms [28, 29]. The public trusts health professionals and authorities to recommend screening only when it is beneficial. Although public awareness of overdiagnosis is low, people value this information and expect it to be discussed when making screening decisions [30–33]. International bioethical treaties and legislation further support the public’s right to informed consent, which requires adequate information about the risks of overdiagnosis before undergoing screening [34, 35].

Despite this, little is known about HPs’ awareness of and perspectives on overdiagnosis. A recent systematic review identified no studies specifically examining HPs’ awareness or understanding, and among HPs who appeared familiar with the phenomenon, views differed regarding its relevance and whether screening candidates should be informed about it [36]. Understanding whether HPs recognise overdiagnosis, how they interpret it, and whether it affects their screening practices is therefore essential to support safe care and informed decision-making.

Therefore, this study examines whether and how health professionals understand overdiagnosis and how this shapes their screening practices in breast, lung, and prostate cancer screening.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted an interview study with HPs involved in breast, lung, or prostate cancer screening in Flanders, Belgium. Flanders has a publicly organised mammography programme that invites women aged 50–69 years to undergo biannual screening. Women may also enter the programme via referral from their general practitioner (GP) or gynaecologist. In addition, opportunistic screening occurs, for example, with alternative tests (e.g. ultrasound) and among women outside the programme’s age range. Prostate cancer screening occurs only opportunistically, although advocacy for an organised programme is ongoing. Lung cancer screening is not routinely offered; however, in 2025, a pilot low-dose CT screening programme was launched as a feasibility study [37]. GPs are expected to encourage women to participate in mammography screening, but no financial incentives, such as pay-for-performance schemes, are in place.

We broadly explored HPs’ views on the potential harms of screening, and this paper reports findings specifically related to overdiagnosis.

Participants and recruitment

We invited HPs working in public health and in individual clinical settings. We sought to explore understanding and perspectives within a broad sample of professionals involved in screening, across different professional roles and responsibilities. Most participants were therefore recruited purposively based on their professional involvement in screening or their position in the public screening debate. Knowing that the screening debate is highly polarised [38], we explicitly sought to include HPs who advocate for screening as well as those known for their critical stance. In addition, we recruited a random sample from the list of all physicians licensed to practice medicine in Belgium. This list was retrieved from the website of the national health insurance organisation in March 2023. These physicians were screened against predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria (Table 1) and invited consecutively.

We focused on HPs with discretion to offer screening or who were actively engaged in the screening debate, excluding those who solely perform technical screening procedures (e.g. radiographers).

Participants chose the interview timing, location, and format (in person or online) and received a gift certificate as a token of appreciation. In the invitation email, we described the study as exploring HPs’ perspectives on potential harms of screening, but did not mention our specific interest in overdiagnosis.

Data collection

Interviews were conducted between 2023 and 2025, in person, via video call, or by telephone and were audio-recorded. VP conducted all interviews using a pilot-tested topic guide that was adapted iteratively (Supplementary file 1). Interviews began by exploring participants’ professional involvement in screening and subsequently addressed perceived harms. When participants spontaneously mentioned overdiagnosis, we explored their understanding, perceived consequences, and professional responses. When overdiagnosis was not raised spontaneously, the topic was introduced explicitly to assess familiarity with the concept. Interviews concluded with questions about when participants first learned about overdiagnosis and what had influenced their position on screening. Interviews lasted 25 to 103 min (mean 58 min).

Data analysis

Interviews were transcribed using Amberscript. VP reviewed all transcripts, corrected errors, and added paraverbal cues, such as hesitations or laughter.

We adopted an inductive analytical approach, guided by the research question and following established qualitative research principles, emphasising iterativity, constant comparison, and reflexivity [39, 40].

VP and EL independently read all transcripts. VP produced narrative reports and conceptual schemes, which were discussed with EL to explore alternative interpretations and challenge assumptions. Data collection and analysis proceeded iteratively, and although data sufficiency had already been reached, we continued interviewing to ensure the inclusion of potentially divergent perspectives.

Initial codes were organised into a coding tree using MAXQDA-software. VP recoded all transcripts, refining codes through renaming, merging, and adding new codes. These codes served as building blocks for constructing storylines and positions, which were repeatedly discussed among the two lead researchers (VP and EL) and the author group. This process involved sustained engagement with the data, ongoing verification, repeated analytical discussions, and peer debriefing with social researchers who were not directly involved in the analysis. Throughout all stages of the study, VP kept a research diary, the contents of which were regularly brought into the analytical discussions with the research team. Particular attention was given to identifying and critically examining differences in subjective interpretations and potential biases.

During the analysis, we noticed that participants’ perceptions of overdiagnosis could only be understood in light of their broader positioning on screening. We therefore situated them along two analytical axes: knowledge about overdiagnosis and enthusiasm for screening. We report our findings as storylines that participants hold regarding overdiagnosis and screening. We borrow the concept of storylines from Positioning Theory, a discursive framework used in health sociology and psychology [41]. Through storylines, participants reveal how they position themselves and others in relation to an often dilemmatic, value-laden topic. Positions help explain why people choose to act as they do in given circumstances by relating these actions to how they perceive their duties, obligations, and rights.

Researchers’ positionality

(VP) I graduated as a GP in 2000 and have been working in community health centres since then. I also teach a prevention curriculum to medical students and GP residents, and developed an interest in the ethical implications of prevention and screening. I regularly serve as an expert on advisory groups or expert committees focused on screening and prevention.

(EL) I obtained a Doctorate in Clinical Psychology in 2013 and have since worked in academia, focusing on health and illness, including screening promotion. I also maintain a clinical practice, treating patients with psychological suffering due to illness. I consider myself relatively external to the research topic, which allowed me to approach participants’ accounts and moral dilemmas with curiosity and an open, non-judgmental stance.

Awareness and positioning

Only a few participants, all from the purposive sample, spontaneously raised overdiagnosis as a harm of screening. When prompted, however, most HPs recognised the term and associated it primarily with prostate cancer, often citing the well-known phrase that “most men die with prostate cancer, not from it”.

In articulating their views, participants developed distinct storylines about their position towards screening and their approach to overdiagnosis: (1) routine screening, in which HPs see screening as self-evident and struggle to position overdiagnosis, (2) determined screening, in which HPs advocate screening, de-problematise overdiagnosis, and try to mitigate it, and (3) cautious screening, in which HPs take a step back from screening and raise awareness about overdiagnosis (Fig. 1; Table 3).

This positioning was dynamic, and some HPs adopted different positions depending on the screening type. For example, some were determined about lung cancer screening but more cautious about prostate screening, or held a routine screening position regarding breast screening. Figure 1 represents HPs’ positioning regarding the screening type that was most prominent during the interview.

Routine screening

HPs who relied on a routine screening storyline did not mention overdiagnosis as a potential harm. Screening was self-evident to them, largely unquestioned, and perceived as a core professional practice grounded in guidelines and recommendations from health authorities.

Most were familiar with the term ‘overdiagnosis’, but held varying conceptual understandings. Some conflated it with overtesting, false positives, overcalling benign precursor lesions, or excessive downstream diagnostic procedures. (See supplementary file 2 for illustrating quotes). Others described overdiagnosis as a cancer that would not be lethal if left untreated or for which treatment does not affect prognosis. While these latter definitions acknowledge the lack of therapeutic or prognostic benefit, they leave ambiguity as to whether the cancer would have become symptomatic without screening. After clarification, all recognised the phenomenon of “unnecessarily turning healthy people into patients”, and some spontaneously reflected on the suffering this may cause. However, they primarily attributed it to screening the elderly and were not necessarily aware that it also occurs in the screening they endorsed.

The interview appeared to be one of the first occasions to prompt explicit reflection on overdiagnosis. Many struggled to find their position, and their accounts illustrate this on several levels. First, HPs often reverted to their initial misunderstanding of the concept, even though earlier in the interview, there had been recognition and agreement regarding the study definition. Second, they trusted that health authorities would recommend screening only when the benefits clearly outweigh the harms, and found it difficult to reconcile overdiagnosis with existing recommendations. Third, they struggled to see how acknowledging overdiagnosis could be translated into practice. Most did not question screening and focused on decision-making after diagnosing a screen-detected cancer and emphasised the uncertainty – or even impossibility - of identifying which patients are overdiagnosed.

Because in the unnecessary diagnosis or the overdiagnosis, there is actually an unknowability in that definition, that you as an individual would never know whether it would have been unnecessary or not. (PH5, M (male), Sr (senior (= at least 15 years of professional experience)))

This made presumed actions, such as informing patients that their cancer might be overdiagnosed or withholding treatment, morally unacceptable.

Because suppose you’re sitting there, and you’ve been diagnosed with breast cancer, and I say – but I can’t really say that, because I don’t know how it will evolve – but suppose I say, “It may be that you shouldn’t have known, because you would have died within x amount of time anyway. Well, that’s a bit strange, isn’t it? (GP3, M, Jr (junior (= less than 15 years of professional experience)))

I’m not going to say… “if they find cancer, then yes, it could be nothing, so just leave it”. Come on, I’m not going to say that. If it’s something malignant, something suspicious, then it has to go! (GYN1, F (female), Sr)

Some HPs briefly considered forgoing screening to avoid overdiagnosis, but immediately rejected this option. Clinicians, in particular, saw screening as a professional duty, which also provided them with a sense of security. They described anticipated guilt if a patient were to present with advanced cancer that they might have missed by not offering screening. Early detection, ideally pre-symptomatic, was central to how they conceptualised their professional obligations to their patients.

Yes, but in your daily practice, I always find it difficult when that patient is sitting in front of you. The man you didn’t offer [prostate] screening to, and who then gets it anyway. And then you’re left with not having detected it in time, or having it… Yes, and I think that as a GP, you just don’t want that… I would also feel a bit like I had failed as a doctor, thinking: “why didn’t I pick up on that earlier? … knowing that there are reliable tests … and that they exist. (GP7, F, Sr)

If a patient made a well-informed choice to decline screening, HPs stated that they would respect the patient’s preferences. However, many reported that this would still feel uncomfortable. Some anticipated future repercussions, such as complaints or criticism, and would therefore note the patient’s refusal of the screening offer in the medical record.

Yes, in that case, I can support that, if I am sure that they understood it correctly […]. Yes, yes, but I find that … it still feels safer if they do it, you know … when there is something… or just that, if there were to be something, it could be picked up quickly. (GP4, F, Jr)

No, because every patient has their own… temperament and their… patients decide about their own bodies, right? So yes … I accept that, right? Yes, I’ll say Okay, we did suggest it. I’ll write that down, for now”… If lung cancer is diagnosed two years later, they shouldn’t come, uh, hold me liable for that, because I did suggest it. (PNEU4, M, Sr)

Ultimately, these HPs saw no actionable options to translate their awareness of overdiagnosis into practice. They restated their commitment to screening or steered the discussion to other topics with which they seemed more comfortable.

Clinicians provided no information about overdiagnosis during pre-screening discussions and were only vaguely aware of existing informational materials and decision aids. Public health practitioners stated that overdiagnosis is adequately addressed in the information supporting the breast screening programme.

Determined screening

HPs expressing a determined screening storyline were aware that overdiagnosis might occur, yet remained convinced that screening is beneficial.

Many justified their position primarily through experiential knowledge. They spoke of patients with painfully advanced cancer, for whom no or only burdensome treatments remained. They described patients’ disappointment at not being screened and their own feelings of powerlessness. They were convinced that screening could have prevented these outcomes, and referred to the scientific evidence demonstrating screening benefits to strengthen their argument. Others adopted a population perspective, highlighting evidence of population-level benefits, while acknowledging that only a few individuals would benefit.

I would say … in a population … breast cancer screening is good for the population, but not always for the individual patient. The … the … overdiagnosis, and the chance that we detect breast cancer is greater than if we didn’t screen, and those are often tumours that would never cause you any problems in your life. (RX1, M, Sr)

HPs who positioned themselves as determined screeners generally demonstrated a good understanding of overdiagnosis but did not consistently perceive it as a relevant harm in the screening they advocate. Their arguments varied by screening context.

HPs advocating lung cancer screening argued that overdiagnosis occurs mainly when screening individuals with limited life expectancy and stressed the professional responsibility to discontinue screening when age or co-morbidities make benefit unlikely. They seemed to perceive “competing causes of death” as the main mechanism causing overdiagnosis. Some explicitly denied the existence of indolent lung cancers.

unlike prostate cancer, you die with prostate cancer but not from prostate cancer. I think that’s still the rule. So I think it’s wrong, a delusion, to say that you can die with lung cancer, but not from lung cancer (PNEU3, M, Sr)

At the same time, when presented with the high overdiagnosis estimates observed in RCTs, they attributed this to ground-glass opacities. As clinicians, they were familiar with these lesions, often incidentally detected on clinically indicated scans and described them as slowly progressing and occasionally invasive, but seemed not to regard them as true lung cancers.

In breast cancer, HPs justified their position using numerical and value-based arguments. Some cited data indicating that more women avoid death from breast cancer than risk overdiagnosis, however acknowledged that some women will benefit and others will be harmed.

So if you split that into age categories, between fifty and sixty. I’ve done that exercise before; I include that in my teaching, then you save four lives, and you have three overdiagnoses. That’s the balance. But they are different people, you know. (GYN4, M, Sr)

Others acknowledged that more women may be overdiagnosed, but valued saving a single life over several overdiagnoses.

In the case of breast cancer, we believe it is worthwhile. However, it is indeed the case that five women are diagnosed with breast cancer for no reason, are treated, suffer… for one… and yes, that is the solidarity we need at the population level to save that one woman’s life. (RX1, M, Sr)

Urologists acknowledged that overdiagnosis has historically been a problem in prostate cancer, but they now have established pathways to limit it. They attributed ongoing overdiagnosis to GPs who continue to screen older men despite recommendations against it. Overdiagnosis itself was not considered problematic as long as it did not lead to overtreatment.

While they frequently expressed empathy toward patients suffering from advanced cancer, they mainly maintained a detached stance toward overdiagnosis, sometimes describing it as “collateral damage”. With few exceptions, they did not articulate concern that unnecessary diagnoses cause significant suffering.

… it is an intervention that, let’s say, causes minor morbidity and mortality in the long term. Nowadays, with uniportal VATS surgery, it is even possible for people to go home after just one night. (PNEU3, M, Sr)

she got that label and underwent that treatment, etc., all for nothing. Yes, yes. That’s unfortunate, but she will survive. (RX1, M, Jr)

Unlike routine screeners, HPs in this group had already reflected on overdiagnosis and incorporated it into their overall evaluation of screening. They regretted that overdiagnosis exists but accepted it as an inevitable consequence of trying to save lives: “Of course, overdiagnosis exists, … but then again, there is no alternative, it’s either that, or waiting for men to present with metastases and fractures” (URO3, M, Jr).

Overall, they viewed overdiagnosis as far less relevant than the benefits of screening, and their accounts show several discursive responses that de-problematise overdiagnosis and reconcile it with a continued commitment to screening. (Table 4)

Overview of de-problematising discursive responses used in the determined screening storyline

HPs in this group described three strategies to mitigate overdiagnosis: (1) harm reduction through organised screening, (2) overdiagnosis-limiting diagnostic strategies, and (3) avoiding overtreatment.

First, they advocated organised screening as a harm-reduction strategy. Screening was seen as a reality. Leaving it to individual practitioners increases the risk of inappropriate practices, such as screening people with limited life expectancy or using unsuitable tests (e.g. breast ultrasound). Some argued that maintaining breast screening programmes would keep the risk of overdiagnosis limited. Urologists similarly advocated for organised screening, arguing that removing prostate screening from GPs could reduce overdiagnosis while improving effectiveness and equity.

Second, they described diagnostic strategies to prevent detection of inconsequential abnormalities (e.g., not introducing breast tomosynthesis) or to limit progression from detection to diagnosis (e.g. using MRI to reduce prostate biopsies).

Finally, urologists promoted active surveillance to avoid overtreatment. They accepted the additional workload of monitoring and counselling as the cost of enabling earlier detection of aggressive disease. Their accounts reflect a deep personal engagement to reassure men that AS is safe and to alleviate anxiety.

… and [these men] then go home and they don’t care. You understand? Because I, because a urologist must be educated that you have to take this [low-grade prostate cancer] lightly and that you have to offer this lightness to your patient. (URO5, F, Jr)

In their experience, anxiety only peaks around the follow-up visits, but otherwise remains limited. However, they acknowledged that some men cannot live with this anxiety and eventually opt for active treatment. They saw it as the urologists’ responsibility to protect men from overtreatment, and admitted that historically, the urological community has been responsible for substantial overtreatment. Even today, in their view, urologists sometimes turn to prostatectomy too readily. They suggested that this is partly driven by urologists’ own uncertainty and fear that the cancer might progress, as they have all encountered cases of low-grade prostate cancers that nevertheless metastasised under AS.

Active monitoring was mentioned briefly in discussions of slow-growing lung cancers, whereas in breast cancer, current treatment practices do not include active surveillance.

Among HPs who personally initiate screening, only a few reported addressing overdiagnosis in the pre-screening information they provide.

Cautious screening

Some HPs held a cautious position towards screening. Clinicians described a shift from a previously uncritical or positive stance toward screening to a more critical one, prompted by learning about its limited effectiveness, weak evidence base, and associated harms. This often led to disillusionment with their professional community. For many, these insights were enabled by opportunities to take an outsider’s perspective on their community’s pro-screening discourse.

I actually had the feeling that I had been a bit duped, that’s how I felt. That, as a medical student, I had never really been taught about it properly, and that, as a GP, I had been pushed into a specific pattern and that I had fallen for it too, and had actually copied it rather uncritically, and then by reading more deeply about it and researching it, I felt as if a whole Pandora’s box had opened up, something I knew nothing about, and that was actually quite a significant moment. (GP2, F, Sr)

Most spontaneously identified overdiagnosis as a harm and attended to the psychological and existential suffering caused by a cancer diagnosis itself, regardless of treatment.

… because it’s not clear whether you’re the one who has been overdiagnosed or if you have a genuine diagnosis. So for those patients, it’s the same thing, and they all start to feel like patients with everything that goes with it: the treatments, the psychological stress, the possible aftermath that can last for years. (GP2, F, Sr)

They perceived unnecessary treatment as an additional burden. One GP spoke with indignation about treatment consequences: “… when I see the atrocities that happen to women with DCIS, for example, which is often detected during screening […] they systematically label it as breast cancer and then subject them to horrible treatment” (GP5, M, Sr). Some HPs disagreed with framing active surveillance as a solution but rather as a source of anxiety and further overtreatment: “watchful waiting is also stressful waiting” (PH1, M, Sr).

These HPs also highlighted systemic impacts because overdiagnosis diverts healthcare resources and complicates care for genuinely ill patients. Some noted that overdiagnosis substantially affects cost-effectiveness analyses (CEAs) and warned that many CEAs fail to account for it.

One of the most important factors was overdiagnosis. That was also a very influential variable for the outcomes, economically speaking, […] Our colleague had compiled an overview of the literature. Twenty-three previous recent evaluations and more than half do not even take this into account. So, uh. The fact that even people who specialise in researching this also neglect it. I found that quite striking, and it was 24% in one analysis, 9% in another, and 38% in another. This was not about two, three, or 4%. It was quite significant. (PH6&7, F&M, Sr)

Some participants discussed how overdiagnosis also affects them as clinicians. Not only does it increase their workload, but active surveillance also places significant stress on the treating physician due to the uncertainty and unpredictability of a low-grade cancer. And more subtly, overdiagnosis can drive an invisible wedge between the HP and the patient with a screen-detected cancer. HPs are aware that this might as well be an overdiagnosed cancer, but feel they cannot share this with their patient.

Yes, because I find that very difficult. If you have a patient who has participated in [breast cancer] screening and something is found and treatment is started, and that’s someone where you say: yes, statistically, this could be overdiagnosis, but you can’t do anything with it. There’s nothing you can do with that. (GP2, F, Sr)

Cautious screeners described taking a step back from promoting screening. Depending on their role and the screening context, they may recommend against organising the screening as a programme or stop proactively offering screening to patients. However, participants indicated that if people requested screening, they would support them in making an informed choice. Some emphasised their obligation to avoid harm, while others prioritised facilitating an informed choice in what they considered a preference-sensitive intervention. Beyond clinical practice, these HPs raised awareness about overdiagnosis by teaching medical students and residents, providing transparent analyses to policymakers, and speaking out publicly. Some acknowledged a duality in their position: outspoken and critical in scientific or public roles, but more cautious in clinical encounters. They reported that they would never refuse prostate screening or advise against mammography, and showed a greater tendency to proactively inform men about the risks of prostate cancer screening, sometimes even discouraging them, whereas for breast cancer screening, they took a more reactive approach.

At the same time, participants acknowledged that screening can, in some cases, prevent metastatic disease and premature death. Still, they viewed these benefits as limited and noted that aggressive tumours will often escape early detection.

HPs who challenged dominant pro-screening discourses reported hostile reactions and accusations of being indifferent toward patients dying from cancer.

But he said: ‘Why are you so against it?’. I said: ‘We’re not against it, but you have to be a bit critical’. But [famous screening advocate], for example, and a few other doctors dare to say, ‘Yes, but you’re actually murderers, aren’t you?’ (PH2, F, Sr)

They were aware that their position ran counter to mainstream discourse and frequently drew on scientific evidence, which served as an anchor to legitimise their stance. While struggling to understand how colleagues could continue to advocate for screening given the current state of the evidence, they remained attentive to emerging research and acknowledged that future findings might prompt them to reconsider their position.

Discussion

Principal findings

Health professionals rarely mentioned overdiagnosis as a harm of cancer screening. Although most HPs were familiar with the term, many initially conflated it with other screening issues, such as overtesting or excessive downstream diagnostic procedures. Some HPs demonstrated a thorough understanding of the phenomenon; however, this did not necessarily translate into perceiving it as a relevant harm. How HPs perceived and responded to overdiagnosis depended not only on their knowledge but equally on their broader positioning toward screening.

Meaning and implication

In this study, participants adopted three distinct storylines to express their position: routine, determined, and cautious screening, each reflecting different ways of balancing professional responsibility, knowledge, and moral commitments.

Routine screening functions as a default position, marked by an enthusiasm for screening that is socially embedded [30, 42–48], reinforced by public health messaging, cultivated during medical training [49], and aligned with professional norms. These HPs rely on screening guidelines, place trust in organised screening programmes, and rarely consider the possibility of overdiagnosis. In contrast, both determined and cautious screening involve more explicit engagement with evidence on benefits and harms, including overdiagnosis, but lead to divergent assessments of its significance and how it should be addressed in practice. HPs adopting a determined screening storyline share the enthusiasm but rely on a more explicit, evidence-informed rationale to advocate screening and to manage its harms. Cautious screeners, by contrast, question the value of screening more fundamentally, citing limited effectiveness, a contested evidence base, and substantial downsides. Across both positions, increased knowledge fosters a more deliberate engagement with overdiagnosis. In cautious screening, HPs see limited value in screening, making overdiagnosis a critical concern and forgoing screening a valid strategy to avoid it, whereas in determined screening, HPs seek to safeguard the benefits of screening and therefore focus on mitigation strategies (e.g. advocating age limits, limiting prostate biopsies with MRI). Between the two positions, HPs articulate diverging views on active surveillance as a response to overdiagnosis. Within determined screening, HPs present AS as a strategy that protects patients from the harmful side-effects of overtreatment, with limited psychosocial consequences and anxiety peaking only around follow-up exams. HPs with a cautious screening position challenge this view, arguing that AS increases psychological burden, resource use and opportunity cost.

Most participants holding determined or cautious positions had academic or research backgrounds, suggesting that achieving a thorough understanding of the benefits and harms of screening is challenging in routine clinical practice. Previous research has already shown that HPs frequently struggle to accurately estimate the benefits and harms of screening [50, 51] and to interpret key screening statistics [49, 52–54].

In addition to differences in epistemic position, another key factor appears to be the perceived actionability of strategies to mitigate overdiagnosis. Accounts from participants with a routine screening storyline illustrate how new information about overdiagnosis is processed in everyday reality. The information provided during the interview conflicted with their prior beliefs about screening, making it difficult to integrate it into their existing understanding. While some participants expressed curiosity, many did not perceive the information as relevant or applicable. This form of cognitive dissonance, described as the ‘perception gap’, occurs when new information that conflicts with preconceptions and current practices is either ignored or reinterpreted to maintain coherence with current behaviour [32, 55, 56]. For routine screeners, none of the mitigation strategies they considered was perceived as morally acceptable, leaving no actionable way to integrate this new information and consolidate it as knowledge. All options implied “not doing” established practices and triggered ‘loss aversion’ [57], a well-documented barrier in de-implementation research [58]. By contrast, HPs with other storylines had already implemented overdiagnosis-mitigating strategies, some of which also entailed discontinuing practices (e.g., stopping the promotion of screening). This suggests that information about overdiagnosis is more likely to be consolidated as knowledge when it can be enacted through concrete practices, in interaction with screening candidates, patients and professional peers.

HPs’ perspectives on overdiagnosis are best understood in light of this dynamic interplay between knowledge and positioning. These appear to be recursively interdependent: knowledge about screening shapes positioning, which in turn influences how new information about overdiagnosis is interpreted and integrated, potentially reinforcing or modifying that positioning over time. Professional, personal and contextual factors further condition this process. Positioning also influences which mitigation strategies are perceived as morally acceptable; when strategies are deemed unworkable, information remains abstract, thereby reinforcing existing positioning.

Comparison with existing research

To our knowledge, this is the first study examining HPs’ awareness and understanding of overdiagnosis in the context of screening.

Our finding that most HPs recognise the term, but do not actively mention it as a screening harm, suggests that for the majority, it plays little or no role in their decision-making. Daniel Kahneman described this phenomenon as “WYSIATI—What You See Is All There Is”, whereby people reason only with readily available information; as he noted: “Information that is not retrieved from memory might as well not exist” [59]. In this sense, although most HPs have passive knowledge, only a minority appear genuinely aware of overdiagnosis as screening harm.

We also found that many HPs initially conflate overdiagnosis with false positives or excessive diagnostic procedures. This places the high questionnaire-based awareness rates reported in our previous systematic review in a different light [36]. Our findings suggest that such measures may overestimate meaningful awareness and should be interpreted cautiously in the absence of insight into how respondents actually understood the term “overdiagnosis”.

Our findings further align with earlier qualitative studies examining how HPs frame overdiagnosis [60–62]. These studies informed our systematic review, which identified an association between HPs’ perception of overdiagnosis and their enthusiasm for screening, although the direction of this relationship remained unclear. The present study extends this work by showing that HPs’ broader positioning toward screening shapes how overdiagnosis is understood, evaluated, and acted upon. This positioning is grounded in HPs’ knowledge of screening benefits and influenced by professional roles and responsibilities, institutional contexts, and personal experiences, values, and moral commitments.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is its comprehensive design, which elicited perspectives from individual clinicians and public health officials, recruited a large cross-sectional sample, and examined the subject across multiple cancer screenings. Moreover, our inductive, reflective, and iterative analytic approach enabled a rich, nuanced understanding of HPs’ reasoning, acknowledged researchers’ preconceptions, and guarded against unwarranted interpretations. Using storylines and positioning helped us to present participants’ considerations in an integrated manner rather than fragmenting them into separate themes. Two notable limitations are the limited number of clinicians critical of lung cancer screening (which we were unable to identify, despite extensive efforts) and the interviewer’s positionality. Several urologists expressed unease with how GPs allegedly approach prostate cancer screening. As the interviewer was also a GP, this may have prompted more explicit opinions on this topic. Similarly, some participants knew the interviewer’s stance on screening, which could align with or completely oppose their own. Given the polarised debate on screening and overdiagnosis [63, 64], there was a risk of tension or exaggerated positions. Nonetheless, all interviews went well, often exceeding 90 min, and evolved from a formal interview (in which positions were marked) into a collegial exchange of ideas, reflecting a trusting exchange of information between interviewer and participant.

Together, our findings indicate that overdiagnosis is rarely foregrounded as a harm of screening and that familiarity with the term does not necessarily imply conceptual understanding or practical engagement. How HPs respond to overdiagnosis depends not only on what they know, but also on how they position themselves toward screening and the moral and practical options they perceive as acceptable.

Future research and recommendations

When screening is discussed in policy contexts, it is essential that all stakeholders correctly understand overdiagnosis. Misconceptions may be more problematic than ignorance, as they can unknowingly lead to misguided policy choices.

More broadly, our findings indicate that diversity in HPs’ positions on screening is a reality. Across these positions, however, HPs share a commitment to act in their patients’ and the public’s best interests. Precisely because of this shared moral commitment, more careful and explicit approaches to overdiagnosis are likely to be welcomed. To support such approaches, HPs require, first, a more accurate and nuanced understanding of screening, with realistic expectations and accurate numerical estimates of benefits and harms. This foundational understanding should be established early in medical education and reinforced through continuing professional development, guidelines, and screening recommendations. Specific information about overdiagnosis should be accompanied by practical examples of context-sensitive strategies that HPs can enact in daily practice (“others do this”).

Moreover, addressing overdiagnosis requires recognising the emotional, moral, and societal dimensions of screening practice. Efforts to prevent overdiagnosis should support HPs across all these domains, enabling them to mitigate overdiagnosis and provide safe and effective screening. One potential approach is the use of decision aids (DAs), which are intended to empower people to make informed choices about their health and may foster shared responsibility between the HP and the screening candidate [65]. Although research shows that DAs reduce decisional conflict among lay users [66], little is known about HPs’ perspectives on using DAs [67] or about their effectiveness in alleviating HPs’ moral distress.

Future research should examine the transferability of these findings across different healthcare systems (e.g., systems with pay-for-performance incentives). In addition, although we suspect that ‘routine screening’ is the most common position among HPs, these data do not allow such inferences. Quantification of these findings would be valuable. Further research is needed to identify effective ways to enhance HPs’ understanding of screening benefits and harms, and to determine how educational, organisational, or policy-level interventions can support HPs in engaging with the emotional, moral, and societal dimensions of changing screening practice.

Conclusion

Most health professionals recognise overdiagnosis, yet few spontaneously identify it as a potential harm of screening. How overdiagnosis is understood and acted upon is shaped by broader positions toward screening, which are grounded in understandings of screening benefits and influenced by professional contexts, moral commitments, and personal experiences. These positions influence how new information is interpreted and whether strategies to address overdiagnosis are seen as actionable in practice. More comprehensive engagement with evidence on both the benefits and harms of screening may support more deliberate and reflective approaches to overdiagnosis. These findings highlight the need for educational initiatives, guideline development, and policy approaches that support health professionals in engaging more explicitly with the trade-offs of screening.
