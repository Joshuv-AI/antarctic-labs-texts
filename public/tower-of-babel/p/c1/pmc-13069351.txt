Ocular Glymphatic System: Its Modulation and Role in Intraocular Tumors
Authors: Jasmine H. Francis, David H. Abramson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The glymphatic system is a glial-based perivascular network that clears metabolic waste from the central nervous system (CNS), and its dysfunction is related to neurodegenerative disease. This review describes a correlate ocular glymphatic system in the eye and describes how this might relate to ophthalmic diseases. This review article summarizes the published literature on the CNS and ophthalmic glymphatic system and how its dysfunction relates to disease. There are associations in the pathogenesis among neurodegeneration, glaucoma, and age-related macular degeneration (AMD), which could be explained through glymphatic dysfunction in the CNS and eye, respectively. The protective effects of exercise and sleep further demonstrate associations among neurodegeneration, glaucoma, and AMD. We provide new insights on the role of the glymphatic system and intraocular tumors, proposing that tumors may hijack the glymphatic system as a bidirectional pathway of communication between the eye and the brain. New therapies for enhancing CNS glymphatic flow are discussed. Dysfunction of the glymphatic system may be a unifying pathogenesis among neurodegeneration, glaucoma, and AMD. Future research should analyze whether CNS-focused therapeutics may improve ocular disease. The role of the glymphatic system in the pathogenesis of intraocular tumors warrants further investigation.

In 2012, Iliff and Nedergaard described an alternate fluid transport system, referred to as the glymphatic pathway, that was initially believed to be unique to the brain.1 Based on their study, the glymphatic pathway is a glial cell–based perivascular network driving clearance of metabolic waste from brain parenchyma via cerebral spinal fluid (CSF).1,2 In this system, CSF flows from the subarachnoid space to the periarterial space. Then, CSF enters the brain parenchyma and mixes with interstitial fluid (ISF), and during this exchange metabolic waste from the ISF is driven toward the vein. Finally, CSF/ISF leaves via the perivenous space and is collected by meningeal lymphatic vessels and subsequently cervical lymph nodes.1,2 Fluid exchange is facilitated by water channel aquaporin-4 (AQP4) located on the astrocytic end feet. The system has no dedicated pump but, instead, is powered by convective flow from arterial pulsation and respiration. The term “glymphatic” was adopted to convey similar flow mechanisms to lymphatics and dependence on a glial-based infrastructure. In addition to waste removal, the pathway disperses nutrients such as glucose and lipid and traffics immune effectors to aid in signaling.3 Dysfunction of the glymphatic system is believed to contribute to the basis for neurodegenerative disease and has prompted efforts to optimize its function.

Herein, this review describes published evidence and theories on the ocular glymphatic system, its relationship to ophthalmic diseases such as age-related macular degeneration (AMD) and glaucoma, and how this links to neurodegenerative disease. We provide original hypotheses regarding the interplay of the glymphatic system and ocular disease, such as (1) the putative role of the glymphatic system in the pathogenesis of intraocular tumors, specifically that tumors may hijack the glymphatic system; and (2) the recognition that the glymphatic system may support clearance of the intraretinal fluid that accumulates in the presence of choroidal tumors and radiation retinopathy. Finally, we review new therapeutic options to optimize glymphatic transport including those that are too novel to yet be clinically validated but have preclinical mechanistic merits and show early clinical promise in central nervous system (CNS)-related disease.

What Do We Know About the Glymphatic System and the Eye?

Three years after the CNS glymphatic system was identified, Denniston and Keane4 proposed, in this journal, a similar pathway in the eye—the “ocular glymphatic system”—and we expound upon that idea. Given an embryological derivation common to the CNS and the optic nerve and retina, it is not surprising that the glymphatic system is documented in the eye. The optic nerve and retina share the presence of glial cells possessing AQP4 water channels on their astrocytic end feet with the brain and spinal cord. And, physiologically, all three are highly metabolically active in order to meet the demands of their function, although this creates an accumulation of neurotoxins and potentially harmful metabolites and a need for their clearance.

Subsequent preclinical work and human observations have expounded upon this to demonstrate glymphatic flow from the brain to the eye (retrograde flow).3,5–9 Furthermore, rodent work by the Nedergaard group provided evidence demonstrating reverse flow from the eye to brain (anterograde flow), giving plausibility to the bidirectionality of the transport mechanism.3,10 A continuous network of paravascular channels surrounds the central retinal vessels in the optic nerve with branches that extend to the retina.11,12 In the optic nerve, CSF from the subarachnoid space enters the optic nerve parenchyma along the periarterial space to the laminar cribrosa and leaves the optic nerve via the perivenous space, draining to the meningeal lymphatics and subsequently the cervical lymph nodes5,10,13 (Fig. 1). In the retina, fluid is taken up from the vitreous by the retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) via astroglia AQP4 channels and may then be transported across the lamina cribrosa into the perivenous space of the optic nerve to the meningeal lymphatics, finally draining to the cervical lymph nodes10 (Fig. 1).

Translaminar passage (movement across the laminar cribrosa, a biological gateway between the intraocular eye and the retrobulbar optic nerve and CNS) is solute size dependent and relies heavily on both a pressure gradient to promote flow and the integrity of the laminar cribrosa to modulate passage.13 In normal physiological conditions, the intraocular pressure (IOP) is slightly greater than the intracranial pressure (ICP), facilitating exit from the eye into the nerve.13 However, pathological conditions such as aging, can facilitate retrograde flow from the brain to the eye.3,9 Identification of amyloid-beta deposits along retina vessels in Alzheimer's disease animal models suggests a retrograde ocular glymphatic system and communication of CSF substances to the retina via translaminar passage.3,6

Interestingly, based on murine models, pupillary constriction was discovered to be a physiological pump to anterograde flow, enhancing flow from the retina to the optic nerve perivascular spaces.10 Therefore, we propose it possible that suspension of the pupil in a static state, either pharmacologically or physically (by trauma or surgery), may impair the pump mechanism and normal glymphatic clearance and result in an accumulation of toxic waste in the retina; if validated, long-term pharmacologic pupillary suspension may want to be avoided if clinically indicated. In the reverse, we propose that the ocular glymphatic system could be bioelectrically stimulated through intentional exposure to flickering light to create a pupillary constriction pump mechanism.

Possible Roles of the Glymphatic System in the Pathophysiology of Glaucoma, AMD, and Dementia

Multiple large cohort studies and meta-analyses have demonstrated a strong association between dementia and glaucoma (particularly open-angle and normotensive subtypes) and between dementia and AMD.14–20 The question, then, becomes could the glymphatic system play a role in each of these conditions? First, dementia, glaucoma, and AMD have increasing age as a risk factor. Glymphatic function not only worsens with age but also has a bidirectional pathological relationship such that glymphatic dysfunction also accelerates aging. Dilation of perivascular spaces on magnetic resonance imaging likely indicates impaired glymphatic clearance from stagnant flow, and human studies consistently demonstrate this increases with age.3 Under normal conditions, the glymphatic system aids in removing reactive oxygen species (ROS), other neurotoxins, and inflammatory cytokines from the CNS extracellular space. However, CNS studies have shown that, when this is impaired, ROS accumulate in the microenvironment and trigger inflammasomes in microglia, which drives neuroinflammation and compromises glymphatic clearance, partially through ROS damage of AQP4 channels.21–23 Protein accumulation and impaired clearance further result in mitochondrial dysfunction and aging and perpetuate the cycle of neurodegeneration.21–23 Although this pathophysiology refers to the CNS, there is growing evidence of a similar molecular mechanism in the ocular glymphatic system. In mice, it has been shown that a polarity disorder of the AQP4 in the retina and optic nerve leads to poor substance exchange between the perivascular space and the interstitium,6 substantiating intact AQP4 as being crucial to the ocular glymphatic system outflow.

Dementia, glaucoma, and AMD share other common vascular risk factors: smoking, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, diabetes, obesity, and arteriosclerosis.24 One of the key drivers of glymphatic flow is arterial pulsatility. Loss of arterial elasticity caused by aging and other overlapping metabolic risk factors converge to weaken the pulsatile force in the arteries and may thereby impair the efficiency of glymphatic transport in each of these conditions.11

Furthermore, all three diseases overlap in their production of neurotoxins. In dementia, accumulated amyloid-beta and tau proteins are central to the downstream synaptic dysfunction, neuronal death, and cognitive decline.25,26 Similarly, in glaucoma, tau levels are elevated in the vitreous; accumulated amyloid-beta occurs with elevated IOP and is neurotoxic to the RGCs, resulting in apoptosis and axonal damage.27,28 Finally, amyloid-beta plays a key role in the progression of AMD by promoting drusen formation, inducing inflammation and complement activation, and impairing retinal pigment epithelium function.29 Tau has a contributory role in AMD by instigating neuroinflammation, breakdown of the blood–retina barrier, and degeneration of RGCs.30 An imbalance between the accumulation of amyloid-beta and tau and their defective clearance by glymphatic transport provides a unifying hypothesis connecting dementia, glaucoma, and AMD to deficiencies in glymphatic function.

Lifestyle Modulators That Support the Role of the Glymphatic System in the Pathophysiology of Glaucoma, AMD, and Dementia

Due to the role of the CNS glymphatic system in clearing metabolic waste, which is a key feature in neuroprotection, there have been many efforts made to optimize its function. Exercise and sleep (both quality and positioning) are two modifiable lifestyle amendments that may improve glymphatic function. Similarly, there is evidence that these same two modifications may positively influence the outcome of neurodegeneration, glaucoma, and AMD. Finally, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) have protective effects in all three diseases, and in this section we explore theoretical reasons for these beneficial effects.

Published Information on Exercise

Voluntary exercise increases CNS glymphatic flow in awake mice.31–34 This is mechanistically achieved by enabling CSF/ISF exchange (more apparent with swimming rather than running), upregulating AQP4 expression, improving AQP4 polarization, and subsequently increasing metabolic waste clearance.35 Evidence shows that regular exercise significantly reduces the risk of neurodegenerative disease, and epidemiologic studies demonstrate dose-dependent protective effects.36 With respect to ocular disease, both AMD and glaucoma are associated with exercise habits and sleep patterns: Physical activity is statistically protective against the development of AMD,37,38 and more physical activity is associated with slower rates of visual field loss in glaucoma (although this is not true of some types of glaucoma, such as pigment dispersion, which characteristically occurs in younger people and may worsen with rigorous exercise). In Alzheimer's, exercise has been shown to decrease amyloid accumulation and reduce tau pathology, subsequently avoiding neuroglial alterations and improving mitochondrial dysfunction.39,40

Published Information on Sleep

Glymphatic system transport operates at double efficiency during deep slow-wave, non–rapid eye movement (REM) sleep compared to wakefulness.9,41 Deep sleep–enhanced glymphatic flow in the brain is supported by a number of mechanisms, including decreased noradrenergic tone resulting in almost 60% increased ISF volume and synchronized vascular oscillations which serve as a pump mechanism to CSF influx.42 Furthermore, side sleeping, or the lateral decubitus position, particularly on the left side, optimizes cerebral venous flow and reduces resistance in extracranial veins, thereby facilitating unhindered CNS glymphatic flow. Interestingly, epidemiological studies reveal healthy individuals prefer sleeping in a lateral position compared to the supine position of patients with neurodegenerative diseases.43 In addition, many studies have shown that the quality of sleep impacts dementia risk, and the American Heart Association emphasizes that sleep disorders are a potentially modifiable risk factor for dementia prevention.44 One study demonstrated that poor sleep quality increased Alzheimer's risk by 24% and all-cause dementia risk by 17%.44 Specifically, greater slow-wave deep sleep may protect against brain amyloid-beta accumulation.45 In addition to increased inflammation, vascular dysfunction, and circadian rhythm dysfunction, impaired glymphatic clearance of amyloid-beta and tau proteins is one mechanism linking sleep disturbance to dementia.46

With respect to sleep and ophthalmic disease, insomnia or poor sleep quality is associated with an increased risk of developing AMD.47 One study showed that fewer than 6 hours of sleep increases the odds ratio of neovascular AMD by 3.29-fold compared to 7 to 8 hours of sleep.48 The current literature does not address lateral decubitus positioning in AMD patients but does show that sleep quality and duration have a modest but beneficial effect on reducing the risk and progression of AMD.48,49 The connection between glaucoma and sleep is more tenuous due to unclear cause-and-effect associations and the recognition that glaucomatous damage to RGCs impairs light input to the circadian system, resulting in poor sleep quality.50 However, it has been revealed that the severity of glaucoma is associated with worse sleep outcomes.50 Similar to sleep associations in Alzheimer's, in which sleep duration displays a U-shaped relationship with dementia risk,49 fewer than 6 hours of sleep is associated with a two to three times higher odds ratio of visual field defects and glaucomatous disc changes.51,52

Published Information on GLP-1RAs

Besides lifestyle modifications, recent evidence has indicated that GLP-1RAs protect against neurodegenerative disorders, such as Alzheimer's and Parkinson's,53 as well as AMD54 and glaucoma (in both diabetic and non-diabetic cohorts).55–57 Although mechanistically multifaceted, given this shared drug-associated protection, is it logical to question whether this protective effect is partially explained through GLP-1 agonist–induced enhancement of glymphatic clearance. Indeed, there is evidence for GLP-1RA–facilitated CNS glymphatic function, as a preclinical study demonstrated that GLP-1 receptor activation restored glymphatic transport following traumatic brain injury, and the addition of an AQP4 inhibitor eliminated this effect.58 These striking findings have complex theoretical explanations proposed by others; for example, GLP-1 agonists are anti-inflammatory (so as not to impede glymphatic clearance), vasoprotective, and inhibitory of pathological angiogenesis (to maintain the intact perivascular pathways relied upon by the glymphatic system), thus enhancing cerebral blood flow and arterial pulsatility (the power source behind glymphatic flow).58

Hypothesis Regarding Exercise, Sleep, and GLP-1RAs

There is no direct evidence that the beneficial effects of exercise, sleep, and GLP-1RAs on AMD and glaucoma can be explained through ocular glymphatic activation. However, even though the mechanism of protection afforded by exercise, sleep, and GLP-1RA is likely multifactorial, we propose that the potential clinical improvements in glaucoma and AMD seen with these three modifications could, in part, be due to enhanced glymphatic clearance. It is possible that future studies will extend our knowledge regarding the brain and determine whether exercise, sleep, and GLP-1RAs may facilitate ocular glymphatic flow and modulate amyloid and tau clearance from ocular tissues. However, regarding the latter, this anticipation must be tempered by the recognition that GLP-1RAs may be associated with visually devastating non-arteritic ischemic optic neuropathy and other ophthalmic adverse events.59,60

One point of perplexity regarding sleep and glaucoma is the acknowledgment that supine sleep positioning elevates IOP, but lateral decubitus positioning, which facilitates CNS glymphatic flow and theoretically ocular glymphatic flow, also increases IOP. It is possible that the beneficial glymphatic outflow effects of lateral decubitus positioning are countered by undue direct pressure imposed on the eyeball by side sleeping. However, head elevation by at least 30° may mitigate the potentially harmful impact of supine or lateral decubitus sleep positioning.61 In summary, the beneficial effects of sleep on glaucoma risk and progression require attention to both sleep quality and ideal sleep positioning.

Is It Possible for Intraocular Tumors to Hijack the Glymphatic Pathway?

Established and Published Information on Cancer Accessibility to the CNS Glymphatic System

Human fluid transport is an intricate process with specialized systems, and it may be co-opted by malignancies to facilitate their migration. Tumors metastasize via several routes including hematogenous, lymphatic, and transcoelomic (across body cavities), as well as, far less commonly, in a perineural or paravascular fashion. The glymphatic system is one of these latter fluid transport systems, and the identification of amyloid-beta deposits along retina vessels in an Alzheimer's disease animal model suggests a retrograde ocular glymphatic system and accessibility of the CSF contents to the retina.3,6 In addition to transporting nutrients and protein deposits, stray malignant cells have been discovered in the glymphatic fluid of the CNS,62,63 demonstrating that, in the brain, this pathway is accessible and utilized by malignant cells.

Hypothesis Regarding Cancer Accessibility to the Ocular Glymphatic System

Malignant cells have not been demonstrated in the ocular glymphatic system, but, given the overlap between the CNS and ocular glymphatic systems, we hypothesize that accessibility of malignant cells to the ocular glymphatic system is possible via retrograde flow from the CNS via the optic nerve to the retina, similar to the route traveled by amyloid in animal models. Furthermore, a bidirectional flow between the brain and eye has been proposed by others. Even though brain-to-eye transport is supported by evidence obtained through both animal models and human studies,3,5–8 current study of the reverse direction, from the eye to the brain, is restricted to demonstrations in animal work, and such bidirectional flow is pending validation in humans.3,10 We propose that intraocular tumor cells may hijack the glymphatic system as a route of transport, and perhaps even bidirectionally, between the eye and the brain, such as in vitreoretinal lymphoma.

Published Information on Vitreoretinal Lymphoma

Vitreoretinal lymphoma characteristically overlaps with the presence of primary CNS lymphoma (PCNSL); however, the route of entry between these two immune-privileged sites remains elusive. Unlike low-grade mucosa-assisted lymphoid tissue lymphoma, which infiltrates the conjunctiva, orbit, and choroid and spares the CNS, high-grade diffuse large B-cell lymphoma instead has a different distribution in the eye with a propensity to involve the optic nerve, retina, and vitreous. it has been recognized that lymphoma cells (particularly PCNSL) enter the glymphatic system via the periventricular spaces, which is adjacent to the subarachnoid space and the origination point of the glymphatic pathway.62,63

Hypothesis Regarding Vitreoretinal Lymphoma

It would follow that these PCNSL cells have access to the glymphatic system pathways, including those that lead to the eye. We propose that PCNSL cells traverse the normal flow of the glymphatic system to enter the perivascular spaces within the optic nerve. In a pathological state created by the presence of malignant cells and a secondary heightened neuroinflammatory environment, these cells may pass the laminar cribrosa and enter into the retina and subsequently the vitreous (Fig. 2). This may explain the observation of lymphoma infiltrates within the retina (intraretinal disease), the inner retina, vitreous, and optic nerve head (Fig. 3).

Hypothesis of vitreoretinal lymphoma hijacking the ocular glymphatic system. The “brain-to-eye valve hypothesis” posits that elevated ICP triggers a valve-like mechanism to allow retrograde entry of CSF substances from the brain and into the eye.3,28 We postulate that, if PCNSL causes elevated ICP, this may propel a flux of lymphoma cells from the CNS and into the eye via the ocular glymphatic system. We posit that cells may exit using the glymphatic system of the retina, which communicates with the optic nerve, past the laminar cribrosa to the perivenous space, potentially reentering the CSF via the subarachnoid space. In summary, given the clinical features of vitreoretinal lymphoma and the established presence of lymphoma cells in CNS glymphatic fluid, we propose that one route of bidirectional communication between the eye and brain could be enabled via the glymphatic system.

Established Information on Choroidal Tumors

Next, we address the question of what role the glymphatic system may play in choroidal tumors, noting that the choroidal location theoretically renders these tumors relatively inaccessible to the retinal or optic nerve gateway of the ocular glymphatic system. Both benign and malignant choroidal tumors may instigate either subretinal fluid and/or intraretinal fluid (Fig. 4), with this being one of the main mechanisms of visual symptoms and vision decline. In fact, this fluid is an unmet area of therapeutics for patients: If we could better understand the flow dynamics of intraretinal and subretinal fluid, we may improve its drainage from the eye and better protect or even reverse vision loss. It is recognized that subretinal fluid exits via the retinal pigment epithelium to the fenestrated choriocapillaris followed by venous drainage of the choroid (vortex veins to the superior/inferior ophthalmic veins to the cavernous sinus). This is in contrast to intraretinal fluid, which is cleared by Müller glial AQP4 channels (oriented with end feet abutting the vitreous and processes surrounding blood capillaries in the mid/outer retina), allowing fluid to exit via blood vessels, including the central retinal vein.

Hypothesis Regarding Choroidal Tumors

We propose that glymphatic transport may further support intraretinal fluid clearance such that fluid is taken up by RGCs and cleared along the optic nerve via astroglial AQP4 channels and into the perivenous space. Furthermore, poor venous outflow or venous hypertension signaled by pachychoroid or created by a choroidal tumor could increase pressure in the central retinal vein and perivenous environment, thereby impairing ocular glymphatic outflow, which utilizes the perivenous space for efflux of fluid and waste.

Published Information on Radiation Retinopathy

Evidence shows that whole-brain or radiation for nasopharyngeal carcinomas may contribute to CNS glymphatic dysfunction, as suggested by magnetic resonance imaging studies in humans.63,64 Furthermore, the brain glymphatic system removes inflammatory mediators such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF); however, when the system is impaired, clearance may be delayed, thereby prolonging signaling effects of molecules such as VEGF.65 Could a similar mechanism of radiation injury transpire in the eye? Radiation damage to the posterior eye predominantly manifests as intraretinal fluid and vasculopathy partially related to elevated VEGF (Fig. 4).

Hypothesis Regarding Radiation Retinopathy

We propose that, like the CNS, radiation-damage to the ocular glymphatic system may compromise its fluid drainage and be one contributor to the intraretinal fluid of radiation maculopathy. Furthermore, the pathological angiogenic component of radiation retinopathy, which is driven by hypoxia-induced VEGF, may be further heightened by delayed removal of VEGF by the defunct ocular glymphatic system. Prolonged VEGF exposure creates a pathologic proangiogenic intraocular environment, resulting in the aberrant neovascularization and increased vascular permeability that are seen in radiation retinopathy and optic neuropathy. Furthermore, the impaired ocular glymphatic clearance could theoretically contribute to toxic tumor syndrome, as uncleared and elevated toxins from radiation-destroyed tumor cells could trigger aggressive secondary vasculopathy. However, it should be noted that no study has examined whether regressed tumor toxins are present in ocular glymphatic fluid. Future studies will determine whether enhanced glymphatic clearance may improve this sight-threatening consequence of radiation.

Published Information on Central Serous Chorioretinopathy

Deregulators of the CNS glymphatic system have been recognized to include stress and steroids. In fact, both converge in a similar mechanism in which glucocorticoid and adrenergic signaling downregulates AQP4 expression and disrupts astrocyte polarization, thus impairing CNS glymphatic clearance.66 It is curious to note that these two triggers are also implicated in the pathophysiology of central serous chorioretinopathy (CSC).67–69 Accordingly, avoidance of steroids and stress has the potential to improve the subretinal fluid and visual symptoms of CSC.67–69

Hypothesis Regarding CSC

There is an absence of studies investigating whether stress and glucocorticoids influence the flow of the ocular glymphatic system and in CSC, the expection is for the subretinal fluid to extravasate via the RPE and choriocapillaris in a manner that is remote from the glymphatic system outflow. However, it raises the question as to whether the relationship between impaired outflow of subretinal fluid in CSC is connected to impaired glymphatic outflow by way of its stress- or glucocorticoid-associated dysfunction. We propose that the flow dynamics and fluid regression in CSC may in fact partially rely on glymphatic clearance, and that, following stress reduction and steroid cessation, normal glymphatic transport may resume.

Future Therapeutics to Enhance Glymphatic Function

Published Information on Future Therapeutics

Given that glymphatic clearance is optimally functional during deep, slow-wave non-REM sleep, it is logical that evidence points to the beneficial effects of enhancing this stage of sleep. Some sleep medications, such as zolpidem, have an opposite effect by suppressing norepinephrine oscillations and reducing glymphatic flow.42 On the other hand, dual orexin receptor antagonists (DORAs) are a unique class of sleep medications that preserve natural sleep architecture and promote deeper slow-wave sleep. This drug-induced enhancement of deep, slow-wave non-REM sleep explains the observation that DORAs reduce amyloid-beta accumulation and improve synaptic plasticity.70 Accordingly, preliminary evidence suggests that DORAs influence dementia pathology and disease progression.71,72

Another new area of exploration is vagus nerve stimulation (VNS), which has received Federal Drug Association approval for drug-resistant epilepsy, stroke rehabilitation, and most recently rheumatoid arthritis.73 Clinical work indicates that VNS can enhance glymphatic transport, as evidenced by increased CSF tracer penetrance.74 Also, in preclinical models, the demonstration of improved cognitive function in models of vascular cognitive impairment corroborates these findings.75,76 The ability of VNS to modulate parasympathetic tone, vascular pulsatility, and even astrocytic AQP4 function supports the theoretical assertation that VNS may enhance glymphatic clearance.

DORAs and VNS are scientifically substantiated in preclinical and some human-based preliminary studies but are too novel to yet be fully clinically validated for neuroprotection. However, DORAs and VNS are currently in clinical trials to determine their therapeutic value in dementia (e.g., NCT06274528, NCT07213349, NCT06923007). Transcranial photobiostimulation (PBM) not only optimizes mitochondrial function through absorption by cytochrome C, but is also shown to enhance glymphatic flow via many mechanisms including dilatation of meningeal lymphatic vessels through nitric oxide release.77 This has been demonstrated in humans,78 and, in a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, PBM significantly improved cognition.79 Specific to ocular disease, photobiomodulation statistically improves both anatomical and clinical outcomes in dry AMD.80

Finally, emerging work in both transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) and 40 Hz transcranial vibration stimulation (TVS) has demonstrated increased glymphatic flow and has exhibited promise in cognitive improvement in both preclinical and human studies.82–85

Hypothesis Regarding Future Therapeutics

Given the link among dementia, glaucoma, AMD, and potentially intraretinal fluid created by choroidal tumors and radiation retinopathy, could CNS-directed ventures aimed at improving glymphatic flow be borrowed to improve ocular disease? The effects of DORAs, VNS, TMS and TVS on glymphatic flow and their therapeutic effects have been reported in CNS disease. However, their support in ocular pathology has not been established, and their potential application to ophthalmic treatment remains hypothetical. A future avenue of work could explore whether DORA-, VNS-, TMS- and TVS-enhanced glymphatic flow could be useful for ocular diseases such as AMD, glaucoma, and even the intraretinal fluid associated with some choroidal neoplasms and radiation retinopathy. DORAs are post-market, and transcutaneous auricular VNS devices are non-invasive for home use; therefore, future studies may answer this question. With regard to photobiomodulation, one may infer that the beneficial effects of PBM on AMD may be partially attributed to enhanced glymphatic flow, and perhaps PBM could be extended to treat other ocular diseases in which the underlying pathophysiology involves a dysfunctional glymphatic system.

Conclusions

There are similarities between neurodegeneration and visual degeneration due to ophthalmic disease such as glaucoma and AMD. We have discussed compelling links indicating that a dysfunctional glymphatic system may be one piece of the puzzle implicated in the biological mechanism of neurodegeneration, glaucoma, and AMD: Specifically, that reduced glymphatic outflow garners accumulation of toxic metabolic waste which can contribute to the pathophysiology of disease. The protective effects of exercise, sleep, and GLP-1RAs are multifactorial, and enhanced glymphatic flow may be one unifying contributor. In addition, as is common in other human fluid transport systems, clinical findings and multimodal imaging support the proposal that vitreoretinal lymphoma may hijack the the glymphatic system as a bidirectional pathway of communication between the eye and the brain. With new glymphatic-enhancing interventions in clinical trials for dementia and given the link among neurodegeneration, glaucoma, and AMD, future studies may demonstrate the utility of these CNS-focused therapeutics in potentially improving ocular disease.
