Cryo-EM structure of TRPM1 reveals a non-canonical architecture with an inverted transmembrane domain
Authors: Michael Fabrizio, Mackenzie Brewer, Nebojša Bogdanović, Chen Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransient receptor potential melastatin 1 (TRPM1) is a membrane protein essential for vision in dim light, and mutations in TRPM1 cause complete congenital stationary night blindness. Although TRPM1 shares sequence similarity to other TRPM ion channels such as TRPM3, whether it independently functions as an ion channel remains controversial. This controversy is largely caused by TRPM1’s challenging biochemical behaviors that prevent detailed molecular characterization. In this work, we isolate TRPM1 and determine its structures using cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM). The structures reveal a canonical tetrameric fold in the intracellular domain, consistent with other TRPM family members that are ion channels. Surprisingly, in the transmembrane domain, despite the presence of the conserved voltage sensor-like domain (VSLD) and pore domain (PD) in a domain-swapped fashion, the VSLD and PD are arranged with an opposite handedness compared to other related channels. This inverted transmembrane domain allows the formation of a large pore-like structure that supports the role of TRPM1 as an ion channel. This non-canonical architecture of TRPM1 may also confer unique permeation and pharmacological properties.Subject terms: Cryoelectron microscopy, Biophysics

Introduction

In the retina, rod photoreceptors have the ability to detect single photons and transduce this information to bipolar cells through chemical synapses1,2. In these bipolar cells, a signaling cascade involving metabotropic glutamate receptor 6 (mGluR6) and transient receptor potential melastatin 1 (TRPM1) plays a critical role in receiving light-driven signals from rods2–8. When there is light, TRPM1 is believed to be directly or indirectly responsible for a constitutive depolarizing current through the bipolar cell membrane2–4,6–8. In the dark, however, this TRPM1-associated current is turned off by free G proteins (Gα and Gβγ) produced by mGluR6 upon its activation (Fig. 1A)2–4,6–10. This repolarizes the bipolar cell membrane and terminates the transduction of the vision signal2–4,6–10.

As the rod photoreceptor is mainly responsible for night vision, defects in this mGluR6-TRPM1 pathway, such as nonsynonymous mutations in mGluR6, TRPM1 and other proteins involved, lead to complete congenital stationary night blindness (cCSNB)2,11–17. So far, more than 11 mutations in TRPM1 have been found in patients who suffer from cCSNB2,11–17. Some of these mutations have been shown to influence TRPM1 trafficking to the dendritic tips in the rod bipolars cells14, however, the majority of the TRPM1 cCSNB mutants remain uncharacterized.

According to its amino acid sequence, TRPM1 belongs to the transient receptor potential melastatin (TRPM) family that contains eight family members (TRPM1-8) in total18,19. Decades of electrophysiological and structural analyses have unambiguously shown that the rest of the TRPM family members (TRPM2-8) function as ion channels20–31. For example, as TRPM1’s closest homolog with ~67% sequence identity and ~76% sequence similarity, TRPM3 has been demonstrated by patch clamp experiments to be a non-selective cation channel in both dorsal root ganglion neurons32 and HEK cells after heterologous expression22,23. The recent cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of TRPM3 reveal a canonical gate in resting and activated conformations33–35, supporting its role as an ion channel. However, despite the strong sequence homology between TRPM1 and TRPM3, whether TRPM1 itself is a bona fide ion channel is under debate2. This debate largely stems from the inability to reproducibly reconstitute the ion channel activity of TRPM1 after heterologous expression in common cell lines7,9,36,37. In fact, only one study comprehensively characterized TRPM1 current in HEK293 cells after overexpression, but the authors had to remove a large intracellular loop or produce heteromers of TRPM1 with TRPM3 to confidently record the TRPM1-dependent current37. This situation is further complicated by TRPM1’s controversial pharmacological properties6,8,9,38–40, making it difficult to correlate the results in native bipolar cells to those after heterologous expression. For example, capsaicin was believed to activate the endogenous TRPM1-dependent current in the bipolar cells6,8,9,38, however, some studies also reported the presence of this capsaicin-induced current in the bipolar cells from TRPM1 knockout mice6,39, and none of the studies can reconstitute the capsaicin sensitivity with heterologously expressed TRPM19,40. Other pharmacological ligands such as capsazepine8,9 and voriconazole41 have also been observed to modulate TRPM1 activity. However, similar to capsaicin, their effects were mostly tested in native bipolar cells and cannot be consistently reproduced in heterologous systems, making it difficult to distinguish whether they result from direct TRPM1 binding or indirect modulation by intermediate molecules. Finally, a low-resolution negative-stain EM structure of TRPM1 reported a dimeric configuration, inconsistent with other TRPM channels that are tetramers42. This observation further raises the skepticism over the function of TRPM1 as an independent ion channel.

In this work, we aim to advance our understanding of TRPM1 function on the molecular level. To this end, we produced recombinant TRPM1 and determined its molecular structure by cryo-EM. We also characterized its ability to permeate Ca2+ in HEK293 cells.

Results

Design and validation of the TRPM1 cryo-EM construct

We chose the rat TRPM1 (rTRPM1) because the only comprehensive study that claimed to have successfully recorded TRPM1-dependent current in HEK293 cells used the rat construct37. To further improve protein yield and homogeneity, we deleted the last 271 amino acids based on its intrinsically disordered structure predicted by PSIPRED43 and AlphaFold244, as well as the sequence alignment with the TRPM3 cryo-EM construct that lacks the C-terminus33. From now on, we will refer to this TRPM1 construct as rTRPM1EM. We first set out to determine whether the full-length rTRPM1 (rTRPM1FL) and rTRPM1EM are functional in HEK293 cells, under the hypothesis that TRPM1 functions as a bona fide ion channel. We chose Ca2+ imaging as a readout for TRPM1 activity, as the TRPM1-dependent bipolar cell current has been reported to be non-selective for cations such as Ca2+ 8, 38, 45–49. For Ca2+ imaging, we created a stable cell line using the commercially available Flp-In™ T-REx™ 293 cell line where the expression of jGCaMP7f50 was controlled by a doxycycline-inducible promoter (HEK293doxy-jGCaMP7f). We validated the cell line with Ca2+ ionophore A23187, in which ~80% of the cells became fluorescent in the presence of 3 mM Ca2+ (Supplementary Fig. 1). We then transiently introduced TRPM1 constructs in HEK293 cells via the BacMam method51,52. For cells transduced with both rTRPM1FL and rTRPM1EM, we observed fluorescence increases upon Ca2+ addition among a subpopulation of cells, likely due to the expression of TRPM1 on their surfaces (Fig. 1B, C). Most control cells do not display fluorescence increase in response to Ca2+ (Fig. 1B, C). In the small percentage of the control cells that showed Ca2+ permeation, their fluorescence increased by a much smaller degree than the cells transduced with TRPM1 (Fig. 1B, D). These observations suggest that the expression of both the full-length rTRPM1 and the truncated rTRPM1EM introduced a constitutive Ca2+ flux in HEK293 cells. This supports the role of TRPM1 as a bona fide ion channel, which is unaffected by the large truncation introduced into our cryo-EM construct (rTRPM1EM). A similar TRPM1-dependent Ca2+ influx in HEK293 cells has also been documented in a previous study53.

Overall structure of TRPM1

We first solubilized rTRPM1EM in detergent glycol-diosgenin (GDN) under similar conditions as those used for the TRPM3 channel33. However, the protein yield is poor (~80 µg protein from 4 L HEK293 GnTI- cells) (Supplementary Fig. 2A), and subsequent cryo-EM analyses failed to resolve the transmembrane domain (TMD) (Supplementary Fig. 3A). To further stabilize the TMD, we incorporated 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC), the most abundant lipid molecule on the plasma membrane, into the GDN micelles with a detergent-to-lipid ratio of 10 to 1. The mixed micelle substantially improved the protein yield by ~10-fold (Supplementary Fig. 2B). More importantly, the mixed micelle allowed us to determine the structure of TRPM1 with an overall resolution of 3.6 Å with partial TMD density resolved (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. 4). Using focused classification and refinement, we improved the cryo-EM map of TRPM1 intracellular domain (ICD) to 3.5 Å (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 5A). Despite the minimal change in the absolute value of the resolution, the map quality of ICD was substantially improved, especially in periphery regions (Fig. 2B).

The ICD of TRPM1 displays a 4-fold symmetrical structure with two layers of the TRPM Homology Regions (MHRs) that are very similar to other TRPM channels (Fig. 2B)54. This observation contrasts with an earlier negative-stain EM structure showing a dimeric structure of TRPM142.

We then mapped TRPM1 mutations that cause complete congenital stationary night blindness (cCSNB)11–17 onto our structure (Fig. 3). These mutations are distributed evenly across the TRPM1 structure, with 10 mutation sites in the ICD and 1 in the TMD (Fig. 3). Interestingly, the mutation in the TMD (F1120S) has been previously reported to inhibit proper TRPM1 trafficking in native bipolar cells14. Notably, Fig. 3 only displays the mutations within regions visible in our cryo-EM map. As suggested by Fig. 3, most of the mutational sites in ICD participate in maintaining the structural integrity of TRPM1, but their precise roles need to be evaluated by functional assays in the future.

The unique architecture of the TRPM1 transmembrane domain

In our consensus map (Fig. 2A), only the voltage sensor-like domain (VSLD) is visible in the TMD. To resolve the architecture of the entire TMD, we set out to improve the cryo-EM map by three-dimensional (3D) classification. Our initial hypothesis was that the rotational heterogeneity between TMD and ICD led to poor map quality. Therefore, we first aligned the ICD by focused refinement, then classified inside the TMD without alignment. This strategy indeed allowed us to resolve the cryo-EM density for the pore domain (PD), but at a much lower intensity than the VSLD (Supplementary Fig. 6). This result suggests that heterogeneity between the VSLD and PD within the TMD also contributed to the structural dynamics. Therefore, instead of classifying the entire TMD, we first classified the PD, then classified the VSLD among the particles that share a similar PD (Supplementary Fig. 4). This strategy allowed us to determine the structure of the entire TRPM1 channel to an overall resolution of 4.3 Å using C1 symmetry (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. 5B). Even though the local resolution in the TMD is around 5–6 Å (Supplementary Fig. 5B), the cryo-EM map is unambiguous for the transmembrane helices and the connections between them (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. 7). With this map, we attempted to build a model for the TMD of TRPM1 according to its sequence and structural similarity with TRPM3 in the VSLD and the TRP helix (Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table 1).

To our surprise, TRPM1 TMD displayed a unique arrangement in which the PD from one TRPM1 protomer contacts the VSLD of the neighboring protomer in a clockwise orientation when viewed from the extracellular space (Fig. 5A). A similar arrangement can also be observed in our consensus map (Fig. 2A) when viewed with low contour levels. This TMD configuration is opposite to that of TRPM333–35, the closest TRPM family member to TRPM1 (Fig. 4 and Fig. 5A–C). This TMD configuration is also opposite to those from all published structures of ion channels with similar architectures, including all TRP channels, voltage-gated potassium channels, voltage-gated sodium channels, and voltage-gated calcium channels34,54–63. In other words, the TMD in TRPM1 is arranged with an inverted handedness compared to all other related ion channels (Fig. 5A–C). Notably, the handedness here refers to the handedness of the arrangement between VSLD and PD, not the handedness of the cryo-EM map. In fact, the handedness of the VSLD region alone is conserved between TRPM1 and TRPM3 (Fig. 4A, B).

Substantial alterations in the packing of TMD helices are necessary to achieve this inverted configuration in TRPM1. Specifically, to reach the VSLD at the opposite side from the one in TRPM3, the TRPM1 PD helices (S5 and S6) need to stretch out and extend at much shallower angles with respect to the axis perpendicular to the membrane plane, resulting in a unique curvature of S6 at its junction between the TRP helix (Fig. 4A). This possibly destabilizes S5 and S6 so that they adopt alternating conformations in neighboring protomers, making the TMD of TRPM1 2-fold symmetrical (Fig. 5). Indeed, refinement imposing C2 symmetry yields a better map (Supplementary Fig. 8). In one set of TRPM1 protomers, the S5 and S6 contact S1 and S4 at the extracellular side and run in parallel in the membrane (yellow and cyan in Fig. 5B, C). In the other set of TRPM1 protomers, only S6 contacts S1 from the extracellular side, while the S5 splays apart until forming close contact with S6 inside the membrane (blue and salmon in Fig. 5B, C).

The putative ion permeation pathway in TRPM1

The unconventional organization of the TRPM1 TMD requires unique structural elements to support the central ion permeation pathway. All ion channels with known structures use S6 to form the pore. However, in rTRPM1EM, S5 appears to line up the central cavity at the extracellular side (Figs. 5B, C, 6A, B). Nevertheless, at the intracellular side, it is still S6 that forms the cavity comparable to the gate in other related channels (Figs. 5B, C, 6A, B). However, this observation does not automatically imply that the equivalent gate residues in TRPM3 also regulate TRPM1 ion permeation. On the contrary, the unique TMD in TRPM1 likely invokes the need for alternative gating mechanisms to control ion permeation in TRPM1.

Globally, the inverted architecture in the TRPM1 TMD leads to an expansion of the central cavity (Fig. 5A, B). Due to our limited resolution in the TMD, we cannot precisely measure the diameter of the pore, but it is estimated to be at least ~10 Å, as the distance between the Cα atom at the narrowest point (Y1013) is ~17.5 Å (Fig. 5D). We then compared the central cavity between the narrowest point observed in rTRPM1EM and the intracellular gate in other TRPM channels that were reported to be in the open or activated states (Fig. 5D). rTRPM1EM exhibited the second largest opening with a Cα-Cα distance from opposing protomers at ~17.5 Å, about 1.5 Å narrower than the gate in the open-state TRPM264 (Fig. 5D). The Cα-Cα distance between the other pair of opposing protomers in rTRPM1EM is much larger due to the 2-fold symmetrical structure (Fig. 5D). On the contrary, the Cα-Cα distances at the intracellular gate in open TRPM765, open TRPM531 and activated TRPM334 are 14.3 Å, 13.4 Å and 11.1 Å, respectively. This observation is consistent with the hypothesis that TRPM1 itself is a constitutively open ion channel6,8,36, which is also supported by the results from our Ca2+ imaging experiments (Fig. 1B–D). However, we cannot rule out the possibility that some lipid molecules can invade this pore in the cell membrane.

We then move on to test if the putative ion permeation pathway observed in our structure is responsible for the TRPM1-dependent Ca2+ permeation observed in cells. To achieve this, we chose the positively charged methanethiosulfonate (MTS) reagent, (2-(trimethylammonium)ethyl) methanethiosulfonate bromide (MTSET), which is widely used in ion channel research66–68. The MTSET can readily conjugate to solvent-accessible cysteine residues engineered at the channel pore and introduce a bulky side chain to prevent ion permeation (Fig. 6C). We first confirmed that MTSET did not influence the Ca2+ permeation ability of the wild-type (WT) rTRPM1EM (Fig. 6D, E). We then introduced the Y1013C mutation on S5 inside the pore that is accessible from the extracellular side (Fig. 6A–C). Notably, Y1013 forms the narrowest point in the putative pore in the rTRPM1EM structure (Fig. 5D). From similar Ca2+ imaging assays, this mutant was still able to permeate Ca2+ (Fig. 6D, E), suggesting this cysteine mutation did not alter its function. However, MTSET treatment substantially reduced the Ca2+ signals from this mutant (Fig. 6D, E). This result suggests that the Y1013C on S5 likely directly contributes to constructing the pore. This observation further supports the hypothesis that our cryo-EM structure of TRPM1 captures a state that is close to its functional state in the HEK293 cell membrane.

The VSLD of TRPM1 is structurally conserved with the VSLDs from all other TRP channels (Fig. 4A, B). As a few TRPM3 ligands only interact with the VSLD, the binding pockets for these ligands are preserved in TRPM1 (Fig. 7A, B). Additionally, sequence alignment suggests that all key residues directly interacting with the ligands are also conserved (Fig. 7A, B). These ligands include primidone (inhibitor)34,35, CIM-0216 (activator)34,35 (Fig. 7A), and phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2, obligatory)33 (Fig. 7B). Therefore, our TRPM1 structure suggests that all these TRPM3 ligands potentially interact with TRPM1.

We then tested if these ligands also regulated the function of TRPM1. We chose to focus on primidone and CIM-0216, as our Ca2+ imaging assay required the reagents to cross the cell membrane. Consistent with the structure comparison, pre-treatment with primidone substantially reduced the Ca2+ signal (Fig. 7C, D, right panels), suggesting that primidone interacted with and inhibited TRPM1. CIM-0216, however, did not significantly change the Ca2+ signal (Fig. 7C, D, left panels). The observation can be simply explained by the assumption that the majority of TRPM1 on the cell surface adopts a wide-open pore, as in our cryo-EM structure, that cannot be further activated. It is also possible that some population of TRPM1 on the cell surface adopts a non-conductive state unresolved by our cryo-EM analyses. However, the predicted binding of CIM-0216 does not induce pore opening in this population of TRPM1, possibly because its unique TMD configuration relies on alternative gating mechanisms.

Contrary to the VSLD ligands, pregnenolone sulfate (PregS) engages at a site between the VSLD (S1) and PD (S6 and the pore loop) in the TRPM3 channel (Fig. 7E)35. Due to the altered helix packing in the TRPM1 TMD that is arranged with an inverted handedness, the PregS binding site is no longer conserved in TRPM1 despite high sequence conservation in key binding residues—PregS will sterically clash with S6 in both types of TMD-PD interface in the 2-fold symmetrical TRPM1 (Fig. 7E). Indeed, PregS failed to increase the TRPM1-dependent Ca2+ influx in HEK293 cells (Fig. 7F, G). Nevertheless, this observation can also be explained by the already wide-open pore in TRPM1 that may not be further activated by ligands.

Discussion

In this work, we determined the molecular structures of TRPM1 using cryo-EM. To our surprise, the structure revealed an unprecedented architecture with a 4-fold ICD, 2-fold TMD, and an inverted arrangement within the TMD, in contrast to the structure predicted by AlphaFold2. This finding provides an example of this ion channel fold and opens possibilities for the mechanisms of allosteric modulation in TRP channels. This finding also highlights the importance of experimental structural biology — we need to be cautious when predicting functional properties (such as pharmacology) based on sequence homology alone. Nevertheless, we want to point out that this unique TMD fold was obtained from rat TRPM1 in an unliganded state in this study. It is very interesting to learn whether this fold is conserved among TRPM1 from other species, whether this fold can be converted to the canonical fold upon ligand-induced channel closure, and whether other TRP channels can also adopt this fold under certain conditions.

Whether TRPM1 independently functions as an ion channel has been under debate for decades. In this work, we provide structural and functional evidence supporting the role of TRPM1 as a bona fide ion channel. Our cryo-EM structure reveals a large cavity formed by the inverted TMD in TRPM1, allowing for ion conduction (Fig. 5A). Meanwhile, our functional measurements in HEK293 cells support the ability of TRPM1 to permeate Ca2+ (Fig. 1B–D). Our structural and functional data are also consistent as: (1) MTS blocks Ca2+ permeation by its conjugation to a specific residue at the pore based on our cryo-EM structure (Figs. 6D,E), (2) the pharmacological actions of primidone and PregS on TRPM1 are consistent with the predictions from structure comparison (Fig. 7). Although we have provided evidence that TRPM1 independently functions as an ion channel, future studies are necessary to dissect the structural and functional interplay in TRPM1 gating. We also want to note that, even though we emphasize TRPM1 can independently function as an ion channel, we strongly believe the native environment in retina bipolar cells, including the local lipid membrane and protein partners, are essential to fully reconstitute the activity and pharmacological properties of TRPM1.

One hypothesis based on the inverted VSLD and PD arrangement in TRPM1 is that TRPM1 cannot form heteromers with related channels, unlike many other ion channels, such as the TRP channels69–71. If this hypothesis is true, this unique structure of the TRPM1 TMD plays a protective mechanism to prevent the formation of heteromers that may possess altered channel properties. However, as the TRPM1 TMD is quite unstable in our structure, we cannot rule out the possibility that the presence of another ion channel with a canonical TMD can switch the handedness of the TRPM1 TMD upon heteromer formation. If this is true, heteromer formation could play important roles in regulating TRPM1 activity under physiological conditions.

Finally, we observed a large degree of structural heterogeneity in the TMD of TRPM1 (Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 6). This heterogeneity could result from the unique inverted arrangement in the TMD that has reduced stability. However, we should point out that our structure was determined in mixed micelles composed of both detergent (GDN) and lipid (POPC). As POPC is essential for stabilizing the TRPM1 structure (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3), it is likely that better membrane mimetics, such as small lipid vesicles, can substantially stabilize the TRPM1 TMD and reveal its organization at a much higher resolution.

Methods

All plasmids and cell lines generated in the manuscript are available upon request.

Cell lines

Sf9 cells (ATCC Cat# CRL-1711) were cultured in Sf-900 II SFM medium at 27 °C. HEK293S GnTl- cells (ATCC Cat# CRL-3022) were cultured in Freestyle 293 medium supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C. Flp-In™ T-REx™ 293 cells (Thermo Fisher, Cat# R78007) were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS,1 mM L-glutamine, 100 μg/mL ZeocinTM, and 15 μg/mL blasticidin at 37 °C. HEK293doxy-jGCaMP7f were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS,1 mM L-glutamine, 100 μg/mL hygromycin and 15 μg/mL blasticidin (DMEM B + H) at 37 °C. We did not authenticate any of the cell lines. HEK cells are one of the commonly misidentified cell lines (https://iclac.org/databases/cross-contaminations/).

Construct design

cDNA encoding rat TRPM1 (rTRPM1) was synthesized by Twist Bioscience. Both full-length rTRPM1 (rTRPM1FL) and rTRPM1EM (rat TRPM1 ΔC271) were cloned into the modified pEG BacMam vector with (for cryo-EM) or without (for Ca2+ imaging) a C-terminal PreScission protease cleavage site followed by enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP)52 using NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly enzyme (NEB). The Y1013C mutation (rTRPM1EM, Y1013C) was introduced into rTRPM1EM using NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly enzyme (NEB).

Baculovirus generation

Bacmids carrying rTRPM1EM were generated by transforming DH10Bac E. coli cells with the corresponding pEG BacMam vectors. Baculoviruses were produced by transfecting Sf9 cells with bacmids using Cellfectin II. Baculoviruses were harvested from Sf9 cell media by filtering the media through a 0.22 μm filter, which were then supplemented with 2% FBS for storage at 4 °C. Baculoviruses after three or four rounds of amplification were used for cell transduction. Baculoviruses used for cell transduction were stored for no more than 1 month.

Ca2+ imaging

The HEK293doxy-jGCaMP7f cell line was generated according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, Flp-In™ T-REx™ 293 cells were co-transfected with 2000 ng pOG44 and 400 ng jGCaMP7f in the pCDNA5 vector using Lipofectamine 3000. After incubation at 37 °C for 1 day, the cells were subjected to selection with 300 μg/mL hygromycin in the presence of 15 μg/mL blasticidin. Individual clonal cells were isolated using cloning cylinders and subsequently maintained in 100 μg/mL hygromycin and 15 μg/mL blasticidin (DMEM B + H).

For Ca2+ imaging experiments, adherent HEK293doxy-jGCaMP7f cells were grown to 80–90% confluency at 37 °C in 35 mm × 10 mm tissue culture dishes in DMEM B + H. The cells were then infected with 10% (v/v) rTRPM1EM baculoviruses carrying rTRPM1FL, rTRPM1EM and rTRPM1EM-Y1013C, or Sf-900 II SFM media as a vehicle control. After incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, the media was replaced with an expression media consisting of DMEM B + H further supplemented with 1 µg/mL doxycycline and 5 mM sodium butyrate. The cells were incubated at 30 °C for another 24 h before Ca2+ imaging experiments.

Immediately prior to imaging, transduced HEK293doxy-jGCaMP7f cells were washed twice with a Ca2+-free bath (1.06 mM KH2PO4, 2.97 mM Na2HPO4, 155 mM NaCl; or 10 mM Na-HEPES pH 7.4, 145 mM NaCl, 3 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 10 mM D-Glucose) to emulate physiological conditions. Bright-field and GFP fluorescent images were captured using the ECHO Revolve microscope at 20× magnification in the inverted configuration. Both the bright field and the fluorescent images were then taken under a series of conditions as follows: (1) Basal images were captured in the Ca2+-free bath; (2) For inhibition experiments, primidone-treated images were captured following addition of 50 µM primidone and 1 min incubation at room temperature; (3) Ca2+-treated images were captured following direct addition of 3 mM Ca2+ and gentle mixing; (4) For activation experiments, PregS-treated (Tocris 5376) and CIM-0216-treated (Tocris 5521) images were captured following direct addition of 20 µM PregS or 10 µM CIM-0216 after Ca2+ addition. For MTS-treated experiments with rTRPM1EM, or rTRPM1EM, Y1013C, 100 µM MTSET (Biotium 91021) was added to the cells in a Ca2+-free bath and incubated at room temperature for 5 min prior to capturing the basal image. To quantify the fluorescent images, we used an unbiased automated pipeline involving ilastik v1.4.172 and CellProfiler v4.2.873. Specifically, bright-field cell images were segmented using pixel-based classification in ilastik to generate a probability map that describes the probability of every individual pixel to be intracellular, membrane, or background. These probability maps were then fed into the CellProfiler program to mask individual cells in the fluorescence images. The fluorescence intensity of every cell under different conditions was then extracted and plotted. The box plots were generated by Qtiplot v1.2.1. The paired and independent t-tests were performed by Qtiplot v1.2.1 with significance levels of 0.01 or 0.05.

TRPM1 expression and purification

rTRPM1EM was expressed in HEK293S GnTl- cells utilizing the BacMam method51,52. Briefly, HEK293S GnTl- cells were grown in suspension at 37 °C until a density of ~3.5 × 106 cells/mL was reached, then 12% (v/v) baculovirus was added. The cells were grown at 37 °C for another 9.5 h, after which 10 mM sodium butyrate was added, and the growth temperature was shifted to 30 °C. The cells were kept at 30 °C for another 41 hr before harvest.

A POPC-GDN lipid–detergent mixture was prepared as the following. First a 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC) stock solution was prepared by drying down 10 mg POPC in chloroform (1 mL of 10 mg/mL POPC, Avanti Polar Lipids) in a glass tube under a gentle stream of argon. The lipid was then washed with ~2 mL pentane, followed by overnight drying in a vacuum desiccator to eliminate residual solvent. The next day, the resulting lipid film was hydrated with 800 µL of ultrapure water and sonicated until opalescent, yielding a suspension of 12.5 mg/mL POPC small unilamellar vesicles. To prepare the lipid–detergent mixed micelle, 240 µL of the POPC suspension (12.5 mg/mL) was combined with 500 µL of 6% (w/v) GDN solution, resulting in a final 1:10 (w/w) lipid-to-detergent ratio. The mixed micelles were incubated overnight at room temperature to ensure thorough solubilization.

Protein purification was carried out at 4 °C and was completed within 12 h. Cell pellet from a 4 L culture was resuspended in 200 mL buffer containing 20 mM Na-HEPES pH 7.4, 500 mM NaCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 0.5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT supplemented with 1 mM benzamidine, 100 µg/mL Trypsin inhibitor, 5 µg/mL E-64, 11 µg/mL pepstatin A, 11 µg/mL leupeptin, 10 μg/mL aprotinin, 100 µg/mL AEBSF, and ~200 µg/mL DNase. The cell pellet was stirred until homogeneous and gently disrupted using a Dounce homogenizer. The resulting lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 39,000 × g for 30 min. The crude membrane pellet ( ~ 44 mL) was resuspended with a Dounce homogenizer in 121 mL buffer containing 25 mM Na-HEPES pH 7.4, 625 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM CaCl2, 0.625 mM MgCl2, 1.25 mM DTT supplemented with 1.25 mM benzamidine, 125 µg/mL Trypsin inhibitor, 6.25 µg/mL E-64, 13.625 µg/mL pepstatin A, 13.625 µg/mL leupeptin, 12.5 μg/mL aprotinin, 125 µg/mL AEBSF, ~250 µg/mL DNase. The membrane suspension was then mixed with 35 mL 6% GDN (m/v, dissolved in H2O) to bring the final concentration of GDN to 1.05%. The mixture was stirred for 2 h and then the solubilized membranes were clarified by centrifugation at 39,000 × g for 30 min. The resulting supernatant was incubated with 3 mL GFP nanobody-coupled resin (6 mL 50% slurry) for 1 hr at 4 °C. The resin was first washed with 90 mL wash buffer 1 containing 20 mM Na-HEPES pH 7.4, 500 mM NaCl, 150 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 2 mM ATP, 1 mM DTT, and 0.00405%/0.000405% GDN/POPC (m/v).

The resin was then washed with 30 mL wash buffer 2 containing 20 mM Na-HEPES pH 7.4, 500 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT and 0.00405%/0.000405% GDN/POPC (m/v). The resin was resuspended with 3 mL wash buffer 2, and PreScission protease was added to the resin to a final concentration of ~0.05 mg/mL. After 1.5 h, eluted protein was collected from the flow-through and combined with subsequent washes with 12 mL wash buffer 2. The protein was then concentrated to ~800 µL using a 15 mL Amicon concentrator with 100 kDa molecular weight cutoff. Concentrated protein was then clarified by centrifugation at 20,817 × g for 3 min before being further purified by size-exclusion chromatography using a Superose 6 Increase column (10/300 GL) equilibrated with a buffer containing 20 mM Na-HEPES pH 7.4, 500 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 0.00405%/0.000405% GDN/POPC (m/v). Peak fractions were pooled and concentrated to 5.4 mg/mL using a 2 mL Amicon concentrator with 100 kDa molecular weight cutoff.

Cryo-EM grid preparation and data acquisition

To prepare cryo-EM grids, 3.5 µL of purified rTRPM1EM protein sample at 5.4 mg/mL was pipetted onto a glow-discharged Quantifoil R1.2/1.3 400 mesh holey carbon Au grid. After 20 s at 16 °C with a humidity of 100%, the grid was blotted for 4 s with a blotting force of 3 and flash frozen in liquid ethane using an FEI Vitrobot Mark IV (FEI).

For cryo-EM data acquisition at the S2C2 national facility, the grid was loaded onto a 300 keV Titan Krios G3i transmission electron microscope equipped with a SelectrisX energy filter with (10 eV slit width). The sample was collected with a Falcon 4i detector, and micrographs were collected using EPU v3.5 software. The data collection parameters were as follows; total micrographs: 18,322, total dose: 50 (e−/Å2), exposure time: 5.06 (s), frame time: 0.1265 (s), physical pixel size: 0.954 (Å), dose per frame: 1.25 (e−/Å2/frame), dose rate: 8.99 (e−/pixel/s), and a nominal defocus range of −0.6 to −1.8 (μm).

Cryo-EM image processing and model building

Cryo-EM movie stacks were gain-normalized and corrected for beam-induced motion using CryoSPARC v4.7.174 Patch Motion Correction. Parameters for the contrast transfer function (CTF) were estimated from motion-corrected summed images using CryoSPARC v4.7.1 Patch CTF Estimation.

All subsequent cryo-EM image processing was performed on dose-weighted motion-corrected summed images. The initial data processing was done in CryoSPARC v4.7.1. Briefly, particles were first picked by the automatic Blob Picker and extracted, which were then sorted by several rounds of 2D classification. The selected particles after 2D classification were subjected to ab-initio reconstruction in C1. The resulting reconstructions were subsequently used as models for heterogeneous refinement to recover some good particles discarded by 2D classification. Finally, the good particles sorted by heterogenous refinement were moved forward with further 3D classification in RELION v5.0-beta-0-commit-832d0775,76. These particles were first sorted by a global classification, and the resulting particles that gave reconstructions with channel features were pooled and refined first without a mask, then with a mask covering only the intracellular domain (ICD). To increase the resolution in the ICD, particles were symmetry-expanded in C4 and were then subsequently refined with a mask covering the ICD. This is roughly equivalent to refinement with C4 symmetry. To resolve the transmembrane domain (TMD), the non-expanded particles were classified without alignment, focused on a mask covering the pore domain (PD). The particles that displayed the most continuous pore density were then pooled and further classified without alignment using a mask covering only the voltage sensor-like domain (VSLD). The final particles were further refined in CryoSPARC v4.7.1 in C1 or C2 locally using the mask covering the entire TRPM1. For all cryo-EM maps, the mask-corrected FSC curves were calculated in cryoSPARC v4.7.1 and the resolutions were reported based on the 0.143 criterion77.

The initial model was obtained by docking the TRPM3 model (PDB ID: 8ED7) into the cryo-EM map of TRPM1. The sequence was then edited, and the coordinates were manually adjusted to fit the cryo-EM map using Coot v0.9.878. Particularly, the TMD was modified by cutting out individual S5 and S6 helices in TRPM3 and rotating them independently to fit the densities in the TRPM1 map. To facilitate model building with an intermediate-resolution cryo-EM map, EMReady v1.2.179 was utilized to enhance map quality, especially in regions exhibiting weak density. EMReady was able to substantially improve the map in the ICD region, however, only minimal improvement was observed in the TMD region. Therefore, we utilized the EMReady-enhanced map generated from C4 symmetry-expanded particles to aid model-building in the ICD. On the other hand, to build the TMD helices, we used the map refined in either C1 or C2, as the C4 symmetry is broken in the TMD. Due to the limited resolution in the TMD, we used ISOLDE v1.7.180 to improve geometry and model-to-map correlation. The models were then iteratively edited and refined in Coot v0.9.8, ISOLDE v1.7.1, and PHENIX v1.21.2-541981. The quality of the final models was evaluated by MolProbity82 in PHENIX v1.21.2-5419. All figures were prepared using UCSF ChimeraX v1.783.
