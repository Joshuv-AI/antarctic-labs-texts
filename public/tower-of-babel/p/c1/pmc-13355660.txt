Occurrence and clinical utility of observable signs for concussion assessment in elite cricket
Authors: Jaideep Sharma, Leigh Golding, Anna E. Saw, John W. Orchard, Alex Kountouris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObservable signs are an important component of multimodal concussion assessment, with further research needed to understand and improve their applicability to cricket.MethodsParticipants were elite Australian cricket players who sustained a concussive head impact during a match between July 2015 and June 2024 with video of the incident available. Videos were coded for established signs of concussion and cricket-specific signs potentially indicative of concussion. A random selection of concussive and non-concussive match head impacts were coded to investigate clinical utility of observable signs.ResultsFrom 53 videos of concussive incidents (male n = 41, female n = 12), the median number of established observable signs was 0 (IQR 0-0). The median number of cricket-specific signs per concussive incident was 2 (IQR 1–4). Ten (19%) concussive incidents had no established or cricket-specific signs. The positive predictive value of each video sign for concussion was not significant. Other players or umpire showing urgent concern for the impacted player was the most commonly observed cricket-specific sign. One video angle was sufficient to observe signs in most instances.ConclusionsElite cricket players who sustain a concussive head trauma infrequently present with established observable signs. Practitioners should be alert to other observable signs to guide whether a player needs to be immediately removed from the field of play for further assessment. Advancements in video quality and availability would further support the match-day doctor.

Key Points •Established signs of concussion are infrequently observed in cricket, underscoring the distinct presentation of concussion in cricket compared to collision sports.•Several cricket-specific signs such as urgent concern from others, the player holding their head, walking away from play, or falling to their knees may complement established signs when informing whether a player needs to be removed from play for assessment.•Video review is generally useful for identifying signs, though interpretation remains subjective and limited by current video availability and quality.

Introduction

Concussion is a topical issue in sport due to considerable short- and long-term health implications for athletes [1]. Early identification of a concussed athlete is best approached through multimodal assessment [1]. At the time of incident and immediately following, signs of concussion may be observed directly or assisted by video review. A recent systematic review of 29 studies which have investigated observable signs of concussion in sport concluded that observable signs had a low frequency and sensitivity, yet were an important component of multimodal assessment [2]. The review also highlighted a need for sport-specific research and inclusion of females [2].

Collision and contact sports (Australian Rules Football, Rugby League, National Football League, National Hockey League) have conducted studies in male cohorts focussing on key observable signs such as lying motionless, tonic posturing, showing no protective action on impact with ground, being slow to get up, motor incoordination, and having a blank or vacant look [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. These signs are consistently considered useful across sporting codes and an international consensus on definitions has been achieved [9]. The presence of observable signs does not necessarily indicate concussion, but may be used to guide whether an athlete needs to be removed from the field of play for further assessment [9].

Whilst the aforementioned observable signs are incorporated into guidelines in cricket [9], they are less commonly observed in concussed cricket players [10]. Concussive incidents in cricket typically involve impact from a ball and the player may not necessarily go to the ground [11]. Batters, wicket-keepers, and close-in fielders wear a helmet which interferes with observation of the player's face to assess for a blank or vacant look. However, a player removing or checking their helmet may be an additional observable sign [10]. Other signs shown to have clinical utility in a cohort of elite male cricket batters were the player taking more than 4 s to face the next delivery, the player stumbling, and concern shown by other players [10]. Further research is required to establish the utility of these proposed signs in other cricket cohorts, including female and non-elite players, and extend research beyond batters.

Time to recover from concussion and return to play is variable, with research to-date identifying several factors relating to the individual (e.g., concussion history, psychological health), number and severity of symptoms, and concussion management (e.g., time to diagnosis) [12]. It is not known whether the presence or absence of observable signs at the time of the incident is associated with return to play time. Research describing both observable signs and return to play times provides the opportunity to explore this.

The purpose of this study is to review video of elite male and female cricket players who sustained a potentially concussive head impact to: i) describe the occurrence of established and cricket-specific observable signs; ii) explore the clinical utility of established and cricket-specific observable signs, and; iii) explore whether the presence of observable signs is associated with time to return to play. A secondary objective is to determine whether the quality of video footage currently available in elite cricket is sufficient to reliably observe signs.

Methods

The methods complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and Australia's National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research. Ethics approval was obtained from La Trobe University Human Ethics Committee (HEC20058). Retrospective data was retrieved from Cricket Australia's online Athlete Management System (AMS) (Fair Play AMS Pty Ltd) for the 9 seasons, 1 July 2015 to 30 June 2024.

Participants were elite Australian male and female cricket players participating in State or National programs, including pathway players over 16 years. Cases of concussion were identified from medical records coded with a diagnosis of concussion between 1 July 2015 and 30 June 2023. Concussion is diagnosed at the time of injury (and following days) by the team doctor performing a clinical assessment, assisted by standardised assessments (Sport Concussion Assessment Tool (SCAT) [13] current at the time, CogSport or Cognigram (Cogstate)) [14]. Video may have been used at the time to assist assessment if available. Injury data extracted included incident details and return to play time. Return to play time was defined as the number of days a player was deemed unavailable to play or train, whilst completing a graded return to play protocol.

Cases were included in analysis if a video of the incident was available (n = 47). This limited the cases to domestic (national) and international matches, where video was recorded by staff working within Australian Cricket or television broadcast footage.

Videos were coded by two doctors with experience assessing and managing concussion in cricket and other sports: one a sports and exercise medicine physician and cricket team doctor and one a sports and exercise medicine registrar with two years’ experience. A training phase involved both doctors coding ten videos together to ensure definitions were clear. An inter-rater reliability phase involved both doctors independently coding 20 videos randomly selected using a random number generator. The registrar then proceeded to code all remaining videos. After 6 months had passed, the registrar then re-coded 37 videos (10 videos used in training phase not re-coded), blinded to previous coding, to establish intra-rater reliability.

Videos were coded for: a) quality of video to be able to determine the presence of observable signs, b) presence of established signs of concussion (Table 1), and c) presence of cricket-specific signs which may be indicative of concussion (Table 2). Established signs were subdivided as either clearly or possibly observed, lending to categorisation of head trauma (Table 1). This study did not include blank or vacant look as an established sign due to the likelihood of the cricket helmet obstructing the view and the high degree of subjectivity requiring the rater to be familiar with the player [4]. To evaluate the clinical utility of the video signs, a random selection of 34 match head impacts (6 concussive) from the 2023-24 season were coded. The registrar coding the video was blind to the recording of video signs, categorisation, and diagnosis by the treating doctor at the time of the incident.

Data extraction, coding, and analyses were completed on a spreadsheet (Microsoft Excel, Microsoft 365, Microsoft Corporation, Washington, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to characterise the players included in the sample and the presentation and management of concussions. Return to play data was not normally distributed (Shapiro–Wilk p < 0.001), so was described using medians and compared using independent samples Mann–Whitney U tests. Proportions were calculated with a Wilson 95% confidence interval (CI).

Intra-rater and inter-rater agreement of the presence/absence of video signs and categories was cross-tabulated and evaluated using the Kappa statistic (K) (SPSS, version 28 IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Instances coded as “not applicable” (e.g., batting-specific sign for a non-batter) were excluded from agreement analyses, as these did not require a rating decision. The kappa statistic returns a value between −1 (perfect disagreement) and +1 (perfect agreement), with values above 0 reflecting agreement above what would be expected by chance. Typical qualitative interpretation of kappa values is K < 0 poor, K = 0–0.20 slight, K = 0.21–0.40 fair, K = 0.41–0.60 moderate, K = 0.61–0.80 substantial, and K = 0.81–1.00 almost perfect [15]. The diagnostic value of individual video signs was evaluated using cross-tabulation to calculate positive predictive values (PPV) and Fisher's exact p-values.

Results

Concussion

Fifty-three videos of concussive incidents were available to be coded from the nine season study period. Of the 53 incidents, 41 involved male players and 12 were to female players. The average age of players at the time of incident was 24.1 ± 4.7 years. The most common mechanism of injury was a batter hit by a ball bowled by a pace bowler (n = 36). Other mechanisms were fielders or wicket-keepers hit by a ball off the bat (n = 5), impact with the ground (n = 6), or impact with a fence or another player (n < 5).

The median number of established signs per concussive incident was 0 (interquartile range (IQR) 0-0). The median number of cricket-specific signs per concussive incident was 2 (IQR 1–4). Ten (19%) concussive incidents had no established or cricket-specific signs. From the established video signs, three concussive incidents were coded as Category 1 (6%), 7 cases were coded as Category 2 (13%), and the remaining 43 cases were Category 3 (81%). Return to play time was a median of 9 days (IQR 7–13). There was no difference in return to play time between concussive incidents coded as category 1 or 2 (9 days, interquartile range 8–11) compared to category 3 (9 days, 6–13) (p = 0.450). There was no difference in return to play time between concussive incidents: with and without any established or cricket-specific signs (p = 0.889); 2 or more, or less than 2 signs (p = 0.833); 3 or more signs, or less than 3 signs (p = 0.374); whether others showed concern or not (p = 0.184), or; other individual signs (all p > 0.05).

Clinical utility

Twenty-eight additional videos of non-concussive incidents were coded from the most recent season, totalling 81 videos to assess the clinical utility of video signs. One video angle was available for all but two incidents which had multiple angles. The video continued to show the injured player for a sufficient time after the incident to be able to assess the player's behaviour in 94% of cases. The player's facial expressions were clearly visible on 12% of cases.

Inter-rater reliability for categorisation from established signs was fair (K = 0.333) and intra-rater reliability was perfect (K = 1.000). Discussion

The application of video review to identify concussive and possibly concussive head impacts in cricket is still evolving. This study aimed to first describe the presence of established and novel cricket-specific observable signs in concussive incidents in elite cricket, and second assess the clinical utility of these signs.

Of the 53 concussive incidents reviewed, only 6% featured clear established signs of concussion (Category 1). A further 13% featured possible established signs of concussion (Category 2). The predominant sign observed was balance disturbance, however inter-rater reliability was poor and presence of this sign was not a good predictor of concussion diagnosis. This may be attributed to balance disturbance being subjective and sometimes difficult to discern from normal player movements (e.g., does the batter usually stumble after playing a shot). Although established signs of concussion are uncommon in cricket, their inclusion in head trauma assessment remains necessary, as their occurrence signifies that a player must be immediately removed from play for further assessment. The majority of concussive incidents (81%) did not feature any established signs of concussion (Category 3), highlighting the different presentations of concussion between cricket and collision and contact sports and need for cricket-specific research.

Four cricket-specific signs had moderate reliability and a 50% positive-predictive value: urgent concern of other players or umpires, the player holding their head, walking away from play, or falling to their knees had moderate reliability. Consistent with previous research [10], the observation of the urgent concern of others is a key sign and reflects the integration of information such as the magnitude of impact and impacted player's behaviour in the context of other impacts witnessed and the player's usual behaviour. Other signs reflect the player taking a moment of time to recover and process the impact, whether adopting a more resting position, walking away from play, adjusting equipment, or checking their head or helmet. Observation of any such signs may be a valuable addition to the established signs, indicating the player could be removed from play for further assessment.

The presence or absence of observable signs, whether considered individually or collectively, was not associated with time to return to play in this cohort. There are several possible explanations for this finding. Firstly, observable signs may not be an indicator of concussion severity. Secondly, time to return to play is influenced by multiple individual and contextual factors not captured in this study [12]. Thirdly, the elite players in this cohort were required to step through a graduated return to play protocol which effectively set a minimum time and resulted in a low variation in return to play times.

The quality of video footage currently available in elite cricket was sufficient to reliably observe most signs, with the exception of facial expression. In practice, the utility of video to inform the match-day doctor's assessment and decision-making could be supported by access to a live video feed with instant review, and a second person to support the doctor's simultaneous requirements to review the video and run on to the field to assess the player. Because of the relatively lower rate, compared to collision sports, of both concussion itself and observable signs, concussion spotters would have far lower yield in cricket. In contexts where a team is reviewing the video, high quality multi-angle footage and clear definitions of signs are more important [4]. Continuing to progress video availability, quality, and definitions of observable signs will also facilitate future research.

Limitations of this study pertain to the retrospective design which limited the inclusion of incidents to those occurring at a match with video footage available which may have introduced a sampling bias and an insufficient number of incidents with video signs present to assess the clinical utility of signs. Nevertheless, the match incidents with a single camera angle included reflect the current real-world application of video signs in head impact assessment. Improvements in video quality and additional video angles would improve the assessment of video signs in practice and for future research purposes.

In conclusion, elite cricket players who sustain a concussive head trauma infrequently present with established observable signs. Practitioners should be alert to other observable signs, such as the urgent concern of other players and umpires and the player taking a moment of time to recover and process the impact. Players, umpires, coaches, and other personnel may provide an important supporting role in recognising and drawing attention to these behaviours. These observable signs are unlikely to be specific to elite players and may also have relevance in community cricket, where they could assist in identifying players who warrant removal from play for further assessment.

The quality of video footage currently available at the elite level is sufficient to guide whether a player needs to be immediately removed from the field of play for further assessment, however the subjectivity of many signs means interpretation can vary between practitioners. Advancements in video quality and availability would further support the match-day doctor and future research.
