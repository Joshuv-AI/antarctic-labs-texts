Boosting microparticle tracking with neuromorphic cameras by optical modulation
Authors: José Huenchual-Escobar, Pablo Solano, J Staforelli, Esteban Vera, Jorge Tapia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present a robust, non-invasive strategy to optimize the detection and tracking of Brownian microparticles using event-based cameras inspired by neuromorphic vision, enhancing their functionality beyond the internal sensor settings. By introducing artificial sway into the sensor plane with a steering mirror, we significantly increase the event recording rate, thereby improving the spatiotemporal resolution of the tracked particles. From the spatial distribution of detected events, we identify the positions of isolated particles without prior knowledge of their shapes, bypassing the limitations of tracking algorithms based on particle centroids. In our experiment, we modulated the mirror at 1 kHz, achieving up to a 400-fold enhancement in temporal resolution. To test our method, we characterize the Brownian motion of a microparticle by calculating the variance of its position and estimating the diffusion coefficient of the medium at various temperatures through the mean-square displacement calculation. Using permutation entropy, we confirm that our modulation does not affect the stochastic nature of the particle movement.Keywords: Event-based cameras, Brownian motion, Particle tracking, Detection enhancementSubject terms: Optical sensors, Imaging techniques, Optoelectronic devices and components

Introduction

Detection and tracking of Brownian microparticles play a vital role in numerous scientific fields exploring the microscale, from physics to biology1,2. The nature of these problems challenges us to detect sub-micrometer displacements at speeds below milliseconds. Here, efforts are focused on enhancing real-time data acquisition frequencies to overcome the temporal resolution limitations of traditional imaging detectors, such as complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) or a charge-coupled device (CCD)3.

Inspired by the human visual system, event-based cameras (EBCs) capture changes in light intensity rather than full frames4,5. This asynchronous sensing paradigm offers unique advantages that have not been thoroughly explored in the study of the micro regime. EBCs can detect rapid changes in light intensity with microsecond precision, enabling the tracking of fast particles. Moreover, by only capturing changes, EBCs significantly minimize data generation, which reduces computational costs and power consumption. Due to these features, using EBCs for detecting and tracking micro-objects has garnered interest over the past two decades. One pioneering work in this area, by Drazen et al.6, implements velocimetry of 950 m microspheres by converting events into frames that can be processed with standard imaging particle tracking software. Shortly thereafter, Ni et al.7 demonstrated the tracking of 12 m microspheres, detecting Brownian motion by combining the Hough circle transform and the centroid method. In addition, other applications related to fast tracking or detection of faint emission sources have been developed in different fields, including electronic microscopy, single-molecule localization microscopy, and stereo velocimetry for 3D object reconstructions8–16. However, none of these efforts have focused on improving event statistics through optical techniques that do not depend on the nature of the object of interest.

Imaging sensors can be classified into two categories: classical sensors that rely on image intensity acquisition and non-classical sensors that focus on event capture. Classical sensors may differ in their acquisition architecture. For example, in CMOS technology, pixel acquisition occurs sequentially, resulting in issues such as the rolling shutter effect, which causes images of objects to appear stretched or incomplete. This problem is closely associated with the data acquisition speed and streaming capacity. As a result, CCD sensors are used to address this challenge due to their parallel architecture for pixel data transmission. Nevertheless, both classical sensors share an essential characteristic: they rely on continuous data streaming defined by the frames per second (FPS), and the only method to increase streaming speed is to reduce the region of interest (ROI). For faster acquisition, decreasing ROI limits the number of pixels an object can occupy in the image, restricting the sampling of its shape, and ultimately the spatial resolution. This limitation is evident when tracking a particle using the centroid method; increasing the number of pixels occupied by the particle reduces the statistical error in estimating its centroid, albeit at the cost of lower temporal sampling. Refer to Fig. 1 for a visual representation of this issue.

Comparison of temporal () and spatial () resolution for classical and event-based cameras. Classical cameras acquire images with a fixed number of pixels that refresh simultaneously to form a frame. The ROI size generally determines their acquisition frequency, while the pixel-to-length ratio defines their spatial resolution. In contrast, the acquisition frequency of event-based cameras is independent of the ROI, allowing for a high acquisition rate regardless of sensor size. However, the number of detected events limits the ability to identify an object and its position, and increasing the time window to accumulate more events reduces the acquisition rate. This means that the sample, not the camera, constrains the spatiotemporal resolution. A larger stream of events improves both temporal and spatial resolution to reach the optimal acquisition rate and pixel size of the hardware (red dashed line).

In contrast, the second category changes this paradigm. The images are replaced by events associated with the activation of logical pixel values due to variations in brightness. For instance, the motion of an object alters the light intensity in the image plane at the camera sensor, thus recording events in multiple pixels depending on the shape of the object and its direction of motion. A key difference between EBCs and the classical detectors is that the acquisition frequency for this type of sensor, usually around 1 MHz, is indifferent to the ROI. A major challenge when using EBCs to observe a specific phenomenon, such as a moving particle, is capturing enough events related to that motion. A larger number of events in the image provides more data for accurately determining the centroid of the object without sacrificing image size or acquisition speed, while a faster rate of events reduces the time between acquisitions, thus improving the temporal resolution. A combination of a large number and a fast rate of events leads to a larger stream of events that translates into an improved spatiotemporal resolution of the observed object (see Fig. 1).

To convert the detected events into positions, an algorithm is required. As mentioned above, single-particle tracking (SPT) techniques are commonly used, and significant efforts have been devoted to identifying the most effective methods for localizing single particles. For example, under simulated conditions, cross-correlation is a suitable algorithm for large particles, while Gaussian fitting of the intensity distribution is preferred for point-like particles17. However, these approaches often involve elements that differ substantially from the objectives of this work, such as the use of fluorescence imaging or simulated CCD data. Furthermore, improvements in SPT algorithms have primarily aimed to improve quantitative analyses of endosomal motility18. In such cases, the presence of large, immobile fluorescent structures can lead to an underestimation of the diffusion coefficient for Brownian motion. This bias can be mitigated by applying denoising techniques. These algorithms have also been compared to methods such as image correlation spectroscopy to assess their performance. In parallel, subpixel localization algorithms based on computing the best fit center of radial symmetry have been developed19, achieving tracking accuracies near theoretical limits, such as Gaussian fitting.

Although these SPT techniques have been extensively studied, refined, and benchmarked, their typical application involves considerations that are not necessary for the present study. In particular, many rely on fluorescent labeling and specialized imaging setups. Even more important, these methods operate on frames. In contrast, our approach in this paper involves an algorithm that extracts positional information directly from the event data, based on the observation that events tend to occur along the particle’s edge. To our knowledge, this method has not been previously reported in the literature and therefore no direct comparison with existing SPT techniques has been established. Our approach requires only the events detected from the scene, without the need for coherent illumination, labeling, or frame-based acquisition. The details of the algorithm are provided in Supplementary Methods 1.

It is important to consider that the detection at sub-millisecond time scales is a key challenge in Brownian particle tracking. A well-suited class of tools for this challenge are real-time feedback tracking methods such as 3D-DyPLoT20, 3D-SMART21, orbital tracking22,23, and TSUNAMI24,25. These are well-established and powerful; however, they typically rely on more expensive and calibration-dependent optical instrumentation, including coherent laser illumination and photodiode-based position detection. In contrast, our method relies on cheaper components and is easier to adapt to existing imaging systems, offering a flexible alternative for tracking under general experimental conditions.

The performance of EBC depends strongly on the diversity of scene motion and the intensity of background-object contrast, among other factors. This requires minimizing background noise while maximizing events associated with the observed moving object. Systems that generate few events limit the potential of EBC. To address this, event generation must be increased, typically by modifying the sample or adjusting camera bias values. However, increasing events without impacting the physical phenomenon is challenging. For example, changing the temperature of the sample or using dyed particles can enhance motion diversity and contrast, but may denature live specimens. Indirect methods, such as variable illumination, can influence self-propelled particles that depend on phototaxis dynamics, as observed in certain bacteria and microalgae, or on photocatalytic propellants, as seen in Janus microparticles26, affecting both the background and the object. Furthermore, increasing the bias threshold further amplifies noise.

This work proposes a method to increase the event stream while avoiding these issues. We introduce a controlled relative motion between the camera and the image plane, which can be applied just before the light reaches the EBC, ensuring that the observed object dynamics remain unchanged. This motion is introduced into the system by a piezoelectric-controlled steering mirror. We characterize the Brownian motion of a spherical microparticle suspended in water as a testbed for our method. We can isolate the particle movement by leveraging background homogeneity in the image. This strategy enables us to controllably increase the streaming of events, thereby improving both the temporal resolution and the event-counting statistics for accurate estimation of the center of the particle. We test our technique by measuring relevant physical parameters, in particular the relation between diffusion coefficient and temperature for Brownian motion, and performing statistical analysis of the resulting trajectories, ensuring the method is artifact-free.

The article is organized as follows. First, the Methods section outlines the optical setup, detection method, and data processing protocol. Next, the Results section presents the calculation of the diffusion coefficient at various temperatures, utilizing a variance-based method for the measured particle position. Furthermore, we used a mathematical tool known as permutation entropy to verify that our artificial modulation did not introduce artifacts into the stochastic nature of the detected particle events. Finally, the discussions are presented.

Methods

Experimental configuration

Figure 2 shows a schematic of the optical setup. The setup is placed on an optical table (1.5 2 m, Thorlabs) for active mechanical isolation. We used a confocal microscope (Eclipse TE-2000U, Nikon) to observe polystyrene spherical microparticles with a nominal diameter of 3 m (n = 1.59, Kisker-Biothec PPS-3.0). The particle size was measured in the laboratory and the typical mean diameter is found to be 3.2 m with a spread of m. For this, CMOS images of adjacent particles were used. The sample holder consists of standard number-0, 0.13 to 0.17 mm thick, glass coverslips sealed with colorless nail polish; a 2450 mm one serves as the bottom and a 2020 mm as the top. The microparticles are suspended in a solution of deionized water and sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) to prevent aggregation. We implemented Kohler illumination to ensure a uniformly illuminated bright field of view. The image is formed from light transmitted through an x100 objective lens (MRD01991, Nikon), achieving a pixel-to-spatial resolution of 51.0 nm/pixel, measured with a microcalibration test target (USAF 1951, Thorlabs). We used a 1280x124 CMOS sensor camera (DCC1645C-HQ, Thorlabs) to optically inspect the sample, identifying, counting, and measuring the radii of the microparticles.

Optical system implemented to enhance the event acquisition rate in EBS-based microtracking. It comprises three stages. The first stage (green) includes a microscope setup with a visible light source illuminating a microsphere sample, a 100x objective lens with a numerical aperture of 1.4, and a beam splitter that directs light toward both an observation port and a piezoelectric mirror (PZM). The second stage (pink) provides precise temperature control for the sample and consists of a heat gun, a controller, and a thermocouple. The third stage (cyan) controls the motion by utilizing a PZM to create periodic sample movements on EBS, thereby increasing the event generation rate. The PZM is controlled by a piezo controller connected to an arbitrary function generator.

An external heat source, specifically a hot air gun, is placed 15 to 20 cm from the sample holder, the distance being adjusted to achieve the target temperature. The hot air gun provides gentle airflow at a 30 angle of incidence to minimize vibration. Although the temperature of the hot air ranges from 100 C to 400 C, the actual temperature of the sample is significantly lower due to distance and air convection. The temperature in the sample holder is digitally monitored using a thermocouple (TM-902C, Lutron) attached to the bottom, away from the direct airflow of the heat gun. Thermal paste guarantees proper thermal contact between the thermocouple and the glass holder. We varied the temperature from 18 C to 90 C, allowing us to control the environmental conditions that affect the Brownian motion of the microparticles.

To ensure the experimental conditions to which the particles are exposed, we monitored temperature uniformity on the coverslip with a thermal camera prior to measurements for all the temperatures used. This allowed us to observe and ensure that this condition is met. Furthermore, uniform temperature is expected within the 10 l sample due to its small volume and the high thermal conductivity of water.

Image acquisition

We use an EBC (Gen3S1.1, Prophesee) with a sensor that has a resolution of 640 x 480 pixels, each pixel measuring 15 m, and a dynamic range of 120 dB. Events were recorded using Metavision Studio version 3.1.2, the manufacturer-provided software, which offers a graphical user interface (GUI) that enables control over critical sensor parameters required for acquisition. The acquisition process utilizes the entire sensor size. Although numerous microparticles were present in the EBC field of view, we focused exclusively on freely moving single microparticles within a specified ROI in the images. Once a particle is selected, the ROI and illumination conditions are configured and kept constant throughout the recording. Subsequently, the EBC GUI parameters are optimized to improve event detection. Despite these efforts, the number of detected events remains consistently insufficient, regardless of parameter adjustments. This limitation compels us to artificially boost the stream of events by steering the image of the sample onto the EBC sensor, in a controllable manner.

The EBC is positioned at the image plane approximately 70 cm away, a distance carefully selected to facilitate gentle adjustments of a steering mirror (BB1-E02, Thorlabs) in the image-forming path. This mirror is mounted on a kinematic gimbal mirror mount (KX1, Thorlabs) for precise steering control. A tilt angle is driven by a piezoelectric actuator connected to a 3-axis Piezo Controller (MDT693A, Thorlabs) and controlled by a single-channel arbitrary function generator (3021B, Tektronix). The steering mirror increases the recorded stream of events by modulating the light from the sample in the image plane. As a result, a strong signal compensates for the insufficient number of events needed to estimate the position of the center of the microparticle every 50 s. In this work, we steer the mirror with a sinusoidal signal at 1 kHz, a frequency selected as a deliberate compromise between temporal resolution and hardware limitations. While lower frequencies would still enhance event generation compared to no modulation conditions, the maximum achievable temporal resolution would be reduced. The upper frequency limit is constrained by two main factors: the piezoelectric actuator, which shows deteriorating performance at frequencies above several kHz, and the response characteristics of the pixels on the EBC. Operating at 1 kHz ensures clean, distortion-free sinusoidal motion without ringing from the actuator by remaining within its linear response range. Additionally, this frequency prevents the particle edge from passing over pixels faster than their refractory period, which would lead to missed events and even incomplete trajectory reconstruction. Although this modulation frequency can be adjusted based on the specific dynamics of the sample being investigated, careful consideration of the hardware performance limits is essential to maintain reliable, artifact-free operation.

Being able to determine the position with 50 s worth of events means that our system works at 20000 fps, while for a similar ROI, our CMOS camera operates at 60fps. This is a improvement factor of over 100 in the detection rate for cameras of equivalent cost.

Another aspect considered during the implementation was the transmission of vibration between devices, especially those originating from the device driving the mirror. To avoid this problem, several aspects were considered. First, the experimental setup was mounted on a levitating optical bench (PTR12113, Thorlabs) which, according to the manufacturer, can decouple the optical system from a broad range of frequencies and attenuate the transfer of vibration between components separated 15 cm between them, meaning that all optomechanics and key elements involved in the experiment do not transfer vibrations between them. The only critical situation is the closeness between the piezoelectric-driven mirror and the microscope. However, it is entirely expected that the mirror operating frequency will not be transferred to the sample because of the large mass of the microscope and the isolation provided by its rubber base.

To promote experimental reproducibility, two key considerations related to the diffusion coefficient must be addressed.

First, while the diffusion coefficient can be estimated through a linear fit of equations 3 or 4 as a function of , this approach poses challenges with experimental data28. Moreover, in our case, the measured position combines Brownian motion with sinusoidal displacement caused by the steering mirror. This combination introduces a periodic component to the MSD curve, obscuring its linear behavior. To address this issue, we employed band-stop frequency filters to remove frequency peaks in the position data induced by the steering mirror. The filters were centered at 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7 kHz, each with a 6 Hz bandwidth. Due to their effective performance, fourth-order Butterworth filters were applied systematically using cascaded second-order sections to preserve causality36,37. After filtering, the data was segmented into 3-second clips. The relevant factor in determining the length of the clips is the number of measures contained within each clip, as a statistically reliable calculation of MSD depends on the number of points used38. Subsequently, the MSD curves were calculated for each segment and the linear model of the equation 3 was fitted with the first 800 points. Taking into account a time step of 50 s, the segment used for the fit spans 50 s to 40 ms. The resulting slope and intercept are then used to estimate the diffusion coefficient and localization error, respectively.

The second important consideration involves the surfactant used in the water to prevent agglomeration of microparticles. SDS is well-known for its ability to lower the surface tension of water, which may affect the viscosity of the medium. However, the precise impact of SDS on water is not thoroughly documented in the existing literature. From our sample preparations, we observe that the concentration of SDS is less than 0.1 [g/100ml], and as noted in the work of Kushner et al.39, the effect of SDS on the viscosity of water is reflected as a variation of for a concentration of 0.1 [g/100ml] at C. Therefore, in this work, we assume that the effect of SDS on the viscosity of water is negligible.

Permutation entropy and statistical complexity

Because the proposed method for externally increasing the number of events relies on modulating the image plane, it might be assumed that the modification of the stream of events in the EBC could alter the dynamics and statistics of the tracked microparticle. Next, we use mathematical tools to prove that this is not the case.

Permutation entropy (PE) is a mathematical tool to quantify the complexity of a time series40. It is useful for analyzing noisy and nonlinear data, making it suitable for various fields, including biology, finance, and microphysics41–46. In particular, our experiment is characterized by fractal Brownian motion, a mathematical model that generally describes the motion of Brownian particles and its PE values have been well studied47,48. PE is robust, nonlinear, computationally efficient, and invariant under monotonic transformations of the data. It is computed through an ordinal pattern analysis. First, a time series is divided into segments of an arbitrary fixed length, called the embedding dimension m. Within each segment, the values are ranked from smallest to largest, creating an ordinal pattern, which is a sequence of numbers representing the relative order of the values. The frequency of each ordinal pattern is recorded in all segments obtained from the dataset. This information is used to construct a probability distribution of the patterns. Finally, the Shannon entropy is calculated on the basis of the probability distribution of the ordinal patterns. Higher entropy values indicate greater complexity in the time series49.

The embedding dimension m defines the number of possible permutations m!, and following Bandt and Pompe’s recommendation40 later revisited by Cuesta-Frau et al.50, it is common to choose values of m such that where N is the length of the series to obtain reliable PE values.

Another relevant parameter for the PE calculation is the embedding delay , which is the time between the initial points of each segment used to calculate the ordinal patterns. The value of embedding delay varies depending on the application, but for calculations of Brownian motion, it is common to use a value of 51.

Finally, the statistical complexity (SC) metric measures the balance between disorder (entropy) and structure in a system, commonly used to analyze systems that are neither entirely chaotic nor entirely ordered. It is observed that modulation significantly affects the number of events, increasing the average number of events per time window 0.4 before modulation to 161.8 after modulation begins. This is equivalent to going from 1 event every 125 s to 1 event every 0.309 s, or a 404-fold increase in the event rate. However, the event rate is not constant when the image is steered sinusoidally; instead, we observe a periodic increase and decrease in the number of events, fluctuating from 0 to around 300. This pattern corresponds to variations in the speed at which the image of the particle moves through the sensor due to the steering mirror, and the frequency of the peaks is twice the steering frequency. This relationship opens the possibility of determining an optimal steering frequency based on a target temporal resolution.

From this we note that, at different moments in the recording, we observe different streams of events and, consequently, different object resolutions. Three segments were selected and displayed for comparison in Fig. 3b. Events are included in a color map and the positions estimated from the algorithm explained in Supplementary Methods 1 are overlaid for reference; the error bars represent the statistical error induced by the algorithm. Frame 1 shows a segment before the modulation is activated; in this case, only four events are registered, making the situation easily influenced by noise. Frame 2 is taken from near a minimum of events with the modulation activated. Here, fourteen events are registered, preferentially distributed along the mirror movement. However, the greater dispersion is not compensated for by this small number of events. Thus, the uncertainty in position is significant. Lastly, frame 3 corresponds to one of the periodic maxima of the signal after modulation was activated, where 300 events were registered with a dispersion similar to that of frame 2, but the increased number of events resulted in smaller spatial uncertainty. The error values in the y component for the three frames are 112, 711, and 82 nm, respectively. The comparatively small value for frame 1, despite the low number of events, is explained by the polarization configuration: the two points above the center have one polarization, while the two below have the opposite polarization. This results in a small standard deviation when the values are grouped.

Trajectories validation through entropy-complexity analysis

To analyze the behavior of the particles in the PE-SC space, we used the same data used for the MSD analysis without frequency filtering. Data were divided into 3-second clips and PE and SC were calculated for each clip using an embedding dimension and an embedding delay . Note that a 3-second clip with an acquisition time of 50 m comprises 60,000 points, satisfying the criteria for a robust PE estimation.

Figure 6 shows the distribution of clips in a complexity-entropy plane using a color map. This figure also compares PE and SC values for known processes, including calculations of fractal Brownian motion (fBm) at different Hurst exponents, H, and sinusoidal functions with different frequencies. Although not shown explicitly, it is important to note that a more thorough analysis of the PE and SE indicates minimal variation in their values when the amplitude and phase of the sinusoidal signal change.

The results indicate that the data points are distributed near the fBm curve, suggesting a statistical complexity similar to Brownian motion for the measured trajectories. Deviations from the expected values may be due to various factors, including noise in the position measurement and the interaction between the particle and the aqueous medium, which generates a motion not entirely Brownian.

Furthermore, there is a clear distinction between the regions of sinusoidal and Brownian motion in the complexity-entropy plane, with experimental data predominantly located in the Brownian processes region. This detection method and data analysis enable us to conclude that the proposed method effectively captures the Brownian behavior of the particles.

The results presented here support the idea that the described technique can be applied to the detection of motion of single stochastic particles7, and is naturally extended to scenarios of multiple stochastic particles through the inclusion of a segmentation step, while also being versatile enough for broader application beyond stochastic particle tracking. The increasing richness of movement information reached through our method makes it a powerful tool for several applications. Some notable examples include the rotation of birefringent particles56, the dynamic tracking of the Hexbug motion to characterize the displacement behavior of living organisms57, and the analysis of membrane fluctuation. For this last case, our technique offers a low-cost alternative for detecting stochastic fluctuations in cellular membranes, such as those of red blood cells, opening the door to simplified rheological measurements, bypassing the need for expensive equipment such as quadrant photodiodes58,59, typically required for high spatial and temporal resolution in these types of experiments.

Furthermore, these results and the challenges observed in the use of EBC and the application of the described technique suggest future research directions to improve its applicability. Examples include comparing the performance of different SPT algorithms to determine which are best suited for data obtained via EBC, identifying the optimal relationship between modulation frequency and acquisition time, incorporating a segmentation stage ideally capable of operating directly on the data of the events, and applying the technique to other types of sample, such as non-spherical objects, smaller specimens, samples with inhomogeneous backgrounds, or drift-corrected biological samples. However, the focus of the present work is the introduction of an illumination technique that enables the extraction of more information from the same system, making possible the use of EBC in samples with a low event stream.

Conclusion

We presented a cost-effective, artifact-free method for controllably increasing the stream of events in an event-based camera, enhancing both temporal and spatial resolution for microparticle tracking. The proposed technique allowed us to study the dynamics of stochastic processes at the microscale without altering their stochastic nature, demonstrated through the permutation entropy in the context of Brownian motion. Using this tool, we demonstrated that the stochastic statistical properties of the motion captured by the event-based camera remained preserved despite the introduction of an oscillatory mirror motion to increase the number of events. This fact validates the extraction of the diffusion coefficient of the medium from the data, which was successfully confirmed by comparing these values to theoretical models. Our results demonstrate that neuromorphic-based camera technology offers exceptional precision in microscopy, particularly for microparticle tracking. This advancement paves the way for innovative research in various fields where precise tracking or calibration of microparticles exhibiting Brownian motion is essential.
