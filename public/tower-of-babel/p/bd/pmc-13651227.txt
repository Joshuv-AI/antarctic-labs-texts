Towards precision medicine in placenta accreta spectrum: results from a survey-based evaluation of interactive academic international webinars
Authors: Albaro Jose Nieto-Calvache, Jose Miguel Palacios-Jaraquemada, Karin A. Fox, Ahmed M. Hussein, Hiba J. Mustafa, Amir A. Shamshirsaz, Rozi A. Aryananda, Conrado M. Coutinho, Egle Savukyne, Helena C. Bartels, Maria Alejandra Suarez-Revelo, Yalda Afshar, Eric Jauniaux
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPlacenta accreta spectrum (PAS) has emerged as a pressing global health concern as cesarean delivery rates continue to rise. Once regarded primarily as a placental disorder, PAS is now understood as an abnormal implantation process occurring within uterine scars. Although cesarean hysterectomy remains the predominant management strategy, growing evidence suggests that individualized, uterine-sparing approaches may reduce morbidity and better align with patient preferences. Traditional histopathologic classifications neither correlate with prenatal imaging findings nor reflect surgical complexity, thereby limiting their clinical utility. The PAS topographic classification, developed from intraoperative observations, offers a practical framework for surgical staging and guides patient-specific management strategies. Standardized prenatal ultrasound can anticipate surgical challenges and facilitate preoperative counseling. Despite this, few hospitals currently use this classification.MethodsThis study qualitatively evaluated the impact of collaborative virtual educational activities designed to disseminate the concepts underlying the topographic classification on the surgical practice of PAS teams. Over a six-month period in 2024, interactive weekly webinars were conducted to discuss the evolving concepts in PAS that justify a shift toward precision medicine. Six months after the webinars concluded, a structured survey documented participants’ perceptions of the impact of these sessions on the management of their PAS patients.ResultsA total of 112 invitations to participate in the survey were sent, and 91 responses were received (response rate: 81.2%). Among respondents, 97% (n = 88) reported that participation in the virtual academic discussions led to some change in their clinical practice. Thirty-four participants (37.4%) indicated that, following the webinars, they felt more confident proposing modifications to the management of patients at risk for PAS within their institutions. Participants reported that following the virtual academic discussions, they intended to use ultrasonographic evaluation to plan surgery (13 participants, 14.2%), implement individualized management based on topographic classification (18 participants, 19.8%), perform bladder dissection prior to fetal delivery in order to confirm placenta accreta spectrum (PAS) and assess its topography before selecting the appropriate treatment approach (8 participants, 8.8%).ConclusionsVirtual academic meetings designed to disseminate the PAS topographic classification fostered global collaboration, improved clinical practice, and enhanced mutual learning among PAS teams. Beyond technology, respectful, interactive discussions created a psychologically safe learning environment. Teleconferencing proved invaluable for education, research, and quality improvement, with continued support from professional societies essential for sustained progress.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09518-y.

Background

Placenta accreta spectrum (PAS) was a rare complication of pregnancy in the early 1900 [1], which has risen in prevalence, following a worldwide continuous increase in cesarean delivery (CD) rates in the last decades [2, 3]. PAS has now become a major health care issue in countries with high CD and fertility rates. The scientific community understanding of the pathophysiology of accreta placentation has shifted from that of a primary placental disorder due to abnormal villous tissue capable of invading an otherwise normal uterine wall to a secondary placental disorder due to villous tissue developing inside a uterine scar or congenital uterine anomaly where the normal decidual and myometrial mechanisms limiting the migration of the extravillous trophoblastic cells are reduced and altered [4, 5]. It is the loss of the normal uterine wall layers during scarification that leads to abnormal villous tissue attachment inside the scarred area, rather than accreta placentation being due to inherently abnormally invasive villous tissue [4, 5].

The main anatomical impact of PAS is on the utero-placental circulation and distortion of the corresponding interface with abnormal attachment of part of the placenta to the uterine wall [5, 6]. Caesarean Section is a major surgical procedure with morbidity and post-operative maternal complications greater than vaginal delivery [7]. Preoperative and intraoperative evaluation of the patient at risk of PAS remains challenging [8] Accreta placentation is synonymous with surgical complexity, particularly in patients with a history of multiple CDs and a placenta previa, as they also often present with major remodeling of the lower uterine segment (LUS) and thick abdominal and pelvic adhesions [8] These are the most complex CD (complexity Level III) and should be managed by a multidisciplinary team (MDT). Around the world, each team differs in makeup and experience based on available resources, patient characteristics, and surgical expertise. Cesarean hysterectomy remains the mainstream management approach in most centers, however there is evidence that uterine-sparing approaches, when technically feasible, are associated with decreased operative time, lower blood loss and need for transfusion, and align with patient preferences [9]. Additionally, some centers opt to leave the placenta in situ as an initial management approach [10]. Each case also differs in the location and extension of the accreta lesion and thus, no single surgical approach fits all cases. Cultural, social, and religious factors may influence the patient expectations regarding management and the psychosocial impact of hysterectomy and resulting infertility [11].

In recent years, literature has highlighted the limitations of traditional histopathological classifications and the need for clinically relevant frameworks. Topographic classification and intraoperative surgical staging have emerged as tools that, combined with prenatal imaging, may guide tailored strategies beyond hysterectomy.

Obstacles to offering patients individualized treatment still exist [20, 21]. Building robust evidence-based management strategies requires inclusive, in-depth discussions about how to overcome these limitations and to facilitate the use of international standardized protocols for collaborative research and advance toward “precision medicine” for PAS [22].

International collaboration, virtual academic meetings, and telemedicine initiatives have facilitated the exchange of expertise across borders, helping to address training gaps and the uneven distribution of PAS cases. Evolving concepts in PAS management in recent years including the use of topography to define severity, uterine-sparing surgery, ultrasound and surgical staging, gross analysis of the surgical specimen, individualized management, and international collaboration were the main focus of the discussions.

To better understand how these initiatives influence clinical practice, we conducted a series of seminal virtual academic discussion sessions over a six-month period, including open, case-based surgical forums that encouraged active participation from all attendees. This article outlines the key concepts discussed during these meetings that support the implementation of tailored management strategies for patients with PAS, as well as participants’ perspectives on the impact of these virtual activities on their clinical practice one year later.

Methods

Qualitative and exploratory, survey-based evaluation of the perceived impact among participants of periodic virtual educational sessions on their approach to managing patients with PAS.

As part of a research protocol aimed at assessing the usefulness and usability of the PAS topographic classification [14], a weekly virtual academic forum with open access to all interested participants was established [23]. PAS teams worldwide were invited through email and periodic reminders. Between January 13 and July 27, 2024, thirty 1–2-h session were held, including 10-min evidence-based theoretical overview, followed by 20 min of prerecorded clinical surgical cases, and 30–90 min of open discussion aimed at addressing questions and achieving consensus (if possible) among participants with differing viewpoints on the topic under review.

One year after the sessions concluded, a survey was conducted among participants to document the perceived impact of these academic discussions on the management of PAS patients within their institutions. Descriptive statistics were used to present the results of the survey.

The primary goal of this series of interactive virtual discussions was to develop multicenter collaborative research [14]. Each session focused on a unique aspect of PAS management. Survey based evaluation of academic session impact

One year after the webinars concluded (in august 2025), we conducted a survey among participants (see Supplementary Material) to explore the impact of the academic discussions conducted during the sessions on their clinical practice. Given that this was the first open, international, and periodic activity designed to discuss evolving concepts in PAS, we developed an exploratory analysis focused on participants’ individual perceptions. No formal institutional data or statistics were requested, only personal opinions regarding the functioning of their PAS programs after participating in the webinars.

Although the webinars were open, free of charge and accessible to anyone interested, an email distribution list was maintained, including recognized PAS researchers, members of the two international societies dedicated to the study of PAS (the International Society for Placenta Accreta Spectrum [IS-PAS] and the Panamerican Society for Placenta Accreta Spectrum [PAS-2]), as well as members of PAS teams who had previously collaborated in research or academic activities with the principal investigator’s group. The same distribution list, comprising 112 recipients, was used to send the survey invitation and two follow-up reminders at one-week intervals.

The main concepts discussed during the meetings support the individualized treatment for PAS patients and international collaboration, are described below.

Why do we need a new classification system for PAS?

PAS was historically classified by histopathologic depth: accreta, increta, percreta [24].These criteria, unchanged for nearly a century, lacked prenatal correlation [4, 5]. High-resolution ultrasound now detects > 95% of at-risk patients, but these old histopathologic criteria prevent accurate ultrasound–intraoperative correlation, limiting planning, research, and training [25].

International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) guidelines proposed combined clinical and basic histopathologic criteria [26–28]. The main histologic criteria for the diagnosis of PAS at birth is still mainly the absence of decidua with direct attachment of villous tissue to the myometrium with no description of the uterine changes associated with scarification and accreta placentation [4, 5]. As a consequence, the clinical and histopathologic do not consistently correlate with prenatal ultrasound imaging findings and do not allow the preoperative evaluation of the level of surgical difficulty and thus guide management of the individual patient [6, 8] (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Limitations of FIGO classification for individualized surgical management selection. Previously, cesarean hysterectomy was the gold standard for PAS management [17, 28, 29]. However, since 2019, individualized management based on surgical difficulty and patient factors is advocated [5, 8, 12, 25]. Currently, the FIGO classification is insufficient [12, 25], and an alternative classification is required to deliver evidence-based precision medicine in PAS.

The PAS topographic classification (Fig. 2) is based on surgical complexity: scar location/dehiscence, relationship with adjacent structures, and complexity of vesicouterine dissection (Table 1) [25, 30]. It guides management (Table 3) and supports intraoperative “surgical staging” for choosing the treatment option for each patient: one-step conservative surgery, total, or modified subtotal hysterectomy [30, 31]. Retrospective [13, 18, 32–34] and prospective [35] studies show correlation with outcomes and topographic classification. Prenatal imaging as a screening tool for surgical planning

Individualized PAS management requires classifying surgical risk via prenatal imaging [23]. The “topographic lens” can be applied through standardized ultrasound (“ultrasound staging”) [32, 35], aiding preoperative planning [26, 36]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), while costly, can map utero-placental topography but requires further study [37].

Many sonographic/MRI signs reflect uterine remodeling, and thus are not specific of accreta placentation [7, 16]. Scores like the placenta accreta index aim to improve detection [38, 39], but reproducibility and utility for surgical planning are limited [40]. More than half of PAS cases are diagnosed intraoperatively [41]. However, some of these cases are false positives [42], which may result in unnecessary hysterectomies.

With topographic classification, detailed imaging specifying lesion location and surgical difficulty is essential for individualized planning, counseling, and consent [25, 42]. Standardized ultrasound sweeps [25, 42–45] (Fig. 3A–B) and inclusion of ultrasound operator graphics descriptions that summarize their ultrasound evaluation in addition to written reports, improve reporting [25, 42, 44] (Fig. 4). Transvaginal evaluations aid management selection since lesions in the LUS and cervix increase complexity [33, 43, 45, 46]. Standardized ultrasonographic sequences have demonstrated reproducibility when performed by expert operators and subsequently reviewed by colleagues in other countries. Even when clinicians lack prior training in telemedicine or are not accustomed to providing an opinion based on images obtained by others, remote consultation can still be feasible. Tele-ultrasonography with remote reading has shown 71.7% agreement with local teams, with 16.9% overestimation and 11.3% underestimation of severity [44]. Tailored management of PAS: Is precision medicine realistic?

Despite multiple expert-based guidelines, evidence remains weak [48]. Only three recommendations have level B evidence: standardized protocols, delivery at high-volume centers, and neuraxial anesthesia. Many “gold standard” practices, including routine hysterectomy for all suspected cases, lack evidence. A feasibility RCT of 60 patients comparing one-step conservative surgery versus hysterectomy [35] found similar outcomes for both procedures; secondary analysis favored conservative surgery for eligible patients. Other strategies, including leaving the placenta in situ, may be appropriate in select or resource-limited cases [10].

Experimental studies in PAS are challenging [35] but feasible with proper methodology and oversight [49]. International collaboration and telemedicine can facilitate these trials [25].

Precision medicine, central to oncology, tailors surgical management based on disease biology while avoiding overdiagnosis and overtreatment [50]. Similarly, recognizing that routine hysterectomy for all accreta cases is excessive supports individualized management.

Radical surgery was once standard for abnormal gynecologic bleeding [51] and breast cancer [15] but is now uncommon. Similarly, improved understanding of PAS pathophysiology has identified distinct clinical subtypes for which a uniform surgical approach is inappropriate [12, 25]. Although challenging, advances in PAS biology, diverse patient expectations, and the safety of different techniques support pursuing individualized management [4] aligned with precision medicine principles.

Before attending the meetings, baseline familiarity with the different PAS-related concepts varied. For several topics, including modified subtotal hysterectomy and the Ward maneuver, approximately one-third of respondents reported no prior knowledge (30.7% and 31.8%, respectively). In contrast, higher baseline familiarity was reported for ultrasound staging (43.9%) and manual aortic compression (43.9%), with participants indicating that they were already using these approaches in clinical practice. After participation, most respondents rated the meetings as highly useful: 75.8% described them as “extremely helpful,” and 22% as “very helpful,” while no participant rated them as not helpful. Sixty-three percent reported that no changes to the meeting format were necessary.

Regarding implementation, participation in the meetings facilitated the introduction or consolidation of multiple procedures and concepts. Between 50.6% and 66% of respondents reported that learning the details and indications during the sessions was crucial to applying specific concepts in practice, including topographic classification (63.7%), ultrasound staging (62.6%), surgical staging (66%), one-step conservative surgery (61.5%), and manual aortic compression (50.6%). In the year following the meetings, 90.1% reported applying the topographic classification, 94.5% ultrasound staging, and 84.6% surgical staging in clinical practice. Participants also described specific changes in management, including increased attention to preoperative ultrasound planning (14.2%), implementation of individualized surgical management based on topographic principles with reduced routine hysterectomy (19.8%), performance of bladder dissection prior to fetal extraction to confirm PAS topography (8.8%), incorporation of additional surgical strategies (9.9%), and increased confidence in proposing institutional changes in PAS management (37.4%).

Discussion

Participation in virtual academic activities aimed at facilitating individualized management of patients affected by PAS was evaluated positively by webinar participants. Most participants (97%) reported changes in the management of their PAS cases, enabling the introduction of new diagnostic or surgical procedures that had not been previously implemented.

Our discussions have highlighted the need to provide support to healthcare providers worldwide, and how the open and bidirectional exchange of technical and practical details regarding the diagnosis and management of PAS is considered highly valuable by professionals from diverse backgrounds, including those with extensive prior experience in the field. The fact that nearly all participants reported the academic sessions as useful and leading to some change in their clinical practice (Table 4) is particularly striking.

Our discussions have revealed how the prevalence is significantly higher in regions with combined high CD and fertility rates, such as Egypt [8, 43], Turkey [56] and several Latin American countries [57, 58], where PAS has become a significant public health concern. In these regions, designing strategies to improve clinical outcomes must focus on addressing resource scarcity and overcoming entrenched practices among obstetric surgeons (resistance to change, inflated ego, reluctance to collaborate with other professionals). Conversely, in regions with low birth rates, such as most European countries, China, Japan and South Korea, the main barrier to incorporating new surgical concepts includes the relatively low frequency of patients with PAS [59]. The low numbers of patients with repeat CD, particularly those with multiple CDs (≥ 2) limits open surgery training opportunities [8], and increase resistance to change within academic circles. Additionally, quality assessment and program improvement, research infrastructure and financial support vary significantly across countries. Global Surgery promotes recognition of regional practices and encourages international collaboration for mutual benefit and more efficient problem-solving [60].

It is estimated that it takes approximately 17 years for a clinically useful intervention to go from publication to reach the population that could benefit from it [61]. This timeline could be shortened by acknowledging the significant gaps in scientific knowledge about PAS and recognizing the need for an international prospective effort to build high-quality evidence, underscoring that applying Global Surgery principles could be extremely beneficial.

At the time of the publications of the FIGO, ACOG and Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists guidelines in 2018 a PubMed search using the key word “placenta accreta” identified 2002 articles since inception [62]. A recent systematic review [48] identified 4,226 MEDLINE records addressing perioperative interventions in the context of planned cesarean hysterectomy for PAS up to February 2022. Most studies were observational, with only three randomized controlled trials (RCTs), the majority of which evaluated vascular interventions conducted in single institutions. Only until 2024 was a multicenter prospective RCT comparing two types of surgical management published [35]. This highlights the “research waste” [63], given the low level of scientific evidence available to support one intervention over another in PAS management. Publications have primarily focused on describing the outcomes of techniques specific to certain hospitals, often relying on historical comparisons within the same hospital, an approach that inherently introduces bias. Many studies rely upon a database only including patients with accreta placentation confirmed by “histopathology” but without describing the criteria used and patients with large dehiscence of LUS and placenta previa with no evidence of abnormal placental attachment could have been included [64]. Furthermore, many authors also include cases of uterine atony and placental retention in their cohort with complete placental delivery [4, 5]. Finally, most publications use blood loss as the main variable to measure, which is challenging to determine accurately whether using estimated or quantitated blood loss, and perhaps not as clinically significant a variable as units of blood products transfused, end-organ injury, need for reoperation or longer-term sequelae.

This study is limited by its exploratory and survey-based design, relying on participants’ self-reported perceptions rather than objective institutional data. The webinars were voluntary, potentially introducing selection bias toward more engaged or experienced participants. Variability in baseline experience with PAS concepts, differences in institutional resources, and heterogeneous local practices may have influenced responses. Additionally, the impact of virtual discussions on long-term clinical outcomes was not directly assessed, and the study did not include formal evaluation of procedural proficiency or patient-level metrics. Finally, while international representation was broad, disparities in access to technology and case volume may limit generalizability of the findings across all settings.

It is now essential to develop high-quality, prospective, collaborative, multicenter studies focused on the needs of patients and MDTs [65]. To ensure that comparisons and observations are valid, participating centers must apply consistent diagnostic criteria, employ standardized surgical approaches and allow for external supervision and auditing. This requires some hospitals to adapt their practices to incorporate newer procedures [35].

Ensuring comparable outcomes across high-volume, resource-limited hospitals and technologically advanced centers with lower patient volumes remains a significant challenge. International collaboration and telemedicine may help reduce these disparities [25].

Our observation suggests that for MDTs experienced in managing PAS, participation in virtual activities with a surgical focus and open discussion of questions is a useful strategy to facilitate the incorporation of new diagnostic and therapeutic interventions.

We plan to continue to use teleconferencing to oversee research standardization, and to provide logistical support to groups that lack local resources for auditing outcomes and designing improvement plans [66]. The success of future virtual collaborations hinges upon each multidisciplinary team’s open-mindedness and willingness to adapt for the sake of clinical excellence and scientific rigor. Societies dedicated to PAS and maternal health play a crucial role in fostering this type of international collaboration and we call upon them for continued support.

Conclusion

Virtual academic discussions on PAS promoted international collaboration and facilitated the adoption of individualized, topography-guided surgical management among multidisciplinary teams. Participation led to increased use of ultrasound and surgical staging, uterine-sparing techniques, and greater confidence in modifying institutional practices. These findings underscore the value of structured virtual education in translating emerging concepts into practice, bridging training gaps, and supporting precision medicine for PAS. Future prospective, multicenter studies with standardized protocols are needed to generate high-quality evidence and reduce global variability in outcomes.
