The effect of antenatal common mental disorders on infant birth weight at the Dabat health and demographic surveillance site, Northwest Ethiopia: a community-based cohort study
Authors: Helina Abebe Kurbi, Solomon Mekonnen Abebe, Netsanet Worku Mengistu, Alemayehu Teklu Toni, Tadesse Awoke Ayele
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAntenatal mental disorders often go unrecognized and untreated, potentially affecting birth weight in low- and middle-income countries. While studies in high-income countries have reported an association between antenatal common mental disorders and low birth weight, evidence from low-income countries such as Ethiopia remains inconsistent and limited. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the effects of common antenatal mental disorders and other psychosocial factors on the risk of low birth weight at the Dabat Health and Demographic Surveillance Site in Northwest Ethiopia.MethodsA community-based cohort study was conducted at the Dabat Health and Demographic Surveillance Site in Northwest Ethiopia. Pregnant women in the second or third trimester were recruited and screened for antenatal common mental disorders using the World Health Organization Self-Reporting Questionnaire-20. Participants were followed prospectively until delivery. The primary outcome, low birth weight, was defined as a birth weight less than 2500 g, measured with a calibrated digital scale within 24 h of delivery. Modified Poisson regression was used to estimate relative risks associated with predictors of low birth weight, and generalized structural equation modeling was used to estimate the direct and indirect effects of antenatal common mental disorders and other psychological risk factors on low birth weight.ResultsThe cumulative incidence of low birth weight among infants born to mothers with common mental disorders during pregnancy was 33%. The risk of low birth weight was 4.27 (95% CI:2.78, 6.56), 2.66 (95%CI:1.81, 3.89), 3.10 (95%CI:1.87, 5.14), and 2.60 (95%CI:1.48, 4.56) times higher for pregnant mothers with antenatal mental disorders, preterm birth, intimate partner violence, and challenges in accessing food, respectively. Antenatal common mental disorders had both direct (β = 2.4) and indirect(β=-0.17) effects on low birth weight, with social support acting as a mediating factor in the relationship between antenatal common mental disorders and low birth weight.ConclusionThe incidence of low birth weight was high in the study area. Antenatal common mental disorders and psychosocial risk factors significantly increased the risk of low birth weight. Interventions that focus on increasing social support and antenatal screening for common mental disorders will help reduce adverse birth outcomes.

Background

Antenatal common mental disorders (ACMDs) refer to anxiety, depression, and somatic symptoms that occur during pregnancy [1]. Globally, 10% of pregnant women experience mental disorders, with higher rates in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) of approximately 16% [2]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis conducted in Ethiopia found that the prevalence of ACMD was 27% [3]. Inadequate detection of ACMD and a lack of available services frequently result in women remaining undiagnosed and untreated, suffering in silence, significantly impairing quality of life, and endangering pregnancy outcomes, especially by impeding fetal development and leading to complications such as low birth weight (defined as a birth weight of less than 2,500 g) [4].

Globally, an estimated 15–20% of all births are classified as low birth weight (LBW), accounting for over 20 million births each year [5]. Approximately 91% occur in low-and middle-income countries (LMICs), with 48% in South Asia and 24% in Sub-Saharan Africa [6]. LBW is a critical indicator of maternal and fetal health outcomes and is associated with neonatal mortality, stunted growth, developmental delay, and long-term chronic complications in adulthood [7].

Antenatal CMD has become a global public health concern due to its impact on maternal and infant health outcomes. A growing body of evidence suggests that antenatal CMDs are associated with adverse birth outcomes, including birth weight [8, 9]. Evidence from the umbrella review shows that ACMD significantly increases the risk of low birth weight. These risks are higher in low-income rural areas where socio-economic challenges and limited mental health care exacerbate the effects of antenatal CMD [10]. Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain the causal relationship between ACMD and LBW. ACMD can lead to LBW through physiological, behavioral, and psychosocial pathways. Physiologically, mental distress during pregnancy is associated with increased stress levels, which affects the release of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis [11, 12]. This stimulates stress hormones such as cortisol and catecholamines, resulting in placental hypo-fusion and subsequently reducing oxygen and nutrients to the fetus, ultimately leading to adverse birth outcomes such as low birth weight and preterm birth [13].

Moreover, CMD during pregnancy has been linked to symptoms like fatigue, irritability, sleep difficulties, and poor concentration, which affect their self-efficacy and pregnancy preparedness, leading to low attendance at ANC visits, poor nutrition, and reduced self-care. These behavioral factors can increase the risk of low birth weight [14]. Additionally, ACMD is often associated with poor social support, exposure to violence, and previous traumatic events, all of which can amplify stress and worsen symptoms of depression and anxiety during pregnancy, and increase the risk of low birth weight [15, 16].

Previous studies on the association between ACMD and LBW in LMICs, including Ethiopia, have yielded conflicting results [8, 17–19]. Some studies have reported a significant association between ACMD and LBW [9, 20–26], while others found a null association [27–30]. The heterogeneity in methodologies and populations contributes to these variations, highlighting the need for further research. Additionally, these conflicting findings may be attributed to variations in how ACMD is measured and to contextual factors, including unique sociocultural and healthcare contexts that may influence these outcomes.

Furthermore, limited studies done in Ethiopia have examined the association between ACMD and LBW [21, 22]. Although Dadi et al. [31] examined the structural pathway between antenatal depression and adverse birth outcomes, their study mainly focused solely on depressive symptoms, which overlooks the concurrent anxiety and somatic symptoms. Moreover, their study did not assess intimate partner violence and life-threatening conditions. A globally known risk factor significantly confounds the association between maternal mental health and birth weight. A crucial gap remains regarding the comprehensive concepts of how common maternal mental disorders impact infant birth weight. In contrast, significant confounders such as intimate partner violence and life-threatening conditions were not adjusted.

To enhance the comprehensiveness of the findings, it would be practical to consider a broader range of symptoms that pregnant women might face, including antenatal depression, anxiety, and somatic symptoms. CMD constructs can be essential measures of community well-being, particularly when combined with other psychosocial risk factors that may indicate population well-being and risk [32]. This lack of consensus underscores the need for a more comprehensive and methodologically sound investigation that explores potential causal pathways among psychosocial stressors, antenatal CMD, and LBW. Therefore, addressing this research gap could significantly enhance understanding of the complex relationship.

Given the inconsistent findings and the limited research on causal path analysis, the current community-based cohort study was conducted to identify the incidence and predictors of LBW, focusing on the outcome of exposure to antenatal CMD and other psychosocial risk factors during pregnancy in Northwest Ethiopia. We also determined the causal mechanisms between stressors, mediators, and LBW to assess the direct and indirect association between the identified covariates and low birth weight using Generalized Structural Equation Modeling (GSEM). GSEM with a logit link function was used to allow estimation with the categorical outcome and mediator variables [33].

Methods

Study setting and period

The study was conducted at the Dabat Health and Demographic Surveillance System (HDSS). The University of Gondar established the Dabat HDSS site in 1996. Currently, the Dabat HDSS site encompasses 13 Kebeles (4 urban and nine rural), more than 21,000 households, and a population of 70,000 in the Dabat district. This system generates longitudinal information on vital events (births, deaths, migrations, and changes in marital status) to estimate population-level causes of death using the verbal autopsy method. Dabat HDSS utilizes health and demographic data, including events, social and economic conditions, pregnancies, births, and disease outbreaks, to inform policies and programs to improve the local population’s well-being [34]. The study was conducted from June 2, 2022, to July 30, 2023.

Study design and population

A community-based prospective cohort study was conducted among pregnant women to determine the effect of maternal common mental disorders on low birth weight. All pregnant women in the second and third trimesters who were located and registered in the HDSS were eligible for the study. Pregnant women unable to communicate due to severe medical or psychiatric conditions were excluded from the study. During fieldwork, no eligible pregnant women met this exclusion criterion. Our established clinical protocol mandated that if severely ill or uncommunicative individuals were identified, research activities be postponed to prioritize urgent clinical referral and transportation to the University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital.

Sample size determination

This research is part of a larger PhD project examining the effect of common perinatal mental disorders on birth weight and infant growth outcomes in the Dabat HDSS. The characteristics of the study population at baseline are described elsewhere [35]. The sample size calculation was estimated based on the estimated effect of antenatal depression on low birthweight [23]. The sample size was calculated using Epi Info version 7 [36]. The double population formula was used with the assumption of a 95% level of confidence, 5% margin of error, 90% power, exposed to non-exposed ratio of 1:2, and the prevalence of low birth weight among those free from antenatal depression of 21%, an effect size of 1.5, and 10% loss to follow-up; as a result, the final sample size was estimated 946.

Exposure measures

The primary exposure variable, ACMD, and all other covariates were assessed during the second and third trimesters of pregnancy. Face-to-face interviews were conducted. ACMD was defined as common mental disorders as an elevated level of psychological distress encompassing symptoms of anxiety, depression, and somatic complaints as measured by the WHO Self-Reporting Questionnaire-20 (SRQ-20) tool. The SRQ-20, developed by the WHO [39], was adapted for use in the Ethiopian context [40]. This tool has twenty yes/no questions about experiences of depression, anxiety, and physical symptoms in the past month, offering a thorough evaluation of psychological distress during that period. The SRQ-20 has been validated among perinatal women in rural and urban populations, with a cutoff ≥ 6, yielding a sensitivity of 90.7% and a specificity of 80.7% [40, 41]. The Cronbach’s alpha for internal consistency was α = 0.81.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the incidence of low birth weight among infants. Birth weight was measured using a SECA scale, accurate to the nearest 0.1 gram, within 24 h of delivery at their home. LBW was defined as a weight of less than 2,500 g at birth [37]. The scale was calibrated and checked correctly. Trained Dabat HDSS data collectors in each village measured birth weight and received prompt information about the women’s deliveries from village informants. Gestational age was calculated using either the last average menstrual period or ultrasonography data obtained from the women during their ANC service. Preterm birth was considered as a birth occurring before 37 completed weeks of gestation [38].

Data collection procedure

Data collection was conducted electronically using the online data kit application tool [39]. Data were collected by 28 trained diploma graduates who have been working at the Dabat HDSS site. In addition, seven MSc holders supervised the data collection process. The team, trained in the district’s local language, received theoretical and practical training before data collection began. The principal investigator maintained regular contact with the team to ensure adherence to proper data collection procedures. A pre-test was conducted on 10% of the sample in the Dabat district, outside the selected kebeles, two weeks before the actual data collection period. All collected data were stored on the Dabat HDSS server. The team uploaded the completed data forms to the server daily via a wireless connection.

Instrument

The study examined the prevalence of common mental disorders during pregnancy by utilizing the WHO Self-Reporting Questionnaire-20 (SRQ-20). This questionnaire includes 20 items that assess symptoms of depression, anxiety, panic, and somatic issues experienced in the past month, providing a general indication of psychological health distress [40]. The SRQ-20 questionnaire has been pre-validated for pregnant and postnatal women in the Butajira population [30]. The Oslo Social Support Scale (OSSS-3) [41] was used to measure maternal social support during pregnancy. The OSSS-3 consists of three items, each measured on a Likert scale. The scores for these items are combined to yield a total score ranging from 0 to 14. A score below 9 signifies ‘poor’ social support, while a score between 9 and 14 indicates ‘strong support.’

The “Life-Threatening Experience Questionnaire” was used to evaluate life-threatening experiences during pregnancy. It consists of 12 items that assess events such as death, illness, conflicts, and property damage. The questionnaire was adapted to fit the local conditions and time frames of the current pregnancy and translated into Amharic. The scale has been rigorously tested and has shown good test-retest reliability, with Kappa values ranging from 0.61 to 0.87. Furthermore, the questionnaire possesses predictive validity and is valuable for assessing and managing life-threatening situations during pregnancy [42].

This study also examined how effectively a pregnant woman can manage stress by utilizing specific coping techniques from the Perinatal Coping Inventory (PCL-4), which consists of four levels [43]. Additionally, the WHO Multicounty Study Questionnaire was used to assess the presence of any intimate partner violence. The questionnaire included questions concerning psychological, physical, and sexual violence, and one affirmative response indicated the occurrence of violence [44].

During the initial interview, pregnant mothers were asked about their reproductive histories, including the number of children, history of miscarriages, stillbirths, and low birth weight. Based on their responses, the researchers classified a history of adverse pregnancy outcomes as having experienced one or more of the following: miscarriage, stillbirth, or preterm birth. The participants were also questioned about their pregnancy planning—whether they intended to become pregnant at the time of conception, planned to become pregnant later, or did not intend to become pregnant at all. Any unplanned or only intended pregnancy during the last period was categorized as unplanned. The Middle-Upper Arm Circumference (MUAC) tape measures maternal nutritional status during pregnancy. An 18–22 cm score indicates underweight, while a 22.5–31 cm score is considered normal [45].

Data analysis management

The data from the online Open Data Kit (ODK) server were imported into Stata version 16 for data management and analysis. Summary measures were used to explore the data. Categorical data were examined and compared using a chi-squared test based on the participants’ exposure status. Typically, binomial and Poisson regression are recommended to estimate the relative risk for low birth weight [46]. However, as they are commonly known for their Convergence, problems may arise, leading them to overstate the relative risk estimate. However, this problem can be rectified using a robust error variance procedure known as the modified Poisson regression model [47]. In our study, we estimated the relative risk using a generalized linear model with identity link and binomial link function (log-binomial), and we encountered convergence issues similar to those in the previously reported literature [48–50].

The incidence risk ratio was selected as the appropriate measure of effect because it is less prone to misinterpretation. Bivariable and multivariable analyses assessed the relationships between covariates and the outcome variable. Crude and adjusted relative risks and 95% confidence intervals were presented for predictor variables with p-values under 0.05. Goodness of fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow statistic and the Omnibus test. Multicollinearity was assessed using each predictor’s variance inflation factor (VIF). All VIF values were below the 5 thresholds, with the highest at 1.10, indicating no significant multicollinearity among the independent variables. Before the final modified Poisson regression model, a formal sensitivity analysis was conducted by removing the psychosocial covariates with small cell counts (social support and stress coping), which were evaluated for their suitability because sparse categories may lead to unstable parameter estimates, and these variables were excluded from the final adjusted modified Poisson regression model.

A mediation analysis using Generalized Structural Equation Modeling (GSEM) was conducted to determine the direct and indirect relationship between the identified covariates and LBW. A GSEM with a logit link function was used for precise estimation while accommodating categorical outcomes and mediator variables [51]. A relative measure of model fit was conducted using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). This approach was informed by expert knowledge of the causal pathways, leading to the selection of the model that exhibited the optimal fit. To analyze the direct, indirect, and total effects of the predictors and mediators, we used the user-defined ldecomp command [52]. The direct, indirect, and total impact results are shown as unstandardized beta coefficients, standard errors, and p-values [53]. Analysis was conducted using Stata version 16.0 (Stata Corp, USA).

Result

Cohort characteristics

During the survey, 888 pregnant women were contacted, and 16 were excluded due to absenteeism or refusal to participate. Following the follow-up in the DHSS Dabat district, 872 pregnant women were interviewed. Finally, 824 of the 872 participants were followed into the postnatal period. During the follow-up, 48 participants were not included for various reasons, such as failure to measure weight within 24 h (n = 25), A sensitivity analysis using a chi-squared test demonstrates that these exclusions were randomly distributed across geographic boundaries, with no significant difference in missing data between rural and urban residents (Pearson chi2 [1] = 0.0081, P = 0.928), stillbirth (n = 7), early neonatal death (n = 5), twins (n = 1), and loss to follow-up (n = 10). The survey response rate was 94.4%.

The multivariable modified Poisson regression model indicated that ACMD significantly increased the risk of low birth weight (ARR = 4.27; 95% CI: 2.78, 6.56). This finding suggests that mothers with common antenatal mental disorders had more than four times the risk of delivering low-birth-weight infants. The risk of low birth weight was 2.66 times higher (95% CI: 1.81, 3.89) for preterm deliveries than for term deliveries. In the past three months, participants without access to food had a notably higher risk of low birth weight (ARR = 2.60; 95% CI: 1.48–4.56). Those who experienced intimate partner violence during pregnancy faced a greater risk of LBW (ARR = 3.10, 95% CI: 1.87, 5.14) than their counterparts (Table 3).

Table 4; Fig. 2 present the results of the mediation analysis using path analysis. ACMD had a significant direct effect on LBW (β = 2.34, SE = 0.34, p = 0.001). ACMD also indirectly affected low birth weight through social support (β = -0.17, SE = 0.09, p = 0.024). The total effect of antenatal CMD (β = 2.20, SE = 0.25, p = 0.001) remained significant, indicating a strong association between maternal mental health and low birth weight. These findings highlight the independent and combined pathways through which ACMD affects LBW.

Preterm birth had a significant direct effect on low birth weight (β = 1.51, SE = 0.32, p = 0.001), confirming that preterm infants were at substantially higher risk of low birth weight. Regarding intimate partner violence, the path analysis indicated that IPV had a direct effect on low birth weight (β = 1.71, SE = 0.26, p = 0.001). Additionally, there was a significant indirect effect of IPV on LBW through social support (β = -0.08, SE = 0.04, p = 0.012), indicating that social support partially mediates this relationship by reducing IPV’s impact on LBW. Furthermore, the total effect of IPV on LBW remained significant (β = 1.61, SE = 0.31, p = 0.001) (Table 4; Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study determined the incidence of low birth weight. It examined the association between antenatal mental disorders and other psychosocial factors and low birth weight at the Dabat Health and Demographic Surveillance Site in Northwest Ethiopia. The incidence of low birth weight was 8.74%, and antenatal common mental disorders significantly increased the risk of low birth weight.

The incidence of LBW in this study was 8.74%, consistent with findings from the public hospital of Addis Ababa (8.8%) [54], Adwa North, Ethiopia (8.8%) [55], and EDHS data analysis conducted from 33 Sub-Saharan Countries (9.76%) [56]. This finding is lower than SRMA studies in Ethiopia [57] and Demographic and Health Survey data (EDHS, 2016) [58, 59]. Low birth weight is a significant contributor to neonatal deaths, as infants born with low birth weight are at higher risk of complications, including respiratory distress, infections, and long-term developmental issues [60]. Addressing the incidence of low birth weight has been identified as a significant area for achieving the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs 2 and 3: Zero Hunger and Good Health and Well-being), which aim to ensure healthy lives and promote well-being for all ages [61], with a primary focus on reducing neonatal mortality.

ACMD significantly increases the risk of low birth weight, indicating a more than fourfold increase. This strong association underscores the critical impact of maternal mental health on low birth weight. These results suggest that the physiological and psychological stress related to hormonal dysregulation and chronic stress leads to changes in the Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Adrenal axis (HPA), which stimulates cortisol production and release [61], potentially restricting the flow of nutrients and oxygen to the fetus [62]. This finding aligns with a recent systematic review [63], which emphasizes the profound effect of ACMD on increasing the risk of LBW compared with their counterparts. Similarly, an umbrella review of the global burden of ACMD and their association with adverse birth outcomes reported an increased risk of LBW with ACMD [10] supporting these study findings.

Additionally, ACMD indirectly impacts low birth weight through social support. The indirect effect was negative and statistically significant, indicating that social support mitigates the adverse effects of antenatal CMD on birth weight [64]. Pregnant women who have good social support are more likely to have improved mental, psychological, and emotional health compared with their counterparts [65]. The Buffering effects of social support on maternal mental health and adverse birth outcomes support this study finding, indicating social support during pregnancy was associated with improved maternal mental health and birth outcomes [20, 66]. The current finding expands the literature by demonstrating that social support not only directly reduces the risk of low birth weight but also mediates the adverse effects of maternal mental disorders. This highlights the urgent need for targeted intervention to address maternal mental health during pregnancy by integrating mental health screening and treatment into routine antenatal care [67], which has not yet been implemented in Ethiopia [68].

The risk of low birth weight was 2.2 times higher among preterm births, consistent with previous studies [69–72]. This finding provides valuable insights into the relationship between preterm birth and LBW. The direct effect of preterm birth on low birth weight was strong and statistically significant, suggesting plausible biological mechanisms that may explain this relationship. Infants born prematurely have less time in the mother’s uterus for growth and development, and the organs affecting their ability to gain weight may not be fully developed. In addition, they may miss out on some essential nutrients they would have received during the last week of pregnancy, which affects their ability to gain weight and, as a result, may cause long-term consequences for the infant [73] consistent with previous studies emphasizing the importance of biological mechanisms such as placental insufficiency, intrauterine growth restriction, and other clinical factors that influence birth weight outcomes for preterm infants [74].

Experiencing intimate partner violence during pregnancy significantly increases the risk of LBW. This finding is consistent with previous research that highlights the negative impact of intimate partner violence on maternal and neonatal outcomes [75, 76]. Furthermore, Participants who faced a loss of food access during the past three months of pregnancy had a significantly higher risk of low birth weight. This finding aligns with previous studies that consistently demonstrate the determining impact of food insecurity on low birth weight. This highlights the need for direct intervention to tackle food insecurity rather than relying solely on psychosocial support, reflecting the unique nature of food insecurity as a structural determinant of health, deeply rooted in socio-economic disparities and systemic inequities.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The main strength of this study was that it was conducted prospectively in a large community-based sample, making the results likely representative of the effects of ACMD on LBW and the incidence and factors predicting low birth weight in the region. We employed two different approaches to assess the effect. Using the modified Poisson regression model improved the precision of estimates and allowed us to interpret the findings in terms of relative risk rather than odds ratio, thereby enhancing interpretability. Finally, the path analysis enabled us to evaluate each factor’s direct and indirect effects, as well as potential mediators of the indirect effect.

However, the current study had limitations. First, assessing antenatal common mental disorders with a self-report tool may lead to over- or underestimation. Second, the typical challenges of classifying preterm births and low birth weight should be acknowledged, mainly because ultrasound-estimated gestational age was unavailable for most women. Measuring preterm births relied on the accuracy of the last menstrual cycle, and birth weight was recorded at home rather than in healthcare facilities. Third, CMD was measured only once at enrollment (during the second or third trimester); therefore, the study could not capture new CMD cases that developed after the initial assessment. This may have led to exposure misclassification, which could have attenuated the observed association. Therefore, any interpretation of these outcomes should be approached with caution. Additionally, a formal sensitivity analysis was performed for covariates with small cell counts (social support and stress coping), and the test confirmed that these small cell counts did not affect the regression estimates or the primary conclusions. Post-estimation and sensitivity analyses confirmed full numerical convergence and stable standard errors and parameter estimates, indicating that these small cell counts did not affect the regression estimates or the primary conclusions. Residual confounding due to measurement and categorization limitations cannot be ruled out. Future large-scale studies are recommended to further investigate these subcategories.

Conclusion

The incidence of low birth weight was high in the Dabat HDSS in Northwest Ethiopia. Preterm birth, limited food access, intimate partner violence, and antenatal common mental disorders were independent predictors of low birth weight. Social Support partially mediated the association between antenatal common mental disorders and low birth weight. As such, interventions that focus on increasing social support and antenatal screening for common mental disorders may reduce the risk of low birth weight.
