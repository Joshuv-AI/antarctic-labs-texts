Association between high-density lipoprotein cholesterol-related inflammatory markers and female infertility: a cross-sectional analysis from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES 2013–2018)
Authors: Siqi Cao, Meiying Cai, Haimei Zhang, Hailong Huang, Liangpu Xu, Na Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInflammation and metabolic dysregulation can disturb the maternal microenvironment, increasing female infertility risk. High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C)-related inflammatory markers have proven useful for diagnosing and predicting inflammatory disorders, and this study investigates their link to infertility in US reproductive-aged women.MethodsData from the 2013–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) were utilized to evaluate the correlation between female infertility and HDL-C-related inflammatory markers. Adjusted multivariable logistic regression models were used to account for confounders, and subgroup analyses and interaction tests were conducted to investigate effect changes. The potential intricate relationship was described using restricted cubic spline (RCS).ResultsThis study included 3,027 women aged 18–45, with 309 reporting infertility. The mean HDL-C-related inflammatory markers of the women in the infertility group were significantly higher than those of the women in the fertility group (P < 0.001). Female infertility was positively associated with Lymphocyte-high-density lipoprotein ratio(LHR), Monocyte-high-density lipoprotein ratio(MHR), and Neutrophil-high-density lipoprotein ratio(NHR) in models 1 and 2. Univariate analysis showed a positive association between Uric Acid-high-density lipoprotein ratio(UHR), MHR and female infertility in other quarter groups(Q2, Q3, Q4) compared to the lowest quarter group (Q1) across all models. Meanwhile, women in the highest LHR quarter group(Q4) exhibited a significantly higher risk of infertility than the lowest (Q1) in all models. And the third quartile (Q3) and the highest quarter(Q4) of Platelet-high-density lipoprotein ratio(PHR) were significantly positively correlated with female infertility in models 2 and 3 (P < 0.05). The RCS plot displayed a linear relationship between HDL-C-related inflammatory markers and infertility risk (P < 0.05). Subgroup analyses and interaction tests revealed a notable PHR and MHR-infertility association in those aged ≤ 35, and a significant NHR-infertility link in non-smokers (P < 0.05).ConclusionsOur investigation revealed a substantial association between elevated HDL-C-related inflammatory markers and a heightened risk of female infertility. Future cohort studies with larger populations are necessary to confirm this relationship, considering the limitations of the cross-sectional design and dependence on self-reported data.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-025-03917-7.Keywords: Female infertility, HDL-C-related inflammatory markers, NHANES

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization, infertility is the inability to conceive after one year of regular unprotected sexual activity and affects approximately 17.5% of the global population [1]. It is a distinct reproductive health condition that induces psychological illnesses in individuals and disrupts familial cohesion, emerging as a significant public health concern that imposes social and economic burdens [2]. Consequently, it is essential to establish dependable and economical biomarkers for the early detection and development of methods for the prevention and treatment of female infertility. Infertility has been reported to be connected with some medical factors (such as abnormalities of the reproductive system), toxic experimental factors, and adverse lifestyle factors (such as psychological stress, smoking, and alcohol consumption) in previous studies [3–5]. Inflammation and metabolic dysregulation are deemed essential contributors in the pathophysiological mechanisms of female infertility [6]. Chronic inflammation influences endocrine homeostasis [7], and women with metabolic syndrome are more likely to undergo abnormal ovulation [8]. Abnormalities in lipid metabolism interfere with the normal functioning of the reproductive system, thereby increasing the risk of infertility [9].

High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) is a component of lipoproteins that is responsible for cholesterol transport, which is known as “beneficial cholesterol” [10, 11]. HDL-C has a role in anti-inflammatory and antioxidant processes and is intimately associated with female fertility [12, 13]. The following HDL-C-related inflammatory markers, Platelet-high-density lipoprotein ratio (PHR), Uric Acid-high-density lipoprotein ratio (UHR), Monocyte-high-density lipoprotein ratio (MHR), Lymphocyte-high-density lipoprotein ratio (LHR), Neutrophil-high-density lipoprotein ratio (NHR) are derived from complete blood counts and biochemical indicators, demonstrating significant potential in the diagnosis and prognosis of various systemic diseases [14, 15]. NHR and UHR display a high correlation with the occurrence of cardiovascular accidents, dysfunction of the digestive system and so on by inciting inflammatory reactions [16–18]. PHR and LHR are novel inflammatory and hypercoagulability markers that represent the severity of metabolic syndrome and inflammation [19, 20]. MHR reflects the balance between chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, showing a crucial status in cardiorenal syndrome and kidney stones over the past studies [21, 22].

Currently, few studies are present to examine the association between HDL-C-related inflammatory markers and female infertility. Consequently, we aimed to address research deficiencies regarding infertility risk factors and predictors, and to provide more tailored and effective preventative methods by utilising a substantial, representative dataset from the 2013–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES).

Methods

Data sources

Participants for this study were chosen from NHANES, a database maintained by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), which collects various health-related data, including demographic characteristics, physical examination results, laboratory findings, and dietary habits through home visits, screening, and laboratory testing conducted by a mobile examination center from a highly complex, multistage, and stratified probability sample that is representative of the civilian population of the United States [23]. Specific data from this investigation are publicly accessible at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs. All participants provided written informed consent, and the NCHS Research Ethics Review Board approved the research methods.

Variables

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Women who participated in the NHANES from 2013 to 2018 provided the data for this cross-sectional study. The inclusion criteria were (1) female participants aged 18–45 years [24, 25]; (2)Women with complete and explicit infertility information. The exclusion criteria were (1)Women who had a history of hysterectomy, bilateral oophorectomy or be pregnant, breastfeeding now [26–28]; (2)Female participants with missing values for complete blood count (CBC) or biochemical indicators; (3)Female participants with missing data for covariates, such as the ratio of family income to poverty (PIR), BMI, and hypertension; (4) Female participants who on contraceptives.

Assessment of infertility

The NHANES Reproductive Health Questionnaire (Questions RHQ074, RHQ131, RHQ200, RHQ305, RHD143 and RHD280) was used to obtain self-reported infertility information. Question RHQ074: “Have you ever attempted to become pregnant for at least a year without becoming pregnant?”"Infertility"was defined as the response of"yes","fertility"as the response of"no", and"missing"as the absence of an answer. Question RHD280: “Have you had a hysterectomy, that is, surgery to remove your uterus or womb?” Question RHQ305: “Have you had both of your ovaries removed (either when you had your uterus removed or at another time)?” Question RHQ200: “ Now breastfeeding a child?” Question RHD143: “Are you pregnant now?” Participants who answered “yes” to any of these issues were out of criteria. Furthermore, infertility was further divided into primary infertility group and secondary infertility group based on the answer from a question “Ever been pregnant?” (RHQ131). A"yes"response from a woman with infertility signifies a secondary infertility case, while a"no"response indicates a primary infertility case.

Management of laboratory data

The participant's CBC including lymphocyte count (LC), platelet count (PC), neutrophil count (NC), monocyte count (MC), HDL-C and Serum Uric Acid (SUA) were collected from laboratory data included in NHANES to calculate the following HDL-C-related inflammatory markers: PHR = PC/HDL-C, UHR = SUA/HDL-C, MHR = MC/HDL-C, LHR = LC/HDL-C, NHR = NC/HDL-C.

Covariate analysis

The following covariates were selected based on their potential influence on, or possible association with, the relationship between HDL-C-related inflammatory markers and infertility risk [29–32] through a standardized questionnaire and physical examination, including age, BMI, race (Mexican American/Non-Hispanic white/Non-Hispanic black/Other race), marital(No/Yes), education(Below high school/High school and above), Poverty-income ratio (PIR)(Not poor/Poor), drink(Heavy_drinker/Light_drinker/Moderate_drinker), smoke(No/Yes), hypertension(No/Yes) and diabetes(No/Yes). Age was divided into two groups based on clinical significance (< 35 and ≥ 35 years) [5, 24, 33]. We divided BMI into three categories:"Normal"(< 25 kg/m2),"Overweight"(25 ≤ BMI < 30 kg/m2) and “Obesity”(≥ 30 kg/m2). The PIR was employed as an indicator of familial income, where it was classified as"Poor"(PIR < 1.0) or"Not poor"(PIR > 1.0). Alcohol status was defined as follows:'Light_drinker'(≤ a cup per week),'Moderate_drinker'(2–8 cups per week), and'Heavy_drinker'(> 8 cups per week) based on the quantity of alcohol consumed per day. Self-reported hypertension was assessed by an affirmative answer to the question “Have you ever been told by a doctor or other health professional that you had hypertension, also called high blood pressure?”. Participants who answered"yes"are classified as having hypertension. The average values of systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were calculated. Hypertension was also defined with an SBP ≥ 140 mmHg and/or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg [34]. Diabetes was determined as follows: (1) self-report of a diagnosis of diabetes; (2)use of diabetes medication or insulin; (3)fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L or 2 h oral glucose tolerance test level of ≥ 11.1 mmol/L; (4) random blood glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L; (5) glycohemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5% [35]. Participants with missing covariates would be excluded.

Study population

A total of 4,323 female participants aged 18–45 with completed Reproductive Health Questionnaire data were selected from 29,400 individuals in the NHANES 2013–2018 after applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Then, 656 participants who were missing infertility information, 190 participants who were missing data to calculate HDL-C inflammatory markers, 407 participants who were pregnant now, hysterectomized, or breastfeeding, and 43 participants who were missing Body Mass Index(BMI) data were excluded; the remaining participants were enrolled in the final sample for final analysis (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were represented by the median (M), first quartile (Q₁), and third quartile (Q₃), while categorical variables were expressed as percentages (%) and numbers (n) in this study. The chi-square test (for categorical variables) or the Mann–Whitney U-test (for continuous variables) was used to describe the distribution between the infertility and fertility groups.

Since the biomarkers are biologically interrelated (all include HDL in the denominator) and part of a shared lipid metabolic pathway, a strict Bonferroni correction was considered overly conservative and at risk of increasing Type II error. Instead, we emphasize the consistency and robustness of associations across different modelling approaches (continuous and quartile analyses) and subgroup analyses to to evaluate the association between HDL-C-related inflammatory markers and other exposure factors and female infertility. Odds ratios (ORs) with the corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were calculated. Multicollinearity among the independent variables was evaluated using the variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis. A threshold of VIF < 10 was used to indicate the absence of significant multicollinearity and confirm the robustness of the regression analysis.

Furthermore, we investigated the intricate correlation between infertility and HDL-C-related inflammatory markers by employing the Restricted Cubic Spline (RCS) curve, which is a segmented polynomial method with smooth curves by fitting a cubic function. Statistical significance was determined at a P-value of less than 0.05, and all statistical analyses were conducted using R (4.3.3), along with Zstats v1.0 (www.zstats.net).

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

There were 3027 female participants included in our analyses, with 309 belonging to the infertility group and 2718 to the fertility group (Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics of included women from NHANES 2013 to 2018 were shown in Table 1. We discovered that self-reported infertility was more common in women who were older, married, overweight, with diabetes and had a greater tendency to smoke and drink(P < 0.05). There were no statistical discrepancies between the two groups in terms of smoke and diabetes after adjusting for potential covariates(Table S1). Meanwhile, there are significant differences in UHR, PHR, LHR, MHR, and NHR between women with infertility and those with fertility, with higher levels of HDL-C-related inflammatory markers in the infertility group (P < 0.05). Relevant inflammatory and metabolic makers including lymphocyte, monocyte, SUA and HDL-C show apparent disparities in two groups(P < 0.05). No significant differences were observed between the two groups concerning race, education, PIR, and hypertension (P > 0.05).

Associations between HDL-C-related inflammatory markers and female infertility

All VIF analysis values are below 10, indicating the absence of significant multicollinearity among the independent variables. The Multivariable logistic regression analysis revealed a significant correlation between HDL-C-related inflammatory markers and female infertility (Table 2). Female infertility had a positive correlation with LHR, MHR and NHR in model 1, with ORs of 1.34(95% CI: 1.18- 1.52; P < 0.001), 3.03(95% CI: 1.76- 5.22; P < 0.001), 1.09(95% CI: 1.03- 1.15; P = 0.005), respectively. The correlation between LHR, MHR and female infertility is consistent in models 2 and model 3 after adjusting for confounders (P < 0.05). Nonetheless, the substantial connections between NHR and infertility were not stable in model 3, with an OR of 1.03(95%CI: 0.97–1.10; P = 0.348).

Additionally, we conducted deeper research by transforming HDL-C-related inflammatory markers into categorical variables based on quartiles (Table 2). Though the effect of continuous UHR and PHR in relation to infertility was not notable, with ORs of 1.01 in all models. The values of UHR and PHR cover a wide range, so even a 0.01 increase is considered small. Univariate analysis model 1 showed a robusts and positive correlation between UHR and female infertility in the second quarter group (Q2), the third quarter group (Q3) and the fourth quarter groups (Q4) (P < 0.05), with ORs of 1.53(95% CI:1.05–2.22; P = 0.026), 1.72(95% CI:1.19–2.48; P = 0.004), 2.22(95%CI:1.56–3.17; P < 0.001) compared to the lowest UHR quarter group (Q1), as well as in model 2 and model 3(P < 0.05). We may achieve a similar revelation regarding the correlation between MHR and female infertility. Meanwhile, Women in the highest LHR quartile (Q4) demonstrated a significantly elevated risk of female infertility compared to those in the lowest LHR quartile (Q1) across all models (Q4 Model 1: OR = 1.92, 95% CI: 1.38–2.67, P < 0.001; Q4 Model 2: OR = 2.05, 95% CI: 1.45–2.90, P < 0.001; Q4 Model 3: OR = 1.66, 95% CI: 1.14–2.42, P = 0.008). In comparison to the lowest quartile (Q1), the fourth quartile (Q4) of NHR exhibited a positive correlation with female infertility, presenting an OR of 1.55 (95% CI: 1.11–2.17; P = 0.010) in model 1 and 1.64 (95% CI: 1.15–2.34; P = 0.006) in model 2. However, the highest quartile (Q4) of NHR was not significantly positively correlated with female infertility in model 3, with an OR of 1.24 (95% CI: 0.84–1.83; P = 0.276). The results of the correlation between PHR by quartile and female infertility were analogous to NHR.

Associations between HDL-C-related inflammatory markers and the risk of primary and secondary infertility

Given that there may be differences in factors between primary and secondary infertility, we further carried out a separate analysis in these two groups (Table 3). The majority population were secondary infertility(n = 251). HDL-C-related inflammatory markers were significantly higher in primary infertility than in secondary infertility except for UHR(P < 0.05).

There was no significant interaction for the covariates of race, marital status, education level, drink, PIR, hypertension, and diabetes, suggesting that the association between HDL-C-related inflammatory markers and risk of infertility remained consistent across these different subgroups(P > 0.05). This connection appeared to be more pronounced among individuals without hypertension or diabetes, which may be attributed to the inflammation and metabolic disorders caused by abnormal values of blood or glucose [36]. However, the lack of statistical significance suggests that these changes were not sufficient to alter the overall relationship.

Discussion

Our research demonstrated a positive correlation between high levels of HDL-C-related inflammatory markers and female infertility after adjusting for confounding factors, following a nonlinear curve relationship. In addition, further subgroup analysis and interaction tests revealed an enhanced association between PHR or MHR and female infertility among people under the age of 35, alongside a more pronounced association between NHR and female infertility in non-smokers, underscoring the necessity of accounting for these variables in future research to attain greater accuracy.

Female infertility is a multifaceted condition with a complicated etiology, impacted by various biological factors and mechanisms. Chronic inflammation might adversely affect endometrial function by elevating oxidative stress variables or disrupting endometrial tolerance [37, 38]. Intrafollicular inflammatory reactions may alter follicular fluid composition, resulting in diminished oocyte quality, potentially causing infertility. Metabolic disturbances induce an illness status of maternal, leading to impaired fertility [39].

Our analysis revealed a potentially robust positive correlation between HDL-C-related inflammatory markers and female infertility. It was speculated that the potential mechanism may be related to two factors.

HDL-C possesses anti-inflammatory and antioxidant characteristics, and its presence in the follicular fluid may modulate the local inflammatory state [32]. Modified cholesterol levels may impact the progression of female reproductive disorders by affecting sex hormone activity after interacting with sex hormone binding globulin (SHBG), ovarian function, and endometrial receptivity [40, 41]. Previous studies have shown that HDL contributes to the intra-follicular homeostasis of cholesterol and has a role in embryo development [42, 43]. SR-BI (HDL receptor) knockout mice exhibit infertility [44]. Currently, there are several studies about the relationship between HDL-related inflammatory markers and a multitude of diseases, including hypertension, diabetes and metabolic syndrome (MetS) [45–47], all of which are intricately linked to infertility [48, 49]. The Non-high-density to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio(NHHR) has been proven to correlate positively with female infertility [50].

Aberrant function and elevated levels of lymphocytes have contributed to the pathogenesis of infertility [51]. Monocytes can facilitate arteriosclerosis by causing localized ischemia and inflammation [52]. The controlled activation of thrombocytes plays a vital role in ovarian function, endometrial thickening and receptivity, implantation as well as placentation and embryonic developmentis, which is required in reproduction and fertility [53]. Hyperactivated neutrophils can generate matrix metalloproteinases and reactive oxygen species, which account for periodontitis [54]. Elevated SUA levels are strongly associated with metabolic syndrome, such as obesity, hypertension, hyperglycaemia and hyperlipidaemia [29, 55]. The intricate interactions among lymphocytes, neutrophils, PLT, monocytes, SUA and HDL-C activate immune response and lead to metabolic dysregulation, potentially contributing to the pathophysiology of female infertility.

Furthermore, stratified analyses suggest that women in the age group of < 35 or non-smokers may experience a greater risk of infertility with an increase of HDL-C-related inflammatory markers, which may be attributed to high levels of psychological stress and active inflammation of the reproductive system of young women [56]. The effects of HDL-C-related inflammatory markers in women aged > 35 years or smokers were weakened, which may be mainly caused by the decline of ovarian function, abnormality in reproductive endocrinology or any other possible injury in the reproductive system [33, 57]. The pronounced connections identified in these groups of women underscore the necessity for early detection and control of metabolic and lipid-related concerns in this demographic to mitigate the risk of infertility effectively.

This study is the inaugural investigation into the correlation between HDL-C-related inflammatory markers including UHR, PHR, LHR, MHR, NHR and infertility based on the US NHANES data of women aged 18–45 years, which has considerable value for research about the association between infertility and the imbalance of immunology. Given that these criteria are cost-effective, simple to implement, and strongly correlated with infertility rates, more research into the underlying mechanisms linking these HDL-C-related inflammatory indicators to female infertility is warranted. Crucially, our newly identified HDL-related inflammatory indicators would be useful for risk stratification in the future, enabling prompt and effective intervention for this condition. Guidance for individualized treatment may be considered to be developed based on the patient's level of HDL-C-related inflammatory markers. For example, anti-inflammatory and lipid-regulation drugs may be targeted to improve the internal environment of the reproductive system and increase the chances of conception [58, 59]. The changes in HDL-C-related inflammation indexes can be used as one of the reference indicators for predicting the effectiveness of infertility treatment. However, any talk about using these markers to guide therapy should be marked as hypothetical in face of limited clinical data.

This investigation also has some limitations. Firstly, the outcome variable of female infertility was determined by self-reported replies without clinical verification and may result from factors external to the woman's physiology, which may introduce the possibility of recall bias and misclassification, women who say they are infertile might be talking about cases where the male partner is to blame or where the cause is something that can be fixed but wasn't clear from the survey question. Secondly, our multivariable models incorporate general health and lifestyle factors, yet exclude specific infertility aetiologies, there exists a danger that unintegrated confounders (e.g., menarche, medication use, various gynaecological conditions) may affect both inflammation indicators and the risk of infertility. Moreover, the study was cross-sectional, rendering it unfeasible to elucidate the particular mechanisms behind the association between female infertility and HDL-C-related inflammatory markers. Ultimately, the conclusions cannot be universally extended to other districts, as the significance of these discoveries is confined to the American demographic. Another potential limitation of our study is the risk of false positives due to multiple comparisons. While we did not apply a formal correction method, we emphasize the consistency of findings across various models and subgroups as evidence of robustness. Consequently, more research should be conducted to validate these findings within a more heterogeneous population.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study identified a significant link between high levels of HDL-C-related inflammatory markers and an increased risk of female infertility, especially in women under 35 and non-smokers. Inflammatory markers associated with HDL-C may serve as responsive biomarkers, providing new perspectives on the prevention and therapy of female infertility. Future cohort studies including more diverse groups are necessary to confirm this association.
