Impact of the FDA roadmap and perspectives for regulatory use of new approach methodologies in Japanese pharmaceutical companies
Authors: Tetsuro Araki, Hitoshi Naraoka, Yuta Asakawa, Toru Usui, Dai Kakiuchi, Seiichiro Kurashige, Tadahiro Shinozawa, Kimika Nakamura, Takumi Noda, Satomi Nishikawa, Mutsumi Suzuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•JPMA surveyed the industry impact of the FDA roadmap.•Most companies recognized the roadmap, but concrete actions were limited.•NAMs research experience was limited, and regulatory use was rare.•Harmonized guidance is needed to promote regulatory use of NAMs data.Keywords: New approach methodologies, Drug development tools, Regulatory acceptance, Japan, Industry survey

Introduction

In recent years, pharmaceuticals have rapidly diversified from traditional drug discovery centered on small molecules to new modalities such as antibodies, oligonucleotides, antibody-drug conjugates (ADC), peptides, and even gene and cell therapies. Accordingly, the requirements for efficacy and safety evaluation at the non-clinical stage have become more advanced and complex, and the appropriate prediction of human-specific pharmacological effects and adverse reactions has become an important challenge. Conventional animal models using rodents and non-rodents (e.g. dogs and rabbits) have shown certain usefulness for safety evaluation of small molecules (Tamaki et al., 2013; Onakpoya et al., 2016; Monticello et al., 2017; Clark and Steger-Hartmann, 2018; Bailey and Balls, 2019; Olson et al., 2000); however, for biologics, species differences in target cross-reactivity can sometimes prevent adequate prediction of human responses. Therefore, evaluations using non-human primates (NHPs), which have high physiological similarity to humans, have become increasingly important (Prior et al., 2020).

On the other hand, animal studies, even those using NHPs, have repeatedly been reported to fail in adequately predicting pharmacological effects and toxicity in humans. Especially for new modalities such as biologics targeting the immune system, the limitations of animal studies in reproducing human-specific reactions arising from species differences have become apparent. A well-known case in which severe adverse events occurred in the first-in-human trial despite no significant toxicity observed in animal studies is the clinical trial of the anti-CD28 antibody TGN1412 (Suntharalingam et al., 2006). This case highlighted that animal studies do not necessarily appropriately reflect human immune responses leading to severe adverse events, prompting a reconsideration of the approaches to non-clinical safety evaluation. Furthermore, even for small-molecule drugs, hepatotoxicity and cardiotoxicity often arise in human clinical trials (Onakpoya et al., 2016), suggesting limitations in the predictive value of animal studies, possibly due to interspecies differences (Martignoni et al., 2006). Consequently, extrapolating animal study results to humans remains a long-standing challenge.

The trend toward reconsidering the use of animal studies was further accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic. During the pandemic, global shortages and soaring procurement costs of laboratory animals, especially NHPs, became apparent, raising sustainability issues for non-clinical safety evaluations relying on conventional animal studies (FDA, 2025). Restrictions on access to NHPs for animal studies and rising procurement costs have contributed to delays in research plans and trial implementation, especially in the development of biologics and other new modalities, highlighting that excessive dependence on animal studies can reduce the resilience of drug discovery and development. During the rapid development of therapeutics and vaccines against COVID-19, mRNA vaccines in particular were commercialized in a short period, significantly transforming the conventional pharmaceutical development paradigm. This experience clearly demonstrated the necessity of evaluation methods more closely aligned with human physiology, to support the acceleration of drug development.

New Approach Methodologies (NAMs) have attracted attention in recent years. NAMs are a comprehensive concept encompassing evaluation methods that replace or complement conventional animal studies, including in vitro, in chemico, in silico, and/or ex vivo methods, such as culture systems using human-derived cells and/or induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cells, microphysiological systems (MPS), organoids, and toxicity prediction models using artificial intelligence (AI). These methods have the potential to evaluate human-specific molecular responses and organ functions more directly than animal studies and are expected to contribute to improving the clinical predictivity of non-clinical evaluations (Baran et al., 2022; Beilmann et al., 2025).

In this context, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Modernization Act 2.0 was enacted in 2022 in the United States (US), which removed the mandatory requirement for animal studies in drug development. This legislative change enabled a shift toward non-clinical approaches that do not require animal use (US Congress, 2022). In April 2025, FDA published a roadmap aimed at reducing animal studies in non-clinical safety assessment, indicating a phased transition from conventional animal tests to NAMs, primarily focusing on monoclonal antibody therapeutics (FDA, 2025). This roadmap is based on multiple social needs such as accelerating drug development, improving clinical predictivity, and promoting the 3Rs (Replacement, Reduction and Refinement), and is expected to have a significant impact on future international regulatory trends. The roadmap states that FDA could employ its drug development tools (DDT) qualification program such as the Innovative Science and Technology for Advancing New Drugs (ISTAND) program to formalize the acceptance of DDT in the context of regulatory process (FDA, 2020). In fact, over 20 DDT projects have been reviewed in the ISTAND program (https://force-dsc.my.site.com/ddt/s/; accessed 1 April 2025). The European Medicines Agency (EMA) has also had a similar qualification system for “novel methodologies for drug development” since 2009 (EMA, 2009).

On the other hand, in Japan, clear guidance and frameworks for systematically evaluating and qualifying NAMs for regulatory use have not yet been established, and concerns have been raised that regulatory acceptance by authorities and implementation within industry may lag behind those in Europe and the US. In this context, there is limited systematic data on how the FDA roadmap is perceived by pharmaceutical companies in Japan and the challenges and expectations associated with the regulatory use of NAMs.

In this study, we conducted a survey among member companies of the Japan Pharmaceutical Manufacturers Association (JPMA) to assess the awareness of the FDA roadmap, the current internal use of NAMs, barriers to regulatory use, and expectations for regulatory authorities. This survey aimed to identify the challenges for promoting the implementation of NAMs in Japan and provide evidence to inform future system design and discussions on international harmonization.

Methods

Study design

We performed a cross-sectional, company-level survey of JPMA member companies to provide evidence for policy recommendations to the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare of Japan (MHLW) or the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA) regarding the FDA roadmap and the regulatory use of NAMs in Japan. The working definitions of DDT, NAMs and qualification for this survey were provided to the respondents with reference to the documents issued by the FDA and the EMA (Table 1).

Participants and data collection

The sampling frame comprised 57 JPMA member companies. Requests for responses were sent to JPMA representatives in the non-clinical departments of each company. Data were collected via Microsoft Forms from 18 July to 1 August 2025. We received 34 responses (a 60% response rate). Respondents reported their company type (28 domestic and 6 overseas-owned companies) and provided single- or multiple-choice answers along with optional free-text responses. Only one response was accepted per company. Responses were collected in an identifiable form to allow follow-up questions if needed. The data were anonymized when creating the charts, and only anonymized data were provided during manuscript review and data validation.

Content of the survey

The survey consisted of 11 questions in Japanese, which covered (i) awareness of and internal responses to the FDA roadmap; (ii) internal research on implementation of NAMs; (iii) drivers to use NAMs for regulatory submission; (iv) experience with regulatory submission (e.g., Investigator’s Brochure (IB) and Common Technical Document (CTD)) and issues; (v) enablers of internal consideration; (vi) perceived barriers to regulatory use; and (vii) actions expected from regulatory authorities. For respondents with experience in regulatory submissions, we sent additional questions for further clarification. The English translation of the survey form is provided as Supplemental File 1.

Measures and analysis

We conducted descriptive analyses (counts, proportions) for closed-ended items. As the objective was mapping the current situation rather than hypothesis testing, no inferential statistics were performed. Given that only six overseas-owned companies responded, subgroup comparisons between domestic and overseas-owned companies were interpreted cautiously and were not used to draw firm conclusions. Free-text comments such as “No answer” under the “Others” option in Fig. 3, Fig. 5, Fig. 6 were not described in the Results section.

Results

Awareness and internal response to the FDA roadmap

We asked JPMA member companies about their awareness of the FDA roadmap and their response strategies (Fig. 1). The most common response was “Aware, but response undecided/unknown” (18/34, 53%). Smaller groups indicated “Planning to consider” (5/34, 15%) or “Already considering” (3/34, 9%), while “Aware of existence but not content” (4/34, 12%) and “Unaware until this survey” (2/34, 6%) were less frequent; responses of “Others” indicated that decisions were being considered by overseas headquarters or that visibility within Japan affiliates of the overseas-owned companies was limited (2/34, 6%).

Company responses to the FDA roadmap. Horizontal bars indicate the percentage of the respondents (n = 34). The numbers written inside the bar indicate the number of companies responding by domestic and overseas-owned companies. The respondents chose each answer.

Status of NAMs research within companies

Second, we asked companies about the status of NAMs research within their organizations (Fig. 2). Fifteen of 34 companies (44%) reported that they had not conducted research on NAMs; eleven companies (32%) had used NAMs in discovery projects and/or internal decision-making; eight companies (24%) had used NAMs in basic research but not yet implemented them in internal projects; four companies (12%) had experience using NAMs in regulatory submissions, such as cited in IB and/or CTDs and one company (3%) was considering for regulatory submissions. Three overseas-owned companies selected “Others” because they did not have non-clinical research departments in Japan.

Status of NAMs research within companies. Horizontal bars indicate the percentage of the respondents (n = 34). The numbers written inside the bar indicate the number of companies responding by domestic and overseas-owned companies. The respondents could choose multiple answers.

Drivers to use NAMs

Next, we asked respondents to select drivers for NAMs use from multiple choice options (Fig. 3). More than half of the respondents selected promotion of 3Rs principle” (25/34, 74%), evaluation of human-specific response (24/34, 71%), and clinical relevance and success (22/34, 65%). Between one-quarter and one-half of respondents selected shortening the development period (16/34, 47%) and reducing development-stage costs (15/34, 44%), and evaluating new modalities (10/34, 29%). Respondents who selected “Others” (3/34, 9%) provided the following free-text comments: “Currently, there are no problems without using NAMs” and “When required for approval”.

Regulatory submission experience and issues

Most companies had no experience including NAMs data in IBs and/or CTDs (29/34, 85%). Only 5 of 34 (15%) companies reported experience submitting NAMs data to health authorities (Fig. 4).

Companies A, B and C had submitted NAMs data for small-molecule products, in the context of occupational safety, eye irritation and clinical relevance, respectively.

Company D submitted NAMs data on cytokine release for an antibody product. The test article showed strong positive results in a novel method, whereas no symptoms of cytokine release were observed in the conventional assay methods, and they decided to include the data in a regulatory submission after internal discussions. They received and responded to several inquiries from the health authority regarding the context of use and clinical relevance of the result. However, after regulatory review, they found that non-specific positive reactions were observed in certain antibody formats (not disclosed) and later removed the data from the IB.

Company E reported two cases involving antibody products. In the first case, they evaluated off-target binding potential in humans, following consultation with the EMA. They obtained complementary data for clinical risk assessment given the limitations of animal toxicity studies. However, they observed more off-target binding reactions than expected and needed to conduct additional functional assays for each potential off-target binder.

In the other case, they evaluated the potential effects of their product on FcγRs. FcγRs are broadly expressed in hematopoietic cells and the antibody-FcγR interactions are involved in activation or inhibition of several immune reactions (Nimmerjahn and Ravetch, 2008). They confirmed that the Fc region of their product cross-reacted with human FcγRs, but not with mouse or cynomolgus monkey FcγRs. Because of interspecies differences, they evaluated whether their product could affect FcγRIIb phosphorylation and B-cell apoptosis, as well as platelet activation via FcγRIIa stimulation using human cells. As a result, they obtained data on potential effects on human FcγRs and submitted these data to the FDA.

Enablers to use or consideration of NAMs

We asked the 29 companies that had not used NAMs data in regulatory submissions which factors would enable or facilitate the use or consideration of NAMs (Fig. 5). The top enablers were international guideline development (e.g., International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use (ICH)) (25/29, 86%), new qualification guidance in Japan (21/29, 72%), and individual test guidelines (e.g., OECD) (21/29, 72%). “Others” (2/29, 7%) included “No answer” and “Answered in the next question”.

Barriers to regulatory use of NAMs

Next, we asked respondents about barriers to the use of NAMs data for regulatory applications (Fig. 6). The leading barriers were lack of harmonized international guidance or guidelines (26/34, 77%), lack of guidance in Japan (24/34, 71%), insufficient internal and/or external experience (24/34, 71%), and uncertainty about required datasets and endpoints (23/34, 68%). Additional barriers included unclear quality expectations (17/34, 50%), no hindrance to development without NAMs (17/34, 50%), strategic risk (14/34, 41%), scientific gaps (10/34, 29%), lack of early consultation channels (9/34, 27%), regulatory affairs or data quality concerns (6/34, 18%), and economic constraints (3/34, 9%). Companies that selected “Others” (3/34, 9%) provided the following free-text form: “Since existing NAMs are built for small molecules, they may not meet the needs of biologics and may require modification” and “The accuracy of NAMs is unknown”.

Expected actions from regulators

Finally, we asked respondents which actions they expected regulatory authorities to take (Fig. 7). A new topic proposal at ICH or international harmonization was the most frequently selected response (27/34, 79%), followed by issuance of DDT qualification guidance in Japan (23/34, 68%), publication of a database of qualified NAMs (22/34, 65%), establishment of a DDT qualification system in Japan (17/34, 50%), establishment of a professional consultation desk (16/34, 47%), promotion of the development of NAMs through industry-academia-government collaborations (16/34, 47%), flexible response to GLP application (13/34, 38%), and conduct of pilot programs (5/34, 15%). “Others” (3/34, 9%) included the following free-text comments: a request for clear indication of which in vivo tests can be replaced and international harmonization of regulatory acceptance of NAMs.

Other comments

At the end of the survey, we received free-text comments on this topic. Three respondents provided comments regarding: (1) request for the issuance of guidance or guidelines on points to consider when proposing a Context of Use for NAMs, (2) expectation for the promotion of the 3Rs and human-relevant drug evaluation, and (3) a request for practical criteria and clarification of limitations depending on dosage forms, modalities and animal species, respectively.

Discussion

FDA roadmap’s impact and gaps in Japan

This survey showed a high level of awareness of the FDA roadmap (FDA, 2025) among JPMA member companies, but some respondents may not yet have been aware of it, as this survey was conducted only three months after the release of the roadmap. The impact of this roadmap is expected to extend not only to global pharmaceutical companies operating in Japan, but also to domestic companies because the US is the largest market for pharmaceuticals (IQVIA, 2025) and can influence global trends toward reducing or phasing out animal studies. In fact, the FDA stated that ICH guidelines (e.g. ICH S6) should be revised to reflect the use of NAMs (FDA, 2025), to ensure global regulatory alignment in the roadmap. Seven months after the release of the FDA roadmap, the UK government issued a policy paper on “replacing animals in science” (UK Government, 2025). The UK government committed to driving the development and regulatory uptake of alternative methods with clear timelines and aimed to establish international leadership and cooperation. It is not an exaggeration to say that regulatory activities surrounding NAMs are intensifying across multiple countries.

Almost half of the respondents indicated that they had started NAMs research mainly for basic research and/or drug discovery although 15 of 34 companies had not conducted NAMs research yet. Three respondents were from the Japanese branch of overseas-owned pharmaceutical companies that do not have non-clinical research facilities in Japan. Nevertheless, many companies have strong needs to promote the 3Rs, the evaluation of human-specific responses that cannot be captured in animals and improving clinical predictivity and the probability of success in clinical trials. A large gap exists between the current status of NAMs research and the expectations for the usefulness of NAMs in Japanese pharmaceutical companies.

There may be two possible reasons for this gap. First, information sharing about NAMs among stakeholders, including pharmaceutical companies, health authorities and NAMs developers, remains insufficient to support the use of NAMs in drug research and development. Many scientific papers on NAMs have been published in recent decades (Portela et al., 2026), but case reports on regulatory applications remain limited in both number and diversity of context of use (Powles-Glover et al., 2025; Dao and Sadrieh, 2026; Yao et al., 2026). Although respondents expected that the use of NAMs could promote the 3Rs, evaluate human-specific responses and increase clinical success rates, it seems difficult to determine whether and how the application of NAMs can impact their drug development (e.g. waiving animal studies or adjusting clinical trials designs, etc.) within each company. The currently unclear impact on R&D could lead pharmaceutical companies in Japan, especially small-sized ones, to hesitate to invest in NAMs research. Second, the regulatory acceptance criteria for NAMs are ambiguous due to the lack of clear guidance or guidelines for the qualification of NAMs in Japan, such as DDT Qualification Guidance issued by the FDA. Many respondents pointed out the lack of internationally harmonized and/or domestic guidelines about NAMs, the lack of information and experience related to NAMs, and unclear datasets and/or data quality required for the use of NAMs as barriers to the regulatory use of NAMs. At the PMDA, current uptake of NAMs in drug applications is decided on a case-by-case basis and consultation channels are limited. The survey clarified that new DDT qualification guidance in Japan similar to the FDA’s DDT Qualification Guidance and international harmonization were strongly requested by respondents. Guidance with clear criteria and processes for the qualification of NAMs would strongly promote the uptake of NAMs by pharmaceutical companies. This view is supported by an opinion paper authored by the MPS Team within the Consortium for Safety Assessment using Human iPS Cells (CSAHi-MPS), a Japanese industrial consortium (Naraoka et al., 2025). Under the current product-specific, case-by-case review framework employed by the PMDA, regulatory assessments necessarily involve a substantial amount of confidential, product-specific information. Consequently, the sharing of information and experience related to the regulatory application of NAMs across pharmaceutical companies may be limited. More frequent information sharing and consultation opportunities between health authorities and pharmaceutical companies could facilitate consideration of NAMs introduction into research portfolios.

Although the number of responses was limited, there were several cases of regulatory submission of NAMs reported in this survey. These cases revealed that regulatory submission of NAMs offered advantages such as reduction of animal use and costs, supplementation of risk assessment when in vivo models are limited, and promotion of proactive internal discussions. However, challenges such as regulatory uncertainty, additional internal decision-making time, inquiries from health authorities, and the risk of additional studies for qualification or withdrawal due to insufficient scientific validity were also observed.

The FDA has already conducted its own DDT qualification programs, especially the ISTAND program, which focuses on innovative DDTs, including NAMs such as MPS, spheroids, and AI-based tools. The ISTAND program started as a pilot program in November 2020, in the midst of the COVID-19 pandemic, and became a permanent program in July 2025. During this period, the FDA issued guidance for industry on considerations for mitigating NHP supply constraints in the development of biological products, which led to increased NAMs research in pharmaceutical and biotechnology companies in the US (FDA, 2022). According to the survey conducted by Biotechnology Innovation Organization (BIO), >10 out of 24 companies had experience with discussions on NAMs with health authorities, mainly the FDA (Shenton et al., 2025). The EMA has also established a qualification system for novel methodologies (EMA, 2009) and has provided opportunities for early dialogue with the EMA through the Innovation Task Force (ITF) briefing meetings (Edwards et al., 2025).

In contrast, our survey showed only 5 of 34 responding companies had experience with regulatory submissions involving NAMs data. Global companies may have centralized NAMs strategy teams and more direct experience interacting with the FDA or the EMA. Six overseas-owned companies responded to this survey, but their overseas headquarters may drive regulatory strategy for NAMs independently of Japanese affiliates. In Japan, however, the lack of interactions between health authorities, such as the PMDA, and pharmaceutical companies may have limited opportunities for the regulatory use of NAMs. Domestic Japanese companies, especially smaller companies, may have fewer opportunities for early dialogue with the FDA and/or the EMA and may rely more heavily on domestic regulatory guidance. The absence of a clear Japanese qualification pathway may have a stronger impact on domestic companies than on global companies already operating under FDA/EMA frameworks.

Against this background, in 2023, the Japan Business Federation (Keidanren) submitted a regulatory reform request to the Cabinet Office of Japan, seeking to enable data generated using advanced evaluation methodologies, such as MPS and AI as application data to improve the efficiency of pharmaceutical development. In response, the Council for Promotion of Regulatory Reform of the Cabinet Office included a statement on creating an enabling environment, including the development of guidance into the Regulatory Reform Implementation Plan for fiscal year 2024. Currently, under Pharmaceutical Regulatory Harmonization and Evaluation Research Project by the Japan Agency for Medical Research and Development (AMED), discussions are underway among the MHLW, the PMDA, the National Institute of Health Sciences (NIHS) and the JPMA regarding the development of draft guidance and necessary qualification processes.

Recently, the PMDA launched a website on NAMs (PDMA, 2025a) and issued three “early consideration” documents on the use of the weight of evidence (WoE) approach in pharmaceutical development: one general document on the use of the WoE approach in pharmaceutical development (PDMA, 2025b) and two specific documents focusing on developmental and reproductive toxicity studies using NHPs (PDMA, 2025c) and general toxicity evaluation of monoclonal antibodies (PMDA, 2026), respectively. Such documents are valuable for pharmaceutical companies because they clarify the PMDA’s current thinking and future direction. However, given that many of the companies responding to this survey expressed strong interest not only in the issuance of guidance on DDT qualification in Japan and the development of internationally harmonized guidelines through ICH, but also in the creation of a public database of qualified NAMs, the establishment of a DDT qualification system, and the provision of an expert consultation desk, further publication of relevant regulatory documents would be beneficial for promoting the regulatory use of NAMs in Japan.

Limitations of this survey

The survey was cross-sectional, based on self-report, and limited to JPMA members, with a 60% response rate; some items allowed multiple responses, and free-text interpretations are qualitative. This survey may not represent all Japanese pharmaceutical, biotechnology companies, contract research organizations (CROs), or NAMs developer communities. Respondents may be biased toward companies with greater interest in NAMs. Requests to complete the survey were sent to contact persons in the non-clinical research departments of JPMA member companies. However, it was not confirmed which department or function the respondents belonged to although most respondents appeared to be responsible for non-clinical safety assessment. In addition, company-level responses may not capture differences among research, development, toxicology, and regulatory affairs functions within the same company. The extent of information disclosure depended entirely on each company’s policy. It was unknown whether respondents used NAMs in their drug discovery research. In particular, respondents from overseas-owned companies may have had limited access to research information from their global headquarters. In addition, the range of NAMs might differ among responding companies. For example, Company B introduced an eye irritation test using bovine corneas as one of NAMs (Table 2). However, this test method has already been published as OECD test guideline No 437 (OECD, 2025). Other responding companies might not regard this test as one of NAMs. It should be noted that the results of this survey may not fully reflect the current status of NAMs research in Japanese pharmaceutical companies. Further investigations, such as comprehensive analyses of application documents and global surveys in collaboration with pharmaceutical stakeholders in other regions such as the Preclinical Safety Leadership Group of the International Consortium for Innovation and Quality in Pharmaceutical Development (IQ DruSafe) and the IQ MPS Affiliate may be needed in the future.

Recommendations for health authorities

The results provide actionable signals to guide policy and practice. Near-term actions that directly address industry needs include: (i) a new topic proposal to ICH and alignment with evolving international standards; (ii) qualification guidance in Japan clarifying necessary datasets, endpoints, and data quality (including whether GLP compliance is required); (iii) formal qualification pathways and transparent public databases; and (iv) early consultation channels to reduce uncertainty in first-of-kind uses. We believe that the early establishment of DDT qualification guidance in Japan could contribute to global harmonization of the regulatory use of NAMs, resulting in the promotion of more human-relevant and more predictive non-clinical research, improved success rates in clinical development, and ultimately contributing to human health while promoting the 3Rs. Resolving uncertainties regarding the regulatory acceptance of NAMs is expected to enhance the advantages of conducting drug development and regulatory submissions in Japan, and improve access to new drugs for Japanese patients.

In this survey, several respondents considered that there was no hindrance to clinical trial notifications and marketing authorization applications even without including NAMs in their clinical trial notifications or new drug applications. This may, in part, be attributable to the lack of established evaluation methodologies based on NAMs, as well as to the fact that animal-based nonclinical studies, which were originally developed in the context of small-molecule drugs (ICH, 2009; ICH, 2012), have continued to be considered appropriate to a certain extent even for biologics. Policies incentives that encourage greater use of NAMs by pharmaceutical companies would be effective. For example, shortening the review period could lead to earlier market launch, and if pricing premiums are applied under the public drug price system in Japan, this could improve the prospects for investment recovery by pharmaceutical companies and could further promote new drug development using NAMs. In addition, establishing an award or certification system for companies that promote the 3Rs using NAMs would also enhance corporate value, which may further motivate pharmaceutical companies to utilize NAMs.

Possible future role of JPMA

Finally, we discussed the possible future role of the JPMA. This survey revealed that Japanese pharmaceutical companies were aware of the FDA roadmap and interested in implementing NAMs in drug development. The JPMA has already started discussions with PMDA and NIHS under the AMED project mentioned above to propose a new DDT qualification system and guidance for NAMs in Japan. In addition, JPMA should inform member companies about the global trends in NAMs through webinars and presentations at scientific conferences and promote the implementation of NAMs into member companies’ development strategies. Providing case studies or training materials by the JPMA could be useful not only for JPMA member companies but also for reviewers in regulatory authorities and CROs.

Communication among various stakeholders is also important. For example, academic researchers and suppliers involved in NAMs development may benefit from understanding the needs of pharmaceutical companies. This survey clarified the current status of JPMA member companies in terms of the implementation of NAMs in drug development, but further surveys focusing on challenges in drug discovery research and development within JPMA member companies could help guide future research by NAMs developers. CSAHi-MPS is a Japanese consortium initiated by the JPMA, with members from pharmaceutical companies, CROs, MPS suppliers and academia (Naraoka et al., 2025). It has organized hands-on seminars for potential MPS users from pharmaceutical or food companies and CROs. From the perspective of practical feasibility, the implementation of NAMs may depend not only on direct cost savings but also on validation costs, availability of technical expertise, assay standardization, access to qualified service providers, and regulatory predictability. Although cost reduction was selected as a driver by 15 of 34 respondents and economic constraints were selected by only 3 of 34 respondents, future surveys should more directly evaluate cost-benefit considerations more directly, including the perspectives of NAMs developers, MPS suppliers, and CROs. We believe that The JPMA should play a central hub role to actively promote the implementation of NAMs through more proactive communication with academia, including scientific societies, other industries (e.g. food, cosmetics and chemical manufacturers, CROs and NAMs suppliers) and regulatory authorities in Japan and other regions.

Beyond the regulatory guidance and qualification procedures, the implementation of NAMs faces a number of practical challenges. For example, access to NAMs technologies, expertise, and service providers may be limited not only in Japan but also in other Asia-Pacific countries, as many NAMs developers are based in the US and Europe. In this context, stakeholder organizations, including non-governmental organizations such as CSAHi-MPS and the Health and Environmental Sciences Institute (HESI), can play important roles by facilitating precompetitive data sharing, introducing regulatory case studies, conducting collaborative validation studies, and supporting the implementation of NAMs in collaboration with the JPMA and regulatory authorities. In parallel with the development of new ICH guidelines, such collaborative efforts could help bridge the gap between scientific innovation and regulatory adoption of NAMs on a global scale.

Conclusion

The survey of pharmaceutical companies in Japan revealed that they have strong interest in promoting the 3Rs and reshaping their non-clinical research strategy through the implementation of NAMs. While the lack of clear regulatory direction from health authorities has contributed to hesitation in initiating NAMs research, pharmaceutical companies also need to adopt a more science-driven approach to evaluation. Both internationally harmonized guidelines for the regulatory use of NAMs and a DDT qualification system in Japan are necessary to promote the 3Rs and more human-relevant non-clinical research. The JPMA could serve as a central hub by engaging in more proactive communication with stakeholders in Japan and worldwide, including industry, academia, NAMs developers and suppliers, and regulators, to promote the implementation of NAMs for regulatory submissions.
