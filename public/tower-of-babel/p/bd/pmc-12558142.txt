Symposium report—ethical and social considerations regarding return of secondary findings in atomic bomb survivors
Authors: Noriaki Yoshida, Douglas R Stewart, Arikuni Uchimura, Ken-ichi Arita, Yvonne Bombard, Megan Frone, Takao Hinoi, Kenji Kamiya, Shigeru Katamine, Junji Kayukawa, Kazunori Kodama, David T Miller, Kiyonori Miura, Kaori Muto, Fuji Nagami, Hidewaki Nakagawa, Asao Noda, Waka Ohishi, Osamu Tanabe, Chikashi Terao, Preetha Rajaraman, Kazuto Kato, Leslie G Biesecker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Radiation Effects Research Foundation (RERF) conducts research on the health effects of atomic bomb (A-bomb) radiation, supported by the long-term cooperation of survivors and their children. In response to concerns from A-bomb survivors, their offspring, and the global community, RERF has investigated transgenerational radiation effects for many years. Currently, RERF is planning the Trio Genome Study (TGS), which will utilize advanced genome sequencing to examine potential genetic effects on the offspring of survivors. This study could yield significant findings but also presents ethical challenges, particularly in ensuring that results are returned responsibly to the community. RERF first held an international workshop to address ethical, legal, and social issues related to genomic analysis in 2020, followed by formation of stakeholder committees to align the study with community perspectives. Most recently, in December 2024, an international symposium further explored frameworks important to the TGS, including risk communication, benefit-sharing, community involvement, and return of secondary findings. In genomic analysis, secondary findings comprise a deliberate search for pathogenic variants in an established list of genes in disorders associated with life-threatening manifestations. Returning these secondary findings to research participants is crucial from the perspective of benefit-sharing. Discussions emphasized the need for transparency in the return of secondary findings, especially those with actionable health implications, including sharing the rationale behind providing information about genetic variants to research participants. These frameworks will guide not only future RERF studies but also genomic research worldwide, ensuring that the findings benefit participants and communities.Keywords: Genome study, ELSI, return of secondary findings

Introduction

In August 1945, atomic bombs (A-bombs) were dropped on the cities of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, Japan, resulting in a devastating toll on human lives. Although the exact figures are not known, ∼38% of the population in Hiroshima and 28% in Nagasaki had perished by the end of that year. For those who survived, along with the acute effects on health, was a great fear of the unknown. A key concern was the possibility of long-term health effects of radiation on the survivors themselves, as well as on their children. The Atomic Bomb Casualty Commission (ABCC), formed in 1947, aimed to address these questions by setting up long-term epidemiological cohorts as well as genetic studies of survivors and their offspring. This research was transitioned to the binational Radiation Effects Research Foundation (RERF) in 1975, supported by the governments of Japan and the USA. The key questions of long-term radiation-related health effects on survivors, and potential transgenerational effects on their offspring, continue to drive the mission of RERF today: to help improve the health and well-being of the survivors, their children, and radiation-exposed populations around the world.

RERF's research is supported by the long-term cooperation of A-bomb survivors and their children who have suffered great physical and mental damage as a result of the bombings. From a scientific standpoint, the distinctive significance of RERF’s cohorts stems from the combination of their large size, accurate information on individual radiation dose, and long-standing, high-quality follow-up of disease outcomes. The health examinations and the collection of longitudinal serial biosamples from cohort participants provide an opportunity to gain further insight into the pathogenesis and molecular basis of diseases associated with radiation exposure. This unique and valuable resource of survivors and their offspring demands that the best possible radiation effects research be conducted for their benefit, as well as for the benefit of populations worldwide. It also demands the highest possible ethical standards in conducting this research. Future research at RERF will increasingly focus on studies using stored biosamples from A-bomb survivors and their children to elucidate the effects of radiation exposure on human health at the molecular level (Kamiya et al., in preparation). A prime example of such studies is the planned Trio Genome Study (TGS). TGS is based on a continuation of RERF's work on epidemiological and clinical studies of the transgenerational effects of radiation. TGS aims to evaluate the potential for heritable genetic effects of radiation exposure on the children of A-bomb survivors by utilizing the latest genome sequencing using blood samples from A-bomb survivors and their children, with at least one parent being a survivor [1]. While this is a scientifically important question and of great interest to many survivors and their offspring, the study also presents several ethical challenges. These include the risk of genetic discrimination, protection of individual privacy, responsible return of results to study participants, and scientific and social welfare considerations. Particularly with respect to social welfare, the results of such research may influence public and government decision-making regarding victims’ social benefits and other important issues. In December 2020, RERF held its first international workshop on ethical, legal, and social issues (ELSI) to explore these complex issues. Following extensive discussions in the workshop, the concepts of “trust,” “compromise,” and “relationship building” were established as guiding principles for TGS [1], encompassing relevant stakeholders, scientific objectives, and Japanese society as a whole. In line with this framework, RERF organized a TGS briefing and dialogue session with study participants, the local community, and media representatives.

While the primary aim of the TGS is to evaluate potential hereditable genetic effects of radiation exposure, RERF recognized that it has broader obligations to the participants, including the need to appropriately address secondary findings that would also result from this study. Secondary findings comprise sequence variants that are unrelated to the primary aims of the research study but that are deliberately interrogated as they are recognized to be medically actionable with important health implications for the individual. For example, the identification of a pathogenic (P) variant in MLH6, a variant in a gene related to hereditary cancer risk, in the parent of a trio sequenced for a study of childhood autism. The return of secondary findings in genetic research stems from a respectful view of study participants. It is firmly grounded in the ethical principle of beneficence and is bounded by reasonable limitations in its scope. While policies of secondary findings are primarily focused on clinical genomic testing scenarios, these policies have been adapted to the research context. The policies for return of genomic results in research are highly analogous to policies for return of imaging findings, e.g. the detection of an arteriovenous malformation on a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan performed in functional MRI research. In imaging research, the institution has a responsibility of due diligence to actively search for such a lesion, even though it has nothing to do with the primary research aims. Genomics is not different.

Return of secondary findings may be viewed as a form of “opportunistic screening,” which offers clinical utility [2]. This is different from incidental findings, which are unintentionally discovered. The conceptual framework of secondary findings and the original 56-gene list were first published by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) in 2013 [3]. The gene list maintained by the ACMG Secondary Findings Working Group is widely used globally to guide the return of results secondary findings [4].

For the TGS specifically, identifying germline genetic variants with known deleterious effects is not the primary objective; rather, the TGS will evaluate the frequency and possible effects of radiation-associated de novo variants (mutations) of currently unknown biological significance in offspring. Nonetheless, secondary variants may still be detected. To address this and related concerns, an ad-hoc advisory stakeholder meeting convened by RERF, comprising A-bomb survivors, children of A-bomb survivors, and related persons in Hiroshima and Nagasaki, met multiple times in 2021 and 2022 as detailed in a report (https://www.rerf.or.jp/uploads/2022/08/AdvicefromStakeholderCommittee2022.pdf) [5]. The report concluded, in part, that “It is important to return information on mutations found by chance in the course of the study to participants when it will lead to the promotion of their health.” The specific language (“by chance”) in this recommendation suggests incidental findings, rather than secondary findings, which arise from a deliberate search. Regardless, the responsible return of secondary and/or incidental findings requires consideration of multiple aspects, including potential health, social, and psychological impacts. This approach is particularly necessary for the research participants of survivors and their families at RERF. Therefore, RERF has been motivated by the desire to adhere to the highest ethical standards on the return of results in a research setting to A-bomb survivors and their families. Against this background, RERF invited both Japanese and international experts to an international symposium to discuss and provide guidance on the ELSI of RERF's planned TGS research. The symposium was held at the RERF Hiroshima laboratory on 12–13 December 2024, both in-person and online. The invited participants and presentation titles are shown in Table 1.

Genetic research at Radiation Effects Research Foundation and related ethical, legal, and social issues activities

Genetic effects research at Radiation Effects Research Foundation

In humans, the heritable genetic effects of radiation exposure have been a major concern in the scientific community as well as for the general public. This has been an important research focus since the establishment of RERF's predecessor. To investigate transgenerational effects following parental radiation exposure, numerous studies have been conducted. For example, epidemiological and clinical studies have been conducted on children of A-bomb survivors in Hiroshima and Nagasaki, investigating birth defects, sex ratio, mortality, and incidence of cancer and multifactorial diseases [6–10] (Nakamura et al., in preparation). Additionally, cytogenetic and biochemical studies have explored transgenerational effects from various perspectives. However, these molecular studies have been limited in scale and statistical power, with restricted technological capabilities [11–14]. So far, heritable effects following exposure to ionizing radiation have not been clearly elucidated in humans. Recent advances in high-throughput sequencing technologies have made whole-genome sequencing (WGS) feasible, allowing us to investigate de novo mutations at a genome-wide scale.

Numerous WGS studies on parents and their children (trio analysis) have been conducted worldwide to characterize spontaneous de novo germline mutations. These studies have revealed that ∼70 base substitution mutations occur per generation and that the numbers increase with the age of the parent at birth [15, 16]. Several studies on the transgenerational effects of radiation have also been published. For example, whole-genome studies have been conducted for three high-dose-exposed families in Nagasaki [17] and ∼130 offspring born to radiation-exposed parents by the Chernobyl nuclear plant accident [18], with no detectable effects of radiation exposure in either study. In the TGS, RERF plans to conduct WGS using blood samples collected since 1985, covering 290 family trios in both the high-dose-exposed and control groups. In TGS, the higher doses and dose rates of the study participants (including both paternal and maternal exposure cases) and the larger number of participants should allow for a more precise analysis than has been possible in the past. As a result, the TGS analysis may yield new findings not previously observed. To ensure that these new findings do not cause confusion in the community, it is essential to increase public understanding of TGS.

Dialogue with the community on the Trio Genome Study at Radiation Effects Research Foundation

The Japanese Ethical Guidelines for Scientific Research state that when there is a possibility of revealing the genetic characteristics of a population with certain traits, such as local residents, efforts must be made to gain their understanding. This point was also emphasized in the RERF ELSI workshop held in 2020 [1]. In line with this guidance, RERF has been engaged in dialogue with the public and conducted activities to enhance mutual understanding. A central role is played by the Stakeholder Committee, which consists of external members, including A-bomb survivors and the children of A-bomb survivors. To date, RERF has held meetings of two types of Stakeholder Committee. One is the committee on the use of archived samples, which met from 2018 to 2020, and the other is the committee on the genome sequencing analysis for second-generation A-bomb survivors, held from 2021 to 2022. In the latter, the committee heard opinions from external members on a range of issues, including whether or not to conduct the research and return secondary findings to participants, how to manage the genomic data, and the conditions for registering genomic data in public databases. RERF received advice from the committee in 2022, and the recommendations were presented at the December 2024 symposium [5]. The committee Chair summarized the committee’s process and recommendations (key points are listed in Table 2). Reflecting the content of this recommendation, a research protocol for the TGS was developed in 2023. In addition to the Stakeholder Committees, the following activities were conducted to promote understanding of the genome research plan at the community level: (i) briefing sessions for RERF research participants (two in Hiroshima and one in Nagasaki), (ii) public lectures for general citizens (one in Hiroshima and one in Nagasaki), and (iii) briefings for the local liaison councils, media representatives (two in Hiroshima and one in Nagasaki) and RERF open house visitors (four in Hiroshima and two in Nagasaki), to promote understanding on a broader national level. These activities were widely covered in newspapers and on television, and through these reports, information about the TGS was conveyed to a wider audience. Survey responses following the public lectures in Hiroshima and Nagasaki indicated that the majority of respondents considered TGS as “necessary” or “somewhat necessary,” and expressed their desire for the research to begin as soon as possible. Regarding the return of secondary findings to participants, many survey respondents expressed their desire to be informed if there is any information that would be beneficial to them.

Further discussion on ethical, legal and social issues in the Trio Genome Study

Following the introduction of recommendations from the 2022 Stakeholder Committee, five A-bomb survivor (“Hibakusha”) narratives were presented by a physician engaged in Hibakusha medicine in the symposium. Hibakusha have suffered tremendous physical and/or psychological damage as a result of the atomic bombings, including discrimination against themselves and their children, but all are seeking to live their lives in their own way. Many are concerned about the effects on their children and grandchildren, and some are positive about the implementation of the TGS, while others are cautious. The message was conveyed that RERF should engage in research while considering the varying positions among each of these Hibakusha.

The ethical aspects of the relationship between genome research and community engagement with burdened historical memories was also discussed in the symposium. It was noted that the forms and perceptions of genetic discrimination in Japan are inscribed in the history of the A-bomb survivor community, with Hibakusha having historically been victimized. RERF was advised to deepen its engagement with communities, including Hibakusha and local residents, with regard to risk communication concerning societal genetic discrimination and other related issues. Risk communication here refers to the process by which experts (e.g. researchers and government agencies) and the general public (including citizens and stakeholders) share information about specific risks and foster mutual understanding. The objective is not merely to provide information, but to unify risk perceptions and explore appropriate countermeasures and responses through two-way dialogue. It was pointed out that the return of secondary findings should also be considered as an issue of benefit-sharing. Benefit-sharing refers to the equitable distribution of benefits derived from research to the research participants and their communities. It is not only about enabling science and medical institutions to learn from research findings, but also about ensuring that the individuals and communities that contributed to the research can share the benefits.

Community involvement is essential to achieving this goal, and the return of results should also be considered from this perspective. In this context, expert symposium participants noted that, based on feedback received during public education sessions held by RERF, a broader definition of follow-up should be considered, extending beyond merely returning research results to also include facilitating the opportunity for regular health checkups and, if necessary, referring participants to medical institutions for therapeutic interventions. One speaker highlighted an international genome project for dioxin survivors in Vietnam [19, 20], as a project with similarities to RERF's TGS in both scientific and bioethical aspects. During the planning stages of the project, the importance of Vietnamese society, history, and healthcare was emphasized, and a decision was made to proceed in a way that maximizes benefits for the local community.

Secondary findings return

Secondary findings return of results: principles

One of the main goals of the symposium was to understand international best practices for the return of secondary findings in genomic analysis. To this end, experts playing key roles in the development of the ACMG Secondary Findings list, the current global standard, provided an overview of the history, philosophy, and process of developing the list. Speakers also presented the principles of data return and highlighted key factors for successfully implementing this process. In genomic research, primary findings typically involve the identification of disease-related variants as the main purpose of the research. Secondary findings, by definition, comprise a deliberate search for P and likely pathogenic (LP) variants in an established list of genes for a specified set of associated genetic disorders. These genetic disorders are ones that have a known latent, asymptomatic period, but are associated with severe or life-threatening later manifestations that have a well-defined preventive care regimen that has the potential to reduce morbidity and/or mortality. The paradigm of secondary findings in clinical sequencing is now well established in many countries [21]. It comprises a concise informed consent discussion, coupled with a provision to allow for passive or active opting out of receiving the findings. It is helpful to frame a discussion about secondary findings as an opportunity for an individual who has disease susceptibility, but is unaware of it, to become aware of it and take actions to improve their health.

Although the ACMG Secondary Finding gene list was originally created for use in clinical settings, it is now also being used for research [22]. The list is characterized by its evidence-based approach, transparent review process, input from clinicians, industry, and advocacy groups, and broad acceptance [23]. Additionally, it is designed as a minimum list. The current ACMG Secondary Findings gene list (version 3.2) comprises 81 genes [4]. Note that the 81-gene list is only a recommendation, and some clinical laboratories have further expanded that list, as have some research programs that return secondary findings. The classification of germline sequence and copy-number variants should follow internationally accepted guidelines [24, 25].

A number of studies have evaluated the overall yield of secondary genomic findings using the above-mentioned ACMG 81-gene list. Excluding HFE-associated hemochromatosis (whose frequency varies widely in individuals of various geographic origins), the range of individuals identified with a P or LP variant in one of the genes is 2%–4%. In general, individuals who do not have a secondary finding do not receive a clinical test report indicating the absence of a secondary finding, to reduce the misunderstanding that the absence of a secondary finding implies that there is no chance they have such a disorder. Indeed, the sensitivity of secondary findings testing is not high (although the positive predictive value is robust). Any individual with a negative secondary findings analysis who is subsequently found to have a personal or family history of one of the associated disorders should undergo diagnostic testing for that condition (which has a higher sensitivity).

It is important to recognize the limitations of the scope of obligations of the research team. It is the research team’s responsibility to identify reportable secondary findings and to mobilize the resources to provide that to the participant in a manner consistent with clinical care quality standards. However, once that result is returned to the participant, with respect to that genomic variant, they are now a clinical patient and not a research participant (with respect to that finding) and it is the responsibility of the participant and of their health care system to manage the downstream clinical consequences and needs for that condition. The research program is responsible for providing the results and the initial counseling, preparing the individual for the potential results that may be found and their implications for clinical management, but not to pay for, or provide the downstream medical care.

It is also worth reinforcing that the overwhelming majority, and likely all, secondary findings in the study are NOT related to the topic of the study, which is the biomedical consequences of the radiation exposures from the two atomic explosions. As noted above, the prevalence of secondary P and LP variants is well-established, and it is extremely unlikely that a study of this size would have the statistical power to detect a significant increase (compared with baseline) in such variants in the secondary findings genes that could be attributed to the radiation exposure. The return of secondary findings is a compassionate act based on the duty to rescue that informs individuals of a potential disease risk that is universal to all genomics research participants.

Secondary findings return of results: practical considerations

Many studies that return secondary findings use an informed consent process with a passive “opt-out” process. Study participants are informed during the consent process that—if in the course of research a genetic sequencing variant is found that may have potential implications to their health—they will be contacted to proceed with confirmatory orthogonal testing and discussion of the specific results and recommended clinical management. Participants are not asked explicitly to opt in or out of the return of secondary findings, but may verbalize this preference at any time. The necessary secondary step of confirmation of any research results found by obtaining another sample from the participants also provides another opportunity for the participant to decline proceeding if they would prefer to do so. During the informed consent process, information regarding the potential consequences of the identification of a P/LP variant should be explained, including potential healthcare or societal discrimination concerns; the potential need for clinical follow-up which may or may not be covered by the health system; and the possibility that family cascade testing may be indicated for at-risk family members. The informed consent process provides participants with the opportunity to ask questions and to express concerns they may have about receiving secondary findings which the study team can then address. Digital tools, like the Genetics Adviser platform, can help streamline consent and result delivery while enhancing patient-centered care and efficiency [26–28].

Among the research participants at RERF, the survivors of A-bomb exposure are over 80 years old, and their children are also aging, but their relatives may be of reproductive age who could inherit P variants. Therefore, it may be necessary to provide appropriate information to study participants about inheritance patterns and recurrence risks. As a means of sharing variant information with at-risk family members, preparing letters could help facilitate smooth communication between study participants and their family members and their medical providers. The research team will need to have a process to return positive results to participants and to assist these participants in identifying healthcare providers or centers for any recommended management. This may include facilitating referrals for follow-up care, answering follow-up questions, and providing syndrome- or gene-specific resources.

Necessary infrastructure for the identification and return of secondary findings includes genetic laboratory professionals who are skilled at, and meet clinical competencies for, identifying and reporting genomic test results for the genes on the list of those that are slated for potential return. It is important that either the research genomic processes meet clinical standards or there is a clinical-grade replication of the research sequencing result if the research process is not clinical grade. It is crucial to recognize that, by definition, secondary findings are returned to individuals who do not have a compelling personal or family history of the disease associated with the returned gene variant. This absence of clinical evidence of the finding removes a key validation of the genomic result. When a rare variant is identified in a gene and that variant-gene information matches the phenotype of the tested individual, it is extraordinarily likely that that finding is analytically valid. However, in the absence of a matching phenotype, the possibility of a sample swap is real. In many research studies, the sample mislabeling rate is on the order of 1/1000. For this reason, it is good policy to perform the repeat clinical validation of the genomic test result on a second sample, collected independently of the originally sequenced research specimen.

An oft-expressed concern is that the person being tested will have such a severe, negative reaction to the result that they will suffer long-term depression or anxiety from the result receipt. Numerous studies have shown that in nearly all cases, those who have such reactions are a minority and the degree of the reaction is short-lived. In nearly all cases, the person being tested will return to their baseline levels of anxiety or depression within 3–6 months [29, 30]. What is critical to recognize is that they return to their baseline, not necessarily to normal. Depressive and anxiety symptoms are common in the general population. Individuals who enter the return of results process with recognizable preexisting symptoms need to be offered support and health care appropriate for those symptoms to support the return of results process. Patient/participant’s should be encouraged to receive their results with the appropriate supportive care.

Genomic analysis and return of results in clinical settings in Japan

At present, there are two major biobank projects in Japan: Biobank Japan and Tohoku Medical Megabank Organization (ToMMo). In addition, there are various initiatives focusing on medical institutions, such as the Undiagnosed Diseases Initiative, Cancer Genomic Information Management Center (CGIMC), and whole-genome sequencing projects targeting cancer and rare diseases. These initiatives aim to promote genomic medicine and research within Japan's healthcare system. However, there are challenges for the implementation of genomic medicine in Japan, such as the negative perception of “genetics (IDEN)” in traditional Japanese social culture, a lack of understanding of diversity due to a homogeneous population structure, and barriers to sharing genomic and medical information both domestically and internationally. In particular, there are many “siloed” databases within Japan with no established system for sharing and circulating genomic and health information between them. This poses a significant challenge in interpreting the diverse genomic variants of individuals. A potential solution to this issue is the ongoing Medical Genomics Japan Variant Database Search project, which holds promise for addressing this gap [31].

The ToMMo has led the first major attempt in Japan to return genomic information from a large-scale cohort study of the general population. An overview of the Tohoku Medical Megabank Project was provided, focusing on efforts in the areas of public relations, ethics, and the return of genomic information. In this case, a system for returning genomic information was established several years after obtaining informed consent in the original cohort study, giving careful consideration to the conditions under which genetic information would be returned to participants. The ToMMo process, which included due consideration of ELSI, could serve as a valuable reference for implementation at RERF. Examples of genomic information returned in the ToMMo project included variants related to familial hypercholesterolemia, pharmacogenomics, and hereditary cancers [32–34]. Experiences gained from addressing challenges faced in practical operations of return of results in the Tohoku region will provide valuable guidance to future efforts at RERF.

Speakers from Hiroshima University and Nagasaki University presented an overview of their genomic medicine centers, established to address the increasing application of genomic analysis in clinical practice. These centers feature multidisciplinary teams including physicians from various specialties and genetic counselors, and clear protocols for evaluating identified variants. The return of results at these university hospitals is implemented for patients with specific diseases, based on the nature of the tests and condition of the participants. As leading institutions in Hiroshima and Nagasaki prefectures, both universities hold public information sessions for citizens. These university hospitals serve as key regional resources, and since many of RERF’s research participants reside in Hiroshima or Nagasaki, RERF could ensure appropriate follow-up by closely collaborating with both universities, which already have well-established genomic medicine frameworks, including return of results and subsequent counseling systems.

Expert recommendations

Based on its social responsibility as a research institution conducting studies on A-bomb survivors and their second-generation descendants, RERF shared its proposed plan to return medically actionable secondary findings obtained during the genome sequencing process to research participants after informed consent, which would provide the individual the opportunity to opt out, if desired. The symposium participants expressed their support for this policy and emphasized the need to implement the process in a transparent manner, including the rationale behind providing information about genetic variants to research participants. Additionally, expert participants acknowledged that RERF recognizes the particularly significant meaning of the TGS for A-bomb survivors, their descendants, and communities such as Hiroshima and Nagasaki. They stressed the importance of delivering the research outcomes while as many survivors as possible are still alive. Since research participants may receive information about genetic variants that could improve their health, the study should be conducted swiftly while ensuring a careful framework for communication and return of results.

Evaluation of the potential hereditary effects of A-bomb radiation, the primary objective of the TGS, has been one of the most critical missions assigned to RERF since its predecessor, ABCC, was established. In accordance with RERF’s mission statement—“To conduct research for peaceful purposes on the medical effects of radiation on humans and the diseases it causes, to contribute to the health and welfare of A-bomb survivors, and to improve public health worldwide” (Article 3 of the Articles of Incorporation)—every effort must be made to advance this research. Even after launching this important study, RERF should continue and further expand its participant and community engagement. In addition to conducting research at the highest scientific standards, RERF must ensure that accurate information on the genetic effects of radiation is effectively communicated and shared with A-bomb survivors, their descendants, and the broader public.

Outcomes and future directions

Discussions at the ELSI symposium outlined the necessary framework for return of results and the key considerations in establishing such a system for the TGS and other genomic studies at RERF. The return of secondary findings from genomic analysis conducted at RERF should be carried out in a fair and transparent manner, with the process clearly explained not only to research participants but also to the broader public. RERF must work in accordance with both domestic and international ethical guidelines while also incorporating the perspectives of A-bomb survivors. Research at RERF is made possible through the dedicated cooperation of A-bomb survivors and their second-generation descendants. In line with the organization’s mission to contribute to the health and welfare of A-bomb survivors and to improve public health worldwide, it was reaffirmed that RERF must actively engage with the public not only in sharing research findings but also throughout the research process.
