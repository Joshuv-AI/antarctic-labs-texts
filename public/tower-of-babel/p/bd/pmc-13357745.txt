The association between sleep disturbance and stimulant use: A cross‐lagged examination
Authors: Viviana E. Horigian, Renae D. Schmidt, Daniel J. Feaster, Rui Duan, Sophia T. Gonzalez, Kathryn Hefner, Dikla Shmueli‐Blumberg, Ashley Vena, Landhing M. Moran, Chloe A. DiCamillo, Shirin Shafazand, Susan Tross, Judith Feinberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsSleep disruptions increase the risk of substance misuse. Substance use—especially stimulants—can increase acute and chronic sleep dysfunction. This study aimed to estimate the associations between sleep disturbance and stimulant use over time among participants with stimulant use disorder (StUD).DesignIn this secondary analysis, a Random Intercept Cross‐Lagged Panel Model (RI‐CLPM) was used to assess sleep disturbance and stimulant use over 8 weeks among participants with StUD.SettingUnited States of America.ParticipantsThe analysis included 793 participants with StUD enrolled across 3 randomized controlled trials in the National Institute on Drug Abuse's Clinical Trials Network (CTN): CTN‐0037, CTN‐0048 and CTN‐0068.MeasurementsSelf‐reported sleep disturbance was harmonized as a binary indicator across trial measures at each week. Stimulant use days per week were captured by Timeline Follow Back. Baseline covariates included age, sex, race/ethnicity, employment status, presence of depressive symptoms, any psychiatric history, treatment arm and trial.FindingsSleep disturbance was associated with a higher average number of stimulant use days the following week [β = 0.15, 95% confidence interval (CI) = 0.09, 0.22, P < 0.001], and greater stimulant use was linked to increased odds of subsequent sleep disturbance (odds ratio = 1.20, 95% CI = 1.14, 1.26, P < 0.001).ConclusionsHigher‐than‐usual stimulant use appears to be associated with increased likelihood of sleep disturbance the following week, and vice versa.

INTRODUCTION

The relationship between drugs of misuse and sleep disruption is bidirectional, intertwined and complex [1, 2]. Substance use often disrupts sleep patterns, leading to poor sleep quality, insomnia, fragmented sleep, altered duration of sleep stages, excessive sleepiness (hypersomnia) and a disruption of the neurophysiology responsible for regulating the sleep–wake cycle [3, 4]. These sleep disruptions can contribute to the development and maintenance of substance use disorders (SUDs) by increasing stress, irritability and craving, which can threaten recovery [5]. Conversely, sleep disturbances can also be a risk factor for the development of SUDs [6]. Individuals with chronic sleep problems may turn to substances like alcohol or drugs to self‐medicate, further exacerbating the cycle of sleep disturbance and substance use [7]. Moreover, the effects of stimulant and other substance use on sleep disruption may vary depending on the phase of use (i.e. acute intoxication, withdrawal, abstinence), highlighting the complexity of these interactions [8]. For example, results have shown increased sleep onset latency and decreased total sleep time during periods of intoxication, yet increased total sleep time, with increased wakefulness after sleep onset and decreased sleep efficiency, during withdrawal.

While prior studies have broadly highlighted the interplay between sleep disruption and substance use, emerging evidence suggests this relationship may be particularly relevant in the context of stimulant use. Despite the growing burden of stimulant‐related harms, including rising rates of overdose [9], there are currently no US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)‐approved, empirically supported medications for stimulant use disorder (StUD). Therefore, identifying and addressing concomitant comorbid conditions among individuals with StUD could represent a relevant approach to lessen the burden of disease [10]. In fact, there is a growing body of work demonstrating the clinical relevance of addressing sleep problems among individuals with StUD. A recent review, led by Chiappini and colleagues in 2025, summarized the pharmacological and non‐pharmacological interventions for treating insomnia among individuals with StUD [11], underscoring sleep as a clinically relevant and potentially modifiable treatment target.

The impact of insomnia symptom reduction on subsequent stimulant use has also been established. Sleep disturbance in clinical populations has been associated with cocaine use disorder (CUD) outcomes, as demonstrated by a secondary analysis of a randomized clinical trial in cocaine‐dependent participants, where baseline sleep disturbance, mediated by mood and craving, predicted worse CUD outcomes [12]. Bjorness and colleagues [13] summarized the results of pre‐clinical (rodents and monkeys) and clinical (with a majority of non‐treatment seekers) studies of sleep disturbance and cocaine use. Findings demonstrated that experimentally induced sleep deprivation can increase cocaine seeking, and there is also evidence that sleep disturbance could influence cocaine reward behavior. However, the clinical studies in this review were considerably limited by: the variability in study design (with cohort, randomized and non‐randomized parallel controlled studies); the number of individuals included, with most studies including fewer than 20 participants; the variability in populations and outcomes; and the limited number of assessments performed within the studies.

Notably, research exploring the reciprocal relationship between sleep and substance use—critical for optimizing treatment outcomes—has been limited. Studies have emphasized and leveraged methodologies to uncover the nuanced and interrelated relationships between sleep and substance use, though the results were mixed and did not consider stimulant use specifically [14, 15, 16, 17]. Further elucidating the complex temporal relationship between stimulant use and sleep patterns among individuals seeking treatment for StUD may unveil patterns, such as whether poor sleep predicts next‐week stimulant use, or whether stimulant use destabilizes sleep during treatment. Identifying these dynamics could inform the timing and delivery of targeted sleep management or StUD interventions. The current study expands knowledge derived from previous studies by exploring the temporally dynamic interrelationship between sleep disturbance and stimulant use across three randomized clinical trials conducted within the National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA) National Drug Abuse Treatment Clinical Trials Network (CTN), using a longitudinal design to assess directional associations over time. We hypothesized that sleep disturbance—difficulty falling or staying asleep or getting too much or too little sleep—will predict stimulant use and that stimulant use will predict sleep disturbance at subsequent timepoints, respectively.

METHODS

Study sample

This secondary analysis study used data from three randomized clinical trials—CTN‐0037 [18, 19], CTN‐0048 [20, 21] and CTN‐0068 [22, 23]—implemented within the CTN, which provides an infrastructure for the development and delivery of various treatment options for individuals with StUDs across diverse settings [24]. The trial characteristics are described in Table 1. The three trials were selected because they evaluated sleep disturbance and stimulant use (cocaine, amphetamines, methamphetamines and/or other stimulants, excluding caffeine and nicotine) on a weekly basis for at least eight consecutive weeks after baseline. Participants were included if they completed a threshold of attending at least six study visits within these 8 weeks, to ensure sufficient temporally linked data for analysis purposes. Trial data were obtained from the NIDA Data Share website (https://datashare.nida.nih.gov), a publicly accessible platform that hosts the data from completed CTN trials [25]. The current study only used de‐identified data from trials that had secured approval from their respective Institutional Review Boards and was therefore exempt from further ethical review.

Sleep disturbance

Sleep disturbance was evaluated using survey items, serving as proxy measures, derived from different validated measures across the three trials. These items were not validated as standalone sleep measures but were selected to capture comparable sleep‐related symptoms. Sleep disturbance was operationalized as a binary measure: being present or absent. For CTN‐0037, two items from the Concise Associated Symptoms Tracking (CAST) assessment [26] were utilized: participants reporting ‘Agree’ or ‘Strongly agree’ on a five‐point (Strongly agree–Strongly disagree) Likert scale to ‘I have been having more trouble sleeping than usual’ and/or ‘I slept very little last night’ over the past week were coded as having a sleep disturbance that week. These two items, although not utilized previously as a validated standalone measure for sleep disturbance, comprise the CAST insomnia domain and have demonstrated strong internal consistency and clinical relevance to sleep‐related symptoms in validation studies [27]. For CTN‐0048, one item from the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) [28] was utilized: participants reporting that ‘I wake up 1–2 hours earlier than usual and find it hard to get back to sleep’ or ‘I wake up several hours earlier than I used to and cannot get back to sleep’ were coded as having a sleep disturbance that week. The BDI sleep item has been used in prior studies as an indicator of sleep disturbance, although it has not been independently validated as a comprehensive measure of sleep disturbance [29]. Finally, for CTN‐0068, one item from the nine‐item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ‐9) [30] was used: participants reporting ‘Trouble falling or staying asleep, or sleeping too much’ on several days, more than half the days or nearly every day over the last week were coded as having a sleep disturbance that week. This PHQ‐9 sleep item has been examined as a proxy measure of sleep disturbance and demonstrates good correlation with dedicated insomnia instruments, showing promise as an effective screener to identify sleep disturbance in primary care settings [31]. Notably, the PHQ‐9 item captures both reduced and increased sleep (insomnia or hypersomnia), whereas the CAST and BDI items capture only reductions in sleep. Thus, the measure in CTN‐0068 may reflect a broader spectrum of sleep disturbance.

Stimulant use

Participant stimulant use was assessed via timeline follow back (TLFB) [32] across all three trials. The TLFB is a well‐validated and widely used tool in substance use research, shown to produce reliable self‐reports of drug and alcohol use compared with biological measures like urine drug screens [33], and has demonstrated strong psychometric properties for substances such as alcohol, cannabis, cigarettes and cocaine over recall periods of up to 12 months in both treatment‐ and non‐treatment‐seeking populations [34]. Participants completed the TLFB assessment during their weekly visits and reported their use for each day during the previous week. Any day in which a participant reported using amphetamines, cocaine, crack, methamphetamine and/or other stimulants was counted as a stimulant use day. As the TLFB also captures open‐ended ‘other drug’ fields, to be specified and populated by the trial’s research assistants, the list of additional specified substances was reviewed by the research team for any stimulants; for example, entries such as ‘bath salts’ were included in the list of stimulants to be counted.

Assessment intervals

To establish equivalent time intervals for calculating the count of stimulant use days and the assessment of sleep disturbance, for the purposes of this analysis a week included the 7 days prior to each study visit where sleep was assessed. If an evaluation of sleep was conducted at less than 7 days since the previous visit, the data collected for that given visit was not included in the analysis. Figure 1 depicts an example of this counting pattern, wherein days 0–1 and days 9–13 were not included in the analysis owing to a longer (day 0–day 9) or a shorter (day 9–day 12) count of days between assessments. As we utilized baseline information across the trials as control variables within the model, and the baselines varied in terms of time from baseline to randomization, we treated baseline as a cross‐sectional, one‐time assessment regardless of the length of time to randomization. Covariates

Several baseline covariates were included in the models. Self‐reported demographic characteristics included age, sex, race/ethnicity, education (dichotomized as high school or less or more than high school) and employment status (employed, unemployed or other). Owing to their proven impact on sleep and/or substance use, depression and psychiatric history were also included [35]. A binary indicator of depressive symptoms (present/absent) was created using a single yes/no item on the Addiction Severity Index Lite [36] question ‘Experienced serious depression—sadness, hopelessness, loss of interest, difficulty with daily function (in the past 30 days)?’ for CTN‐0037 and CTN‐0048, and two items within the PHQ‐9 validated to screen for depressive symptoms based on a score threshold of 5 [37] for CTN‐0068, indicating ‘mild depressive symptoms’. A binary indicator of psychiatric history (present/absent) was created using the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview 6.0 [38] for CTN‐0037 and the participant Medical History Form for CTN‐0048 and CTN‐0068. Psychiatric conditions included anxiety disorder, bipolar disorder, eating disorder, psychotic disorder/episode, suicidality and violent/homicidal behavior. The trial number (CTN‐0037, CTN‐0048 or CTN‐0068) was included as a baseline covariate (fixed effect) to account for the diverse settings, targeted populations and interventions being tested. Finally, treatment arm was included to account for differences in treatment exposure. Most participants were assigned to either the control or the experimental arm. However, in CTN‐0068, 89 participants were re‐randomized from the control arm to the experimental arm after week 6, as specified in the trial design. These participants were classified as a separate ‘control‐then‐experimental’ group. To harmonize key variables across trials, including sleep disturbance, depression and psychiatric history, members of the research team with expertise in mental health, substance use and clinical trial methodology reviewed the available measures from each study and reached consensus through structured discussion on how to map these variables onto common constructs, prioritizing clinical relevance, conceptual alignment and consistency with prior literature. Other covariates—such as pain—potentially related to sleep were considered, but they were either not available across all three trials or not assessed consistently enough to allow for conclusive inclusion in the models. Quality of life was also considered; while assessed at baseline across all trials, it was not included because it overlapped with measures of sleep disturbance and/or depressive symptomatology.

Analytic plan

Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, means and standard deviations, were calculated for variables within each trial and across trial participants. To assess the reciprocal associations between sleep disturbance and stimulant use across 8 weeks, we conducted a random‐intercept cross‐lagged panel model (RI‐CLPM), controlling for relevant covariates. Traditional cross‐lagged panel models estimate how each variable predicts itself and the other variable at subsequent time points, but they do not distinguish between stable, trait‐like differences across individuals and within‐person changes over time. The RI‐CLPM extends the traditional model by introducing random intercepts, which separate stable between‐person variance from within‐person dynamics [39]. This allows for a more accurate estimation of how week‐to‐week fluctuations in sleep and stimulant use are related within individuals [39]. Models were estimated using the weighted least squares means and variance adjusted (WLSMV) estimator, which relies on pairwise available data during estimation; participants contributed to model parameters whenever relevant data were available. The RI‐CLPM model fit was evaluated using standard indices: the comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). Model fit was considered acceptable when CFI > 0.90 and RMSEA/SRMR < 0.10, and good if CFI/TLI > 0.95 and RMSEA/SRMR < 0.08, based on standard benchmarks [40, 41]. Constraints were imposed in the RI‐CLPM to test whether cross‐lagged effects remained stable over time, enhancing model parsimony and interpretability. For all analyses, two‐tailed P‐values of <0.05 were considered statistically significant. The RI‐CLPM was performed using Mplus 8.10 [42]. All other data harmonization and analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 [43]. The reporting for this study follows the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for observational studies [44]. This analysis was not pre‐registered; the results should be considered exploratory.

RESULTS

A total of 793 participants who met the criterion of attending at least six visits within 8 weeks of the established study period were included in this analysis: 228 participants from CTN‐0037 (75.5% of the enrolled study sample), 262 participants from CTN‐0048 (86.8%) and 303 participants from CTN‐0068 (75.2%). Across the three trials, 70.4% of participants were male, 44.6% identified as non‐Hispanic White, 38.5% identified as non‐Hispanic Black and 12.2% identified as Hispanic. The mean age was 42.6 years (SD = 10.3 years). Nearly half of the participants (46.9%) were employed, and similarly, just over half (50.7%) had received more than a high school education. About one‐third of the participants (34.6%) reported symptoms of depression at baseline, while 61.7% had any psychiatric history. Among the sample, 55.1% were enrolled in the control arm of their respective trial. At baseline, 47.8% of participants reported sleep disturbance, which dropped to 38.9% in week 1, 36.1% in week 2 and continued decreasing to 32.4% in the final week of the current analysis. The mean number of stimulant use days also decreased over time, with 3.0 days (SD = 2.9 days) recorded at baseline, 2.5 days (SD = 2.7 days) recorded during week 1, 2.4 days (SD = 2.6 days) recorded during week 2 and 1.9 days (SD = 2.3 days) recorded during the final week. Participants were assessed for use of other substances during the assessment window, including alcohol, cannabinoids, hallucinogens, opioids and sedatives. Given that the three trials required StUD as an eligibility criterion, the majority of reported substance use each week was attributable to stimulants across the combined sample. In preliminary exploratory analyses, models accounting for polysubstance use showed a pattern of associations with sleep disturbance that was consistent with the stimulant‐specific models. Therefore, subsequent analyses focused on stimulant use as the primary substance of interest for each trial outcome, consistent with trial eligibility criteria and to examine stimulant‐specific associations with sleep disturbance.

The within‐person cross‐examination of sleep disturbance and stimulant use within the RI‐CLPM (Table 3) revealed that experiencing sleep disturbance was associated with a higher average count of stimulant use days during the subsequent week at all timepoints (β = 0.15; 95% CI = 0.09, 0.22; P < 0.001). It also showed that increased stimulant use (i.e. more stimulant use days than an individual’s average use days in a given week) was associated with higher odds of experiencing subsequent sleep disturbance at all timepoints (OR = 1.20; 95% CI = 1.14, 1.26; P < 0.001). Sleep disturbance was positively associated with subsequent sleep disturbance on a weekly basis following week 2. Stimulant use was consistently positively associated with subsequent use on a weekly basis. The model demonstrated good fit to the data, with RMSEA = 0.025 (90% CI = 0.020, 0.030), CFI = 0.974, TLI = 0.969 and SRMR = 0.050. Further, the failure of the chi‐square test of difference (P = 0.09) indicated that the constraints did not significantly degrade the model fit, supporting their appropriateness and allowing for more robust inferences regarding the cross‐lagged associations. We also tested imposing constraints on the autoregressive relationships; however, this test was significant (P < 0.001) and we therefore did not constrain these coefficients. Post hoc analyses examining the model among participants with four or more visits in 8 weeks found no notable differences in the directionality or size of the relationships as compared against results among participants with six or more visits. The RI‐CLPM also showed that having a psychiatric history (OR = 1.60; 95% CI = 1.25, 2.06; P < 0.001) and having depressive symptoms (OR = 2.23; 95% CI = 1.82, 2.75; P < 0.001) at baseline were both associated with higher between‐person (i.e. over time) sleep disturbance. Being in the experimental‐only group was associated with lower stimulant use across time (β = −0.36; 95% CI = −0.56, −0.16; P < 0.001) as compared with being in the control‐only group, while being in the control‐then‐experimental treatment group was associated with higher stimulant use across time (β = 0.52; 95% CI = 0.23, 0.81; P < 0.001). Enrollment in CTN‐0037 or CTN‐0048, as compared with CTN‐0068, was associated with lower sleep disturbance across time (CTN‐0037, OR = 0.18; 95% CI = 0.14, 0.24; P < 0.001; CTN‐0048, OR = 0.07; 95% CI = 0.05, 0.11; P < 0.001) and lower stimulant use across time (CTN‐0037, β = −3.82; 95% CI = −4.22, −3.42; P < 0.001; CTN‐0048, β = −2.94; 95% CI = −3.24, −2.63; P < 0.001).

DISCUSSION

Results of this study demonstrate that individuals with sleep disturbance were more likely to report increased stimulant use days the following week, and that, likewise, individuals with higher counts of stimulant use days were more likely to report sleep disturbance in the ensuing week. Additionally, having sleep disturbance in a given week after week 2 was associated with higher odds of having a sleep disturbance in the following week. Likewise, stimulant use in any given week was positively associated with stimulant use in the following week.

Not surprisingly, individuals with a history of psychiatric conditions and individuals with depressive symptoms at baseline were more likely to experience sleep disturbance. These results are consistent with findings in the literature. A population‐based study by Geoffroy and colleagues [45] highlights that about 92% of patients with a major depressive episode will present with sleep complaints, with insomnia being more prevalent than hypersomnia. Similarly, Freeman and colleagues [46] highlight the importance of sleep disturbance in the presentation and perpetuation of many psychiatric disorders.

To our knowledge, this is the first study examining the association between sleep disturbance and stimulant use over time, using a RI‐CLPM, in a population seeking treatment for StUD. This secondary analysis has several strengths. It leveraged a large data set consisting of publicly available data via NIDA Data Share [25] from three randomized clinical trials conducted in multiple sites and settings within the National Drug Abuse Treatment CTN, increasing the generalizability of the findings. The methodological approach of cross‐lagged examination enabled the assessment of dynamic, temporally sensitive variables and interactions. In fact, the RI‐CLPM approach offers notable strengths, particularly in distinguishing between stable, trait‐like differences across individuals and within‐person fluctuations over time [39]. By accounting for stable between‐person variance through random intercept factors, it provides a more precise estimate of within‐person processes, reducing potential confounding from individual differences. This approach also strengthens the ability to draw causal inference in longitudinal data by isolating the dynamic, within‐person associations between constructs rather than conflating them with stable traits [47].

It is important to reiterate that the trials included in this study differed in interventions and in their potential impact on sleep. For example, high‐dose exercise (CTN‐0037) may improve sleep [18, 19], whereas common side effects of buprenorphine (CTN‐0048) include ‘difficulty sleeping’ and ‘sleepiness’ [20, 21], and bupropion (CTN‐0068) may contribute to ‘sleeplessness’ [22, 23]. These differences were explicitly noted in the trial protocols and represent a limitation of combining data across trials. However, by including both trial and treatment arm as covariates and employing a random intercept cross‐lagged modeling approach, we focus primarily on within‐person changes over time. This allows us to examine whether participants experience deviations from their typical sleep or stimulant use week‐to‐week, regardless of intervention type, while still leveraging the larger sample size and heterogeneity across trials as a strength.

Some limitations of the present analysis should be noted. First, different measures were used to derive sleep outcomes across studies. In two of the studies, these measures were derived from depression measures. Although some precedent exists for using individual items from standard validated questionnaires as proxies for sleep disturbance, these items have not been validated specifically as independent measures of sleep disruption. Thus, findings should be interpreted with caution, understanding that these measures may not capture sleep disturbance with the same precision as dedicated sleep instruments. Additionally, to harmonize the measures, the authors arrived at an expert consensus on what would constitute the binary outcome of sleep disturbance using each measure. While the consensus approach represents strengths, it carries the limitation of harmonizing different measures across trials. Relatedly, the dimensions of sleep disturbance differed across trials, with CTN‐0068 capturing both insomnia and hypersomnia, whereas CTN‐0037 and CTN‐0048 captured only reductions in sleep. This difference may contribute to the observed variation in sleep disturbance levels by trial and should be considered when interpreting cross‐trial comparisons. Second, it was necessary to harmonize time frames to account for 7 days of concurrent sleep and stimulant use assessed days, leading to the use of some skip patterns where time frames were shorter or longer, resulting in a loss of information. Third, the findings of this study derive from a treatment‐seeking population willing to participate in research and might not be generalizable to all individuals suffering from StUD. Fourth, outcomes were derived from self‐reported measures and could be limited by recall bias. A final important limitation of this analysis was the inability to account for several potential contributors to sleep disruption beyond those included, such as housing instability, occupational roles/hours, physical pain and health conditions such as sleep apnea. While these factors are well documented in the literature as relevant to sleep outcomes, they were not captured across the three trials included, limiting our ability to examine them as covariates. Future analyses should explore the impact of these and other contextual factors, which are critical for understanding and addressing sleep disturbance in the context of SUDs.

Sleep disturbance has been claimed to be a causal factor of several psychiatric disorders and is typically underdiagnosed in patients with StUD. Freeman and colleagues [46] recommend that sleep disorders be treated as part of the constellation of problems in patients with mental health conditions, as it might improve quality of life and decrease the negative impact of these conditions. Screening for and addressing sleep disturbance as part of the overall management plan for stimulant use disorder could offer an avenue for reducing harm and consequences of stimulant use. A first step in this direction would require a uniform assessment of sleep with validated measures, including objective measures of sleep quality, in the clinical population. Future research could determine whether addressing and managing sleep disturbance improves StUD outcomes, and therefore inform prevention and intervention strategies for a condition that is lacking therapeutic options. Furthermore, examining the impact of co‐occurring substance use—such as alcohol, cannabinoids or polysubstance use—on sleep disturbance may further clarify the complexity of these relationships and enhance the applicability of future interventions.
