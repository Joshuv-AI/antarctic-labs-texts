Impacts of kaempferol on α-dicarbonyls mediated protein modification during simulated heat-treatment of β-lactoglobulin
Authors: Yanghe Liu, Yu Gong, Huijie Pan, Zhongchen Guo, Zhenxing Tang, Yicheng Zhang, Jiahui Han, Jinsong Liu, Chunsong Xie, Jun Xu, Qin Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThermal processing of dairy products generates α-dicarbonyl compounds such as methylglyoxal (MGO) and glyoxal (GO), which are key precursors of advanced glycation end products (AGEs). This study presents an integrated assessment of kaempferol (Kae), a flavonol polyphenol, in modulating MGO/GO-mediated AGEs formation and physicochemical alterations in β-lactoglobulin (β-LG). Kae (0.1–2 mM) dose-dependently reduced MGO/GO (1 mM)-mediated formation of fluorescent-AGEs and glycoxidation products. Further loss of protein sulfhydryl content, surface hydrophobicity and intrinsic tryptophan fluorescence were observed at Kae concentration of 2 mM, showing concentration-related dual effects of Kae. Western blot analysis revealed the binding of MGO/GO to β-LG was interrupted by Kae. Targeted sequestration of MGO resulting in the formation of mono−/di-MGO-Kae was confirmed by LC/MS. Molecular docking simulation showed Kae's preferential binding to β-LG, shielding potential glycation sites from MGO/GO. These findings collectively suggest a mitigation strategy utilizing phenolic nucleophiles to counteract MGO/GO-mediated modifications in dairy proteins.Keywords: α-Dicarbonyls, Methylglyoxal, Glyoxal, Kaempferol, β-Lactoglobulin, Protein modification

Introduction

Dairy-based foods are consumed worldwide due to the exceptional nutritional quality of milk proteins, which are rich in essential amino acids with notably high digestibility (van Lieshout et al., 2020). In the manufacturing of dairy products, heat treatment is essential to ensure microbiological safety and extend shelf life. However, undesirable protein structural modifications can be introduced during pasteurization and sterilization. One of the common types of modification is a non-enzymatic reaction between reducing sugars and free amino groups, known as the Maillard reaction (Maillard, 1912). The Maillard reaction produces a diverse spectrum of chemical species, ranging from intermediate compounds such as furfurals and α-dicarbonyls to late-stage products exemplified by advanced glycation end products (AGEs) (Lund et al., 2022). These Maillard reaction products (MRPs) might potentially modify the physicochemical properties and functionality of dairy proteins. Emerging evidence suggests that dietary exposure to harmful MRPs and their bioaccumulation exhibit significant correlations with the pathogenesis of chronic degenerative disorders such as diabetes mellitus, nephropathies, and Alzheimer's disease (Li et al., 2021).

As key intermediates generated during the Maillard reaction, α-dicarbonyls (α-DCs) have been implicated as an influencing factor closely related to the color, flavor, texture, digestibility, and nutritional value of thermally processed foods (Kong et al., 2015). Methylglyoxal (MGO), glyoxal (GO), and 3-deoxyglucosone (3-DG) are the major α-DCs derived from the Maillard reaction. With two reactive carbonyl groups, α-DCs have been proposed to be critical precursors of typical AGEs through carbonyl-amine conjugation (Zhao, Sheng, et al., 2020). Specifically, GO is nucleophilicly attacked by the ε-amino groups of lysine residues to form Nε-(carboxymethyl)lysine (CML), and the reaction between MGO and lysine residues ultimately yields Nε-(carboxyethyl)lysine (CEL). During thermal processing of food, the concentrations of α-DCs have been reported to increase greatly (Jiang et al., 2013). The α-DC mediated protein glycation resulted in accelerated depletion of free amino groups and sulfhydryl groups, and disruption of secondary structure compared to glycation with parent reducing sugars in the major whey protein component β-lactoglobulin (β-LG) (Zhao, Zhang, et al., 2020). Protein aggregation and reduced digestibility in dairy proteins have also been found in α-DCs-derived glycation (Lund et al., 2022). Given the ubiquitous existence of α-DCs in processed food and their critical involvement in the formation of AGEs, developing targeted interventions to regulate α-DC generation and enhance their elimination represents a strategic approach for mitigating the detrimental effects on dietary proteins.

Previous studies have demonstrated that naturally occurring polyphenols can inhibit the formation of Strecker aldehydes, furfural and α-DCs in UHT milk (Zhu et al., 2020a). Mechanistic studies have established that polyphenols capture α-DCs through nucleophilic addition reactions, where electron-rich phenolic hydroxyl groups target electrophilic carbons in α-DCs. Kinetic studies revealed that certain polyphenols react with methylglyoxal (MGO) with significantly higher second-order rate constants than amino acid nucleophiles (Kokkinidou & Peterson, 2014; Sang et al., 2007; Zhu et al., 2020b). Such kinetic advantages establish the role of polyphenols as preferential α-DC scavengers, thereby suppressing AGE formation. Several phenolic compounds (such as procyanidin, resveratrol, green tea catechins, caffeic acid, and gallic acid) have been used to mitigate the formation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) in dairy products through elimination of α-DCs (Jansson et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2020). While these studies have primarily emphasized their inhibitory efficacy, the introduction of exogenous polyphenolic additives as AGEs inhibitors in complex food matrices necessitates a concurrent evaluation of their effects on protein physicochemical properties. On the other hand, the impact of polyphenols on the molecular configuration of dairy proteins has been extensively studied, mostly in the absence of α-DCs. It is therefore essential to investigate structural alterations under simultaneous exposure to both α-DCs and phenolic compounds to minimize undesired potential side reactions when polyphenols are employed as food additives.

Kaempferol (Kae), a naturally occurring flavanol found in a variety of fruits, vegetables, and other plant-derived foods and beverages, was selected to mitigate harmful glycation reactions in this approach. Currently, Kae is primarily utilized as an important natural active ingredient and reference standard in scientific research, health products, and pharmaceutical development, rather than being directly employed as a food additive or drug. In the U.S., Kae is commercially available as a dietary supplement. Motivated by Kae's strong carbonyl-trapping ability, this study aims to investigate its effect on MGO/GO-mediated protein glycation, thereby elucidating its mechanisms of action and explore its potential as a natural antiglycative food additive. To this end, a simulated heat treatment model containing β-LG, MGO/GO, and Kae was established for the integrated investigation. The anti-glycation activity of Kae was evaluated by the formation of fluorescent AGEs and glycoxidation products. The physicochemical changes of β-LG were revealed by determination of sulfhydryl content, surface hydrophobicity, intrinsic tryptophan fluorescence, and surface microstructure. Western blot analysis was employed to visualize MGO- and GO-modified proteins. The anti-glycation mechanism of Kae was probed by the quantification of residual MGO/GO, the detection of MGO-Kae adducts, and molecular docking simulation.

Materials and methods

Materials

The 40 % (w/v) aqueous solution of MGO and GO were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Co., LLC (St Louis, MO). Anti-MGO-modified proteins antibody (ab243074), Anti-Carboxymethyl Lysine antibody (ab27684), and their corresponding secondary antibodies were all obtained from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Brilliant blue R250, β-LG (>95 % purity, from bovine milk), 1-anilino-8-naphthalenesulfonate (ANS), o-phenylenediamine, 5,5′-dithiobis (2-nitrobenzoicacid), trichloroacetic acid, and all other analytical grade reagents were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (China) or Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. (Japan).

Preparation of MGO/GO-induced protein modification model systems

The β-LG-MGO-Kae and β-LG-GO-Kae reaction system was established according to the previous report of Zhang et al. (2020). Briefly, MGO (1 mM) was incubated with β-LG (1 mg/mL) in the absence or presence of Kae (0.1, 1, and 2 mM) in phosphate buffer (10 mM, pH 7.4). In the reaction system of β-LG-GO-Kae, GO (1 mM) was incubated with β-LG (1 mg/mL) in the absence or presence of Kae (0.1, 1, and 2 mM) in phosphate buffer solution (PBS, 10 mM, pH 7.4). The mixture (8 mL) was sealed in a glass vial (10 mL) with heat treatment in water bath at 75 °C for 2 h. The heated samples were either analyzed immediately or stored at 4 °C and analyzed within the same day. Native β-LG was also heated independently as a control, and all samples were prepared in triplicates.

Fluorometric quantification of AGEs and glycoxidation products

Fluorescent-AGEs levels were determined following the protocol established by Kong et al. (2015). After incubation, each sample was centrifuged at 10000 g and the supernatant was collected. Fluorescence intensity was measured on a Varioskan™ flash spectral scanning multimode reader (Thermo Scientific, US). The fluorescent intensity of AGEs was recorded at 430 nm when excited at 340 nm. The glycoxidation products accompanied in the formation of AGEs were also monitored fluorometrically according to Ni’s method (Ni et al., 2021). Briefly, the contents of N′-formylkynurenine, kynurenine, and dityrosine were quantified based on their characteristic fluorescence at excitation wavelength/emission wavelength of 330/415 nm, 365/480 nm and 325/434 nm, respectively, with a slit width of 5 nm. The inhibition rate of Kae on fluorescent products was expressed as percentage inhibition of the corresponding control.

Protein sulfhydryls

The total sulfhydryl content of β-LG was determined using the method described by Ellman (1959) with minor modifications. Briefly, 100 μL solution of β-LG (1 mg/mL) was mixed with 1.6 mL urea-Tris solution (with 8.0 M urea dissolved in Tris-glycine buffer), then 200 μL 5,5′-dithiobis (2-nitrobenzoicacid) (DTNB,10 mM) was added. The reaction was conducted at room temperature for 30 min in the dark. Absorbance of the sample at was monitored at 412 nm. Quantification of protein sulfhydryls was achieved by applying a standard molar extinction coefficient of 13,600 M−1 cm−1.

Protein surface hydrophobicity

Protein surface hydrophobicity was determined according to Sarmah's method (Sarmah et al., 2022), employing 8-anilino-1-naphthalenesulfonic acid (ANS) for fluorescence-based detection. After incubation, each sample was diluted with PBS to concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 0.5 mg/mL. A volume of 6 μL of ANS solution was mixed with 400 μL diluted sample and the fluorescence intensity was measured at 365/484 nm (excitation/emission wavelength). The slope of the linear regression curve plotting fluorescence intensity against protein concentration was calculated as protein surface hydrophobicity.

Protein intrinsic fluorescence

Intrinsic fluorescence spectra across different experimental groups were acquired for using an adapted version of Wu's established method (Wu et al., 2009). The fluorescence profile of protein was recorded at excitation wavelength of 295 nm and emission wavelength of 300–400 nm.

SDS-PAGE analysis

The MGO/GO-induced modification of protein profile was analyzed by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), using 12 % separation gel and 5 % concentrated gel. A volume of 10 μL sample was mixed with the same volume of sample buffer (containing 10 % β-ME), and the mixture was heated at 95 °C for 5 min. Separation of proteins was performed in a Bio-Rad Mini-PROTEAN 3 Cell device (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc.). Protein bands were visualized by staining with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 following electrophoresis.

Western blotting

After electrophoresis, the protein was transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane by a Trans-Blot® SD semi-dry electrophoretic transfer cell (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA). The PVDF membrane was blocked by freshly prepared 5 % skim milk in PBST (0.05 % Tween 20 in PBS, pH 7.4) overnight at 4 °C and then washed three time with PBST. Sequentially, the PVDF membrane was incubated with the primary mouse monoclonal antibody of MGO-modified proteins (1:10000 dilution, ab243074, Abcam) for 2 h at room temperature, followed by HRP-conjugated goat anti-Mouse IgG secondary antibody (1:5000 dilution, ab205719, Abcam) for 1 h. The GO-modified proteins were detected by a rabbit polyclonal to Carboxymethyl Lysine (1: 5000 dilution, ab27684, Abcam) primary antibody and HRP-conjugated goat anti-Rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:5000 dilution, ab205718, Abcam). Immunoreactive bands were detected by Pierce ECL Western blot substrate (Pierce Biotechnology, Rockford, IL) and imaged with a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc XRS+ System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc.).

Protein microstructure

The surface structure of β-LG was observed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM, model S-4800, Hitachi Co., Tokyo, Japan). The sample was evenly coated on the conductive tape and dried before it was glued to the sample holder with conductive adhesive. Gold coating was sprayed in a vacuum spray plating instrument (about 15 nm thick). Protein surface microstructure was observed and photographed under 10,000 × magnification at an acceleration voltage of 3 kV.

MGO/GO scavenging activity

Briefly, 1 mM MGO/GO was incubated with Kae (0, 0.1, 1, and 2 mM) in 10 mM PBS (pH 7.4) and the mixture was kept in 75 °C for 120 min. Acetic acid (2 μL) was added at the end of the reaction. The reaction mixture (1 mL) was derivatized with 0.5 mL o-phenylenediamine (OPD, 20 mM) for 30 min before HPLC quantification of residual MGO/GO.

The scavenging effects of Kae on MGO and GO were evaluated using a Waters ACQUITY Arc HPLC system equipped with a 2489 UV/Vis detector (Waters Corp., Milford, MA). Derivative MGO-OPD and GO-OPD were separated by an YMC Pro C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm) when temperature was kept at 25 °C. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (ultrapure water containing 0.2 % acetic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile). Gradient elution program was conducted as follows: (0–15) min 90 %–10 % A; (15–20) min 10 %–90 % A; (20−30) min 90 % A. The analysis was performed at a flow rate of 1 mL/min with 10 μL injection volume. The levels of MGO-OPD and GO-OPD were monitored at the wavelength of 312 nm.

Analysis of Kae-MGO adducts

The possible reaction adducts of MGO (1 mM) and Kae (2 mM) in the absence and presence of β-LG (1 mg/mL) was monitored by UPLC-MS analysis. The analysis was performed on a Waters UPLC I-Class Xevo TQ-S micro system (Waters Corp., Milford, MA). Chromatographic separation was conducted on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) maintained at 30 °C. A linear gradient program was executed as follows: 0–10 min, 10 % to 90 % B at a constant flow rate of 0.2 mL/min, with a 2 μL injection volume. For mass spectroscopic detection of Kae-MGO adducts, the optimized parameters included: electrospray ionization in negative ion mode (ESI-); capillary voltage 4.0 kV; ion source temperature 150 °C; and full-scan acquisition across m/z 40–600. Data acquisition and spectral interpretation were performed using MassLynx 4.2 software (Waters Corp., Milford, MA).

Molecular docking

Simulation of the docking process was primarily performed using the AutoDock Vina program (version 1.5.6). As target protein, β-LG (PDB ID: 5IO5) was selected to bind with three ligands: MGO, GO and Kae, respectively. Conformational information of these molecules was acquired from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (www.rcsb.org) and PubChem database (pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). The PyMol software (version 2.6.2) was utilized for preprocessing of the receptor and visualization of the docking results. Furthermore, the additional interactions including hydrophobic interactions, salt bridges and π-stacking were revealed through the Protein-Ligand Interaction Profiler tool (PLIP).

Statistical analysis

All tests were carried out in triplicates and values were expressed as mean ± standard error (SD). The SPSS Statistics 20 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) was used for statistical analysis. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) together with Duncan's multiple range test was used to identify significant differences at the level of p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Quantification of fluorescent AGEs

It is known that late-stage Maillard reaction-generated AGEs exhibit dose-dependent fluorescence that quantitatively tracks MGO/GO-induced non-enzymatic modification on proteins. Mature β-LG comprises 162 residues, including a significant proportion of essential and branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), notably leucine. According to UniProt entry P02754, the sequence of β-LG features specific residues critical for functioning and stability, including 15 lysine (Lys) residues, and reactive aromatic side chains from 2 tryptophan (Trp) and 4 tyrosine (Tyr) residues. The modification of surface-accessible lysine and arginine (Arg) residues, as well as the N-terminal amino groups, has profound effects on the protein's structure, function, and nutritional quality of β-LG (Sobhy et al., 2020). As demonstrated in Fig. 1A, during simulated thermal processing (75 °C for 2 h) of β-LG, the MGO treatment exhibited higher AGEs accumulation (67.09 A.U.) when compared to the GO treatment (38.13 A.U.), suggesting distinct chemical reactivity disparities between these two α-DCs (Zhang et al., 2023). Such difference can be attributed to MGO's enhanced reactivity, which results from its additional methyl group and increased electrophilicity relative to GO. The addition of Kae induced dose-dependent inhibition of fluorescent AGEs formation across three tested concentrations (0.1, 1 and 2 mM). At the concentration of 0.1 mM, Kae inhibited MGO and GO-mediated β-LG glycation by 71.91 % and 56.59 %, respectively. The inhibition efficacy at 2 mM Kae were 96.88 % for MGO and 93.25 % for GO, both exceeding 90 %. Kae and its glycosides were reported to suppress the formation AGEs in fructose/glucose-BSA, glucose-lysine and glucose-lysine-arginine glycation models (Li et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2024; Xue et al., 2022). While the studies mentioned have demonstrated that Kae can block the formation of fluorescent AGEs derived from reducing sugars, this study confirms its ability to interrupt intermediate-mediated production of fluorescent AGEs, thereby laying a foundation for the subsequent investigations.

Significant differences (P < 0.05) within the MGO and GO groups are indicated by different uppercase (A-D) and lowercase (a-d) letters, respectively.

Quantification of glycoxidation products

Glycoxidation is the oxidation-driven degradation of glycated proteins. Early glycation products are highly prone to oxidation, a process that triggers a cascade of deleterious reactions, including protein cleavage and cross-linking. The resulting glycoxidation products are widely used damage markers that are intimately linked to glycation-induced protein modification (Tian et al., 2024). These include fluorescently detectable tryptophan oxidation products (kynurenine and N′-formylkynurenine) and cross-linked tyrosine (dityrosine), providing direct evidence of irreversible protein modification. As illustrated in Fig. 1, Kae demonstrated potent inhibitory effects on all these glycoxidation products of N′-formylkynurenine (Fig. 1B), kynurenine (Fig. 1C), and dityrosine (Fig. 1D) in both β-LG-MGO-Kae and β-LG-GO-Kae systems. At the minimal concentration (0.1 mM), Kae achieved >50 % inhibition rates across all monitored oxidation markers in MGO systems. Maximal inhibition rates reached 96.76 % (N′-formylkynurenine), 92.08 % (kynurenine) and 92.08 % (dityrosine) at the Kae concentration of 2 mM. Consistent with the results in β-Lg-MGO system, Kae exhibited comparable dose-dependent suppression of glycoxidation products in the β-Lg-GO-Kae system. These findings agree with previous mechanistic studies confirming the anti-glycation capacity of polyphenolic compounds in both fructose/MGO-BSA models (Ni et al., 2021) and β-Lg-GO/MGO models (Wang et al., 2021). The observed reduction in glycoxidation products by Kae addition, coupled with its suppression on fluorescent AGE formation, indicates that Kae acts by interrupting key oxidative pathways: the cleavage of tryptophan (forming kynurenine and N′-formylkynurenine) and the cross-linking of tyrosine (forming dityrosine).

Determination of protein sulfhydryl

Protein sulfhydryl contents serve as an important indicator for assessing protein redox status (Li et al., 2021). As shown in Fig. 2A, glycation with MGO or GO (1 mM) caused only minor insignificant fluctuations in the sulfhydryl content of β-LG. The progressive depletion of protein sulfhydryl groups in human serum albumin (5 μM) was observed upon incubation with a high concentration of MGO (5.55 mM) at 37 °C over a 7-day period. This study also found that supplementation with 0.1 and 1 mM Kae resulted in no statistically significant loss of protein SH groups in β-LG treated with either MGO or GO. Notably, in GO-treated groups, the highest Kae concentrations (2 mM) resulted in significant reduction on sulfhydryl content, showing a 12.57 % decrease compared to native β-LG. At elevated Kae dosages, exacerbated loss of sulfhydryl groups can be attributed to the binding of oxidized Kae-derived quinones to protein cysteine residues (Lund et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2023). Phenolic compounds often exhibit concentration-dependent, dual effect on SH groups during glycation. At low-to-moderate concentrations, the phenolics act protectively by scavenging α-DCs, thereby preserving SH groups from consumption by α-DC. At high concentrations, in contrast, the phenolics themselves contribute to SH depletion. Since many studies employ anti-glycation phenolic compounds at high concentrations (often in the mM range) which exceeds typical application levels, more careful consideration of the type and dosage of polyphenolic anti-glycation agents is essential for practical applications.

Measurement of protein surface hydrophobicity

The fluorescent probe ANS specifically interacts with hydrophobic regions of proteins through non-covalent hydrophobic interactions, and thereby enhanced fluorescence intensity indicating greater exposure of protein hydrophobic residues. As in Fig. 2B, glycation-induced structural modifications significantly reduced the surface hydrophobicity of β-LG, with hydrophobicity values decreasing from 24.40 in native protein to 15.19 in β-LG-MGO system and 15.62 in β-LG-GO system, respectively. It has been proved in bovine serum albumin (BSA) and β-casein (β-CN) that MGO/GO-derived glycation drastically suppresses the surface hydrophobicity, reflecting extensive conformational reorganization of proteins (Zhao et al., 2020). When Kae (0.1, 1 and 2 mM) were incorporated into the β-LG-MGO/GO system, a progressive reduction in surface hydrophobicity was observed. The observed reduction in surface hydrophobicity can be attributed to Kae's preferential binding to hydrophobic patches on β-LG. This interaction, driven by non-covalent forces such as π-π stacking and hydrophobic effects between the aromatic rings of polyphenol and protein surface, masks the hydrophobic sites, thereby preventing access by the ANS probe (Ke et al., 2023). Furthermore, elevated concentrations of phenolic compounds also promote protein aggregation (Ke et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). Consequently, optimizing the dosage of anti-glycation agents is crucial to prevent the impairment of protein surface hydrophobicity.

Measurement of intrinsic fluorescence

The intrinsic fluorescence of β-LG primarily originates from tryptophan residues sequestered within its hydrophobic core, which are highly responsive to protein conformational changes and microenvironmental alterations. As shown in Fig. 3, MGO/GO-induced protein modifications significantly decreased the fluorescence intensity in both β-LG-MGO and β-LG-GO systems. Diminished fluorescence intensity suggested partial unfolding of proteins and increased solvent exposure of tryptophan residues that collide with fluorophores. Remarkably, Kae's intervention (0.1, 1 and 2 mM) demonstrated a further concentration-dependent fluorescence quenching, both in β-LG-MGO-Kae and β-LG-GO-Kae system. This may be attributed to the formation of protein-polyphenol complexes, where Kae undergoes oxidation to quinone intermediates which subsequently bind to the protein (Liu et al., 2024). Polyphenolic compounds may also form non-fluorescent complexes with proteins through non-covalent interactions, such as hydrophobic binding and hydrogen bonding, often within hydrophobic cavities adjacent to tryptophan residues, leading to the exposure of tryptophan residues to a more hydrophilic environment (Lund, 2022; Meng & Li, 2021). Therefore, high doses of phenolic anti-glycation agents may compromise protein structure at different sites, as manifested by reductions in sulfhydryl groups, surface hydrophobicity, and intrinsic fluorescence.

SDS-PAGE analysis of protein profile

The effect of Kae on MGO/GO-induced protein modification based on the profile of protein molecular masses was assessed by SDS-PAGE (Fig. 4). Compared to native β-LG, the band of β-LG (18 kDa) became less abundant accompanied with broadened band in MGO-modified β-LG. However, GO-modified β-LG exhibited lower-molecular-weight bands. The presence of both high and low molecular weight proteins indicates possible protein aggregations, intramolecular crosslinking and degradation (Kong et al., 2015; Xu et al., 2025). In the presence of Kae (0.1, 1 and 2 mM), the electrophoretic band of β-LG was less broadened, with increased band intensity when compared to MGO/GO-treated β-LG. The protective effect of phenolic compounds (e.g., genistein) against MGO/GO-induced β-LG modification have also been manifested by the restoration of band intensity and inhibition of protein cross-linking in SDS-PAGE analysis (Kong et al., 2015). Precise identification of specific binding sites of MGO/GO to β-LG was further visualized through Western blotting analysis, providing a molecular-level correlate to the protective effect on protein profile.

Detection of MGO/GO-modified proteins by Western blotting

As demonstrated in the representative Western blot in Fig. 5A and B, covalent modification by MGO and GO was observed not only in the native β-LG band but also in bands of higher molecular weight, revealing the presence of a cascade of carbonyl-amine induced intermolecular cross-linking and subsequent protein aggregation. The co-incubation of Kae (0.1, 1 and 2 mM) led to the weakened immunoreactive intensity in a dose-dependent manner, suggesting the interruption of covalent binding of both MGO and GO to β-LG. Western blot analyses in prior studies validated the inhibitory effects of phenolic compounds on the covalent binding of reactive carbonyls (e.g. acrolein, malondialdehyde) to WPI, as evidenced by reduced protein‑carbonyl adduct formation (Yao et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). Taken together, our observation indicated that Kae disrupts protein modification by acting as a sacrificial nucleophile that competitively reacts with MGO/GO, thereby intercepting MGO/GO binding to proteins and averting downstream consequences of cross-linking and aggregation (Wu et al., 2024; Xue et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2020a).

Western blot analysis of MGO-modified (A) and GO-modified proteins (B) in β-LG treated at 75 °C for 2 h in the absence or presence of Kae. Line 1: β-LG control; Line 2: β-LG + MGO/GO; Line 3–5: β-LG + MGO/GO+ Kae (0.1, 1 and 2 mM).

Observation of protein microstructure

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis revealed significant morphological alterations in β-LG following modification by MGO/GO (Fig. 6). Native β-LG exhibited uniform spherical morphology with smooth surface and average particle diameter. In contrast, MGO/GO-induced modifications resulted in extensive protein aggregation, which manifested as irregularly shaped particles with rough surface textures and heterogeneous size distribution. These structural transformations, indicative of enhanced protein aggregation and particle size enlargement mediated by MGO/GO, mirror previous findings on glycation-induced microstructural changes in myofibrillar proteins (Wang et al., 2022). Notably, the addition of Kae (0.1, 1 and 2 mM) partially attenuated the progression of protein cross-linking and aggregation, as evidenced in Fig. 6 (C-E, G-I). The observation suggests a protective mechanism analogous to that of catechin derivatives, whereby the protein structural integrity is maintained through the inhibition of protein cross-linking and aggregation, but in a glucosamine-myofibrillar protein glycation model (Zhu et al., 2021).

Scavenging of MGO/GO by Kae quantified by HPLC analysis

Previous studies employing HPLC analysis have demonstrated that various flavonoid compounds (including rutin, quercetin, diosmetin, hesperidin, hesperetin, and phloretin) exhibit distinct scavenging capacities against MGO, while their efficacies in capturing GO are relatively consistent (Bednarska & Fecka, 2021). Zhu et al. has observed that Kae's MGO-trapping efficiency exhibited both concentration- and time-dependent enhancement, with higher clearance rates achieved at elevated concentrations and prolonged incubation periods (Zhu et al., 2020b). Fig. 7(A) demonstrates that Kae has a dose-dependent scavenging effect on both MGO and GO. After incubation of Kae and MGO/GO at 75 °C for 2 h, the scavenging rate of Kae (0.1, 1 and 2 mM) for MGO was 12.93 %, 89.86 %, and 94.47 %, respectively. The scavenging rate of Kae (0.1, 1 and 2 mM) for GO was 71.34 %, 91.41 % and 92.30 %, respectively. These results clearly indicated that Kae could capture the majority of MGO and GO, with near-complete elimination observed at 2 mM concentration of Kae. Previous studies also confirmed the trapping capacity of Kae with A-ring of the meta-positioned of hydroxyl groups in phosphate buffer both at 37 °C (Zhu et al., 2020b) and 120 °C (Li et al., 2025). The heterocyclic C-ring and its C3 hydroxyl group contribute substantially to improve the scavenging efficiency through electronic and steric effects (Zhu et al., 2014; Zhu et al., 2020b). The unique structural configuration, particularly the strategic distribution of hydroxyl groups across all three rings, collectively explains Kae's exceptional efficacy in scavenging both MGO and GO in glycation systems. The proposed reaction mechanism is not a simple, single-step scavenging event. Instead, it appears to be a progressive process where Kae can sequentially trap multiple MGO molecules. As supported by the work of Du et al. (2024) on a similar phenolic compound mangiferin, the primary adduct mangiferin-monoMGO still retains scavenging activity. This means over a prolonged time, the residual Kae (both the parent compound and its mono-adducts) remains potent enough to continue scavenging MGO in the system. This leads to the formation of more complex multi-adduct species, such as phenol-diMGO adducts, resulting in stepwise neutralization of MGO.

LC-MS analysis of Kae-MGO adducts

As shown in Fig. 7(B-G), the total ion chromatograms (TIC) revealed the emergence of new peaks in addition to the Kae peak (RT = 7.41 min) after incubation of Kae with MGO. The new peak at the retention time of 6.14 min represented Kae-monoMGO conjugates with a molecular weight of [M-H]− m/z 357 and nucleophilic addition could occur in the C-3 and/or C-5 in the A ring of Kae. And the new peak at the retention time of 5.28 min was assignable to Kae-diMGO conjugates with the molecular weight of [M-H]− m/z 429. The result is consistent with the report of Li et al. (2025), confirming the MGO-trapping capacity of Kae. In the presence of β-LG, Kae-monoMGO and Kae-diMGO adducts were also identified, indicating Kae worked as a sacrificial nucleophile, which competitively reacted with MGO and partially eliminated them before covalent binding to β-LG. In previous studies on the addition of polyphenols before or after UHT treatment of milk, the reaction between certain polyphenols and MGO was faster than that between MGO and some nucleophilic sites on proteins, and MGO-epicatechin adducts was found in UHT milk during storage, confirming the capture of MGO by polyphenols in a dairy model (Zhu, Poojary, Andersen and Lund, 2020a, Zhu, Poojary, Andersen and Lund, 2020b). Notably, Kae retains a high efficacy for trapping MGO at physiological temperature (37 °C) (Zhu et al., 2020b). Since a typical human diet contains several tens to several hundred milligrams of polyphenols per meal, it would be interesting to investigate if the polyphenols retain their ability to scavenge α-DC throughout the processes of internal digestion and absorption.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking simulations were employed to characterize the molecular interactions between β-Lg and potential ligands, providing detailed insights into binding affinities and interaction forces. Our analysis revealed distinct binding energy profiles, with Kae exhibiting the strongest affinity (−6.6 kcal/mol) to β-Lg compared to MGO (−3.2 kcal/mol) and GO (−2.9 kcal/mol). Spatial analysis of binding sites demonstrated that all three ligands contacted with surface-exposed regions rather than the central calyx domain (Fig. 8). Specifically, MGO and GO both formed polar interactions with Arg-124 and Thr-125 residues, while Kae tended to establish hydrogen bonds with Leu-10 (2.5 Å), Pro-50 (2.2 Å), and Thr-76 (2.2 Å) in the surface loop region. Remarkably, PLIP analysis further identified additional hydrophobic interactions contributing to Kae's superior binding stability. These findings suggest that phenolic compounds like Kae may competitively protect critical glycosylation sites, thereby inhibit protein glycation and enhance structural stability, which is consistent with previous studies (Al-Bagmi et al., 2024).

Conclusion

This study revealed that Kae exerts potent inhibitory effects on MGO/GO-induced generation of AGEs during simulated heat-treatment of β-LG. The attenuation of fluorescent-AGEs, glycoxidation products, as well as Western blot visualization of MGO/GO modified proteins, collectively provided compelling evidence of Kae's capacity to mitigate protein modification by MGO/GO. Furthermore, HPLC, LC-MS and molecular docking analyses confirmed the role of Kae as sacrificial nucleophiles in competitive inhibition of the binding of MGO/GO onto β-LG. However, the practical application of Kae faces a substantial challenge stemming from its concentration-dependent dual effects. While it effectively inhibits AGE formation, its incorporation induces conformational changes in dairy proteins, as reflected by the reduction in sulfhydryl content, surface hydrophobicity, and intrinsic fluorescence. These alterations indicate substantial protein-phenol interactions, which may negatively impact critical functional properties like gelation, stability and sensory properties. Furthermore, while polyphenols effectively form adducts with reactive carbonyls, the fate of these complexes during in vivo gastrointestinal digestion remains unclear. More complete understanding of the polyphenols' health impacts requires further investigation into their stability, underlying metabolic pathways, and the full spectrum of their biological activities.
