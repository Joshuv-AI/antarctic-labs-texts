The deubiquitinase OTUD3 stabilizes IRP2 expression to reduce hippocampal neuron ferroptosis via the p53/PTGS2 pathway to ameliorate cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury
Authors: Dan Hou, Yujie Hu, Tian Yun, Hongxin Li, Guoshuai Yang, Dan Yu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIschemic stroke (IS) is known for its high morbidity, disability and mortality rates, and studies designed to explore its pathophysiological mechanisms and identify novel therapeutic strategies are urgently needed. We aimed to probe the effects of the deubiquitinase OTUD3-IRP2-p53/PTGS2 pathway on cerebral ischemia‒reperfusion (I/R) injury and hippocampal neuron ferroptosis.MethodsA cerebral I/R mouse model was established. Furthermore, lentiviral vectors that overexpressed OTUD3 and knocked down IRP2 were constructed, and a series of assays were performed to probe the OTUD3/IRP2/p53/PTGS2 mechanism. An oxygen‒glucose deprivation and reoxygenation (OGD/R) model of mouse hippocampal neurons was constructed. Then, OTUD3 and IRP2 were knocked down and overexpressed, and p53 was overexpressed to explore the mechanism of the OTUD3/IRP2/p53/PTGS2 pathway.ResultsOTUD3 and IRP2 were expressed at low levels in cerebral I/R models. OTUD3 promoted IRP2 expression to protect damaged hippocampal neurons. Moreover, IRP2 affected ferroptosis in hippocampal neurons. In addition, IRP2 inhibited p53. After IRP2 and p53 were overexpressed, IRP2 regulated the p53/PTGS2 pathway and affected ferroptosis in hippocampal neurons. In vivo, after overexpressing OTUD3 and knocking down IRP2, we found that overexpression of OTUD3 promoted IRP2 expression to reduce ferroptosis in hippocampal neurons and improve cerebral I/R injury via the inhibition of the p53/PTGS2 pathway.ConclusionsThe deubiquitinase OTUD3 stabilized IRP2 expression to reduce hippocampal neuron ferroptosis via the p53/PTGS2 pathway to subsequently ameliorate cerebral I/R injury.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-024-02095-w.

Introduction

Ischemic stroke (IS) is a serious neurological disorder caused by the narrowing or occlusion of blood vessels in the brain and is known for its high morbidity, disability and mortality rates [1]. The obstruction of blood flow results in a lack of oxygen and nutrients, causing an ischemic chain reaction in the brain [2, 3]. Among these processes, ischemia/reperfusion (I/R)-induced neurological damage is long-lasting and accompanied by multiorgan dysfunction. The immune response, manifested by a systemic inflammatory response, plays an important role in this process [4]. Currently, in clinical practice, no effective therapeutic strategies are available to facilitate neural functional recovery other than rehabilitation [5]. Therefore, studies designed to explore its pathophysiological mechanisms and identify novel therapeutic strategies are urgently needed to ensure the effective treatment of IS [6].

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death triggered by the toxic accumulation of lipid peroxides on cell membranes that can accelerate acute central nervous system (CNS) injury [7]. IS is a complex brain disease regulated by multiple cell death pathways, including ferroptosis [8]. Ferroptosis can be regulated by p53 and its signaling pathways, as well as by the tumor-associated mutant p53 [9]. p53 acts as a positive regulator of ferroptosis by inhibiting the expression of solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11) [10]. SLC7A11 is a cystine transporter and ferroptosis inhibitor [11]. SLC7A11 has the function of transporting cystine for glutathione biosynthesis and antioxidant defense [12]. In addition, prostaglandin-endoperoxide synthase 2 (PTGS2), also known as cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), may be a direct downstream gene that is expressed in response to ferroptosis and is a marker of ferroptosis [13]. However, the mechanism of p53/PTGS2 in IS remains unclear. Therefore, we wanted to investigate the potential mechanism of p53/PTGS2 and ferroptosis in IS.

Ovarian tumor domain-containing protein 3 (OTUD3), a key (ovarian tumor protease) OTU family deubiquitylase, plays context-dependent roles in cancers [14]. OTUD3 has been identified as a tumor suppressor that is intimately associated with tumorigenesis [15]. Iron-binding protein 2 (IRP2) is a major regulator of the cellular iron pool and is usually activated when cellular iron levels are low, triggering an iron starvation response similar to that of a varactor [16]. Intracellular IRP2 activity and stability are finely regulated by ubiquitin modifications. OTUD3, a deubiquitinating enzyme of IRP2, interacts with IRP2 in the cytoplasm, and deubiquitinates and stabilizes the IRP2 protein in an iron-independent manner. In addition, the absence of OTUD3 leads to disturbed iron metabolism [17]. Nevertheless, the underlying mechanism of OTUD3-IRP2-mediated ferroptosis in IS remains to be further explored.

Therefore, based on the above background, we constructed a cerebral IS model and explored the effects of the OTUD3-IRP2-p53/PTGS2 pathway on cerebral IR injury and ferroptosis in hippocampal neurons. Our study provides novel insights into the underlying mechanism of IS and may introduce novel molecular biomarkers for IS.

Materials and methods

Establishment of the cerebral IR model

Male BALB/c mice (25 g, 8 weeks) were purchased from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. After one week of adaptive feeding, the formal experimental operation began. Experiment I included the Sham and I/R groups. Experiment II included the Sham + vehicle, I/R + vehicle, I/R + oe-OTUD3, and I/R + oe-OTUD3 + si-IRP2 groups. Each group consisted of 12 mice. A 3% sodium pentobarbital (80 mg/kg) solution was injected intraperitoneally for anesthesia. The mice were fixed in a supine position after anesthesia. The neck of each mouse was shaved and disinfected, and then a 1.5 cm median incision in the neck was created to expose and isolate the left common carotid artery (CCA), internal carotid artery (ICA), and external carotid artery (ECA). Monofilaments of 0.16 mm in diameter (tip diameter, 0.20 mm) were inserted into the ICA via the severed end of the ECA and gently pushed 7–8 mm to open the CCA. Sixty minutes after vascular occlusion, the monofilament was withdrawn to achieve blood reperfusion in the ICA. The mice in the Sham group received the same surgical protocol without monofilament implantation [18]. Postoperatively, animals were kept warm until awakening. Scoring was performed 72 h postoperatively.

In addition, lentiviral vectors that overexpressed OTUD3 or knocked down IRP2 (HonorGene) were constructed. Thirty minutes before modeling, the mice in Experiment II were injected once with the corresponding preparation at 0.6 mg/kg (injection volume, 5 μL) in the lateral ventricle, and the same amount of vehicle was injected into the Sham + vehicle group [19]. The process of lateral ventricle injection was as follows: The mice were anesthetized and fixed to a brain locator. The head was prepared by shaving and disinfecting. An approximately 1.5 cm incision was made in the middle of the head. A blunt instrument was used to separate the subcutaneous fascia and expose the anterior fontanelle and surrounding skull. The anterior fontanelle was used as the reference point, and the injection coordinates were 0.46 posterior to the anterior fontanelle, 1.25 to the right, and 2.25 deep. The injection volume was 5 μL (1 μL/min), and the needle remained in place for 5 min after injection. The needle was slowly inserted and withdrawn at a constant speed. The mice in Experiment II were subjected to the Morris water maze (MWM) test 5 days prior to modeling, 4 times per day. The animals were scored after 72 h of reperfusion and sacrificed (the MWM test was performed before the mice in Experiment II were sacrificed). After surgery, the mice regained consciousness and exhibited circling and tail lifting toward the opposite side of the injection. The forelimbs could not be fully extended when leaning to one side, and they could not walk normally, indicating that the model was successfully established. No significant abnormalities were observed in the Sham group. During animal modeling, the survival rate of the mice ranged from 80 to 90%.

Isolation and culture of mouse primary hippocampal neurons

After sacrifice, the whole bodies of the mice were immersed in 75% alcohol, sterilized for 3 min, and transferred to a sterile operating table. The soft membranes and blood vessels were removed aseptically on an ultraclean workbench, and the fetal rat cortical tissue was separated. The tissue fragments were cut with scissors and placed in a 15 mL centrifuge tube. Five milliliters of 0.25% pancreatic digestion solution was added, mixed well, and digested at 37 °C for 15 min. We inverted the centrifuge tube every 5 min. An equal volume of DMEM containing 10% FBS was added to terminate the digestion, the mixture was centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. DMEM with 10% FBS was added for resuspension, and a cell suspension was generated by gently blowing the mixture 20 times with a straw. The filtrate was collected through a 70 μm strainer. After centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min, the supernatant was discarded, and the precipitates were resuspended in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and inoculated into 6-well plates. On the second day, all media were replaced with complete neuron medium (containing 2% B27), and the medium was replaced with a half-volume every 2–3 days.

Identification of primary hippocampal neurons in mice

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining was used to detect NSE expression in mouse hippocampal neurons. The sections were removed and washed with PBS 2–3 times. The sections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, washed with PBS, and blocked with 0.3% Triton X-100 at 37 °C for 30 min. The sections were blocked with 5% BSA for 60 min at 37 °C. The sections were incubated with the primary antibody against NSE (ab53025, 1:100, Abcam) overnight at 4 °C and then with the fluorescently labeled anti-rabbit IgG antibody (AWS0005b, 1:200, Abiowell) for 90 min at 37 °C. DAPI working solution was used to stain the nuclei for 10 min at 37 °C, and PBS was used to rinse the nuclei. The slices were sealed with buffered glycerol, stored in the dark, and observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Cell treatment

An oxygen‒glucose deprivation and reoxygenation (OGD/R) model was established using the OGD/R method. Mouse hippocampal neurons were cultured in glucose-free DMEM in a humidified incubator (95% N2 and 5% CO2) for 2, 4, 8 or 12 h. Mouse hippocampal neurons were returned to normal DMEM and cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for reoxygenation (R) for 24 h. Mouse hippocampal neurons in the Control group were not subjected to any OGD/R treatment. OTUD3 and IRP2 were knocked down and overexpressed, and p53 was also overexpressed. oe-OTUD3, oe-IRP2, and oe-p53 plasmids and si-OTUD3 and si-IRP2 were obtained from HonorGene. Lipofectamine 2000 (11668-019, Invitrogen) was used to transfect the above siRNAs and plasmids.

Modified neurological severity score (mNSS)

The mNSS was used to measure neurological function in mice according to a previous study [20]. A point was awarded for an abnormal response to a specific task or test. Therefore, the higher the score, the more severe the impairment.

2,3,5-Triphenyl tetrazolium chloride (TTC) staining

TTC was used to evaluate the infarct size in the brain. The brain tissue of each mouse was collected and sliced (5 slices) at 2 mm intervals. The slices were placed in TTC, covered with tin foil and incubated at 37 °C for 15–30 min, after which they were turned occasionally to ensure uniform contact with the staining solution.

MWM test

MWM tests were performed on the mice to assess their cognitive function [21, 22]. Before modeling, the mice were trained in positional navigation for 3 consecutive days, and the mice were able to find the hidden underwater platform within a specified time (120 s). After modeling, spatial exploration experiments were conducted to measure the time the mouse first landed on the platform within 120 s, and the escape latency, target quadrant dwell time and travel time were calculated.

Hematoxylin‒eosin (HE) staining

HE staining was used to detect lesions in the hippocampal CA1 region of the mouse brain. Mouse brain tissues were collected and subsequently subjected to embedding, dewaxing and sectioning. The tissues were subjected to successive hematoxylin (AWI0009, Abiowell) and eosin (AWI0020, Abiowell) staining. The sections were immersed in a xylene solution and sealed with neutral gum, and then, changes in brain morphology were observed via microscopy.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT‒PCR)

OTUD3, IRP2, SLC7A11, p53, and PTGS2 levels were measured using qRT‒PCR. First, total RNA was extracted with a TRIzol total RNA extraction kit (15596026, Thermo), and its concentration and purity were determined. Then, the mRNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA with an mRNA reverse transcription kit (CW2569, CWBIO). The relative levels of genes were examined using an ABI 7900 system with an Ultra SYBR mixture (#CW2601, CWBIO). The primers used are shown in Table 1. Western blot

First, different groups of samples were treated with RIPA buffer (AWB0136, Abiowell) to extract total protein. The proteins were separated on SDS‒PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes. Next, the membranes were immersed in 5% skim milk. The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against OTUD3 (1:1000, ab270959, Abcam), IRP2 (2 µg/mL, ab232994, Abcam), Bax (1:6000, 50599-2-Ig, Proteintech), Bcl-2 (1:3000, 26593-1-AP, Proteintech), cleaved caspase 3 (1:1000, #9661, CST), SLC7A11 (1:5000, ab175186, Abcam), p53 (1:1000, AWA42607, Abiowell), PTGS2 (1:3000, ab179800, Abcam), or β-actin (1:5000, AWA80002, Abiowell). The membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (AWS0002, 1:6000, Abiowell) or HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (SA00001-1, 1:5000, Proteintech) secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1.5 h. The membranes were incubated with ECL reagent (AWB0005, Abiowell) and placed on the imaging system for analysis. The expression level of each protein was analyzed using Quantity One 4.6.6, with β-actin serving as the internal reference protein.

Flow cytometry

The cells were collected by digestion with EDTA-free trypsin and centrifuged at 2000 rpm to collect approximately 3.2 × 105 cells. Five hundred microliters of binding buffer, 5 μL of APC, and 5 μL of propidium iodide were added. The reaction was performed in the dark. Within 1 h, the cells were observed and detected via flow cytometry.

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL)

A TUNEL apoptosis detection kit (#40306ES50, Yeasen) was used to measure cell apoptosis. Briefly, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. One hundred microliters of Proteinase K were added dropwise to each sample and reacted at 37 °C for 20 min. The cells were immersed in PBS and rinsed three times for 5 min each. Then, 100 μL of 1 × equilibration buffer was added. TdT enzyme incubation buffer was prepared, and cells were washed with 100 μL of 1 × equilibration buffer. The cells were subsequently incubated at 37 °C for 60 min in the dark. Then, 50 μL of TdT enzyme incubation buffer was added, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 60 min in the dark. DAPI was used to stain the nuclei at 37 °C for 10 min. Slices were sealed with buffered glycerol and observed under a fluorescence microscope.

A TUNEL Apoptosis Detection Kit (KGA704, KeyGen Biotech) was used to detect apoptosis in the tissues. The sections were heated at 60 °C for 60 min and dewaxed in water. A 1% periodate mixture was prepared, and the sections were blocked at room temperature. One hundred microliters of Proteinase K were added dropwise to each sample and reacted at 37 °C for 20 min. The sections were rinsed three times with PBS for 5 min each. The sections were incubated with biotin (IH0125, Leagene Biotechnology). Then, the sections were labeled with HRP. DAB (ZLI-9018, ZSGB-BIO) was utilized for color development. The sections were sealed with buffered glycerol and observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

OTUD3 and IRP2 targets were predicted by bioinformatics. Co-IP was performed to verify the interactions between OTUD3 and IRP2 and between IRP2 and p53. First, the cells were collected for protein extraction. The extracts containing the same amount of protein were subsequently immunoprecipitated in lysis buffer containing OTUD3 and IRP3 antibodies and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Protein A/G agarose beads were added to the immunoprecipitation mixture and incubated for 2 h, after which the mixture was washed 3 times. The entire immunoprecipitation mixture was suspended in SDS‒PAGE buffer, boiled, separated on an SDS‒PAGE gel, and transferred to a PVDF membrane. The membrane was first incubated with antibodies against OTUD3 (1:1000, ab270959, Abcam), IRP2 (2 µg/mL, ab232994, Abcam), and p53 (1:1000, AWA42607, Abiowell). The membrane was then incubated with the corresponding secondary antibody. Finally, the membrane was incubated with ECL reagent (AWB0005, Abiowell) and placed on the imaging system for analysis.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The cells or tissues were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde (DF0156, Leagene) and osmium acid (18,456, TED PELLA INC) for 2 h. The cells or tissues were fixed with ethanol and dehydrated. Subsequently, the samples were incubated with a mixture of propylene oxide and epoxy resin (1:1) for 1–2 h. Samples were incubated with pure epoxy resin for 2–3 h, embedded and baked in a 40 °C oven for 12 h. Then, the samples were baked in the oven at 60 °C for 48 h. Embedded blocks were removed and trimmed for ultrathin sectioning. Copper mesh was used to retrieve the slices. Electron staining (lead and uranium staining) was performed, and the samples were photographed with a JEM-1100 TEM (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) for observation.

Biochemical detection

MDA (A003-1-2, Nanjing JianCheng Bioengineering Institute), GSH-PX (A005-1-2, Nanjing JianCheng Bioengineering Institute), and GSH (A006-2-1, Nanjing JianCheng Bioengineering Institute) kits were used to evaluate the MDA, GSH-PX, and GSH levels in brain tissues, peripheral blood and cell supernatants.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS)

DCFH-DA (with a mother liquor concentration of 10 mM) was diluted to 10 μM. The cell culture medium was removed, and diluted DCFH-DA was added to cover the cells. The cells were washed with serum-free cell culture mixture to fully remove the DCFH-DA that did not enter the cells. The cells were digested with trypsin, collected, and detected via flow cytometry.

Statistical analysis

The measurement data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software, with the means ± standard deviation reported as the measure of statistical significance. For comparisons between two groups, Student’s t test was used, whereas for comparisons between multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed. The difference was considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

OTUD3 induced IRP2 expression to protect damaged hippocampal neurons

Next, we isolated and cultured mouse primary hippocampal neurons and identified them. The soma of mouse primary hippocampal neurons was nearly spherical or polygonal and surrounded by short axons with few branches (Fig. 2A), and IF staining revealed positive expression of NSE in these cells (Fig. 2B). These findings indicated that we successfully isolated and identified mouse primary hippocampal neurons. A previous study reported the targeting relationship between OTUD3 and IRP2 [17]. Co-IP further confirmed the interaction between OTUD3 and IRP2 (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, we constructed an OGD/R cell model and transfected the cells with OTUD3 interference or overexpression constructs. Compared with those in the OGD/R + NC group, OTUD3 expression was elevated in the OGD/R + oe-OTUD3 group and repressed in the OGD/R + si-OTUD3 group. These findings confirmed that we successfully interfered with and overexpressed OTUD3 (Fig. 2D and E). Compared with the Control group, the OGD/R group presented higher Bax and cleaved caspase 3 levels, lower Bcl-2 levels, and higher apoptosis levels. Compared with the OGD/R + NC group, the OGD/R + si-OTUD3 group also presented higher Bax and cleaved caspase 3 levels, lower Bcl-2 levels, and increased apoptosis. In the OGD/R + oe-OTUD3 group, Bax and cleaved caspase 3 levels were decreased, Bcl-2 expression was increased, and apoptosis was decreased compared with those in the OGD/R + NC group (Fig. 2F–H). In addition, ferroptosis increased in the OGD/R group compared with the Control group. Compared with that in the OGD/R + NC group, ferroptosis was increased in the OGD/R + si-OTUD3 group, and ferroptosis was decreased in the OGD/R + oe-OTUD3 group (Fig. 2I). Compared with those in the Sham + vehicle group, the GSH and GSH-PX levels were decreased, and the MDA level was increased in brain tissues or peripheral blood of the I/R + vehicle group. After overexpressing OTUD3, the MDA level decreased, and the GSH and GSH-PX levels increased. After further interference with IRP2, these changes were reversed (Fig. 6H and I). Additionally, the I/R + vehicle group presented an increased brain infarct size compared with the Sham + vehicle group. After overexpressing OTUD3, the brain infarct area decreased. Discussion

IS is a devastating neurological disease and a major cause of disability and death worldwide, for which treatment options are limited [23]. Neuronal damage after IS is usually caused by ferroptosis and is an important cause of ischemic injury. However, the mechanism of resistance to ferroptosis in individuals with IS remains unclear [24]. In this study, we explored the specific mechanism of the OTUD3-IRP2-p53/PTGS2 pathway in cerebral IS injury through in vivo and in vitro assays, which involved ferroptosis. We found that the deubiquitinase OTUD3 stabilized IRP2 expression to reduce hippocampal neuron ferroptosis via the p53/PTGS2 pathway to ameliorate cerebral I/R injury. This study is the first to report the involvement of the OTUD3/IRP2/p53/PTGS2 pathway in IS.

OTUDs block the degradation of ubiquitin-dependent proteins and are involved in multiple signaling pathways [25]. OTUD3 is an acetylation-dependent deubiquitinase that restricts innate antiviral immune signaling [26]. Job F et al. reported that OTUD3 deficiency resulted in impaired formation of cranial structures, particularly cranial neural crest-derived structures, as well as motor defects. Thus, OTUD3 is a neuron-enriched deubiquitinase involved in the normal development of the nervous system [27]. The deubiquitinase OTUD3 has been shown to mediate endoplasmic reticulum stress to inhibit dopaminergic neuronal apoptosis by regulating Fortilin stability [28]. However, the role of OTUD3 in IS is not clear. In this study, we found that OTUD3 was expressed at low levels in cerebral I/R models. Therefore, we next further explored the mechanism of OTUD3.

IRP controls the translation of ferritin and amyloid precursor protein mRNAs [29]. IRP2 plays a key role in maintaining iron homeostasis [30]. Studies have shown that IRP2 knockout mice exhibit dysregulation of iron metabolism and neurodegenerative diseases [31]. The deubiquitinase OTUD3 prevents Parkinson’s disease by stabilizing IRP2 [17]. Decreased IRP2 expression was confirmed to be responsible for buddlejasaponin IVb-mediated ferroptosis suppression by modulating iron transport-related proteins and alleviating iron overload [32]. However, few studies have been performed to examine the role of IRP2 in IS. In this study, we revealed that IRP2 was expressed at low levels in cerebral I/R models and affected ferroptosis in hippocampal neurons. In addition, OTUD3 induced IRP2 expression to protect damaged hippocampal neurons. This study is also the first to report the role of OTUD3-targeted IRP2 in IS. Overall, our study provides new insights into the posttranslational modification of OTUD3 in IS.

Studies have shown that ROS-mediated inflammation, excitatory toxicity and programmed cell death of various neurovascular units play important roles in the pathological cascade during hypoxia and reperfusion after stroke [33]. As a result of oxidative stress, ferroptosis is characterized by lipid peroxidation and glutathione depletion [34]. p53 plays an important role in neuroinflammatory diseases, such as IS, and may be a key target in regulating neuroinflammatory responses [35]. Studies have shown that the PTGS2/PGE2 pathway inhibits cerebral ischemia‒reperfusion-induced ferroptosis [36]. Pu et al. reported that OTUD3 induced growth inhibition and apoptosis in invasive breast cancer cells through the direct deubiquitination and stabilization of p53 [37]. However, few studies have examined the effect of OTUD3-targeted IRP2 on p53. We verified the targeted inhibition of p53 by IRP2 in this study. In addition, IRP2 regulated the p53/PTGS2 pathway and affected ferroptosis in hippocampal neurons. This study is also the first report of the targeted inhibition of p53 by IRP2 in IS. Finally, through in vivo experimental verification, we verified the mechanism of this new pathway in IS; namely, overexpression of OTUD3 induced IRP2 expression to reduce ferroptosis in hippocampal neurons and improve cerebral I/R injury via the inhibition of the p53/PTGS2 pathway.

In conclusion, we preliminarily evaluated the effects of the OTUD3/IRP2/p53/PTGS2 pathway on IS and determined whether its potential mechanism of action was related to ferroptosis. Through in vivo and in vitro assays, we found the deubiquitinase OTUD3 stabilized IRP2 to affect the p53/PTGS2 pathway and reduce ferroptosis in hippocampal neurons, thus modulating the development of IS. Our analysis of the function of OTUD3 serves as a foundation for exploring its interactions with IRP2, p53, and PTGS2, which are crucial in the context of IS pathophysiology. By focusing on OTUD3, we aimed to elucidate the intricate network of molecular events that contribute to the development and progression of IS, providing new avenues for therapeutic intervention. In summary, our results suggest a novel mechanism and provide novel targets and strategies for treating IS.
