Burden of scrub typhus in north-eastern region of India: A systematic review and meta-regression analysis
Authors: Riviarynthia Kharkongor, Worthing Yanglem, Barilin Dkhar, Mercylin Lyngdoh, Balaplielad Warlarpih, Sandra Albert, George M. Varghese, Rajiv Sarkar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivesScrub typhus is endemic in India and neighbouring countries. India’s north-eastern region (NER) is geographically and ecologically distinct from the rest of the country, with variations in climate, ecology, land use patterns, and population behaviours, heightening the risk of scrub typhus among the residents. Scrub typhus remains an under-explored zoonotic disease in the region.MethodsA systematic review was conducted to estimate the burden of scrub typhus in the NER of India. Six databases were searched for studies on the prevalence, seroprevalence, and case fatality rate of scrub typhus in NER. A meta-regression analysis was conducted to ascertain the sources of heterogeneity.ResultsA total of 718 studies were retrieved from the included databases. Twelve hospital-based prevalence studies spanning 7 States and 4 community-based seroprevalence studies spanning 5 States met the eligibility criteria. The prevalence of scrub typhus among patients with febrile illness and/or clinically suspected cases ranged from 7-36%, while seroprevalence among healthy individuals ranged from 0.8-45%; the case fatality rate ranged from 3-13%. Multivariable meta-regression identified region (P=0.004) and sample size (P=0.003) as significant moderators for prevalence, but not for seroprevalence.Interpretation and conclusionsThis review highlights the under-recognised burden of scrub typhus in the NER of India. Studies, though limited, underscore the need for better epidemiological data, enhanced surveillance, and community-based research, particularly in high-risk areas.

Orientia tsutsugamushi, the causative agent of scrub typhus, is an arthropod-borne gram-negative obligate intracellular bacillus.1-3 It is a common cause of undifferentiated febrile illness,4 manifesting with symptoms such as fever, headache, rash, and eschar at the bite site.5-7 The disease is diagnosed using various serological assays, such as Weil-Felix test, indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA), immunochromatographic tests (ICT), enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), as well as polymerase chain reaction (PCR).8 Scrub typhus is known to be indigenous to the tsutsugamushi triangle that encompasses South and Southeast Asia, Northern Australia, and the islands of the Indian and Pacific Oceans, with mortality ranging from 0-70%.9,10 It is a major public health concern in the Asia-Pacific region,11 although recent reports indicate that the infection may be more widespread.12

Scrub typhus is increasingly being recognised as an important cause of acute febrile illness in India, due to its evolving economic and ecological landscape.10,13 The north-eastern region (NER) of India, which is geographically and ecologically distinct from the rest of the country, with dense forests, mountains, valleys, and high biodiversity, provides a suitable environment for Orientia tsutsugamushi.14 There is also documented genotypic variability in the O. tsutsugamushi strains in the NER of India, with both Kato-like sequences and Karp-like strains, as well as Gilliam and Neimang-65 strains.15 Additionally, the high level of human and animal interaction,16 make the people of this region particularly vulnerable to the disease. Despite being one of the main causes of acute undifferentiated febrile illnesses (AUFI),17,18 accurate estimation of the burden of scrub typhus in this region is lacking. This study aims to assess the burden (prevalence, seroprevalence, and CFR) of scrub typhus in the NER of India. This information can help shape targeted policies, improve management strategies, and develop effective public health interventions.

Methods

This study was jointly undertaken by the Indian Institute of Public Health, Shillong, Meghalaya, and Christian Medical College, Vellore, Tamil Nadu, India.

Study area

The NER of India is situated between latitudes 22°N and 29°5’N and longitudes 88°E and 97°30’E, covers an area of nearly 2,62,379 km2, and shares borders with four countries: Bangladesh, Bhutan, China, and Myanmar.19 It consists of eight States, i.e., Assam, Meghalaya, Manipur, Tripura, Arunachal Pradesh, Mizoram, Nagaland, and Sikkim. All the States of NER were included in this review.

Study eligibility

The study protocol for this systematic review was registered in PROSPERO (ID: CRD42024488614). Six databases (PubMed, Google Scholar, Global Index Medicus, BASE-Bielefeld Academic Search Engine, National Digital Library of India, and JSTOR) were searched for pertinent studies using a search strategy combining keywords and related database-specific subject terms (Supplementary Table I). This review was performed according to the PRISMA guideline for systematic reviews.20

Inclusion criteria

The review included observational studies, cohort studies, and clinical trials/experimental studies that reported or assessed laboratory-confirmed scrub typhus cases and case-fatality rates conducted in the NER of India. Participants of all age groups were included. Confirmation of IgM antibodies was used to determine the prevalence of scrub typhus among patients with febrile illness and/or clinically suspected cases, while IgG antibodies were used to determine the seroprevalence among healthy individuals.

Exclusion criteria

Case series with less than 10 participants, scientific correspondence, posters, and conference abstracts that did not provide data on the prevalence or seroprevalence of scrub typhus were excluded. Studies that reported co-infection with other diseases such as malaria and leptospirosis, without disaggregating individual cases of scrub typhus, were also excluded.

Data extraction and management

Details of the identified studies were extracted and exported to Rayyan software (Rayyan-AI Powered Tool for Systemic Reviews, 2021),21 which was also used to detect duplicate studies. Following this, manual screening of the titles and abstracts was conducted. After identification of the relevant studies, two reviewers independently conducted full-text screening and quality assessment; differences were resolved through mutual agreement. For studies that met the eligibility criteria, the following information was extracted: year of publication, location, diagnostic method used, sample size, number of positive samples, prevalence, and seroprevalence, along-with 95% confidence intervals (CI), as available; prevalence by gender and area of residence (urban/rural) were also extracted, where available. For hospital-based studies, the case fatality rate (CFR) was extracted, if reported.

Risk of bias assessment

To assess the methodological quality of studies, a risk of bias assessment checklist for prevalence studies, developed by Hoy et al,22 was used. The checklist provides a score, ranging from 0 to 10, for each study. Based on these scores, the studies were classified as having low (0-3), moderate (4–6), or high (7-10) risk of bias.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was conducted using R Studio (version R 4.4.0) with the R package ‘meta’.23 A random effects meta-analysis was conducted, and heterogeneity between studies was assessed using I2 statistic, between-study variance (τ2), and predictive intervals.24 Statistical significance, with a two-sided α level of 0.05 and 95% confidence intervals, was assessed. Potential sources of heterogeneity were explored using forest plots and meta-regression analysis.25 Doi Plot and Luis Furuya Kanamori (LFK) index were used to evaluate publication bias.26

Results

Search results

The search results across the five databases identified a total of 718 studies. Of these, 16 studies fulfilled the eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis ( Fig. 1). Although the search covered the period from 2000 to 2024, the studies meeting the inclusion criteria were all published between 2012 and 2023. All the studies included in this review had a low risk of bias (Supplementary Table II), with scores ranging from 7 to 9 (average score of 8). The inter-rater reliability between two reviewers showed 91% agreement with a Cohen’s kappa of 0.66 (95% CI: 0.52-0.78), indicating moderate agreement between the reviewers.27

Diagnostic methods used

The diagnostic methods used for confirming scrub typhus varied across the included studies. The twelve hospital-based studies mostly employed either IgM ELISA (7/12,58%) or immunochromatographic (ICT) test (3/12, 25%) or both (1/12,17%) for disease detection; one study did not specify the diagnostic assay used. In contrast, all four community-based studies assessing scrub typhus seropositivity among healthy volunteers utilised an indirect ELISA test for IgG antibody detection.

Prevalence of scrub typhus

Twelve of the included studies, from seven States, were hospital-based and reported the prevalence of scrub typhus among patients with febrile illness and/or clinically suspected cases of scrub typhus. The majority were cross-sectional, with only one cohort study reporting baseline data on the proportion of scrub typhus positive cases among patients with AUFI. A substantial number of these studies were conducted in Assam (4/13, 30%), followed by Mizoram (2/13, 15%), Manipur (2/15,17%), and Sikkim (2/15, 17%). The studies included patients ranging from 70 to 4081, with a median (IQR) of 229 (187-383) patients. Scrub typhus prevalence varied from 7 to 36%, with the highest prevalence reported from Sikkim and the lowest reported from Mizoram.

Case fatality of scrub typhus

Three studies across three States, i.e., Arunachal Pradesh, Meghalaya, and Sikkim, reported case fatality rates (CFR), which ranged from 3 to 13%. The highest CFR was reported from Sikkim, whereas the lowest CFR was reported from Arunachal Pradesh.

Seroprevalence of scrub typhus

The four seroprevalence studies, involving healthy volunteers, were conducted in the communities across 9 sites in 5 States: two each in Meghalaya, Assam, Nagaland, and Arunachal Pradesh, and one in Tripura. The seroprevalence among 4220 participants (ranging from 145 to 820) spanned from 0.8 to 45%. Meghalaya reported the highest as well as the lowest seroprevalence across the studies.

Meta-regression analysis

The forest plots depicting the pooled prevalence and seroprevalence estimates of scrub typhus, along with confidence intervals, are presented in Figures 2 and 3, respectively. There was statistically significant variation across studies for prevalence (I2=97.4%, P<0.001; τ2=0.44) and seroprevalence (I2=97.9%, P<0.001; τ2=4.68), indicating high levels of heterogeneity. Additionally, predictive intervals for prevalence and seroprevalence estimates ranged from 5-50% and 0-96%, respectively, reflecting wide uncertainty in the estimates. Therefore, a meta-regression was used to explore possible sources of heterogeneity.

In the univariate meta-regression analysis, sample size (P=0.005), publication year (P=0.017), and region (P=0.017) were significantly associated with scrub typhus prevalence, accounting for 27.4%, 39.4% and 81.8% of the observed heterogeneity, respectively. The multivariable model explained 90% of the heterogeneity for prevalence, with region (P=0.047) and sample size (P=0.036) being significant moderators. For seroprevalence estimates, sample size (P<0.001) and publication year (P<0.001) were significant moderators in the univariate model, accounting to 81.6% and 95.0% of the observed heterogeneity; however, they did not remain significant in the multivariable model (P=0.610 for sample size and 0.180 for publication year). Doi plots for prevalence (Supplementary Fig. 1) and seroprevalence (Supplementary Fig. 2) studies revealed major asymmetry (LFK index of 2.25 for prevalence studies and −4.35 for seroprevalence studies).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis estimated the burden of scrub typhus in the NER of India and highlights the challenges in accurately assessing its true burden due to gaps in epidemiological data. The prevalence of scrub typhus ranged from 7-36%, which is in line with the national28,29 and global studies,30,31 indicating scrub typhus to be a common cause of AUFI in this region. It’s distinct topographical features, ranging from dense forest to hills and plains, and biodiversity hotspots32 supporting complex human-vector interactions33 provide an ideal ecological niche for the transmission of Orientia tsutsugamushi.34 A significant portion of the population relies on agriculture for their livelihood,35 which further elevates their risk of exposure to mites (the primary vector of scrub typhus) that thrive in vegetations such as forests, rice paddies, and plantations.36

The observed seroprevalence among healthy volunteers, ranging from 0.8-45%, is also consistent with national28,29 and global estimates.30,31 This is suggestive of ongoing endemic transmission in the region,28 possibly due to its geographical proximity to Southeast Asian countries endemic to scrub typhus, potentially resulting in cross-border transmission, both through movements of humans34 and migratory birds.36 The CFR of scrub typhus, ranging from 3-13%, though within the reported national28,29 and global ranges,30,31 indicates a substantial disease burden. Scrub typhus, which closely resembles other febrile illnesses,37,38 may be missed during routine clinical examination due to a low index of suspicion among healthcare providers,37 resulting in delayed or missed diagnosis. This is further compounded by misconceptions and health-seeking behaviour in the region, e.g., preference for traditional healers,39 resulting in patients delaying medical care and presenting to hospitals only after the disease has progressed to advanced stages with severe complications, increasing the risk of mortality.

A wide variation was observed in prevalence and seroprevalence from the included studies. The substantial heterogeneity and wide prediction intervals observed in the meta-analysis indicated that the pooled estimates were not meaningful; therefore, they were not reported, and a meta-regression was performed to explore sources of heterogeneity. Region and sample size emerged as the significant contributors for prevalences, suggesting that disease burden differs across States in the NER and that studies with different sample sizes yield varying estimates. In contrast to seroprevalence, although sample size and publication year were significant in the univariate analysis, they did not remain significant in the multivariable model, indicating overlapping effects, which may be influenced by other unmeasured factors. Additional studies conducted across diverse geographical locations could be beneficial for accurately elucidating the disease burden.

While this review provides a comprehensive synthesis of existing literature on scrub typhus from the NER of India, it is subject to limitations. Only studies indexed in six databases were searched, potentially missing relevant studies indexed elsewhere. Although only English language publications were included, screening did not identify additional studies in other languages. The inclusion and exclusion criteria identified studies published between 2012 and 2023, with no eligible studies found before 2012 or after 2024. The inclusion of hospital-based studies may result in under-reporting of mild cases. Variability in diagnostic methods and lack of standardised diagnostic criteria and protocols limit comparability across studies.

To conclude, this study reveals the substantial, previously under-recognised burden of scrub typhus in the NER of India. The limited and uneven distribution of studies underscores the need for improved epidemiological data through community-based studies and strengthening of existing surveillance systems, particularly in high-risk settings.
