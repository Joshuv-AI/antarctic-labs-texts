The supracerebellar approach to pineal lesions: A review of history, evolution, and outcomes
Authors: Dia Radi Halalmeh, HusamEddin Z. Salama, Yusuf-Zain Ansari, Barnabas Obeng-Gyasi, . Virtanen Piiamaria S, Marc Moisi, Angela M. Richardson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: The supracerebellar approach is a well-established surgical corridor for lesions of the pineal region and dorsal midbrain. Since its original description in the early 20th century, this approach has undergone substantial refinement, including the development of paramedian, extreme lateral, and combined supra- and infratentorial variants. Advances in microsurgical techniques and visualization have improved safety and expanded its applicability. More recently, endoscopic integration has further enhanced exposure while minimizing surgical morbidity.Methods: This narrative review examines the historical evolution, technical modifications, and contemporary applications of supracerebellar approaches for pineal region pathology. A comprehensive synthesis of classical and modern literature was performed, with particular emphasis on anatomical considerations, operative nuances, patient positioning, and outcomes associated with microsurgical and endoscopic techniques.Results: Technical advancements, including refined patient positioning, microsurgical instrumentation, and endoscopic assistance, have improved visualization of deep midline structures and reduced operative morbidity. Endoscopic supracerebellar approaches offer minimally invasive alternatives with favorable clinical outcomes, particularly for midline lesions. However, limitations persist, including restricted access to laterally extending tumors and risks related to venous injury and positioning-related complications.Conclusion: The supracerebellar approach remains a cornerstone in the surgical management of pineal and dorsal midbrain lesions. Ongoing technical innovations, particularly the integration of endoscopic techniques and hybrid approaches, continue to expand its versatility and safety profile. Future advancements in visualization and instrumentation are likely to further refine this approach and improve outcomes for complex pineal region pathologies.

INTRODUCTION

The pineal and dorsal midbrain region, characterized by its deep-seated anatomical location and adjacency to crucial neurovascular structures, poses a complex surgical corridor for neurosurgeons. Historically, interventions in this area carried a high risk, with operative mortality once approaching 100%. Advances in surgical techniques over the past century have reduced this risk to <4%.[29] Among the various approaches developed, the supracerebellar route has remained the preferred method for accessing lesions in this region, offering direct exposure to the pineal-quadrigeminal complex while minimizing disruption to surrounding anatomy. This article consolidates historical and contemporary perspectives on the supracerebellar approach, reviews its technical evolution, and examines recent outcomes to guide future research and innovation.

INCEPTION OF SUPRACEREBELLAR APPROACH

Pineal region tumors are relatively uncommon, comprising only a small fraction of all brain tumors. Their incidence varies with age, with a higher prevalence in the pediatric population and lower occurrence in adults.[1] Tumors of the pineal gland and third ventricle together comprise approximately 0.4–1% and 1.5–11% of intracranial tumors in adults and children, respectively.[1,6,33]

Before the late 19th century, surgical access to the pineal region was extremely limited, and successful interventions were rare. The principal obstacles stemmed from the deep-seated location of the pineal gland, its proximity to critical neurovascular structures, and limited surgical exposure achievable with contemporary techniques.[19,20,29] As a result, early surgical efforts were associated with exceptionally high morbidity and mortality.

The first attempts at pineal tumor resection were undertaken in 1905 by Sir Victor Horsley, Brummer, and Schloffer using an infratentorial approach; however, these attempts were unsuccessful.[19,42] A subsequent attempt by Horsley in 1909 similarly failed, largely due to inadequate access to the pineal region.[19,29] In 1919, Ludwig Pussep performed the first successful exploration of the pineal region through an occipital approach, though success was limited by the patient’s death on the 3rd postoperative day.[29,34]

Due to the high mortality associated with early surgical attempts to treat pineal region tumors, alternative therapies were pursued. In the mid 20th century, radiotherapy emerged as the primary treatment modality; however, its effectiveness was limited, as only approximately half of pineal tumors are radiosensitive.[23,38] Despite the limitations at the time, surgery remained valuable for obtaining tissue diagnosis and guiding adjuvant treatment. Even in cases where gross total resection was not feasible, surgical intervention also enabled palliative procedures, such as Torkildsen’s shunt placement, to manage hydrocephalus.[36] A major development occurred in 1911, when Fedor Krause proposed the supracerebellar infratentorial (SCIT) approach as a viable surgical corridor to the pineal gland region. This technique culminated in the first successful pineal tumor removal in 1913 by Oppenheim and Krause.[32] Their patient, a 10-year-old child exhibiting papilledema and signs of tectal and cerebellar compression, was positioned in the sitting position with the neck flexed and the tentorium elevated. This positioning allowed visualization of the tumor’s surface, the internal cerebral veins, and both pulvinar nuclei, marking the first successful operative treatment of a pineal lesion.[30,32] Dr. Krause later detailed his experience with this approach in 1926, reporting successful SCIT approaches for pineal region tumors in three patients. Unlike previous attempts, Krause’s approach circumvented the deep venous system. His work demonstrated a foundation for accessing and treating pineal region lesions without causing significant morbidity or mortality.[26,39]

Subsequent advances in surgical technique further refined the SCIT approach. In 1971, Ben Stein incorporated microsurgical techniques into the SCIT approach, significantly improving visualization and surgical precision.[44] Both Krause and Stein favored the sitting position, which reduced the need for cerebellar retraction, lowered venous pressure, and provided a more conventional anatomical orientation due to gravity’s effect on blood pooling in the brain’s deep venous system. However, this position also carries notable risks such as increased risk of air embolism, subdural hematoma, pneumocephalus, and potentially reduced brainstem perfusion, in addition to causing surgeon fatigue and complicating instrumentation.[29]

In response to these limitations, alternative surgical positions emerged with the goal to identify an optimal approach for these procedures. In 1983, Kobayashi et al. introduced the Concorde position, in which the patient was placed prone while the surgeon operated from the left shoulder.[25] This configuration reduced surgeon fatigue and the likelihood of perioperative air embolism, but was associated with a greater risk of cerebellar contusion and increased intracranial pressure.[8] Variants such as the reverse transsphenoidal position and the three-quarter lateral prone position afforded surgeons a more comfortable stance, diminished the risks of brainstem ischemia and air embolism compared to the sitting position, and mitigated the risk of cerebellar contusion relative to the prone Parallel to the evolution of the SCIT approach, other surgical corridors to the pineal region were explored. In the 1920s, Dandy modified the interhemispheric transcallosal approach to access the pineal region through the posterior corpus callosum.[8,29] While this method provided a direct route, it was frequently complicated by injury to the corpus callosum, resulting in cognitive deficits, motor impairments, and partial cerebral disconnection syndromes.[8,29] In addition, it was associated with a significant risk of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leaks and a mortality rate as high as 90%.[29,43]

Efforts to improve the transcallosal route led to multiple modifications. In the 1930s, Horrax developed a modification of Dandy’s approach that involved resection of portions of the occipital and temporoparietal lobes to improve exposure of large pineal region tumors. Although this technique modestly reduced mortality, reported mortality rates remained high, ranging from 20% to 70%.[18,23,29] In 1944, Busch proposed a more anterior approach, utilizing a small cortical incision in the nondominant frontal lobe to access the third ventricle and anterior third ventricular tumors.[5] Further adjustments to the entry site were proposed by various authors in efforts to minimize damage to critical neurovascular structures.[29]

In 1931, Van Wagenen introduced the transcortical transventricular approach, which involved a cortical incision through the right temporoparietal lobes and traversal of the lateral ventricle to reach the pineal region.[46] This technique posed substantial risks, including hemorrhage, infection, and injury to surrounding brain tissue, with many patients experiencing postoperative visual field deficits.[23,29,46]

More recently, in 1990, Van den Bergh introduced the lateral paramedian infratentorial approach, which provided excellent visualization of venous anatomy and a shorter route to the pineal region while preserving critical venous structures. Importantly, this approach eliminated the risks of air embolism and pneumocephalus.[45]

The latter half of the 20th century witnessed the gradual integration of endoscopic techniques into the management of pineal region tumors. The exact inception of endoscopic access to the pineal region is difficult to pinpoint, as these methods evolved incrementally. Early neuroendoscopic procedures targeting intraventricular lesions began in 1963.[17] In 1970, Fukushima introduced the use of a fibroscope for diagnostic evaluation of third ventricular tumors,[12] and by 1973, he expanded this technique using the ventriculofiberscope to obtain biopsies of pineal region masses.[13] A major milestone occurred in 1997, when Ellenbogen and Moores reported the first simultaneous endoscopic third ventriculostomy and biopsy of pineal region tumors.[10] This combined approach rapidly gained acceptance among neurosurgeons, particularly for patients presenting with hydrocephalus. Subsequent case series and studies further refined the indications, safety, and efficacy of endoscopic techniques in pineal region surgery.

Most recently, further refinement of minimally invasive strategies for deep-seated intracranial lesions has been achieved with the development of burr hole microsurgery, as pioneered and popularized by Pitskhelauri et al.[35] This technique represents an evolution beyond traditional keyhole and endoscopic approaches, utilizing a standard 14-mm burr hole combined with microsurgical instrumentation and hands-free microscope control to permit true bimanual dissection through an ultra-narrow corridor. In a large consecutive series, this approach was successfully applied to a wide range of intracranial pathologies, including pineal and third ventricular tumors through the SCIT route, achieving high rates of gross-total or near-total resection with low morbidity. Importantly, burr hole microsurgery preserves the advantages of minimal cortical exposure and reduced brain retraction while overcoming intrinsic limitations of endoscopy, particularly the inability to operate freely with two hands. As such, this technique may be regarded as a microsurgical enhancement of endoscopic philosophy, extending the armamentarium for safe and effective treatment of deep midline lesions such as pineal region tumors.

SUPRACEREBELLAR APPROACHES

Supracerebellar median infratentorial approach

The SCIT approach is a midline corridor most commonly employed for small- to medium-sized pineal region tumors with limited rostral or caudal extension along the sagittal plane. It is particularly suited for lesions located beneath the deep venous complex, below the tentorial edge, and confined to the posterior third ventricle or pineal recess.[31] Tumors extending significantly lateral to the tentorial notch or superiorly into the supratentorial compartment are less amenable to this approach due to restricted visualization and limited vascular control.[31]

The SCIT approach may be performed in the sitting, semi-sitting, lateral decubitus, or Concorde position.[4,25] Many surgeons favor the sitting or semi-sitting position due to gravity-assisted cerebellar descent, which minimizes fixed retraction, improves venous decompression, and enhances visualization of the pineal region and adjacent deep venous structures.[4,29] Additional advantages include reduced pooling of CSF and blood within the operative field and facilitated dissection of adherent veins from the tumor capsule.[4] Some surgeons preferentially employ the Concorde position to mitigate risks associated with the sitting position, particularly venous air embolism (VAE) and intraoperative hypotension, which have been reported more frequently with upright positioning.[29,43] Tilting the operating table forward allows the tentorial plane to become more parallel to the floor, thereby improving visualization and instrument maneuverability within the pineal region.[31] When appropriately executed, the SCIT approach provides surgical exposure comparable to supratentorial routes while avoiding cortical transgression of the parietal and occipital lobes, thus reducing postoperative visual morbidity.[31]

Despite its advantages, the SCIT approach presents several challenges. Gravitational descent of the cerebellum may result in compression of the tonsils at the foramen magnum, increasing the risk of postoperative ischemia or cerebellar injury. Postoperative pneumocephalus remains a recognized concern, particularly in the sitting position. Mottolese et al. demonstrated that maintaining the patient’s head below the level of the heart in the immediate postoperative period may reduce this risk without increasing hemorrhagic complications.[31]

To enhance exposure and safety, several refinements have been proposed, including bilateral extension of the craniotomy toward the transverse–sigmoid junction, routine opening of the cisterna magna to improve cerebellar relaxation, and placement of tentorial stay sutures to widen the operative corridor and improve illumination of deep structures. Continuous intraoperative assessment of dural venous sinus patency using micro-Doppler ultrasonography is essential to ensure venous preservation during dissection.

Supracerebellar paramedian infratentorial approach

The supracerebellar paramedian infratentorial approach represents a lateralized variant of the SCIT corridor and is particularly useful for lesions involving the lateral pineal region, posterior third ventricle, dorsolateral midbrain, posterior tentorial incisura, and superior cerebellar surface, as well as select mesial temporal and parahippocampal lesions.[27] This approach is favored when lateral exposure is required or when preservation of midline venous structures is critical.

First introduced by Yasargil in 1976 to access the superior cerebellar artery and posterior incisural space,[48] this approach exploits the natural medial-to-lateral slope of the cerebellum. Similar to the median SCIT approach, it is commonly performed in the sitting or semi-sitting position with head flexion to reduce the steep angle of the tentorium over the cerebellar surface.[27]

Compared with the classical midline SCIT approach, the paramedian corridor minimizes the need to sacrifice midline venous structures, particularly superior vermian bridging veins, due to the relative paucity of veins along the paramedian superior cerebellar surface.[27] Importantly, this approach allows safe bilateral access around the precentral vein, facilitating its preservation and reducing venous morbidity.

Procedure-related risks primarily involve injury to adjacent neurovascular structures, emphasizing the need for meticulous anatomical knowledge. Special attention must be paid to the trochlear nerve, which courses close to the surgical trajectory and is vulnerable during dissection.[27] Position-related complications, including VAE, hypotension, and hemodynamic instability, remain concerns when using upright positioning.[27]

While the median SCIT approach provides a direct midline view of the pineal region, it often necessitates the sacrifice of midline veins and carries a risk of injury to the torcula. In contrast, the paramedian approach offers improved lateral visualization, reduced venous sacrifice, and enhanced adaptability for lesions extending posterolaterally. Preservation of the falx cerebelli also improves dural closure and reduces postoperative complications such as pseudomeningocele and wound infection.

Supracerebellar extreme/far lateral infratentorial approach

The extreme lateral supracerebellar infratentorial (ELSI) approach was originally developed to access lesions of the posterolateral mesencephalon and was first described by Vishteh et al. for brainstem cavernomas, pilocytic astrocytomas, and posterior circulation aneurysms.[47] With increasing experience, its indications expanded to include petroclival meningiomas, thalamic gliomas, and posteromedial temporal lobe gliomas. This approach provides a more oblique trajectory to the brainstem and lateral midbrain, making it particularly valuable for laterally displaced or exophytic lesions not adequately exposed through median or paramedian corridors.

Supracerebellar transtentorial approach

The occipital transtentorial (OTT) approach, or Poppen’s approach, is primarily indicated for pineal region tumors with significant superior or lateral extension, or lesions extending into the cerebellomesencephalic fissure beyond the reach of infratentorial corridors.[29] It provides wide exposure of the pineal region, quadrigeminal plate, and posterior third ventricle.

Originally described by Heppner in 1959 and popularized by Poppen between 1966 and 1968,[36,37] the approach initially involved substantial occipital lobe retraction, leading to visual complications and surgeon discomfort.[22] Glasauer refined the technique in 1970 by introducing a direct transtentorial route without cortical sacrifice.[14] Jamieson later modified the approach by shifting closer to the midline and sectioning the tentorium away from the straight sinus, reducing cerebellar retraction and improving visualization.[21] Subsequent incorporation of the operating microscope further enhanced safety.[28]

Despite excellent exposure, the OTT approach carries a substantial risk of postoperative visual field deficits, particularly homonymous hemianopia, due to occipital lobe retraction. Cerebellar edema and injury may also occur, limiting its use for patients in whom visual preservation is paramount.[25,43]

Ausman et al. described a modified occipital–parietal transtentorial approach using a three-quarter prone or 45° head position, improving surgeon ergonomics and exposure of the pineal region, midbrain, superior vermis, and third ventricle, while mitigating some limitations of the classical OTT technique.[3,22]

Recognizing the complementary strengths of individual corridors, several authors have explored combined approaches. Sekhar introduced a combined supra- and infratentorial trans sinus approach, integrating SCIT and OTT techniques for large pineal tumors, although at the cost of potential compromise to permanent venous drainage.[40]

Aoki demonstrated the successful combination of OTT and SCIT approaches in the Concorde position for treatment of an upper vermian arteriovenous malformation, achieving complete resection without complications and highlighting the feasibility of tailored, hybrid strategies for complex lesions.[2]

Summary

Surgical approach selection for pineal region lesions should be individualized based on tumor size, direction of extension, relationship to the deep venous system, and degree of supratentorial involvement. In general, the median SCIT approach is best suited for small- to medium-sized midline lesions located beneath the deep venous complex, below the tentorial edge, and confined to the pineal recess or posterior third ventricle. When lesions extend laterally or when preservation of midline venous structures is especially important, the paramedian SCIT route may offer improved lateral visualization with less need for venous sacrifice. For more laterally displaced or exophytic lesions involving the lateral midbrain, the ELSI approach provides a more oblique operative trajectory beyond the reach of standard midline corridors. In contrast, tumors with substantial superior or supratentorial extension, marked lateral spread, or multicompartment involvement are often better addressed through an OTT or combined supra- and infratentorial approach, which affords broader exposure at the expense of greater risk of occipital lobe retraction–related morbidity. OUTCOMES OF SUPRACEREBELLAR APPROACHES

The outcomes associated with the supracerebellar approaches have undergone significant improvements since their inception. Early attempts to resect pineal region tumors were often met with challenges such as limited access to the target area, achieving only partial resections, and encountering high rates of morbidity and mortality. Subsequent introductions of microneurosurgery techniques coupled with advancements in computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging technologies have optimized the outcomes associated with the supracerebellar approaches.

A 2022 retrospective analysis of 62 patients undergoing the SCIT approach for pineal cysts revealed that total cyst resection was achieved in 59 patients, with no major intraoperative complications or patient deaths reported.[11] Remarkably, 89% of patients showed an improvement in their symptoms, experiencing either complete or partial resolution.[11] The authors also highlighted the effective use of both median and paramedian approaches.[11] A comparative analysis indicated that the paramedian approach, while equally efficacious as the midline approach in excising pineal cysts and tumors, exhibited lower complication rates and shorter operative durations, with no cases of patient mortality. In a recently published case series, which concentrated on the lateral SCIT approach, favorable outcomes were observed for 11 patients treated in a semi-sitting position.[9] These outcomes included the absence of complications and a decreased susceptibility to VAE and tension pneumocephalus.[9]

Recent investigations into the OTT approach have revealed notably favorable outcomes in contrast to findings from half a century ago. The inaugural implementation of the OTT approach by Poppen in 1966 laid the groundwork for subsequent enhancements in patient prognosis.[39] A study conducted in 2021 involving 30 patients who underwent the OTT approach for pineal lesions revealed promising results.[22] Among them, 23 patients attained gross-total or near-total resection, while 7 achieved subtotal resection, demonstrating the OTT approach’s relative safety when the craniotomy is performed meticulously.[22]

ENDOSCOPIC SUPRACEREBELLAR APPROACH

The endoscopic supracerebellar approach has emerged as a promising modality for managing pineal lesions, offering improved visualization and access to deep-seated brain structures. Initially explored in 1973 for addressing subparaventricular tumors, endoscopy for the pineal region has undergone progressive refinement.[7]

Operative overview

The patient is typically placed in a sitting position, firmly secured to a rigid Mayfield frame.[7] This positioning is chosen for its ergonomic benefits to the surgeon, although it carries a risk of VAE. Alternatively, a prone position may be adopted with a 15° upper body tilt to facilitate venous drainage.[16] An approximately 3 cm vertical incision is made, followed by a microcraniotomy at the inferior margin of the transverse sinus. This approach permits a semilunar dural opening, minimizing disruption to brain tissue. Navigating along the lower margin of the tentorium, care is taken to preserve the tentorial bridging veins. Upon reaching the deep venous confluence, the endoscope is directed toward the pathology. Coronally oriented arachnoid adhesions are meticulously opened, and dissection commences using a bimanual technique.

The corridor established for the endoscope respects the bridging veins, maneuvering around obstacles to reach the tumor.[7] Neuronavigation is employed to enhance precision by optimizing visualization and ensuring accurate lesion targeting. Given the deep-seated nature of the pineal region and the complex neurovascular structures encountered, precise navigation is essential. The endoscope offers a clear view of these structures, facilitating meticulous maneuvering and dissection.

Advantages of the endoscopic technique

The utilization of a neuroendoscopic approach presents numerous advantages compared to conventional methods. Conventionally, addressing pineal region tumors necessitates large craniotomies.[41] However, the endoscopic approach enables minimally invasive keyhole craniotomies due to its compact design.[42] Shahinian and Ra first demonstrated the achievement of gross-total resection of pineal region tumors through a full-endoscopic approach in the literature, utilizing only a 1.5–2-cm occipital burr hole centered at the inion, without neural tissue violation or cerebellar retraction.[41] Another notable benefit of minimally invasive surgery is the reduction in operative duration.[41] The endoscopic approach offers enhanced visibility and illumination, particularly beneficial for accessing deep-seated brain lesions, and it provides a panoramic view that facilitates better maneuverability of surgical instruments in narrow corridors.[42]

Moreover, Zhu et al. underscored the benefits of this approach in preserving cranial nerve function, minimizing tissue retraction, and significantly reducing the occurrence of VAE or venous embolism.[49] The ability to utilize a smaller bone window diminishes trauma to adjacent brain tissue.[49] In another study, Khoulali et al. employed the endoscopic supracerebellar approach to address a case of mesencephalic enlarged Virchow Robin space fenestration, resulting in symptom improvement and reduction in cyst size.[24] These findings collectively highlight the advantages of the endoscopic supracerebellar approach in managing pineal region tumors, including lower complication rates, shorter surgical duration, and effective tumor resection, rendering it a valuable asset in neurosurgical practice.

Disadvantages of the endoscopic technique

The endoscopic approach, while minimally invasive, is not without limitations, namely its technical intricacy. This contributes to a steep learning curve, particularly for novices to the method, with issues such as instrument conflict and the constraints of two-dimensional imaging.[15] Meticulous planning and evaluation are necessary due to the design of the endoscopic corridor, which requires navigating through narrow spaces and avoiding venous injury during instrument insertion.[7] To mitigate risks, the use of intraoperative frameless navigation is essential to avoid dural sinuses when placing the burr hole. Furthermore, intraoperative bleeding from the tumor’s vascularity can obscure vision, complicating the debulking process and increasing the likelihood of complications.[7]

Tumor size and consistency play crucial roles in determining the feasibility of endoscopic resection. Endoscopic approach may not be suitable for tumors larger than 2.5 cm or for those with hard consistency.[7] Pineal lesions carry a risk of CSF dissemination, which can be as high as 47% depending on the tumor type. Managing this risk necessitates a careful strategy, particularly during biopsy procedures.[7] Moreover, it is imperative to have an experienced anesthesiologist proficient in managing the potential complications associated with the sitting position.

In managing the complications associated with the endoscopic supracerebellar approach, several key strategies are employed. Protection of the transverse sinus through precise bone opening and ensuring cerebellar relaxation through gravity-assisted positioning and controlled CSF release are critical to minimize venous complications.[15] A meticulous preoperative assessment of imaging, including the analysis of tumor position and cranial anatomy, guides the surgical plan.[7] The operative checklist, including light source management and instrument readiness, is vital for a smooth procedure. Accurate neuronavigation, achieved through appropriate preoperative registration methods such as skin fiducials, surface matching, or other localization techniques, is recommended to optimize surgical planning and intraoperative orientation. Intraoperatively, the endoscope must be advanced carefully to avoid resistance and injury, with a surgical assistant managing the endoscope holder and irrigation system. Bimanual dissection techniques under endoscopic view require careful application to avoid venous injury and ensure effective tumor resection.[7] Importantly, the surgical team must be prepared to transition to an open approach if the endoscopic approach becomes challenging or if optimal tumor resection is compromised.[7]

Clinical outcomes

Despite these potential complications, the endoscopic approach has demonstrated significant clinical efficacy. In a study by Gu et al., eight patients underwent this approach for pineal region tumors, resulting in total tumor removal in seven cases and effective subsequent therapy, including chemotherapy and radiotherapy, in the remaining case.[16] All patients recovered well with no tumor recurrence at 1 year follow-up, demonstrating the effectiveness of the approach in tumor resection and patient recovery.[16] Shahrestani et al. reported on six patients treated via this approach, achieving gross-total resection in five out of six cases.[42] This study also highlighted the reduced surgical duration (average of 3.54 h), which is significantly lower than traditional microsurgical approaches, contributing to a lower rate of postoperative complications.[42]

CONCLUSION

Our comprehensive review of the supracerebellar approach demonstrates its enduring importance in the management of pineal region tumors. Over time, this approach has evolved substantially, transitioning from early techniques associated with considerable morbidity to refined, minimally invasive strategies tailored to individual patient and lesion characteristics. The progression from initial exploratory procedures to modern microsurgical and endoscopic methods reflects advances in anatomical understanding, surgical technique, and technological innovation. This review outlines the historical and technical evolution of the supracerebellar approach, highlighting its versatility, effectiveness, and continued relevance in contemporary neurosurgery. Ongoing refinement through emerging technologies and improved operative adjuncts will be essential to further enhance surgical safety, precision, and patient outcomes in this complex neurosurgical region.
