Two-Year Outcomes of a Randomized Controlled Trial of Nonintervention Versus Oral Ibuprofen for Patent Ductus Arteriosus in Premature Infants
Authors: Se In Sung, Misun Yang, So Yoon Ahn, Yun Sil Chang, Won Soon Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOptimal management of patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) in premature infants remains debated, and the long-term impact of different strategies has not been well studied. We previously conducted a randomized clinical trial comparing nonintervention (NI) and oral ibuprofen (IBU) for hemodynamically significant PDA in preterm infants, which showed no significant difference in bronchopulmonary dysplasia or death during hospitalization. In this study, we aimed to present a prespecified longitudinal follow-up investigation to assess the outcomes of persistently open PDA at discharge, as well as growth and neurodevelopmental outcomes at 2 years’ corrected age (CA), based on PDA management strategy.MethodsSurviving infants from the original trial were evaluated at 2 years’ CA. Examiners blinded to the management group assessed spontaneous ductal closure after hospital discharge, growth parameters, and neurodevelopmental outcomes.ResultsAmong 130 survivors, open PDA at discharge was present in 7/66 (11%) NI and 2/64 (3%) IBU infants; spontaneous closure occurred in 3/7 NI infants but 0/2 IBU infants. Device closure occurred in 6% (4/66) of the NI group and 3% (2/64) of the IBU group (P = 0.680) by 2 years’ CA. Growth parameters—height, weight, and head circumference—did not differ significantly between groups. Neurodevelopmental impairment—defined as cerebral palsy, hearing or visual loss, or a Bayley Scales of Infant Development II mental or psychomotor development index below 70—was present in 15% (10/66) of the NI group and 22% (14/64) of the IBU group (P = 0.371).ConclusionThis follow-up study demonstrated that NI for hemodynamically significant PDA in preterm infants resulted in growth and neurodevelopmental outcomes at 2 years’ CA similar to those seen with IBU treatment. The high rate of spontaneous PDA closure and lack of significant differences in long-term morbidities support the safety of NI and raise questions about the benefit of routine pharmacologic closure.Keywords: Patent Ductus Arteriosus; Infant, Premature; Spontaneous Closure; Ibuprofen; Neurodevelopmental Disorders; Growth

INTRODUCTION

Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) in preterm infants is associated with increased mortality and respiratory morbidities, including bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD). Despite the widespread use of cyclooxygenase (COX) inhibitors such as ibuprofen (IBU) to promote ductal closure, multiple randomized trials have failed to demonstrate improved clinical outcomes with routine pharmacologic treatment.1,2,3 Our previous randomized clinical trial compared nonintervention (NI) and oral IBU for hemodynamically significant PDA and found no significant differences in short-term outcomes, including BPD or death during the initial hospitalization.3 However, critical gaps remain in understanding the long-term neurodevelopmental consequences of different PDA management strategies, as many existing studies are outdated, nonrandomized, or lack prospective follow-up protocols.4,5 This uncertainty underscores the need for evidence to determine whether pharmacologic closure offers developmental advantages or introduces risks, particularly in extremely preterm infants.

In this study, we aimed to conduct a prespecified longitudinal follow-up study to evaluate the long-term outcome of hemodynamically significant PDA, including growth and neurodevelopmental at 2 years’ corrected age (CA). This investigation addresses the limitations of prior studies through rigorous randomization in our original trial, standardized management in a single-center unit, and extended follow-up, aiming to clarify whether early IBU treatment improves long-term outcomes or if a noninterventional approach offers a safer alternative for vulnerable preterm infants.

METHODS

Study design and participants

This longitudinal follow-up study was based on our previously published randomized clinical trial that compared NI with oral IBU for the management of hemodynamically significant PDA in preterm infants.3 The original double-blind, placebo-controlled trial enrolled 146 infants born at 23–30 weeks’ gestational age with hemodynamically significant PDA (ductal size > 1.5 mm with respiratory support requirement) diagnosed between postnatal days 6–14. Infants were stratified by gestational age and randomly assigned 1:1 to receive either oral IBU (10 mg/kg initial dose, followed by 5 mg/kg at 24 and 48 hours) or placebo. The primary outcome was BPD or death, which showed noninferiority of NI compared to IBU (44% vs. 50%, P = 0.509). Among the 142 infants who completed the intervention, 12 died during hospitalization (6 in each group), leaving 130 survivors (66 in NI group, 64 in IBU group) available for this follow-up study. The primary outcome of this follow-up study, prespecified in the original protocol, was neurodevelopmental impairment at 2 years’ CA. Baseline pre- and post-natal characteristics were compared between NI and IBU. Other outcomes were prespecified as secondary outcomes.

Follow-up evaluations

Spontaneous PDA closure was confirmed by pediatric cardiologists during newborn intensive care unit (NICU) hospitalization and at outpatient follow-up visits up to 2 years. Device closure decisions were made by individual pediatric cardiologists based on their clinical judgment during outpatient follow-up, without a standardized protocol. The timing and indications for device closure were not systematically documented in this follow-up study. Growth parameters included weight, length/height, and head circumference, assessed with z-scores. Cerebral palsy (CP) was diagnosed by pediatric neurologists and rehabilitation physicians, with severity classified using the Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS): mild (GMFCS levels 1–2) and moderate to severe (GMFCS levels 3–5). Hearing loss was defined as bilateral loss requiring cochlear implantation, and visual loss as bilateral blindness. Cognitive and motor development were assessed using the Bayley Scales of Infant Development II (BSID-II). Cognitive impairment was defined as Mental Development Index (MDI) score below 70, and motor impairment as Psychomotor Development Index (PDI) score below 70. Neurodevelopmental impairment was defined as the presence of any of the following: CP, hearing or visual loss, cognitive impairment, or motor impairment. All examiners were blinded to the original treatment assignments.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared using independent-samples t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests and reported as means and standard deviations (SDs). Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test and reported as numbers and percentages. Growth trajectories were analyzed using z-scores for weight, height, and head circumference at three time points: NICU discharge, 12 months’ CA, and 24 months’ CA. Between-group comparisons at each time point were performed using independent t-tests. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d for continuous variables and Cohen’s h for proportions. Post-hoc power analysis was performed for the primary outcome (neurodevelopmental impairment at 2 years) using G*Power 3.1, with α = 0.05 (two-tailed). Statistical analyses were performed using R, version 4.4.3 (R Project for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Two-tailed P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

Written informed consent was obtained from parents or guardians, and the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Samsung Medical Center approved the study protocol (IRB No. SMC 2013-07-129).

RESULTS

Of the 142 infants in the original trial, 12 (six in the NI group and six in the IBU group) died during NICU hospitalization. The remaining 130 infants (66 in the NI group and 64 in the IBU group) were included in this follow-up study and followed-up up to 2 years’ CA (Fig. 1). There were no differences in baseline characteristics between NI and IBU (Table 1). The follow-up rate based on BSID completion was 88% in the NI group and 94% in the IBU group. Follow-up based on growth measurements at 12 and 24 months was 100% for all 130 surviving infants.

The IBU group showed higher rates of CP (6% vs. 2%, P = 0.200), hearing loss requiring cochlear implantation (5% vs. 0%, P = 0.117), and motor impairment (19% vs. 8%, P = 0.071) compared to the NI group. The effect size for the primary outcome of neurodevelopmental impairment was small (Cohen’s h = 0.18; 95% confidence interval, −0.16 to 0.52). Post-hoc power analysis revealed that with our sample size (n = 130), we had 22% power to detect this observed difference as statistically significant. To achieve 80% power for detecting the observed difference (15% vs. 22%), a sample size of approximately 580 infants would be required.

DISCUSSION

In this follow-up to our randomized trial, we found that NI resulted in long-term outcomes similar to those of IBU treatment. Our original trial demonstrated no significant short-term benefits of IBU over NI for BPD or death,3 and the current findings extend those observations into early childhood. Although the differences in neurodevelopmental outcomes did not reach statistical significance, the consistent pattern of numerically worse outcomes in the IBU group across multiple domains raises important clinical considerations. The trend toward higher rates of motor impairment, CP, and hearing loss in infants exposed to early IBU treatment warrants careful interpretation, particularly given the biological plausibility of COX inhibitor effects on neurodevelopment. These findings suggest that long-term growth and neurodevelopmental outcomes with a NI approach are not inferior to those with IBU treatment. Moreover, increased tendency of adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes observed in the IBU group raises important questions about potential subtle effects of early COX inhibitor exposure on neurodevelopment. Further larger-scale clinical trials with adequate statistical power to detect modest but clinically meaningful differences are needed to clarify this issue.

Previous studies examining long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes following different PDA management strategies have shown inconsistent results. Kabra et al.6 identified an association between surgical PDA closure and increased neurosensory impairment in infants with extremely low birth weight, although this finding is difficult to extrapolate to pharmacological management. Bourgoin et al.7 found no significant differences in neurodevelopmental outcomes at 2 years among medical treatment, surgical ligation, and no treatment groups in a retrospective cohort, although their non-randomized design introduced potential selection bias. More concerning, Termerova et al.8 reported worse outcomes with early targeted IBU treatment compared with expectant management in extremely premature infants. A recent observation by Seo et al.9 suggested the possible superiority of NI over active pharmacologic and surgical treatment based on their single-center experience. However, none of these studies used the rigorous methodology of a prospective randomized controlled trial. Our findings provide valuable data from a randomized design, suggesting that NI is not associated with worse neurodevelopmental outcomes at 2 years compared with pharmacologic treatment.

Growth outcomes following different PDA management strategies have not been directly compared in previous studies.10,11,12 Our findings demonstrate that at 2 years’ CA, there were no significant differences in weight, height, or head circumference z-scores between groups. Fluid restriction in conservative management has raised theoretical concerns about nutritional compromise10; however, these results suggest that, with appropriate nutritional support, normal growth trajectories can be maintained without pharmacologic PDA closure.

A notable finding of our study was the high rate of spontaneous PDA closure in the NI group. During NICU hospitalization, 89% of infants in the NI group achieved spontaneous closure, increasing to 94% by 2 years of age. This high rate challenges the assumption that most hemodynamically significant PDAs require pharmacologic treatment. Our findings align with those of other reports showing spontaneous closure, particularly beyond the neonatal period.12,13,14 The minimal difference in final closure rates between groups (94% in NI vs. 97% in the IBU group) suggests that, for most infants, a watchful waiting approach allows physiologic closure while avoiding potential medication-related risks.

Multiple randomized trials, including our original study, have consistently shown that treating PDA early with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs does not significantly improve short-term outcomes such as BPD, severe intraventricular hemorrhage, necrotizing enterocolitis, or neonatal survival.1,2,3,15 Two recent meta-analyses have further emphasized that early pharmacologic PDA closure offers no clear benefit and may pose potential risks.16,17 While IBU’s COX inhibition effectively promotes ductal closure,18 it may theoretically interfere with neurotrophic and angiogenic processes essential for brain development.19,20,21 Our findings provide important reassurance amid the growing shift toward conservative management: NI yielded comparable neurodevelopmental outcomes at 2 years, with remarkably high rates of spontaneous closure (94%). The absence of significant differences in cognitive and motor development supports the safety of allowing physiologic closure in most infants with hemodynamically significant PDA. Notably, the numerically higher rates of individual and overall neurodevelopmental impairment in the IBU group—though not statistically significant—raise concerns about potential subtle neurodevelopmental effects of early COX inhibition that merit further investigation in larger studies.8,22

This study has some limitations. First, the sample size was powered for the original primary outcome—BPD or death by 36 weeks’ gestation—not for detecting differences in long-term outcomes. The relatively small effect sizes observed (Cohen’s h = 0.18 for neurodevelopmental impairment) combined with limited statistical power (22%) indicate a substantial risk of Type II error. While we cannot exclude clinically meaningful differences between groups, our study was underpowered to detect the modest effect sizes observed. This limitation is particularly relevant given the 7% absolute difference in neurodevelopmental impairment rates, which could be clinically significant despite not reaching statistical significance. Second, the NI strategy was implemented at a single center with experienced personnel, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. The lack of a standardized protocol for device closure and the absence of systematic documentation of closure indications represent another limitation of this study. Despite these limitations, a key strength of this study is its provision of long-term outcome data from a prospective, placebo-controlled, double-blind randomized clinical trial—to our knowledge, the first to report such findings.

In conclusion, this follow-up study of our randomized trial found no long-term disadvantage to an NI approach. Infants who did not receive early IBU therapy had comparable growth and neurodevelopmental outcomes at 2 years of CA with those who were treated. Most infants in the NI group achieved spontaneous ductal closure by 2 years. These findings, combined with the original trial results, support noninterventional management as a preferred approach for hemodynamically significant PDA in preterm infants.
