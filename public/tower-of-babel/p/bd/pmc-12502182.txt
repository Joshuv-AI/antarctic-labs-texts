Investigation of the effect of activator-assisted talocrural joint manipulation on acute sports performance parameters in elite male volleyball players
Authors: Bünyamin Haksever, Kimiya Hajighorbani, Cagla Hediye Cakir, Mehmet Gorkem Isguzar, Bulent Elbasan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe research examined how activator-assisted talocrural joint manipulation affects joint range of motion and balance and jumping performance and dynamic weight transfer parameters in elite male volleyball players immediately after treatment.MethodThe study included 60 elite male volleyball players aged 18–25 years with at least 3 years of professional volleyball experience. Participants were randomly assigned to an experimental (n = 30) and control (n = 30) group. The experimental group received talocrural joint manipulation using an activator device, while the control group received a sham technique. Ankle joint range of motion, Y-balance test, vertical and horizontal jump tests, and pedobarographic weight transfer measurements were performed before and after the intervention.ResultsAfter manipulation, an increase of 4.7% (p < 0.001) in plantar flexion and 3.7% (p = 0.041) in dorsiflexion was observed in the right foot of the study group. In the Y balance test, an improvement of 7% (p < 0.001) in the right-side posteromedial reach distance and 5% (p < 0.001) in the posterolateral reach distance was observed. An increase of 2.3% (p < 0.001) in vertical jump height and 1.3% (p < 0.001) in horizontal jump distance was recorded. A significant increase of 10% (p < 0.001) was observed in the dynamic speed value on the left side. In the control group, changes in all parameters remained below 1%.ConclusionThe activator-assisted talocrural joint manipulation technique delivers quick results for elite male volleyball players by enhancing joint range of motion and balance and jumping performance. The technique should be used in pre-competition preparation for sports which need explosive power and balance.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT07072260, July 9, 2025. Retrospectively registered.Keywords: Athletic performance, Joint mobilization techniques, Proprioception, Volleyball

Introduction

Athletes frequently experience ankle injuries while participating in sports which subsequently impacts their athletic performance. The intense jumping movements in volleyball sports create elevated stress on the ankle joint which increases the risk of chronic ankle instability and injury [1].

The ankle requires proper mobility to achieve optimal athletic performance. Individuals with chronic ankle instability (CAI) have reduced dorsiflexion range of motion, impaired balance control, and reduced function as reported by patients [2]. In sports requiring rapid directional changes, such as badminton, elite players have reported 52.2 speed-dependent movements and 46.1 jumping movements per match, and ankle instability can lead to functional performance losses [3]. The performance parameters jump height and dynamic balance directly relate to ankle joint function particularly in volleyball players [4].

Talocrural joint manipulation is one of the manual therapy techniques used in the treatment of CAI. The goal of joint mobilisation and mobilisation techniques is to enhance dorsiflexion range of motion, balance and patient-reported function [2]. These techniques are a treatment method that improves joint range of motion and potentially enhances athletic performance by affecting mechanoreceptors and proprioceptive inputs [5].

Mechanoreceptor and proprioceptor activation is associated with post-activation performance enhancement (PAPE). PAPE functions by enhancing motor unit recruitment and improving intramuscular coordination and elevating muscle temperature. Research indicates that PAPE enhances jump height by 16.3% among professional volleyball players [6]. In recent years, instrument-assisted soft tissue mobilisation (IASTM) techniques have been used in elite athletes to reduce pain, increase range of motion, and improve strength by mobilising soft tissues with specialised tools [7].

Activator-assisted manipulation provides standardised mechanical applications. The research on IASTM effectiveness shows moderate results especially for injuries located in the shoulder area and lower extremities as well as the thoracic region [7]. Activator-assisted manipulation is a mechanically assisted, low-force manual therapy technique that uses a handheld spring-loaded instrument to deliver a quick, targeted thrust to a specific joint. While IASTM primarily exerts effects on the fascial system, activator-assisted manipulation may additionally stimulate mechanoreceptors through directed joint stimulation. This is supported by findings that manipulative interventions, such as those using mechanically assisted instruments, can have clinical effectiveness comparable to manual therapy [8], and that targeted joint manipulation can acutely improve proprioceptive function in adjacent segments [9]. Proprioception is fundamentally mediated by mechanoreceptors that integrate afferent input and support motor control, as detailed in recent reviews of proprioceptive mechanisms [10].

The combination of ankle joint mobilization techniques with plyometric exercises produces an enhanced effect on ROM improvement and joint stability [3]. Although numerous studies investigate joint mobilization and manipulation on range of motion and functional performance the research on activator-assisted talocrural joint manipulation effects on sports performance parameters in elite male volleyball players remains scarce [2].

Recent evidence indicates that ankle joint mobilization techniques can acutely and meaningfully improve athletic performance parameters. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis by ElMeligie et al. found that Mulligan mobilization with movement significantly reduced pain, enhanced range of motion (SMD ≈ 1.65), and improved balance measures such as the Y-Balance Test in individuals with ankle sprains [11]. Similarly, a meta-analysis by Weerasekara et al. reported immediate benefits on posteromedial dynamic balance following joint mobilization compared to comparators [12]. Moreover, a narrative review by Almansoof et al. underscores the essential role of ankle dorsiflexion in sport-specific skills and injury risk, linking reduced dorsiflexion with altered kinetics, impaired neuromuscular activation, and potential decrements in performance [13].

Most existing research investigates various sports with restricted participant numbers [3]. Research on acute talocrural joint manipulation effects regarding the Y-balance test and vertical and horizontal jump performance in elite volleyball players remains absent from current scientific literature [6]. Research needs to examine how this application influences PAPE mechanisms specifically in elite athletes [14]. The main research question of this study evaluates how activator-assisted talocrural joint manipulation in elite male volleyball players impacts both ankle range of motion and balance performance and jump height and dynamic weight transfer metrics shortly after the treatment. The main goal of this research focused on assessing how this manipulation technique affects joint function by altering mechanoreceptor and proprioceptive responses to enhance athletic performance in volleyball. The investigated manipulation effects on athletic performance and field applicability through Y balance test measurements of vertical and horizontal jump performance and weight transfer parameters. This study addresses a research gap in the literature by investigating acute effects of this technique on elite athletes which will benefit both clinical practice and training approaches by enhancing athlete performance while reducing injury risks.

Method

Study population and sample

This study adheres to the CONSORT guidelines. Study was a single-centre, two-arm parallel-group, superiority randomised controlled trial with a 1:1 allocation ratio comparing activator-assisted talocrural joint manipulation with a sham intervention in elite male volleyball players. The study was carried out at the clinic of Op. Dr. Ozgur Bulbul in Ankara, Türkiye, between 10 May 2025 and 30 May 2025. The sample size was calculated using G*Power version 3.1.9.7, assuming an effect size of Cohen’s d = 0.5, based on prior research [15], with 80% power and a 0.05 α error margin. The study included elite male volleyball players who voluntarily agreed to participate in the study and who routinely visited the clinic. Inclusion criteria were defined as having at least 3 years of professional volleyball training experience, being an active member of a team and club, and being between 15 and 25 years of age. Exclusion criteria were defined as not actively participating in training or matches and having a history of acute musculoskeletal pathology or surgery involving the lower extremities. Initially, 66 volunteers aged 15 to 25 years were screened. Of these, 60 met al.l inclusion criteria and were subsequently randomized into two equal groups (n = 30 each). The final study sample included participants aged 18 to 25 years. Details of this process are visually presented in the diagram (Fig. 1). In the study, two groups were formed: an activator-assisted manipulation application group and a sham technique application control group. Each group consisted of 30 male volleyball players. No important changes to the trial design, outcomes, or analyses were made after the trial commenced.

Study procedures

All evaluations were conducted by an experienced physiotherapist in two stages: before the application (T0) and after the application (T1). In the first stage, the participants’ demographic information (age, height, weight, dominant side) and sports history (age at which they started professional sports, weekly training hours, position played) were recorded. Ankle joint range of motion (plantar and dorsiflexion) was measured using a goniometer and recorded in degrees. Goniometric measurements were taken with the patient lying supine and the ankle in a 90° position. The reliability of the outcome measures used in this study is supported by previously published data. The YBT has demonstrated high intra- and inter-rater reliability in athletic populations, with ICC values reported between 0.85 and 0.91 and 0.99–1.00, respectively [16]. Similarly, goniometric assessment of ankle range of motion is widely accepted as reliable when standardized protocols are followed, with inter-rater ICCs generally exceeding 0.90. Although intra- or inter-rater reliability was not assessed within our sample, the use of a single experienced evaluator throughout the study aimed to minimize measurement variability. The standard Y Balance test kit was used for the Y Balance test, and measurements were recorded in centimetres. In the test, the extension distances in three directions—anterior, posteromedial, and posterolateral—were evaluated [17]. In addition, both vertical and horizontal jump tests are widely used field-based measures of lower limb power, with reported intra- and inter-rater reliability values typically above 0.90 in athletic populations [18]. These measures were included to provide a multifaceted evaluation of performance changes following intervention. For the vertical jump test, the difference between the height the athletes could reach while standing and the height they could reach while jumping was measured in centimetres. The horizontal jump distance was determined in centimetres as the distance between the starting line and the furthest mark left by the athlete’s heel.

Static and dynamic weight transfers were evaluated using the Pedowell 3-dimensional pedobarometric (Medical Products Supplies Companies, Italy) analysis system. The following parameters were evaluated in the pedobarometric analysis: Static load ratio (distribution of weight between the right and left feet, %), Dynamic Contact Area Ratio - Anterior-Posterior (CAR-AP, ratio of the contact area of the front and back parts of the foot, %), Dynamic Contact Area Ratio - Rotation Pattern (CAR-RP, ratio of the medial-lateral contact area of the foot, %) and Dynamic Speed (the speed of foot-ground contact during walking, mm/s) [19, 20]. The system has high test-retest reliability (ICC >0.85) and is widely used in clinical studies for pressure distribution and weight transfer measurements.

Intervention protocol

Participants were randomly assigned to the study and control groups using a block randomisation method. The randomisation process was conducted independently. The researcher who generated the randomization sequence was not involved in the recruitment or data collection processes. Athletes in the study group (n = 30) underwent manipulation using the Activator Adjustment Instrument (AAI) device applied to the talocrural joint. The manipulation was performed by applying a high-speed, low-amplitude force to the anterior and posterior regions of the talocrural joint while the ankle was in a neutral position. Each athlete received a total of six impulses (3 anterior, 3 posterior). This method is used to stimulate mechanoreceptors, increase proprioceptive input, and optimise joint function [21]. The application pressure was standardised to ‘2’ on the AAI device. Participants were asked to report any pain or tenderness during each impulse. If pain intensity reached ≥ 4/10 on a VAS or clinically appreciable focal tenderness occurred, the manipulation was immediately discontinued, and the event was recorded as an adverse event. The control group (n = 30) received a sham technique consisting of 10 repetitions of stroking soft tissue massage around the talocrural joint. The application time was approximately 5 min in both groups. All assessments were repeated immediately after the applications were completed (within 3–5 min on average). Participants were blinded to group allocation using a sham procedure. Due to the nature of the intervention, the therapist delivering the manipulation was not blinded. Outcome assessments and data analysis were performed by individuals blinded to group assignments.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the data was performed using the ‘Statistical Package for Social Sciences’ (SPSS) Version 28.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and conducted by a statistician blinded to the study hypotheses. The normality of the variables was examined both visually (histograms and probability plots) and analytically (Kolmogorov-Smirnov/Shapiro-Wilk tests). Continuous variables were expressed as mean and standard deviation [mean (SD)], while categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages. For intergroup comparisons, Mann-Whitney U test was used. For within-group comparisons (pre- and post-intervention), the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used. The percentage change was calculated using the formula [(T1-T0)/T0] × 100. To ensure consistency in the presentation of P values across all tables, significant P values were reported to three decimal places (e.g., p = 0.001 or p < 0.001), while non-significant P values were reported to two decimal places (e.g., p = 0.07). A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Effect sizes were calculated to complement p-values, with Cohen’s d used to quantify the magnitude of differences between the experimental and control groups, and η² values used to assess the effect sizes for within-group comparisons. There was no missing data in the study, and all participants completed the study. No additional, subgroup, or sensitivity analyses were performed.

Ethical considerations

The protocol followed the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committee of the Gazi University (reference: 2025 − 699).

Trial registration

The trial was registered retrospectively on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07072260, July 9, 2025) due to logistical constraints during the initial planning phase, including delays in finalizing the study protocol and obtaining institutional approval. While prospective registration is preferred, retrospective registration was performed promptly after protocol finalization to ensure transparency and compliance with reporting standards.

Results

When the demographic characteristics of male volleyball players in the study and control groups participating in the research were examined, no statistically significant difference was found between the groups in terms of age averages, height, weight, dominant side, age of starting the sport, weekly training hours, and positions played. This proves that the groups showed a homogeneous distribution and were comparable. The average age in the study group was 18.97 ± 0.96 years, while in the control group it was 19.03 ± 1.10 years. The average heights were 192.93 ± 6.91 cm and 193.40 ± 7.20 cm, respectively, while the average weights were 86.05 ± 7.61 kg and 85.55 ± 8.10 kg, respectively. It was determined that the majority of athletes in both groups were right-handed. In addition, weekly training hours were similar, with 12.79 ± 2.41 h in the experimental group and 12.85 ± 2.50 h in the control group (Table 1).

Discussion

The research examined how activator-assisted talocrural joint manipulation affects sports performance indicators in elite male volleyball players during acute periods. This research revealed that athletes who received manipulation showed substantial improvements in joint range of motion and balance and jump height and dynamic weight transfer parameters. There were significant increases in ankle plantar flexion and dorsiflexion angles, Y-balance test performance, and vertical jump height compared to the control group. These results suggest that activator-assisted manipulation enhances movement capacity by influencing mechanoreceptors and the proprioceptive system.

In this study, significant improvements in joint range of motion were observed after activator-assisted talocrural joint manipulation. A 4.7% increase in plantar flexion and a 3.7% increase in dorsiflexion in the right foot were noteworthy. These findings are consistent with the results of similar studies in the literature. In a study conducted by Abassi and Whiteley, manipulation techniques were also found to provide significant improvements in ankle dorsiflexion angle [22]. However, the 3.7% increase in dorsiflexion in this study is close to the 4.7% increase reported by Abassi and Whiteley in the uninjured leg after exercise and manipulation. Park and You reported that the homogeneous instrument-assisted mobilisation (HIM) technique resulted in an 11.3% increase in active dorsiflexion angle [23]. This finding is higher than the 3.7% increase observed in the current study but reflects the variability in the efficacy levels of different mobilisation techniques. On the other hand, Ikeda and colleagues achieved an 18% increase in dorsiflexion angle in their 6-week device-assisted soft tissue mobilisation study, but this was obtained as a result of a long-term intervention [24]. The focus on acute effects in this study may explain the relatively lower improvement rates. The fact that most athletes were right-handed may have contributed to the more pronounced effect of manipulation on the right side.

In this study, activator-assisted talocrural joint manipulation was found to provide significant improvements in Y Balance Test results. As 7% increase in posteromedial reach distance and a 5.1% increase in posterolateral reach distance on the right side were notable. These findings are consistent with the results of various studies in the literature. In a meta-analysis conducted by Kim and Moon, joint mobilisation was found to provide significant improvements in dynamic balance in individuals with chronic ankle instability (SMD = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.06–0.78) [25]. The 5.22% improvement in anterior reach distance observed in the Y Balance Test in our study supports the finding by Barrera-Domínguez and colleagues that basketball players had higher anterior reach values than handball players [26]. In a study by Wang and colleagues involving volleyball players, significant differences were found only in the anterior direction between dominant and non-dominant legs in the Y Balance Test [27]. However, the more pronounced improvements observed on the right side in this study may be related to the dominant side characteristics of the athletes. In Yin and colleagues’ study, significant improvements in dorsiflexion angle and walking parameters were recorded after joint mobilisation, which may explain the increased dynamic balance ability after manipulation [28].

In this study, a 2.3% increase in vertical jump height was observed after activator-assisted manipulation. This finding is consistent with the results of the study conducted by Ozols and Galeja [29]. Similarly, Panoutsakopoulos and colleagues reported a strong association (r ≥ 0.66, p < 0.05) between ankle joint range of motion and vertical jump performance [30]. This supports the notion that the increase in joint range of motion observed in this study following manipulation may explain the improvement in jump performance. Although this study focused on the spinal region, the 2.3% increase achieved in this study with activator-assisted manipulation applied to the talocrural joint suggests that manipulation techniques applied to different anatomical regions may have variable effects on jumping performance. Although the increase rate in this study was lower, it was still statistically significant and can be explained by similar mechanisms. On the other hand, Masel and Maciejczyk’s study on professional male volleyball players found that the post-activation performance enhancement protocol did not affect jump height [31]. This difference may stem from the use of different techniques and targeted structures (spinal manipulation versus talocrural joint). In a study by Kurak and colleagues involving football players, different warm-up protocols were found to have significant effects on jumping performance [32]. These results support the idea that acute changes in the musculoskeletal system induced by techniques such as manipulation can enhance jumping performance.

In this study, significant improvements were observed in dynamic weight transfer parameters following activator-assisted talocrural joint manipulation. In particular, increases of 10% in the left side dynamic speed value and 2.8% in the right side dynamic speed value is noteworthy. These findings are consistent with similar studies in the literature. In the study by Tolsada-Velasco and colleagues, it was reported that talus mobilisation provided significant increases in ankle dorsiflexion range of motion and that this improvement could be maintained long-term with an appropriate number of sessions [33]. The improvements in dynamic speed parameters in this study can be similarly associated with increased joint mobility. This is because as joint movement capacity increases, the push phase of walking becomes stronger, leading to an increase in speed parameters. Almansoof and colleagues noted in their review that any blockage or defect in the kinetic chain can lead to compensatory patterns and increased demand on distal segments [34]. It can be thought that manipulation improves kinetic chain function by alleviating restrictions in the talocrural joint, thereby increasing dynamic parameters. In Coelho and colleagues’ study, a significant increase in dorsiflexion ROM was observed after a single session of ankle mobilisation, and it was also found that the direction of mobilisation was effective on clinical outcomes [35]. The absence of significant changes in CAR parameters in this study can be explained by the different load parameters (intensity, duration, direction) affecting tissue adaptations in different ways, as noted by Logerstedt and colleagues [36]. The selective effect on joint mechanoreceptors may have improved dynamic velocity parameters by altering load transfer patterns. Despite improvements in several performance parameters, CAR values did not show significant changes post-intervention. This outcome can be explained by the specific nature of the intervention. Activator-assisted manipulation primarily targets joint structures and proprioceptive pathways, rather than plantar load distribution or foot pressure mechanics. As CAR is more closely associated with plantar loading patterns, the absence of significant alteration in this parameter may be considered an expected result. It suggests that while joint-based interventions can acutely enhance mobility and balance through neurosensory modulation, they may not directly influence variables related to plantar contact dynamics.

The presence of a sham control group in this study allowed for a clearer distinction between the specific physiological effects of activator-assisted manipulation. While both groups received hands-on treatment, the sham group primarily experienced superficial soft tissue stimulation. In contrast, the mechanical stimulus delivered by the activator device likely reached deeper structures, including the joint capsule and mechanoreceptors within the talocrural complex. This targeted stimulation may have elicited more robust proprioceptive feedback and neuromuscular activation, which could explain the significantly greater improvements observed in joint mobility, balance, and jump performance in the intervention group. This comparison underscores that the performance improvements observed in the activator group are likely driven by deeper neurophysiological mechanisms specifically activated by the device-assisted manipulation.

The findings of this study offer valuable practical implications for clinical and athletic settings. The observed acute performance improvements, particularly in range of motion and dynamic balance, may be attributed to enhanced firing of ankle joint mechanoreceptors. Based on these effects, activator-assisted talocrural joint manipulation may be considered as a preparatory intervention prior to training sessions or competitive events to optimize neuromuscular readiness and reduce injury risk. Furthermore, in the return-to-play phase following lower extremity injuries, this technique could potentially assist in restoring proprioception and joint mobility, thereby contributing to injury prevention and performance recovery strategies implemented by physiotherapists or athletic trainers.

This study has some limitations. The evaluation of acute effects only provides information about the short-term and acute outcomes of the intervention. The absence of intra- or inter-rater reliability assessment within our cohort is a limitation, and future studies should incorporate such measures to further validate outcome assessments. Further studies with a larger sample size and the use of more valid parameters, especially for performance parameters, are needed. The study benefits from its controlled experimental design and homogeneous participant group and complete evaluation of multiple performance parameters. Future research should investigate athletes from various sports and female athletes while studying the long-term consequences of the intervention. The investigation of activator-assisted manipulation dose-response relationships at different intensity and frequency levels would generate useful clinical practice information. Additionally, given that the intervention involved the use of a mechanical device (activator), it is possible that participant expectations may have contributed to a placebo effect. The visual and tactile stimulus of device-assisted manipulation may enhance perceived efficacy. Future studies may consider including a control group receiving a sham intervention using a similar device to better isolate the specific effects of the manipulation and minimize expectancy-related bias. Also, a methodological limitation of this study is the retrospective trial registration, which may impact transparency.

Conclusion

The research demonstrates that activator-assisted talocrural joint manipulation may work effectively to enhance joint range of motion, balance, jumping performance, and dynamic weight transfer parameters in elite male volleyball players. The technique appears promising for sports performance enhancement because it may benefit mechanoreceptors and proprioceptive system function in the ankle area during volleyball and similar sports that demand jumping and balance. Based on these findings, this technique may be considered as a complementary strategy in pre-competition preparation routines aimed at improving acute neuromuscular readiness. Sports professionals and physiotherapists might consider incorporating activator-assisted manipulation as a non-invasive, time-efficient intervention that can be implemented practically on the sidelines.
