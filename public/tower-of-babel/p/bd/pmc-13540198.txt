Advanced assay design for characterizing anti-drug antibody responses in clinical serum samples
Authors: Gregor P Lotz, Denise Sickert, Roland F Staack, Gregor Jordan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMonitoring anti-drug antibody (ADA) responses is critical for evaluating the safety and efficacy of protein therapeutics. While traditional three-tiered testing (screening, confirmation, titration) reliably identifies ADA incidence, onset and magnitude, modern, complex biologics necessitate adapting our assessment strategies. Consequently, advanced assay designs are required to capture the full biological and clinical impact of these immune responses. Structured to guide bioanalytical scientists through this paradigm shift, this review first explores the transition toward integrated functional assessments, detailing how “active” pharmacokinetic (PK) and pharmacodynamic (PD) assays, neutralizing antibody (nAb) testing, and domain-specific assays help align immunogenicity characterization with a drug’s mechanism of action (MoA). Next, we highlight Model-Informed Assay Development (MIAD) as a transformative tool for optimizing drug tolerance and estimating ADA-Reagent-Drug complex (ARC) formation. We then outline a risk-based, fit-for-purpose (FFP) framework for developing these advanced assays. Furthermore, we provide practical considerations for reporting advanced characterization data in regulatory filings. Finally, we conclude by differentiating benign ADA release from active inflammatory responses driven by the interaction of complex biologics with antigen-presenting cells, and explore future perspectives, specifically how Systems Immunogenicity and Artificial Intelligence (AI) will transition the field from retrospective monitoring to predictive immunogenicity profiling.KEYWORDS: Anti-drug antibody, immunogenicity risk assessment, model-informed assay development, active pharmacokinetics, neutralizing antibodies, clinical relevance of ADA response

Introduction

An ADA response may represent a significant hurdle for both the clinical development of protein therapeutics and overall patient safety. To mitigate these risks, global regulatory authorities have established rigorous frameworks to monitor these responses and evaluate risk across preclinical and clinical phases [1–5]. The evaluation typically centers on two critical dimensions: the probability of a response occurring and the potential severity of its impact on drug exposure, efficacy, and safety [6–8]. Because this initial risk assessment is pivotal, it dictates the sampling and testing strategies required to ensure the resulting data is robust enough to inform therapeutic outcomes [8]. Traditionally, the industry has met these expectations through a three-tiered testing strategy, screening, confirmation, and titration [1–9].

While this tiered approach is reliable for identifying ADA onset, incidence and categorizing response intensities, it nonetheless often lacks the depth needed to decode the true biological and clinical implications of those responses. Consequently, the traditional paradigm is undergoing a significant reevaluation in favor of more integrated, data-rich methodologies. One emerging alternative is a streamlined one-tier approach that leverages signal-to-noise (S/N) ADA data from screening assays to generate more nuanced datasets [10–16]. By integrating this S/N data with PK or PD markers, researchers can gain more comprehensive insights than the conventional “positive/negative” titration model allows [13,15,16]. To successfully implement the S/N approach, early assay development must ensure appropriate technical characteristics, such as a wide dynamic range, absence of hook effect, and proven specificity.

Whether using traditional titration approaches or S/N assessment, assessing ADA response magnitude provides essential data for understanding ADA consequences. There is an increasing shift toward risk-based, advanced assay designs that directly correlate ADA responses with clinical markers. Although Health Authority guidelines provide broad recommendations for these designs [1–5], implementation increasingly involves integrating supplemental assays, such as nAb/cross-reactivity assays, domain specificity, ADA-drug complex analysis, and isotype characterization that provide more granular immunogenicity data. Critically, aligning ADA, active PK, and proximal PD assays with the drug’s MoA enables holistic analysis and allows clinical teams to move beyond mere ADA detection especially for drugs with a higher risk triggering immunogenicity event (Figure 1). This integrative approach helps correlate clinical observations, such as altered clearance, loss of pharmacological activity, or immune-mediated safety events with specific immune response characteristics.

However, because these assays must remain “fit-for-purpose” and tailored to each drug’s unique biology, industry-wide standardization remains challenging. Crucially, implementing these advanced characterization strategies during early clinical trials (Phase 1/2) allows development teams to determine early on whether an ADA response is truly benign or if it actively impacts PK, efficacy, and patient safety. Identifying these specific clinical consequences early is essential, as it directly drives and refines the assay testing strategy for late-stage pivotal studies. By evaluating theoretical immunogenicity risks in early phases, sponsors can adapt their approach to focus Phase 3 strategies exclusively on confirmed clinical risks. To address the challenges of high-risk immunogenic drugs, this review proposes a conceptual strategy for bioanalytical scientists to transition from qualitative ADA testing to mechanistic approaches that assess clinical impact. Specifically, we focus on advanced, fit-for-purpose assay designs, integrating active PK/PD, nAb, cross-reactivity, domain-specificity, isotype characterization, and ADA-drug complexes to directly link humoral immunogenicity to the drug’s MoA.

Beyond incidence: integrated functional assessment of ADA responses in clinical development by advanced ADA characterization assays

MoA based active PK assay (s) are sensitive in vivo readouts for functional drug exposure

The evolution in characterization has shifted ADA analysis from a standalone metric into an integrated functional assessment linked to a drug’s MoA. Central to this approach is the use of “active” PK assays [17–19]. Unlike traditional qualitative or semi-quantitative ADA tests, active PK assays quantitatively measure the “free” drug fraction, the portion with functional binding sites (Figure 2). Because active PK assays inherently detect whether the drug’s target-binding domain remains available or has been compromised by an ADA, they effectively act as highly sensitive, in vivo readouts for both neutralizing activity and ADA-mediated accelerated clearance. By validating these assays prior to clinical study entry using appropriate anti-idiotypic ADA surrogates or stable, eukaryotic-expressed target-antigens, developers can establish a robust PK-ADA relationship, clearly correlating ADA emergence with reductions in exposure (e.g., Cmax and Ctrough) in clinical trials [19]. Establishing these correlations during early-phase trials provides the clinical team with actionable thresholds to guide dose selection and monitoring strategies for late-stage pivotal studies. For instance, the analytical sensitivity of the PK assay can serve as a definitive marker for the point at which ADAs effectively eliminate measurable systemic drug exposure through either neutralization, accelerated clearance, or a combination of both (Figure 2).

The gap between total and free PK represents ADA-drug complexes. Complete loss of free-drug exposure may serve as a decision threshold.

For example, a recent population PK model employing the Stochastic Approximation Expectation Maximization (SAEM) algorithm was also constructed to evaluate ADA impact on the T-cell engager cibisatamab free-drug exposure [20]. The model successfully distinguished ADA-driven exposure loss, demonstrating that ADAs reduced drug levels in 75% of patients, independent of demographics, dosing, or concurrent co-administered drugs [19]. The overall use of such modeling frameworks by integrating active PK and PD data into ADA response characteristics allows developers to simulate optimized dosing regimens. By adjusting dose magnitude, frequency, and duration, this approach can help overcome ADA-mediated exposure loss while ensuring the maintenance of the desired target PD effect [20,21]. Additionally, a Quantitative Systems Pharmacology (QSP) model successfully predicted whether ADA responses would significantly impact drug exposure in 10 of 13 evaluated cases [22]. The molar ratio of ADA to drug concentration emerged as a key predictive metric for assessing clinically relevant immunogenicity risk. To determine this ratio (ADAs cannot be quantified) the authors used an in-silico approach (IG simulator). Although this model has its limitation, this metric can guide mitigation strategies, determining whether dose escalation or dosing interval reduction can effectively sustain therapeutic efficacy despite ADA-mediated clearance [22]. Crucially, such models must distinguish between total and free drug exposure to ensure accurate predictions.

However, active drug assays have limitations. They alone cannot differentiate whether reduced drug levels are due to accelerated clearance or sequestration into inactive ADA-drug complexes [23–27]. Ideally, the parallel integration of total PK assays addresses this limitation more directly, with the difference (delta) reflecting circulating immune complexes (Figure 2). When a reliable total PK assay can be established, this allows for differentiation between clearing ADAs (both total and active drug levels decline) and neutralizing or non-clearing ADAs (total drug remains high, but active drug is suppressed). Practically, however, deriving total PK data often requires chemically dissociating immune complexes, which can introduce analytical variability [24,25]. In scenarios where this dissociation renders the total PK “delta” too noisy or unreliable, developers may instead leverage the inherently more robust active PK assay data. By generating a robust clinical dataset covering a broad exposure range, advanced population PK modeling can be used to mathematically deconvolute these clearance mechanisms. Interestingly, a phase 1 study of the T-cell engager drug Tyrp1-TCB evaluated both complex formation size and clearance rate [26]. The findings demonstrated that small ADA-drug complexes, such as dimers, may not significantly accelerate clearance, whereas higher-order complexes do. This indicates that the clearance effects are strongly influenced by the size of the ADA-drug complex. Moreover, drug-tolerant ADA assays (total ADA) cannot capture information on circulating neutralizing complexes either, limiting their utility. Therefore, in few cases measuring immune complexes might be critical for linking ADA responses to adverse events, such as Type III hypersensitivity, even if causality remains difficult to prove. Additionally, standalone total PK analysis is often not recommended for biologics with a high immunogenicity risk, as it may overestimate relevant drug exposure in the presence of ADA and the probability of weak correlation with clinical impact is higher [27].

While active PK assays are often practically more robust than complex total PK assays, their development requires careful attention to specific analytical nuances not fully covered by conventional guidelines. While current regulatory guidance addresses standard total drug quantification, it lacks specific frameworks for accurately measuring free drug exposure [28]. Crucially, an active PK assay must be demonstrably responsive to the presence of soluble targets or ADAs, a key performance parameter that directly correlates with free-drug exposure [17–19,24,25]. Because current regulatory guidelines primarily focus on mitigating general matrix interference, they do not comprehensively address the dynamic biological binding of soluble targets or ADAs. This distinction necessitates rigorous characterization of capture reagents (e.g., target-antigen proteins or anti-idiotypic antibodies) and selected assay conditions to prevent the premature dissociation of ADA-drug or drug-soluble target complexes during sample incubation [17–19,24,25] which could otherwise artificially inflate the measured free drug levels. Furthermore, from a regulatory standpoint, utilizing active PK as a surrogate for functional neutralization might require early parallel evaluation against cell-based assays to establish strong concordance before health authorities will permit reduced reliance on standalone nAb monitoring.

Model-informed ADA assay development

To precisely correlate drug exposure with clinical impact, an accurate analysis of the ADA response is essential. Traditional screening often falls short due to drug interference and the limitations of surrogate positive controls which often fail to mimic the diverse affinity and polyclonality of patient-derived ADA responses. To address this, a MIAD approach uses simulations to optimize reagent and analyte conditions [29]. By simulating various ADA-versus-assay reagent concentrations and affinities, researchers can identify specific assay conditions, such as ideal dilution factors, and reagent concentration load that ensure high drug tolerance and broad ADA detection independent of only one defined artificial ADA positive control [29]. For instance, while suboptimal reagent levels often miss low-affinity ADAs, MIAD identifies higher concentrations that “force” these weaker bonds to form signal-generating ADA-Assay Reagent Complexes (ARC) [27,29,30]. Recent evaluations highlight that current assay´s QC approaches using reagent-surrogate positive control complexes often fail to capture the variability of ADA-drug complex formation observed in patients [27]. Incorporating an understanding of ARC, including affinity considerations, provides crucial insights into what the assay measures, an aspect largely overlooked. Ultimately, MIAD and ARC represent a transformative shift from conventional QC-based ADA assay development, enabling more accurate estimation of true ADA responses and their clinically relevant impact.

Risk-based and integrated approaches to nAb testing

In these lines, the development of nAb assays for clinical trials has shifted from standalone testing toward a risk-based, integrated approach. Reflecting this paradigm, Partridge et al. advocate for a stratified framework: low-risk therapeutics may forego dedicated nAb testing in favor of integrated PK/PD and ADA analysis, while high-risk agents, such as biologics mimicking endogenous proteins, require early, standalone assessments [31] (Figure 3). This shift is largely driven by the fact that standalone nAb assays are often limited by sensitivity and drug-tolerance issues, which can diminish their clinical relevance. Specifically, there is a well-known analytical gap in the field: cell-based functional nAb assays are generally much less sensitive than standard ADA binding assays. Consequently, these in vitro nAb assays often only detect neutralizing activity when the ADA response is strong (high titer), potentially missing clinically relevant neutralization at lower titers [31]. To overcome this limitation, developers increasingly rely on “active” PK and proximal PD markers, which act as highly sensitive, in vivo indicators to accurately assess the true biological impact of the immune response. By incorporating these markers during early clinical development, teams can determine if a standalone nAb assay is truly necessary to monitor patient safety in late-stage pivotal trials.

Risk-based decision map for integrated approach testing and early nAb assay.

A successful execution of this integrative approach is exemplified by the emicizumab assessment [32], where ADA responses using a drug-tolerant ADA assay were correlated with active PK concentrations to assess their impact on drug exposure. These PK findings were corroborated by proximal PD markers (such as FVIII-like activity, thrombin generation, and activated partial thromboplastin time) to confirm whether a true therapeutic loss of effect occurred. Interestingly, ADAs with neutralizing capacity using an orthogonal in-vitro nAb analysis did not impact the efficacy of emicizumab if no effect on free-drug exposure was demonstrated. In contrast, ADAs leading to decreased emicizumab exposure could decrease efficacy consistently with the drug´s established exposure-response relationship [32]. This highlights that an integrative approach relying on in vivo active PK and PD markers provides a more accurate and comprehensive understanding of how ADAs impact clinical efficacy than isolated in vitro nAb assays alone.

When standalone nAb assay development is warranted based on a drug’s mechanism of action and high-risk categorization, the 2023 white paper provides a comprehensive harmonization framework for validation testing and data reporting [33]. However, translating this framework into practice remains challenging, particularly when attempting to achieve appropriate drug tolerance in nAb and ADA assays. Contemporary immunogenicity testing addresses this challenge by employing specialized pretreatment protocols that chemically disrupt circulating drug-antibody complexes, a step that is especially critical in direct competitive ligand-binding (CLB) assays to separate target-blocking nAbs from high concentrations of residual circulating drug and prevent analytical artifacts [34]. To achieve this isolation, researchers utilize a diverse technical toolkit of acid-dissociation and target-affinity extraction strategies [34–40].

Specifically, the field has seen significant progress in critically differentiating drug-based nAb extraction from target-based drug depletion techniques. Drug-based nAb extraction methods aim to physically pull the ADA out of the sample matrix using the therapeutic drug itself as the capture reagent. For standard purification, Affinity Capture Elution (ACE) isolates ADAs on drug-coated microplates prior to acid elution [37], while Solid-Phase Extraction with Acid Dissociation (SPEAD) leverages biotinylated drugs bound to streptavidin for high-affinity capture [25,39]. To maximize extraction efficiency and sensitivity, Biotin-Drug Extraction and Acid Dissociation (BEAD) refine this approach by pairing magnetic streptavidin beads with double-acid-dissociation loops [36]. For severely drug-saturated matrices, Precipitation and Acid Dissociation (PandA) offers an alternative by utilizing excess drug to force all remaining ADAs into complexes that are precipitated with polyethylene glycol (PEG) before acid release [40]. While highly effective at improving drug tolerance, a critical limitation of these traditional drug-based extraction methods is their reliance on harsh acid dissociation [25]. This shifting of pH inherently risks introducing artifacts or permanently denaturing low-affinity or structurally fragile ADAs, which can skew the clinical immunogenicity profile [41,42].

Conversely, target-based drug depletion offers a more functional alternative across various automated immunoassay platforms [38,43–45].

Instead of capturing the ADA, this technique uses an immobilized target protein to pull free drug and non-neutralizing ADA-drug complexes out of the sample. Because true nAbs block the drug from binding the target, nAb-drug complexes remain safely in the depleted supernatant, effectively enriching the sample for neutralizing antibodies.

The challenges of this strategy were highlighted during the development of an assay for an anti-EGFR therapeutic monoclonal antibody, where researchers successfully integrated target-based drug depletion using biotinylated EGFR reagents with subsequent drug-based nAb extraction to overcome extreme drug and target interference [44].

While this dual approach elegantly isolates nAbs without exposing them to multiple rigorous extraction cycles, its general implementation feasibility remains challenged by the need for highly stable target reagents and the risk of inadvertently depleting weak or partial neutralizing antibodies that still permit some degree of target binding [44].

Although recent optimizations in acid chemistry and pH adjustment have further maximized nAb recovery across multi-specific biotherapeutics [46], to further mitigate the denaturation limitations of acid-based methods, gentler nonacid alternatives like the high ionic strength dissociation assay (HISDA) are increasingly utilized to dissociate immune complexes using magnesium chloride, thereby preventing the protein denaturation associated with harsh chemical exposure [47]. Yet, despite these substantial improvements in chemical extraction, achieving complete therapeutic tolerance remains technically elusive for very high-dose biologics. Consequently, rigid analytical cutoffs may lack clinical relevance under extreme dosing regimens, as demonstrated by a recent study showing that ADA sensitivity depends heavily on positive control antibody selection [48]. Ultimately, rather than applying arbitrary, static sensitivity thresholds, assessing immunogenicity based on clinically relevant drug-to-antibody ratios, aligned with early health authority feedback enables a more accurate, patient-centered evaluation of therapeutic safety and efficacy [48].

High-risk biologics require advanced ADA assay design

High-risk biologics require advanced, risk-based ADA assay design. This is especially critical when the biotherapeutic mimics an endogenous protein with unique or non-redundant biological function [7,8]. For molecules such as cytokine-fusion proteins or highly homologous peptides, ADAs pose a significant safety risk by potentially cross-reacting with endogenous counterparts [3,4,8]. Historical cases, such as recombinant growth factors like erythropoietin (EPO) or coagulation factors, highlight these risks [8,45,49,50]. While fully homologous biotherapeutics may leverage existing immune tolerance, this tolerance is often fragile and can be disrupted by factors like post-translational modifications, expression levels, aggregation or sequence polymorphisms [51,52]. Therefore, health authorities recommend robust cross-reactivity assays and PD evaluations to ensure drug-induced ADAs do not compromise the biological activity or systemic levels of endogenous counterparts through neutralization, accelerated clearance, or induction of autoimmune-like conditions [1–5]. Interestingly, the clinical impact of ADAs varies substantially across therapeutic classes, with insulin and GLP-1 serving as instructive examples [53,54]. Despite high ADA incidence, these molecules maintain efficacy with minimal safety signals, a pattern the FDA recognizes through its draft guideline, which permits immunogenicity study waivers when a thorough analytical risk assessment (IRA) demonstrates low residual uncertainty [6–8,53,54]. The favorable outcomes in these cases may reflect a protective mechanism. ADA-drug complexes can prolong the therapeutic half-life, establishing a reservoir that gradually releases active drug and sustains efficacy despite immune-mediated pressure. This observation underscores an important principle. ADAs do not uniformly threaten clinical outcomes.

Therefore, a comprehensive ADA strategy should combine two complementary approaches. First, employ advanced characterization assays (nAb assays, cross-reactivity testing) to evaluate endogenous protein function. Second, integrate active PK, proximal PD and safety biomarkers to directly assess clinical impact (Figure 1). Together, these methods enable risk-appropriate assay design tailored to each therapeutic’s actual immunogenicity profile.

In addition, implementing ADA domain characterization assays early in the development process can be a more efficient approach before conducting more complex and labor-intensive cell-based ADA cross-reactivity or nAb assays [55]. Because functional cell-based assays are challenging to validate and highly susceptible to matrix interference, leveraging robust ligand-binding domain assays provide a high-throughput method to quickly triage patient samples. ADA domain-specific assays are particularly critical for bi/multi-specific antibodies. While not a mandatory step, these pre-assessments can provide valuable insights during early clinical development. Specifically, they help determine whether ADA responses are directed toward the domain that mimics the endogenous counterpart or other drug-specific domains [56]. This information can be instrumental for sponsors in deciding whether to progress with advanced ADA characterization assays and in designing more targeted nAb assays for late-stage clinical trials.

Additionally, for complex, multi-specific biologics, such as T-cell engager drugs, ADA domain characterization assays may have heightened importance [19]. ADAs targeting the CD3 effector domain could interfere with T-cell activation, directly impacting the drug’s MoA and reducing efficacy. On the other hand, ADAs binding to the target-domain of drug molecules might facilitate immune complex formation. Such ADA-drug complexes could expose multiple free anti-CD3 binding moieties, potentially leading to unintended T-cell crosslinking and systemic ADA-mediated T-cell activation, which could result in adverse events [19,57,58]. The potential risks of ADA-drug immune complexes, including their role in undesired immune activation, have also been described in prior studies [59]. These findings emphasize the critical role of domain-specific ADA characterization in evaluating and mitigating immunogenicity risks, particularly for complex biologics (Table 1).

ADA-drug immune complex formation and isotype characterization

The primary objective of advanced ADA assay design is to characterize and mitigate clinical safety risks. During clinical trials, the manifestation of severe infusion-related or hypersensitivity reactions naturally dictates the discontinuation of the drug to prioritize patient safety, meaning the exact immunological mechanism is secondary for acute patient management. However, for the drug development team, identifying the root cause of these safety signals is not merely an academic exercise to understand how adverse events are triggered; it is a critical regulatory and developmental necessity to determine whether, and how, a safe version of the drug can reach the market. In such scenarios, isotype characterization and ADA-drug complex analysis serve as actionable, hypothesis-driven investigative tools designed specifically to salvage, redesign, or appropriately dose the therapeutic. Because developing assays for drug-specific isotypes (such as IgE) and evaluating their results are highly complex endeavors, their implementation should be strictly guided by a predefined mechanistic hypothesis aimed at securing drug safety [3–5]. When justified, these retrospective analyses directly guide next-generation drug design and market viability. For example, detecting drug-specific IgE indicates a Type I (immediate) allergic reaction, often driven by a specific structural motif. For drug development, this finding means the therapeutic may need to be re-engineered to remove the offending epitope to ensure a safe drug can be brought to market. Notably, the clinical relevance of preexisting drug-specific IgE has been profoundly demonstrated in the case of cetuximab, where IgE antibodies directed against the galactose-alpha-1,3-galactose (alpha-gal) epitope on the molecule’s Fab fragment were linked to severe, immediate hypersensitivity reactions upon first infusion [64]. In contrast, clinical data for panitumumab or cetuximab-ß biologics, intentionally engineered in expression systems that lack the machinery to generate the alpha-gal epitope, demonstrate a near-complete absence of preexisting IgE-mediated hypersensitivity events [65–67]. This clinical divergence provides definitive evidence that target-specific glycan engineering can mitigate acute safety liabilities by eliminating structural immunogenic epitopes [67].

Conversely, identifying high-titer IgG (particularly complement-fixing subclasses like IgG1 or IgG3) and pentameric IgM indicates a risk for Type III (immune complex-mediated) hypersensitivity [68–71]. These isotypes function as high-avidity carriers, forming large, circulating immune complexes [23]. These complexes can lead to divergent clinical outcomes such as exaggerated pharmacological effects and may sustain drug half-life by acting as a reservoir. Unwanted complement activation via the C1q pathway can result in systemic inflammation such as vasculitis or serum sickness-like syndromes. If a drug development team detects these large immune complexes, the immediate consequence for marketability is that they must evaluate whether the clinical risk can be mitigated by altering the dosing regimen. By adjusting the dose to prevent the specific drug-to-antibody ratios that favor large complex formation, drug safety scientists can salvage the drug’s safety profile; otherwise, if the inherent immunogenicity is too high for safe systemic administration, development must be halted to prevent an unsafe drug from reaching the market.

Atiqi et al. illustrates the risks of ADA-drug complex formation for TNF inhibitors (TNFi). While most patients with chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory, or autoimmune diseases on TNFi will develop ADAs, the actual risk of treatment failure or adverse side effects depends heavily on the specific drug used, the size of the antibody complexes formed, and the type of assay used to detect them [60]. Monoclonal antibodies such as adalimumab and infliximab are highly prone to triggering ADAs because of their unique, targetable structural regions (idiotypes), whereas the fusion protein etanercept lacks these regions and rarely causes an immune response [60]. Crucially, the presence of these antibodies alone does not indicate treatment failure. Clinical complications or severe infusion reactions, which occur when large ADA-drug complexes activate the complement system, typically only manifest when ADA levels are sufficiently high to completely neutralize or rapidly clear the drug [60]. Consequently, accurate clinical decision-making requires assessment of both active serum drug levels (active PK) and the specific ADA assay used: drug-sensitive ADA assays (which detect ADAs only when drug levels drop below therapeutic thresholds) are excellent at predicting treatment failure, whereas drug-tolerant assays (which detect total ADAs regardless of active drug presence) often show positive results even in clinically stable patients. In conclusion, an advanced ADA testing strategy for such cases includes an integrative approach using active PK assays in addition to drug-sensitive and/or drug-tolerant ADA assays, whereas nAb assays offer no additional value to support clinical teams [60] (Table 1).

To detect these specific isotypes and subclasses in cases of ADA-related AE events, ligand-binding assays (LBAs) remain a standard approach. By employing reagents that specifically target the ADA subclass, often within an ADA-drug complex detection format, these assays achieve high sensitivity and superior drug tolerance [19,26,72–75].

Furthermore, recent advancements in hybrid immunocapture LC-MS/MS (IC-LC-MS/MS) have enhanced the precision of isotype and subclass quantification [76,77]. These techniques offer a deeper resolution of ADA-related safety concerns, enabling the design of targeted mitigation strategies to improve patient outcomes.

Ultimately, all advanced methodologies adhere to a risk-based, fit-for-purpose approach [75]. This ensures that analytical strategies are tailored to specific clinical and scientific objectives, balancing technical flexibility with robust validation standards and data utility (Figure 1, Table 1).

Framework for planning advanced ADA testing

Advanced ADA characterization assays are designed to evaluate the potential impact of the ADA response, which is influenced by the drug molecule’s biological MoA (Figure 1). However, it must be noted that many proposed strategies still lack broad prospective validation and definitive clinical standardization across therapeutic classes. Therefore, these tools should be viewed as supportive components within a holistic clinical-pharmacology assessment framework rather than standalone clinical prognosticators. The implementation of these assays should follow a fit-for-purpose approach, ensuring that they address specific clinical or scientific questions relevant to the drug biologics in development (Table 1). While a few health authority recommendations and white papers provide high-level technical guidelines for developing ADA characterization assays [1–5,31,33,75,78], they often lack prescriptive requirements for specific validation performance characteristics. This absence of rigid standards can make it challenging to independently define acceptance criteria, however, it also provides researchers with the fit-for-purpose flexibility needed to tailor assays to the specific scientific and clinical questions of a given project. Each assay should be designed to address critical aspects of the drug´s MoA and its specific immunogenicity risk without compromising assay reliability or relevance. Considerations to report data for advanced ADA characterization during clinical development and in regulated filing

Effective visualization of ADA, PK and PD data is essential for interpreting the clinical impact of immunogenicity. Unlike traditional reporting methods that rely on discrete, semi-quantitative titer tiers, comprehensive visualization integrates continuous data on ADA prevalence, incidence, onset, and magnitude to assess how ADA responses influence drug exposure, efficacy and patient safety [78]. This enables clinical teams to identify critical thresholds where ADA impacts key PK parameters, including clearance, Cmax, and systemic exposure (AUC). Whether through longitudinal cohort tracking or the stratification of PK/PD parameters by ADA status, these tools transform raw assay results, moving beyond semi-quantitative titers toward quantitative evaluation using S/N data and modeling.

These visual tools provide the foundation for sophisticated population modeling. By integrating advanced concepts such as ADA affinity and MIAD/ARC, the industry aims to improve predictive accuracy for clinical outcomes, such as bridging findings from Phase I to Phase II/III or predicting long-term effects. While full implementation of these predictive models remains an aspirational goal for future development, this shift toward quantitative integration significantly enhances the clinical relevance of immunogenicity data.

Translating these insights into formal regulatory submissions requires a stringent, risk-based framework. Historically, regulatory submissions focused on standalone assay validation reports. Today, regulatory bodies, such as the FDA and EMA, require a tiered testing strategy that extends beyond simple detection to include characterization of an immune response´s quality, magnitude, and functional impact. Within the Integrated Summary of Immunogenicity (ISI), modeling allows sponsors to synthesize data from various assays to explicitly link ADA characteristics with safety and efficacy outcomes. While bioanalytical assays characterize the immune response, modeling elucidates its clinical significance, transforming raw data into the actionable insights required by health authorities.

Sponsors are expected to synthesize data derived from Validation, Qualification, or FFP reports, depending on the assay’s specific role. While primary screening and nAb assays generally demand full validation to ensure reliability and reproducibility, supporting characterization assays such as those examining isotype or domain specificity may be supported by qualification data demonstrating the assay is scientifically robust for its intended application. By leveraging this adaptability, sponsors can align assay development with a program’s unique needs without compromising consistency.

Conclusion

The progression of protein therapeutics toward increasingly complex, multi-specific, and high-risk designs demands a corresponding evolution in immunogenicity assessment strategies. Historically, the field’s approach to immunogenicity was shaped by early, severe safety events, such as the cross-reactivity observed with recombinant erythropoietin (EPO), which instilled a deep-seated fear of any ADA response. However, as our understanding of immunology has matured, it has become evident that ADAs do not uniformly threaten clinical outcomes. While the traditional three-tiered ADA testing framework remains a regulatory cornerstone, it is no longer sufficient to fully capture the clinical risks posed by modern biologics. As highlighted in this review, a more advanced, risk-based assay design is critical to bridging the gap between the detection of ADA incidence and its actual clinical implications. Specifically, this advanced design allows bioanalytical scientists to effectively distinguish between a benign ADA response (where the immune system is merely activated to release ADAs without impacting safety or efficacy) and active, inflammatory immune activation (such as hypersensitivity driven by large immune complexes).

The adoption of MIAD/ARC marks a pivotal advancement, enabling the optimization of drug tolerance and the sensitive detection of ADA responses across a wide range of affinities. By grounding ADA characterization in the drug’s MoA leveraging tools such as “active” PK assays, proximal PD, and nAb/cross-reactivity evaluations, developers can move from qualitative observations to a quantitative understanding of how antibodies impact therapeutic efficacy and patient safety.

In addition, the shift toward a fit-for-purpose framework introduces the necessary flexibility to address the unique challenges posed by biologics that mimic endogenous proteins or incorporate potent effector functions, such as T-cell engagers. Emerging methodologies are becoming increasingly sophisticated, offering the detailed data required for advanced population PK modeling and informed regulatory submissions.

Ultimately, the goal of enhanced ADA characterization is to transform immunogenicity data into actionable clinical insights. By emphasizing early risk assessment and implementing an integrated testing strategy, drug developers can more effectively predict, mitigate, and communicate the impact of immune responses. This ensures the delivery of safer, more effective biologic therapies to patients.

Future perspectives

The future of immunogenicity risk management and of advanced ADA assay design lies in combining bioanalytical data with computational modeling, which is expected to become the standard practice. Improvements in detection technologies are likely to encourage wider adoption, providing exceptional molecular detail for ADA characterization. This includes measuring isotypes, class switch and determining affinity, capabilities that surpass the limitations of traditional LBAs. The industry is shifting toward a “Systems Immunogenicity” approach, which integrates ADA data with a wide range of clinical variables, including the drug’s MoA, safety and efficacy profiles, and patient-specific factors such as HLA typing, to predict individual immune responses more effectively. A recent study demonstrated the power of a comprehensive, multivariable framework by incorporating non-sequence-related risk factors, such as the route of administration, the type of patient disease, and the drug’s MoA. Using a random forest machine learning model, these variables were combined with traditional sequence data to significantly enhance the accuracy of immunogenicity risk predictions [79]. This advanced framework enables drug developers to more effectively forecast and mitigate immunogenicity risks during early-stage development. The result is improved safety and efficacy for biologics and protein therapies, contributing to better therapeutic outcomes. Regulatory agencies are increasingly focusing on comprehensive assessments, making it essential to adopt integrated methods and standardized advanced assays, such as “active” PK assays, “active” target assays and ADA characterization [80].

The integration of AI and machine learning (ML) is fundamentally transforming these bioanalytical workflows and immunogenicity risk assessments across the pharmaceutical life cycle. In early assay development, computationally simulating complex analyte-reagent interactions, specifically through MIAD platforms, offers a robust mechanism to overcome traditional drug-tolerance bottlenecks, resolve affinity-dependent detectability, and clarify binding kinetics independent of standard positive controls [29]. Beyond method optimization, incorporating core concepts like the amount of anti-drug antibody-reagent complexes at the cut-point into these digital frameworks provides a standardized technical metric, establishing long-sought cross-assay comparability across different bioanalytical methods and therapeutic compounds [30]. Integrating these advanced AI tools with machine learning algorithms will enhance tailored advanced monitoring and will enable bioanalytical scientists to accurately assess even the most complex next-generation biologics. However, capturing these efficiencies requires a parallel shift in our validation paradigms, as traditional validation frameworks often fall short when evaluating the dynamic, non-linear nature of AI-driven tools [81].

Looking toward clinical translation, predictive architectures like Random Survival Forests and QSP models are merging clinical, genetic, and in silico epitope-mapping data to forecast both the probability of ADA formation and its longitudinal impact on safety and pharmacokinetics [82,83]. Despite current limitations of these models, by applying these mechanism-based modeling approaches, developers might be able quantify how immunogenicity alters therapeutic protein exposure and clearance soon [20,21]. Ultimately, this digital transformation will minimize manual analytical burdens and transition immunogenicity management from a reactive, descriptive exercise to a proactive, predictive discipline, safely accelerating the development of complex, next-generation biologics.
