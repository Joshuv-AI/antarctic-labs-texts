Tumor implantation site dictates the immune landscape of syngeneic mouse models of head and neck cancer
Authors: Klara Rasmussen Bollerup Lanng, Anders Etzerodt, Michael Robert Horsman, Brita Singers Sørensen, Martin Roelsgaard Jakobsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSyngeneic mouse models are widely used to study immune-mediated tumor responses. While these models are typically implanted in the flank of the mouse, some experimental setups require alternative sites of tumor implantation, such as the foot. It remains unclear how tumors in the flank and the foot differ in terms of immune cell infiltration, thus introducing an unresolved variable in the interpretation of studies applying these two distinct models. To address this, we compared immune cell profiles in foot and flank tumors using the syngeneic carcinogen-induced oral carcinoma cell lines, MOC1 and MOC2. Tumors were digested into single-cell suspensions and analyzed for infiltrating immune cells using spectral flow cytometry. Our findings reveal substantial location-dependent differences in tumor growth and immune cell infiltration, and demonstrate that implantation site is not a neutral experimental parameter but a determinant of the immune landscape shaped by tumor progression. By clarifying how anatomical context influences immune cell infiltration in a syngeneic model, we can support better alignment and hopefully reproducibility between experimental design and biological questions. The variations we find may have important implications for studies on immune-mediated mechanisms of anticancer treatments and should be considered when determining the desired model for such future investigations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54161-4.Subject terms: Biological techniques, Cancer, Immunology, Oncology

Introduction

There are numerous types of mouse models that can be categorized by tumor origin (xenograft, syngeneic or spontaneous) and implantation site (heterotopic or orthotopic), each with distinct advantages and limitations1. Patient-derived xenograft models, derived from human tumors, are commonly used models for evaluating anticancer drugs2. However, they require complete depletion of the murine host immune system to prevent a graft-versus-host reaction. In contrast, syngeneic mouse models, where mouse tumor cells are engrafted into an immune-competent and genetically matched mouse, allow tumors to be studied in the presence of an intact immune system1. In syngeneic models, tumor cells can be implanted either at the place of origin (orthotopic) or at a different location, such as the flank (heterotopic). Subcutaneous (SC) implantation of tumor cells in the flank is one of the most widely used syngeneic models, because they are easy to evaluate during tumor development. However, certain experimental setups require easier isolation of the tumor than the flank model allows. This applies to pre-clinical studies in the field of radiobiology, where tumors at the hind leg (foot) of mice allow for easier isolation and environmental control of the irradiated area3. Previous research has demonstrated that the tumor microenvironment (TME) changes depending on the tumor’s location. Comparisons between SC and orthotopic tumors has revealed differences in tumor growth4, immune composition5, and response to chemotherapy4 and immunotherapy6–8, in various mouse cancer models. However, results have been diverging and constricted to the specific model and orthotopic origin of the tumor and there is a lack of knowledge on how the immunological composition of the TME differs between flank and foot tumors.

Profound evidence has underlined the importance of the immune system in the natural occurring control of pre-malignant cells and it is today fully recognized that the immunological signature of a tumor can shape and regulate tumor biology, progression and sensitivity towards treatment, including radiotherapy (RT)9,10. In a study on a Danish breast cancer cohort, researchers found that patients with a higher number of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) pre-treatment, gained a better overall survival from post-mastectomy RT than patients with low TIL, indicating a possible correlation between pre-existing TILs and effect of RT11. Another study investigated the prognostic capability of 26 different immune cell types in patients with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) undergoing RT. They found mast cells, activated B cells, effector memory CD4 T cells, NK cells and eosinophils to be associated with better prognosis for these patients12. Evidence from preclinical studies have also found immune infiltration to shape the response to RT. The infiltration of cytotoxic CD8 T cells and conventional dendritic cells type 1 (cDC1s) has been shown to be essential for the radiosensitivity observed in two pre-clinical tumor models13,14. Moreover, depletion of macrophages prior to RT has been shown to improve the anti-tumor effects of the treatment, indicating a macrophage-driven resistance mechanism15. The complex nature of both clinical and preclinical findings highlights the growing need for robust mouse models to explore the mechanisms driving the formation of immune-activating or suppressive environments and their sensitivity towards RT and other anticancer treatments.

While previous studies have focused on the differences between orthotopic and SC tumor models, it has to our knowledge not been investigated how the SC foot tumor model differs from the conventional SC flank tumor model. To study this, we utilized the murine carcinogen-induced oral mouse tumors MOC1 and MOC2 derived from C57BL/6 mice, which are characterized by two remarkedly different growth phenotypes in vivo16. MOC1 has an indolent growth phenotype and responds to both immune checkpoint inhibition (ICI) and radiotherapy14,16,17. In contrast, MOC2 has an aggressive growth phenotype, prone to metastasize and is resistant to different types of treatment, including ICIs and radiotherapy14,16,17. In the present study, we investigated the growth as well as the immune cell infiltration in MOC1 and MOC2 tumors implanted in either the foot or the flank, respectively.

Results

Anatomical location of the tumor affects tumor growth

To evaluate differences in tumor control between the flank and the foot, we measured the growth of the tumors from inoculation to harvest for flow cytometry (Fig. 1A). We observed that MOC1 tumors in both the foot and flank grew notably slower than MOC2 tumors, reaching a size of 200–400 mm3 on day 41 compared to day seven for MOC2 (Fig. 1B and C). For the MOC2 tumors in both the foot and the flank, we observed such an aggressive tumor growth that only one data point for tumor growth was available before the experimental endpoint. At this datapoint, MOC2 flank and foot tumors did not differ in tumor size (Fig. 1C). However, after dissection, flank tumors were found to be significantly bigger than foot tumors, based on weight (Fig. 1C). For MOC1, we found that the flank tumors grew faster and were significantly bigger than the foot tumor on the day of harvest, based on both size and weight (Fig. 1B and C). The difference in tumor control observed between foot and flank models strongly indicates a considerable difference in the TME of the two models.

The degree of aggressiveness in the growth phenotypes of MOC1 and MOC2 have previously been linked to their expression of CD4416. Therefore, we also analyzed the expression of CD44 in the non-immune cell compartment of the tumors, to see if the difference between foot and flank tumors was driven by CD44 expression. We did not detect any difference between the percentage of CD44+ cells between foot and flank tumors in either MOC1 or MOC2, though the total frequency of CD44+ cells in MOC2 was significantly higher than in MOC1, supporting a more aggressive growth (Fig. 1D). Surprisingly, when analyzing the median fluorescent intensity (MFI) of CD44 on the CD44+ population, we found a significantly higher degree of expression in MOC2 foot tumors compared to flank tumors, rejecting a possible association between elevated CD44 expression and increased growth rates of the flank tumors (Fig. 1E).

The anatomical location affects the infiltration of lymphocytes

The immunological composition of a tumor can have a great impact on tumor progression and treatment response18. Therefore, we analyzed the overall infiltration of immune cells (CD45+) to address if the tumor immune microenvironment was divergent between the two anatomical locations. While no difference was observed between MOC1 tumors in the foot and the flank, MOC2 tumors in the flank showed greater immune cell infiltration compared to MOC2 foot tumors (Fig. 2A & Sup. Fig. S1). In line with previous studies, we found that MOC1 had an increased infiltration of immune cells compared to MOC2, regardless of anatomical location, indicating an overall stronger immunologically hot environment (Fig. 2A & Sup. Despite associations between TILs and clinical outcomes, there is a concerning lack of detailed data on how the presence of TILs differs between applied preclinical models. To address this gap in knowledge, we examined the infiltration of T cells in MOC1 and MOC2 tumors implanted in the flank or foot by an antibody panel designed for detecting subsets and activation states of T cells using spectral flow cytometry (Sup. Table S1). The major T cell subsets were identified based on the gating strategy shown in Fig. 2B.The full gating strategy is outlined in the supplementary information (Sup. Fig. S2). We demonstrated that the infiltration of T cells (CD45+CD3+) did not significantly differ between MOC1 foot and flank tumors, which could be partly explained by a large variation in the flank group (Fig. 2C). Likewise, the infiltration of major T cell subtypes; CD4 T cells (CD3+CD4+FoxP3−), CD8 cytotoxic T cells (CD3+CD8+), and Tregs (CD3+CD4+FoxP3+), did not differ between MOC1 foot and flank tumors (Fig. 2C). For MOC2 tumors, the infiltration of T cells was significantly higher in flank tumors, compared to foot tumors (Fig. 2C). This difference was driven by a higher percentage of all subtypes of T cells investigated (CD4 T cells, CD8 T cells and Tregs), as they all showed a significant increase in flank tumors, compared to foot tumors (Fig. 2C).

As the increase in T cells in MOC2 tumors did not result in increased tumor control, we next evaluated the functionality of the infiltrating T cells by assessing their expression of PD-1 and interferon gamma (IFNγ). Expression of check point inhibitor PD-1 on the surface of activated T-cells dampens T cell activity and is one of the main co-inhibitory receptors regulating T cell exhaustion19,20. We found no difference in the percentage of PD-1+ CD4 or CD8 T cells in MOC1 tumors, but interestingly we observed a significant decrease in PD-1+ CD4 and CD8 T cells in MOC2 flank tumors, compared to the foot (Fig. 2D). The expression of IFNγ did not differ between MOC2 foot and flank tumors (Fig. 2E) and could hence not explain the apparent contradictions between increased CD8 T cell infiltration and decreased tumor control. We also noted that IFNγ expression did not differ between MOC1 foot and flank tumors (Fig. 2E).

Altogether, our data demonstrates no differences in the lymphocyte compartment of MOC1 foot and flank tumors. By contrast, we revealed pronounced alterations to the lymphocytic cell compartment between MOC2 foot and flank tumors, including an increased infiltration of CD8, CD4 and Tregs, with reduced expression of the inhibitory marker PD-1, in flank tumors.

The anatomical location of tumor implantation affects the myeloid cell population of both MOC1 and MOC2 tumors

Current literature substantiates the importance of initial recognition and activation of tumor infiltrating myeloid cells for shaping an anti-tumorigenic immune environment rendering a successful T cell mediated anti-tumor response possible21. Therefore, we next examined the infiltration of myeloid cells using an antibody panel designed for recognizing a broad range of myeloid cells, including monocytes, dendritic cells (DCs), macrophages and neutrophils (Fig. 3A, Sup. Neutrophil granulocytes are the most abundant leukocyte in the blood and are the first line of defense to enter inflamed tissue22. Infiltration of neutrophils into tumor tissue has been extensively shown to correlate with worse prognosis across different cancer types23, due to their immunosuppressive and angiogenic activities22. When looking at neutrophil infiltration we found that for MOC1 tumors, foot tumors had a higher infiltration of neutrophil granulocytes (CD11b+Ly6G+) compared to flank tumors, whereas the infiltration of these cells did not seem to be affected by the anatomical location of MOC2 tumors (Fig. 3B).

Upon chemokine attraction, monocytes can enter tumor tissue and give rise to myeloid-derived suppressor cells and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), thereby shaping the tumor microenvironment (TME)24. In neither MOC1 nor MOC2, did we find any significant differences in the infiltration of monocytes (CD11b+Ly6C+MHC-II−) between foot and flank tumors (Fig. 3C).

DCs are essential for maturing T cells to specific anti-tumor killing. Therefore, we assessed the infiltration of the total percentage of DCs (CD11b+CD169−CD11c+), cDC1s (CD11b−CD11c+MHC-II+), cDC2s (CD11b+CD169−CD11c+MHC-II+Ly6C−) and Ly6Chigh DCs (CD11b+CD169−CD11c+MHC-II+Ly6Chigh) in MOC1 and MOC2 tumor models. Our data showed a higher infiltration of cDC1s in MOC1 flank tumors, compared to MOC1 foot tumors (Fig. 3D). None of the other DC subsets were different between foot and flank MOC1 tumors (Fig. 3D). For MOC2 tumors, the total number of DCs was higher in foot tumors, compared to flank tumors (Fig. 3D). No difference was observed for cDC2s, however, as for MOC1, cDC1 infiltration was higher in flank tumors (Fig. 3D). The overall decrease of DCs in the flank, despite the increase in cDC1s, may be driven by an almost complete absence of Ly6Chigh DCs in MOC2 flank tumors compared to foot tumors (Fig. 3D).

In recent years, macrophages have gained increased attention, because their immune-modulatory state can affect the TME, and both promote and inhibit tumor progression and response to anticancer treatment24. The overall infiltration of macrophages (CD11b+CD169+) did not differ between MOC1 foot and flank tumors (Fig. 3E). However, we could detect a difference in the infiltration of immunosuppressive macrophages (CD11b+CD169+CD163+), albeit the percentages of this cell population were low for both anatomical locations (Fig. 3E). Macrophage immunogenicity can be assessed by their expression of major histocompatibility complex II (MHC-II). Accordingly, we analyzed the macrophages by dividing them into two subtypes: MHC-IIhigh and MHC-IIlow macrophages. We found no difference between MOC1 foot and flank tumors for either MHC-IIhigh nor MHC-IIlow macrophages (Fig. 3E). For MOC2 tumors, there was an overall higher infiltration of macrophages in flank tumors, compared to foot tumors. This difference was seen in all macrophage subsets investigated (Fig. 3E). To further investigate if the changes in the macrophage infiltration observed for both MOC1 and MOC2 tumors could be pro- or antitumorigenic we next investigated different macrophage activation markers. CD86 and CD11c markers are expressed on the surface of activated macrophages and linked to a more inflammatory phenotype. Strikingly, the expression of CD86 was lower in flank tumors for both MCH-IIhigh and MHC-IIlow TAMs in both MOC1 and MOC2 (Fig. 3F), indicating a less immunogenic phenotype of TAMs in flank tumors. The expression of CD11c also decreased on immune suppressive and MHC-IIhigh TAMs in the flank, but only in MOC2 (Fig. 3F). To further broaden the immunogenic characterization of the TAMs in these tumor models, we included staining for check-point inhibitor PD-L1. In a recent paper, PD-L1 expression on macrophages were associated with activation and maturation of TAMs, correlating with better clinical outcome for patients with breast cancer25. The expression of PD-L1 was lowered on both MHC-IIhigh and MHC-IIlow TAMs in MOC2 flank tumors, compared to foot tumors. For MOC1, a lowered expression of PD-L1 on MHC-IIhigh TAMs in the flank was observed.

We demonstrated marked differences in the myeloid compartment between foot and flank tumors, for both the MOC1 and MOC2 tumor model. An increased infiltration of cDC1s in flank tumors in both MOC1 and MOC2, could indicate a more immunogenic tumor environment. However, a simultaneous increased infiltration of macrophages shifted toward an immunosuppressive phenotype could explain the decreased tumor control in the flank tumors for both MOC1 and MOC2.

PD-L1 expression on non-immune cells is unaltered between foot and flank tumors in both MOC1 and MOC2

The immunogenicity of tumors can also be affected by tumor-intrinsic factors, like expression of the PD-1 ligand PD-L1. Previous studies have reported that, while MOC1 tumors respond to check-point inhibition with αPD-L1, MOC2 shows resistance17. Therefore, we evaluated the expression of PD-L1 in MOC1 and MOC2 foot and flank tumors in the non-immune cell compartment of the samples (CD45−). We found minimal but significant difference in the percentage of PD-L1+ cells between foot and flank tumors in MOC1 tumors, whereas no differences were observed in MOC2 tumors (Fig. 4A & B). Noticeably, the percentage of PD-L1+ cells is higher for MOC1 than MOC2 tumors, agreeing with the previous reports on MOC2 resistance towards αPD-L1 treatment17.

Discussion

Despite significant advancement in the treatment of cancer patients, the scientific field still faces substantial challenges in replicating data based on mice studies and translating these findings to humans. Several researchers have highlighted that inadequate reporting on the model and experimental conditions can contribute to reproducibility issues26, while inappropriate model selection may lead to limitations in the translational relevance27. A comprehensive understanding of the differences between mouse tumor models can serve as an important tool in overcoming these challenges. In this study, we demonstrated that MOC1 and MOC2 murine oral cancer models displayed significant differences in growth and immune cell composition dependent on the SC implantation site (foot vs. flank), providing a repository of knowledge for future application of these models for evaluating immune-mediated mechanism in relation to cancer biology and treatment response.

Several strategies exist for establishing cohorts of mice bearing homogeneous tumors for experimental studies. Tumors may be generated either by subcutaneous inoculation of in vitro–cultured tumor cells or by transplantation of tumor tissue derived from an in vivo–grown donor tumor. In the latter approach, the donor tumor is excised, mechanically fragmented, and re-implanted into the flank of recipient mice27. The present study employs this in vivo - to - in vivo transplantation strategy, as previous experiments demonstrated that this method resulted in an optimized tumor take rate and reduced latency time for foot tumor establishment compared with cell-based inoculation17.

The foot and flank are two remarkably different anatomical locations on a mouse, representing different structural compositions of tissue, potentially impacting tumor biology. Both MOC1 and MOC2 flank tumors reached a significantly larger tumor size at the experimental endpoint compared to the foot, that could not readily be attributed to the level of CD44 expression. This difference indicates that the TME established in flank tumors hold a more pro-tumorigenic environment than the foot tumors. The augmented environment may be caused by alterations to the immune cell composition of the tumors. We observed a higher infiltration of Tregs in MOC2 flank tumors, that could contribute to enhanced tumor progression. However, this effect may be counteracted by the simultaneous increase in both CD4 and CD8 T cells. Furthermore, changes to the T cell compartment could not readily explain the increased growth of MOC1 flank tumors, as all T cell subtypes were unaffected by the anatomical location of this tumor model. Changes to the myeloid compartment could also contribute to the more rapid growth of MOC1 and MOC2 flank tumors. In our analysis, we found a pronounced increase in the infiltration of CD163+, MHC-IIhigh and MHC-IIlow macrophages in MOC2 flank tumors, compared to foot tumors. CD163+ macrophages elicit immunosuppressive capacities and have been linked to worse prognosis in patients with HNSCC28,29. MHC-IIlow macrophages are likewise considered immunosuppressive as lowered expression of MHC-II on TAMs has been associated with tumor progression30. We also found a decrease in the expression of activation markers CD86, CD11c and PD-L1 on pro-inflammatory MHC-IIhigh macrophages, additionally suggesting a drift towards a less immunologically beneficial environment in the flank tumors. For MOC1 tumors, we also observed a skewing towards an immunosuppressive TME shaped by alterations in the macrophage population. However, the change in growth between foot and flank tumors could also be caused by stromal differences between the foot and the flank. Studies comparing orthotopic and SC tumors have found differences in the abundance of necrosis5,31, vascularization4, and connective tissue4 between the different tumor locations, emphasizing the major impact the tumor implantation site and growth rate has on the tumor microenvironment biology.

A limitation of our study was the apparent differences in tumor growth kinetics and final tumor burden between flank and footpad models. This represents a potential confounding factor when interpreting the impact of anatomical location on the immune landscape as size of tumor may influence immune cell infiltration and profiles. However, an analysis based on similar tumor sizes would have be confounded by time of growth instead, which we anticipate would affect the immune expression profiles.

Changes to the TME can substantially impact the response to anticancer treatment, including RT10, and therefore, how we interpret preclinical results from such setups. Rapid tumor growth frequently leads to a hypoxic tumor environment, which is one of the major culprits in the formation of radioresistance32–34. Hence, the faster growth of both MOC1 and MOC2 flank tumors could potentially lead to increased hypoxia, creating a more radioresistant phenotype of the flank tumors. However, we also found significant changes in cell populations that previously have been associated with RT response. The increased infiltration of CD8 T cells and cDC1s in MOC1 and MOC2 flank tumors, which have been shown to be important for local and distal tumor control14,35, could favor increased radiosensitivity. On the contrary, TAMs have been associated with radioresistance15, implying that the increased infiltration of TAMs in the flank tumor for MOC2, may negatively affect the response to radiation. Additionally, in a study by Knitz et al., inhibition of Tregs combined with a simultaneous use of a DC agonist, could partly reverse the radioresistant phenotype of MOC2 tumors36, indicating that the increased infiltration of Tregs in MOC2 flank tumors could also contribute to an altered radiosensitivity. However, those previous studies were all based on the global depletion of a given cell type, and we cannot definitively state the prognostic effect of pre-existing CD8 T cells, cDC1s, T regs and TAMs, based on these preclinical studies. It should be noted that different studies, using either foot or flank tumor models, rather consistently report radioresistance of MOC2 and radiosensitivity of MOC114,17. Although these results point towards a preserved overall radioresponse between MOC1 and MOC2 foot and flank tumors, investigations into specific mechanisms of radiosensitivity could be biased depending on the location of the tumor.

Tumor implantation sites have been shown to impact the response to ICI. In a recent study, researchers found that while orthotopic implanted MOC1 tumors responded to αPD-1 treatment, the flank implanted tumors did not8. Similar findings have been shown for another murine model of HNSCC7. Studies examining the effect of αPD-L1 monotherapy in MOC1 tumors have also been conflicting by reporting both response17,37 and non-response38 to this therapy. Interestingly, the studies reporting response utilized tumors implanted in the foot17 and the oral cavity37, respectively, while the study reporting a lack of response, used MOC1 cells engrafted in the flank38, underlining the importance of tumor implantation site. These findings emphasize the problematics of reporting a cancer cell line as being either responsive or unresponsive to a certain treatment and should encourage future research to refer to both the cancer cell line and the tumor implantation site, when reporting treatment sensitivity.

Our findings provide evidence of differences in the growth and immunological characteristics of MOC1 and MOC2 tumors depending on the SC anatomical location. MOC2 tumors showed more pronounced changes to both the myeloid and lymphoid compartments depending on the anatomical location, compared to MOC1 – though not adjusted to size at endpoint but on progression time. The analysis of immune cell infiltration implied that the accelerated growth in MOC1 and MOC2 flank tumors could be driven by detrimental macrophage infiltration. The changes in immune cell infiltration and growth rate could impact the treatment response to RT and immunotherapies, and thus this should be taken into consideration when choosing any of the two models.

Methods section

Cell culture

MOC1 and MOC2 cells (Kerafast, USA) were cultured according to the recommended protocol in IMDM MOC line media. IMDM MOC line media consisted of a 2:1 mixture of Iscove’s Modified Dulbecco’s Medium (IMDM) (#12440046, ThermoFisher) and Ham’s F-12 nutrient mix (#21765029, ThermoFisher) with the following supplements: 100 U/ml penicillin and 100 µg/ml streptomycin (#15140122, Gibco), 5% FBS (#F9665, Sigma-Aldrich), 5 µg/ml insulin (#I6634, Sigma-Aldrich), 0.04 µg/ml hydrocortisone (#H0135, Sigma-Aldrich) and 0.005 µg/ml EGF (#PH0315, Gibco). Cells in culture were regularly tested for mycoplasma infection, and cell line authentication by short-tandem repeat (STR) analysis, was performed to ensure authenticity of the cell lines.

Mouse tumor models

Female C57BL/6Jrj mice were purchased from Janvier (Janvier, France) and acclimatized for a minimum of one week before being included in the experiment. The animals were housed in individually ventilated cages (Scanbur, Denmark) in groups of four with ad libitum access to food and drinking water. The temperature was kept between 20–24℃ and the relative humidity was kept at 55 ± 10%. The animal facility used a 12-hour light/dark cycle, with the lights on from 6 AM to 6 PM.

Two million MOC1 or MOC2 tumor cells from in vitro culture were initially implanted in the flank of the mice. When the tumors reached 1000 mm3, they were excised and processed to a single cell suspension in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and frozen in aliquots for future implantations. Upon experiment initiation, freshly thawed MOC1 and MOC2 single cell suspension was implanted in the flank of three donor mice for each cell line. When the tumors reached a size of 1000 mm3 the tumors were excised and processed to a single cell suspension in PBS and approximately 7 µl cell suspension was injected subcutaneously into the right hind leg (foot) or the flank of the mice for tumor development. Mice inoculated with MOC1 were 15 weeks old and mice inoculated with MOC2 were 11 weeks old at the time of inoculation. Length, height and width of the tumor were determined using a caliper and tumor volume was calculated by the ellipsoid volume formula (L x W x H x 6/π), which previously has been demonstrated to give the most accurate estimate of the actual tumor mass39. When the tumors reached a size of approximately 200 mm3, the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation and tumors were dissected for further processing and analysis. Due to subsequent analysis, tumors from the same model had to be harvested and analyzed the same day. Thirteen out of the 20 mice with MOC1 tumors were included in the final flow analysis (8/10 in the foot group and 5/10 in the flank group). Of the excluded mice, 6 had tumors too small and 1 mouse with a flank tumor was euthanized before time because of ulceration. For MOC2 tumors, 17 out of 20 mice were included in the final flow analysis. The three excluded tumors were too small on the day of harvest.

Tumor dissociation

Tumors were dissected from the flank and the foot and placed in tubes with PBS and stored on ice until processing. The tumors were processed to a single cell suspension by a combination of mechanical and enzymatic dissociation using the Mouse Tumor Dissociation Kit and GentleMACS Octo Dissociator or the GentleMACS dissociator from Miltenyi (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. First, tumors were cut into smaller pieces and transferred to C tubes (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) containing 2.5 ml enzyme mix. The samples were run on the Octo Dissociator using the program 37c_m_TDK2 for tough tumors or the gentleMACS dissociator using the program m_impTumor_02 two times followed by 40 min incubation at 37℃ under rotation followed by two times with the program m_impTumor_TDK3. Afterwards, all samples were centrifuged (300xG, 30 s) and resuspended in RPMI containing 2 mM EDTA and passed through a 70 μm cell strainer to get rid of excess clumps. MOC2 tumors were treated with ACK lysing solution (Gibco, USA) to get rid of erythrocytes before proceeding in the protocol. The cell concentration in each tumor cell suspension were determined using a NucleoCounter NC-250 (Chemometec, Denmark) with solution 18 (Chemometec, Denmark). Approximately two million cells from each tumor sample were plated out in two V-bottomed 96-well plates for flow staining and analysis.

Flow cytometry

To discriminate between live and dead cells, samples were stained with live/dead Fixable Blue (Cat. No. L34961, Thermo Scientific) diluted 1:2500 in PBS. The samples were centrifuged (400xG, 5 min) and resuspended in either 100 µl Fixable blue master mix or 100 µl PBS (unstained and live/dead fluorescence minus one (FMO) control) and stained at 4℃ for 20 min protected from light. After live/dead stain, the samples were blocked in αCD16/αCD32, produced by the hybridoma cell line 2.4G2, for 10–20 min at room temperature. The samples were subsequently stained with an antibody panel to identify tumor-associated macrophages and myeloid cells (TAM panel) or tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL panel) (Sup. Tables 1 & 2). Staining mixes for extracellular staining of multi stain samples and FMOs were mixed, according to supplementary Tables 1 & 2, in FACS staining buffer (SB) (PBS + 5% FCS + 2 mM EDTA + 0.09% NaN3). Cells were stained at 4℃ for 30 min protected from light. The plate stained for the TAM panel was washed 3 times in SB before being fixed in 100 µl fixation buffer (PBS + 0.99% formaldehyde) for 30 min, centrifuged (500xG, 5 min) and resuspended in SB before analysis. The plate stained with the TIL panel was washed 3 times in SB, before proceeding with the intracellular staining protocol. Samples were permeabilized and fixated using the eBioscience FoxP3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (#00–5523-00, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Initial fixation and permeabilization was done for 30 min, before proceeding with a wash in permeabilization buffer and staining with antibodies towards FoxP3 and IFNγ for 30 min at room temperature. The samples were subsequently washed three times in permeabilization buffer, before resuspending them in 100 µl SB before analysis. OneComp eBeads compensation beads (#01–1111-42, Thermo Fisher Scientific), were stained with 1 µl of the respective antibodies for 20 min and thereafter treated the exact same way as the samples. Amine Reactive Compensation (ArC) beads were stained with either 2 µl undiluted Fixable blue or PBS for 20 min and subsequently treated as either the TAM panel or the TIL panel.

The samples were analyzed on a 5-laser SONY ID7000 spectral flow cytometer (SONY Biotechnologies, USA), available at the FACS core facility at the Department of Biomedicine, Aarhus University, Denmark. Beads were used as single stain controls to determine the reference spectra for all fluorochromes. The reference spectra were used to perform unmixing in the ID7000 software (SONY Biotechnologies, USA). Upon unmixing, FCS files were exported and analyzed in FlowJo (v. 10.10.0, BD, USA).

Statistical considerations

Graphs and statistical calculations were done with GraphPad Prism (v10.3.1, GraphPad). The nonparametric Mann Whitney test was used to test for significance differences between two independent groups (foot vs. flank). Multiple comparisons between more groups (foot vs. flank and MOC1 vs. MOC2) were compared using two-way ANOVA. The significance level was set to p < 0.05 for all tests, and corrections for multiple comparisons were made using Holm-Šídák method. Asterisks indicate the following p values: (ns) p ≥ 0.05, (*) p < 0.05, (**) p < 0.01, (***) p < 0.001 and (****) p < 0.0001. Further details on statistical testing are stated in the legend of each figure. Detailed statistical analysis readout can be seen in supplementary Table 3.
