Hyperbaric oxygen therapy for late radiation tissue toxicity injury after head and neck cancer: a systematic review of the literature
Authors: Soufiane El Hadji, David N. Teguh, Milan L. Ridderikhof
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHead and neck cancer (HNC), most of which are squamous cell carcinomas, is the seventh most common cancer worldwide. Radiotherapy is a standard treatment for HNC but may lead to late complications and severe complications like osteoradionecrosis (ORN) and impaired wound healing due to tissue hypoxia. Hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) has shown promise in ameliorating these late radiation effects. The purpose of this review is to summarize the extent of the literature on the effectiveness of HBOT in the treatment of late radiation tissue toxicity injuries (LRTTI) specifically in HNC patients.Methods/material and methodsA systematic literature search was performed using PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library on August 12, 2024, including studies published between 2004 and 2022. Studies that included HNC patients with LRTTI and treated with HBOT were selected. Articles were critically appraised using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklists. Data on patient characteristics, HBOT treatment details, and main outcomes were extracted. Primary outcomes assessed included clinical changes, such as the Notani score, while secondary outcomes focused on patient-reported measures such as VAS and OHIP. Descriptive analysis, supported by statistical measures, was used to interpret the results.ResultsA total of 17 studies were reviewed, including 640 HNC patients with LRTTI who were treated with HBOT. In this systematic review, HBOT is presented in the included studies as a reliable and safe treatment for the treatment of LRTTI in HNC patients, with positive outcomes observed in 14 out of 17 studies. Specifically, almost all studies investigating ORN and oral health reported beneficial effects, with significant p-values in multiple cases. Overall, significant p-values were found in 11 studies, with a low incidence of adverse effects reported across the studies.ConclusionThis review suggests that HBOT may be effective in the treatment of LRTTI in HNC patients. However, the supporting evidence is mainly derived from low quality studies with a high risk of bias, limited sample sizes, and inconsistent outcome measures. Additional high quality studies are needed to clarify the true clinical benefits and optimal use of HBOT.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13014-025-02680-1.

Introduction

Head and neck cancer (HNC) is one of the most common cancers worldwide, according to the latest estimates for 2020 [1]. Specifically, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) is the seventh most common cancer, with approximately 890,000 new cases (about 4,5% of all cancer diagnoses worldwide) and 450,000 deaths (around 4,6% of all cancer-related deaths worldwide) per year [1].

In many countries, the incidence of HNSCC is increasing, particularly in younger populations, with a projected annual increase of 30% by 2030 [1].

Late radiation tissue toxicity injuries (LRTTI) are a concern, with an average yearly incidence reaching even 14% in some hospitals [2]. Tissues with slow turnover, such as the brain, kidneys, liver, bowel wall, subcutaneous tissue, fat, and muscle, are prone to LRTTI. Radiation in these areas can cause fibrosis, atrophy, necrosis, and vascular damage, which can lead to telangiectasia and even carcinogenesis [3]. More common LRTTI include dental caries, xerostomia, osteoradionecrosis (ORN), hypothyroidism and impaired wound healing [4, 5].

HBOT involves breathing 100% oxygen at pressures above 1.5 atmospheres absolute (ATA; 150 kPa), typically between 2 and 3 ATA (200–300 kPa). The hyperbaric chamber is the medical device that creates these conditions and allows the delivery of oxygen in quantities that cannot be achieved by other methods [6].

During HBOT, oxygen is physically dissolved into the blood plasma. At a pressure of 2.8 ATA, the level of oxygen dissolved in the plasma reaches roughly 6% by volume. This meets basic metabolic oxygen demands, and arterial PaO₂ levels can rise to 2,000 mmHg. With normal lung function and tissue perfusion, PO₂ levels can exceed 1,000 mmHg [6].

Physiological outcomes of HBOT include temporary benefits such as vasoconstriction, improved oxygen delivery, reduced edema, and activated phagocytosis, as well as anti-inflammatory properties. Long term outcomes include neovascularization, bone regeneration, and increased collagen production by fibroblasts. Clinically, these effects promote wound healing and recovery of tissue damaged by radiation, thus making it relevant for the treatment of LRTTI [6].

This review aims to answer the question: How effective is HBOT in treating late radiation-induced tissue toxicity injuries in HNC patients?

Materials and methods

Literature search

On August 12, 2024, an online literature search was conducted online in three databases: PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library. A comprehensive overview of the literature searches is provided in Tables 1 and 2, and 3 (see Supplementary Materials for all tables).

Study selection criteria

All articles identified by the literature search were initially screened by title and abstract. Articles deemed potentially eligible based on this preliminary screening were further assessed through a full-text review, provided the full text was available.

Inclusion criteria

Articles were selected if they included patients with a history of HNC who had undergone radiotherapy and experienced LRTTI as a result. In addition, only studies in which these patients were treated with HBOT and randomized controlled trials, cohort studies and case series were included.

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria consisted of studies that were not published in English or that were not conducted on humans. In addition, unavailable articles, including abstracts, and case reports were excluded. Studies that included patients without a history of HNC were also excluded.

Critical appraisal

The JBI Critical Appraisal Tools were used for the critical appraisal of the selected articles. These checklists offer a structured framework for evaluating the quality and validity of research studies. Three different checklists were employed: one for randomized controlled trials, another for cohort studies, and a third for case serie [7].

Results

Study selection and quality

A total of 389 articles were identified through the PubMed search, 208 through Embase, and 220 from the Cochrane Library. In total, 17 studies were included in this review [8–24]. A detailed overview is provided in the flow chart in Fig. 1.

The quality of the included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Tool, revealing a stronger methodological rigor in the randomized controlled trials compared to the cohort and case series studies, which generally had more potential for bias. Study designs

Seventeen studies were included, featuring diverse research designs. Randomized controlled trials accounted for six of these [8–13]while seven were cohort studies [14–19, 22] and four studies were case series [20, 21, 23, 24]. In addition, one of the randomized controlled trials combined data from two trials (Table 7) [9].

Patient and HBOT characteristics

Across the 17 included studies, a total of 640 patients, all diagnosed with HNC, were included. The mean age of patients varied across studies, ranging from 20 to 88 years. For further details, refer to Table 8.

In Table 8, all reported information pertains to patient characteristics and the technical characteristics of HBOT.

Patient reported outcome measures

Alongside the previously mentioned outcome measures, the studies included assessments based on patients reported outcome measures. The European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire (EORTC QLQ)-C30 and EORTC QLQ-H&N35 were utilized to assess the quality of life (QoL) among HNC patients [9–11]. Furthermore, the OHIP questionnaire, which consists of six multi-item scales, evaluated the physical, psychological, and social effects on patients. The Groningen Activity Restriction Scale for Dentistry (GARS-D) was employed to assess activities of daily living (ADL) and household daily living activities (HDL), which encompass self-sufficiency in both caregiving and household tasks [11]. In addition, the VAS score served as a subjective measure for acute and chronic pain across multiple studies [8–10]. Table 8 gives a summary of study and patient characteristics.

Use of HBOT for ORN prevention

Four studies have explored the role of HBOT in the prevention and management of ORN [8, 18, 19]. In the HOPON trial, Shaw et al. found no significant difference in the incidence of ORN between the HBOT and non-HBOT control group (6.4% vs. 5.7%; OR = 1.13, p > 0.99). This trial was closed early due to a lower-than-expected ORN incidence, which limited further analysis of HBOT efficacy [8]. The study of David et al. involved 75 patients divided into two groups: Group A (51 patients with overt ORN) and Group B (24 patients treated prophylactically with HBOT for dental extractions). In Group B, 54 teeth were extracted (37 in the radiation field), and all patients except one experienced unremarkable healing [18]. In the trial of Vudiniabola et al., ORN developed in 1 patient (4%) in the HBOT group and 1 patient (15%) in the control group [19].

Use of HBOT for ORN treatment

Forner et al. found a 70% healing rate of ORN in the surgery and HBOT group after one year, compared to 51% in the surgery-only group (p = 0.13, OR = 2.2, 95% CI: 0.7–7.0). The difference was not statistically significant possibly due to limited study power [9]. Recovery rates were lower in the HBOT group (19.3%) versus the placebo group (32.4%) in the trial of Annane et al. (RR = 0.60; 95% CI: 0.25–1.41; p = 0.23), with no significant differences in ulcerations, bone sequestrae, or surgical outcomes [13]. Korambayil et al. demonstrated improved oxygenation and defect coverage following 28 HBOT sessions in a patient with mandibular ORN [17].

Oral microcirculation and histological findings

Svalestad et al. reported significant increases in blood vessel density (p = 0.002), area (p = 0.041), and lymphatic vessel density (p = 0.002) in the sub-epithelial region following HBOT, although no significant differences in cell proliferation rates were found between groups [12]. Similarly, Annane et al. found a significantly higher mean arterial oxygen tension (PaO₂) in the HBOT group (mean PaO₂: 1,850 ± 125 mmHg) than in the placebo group (mean PaO₂: 119 ± 25 mmHg). This result highlighted the substantial increase in oxygen delivery through HBOT, yet it did not result in a significant improvement in recovery rates [13]. In addition, Helmers et al. observed a significant reduction in mean blood vessel diameter from baseline (T0: 20 ± 4 μm) to 24 weeks (T24: 16 ± 5 μm, p < 0.001), along with an increase in buccal mucosal capillary density over time (p < 0.05). However, post hoc analyses did not confirm these differences as statistically significant [14].

Transmucosal oxygen tension

Thorn et al. found a significant increase in transmucosal oxygen tension during HBOT treatment, particularly after five treatments (p < 0.05). The mean transmucosal oxygen tension in irradiated attached gingiva rose from 20.4 mm Hg to 34.7 mm Hg after 30 dives, reflecting an increase from 50 to 86% of normal healthy gingiva levels. The smallest observed increase, from 41 to 69%, occurred in a patient who did not experience healing following sequestrectomy [22].

Implant survival, caries and dental extraction

In the trial of Schoen et al., the implant survival rate was 93.9% for patients not receiving HBOT (with three implants lost), and 85.2% for those receiving HBOT (with eight implants lost). The difference was not significant between the groups. In addition, a minor peri-implant bone loss of 0.7 ± 0.6 mm was observed, with no significant differences between HBOT and non-HBOT patients [11]. In the study of Cankar et al., eight patients maintained their teeth, recording a mean plaque index of 2.4 ± 0.4 SE. All remaining teeth showed active dentin and enamel caries lesions, characteristic of irradiation caries [16]. Furthermore, Korambayil et al. presented a case of a 30-year-old female patient with low tissue oxygen levels of 30 mmHg who, after 16 HBOT sessions, showed an improvement to 65 mmHg, enabling a successful dental extraction [17].

Parotid glandular function

Vidmar et al. found a significant differences in mean T2 values (T2-mapping is an MRI technique that measures how long it takes certain tissues to relax after being magnetized) prior to HBOT were noted between the ipsilateral irradiated parotid (121 ± 20 ms) and contralateral parotid (107 ± 21 ms), as well as the control group (96 ± 12 ms, p = 0.0004) [21]. A positive correlation between T2 variability and irradiation dose was observed in contralateral parotids before HBOT (R = 0.489, p = 0.0287). After HBOT, the ipsilateral gland showed a significant decrease in mean T2 and T2 variability (p = 0.002) [15].

Xerostomia

Forner et al. also found improvements in xerostomia in the HBOT group, but none of the results reached statistical significance [9]. Using the EORTC H&N35 questionnaire, Teguh et al. found significant benefits in the HBOT group compared to non-HBOT controls for three items: sticky saliva (p = 0.01), dry mouth (p = 0.009), and swallowing (p = 0.011), as well as for the Performance Status Scale (PSS) eating in public (p = 0.027), according to the findings of Teguh et al. [10]. Consistent with these findings, Cankar et al. showed an improvement in xerostomia grade, with scores decreasing from 2.63 ± 0.2 to 1.94 ± 0.2 (p < 0.001) before and after HBOT [16].

Microbial levels

From the seventeen studies include, only Cankar et al. studied the effect of HBOT on the oral microbiome, i.e. on the growth of salivary Streptococcus mutans, Lactobacillus, and Candida albicans in patients with head and neck tumors who had undergone radiotherapy. They observed significant decreases in colony densities of S. mutans and Lactobacillus after HBOT (p < 0.001 and p < 0.05, respectively), along with a reduction in C. albicans (p < 0.05) [16].

QoL

While QoL scores, in the study of Teguh et al., during the acute phase (from the end of radiation until 13 weeks post-treatment) did not show significant differences, late side effects were significantly reduced in the HBOT group [10]. However, Schoen et al. found no significant improvement in the EORTC QLQ-C30 scales after HBOT, but emotional functioning improved significantly in the non-HBOT group. Beneficial effects on psychological discomfort were revealed in the OHIP questionnaire, particularly among non-HBOT patients [11].

Pain

The HOPON trial found minor improvements in pain and other acute symptoms within the HBOT group, with significant benefits in swelling (p = 0.0182) and eating (p = 0.004). However, these differences were minimal and not clinically significant [8]. In contrast, using a VAS, Teguh et al. found a significant reduction of pain in the mouth for the HBOT group compared to non-HBOT controls (p < 0.0001) [10]. The trial of Annane et al. indicated that the time to pain relief for patients presenting with pain at inclusion was comparable between the HBOT and placebo arms, reporting a HR of 1.00 (95% CI: 0.52–1.89; p = 0.99), which suggests no significant difference in the time to pain relief between the two treatment groups [13].

Adverse effects/events

Seven out of seventeen studies reported side effects or adverse events [11–14, 17, 19, 21]. Reported side effects and adverse events included osteoradionecrosis, temporary dyspnea, subcutaneous emphysema, reversible myopia, pulmonary oxygen toxicity, seizures (attributed to brain metastasis), and deaths due to carotid rupture, nosocomial pneumonia, or cancer relapse.

Discussion

This review of the literature on the effects of HBOT for patients with LRTTI for HNC highlights that HBOT is a reliable and safe approach. In particular, HBOT may improve outcomes related to ORN, oral health, pain, and QoL.

All but two studies [8, 13] investigating HBOT for ORN reported a positive result after HBOT [9, 17–21, 23, 24]. In addition, three studies reported significant p-values (p < 0.05) [9, 12, 24]. For oral health, all but two studies [11, 15] showed positive results after HBOT [9, 10, 12–14, 16, 17, 22]with significant p-values found in six studies [10, 12, 14–16, 22]. For QoL, only one study [10] showed a positive result and another showed no improvements [11]with significant p-values reported in the former. For pain management, only one study [10] showed positive results after HBOT with significant p-values, while the other two did not [8, 13]. Overall, 14 out of the 17 studies reported positive outcomes after HBOT. In addition, significant p-values were found in 11 of these studies. Clinical benefits, when observed, were often modest (e.g., a 19.6% improvement in dysphagia [9]). These studies reported a low incidence of adverse effects after HBOT [11–14, 17, 19, 21].

Furthermore, this review highlights improvements in oral health measures, such as xerostomia and oral microcirculation, although many of these did not reach statistical significance. This suggests that while HBOT may offer potential benefits, its effects may be subtle or patient specific. For example, Teguh et al. reported significant improvements in xerostomia, supporting the notion that HBOT may be more effective for certain symptoms, whereas Forner et al. found no statistically significant improvements [9, 10]. Similarly, studies examining oral microcirculation and histological changes reported improvements such as increased buccal mucosal capillary and lymphatic vessel density, but no significant overall recovery associated with HBOT [12, 13]. However, Cankar et al. demonstrated a significant increase in salivary flow and salivary pH following HBOT. Nevertheless, it remains uncertain whether these improvements translate into meaningful clinical benefits, particularly given that the observed increase in salivary flow was relatively modest (0.19 ml/min), and no corresponding data on symptom relief or quality of life were reported [16].

In addition, this review highlights mixed findings in the domain of pain management, with some studies demonstrating improvements, though most of these did not reach clinical significance. For instance, the HOPON trial reported slight improvements in pain, but these changes were not deemed clinically significant [8]. In contrast, Teguh et al. showed a significant reduction in VAS pain scores for the HBOT group compared to non-HBOT controls, suggesting that HBOT may be more effective in managing certain types of pain [10]. This is in accordance with earlier studies by Teguh et al., which also demonstrated positive effects of HBOT on pain reduction [25, 26]. However, Annane et al. found no significant difference in the time to pain relief between the HBOT and placebo groups, which highlights the inconsistency of HBOT’s effects on pain [13].

Limitations

Firstly, the clinical heterogeneity among the included studies limits the generalizability of this review’s findings. There was significant variability in HBOT protocols, with treatment pressures ranging from 2.4 to 2.8 ATA, session numbers from 8 to 115, and unclear or inconsistent reporting on whether HBOT was applied prophylactically or curatively, complicating direct comparisons and limiting the interpretability of treatment outcomes. Outcome measures were also inconsistent, including scales such as the VAS for pain and the Notani classification, and follow-up durations varied widely.

In addition, the predominance of low-quality studies (10 out of 17), primarily consisting of small case series and observational cohort studies, introduces a substantial risk of bias due to methodological limitations such as small sample sizes and lack of randomization. Several RCTs lacked blinding, which may have further affected the reliability of their outcomes. Notably, the early termination of the HOPON trial [8] due to unexpectedly low ORN incidence likely underpowered its results. Furthermore, many studies did not adequately control for confounding factors such as radiation dose, concurrent therapies, underlying comorbidities, or surgical characteristics (e.g., type, extent, and technique), all of which may have independently influenced treatment outcomes.

This review showed significant increase in unstimulated salivary flow and improvements in xerostomia after HBOT [9, 10, 15, 16]. In comparison, Fox et al. indicated that HBOT may improve stimulated salivary flow and xerostomia-related QoL [27]. Both reviews emphasize HBOT’s potential for managing radiation-induced overall oral health.

The findings of Vera-Rosas et al., which report long-term improvements in QoL of irradiated cancer patients (including HNC patients) through HBOT, are somewhat consistent with this review but also show important contrasts [28]. While Vera-Rosas et al. highlight significant benefits in physical function and general health, this review showed mixed outcomes. For instance, Teguh et al. found no significant QoL differences during the acute phase but noted a reduction in late side effects in the HBOT group [10]. In contrast, Schoen et al. reported no significant improvements in the EORTC QLQ-C30 scales for HBOT patients, though emotional functioning improved in the non-HBOT group [11]. Both studies recognize some positive QoL changes, but the extent and nature of improvements vary.

Annane et al. found no significant difference between HBOT and placebo [13]. However, Teguh et al. reported a significant reduction in pain with HBOT. In contrast, both Philipsen et al. and Ravi et al. both highlighted more substantial pain relief with HBOT, especially for severe radiation injuries, which is more consistent with Teguh’s findings than with the minimal improvements seen in the HOPON trial and the study by Annane et al. [8, 10, 13, 29, 30].

Implications for practice

Based on this review, there is evidence indicating that HBOT may offer benefits in the treatment of LRTTI in HNC patients. Improvements were observed in outcomes such as ORN, salivary flow, and xerostomia, although not all findings were statistically significant. However, due to the variability of results and the limited number of high-quality randomized controlled trials, further research is needed to establish clear clinical guidelines and identify the patient groups that would benefit the most.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this review suggests that HBOT may offer potential benefits in specific LRTTI outcomes, such as ORN healing and xerostomia. However, the evidence supporting these conclusions comes primarily from low-quality studies, with a high risk of bias in the majority of the studies. In addition, the limited sample sizes and lack of consistency in outcome measures across the studies further limit the reliability of these findings. Therefore, while HBOT shows promise, further high-quality research, including larger RCTs with long-term follow-up, standardized HBOT protocols, and patient stratification by LRTTI severity, is essential to determine its clinical relevance and optimal use in HNC patients.
