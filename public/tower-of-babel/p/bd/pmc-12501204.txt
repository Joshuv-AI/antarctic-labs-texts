Action painting under spectroscopic light: Excited-state exchange interactions behind the vibrant blue in Jackson Pollock’s Number 1A, 1948
Authors: Alexander J. Heyer, Abed Haddad, Racquel Scott, Glen Kowach, Edward I. Solomon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceNumber 1A, 1948 by Jackson Pollock is one of his most iconic action paintings; however, its vibrant blue pigment has remained unassigned. In this study, this pigment is assigned as manganese blue. This assignment offers critical context for conserving his work. This assignment also enables spectroscopic determination of the origin of this vibrant blue color. Spectroscopic techniques were coupled to density functional theory calculations to uncover the chemical origin of manganese blue’s pure hue: Excited-state exchange interactions create multiple absorption features that filter nonblue light. This highlights the ability of molecular inorganic pigments to leverage ligand field effects to create multiple visible absorption features and lattice electrostatics to fine-tune the color.

Manganese blue’s vivid and distinct color gave it widespread use in the 20th century (1). It has been identified across different continents and cultures, including the works of several Soviet-era artists like Konstantin Dorokhov (1906–1960), Sergei Gerasimov (1885–1964), Lado Gudiashvili (1896–1980), and Alexandr Tyshler (1898–1980) (2), as well as Chilean artist Violeta Parra (1917–1967) (3). Additionally, it was recommended in the 1980s as a nonmetameric match for azurite, providing an excellent substitute in restoration projects due to its color stability (4). However, by the 1990s, the pigment was phased out due to regulatory pressure from the German government, which sought to reduce the environmental and health risks associated with the manufacturing process (4). Today, the pigment is rarely available commercially; however, its unique color is still desirable with a phthalocyanine-based substitute often labeled as “manganese blue hue.”

Manganese blue, or barium manganate(VI) sulfate (xBaSO4·yBaMnO4), is typically prepared as first patented in 1935 by IG Farbenindustrie AG through calcination of sodium sulfate, potassium permanganate, and barium nitrate at 750 to 800°C in air (1, 5). While it has been misattributed in the past as barium manganate (or barium manganese oxide), it has since been reported to consist of manganate centers distributed throughout a barium sulfate lattice with a manganate:sulfate ratio of ∼1:9 (4).

The combination of manganese blue’s widespread use, its lack of commercial availability, its vivid blue hue, and its potential for use in laser technology (6, 7) has sparked interest in the chemical nature behind the color center. Brunold and Güdel collected low-temperature, polarized absorption spectra on single crystals of the pigment, elucidating the polarization of the charge transfer transitions (8). Similar charge transfer bands have been observed for manganate doped into other materials (9). Additionally, Accorsi et al. collected reflection data on the pigment with corresponding time-dependent density functional theory calculations to predict the shape and intensity of the spectrum (10); density functional theory calculations were also performed by Birke on the manganate center (11). These studies have identified the energies of the charge transfer bands that determine the color of the pigment and indicated density functional theory can model the spectral shape; however, the chemical nature of the charge transfer bands has not been fully elucidated.

In this study, magnetic circular dichroism and resonance Raman spectroscopies are performed on manganese blue to elucidate the nature of the charge transfer transitions that determine its color. These data are coupled to density functional theory calculations to assign the charge transfer bands and the vibrations of the manganate color center. The pigment has two charge transfer bands that determine its blue color; this study demonstrates these are split in energy by excited-state electron–electron exchange interactions. It is the energy gap between these two charge transfer bands that gives the pigment its distinct blue hue. This illuminates the unique ability of inorganic pigments to leverage ligand field effects on charge transfer excited states to create multiple intense bands that flank a color of interest, creating a uniquely pure hue.

Further, assignment of these bands allows evaluation of the charge transfer energy in the absence of excited-state exchange interactions, which determines the location of the ligand to dπ charge transfer transition energy gap. By comparing manganate doped into different lattices, the effects of lattice electrostatics on this charge transfer energy is elucidated. Different lattice hosts for the manganate center can tune the charge transfer energy gap associated with the state splitting and, thus, the color of the pigment. This uncovers a second distinct advantage of inorganic pigments: the ability to synthesize new pure hues by tuning the color center using lattice electrostatics.

The significance of this pigment stimulates interest in identifying manganese blue in modern paintings. Manganese blue has clear signals in false-color infrared photography, luminescence imaging, and X-ray fluorescence spectrometry (XRF); however, these signals are not definitive and can also reflect other pigments, fillers, or driers in the medium. Using microsamples, manganese blue can be identified with infrared spectroscopy, but the presence of other materials, such as inorganic fillers and paint binders, can make spectral interpretation challenging due to overlapping signals with other compounds in the material. Polarized light microscopy has also been used (12), although this technique is far from a fingerprint identification of the pigment.

The use of large area micro-XRF scanning has engendered speculation about the use of manganese blue in Jackson Pollock’s (1912–1956) Number 1A, 1948 (1948) (Fig. 1), one of the earliest examples of action painting by the artist. Martins et al. speculated the use of manganese blue in this painting as XRF scanning revealed the presence of barium, sulfur, and manganese in the turquoise of the painting (13); however, barium and sulfur could indicate the presence of a barium sulfate filler, and manganese salts are commonly used driers for oil-based paints. Moreover, the use of pigments undetectable by XRF, like ultramarine or synthetic organics, could have given the paint its blue hue. This study extends the assignment of the vibrations of manganese blue from resonance Raman spectroscopy to definitively confirm Pollock’s decision to use manganese blue paint in Number 1A, 1948. While Pollock’s palette from this period has been shown to contain a variety of blues (13–16), here we confirm the use of manganese blue by the artist. This knowledge is necessary for authenticating future works by the artist and for restoration efforts of Pollock’s works. This also provides a convenient handle to identify manganese blue in paintings broadly.

The assignment of manganese blue in Number 1A, 1948 allows deeper comprehension of the work as a whole. Previous research by MoMA identified cadmium yellow and cadmium red in Number 1A, 1948 (13, 17); these semiconductor pigments have deep hues as a result of band gaps that absorb all visible light except the color of interest. Manganese blue uses localized charge transfer excited states that filter light at lower and higher energy than the blue of interest, filtering light outside the blue to reflect a pure hue. We show this energy filter reflects the excited-state exchange interactions that give manganese blue its vibrant color in one of Pollock’s most iconic action paintings.

Results and Analysis

Electronic Spectroscopy.

Resolution of polarized single crystal absorption using low temperature magnetic circular dichroism.

Understanding the color of manganese blue requires chemical insight into the visible absorption features of its color center. Manganese blue is synthesized through a high-temperature, high pH flux reaction under oxygen flow of Ba2+, [SO4]2−, and [MnO4]−. At high temperatures, [MnO4]− decomposes to [MnO4]2−, MnO2, and oxygen gas; the MnO2 formed can further react with oxygen and hydroxide to form water and additional [MnO4]2−. The [MnO4]2− produced replace [SO4]2− centers in a BaSO4 lattice, synthesizing the vibrant blue pigment. Brunold and Güdel pioneered work on this pigment by collecting polarized absorption spectra on single crystals at 10 K (8). The unpolarized spectrum is obtained by averaging these spectra (Fig. 2A). These spectra revealed a vibronic progression peaking around 18,000 cm−1, a second band centered near 24,000 cm−1, and overlapping intensity with some vibronic structure from ∼27,000 cm−1 onward. Although the absorption shows two clear bands at low energy (at ∼18,000 and 25,000 cm−1), the intensity above 25,000 cm−1 can be fit to one or two bands.

(A) Unpolarized absorption collected at 10 K and (B) MCD collected at 5 K and 7 T. Simultaneous Gaussian fit indicated with filled curves.

To resolve the overlapping bands, low temperature magnetic circular dichroism (MCD) spectroscopy was performed on a mull of the powdered pigment (Fig. 2B). MCD is a powerful technique for this application for two reasons: Features have different intensities due to different selection rules and features can be positive or negative (18). Simultaneous Gaussian fitting of the absorption and MCD spectra enables determination of the band energies and widths in manganese blue. All MCD features observed were temperature-dependent, consistent with a paramagnet, the S =12 manganate center (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Below 16,500cm−1, the absorption intensity is low (<35 M−1cm−1) while the MCD is intense. In contrast, above 16,500 cm−1, absorption bands become 27× more intense while the MCD intensity is relatively low. Because MCD intensity derives from spin–orbit coupling (18–20), the more metal-centered the transition, the more intense the MCD relative to the absorption intensity (the C0/D0 ratio) (18, 19). Thus, the bands below 16,500 cm−1 are assigned as LF transitions, and the bands above 16,500 cm−1 are assigned as CT transitions.

Simultaneous fitting of absorption and MCD spectra, maintaining the same νmax and full width at half maximum (FWHM) for each transition, reveals four distinct CT bands at 18,040, 24,750, 27,450, and 30,600 cm−1 (Fig. 2 A and B and Table 1). Thus, by using MCD, the overlapping intensity above 22,500 cm−1 can be resolved into two distinct bands. In the visible region of the spectrum, Band I absorbs from orange to light blue, while Band II absorbs from indigo into the UV. Thus, understanding the chemical nature behind these two band energies, in particular, would provide insight into the electronic structure origin of the vibrant blue color of manganese blue. Although Band I has minimal overlap with other bands, Band II significantly overlaps the higher-energy CT region. To verify our fit of Band II, a Gaussian fit was performed on the z-polarized spectrum from ref. 8 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 and Table S1) as it has little contribution from the higher energy CT bands that are only xy-polarized; this fit does not change the νmax and FWHM of Band II.

Resonance Raman.

To better understand the nature of these CT bands, resonance Raman (rR) profiles were collected for manganese blue. By selectively enhancing Raman-active vibrational modes associated with specific electronic transitions, rR both assigns the vibrational normal modes and provides insight into the excited-state distortions underlying each CT band, enabling their assignment (21). rR was previously attempted on manganate doped into K2[CrO4], but only on the first CT band, limiting analysis (22). The rR spectrum of manganese blue reveals four clear vibrations distinct from the barium sulfate host: two weak, broad transitions at 316 and 355 cm−1, one strong, broad peak at 793 cm−1, and a sharp feature at 1,057 cm−1 (Fig. 3A). These bands can be assigned using the nearby vibrations of the sulfate centers. The low energy features are near the energy of the Raman-allowed sulfate E bends at 452 and 461 cm−1, assigning them as the [MnO4]2− E modes (Fig. 3B). These modes have also been observed in the ground and LF excited states for manganate in vibronic progressions (8, 23). The manganate intense rR feature at 793 cm−1, is equivalent to the intense A1 feature of the sulfate at 988 cm−1 (Fig. 3B). The energies for the E and A1 modes for the [MnO4]2− in manganese blue are similar to those reported for manganate in aqueous solution (24). The 1,057 cm−1 feature has a similar shape and energy to the sulfate A1 mode but is blue-shifted. This feature will be assigned in Calculated vibrations.

(A) Resonance Raman spectra of barium sulfate (black) and manganese blue (blue) collected at 458 nm. (B) Vibrational modes assigned for manganese blue from DFT calculated energies.

The intensities of these features were profiled across a series of wavelengths that span the first three CT bands (Fig. 4 and SI Appendix, Fig. S3). The 316 and 355 cm−1 E vibrations profile together and are enhanced in Band I while losing intensity scanning into Bands II & III. The 793 cm−1 A1 feature has significant intensity in Band I, dips to low intensity in Band II, and then grows to its highest intensity in Band III. The 1,057 cm−1 does not show resonance enhancement as it exhibits similar intensity across all charge transfer bands (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). This indicates that the 1,057 cm−1 feature does not directly stem from the manganate center but is related to its presence in the lattice.

Density Functional Theory.

Geometry optimization and one electron energy levels.

To identify the bands observed by absorption, MCD, and rR profiles (Figs. 2 and 4), density functional theory (DFT) calculations were used to model electronic excitations of the manganate center and understand its resonance-enhanced normal modes. To build a computation model, powder X-ray diffraction (pXRD) data were collected on manganese blue to generate a starting structure (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). pXRD predominantly observes the sulfate centers as these are ∼90% of the Td centers in the material. Based on this crystal structure, a cluster model was built by replacing the central sulfate with manganate, preserving the first sphere of barium cations and sulfate anions, and then capping the edge of the sulfate sphere with protons for charge balance (expanded in SI Appendix, Materials and Methods).

Subsequently, this model was optimized using the B3LYP functional. The resulting manganate is distorted from Td to approximately C2v with one wide ∠OMnO of 121° and one narrow ∠OMnO of 106° opposite the 121° with the z-axis bisecting both (Fig. 5). The wide angle has Mn-O bond lengths of 1.61 Å while the narrow angle has bond lengths of 1.64 Å.

DFT optimized cluster model of manganese blue with the manganate center highlighted. (Color scheme: gray = H, red = O, purple = Mn, green = Ba, and yellow = S).

Calculations were also performed on the isolated C2v manganate core from above with the barium cations and sulfate anions removed to eliminate artifacts from the extended lattice. Several functionals were utilized (vide infra), all resulting in the same relative orbital energies. The d1 Mn(VI) center has its unpaired α electron in the 3dz2 orbital of the e set (in Td), due to the wide ∠OMnO bend into the xy plane, raising the dxy energy (Fig. 6A). This ground state is consistent with electron paramagnetic resonance data (25, 26). In the α manifold, the next lowest energy virtual orbitals above the dxy are the t2 dσ* set (dx2−y2, dxz, dyz). The highest energy-occupied ligand orbitals are the oxo t1 orbitals that are nonbonding with respect to the Mn s-, p-, and d-orbitals. Next, below those are the bonding oxo t2 orbitals.

In the β manifold, the ligand oxo orbitals and the metal d-orbitals are shifted up in energy due to differences in exchange interactions. Also, the unoccupied dxy and dz2 orbitals are reversed in energetic order as the β dz2 is raised due to e−–e− repulsion of adding a second negative charge into the dz2 orbital.

Time-dependent density functional theory.

To calculate the electronic transitions in the absorption and MCD spectra, time-dependent DFT (TD-DFT) calculations were performed on the isolated manganate core. Three functionals were enlisted with varying levels of Hartree–Fock (HF) exchange: BP86 (0% HF, Fig. 6 and SI Appendix, Fig. S6), CAM-B3LYP (gradient of HF exchange, SI Appendix, Fig. S7), and BHandHLYP (50% HF, SI Appendix, Fig. S8). Using the BP86 functional (Fig. 6B), the LF transitions lie at the lowest average energy with the first transition involving excitation within the e dπ manifold by promoting the unpaired α electron in the dz2 orbital into the dxy orbital. This dπ → dπ transition is formally forbidden in both Td and C2v symmetry and appears ∼1,100 cm−1, well below the window of the UV-vis data. The other three LF transitions (dπ→ dσ) lie at higher energies and involve excitation from dz2 into the t2 dσ orbitals. These dπ→ dσ transitions are formally allowed in both Td and C2v symmetry, appearing from 14,400 to 17,700 cm−1. At higher energy, the first and second charge transfer transitions are excitations of the α and β electrons, respectively, from the nonbonding oxo t1 orbitals into the dxy orbital. The oxo t1 has three components; this splits excitation into the dxy orbital into x-, y-, and z-polarized transitions (Fig. 6B). The α transitions occur from 17,200 to 19,500 cm−1 while the β transitions are blue shifted to 20,800 to 25,800 cm−1. This assigns Bands I & II as the α oxo t1→ dxy and β oxo t1→ dxy transitions, respectively. This ∼4,500 cm−1 gap between Bands I & II reflects the larger β t1-dxy energy gap than the α analogue (Fig. 6A).

Bands I & II have been observed by UV-vis spectroscopy for manganate in aqueous solution at 16,529 and 22,989 cm−1, respectively (27). To test the accuracy of the DFT model, TD-DFT calculations were performed on manganate in a water PCM with the three α oxo t1→ dxy transitions averaged and the three β oxo t1→ dxy transitions averaged to calculate Bands I & II, respectively. The water PCM model predicts the experimental features fairly well with Bands I and II appearing 1,617 cm−1 and 305 cm−1 higher in energy than observed experimentally.

Unlike for Bands I and II, the TD-DFT assignment of the third and fourth bands is ambiguous. There are calculated transitions close in energy for two excitations: a slightly lower energy β excitation from the oxo t1 nonbonding orbitals into the dz2 and a slightly higher energy excitation from the α oxo bonding t2 orbitals into dxy, both occurring in the range 26,800 to 30,200 cm−1. These are both xy-polarized, matching the experimental spectra (Fig. 6B). At higher energy, there are transitions from deeper bonding orbitals (e.g. the σ-bonding e and a1 orbitals) as well as transitions from the t1 nonbonding ligand orbitals into the t2 dσ*-orbitals (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Including HF exchange using the CAM-B3LYP functional (SI Appendix, Fig. S7) keeps the same assignment for Bands I and II but swaps the order of the third and fourth bands (SI Appendix, Explanation S1). Thus, TD-DFT alone cannot assign the third and fourth bands; however, rR provides insight into their assignment (vide infra).

Calculated vibrations.

TD-DFT can be combined with calculated vibrations to understand the resonance profiles of the electronic transitions of the active site (Fig. 4). DFT calculates the energy of the barium sulfate E and A1 modes at 433, 466, and 943 cm−1, in reasonable agreement with the vibrations observed experimentally at 452, 461, and 988 cm−1 (SI Appendix, Table S2). For the manganate vibrations of manganese blue, using the lattice model (Fig. 5), DFT calculates the manganate E modes at 362 and 398 cm−1 and the A1 mode at 906 cm−1 (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Table S2). The calculated E modes are close but blue-shifted from the experimental values at 316 and 355 cm−1. The calculated A1 mode at 906 cm−1 is significantly higher than the experimentally observed 793 cm−1 vibration. This overly high value is due to interactions with the barium sites in the lattice that restrict the motion of two manganate oxygen atoms and create excessive mixing with high energy T2 stretches in the models used for the DFT calculations compared to the A1 mode observed experimentally. For the Td manganate model in vacuum, without the barium cations, the A1 value is closer to the experimental value at 867 cm−1 (SI Appendix, Table S2). Thus, the Td manganate model was used in the resonance profile calculations (vide infra).

The DFT-calculated vibrations of the lattice model (Fig. 5) also provide insight into the nonresonance enhanced feature observed experimentally at 1,057 cm−1 (Fig. 3A). Experimentally, this vibration has the same shape as the sulfate A1 mode at 989 cm−1 but is blue-shifted by ∼70 cm−1. DFT calculates the barium sulfate A1 mode at 943 cm−1 but calculates the A1 mode of the sulfates closest to the manganate center at 1,031 cm−1. The inclusion of the manganate center shifts the barium cations toward the manganate and away from the sulfate, increasing the SO-Ba distance while decreasing the S-O bond length from 1.51 to 1.48 Å.

Resonance profiles.

Insight into the resonance profiles for the [MnO4]2− A1 and E modes (Fig. 4) can be gained by combining the calculated vibrations with the TD-DFT transitions. The available laser line energies correspond to Bands I, II, and III, but Band II exhibits a 27% lower absorption intensity. As rR intensity is proportional to ϵ2, this results in an approximately 50% reduction in intensity. Therefore, Bands I and III, with similar absorption intensities, are the focus of this analysis.

The extent of A-term resonance enhancement along a vibrational mode can be calculated using a linear coupling term (LCT) approach (28, 29). Here, the slope, and thus the distorting force, of the excited state relative to the ground state is mapped by calculating the change in the TD-DFT transition energy with distortion along the normal mode. The LCTs for the A1 mode of Td manganate are displayed in Fig. 7A for the four calculated transitions. These slopes are normalized to the largest value, the α oxo t2→ dxy transition. Of the donor orbitals, t2 is the only oxo bonding orbital (Fig. 7B). Thus, the t2 excitation is the only bonding to antibonding transition of the four, creating the largest A1 distortion along the Mn-O bonds (blue in Fig. 7A). The three other transitions are all excitations from the oxo t1 nonbonding orbital, and therefore, excited state distortions are a result of the antibonding acceptor orbitals. The α dxy orbital is the most antibonding with 40% O-character, the β dxy has 37% O-character, and the β dz2 is the least with 30% O-character (Fig. 7B). The trend in decreasing antibonding character of the acceptor orbital reflects the continued calculated decrease in LCTs (−0.96 to −0.66 to −0.59 in Fig. 7A).

(A) Linear coupling terms for the A1 mode of the Td manganate in four CT excitation states. (B) Donor/acceptor orbitals for Bands I-IV.

The manganate significantly distorts along the A1 mode in Bands I & III (Fig. 4), matching the high resonance-enhancement observed for the α oxo t1 and t2→ dxy transitions. Thus, this resonance profile resolves the ambiguity of the assignments of Bands III and IV; Band III is assigned to the α oxo t2→ dxy transition, thus Band IV is the remaining β oxo t1 to dz2 transition (i.e. blue vs. orange slopes in Fig. 7A).

This assignment is consistent with the resonance profile of the E modes that are enhanced in Band I but not Band III. The ground state of [MnO4]2− is 2E in Td symmetry, and the E modes are Jahn–Teller (JT) active (Fig. 8, Left). Thus, the DFT optimized structure of the manganate center in vacuum distorts along the Qx (Fig. 3B) partner of the E mode, widening the ∠OMnO from 109.5° to 110.8° (Fig. 7 C and D, Middle). Band I involves α excitation from the nonbonding t1 orbital into the e manifold, creating a 3A2 parent state on the metal (vide infra) and a hole in the nonbonding t1 ligand set (Fig. 8, Middle). Alternatively, Band III instead involves α excitation from the bonding t2 orbital into the e manifold, creating the same 3A2 parent state on the metal but now with a hole in the bonding t2 ligand set (Fig. 8, Right). To calculate the JT distorting energies for the 3A2 parent state, the t1 hole, and the t2 hole, several small DFT models were generated. For the 3A2 state, one α electron was added to the manganate to generate [MnO4]3− in the S = 1 state. For the t1 ligand hole, two electrons were removed from manganate to have an overall neutral [MnO4]; this is d0 with one electron removed from the t1 orbital. Finally, to evaluate the t2 hole, a TD-DFT calculation was performed on the neutral manganate to excite from the t2 bonding orbital to the t1 nonbonding hole.

Orbital occupancies (i.e. configurations) and corresponding electronic states for the ground state of manganese blue (Left) and its charge transfer excited states (Middle and Right), where color states at the Top are for the e2 configuration on the manganese.

The 3A2 state [MnO4]3− model is not JT active and distorts to tetrahedral from the [MnO4]2−2E ground state (∠OMnO = 110.8°) with a distorting energy of 41 cm−1. The t1 hole neutral [MnO4] model is weakly JT active as the electron hole is in the nonbonding t1 set. This structure optimizes to a ∠OMnO of 110.9° with a weak distorting energy of 0.3 cm−1 from the 2E ground state structure. This is in contrast to the t2 hole model with the hole in the bonding orbital. This creates a large distorting energy of 31 cm−1 toward ∠OMnO = 111.9°. Thus, in Band I, the energetic restoring force of the 3A2 parent state out competes the weak distorting force of the t1 hole; together these restore the structure toward tetrahedral (Fig. 9A), resulting in resonance enhancement. This is contrast to Band III where the 3A2 state’s restoring force is canceled out by the opposing distorting force of the t2 hole (Fig. 9B), creating little overall structural change in the excited state and no observed resonance enhancement.

Multielectron State Analysis and Lattice Tuning of Chromophore.

Multielectron state analysis.

With the band assignments in hand, the CT band energies can be analyzed to understand the origin of the pigment’s color. The CT transitions involve multielectron excited states, whereas TD-DFT is a single-determinant-based method. Therefore, analyzing the transition energies in terms of their multielectron excited states is required for accurate insight. In Td, the ground state is 2E with the single unpaired electron in the e set (Fig. 8, Left). The visible energy CT transitions in Fig. 2 involve oxo ligand 2p excitation into the metal-based e set, creating an e2 configuration with three corresponding parent states: 3A2, 1E, and 1A1 (Fig. 8, Middle and Right). This splitting of the e2 configuration parallels the splitting of the π*2 configuration observed in O2, giving a paramagnetic 3Σg− ground state and 1Δg and 1Σg+ excited states. The oxo t1→ dπ transitions leave a hole in the nonbonding oxo t1 orbitals with little electron exchange coupling with the e2 parent states. Thus, the oxo t1→ dπ CT band splittings (Fig. 8, Middle) can be analyzed in terms of the metal-based parent state energy splittings.

Wavefunctions for these e2 parent states are (SI Appendix, Explanation S2):[1]A23=12[xy(1)z2(2)−z2(1)xy(2)][α(1)α(2)]E#11=12[xy(1)z2(2)+z2(1)xy(2)][α(1)β(2)−β(1)α(2)]E#21=12[xy(1)xy(2)−z2(1)z2(2)][α(1)β(2)−β(1)α(2)]A11=12[xy(1)xy(2)+z2(1)z2(2)][α(1)β(2)−β(1)α(2)]

The 3A2 wavefunction has an α electron in both dπ e orbitals, thus it is described well by the α excitation that generated Band I (also Band III for an α t2 CT transition) (Table 1). The 1E wavefunction has two components. E#1 has simultaneous occupation of both orbitals, but with opposite spins, which is calculated by the β excitation into the empty dxy orbital assigned for Band II. Though the energy of the 1E is reasonably calculated by this transition, the 1E#2 and the 1A1 states involve symmetric and antisymmetric combinations of the doubly occupied dxy and dz2 orbitals. Thus, the energy of the 1A1 state is approximated by β excitation into the semioccupied dz2 orbital and is connected to Band IV. The multideterminant nature of the 1A1 and 1E#2 emphasize the limitations of TD-DFT and the importance of multielectron state analysis.

The relative energies of these states are expressed in terms of the coulomb and exchange integrals of electrons in the dxy and dz2 orbitals (SI Appendix, Explanation S3). This provides insight into the electronic origin of the excited-state splitting:[2]E1E−E3A2=2K(xy,z2)E1A1−E1E=2K(xy,z2)

Thus, the e2 parent states are evenly split by 2K(xy,z2). This matches the TD-DFT assignment of the three oxo t1 CT bands observed in the absorption and MCD spectra where Bands I & II (3A2 & 1E) are split by 6,360 cm−1 while Bands II & IV (1E & 1A1) are split by 6,200 cm−1. Thus, our band assignments obtained by combining spectroscopy and DFT calculations verify this model that the CT bands are split due to the e− repulsion within the e2 parent excited state. Specifically, these bands are split due to excited-state electron exchange interactions (Eq. 2).

The degeneracy-weighted average energy of these states gives the oxo t1→ dπ CT energy in the absence of excited-state exchange (Δ). Because the parent states are evenly split by 2K(xy,z2), Δ can be expressed as a function of the energy of the 1E transition and the exchange interaction (SI Appendix, Explanation S4):[3]Δ=3 ∗ E1E − 2K(xy, z2)3

Using this equation, the Δ of manganese blue is 22,280 cm−1 (Table 3). Thus, the band energies of the two chromophoric CT bands that create the vivid color of manganese blue are defined by 2K(xy,z2) and Δ.

Lattice tuning of chromophore.

Understanding the origin of the color of manganese blue enables extension to other manganate-doped materials. Specifically, rather than the blue observed from [MnO4]2− doped into BaSO4, [MnO4]2− doped into Cs2SO4 exhibits a bright green color, a manganese green material (23). The unpolarized low temperature spectrum of manganese green has been reported and is compared to manganese blue’s in Fig. 10. Both its first and second CT bands are red-shifted by ∼1,200 cm−1 compared to manganese blue. 2K(xy,z2) and Δ were calculated for manganese green as described above and compared to manganese blue (Table 3). The exchange splitting is similar between the two (6,360 vs. 6,230 cm−1) while Δ is red-shifted in manganese green by ∼1,000 cm−1, red-shifting the overall color of the pigment from blue to green. When the entire manganese green UV-vis spectrum is blue-shifted by this difference in Δ, Bands I, II, and IV align very well between the two pigments (SI Appendix, Fig. S9) while Band III does not overlap, further supporting our band assignment (vide supra). Manganese blue and manganese green are further compared to manganate in alkaline aqueous solution where Bands I & II were red-shifted by ∼500 cm−1 compared to manganese green, keeping a relatively similar 2K(xy,z2) while lowering Δ to 20,836 cm−1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 and Table 3) (27). Thus, excitation of nonbonding electrons from the oxo to the Mn results in two CT bands in the visible region split by ∼6,000 cm−1 while the lattice host shifts the energies of these bands to tune the color. The origin of the lattice shift in Δ can be understood in terms of lattice electrostatics as explored in Discussion.

Assignment of manganese blue in Jackson Pollock’s Number 1A, 1948.

In previous research, an artist’s oil paint that contains manganese blue was speculated to have been used by Jackson Pollock in Number 1A, 1948 (13). As a result, Raman spectroscopy was used to decisively identify this pigment. Initially a higher energy laser line (532 nm) was used on Number 1A, 1948 to take advantage of resonance enhancement in Band I (vide supra); however, the large fluorescence background from the oil-based binding medium prevented successful collection. Thus, nonresonance Raman with a lower energy line of 785 nm was used. This line still induces a large fluorescence background (Fig. 11) but with clear and discernible vibrations that are also present for the manganate center of manganese blue at 319, 355, and 795 cm−1 (the features at 451, 462, and 989 cm−1 are the SO42− modes). Within error these energies correspond to the resonance-enhanced Raman features of manganese blue in Fig. 3A. This provides a convenient handle for future identification of manganese blue in other works.

Discussion

Pollock’s use of tube paints directly on the surface of his dripped and poured action paintings is well documented. As early as 1940, Pollock was squeezing tubes of tempera directly onto the canvas without using brushes (30). He brought this technique into some of the earliest of his action paintings, including Number 1A, 1948, where his choice of bright primary colors punctuated the intricate composition with what critic Michael Fried called “hovering spots of bright color [that] provide momentary points of focus (31).” Previous work by MoMA has identified two of these bright colors: a solid solution of cadmium zinc sulfide (Cd1−xZnxS) as the red and a solid solution of cadmium selenide sulfide (CdS1−xSex) as the yellow (Fig. 12, Left) (13, 17). However, the bright blue was yet to be confirmed. In the present study, vibrations observed by Raman and resonance Raman spectroscopies are used to assign this blue in Number 1A, 1948 as manganese blue. While blue pigments including ultramarine and phthalocyanine blue, and to a lesser extent cerulean blue, cobalt blue, and Prussian blue, have been identified in Pollock’s oeuvre, now this palette includes manganese blue. This finding raises new questions about whether this was an isolated instance or whether Pollock used it in other works. It also provides essential information for future restoration efforts. Interestingly, Pollock’s contemporary Willem de Kooning appeared to use manganese blue well into the 20th century, with a reporter describing him as “taking a tube of oil and squeezing a curve of manganese blue across the canvas” (32). Manganese blue’s specific hue appears to have been favored by action painters, which makes it insightful to understand the molecular phenomenon that gives this pigment its attractive hue.

Pigments absorb strongly in the visible region through π→π∗ transitions of extended aromatics, band gap transitions in semiconductors, and the CT bands of metal complexes (33). The energy position and width of these bands determine the light absorbed, and thus, the remaining reflected light observed. The organic dye indigo for example absorbs low energy red and orange light at ∼16,230 cm−1 and in the UV at ∼28,600 cm−1, reflecting the blue and purple light that gives it its characteristic color (34). Indigo also illustrates the requirement for creating pure blue pigments: indigo’s low energy absorption must be paired with a high energy transition ∼25,000 cm−1 that filters out violet light.

Manganese blue satisfies this requirement through excited-state exchange interactions. In the absence of exchange interactions, manganese blue would have a single visible absorption band, the oxo t1→ Mn CT at 22,280 cm−1, absorbing blue light and reflecting bright orange light similar to carotenoid pigments (35). However, metal-centered excited-state exchange interactions split this transition into three: 2E →3A2, 1E, and 1A1. This peak splitting reflects the difference in e−–e− repulsion for the two electrons exchanging within the e(xy,z2) set. This splitting results in two strong absorption bands in the visible: one in the green (excitation to 3A2) and another in the purple (excitation to 1E). The pigment’s blue hue lies in the valley between these two bands (Fig. 12, Right). The strong absorption in the green is essential given the high sensitivity of human vision to green light (36).

Manganese blue accomplishes a difficult task: creating clean hues from colors in the center of the visible spectrum. Creating clean hues at the edge of the visible spectrum is an easier task with semiconductor pigments, like cadmium red, absorbing everything above the red using a well-placed band gap (37), creating a pure red hue. The challenge with a color like blue is the requirement to have two strong absorption features with a gap between them to reflect the desired hue. Semiconductor pigments struggle to accomplish this with their single band gap, and organic pigments often rely on a single π→π* transition. Manganese blue demonstrates the power of molecular inorganic chromophores. By utilizing multielectron charge transfer excited states, inorganic color centers like manganate can split a single transition into the two necessary absorption features in the visible region. Specifically for manganate, the 6,000 cm−1 splitting due to dπ electron exchange interactions allows proper flanking of a desired pure hue.

This raises the idea of tuning manganate’s (or other metal chromophores) excited-state exchange interactions to create new color pigments. For manganese green where manganate is instead doped into Cs2SO4, the CT energy (Δ) red shifts while maintaining the same exchange interaction 2K(xy,z2), and thus maintaining the energy splitting between the two visible bands (Fig. 10). The crystal structures of the lattice hosts BaSO4 (manganese blue) and Cs2SO4 (manganese green) contain sulfate centers with similar geometries (SI Appendix, Fig. S11); however the nearby cations are significantly closer in the BaSO4 lattice vs. the Cs2SO4 lattice (∼2.85 Å vs. ∼3.27 Å O-Cation distance) and of larger charge. Thus, to probe the effect of lattice electrostatics on the manganate centers two small lattice models were built for manganese blue and manganese green (SI Appendix, Fig. S12 and Explanation S5). Manganate in a water PCM was used as a reference point for lattice effects. Upon shifting the manganate from the PCM into the BaSO4 lattice, the TD-DFT predicted transitions for Bands I & II blue-shift by 2,700 to 3,800 cm−1; however, when manganate is placed in the Cs2SO4 lattice, the bands only blue-shift by 250 to 430 cm−1 (Table 4). Thus, the closer divalent cations in the BaSO4 lattice interact with the oxides significantly more than the more distant monovalent cations interact with the oxides in Cs2SO4, tuning the color into the blue. Thus, for an inorganic color center, lattice electrostatics can provide a parameter to tune in synthesizing inorganic pigments. Additionally, the lattice plays an important role in preserving the integrity of the pigment. Manganate is known to disproportionate in solution to permanganate and manganese dioxide. The stable barium sulfate and cesium sulfate lattices isolate the manganate centers, preventing disproportionation, and thus preserving the integrity of the color over time.

The artist Lee Krasner, Pollocks’s wife and ardent champion, recalled that while that artist was not overly descriptive in his decision-making at times, color remained, as she described, “a very personal thing” where Pollock ultimately chose “what he wanted” (38). While Pollock was not aware of the underlying excited-state exchange interactions that produce manganese blue’s unique hue, they nonetheless created the color that he intuitively chose to create the dynamic contrast and depth of Number 1A, 1948.
