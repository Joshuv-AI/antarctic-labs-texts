Clinical and radiologic outcomes of minimally invasive locking plate versus screw fixation for displaced intra-articular calcaneal fractures (DIACFs) via sinus tarsi approach: a comparative study
Authors: Yu-Jui Chang, Chi-Hsiang Hsu, Chien-Chang Liao, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yu-Der Lu, Shan-Ling Hsu, Jen-Hung Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe optimal surgical treatment approach for calcaneal fractures remains controversial. This retrospective comparative study aimed to compare the clinical and radiological outcomes of minimally invasive locking plate fixation with those of screw fixation for displaced intra-articular calcaneus fractures (DIACFs) treated via the sinus tarsi approach (STA).MethodsA total of 38 patients and 40 feet with DIACFs from 2015 to 2020 were retrospectively reviewed with a minimum of 3 years of follow-up. Patients were divided into two groups based on fixation method: minimally invasive locking plate fixation (MIP group) versus screw fixation (SW group) (17 vs 23, respectively). The clinical outcomes were evaluated by the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), Maryland Foot Score (MFS), Olerud-Molander (OM) ankle score, American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) hindfoot score, and occurrence of complications. Radiological outcomes were assessed by radiographs for multiple parameters, including calcaneal height, length, width, and Böhler’s angle.ResultsThe patients were followed up for an average of 63.37 months. There was no significant difference in the final VAS score, MFS, OM score, AOFAS score, and the total incidence of complications between the two groups (P>0.05). Regarding the radiological outcome, there was also no significant difference in the reduction loss of calcaneal height, length, width, and Böhler’s angle (P>0.05) after a one-year follow-up period. However, the rate of implant removal in the MIP group was significantly greater than that in the SW group (P< 0.05).ConclusionsMinimally invasive locking plate fixation versus screw fixation of DIACFs via sinus tarsi approach may yield comparable radiological and functional outcomes. The screw fixation group suggested a shorter surgical time and lower implant removal rate. Both surgical methods are viable options for DIACFs. However, further large-scale prospective studies are warranted to confirm these results.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03208-w.Keywords: Displaced intra-articular calcaneus fracture, Sinus tarsi approach, Minimally invasive

Introduction

Calcaneal fractures represent the most common tarsal bone injuries and account for approximately 1–2% of all fractures in clinical practice [1]. Approximately 75% of calcaneal fractures are intra-articular fractures [2]. The gold standard treatment for Sanders [3] type II and III displaced intra-articular calcaneus fractures (DIACFs) is open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) via the extensile lateral approach (ELA) which provides the advantage of good exposure of the posterior facet and lateral wall [4, 5], despite the high rate of wound complications. Recently, the use of less invasive surgical methods including sinus tarsi approach (STA), percutaneous osteosynthesis, and arthroscopically assisted reduction, has gained popularity because it results in less damage to soft tissue and thus fewer wound complications compared to the ELA [6, 7].

The STA is a minimally invasive technique with good visualization of the posterior facet of the subtalar joint [8]. Compared with the ELA, the STA results in fewer wound complications and a shorter postoperative stay [9–11]. The STA could be a more suitable management for DIACFs on the consideration of wound complications. Osteosynthesis in the STA is usually achieved with screws, plate, or a combination [12, 13].

Percutaneous screw fixation features a low cost, simple modality, and satisfactory clinical outcome for the treatment of DIACFs [14, 15]. Minimally invasive locking plate is also commonly used because they provide high stability of the subtalar joint and good functional recovery [16]. However, soft tissue damage and related neuralgia, as well as implant irritation and the need for a second surgery for implant removal, are concerns for patients and physicians [17]. Currently, there is no clear evidence showing the absolute superiority of either technique.

DIACFs are challenging to manage due to complex anatomy, risk of soft tissue complications, and the potential for long-term functional impairment if not properly reduced. Optimizing fixation methods via less invasive approaches such as the STA is clinically significant, as it aims to balance stable reduction with reduced wound complications and faster recovery. Screw fixation and minimally invasive plate fixation are the main surgical options for DIACFs and are widely used in our institution. Although previous studies have compared these methods, optimal treatment remains under debate. We hypothesized that screw fixation provides clinical and radiological outcomes comparable to minimally invasive plate fixation in the management of DIACFs.

Materials and methods

Patient enrollment and data collection

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chang Gung Medical Foundation. This retrospective study was conducted at a single urban level one trauma center. From January 2015 to December 2020, patients with displaced intra-articular calcaneus fractures (DIACFs) who underwent surgical intervention were identified and reviewed. The inclusion criteria were patients aged more than 18 years, patients with closed fractures, DIACFs with an articular step-off or gap > 2 mm measured at the posterior facet of the subtalar joint on coronal and sagittal computed tomography (CT) images, acute fracture (injury time ≤ 2 weeks), follow-up time not less than three years and those who underwent surgery by open reduction and internal fixation via the sinus tarsi approach. Patients with the following conditions were excluded: (1) previously received ankle or foot surgery; (2) preoperative ankle stiffness; (3) subtalar joint arthritis; (4) associated fractures of the ipsilateral talus, tibia, fibula, metatarsal, or other parts of the ankle and foot; (5) treated conservatively; (6) open fracture; (7) chronic fracture (injury time > 2 weeks); and (8) less than 3 years of follow-up.

Those who were treated with open reduction and screw fixation were allocated to the SW group, and patients treated with minimally invasive locking plates (MINI-Calc Plate, Acumed, Hillsboro, OR, USA) were allocated to the MIP group for analysis. The choice of fixation method (screw fixation vs. minimally invasive locking plate fixation) was determined retrospectively based on the surgeon’s judgment and patient-specific factors, such as fracture morphology, bone quality, and patient preference after informed discussion. As this was a retrospective study, no formal randomization or pre-established protocol guided the selection.

Baseline characteristics and demographic data, including age, gender, side of injury, injury mechanism, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities (e.g., smoking status, diabetes status), bone quality, which was categorized as either normal or poor based on clinical judgment and radiographic assessment, considering cortical thickness, trabecular pattern, and patient age, and type of calcaneus fracture according to the Sanders classification [3], were collected and compared between groups to evaluate potential confounding factors.

Clinical and radiographic variables of interest and follow up protocol

The surgical time, length of hospital stays, time from injury to surgery, occurrence of complications and rate of implant removal were documented for clinical parameter comparisons. The functional outcome assessments included the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), Maryland Foot Score (MFS), Olerud-Molander (OM) ankle score [18], and American Orthopedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) score [19] for each patient were defined as the score obtained at the last available follow-up visit. All patients were scheduled for standardized follow-up visits at 3 weeks, 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months postoperatively. All outcome assessments were conducted by independent orthopedic assistants who were not involved in the index surgery, using standardized scoring systems to minimize subjective bias.

Calcaneus lateral and Harris axial X-rays were obtained before and immediately after surgery to assess the fracture severity and reduction. Further radiological assessments (plain radiographs) were performed on standardized follow-up visits at 3-month, 6-month, and 12-month postoperatively to evaluate the reduction maintenance and monitor fracture healing and implant position. Preoperative computed tomography (CT) scan and three-dimensional reconstruction of the traumatic calcaneus was obtained to determine the Sanders classification [3]. Measurements were performed digitally using the hospital’s PACS system. Radiological outcomes evaluation was based on Böhler’s angle (BA, degrees) [20], as well as calcaneal height (CH, mm), width (CW, mm), and length (CL, mm), were measured on standard lateral and axial radiographs. The radiological parameter measurement method was based on a previous study [21] (Fig. 1). Correction was indicated by the difference in the variable between preoperative and immediately postoperative follow-up. Reduction loss was defined by the difference in the variable between immediately postoperative and 12-month follow-up. All measurements and interpretations of the radiographs were repeated twice by one radiologist who did not participate in any operation. The reliability was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs). For the purposes of this study, all the radiographs were reviewed again by the first author.

Postoperative complications were systematically recorded such as poor healing of surgical wounds (delayed would healing > 3 weeks) [22, 23], surgical site infection, lateral impingement, sural nerve injury, nonunion or implant failure and postoperative radiographic evidence of subtalar joint arthritis. Definitions were based on previously published criteria to ensure consistency across cases.

Wherever feasible, we prioritized objective endpoints to reduce assessor bias when reporting complications. Examples of these endpoints include surgical duration, wound healing time, and implant failure.

Operative procedure

All surgeries were performed by senior orthopaedic trauma surgeons with over three years of experience in orthopaedic trauma surgery. Under general anesthesia, all patients were placed in a lateral decubitus position with the affected side up on an ordinary operating table. A pneumatic tourniquet was routinely applied to the ipsilateral thigh. The approach began with a transverse skin incision of approximately 5 cm along the lateral malleolar tip toward the base of the fourth metatarsal. The peroneal tendons and sural nerve were retracted inferiorly and the extensor digitorum brevis was retracted proximally to expose the posterior facet of subtalar joint. Then under direct visualization, the displaced posterolateral fracture fragment of posterior facet was identified and reduced via the small periosteal elevator with subtalar articular surface as guidance. Once an anatomic reduction was accomplished under the direct vision and fluoroscopic guidance, multiple temporary 1.8-mm or 2.0-mm smooth Kirschner wires were used percutaneously to maintain the reduction. Then the reduction quality was subsequently evaluated by direct visualization and confirmation of the lateral and Harris views on a C-arm radiograph.

In the screw group, once satisfactory reduction was confirmed, one or two (or more if needed, depending on the surgeon preference) 3.5-mm screws were applied from the lateral side of the calcaneus directed medially towards the sustentaculum tali with trajectories parallel to support the fracture fragment of posterior facet (position screw). Another one or two (or more if needed) 3.5-mm or 4.5-mm screws were applied from the calcaneus tuberosity toward the calcaneocuboid joint to maintain the calcaneal length (axial screw). An additional 3.5-mm or 4.5-mm screws was inserted from the posteroinferior calcaneal tuberosity toward the posterior facet fragment as a kickstand to support the calcaneal height and prevent varus collapse [6] (Fig. 2).

For the minimally invasive locking plate group, after anatomic reduction was achieved and temporarily held with Kirschner wires, a pre-contoured calcaneal locking plate (e.g., low-profile 3.5-mm system, 8 holes) was selected based on the fracture size. The plate was inserted subperiosteally and positioned along the lateral calcaneal wall, spanning the posterior facet (Fig. 3). Then the locking screws were delivered to fix the fracture fragments under fluoroscopic control, with at least one screw directed toward the sustentaculum tali for stable buttress support. Final reduction and implant position were confirmed by fluoroscopy before closure in the two groups. Then the wound was closed in sequence, and a splint was applied.

Aftercare and rehabilitation

After surgery, analgesics were given to patients as needed, and a splint was employed for 6 weeks in every patient. Non-weight-bearing ambulation assisted by a walker began from the second day of the surgery until 6 weeks. Partial-weight-bearing ambulation began from postoperative 6 weeks until 12 weeks. After 12 weeks, the patients were allowed to return to light work and with full-weight-bearing.

Postoperative management included standard wound care, regular follow-up imaging, and monitoring for complications. Any wound complications were treated with local wound care and debridement if necessary. Patients with persistent hindfoot pain were evaluated for subtalar arthritis and considered for arthrodesis if conservative measures failed. Sural nerve symptoms were observed during follow-up; surgical exploration was reserved for persistent symptoms.

Statistical analysis

In this study, statistical analysis was performed via software (version 22.014; MedCalc, Ostend, Belgium). The data were analyzed using the Shapiro-Wilk test to assess the normality of the distribution. Continuous variables are presented as the mean and standard deviation (SD). Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages. The Fisher’s exact test or the chi-square test was used to compare the categorical variables. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test or the paired Student’s t test was used to compare the differences before and after surgery. The Mann-Whitney U test or independent Student’s t test was used to compare the outcomes between the two groups.

Statistical significance was set when P-values were < 0.05. All P-values were verified during the revision process to ensure accuracy. All analyses were conducted using complete-case data. Missing data were handled by listwise deletion. No imputation was performed. A post hoc power analysis was performed for the primary outcome to estimate the likelihood of detecting group differences given the final sample size.

Results

Patient characteristics and demographic data

A total of 38 patients with 40 feet met the inclusion criteria were enrolled: 17 feet were allocated to the minimally invasive locking plate fixation (MIP group), and 23 feet were allocated to the screw fixation (SW group) for final analysis (Table 1). The average age of the patients was 48 years at the time of surgery. Most injuries were sustained in fall from a height accidents. The distribution of injury types was as follows: 13 feet of Sanders type II, 25 feet of type III, and 2 feet of type IV. The distribution of Sanders fracture types did not differ significantly between the MIP and SW groups (P = 0.7983), indicating that the groups were comparable in terms of fracture complexity. All patients completed follow-up at standardized time points as planned. No significant loss to follow-up was noted. No significant differences were observed between the MIP and SW groups in terms of demographics or baseline confounding variables (P > 0.05 for all comparisons), as detailed in the Table 1.

Regarding the functional assessments (Table 2), there were no significant differences between the two groups in the final Visual Analog Scale (VAS) score (MIP vs. SW: 1.12 ± 1.50 vs. 1.57 ± 1.31; P = 0.3204), Maryland Foot Score (MFS) (91.65 ± 9.70 vs. 93.96 ± 3.81; P = 0.9602), Olerud-Molander (OM) ankle score (85.29 ± 13.52 vs. 89.13 ± 10.07; P = 0.3097), or American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) score (88.35 ± 9.35 vs. 90.96 ± 7.74; P = 0.3416). The final functional scores reported represent each patient’s last recorded follow-up assessment, which corresponded to a mean follow-up duration of 63.37 months.

Complications

As shown in Table 2, there were no cases of infection, lateral impingement, nonunion, or implant failure in two groups. Among the patients who underwent screw fixation, only one case of poor surgical wound healing (delayed would healing > 3 weeks) was observed. With respect to the minimally invasive locking plate group, two patients experienced poor surgical wound healing. There were three cases of sural nerve injury in the MIP group compared with only one case in the SW group (P = 0.6434). During the follow-up period, two patients developed subtalar joint arthritis in the MIP group compared with three patients in the SW group (P = 0.1205). Only one patient in the SW group needed to undergo a second surgery involving subtalar joint arthrodesis due to post-traumatic arthritis after 5 years of the index surgery. The total incidence of complications was 35.29% in the MIP group and 21.74% in the SW group (P = 0.4947).

Radiological outcomes

Radiographic analysis revealed no significant differences in the calcaneal height, length, width, or Böhler’s angle between the two groups before surgery (Table 3). The reliability for Böhler’s angle and other radiological parameters showed good agreement, with intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) values ranging from 0.78 to 0.85. Concerning the reduction maintenance, there was also no significant difference in the reduction loss of calcaneal height, length, width, and Böhler’s angle between two groups after a one-year follow-up period (P > 0.05 for all comparisons). Discussion

Main findings

This study aimed to compare the clinical and radiological outcomes of minimal invasive locking plate (MIP) to screw fixation (SW) in the treatment of displaced intra-articular calcaneus fractures (DIACFs) via the sinus tarsi approach (STA). Overall, both methods provided comparable functional and radiological outcomes at medium-term follow-up. The screw fixation group showed a significantly shorter surgical duration than the minimal invasive locking plate fixation group and had fewer wound complications and a lower implant removal rate, while radiological parameters such as calcaneal height, length, width, and Böhler’s angle were maintained similarly in both groups.

Comparison of clinical outcomes between the two groups

The screw fixation group demonstrated a significantly shorter surgical duration than the minimally invasive locking plate group (126.65 ± 35.16 vs. 151.59 ± 36.16 min, P = 0.0347). The mean difference was 24.94 min (95% CI, 1.89 to 47.98), with a Cohen’s d of 0.70, indicating a moderate effect size. This finding of shorter surgical time with screw fixation aligns with systematic review by Wang et al. [24], which indicated that screw fixation had a significantly shorter surgical duration than locking plate fixation, at an average of 21.58 min. Some studies have also shown the advantage of the STA in DIACFs for improving functional outcomes [25, 26]. The average American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) score recorded during the follow-up period was 88.35 in the MIP group and 90.96 in the SW group. Both groups presented excellent results, and there was no significant difference between them. These findings suggest that the two fixation methods achieved similar clinical outcomes at medium-term follow-up. Otherwise, the screw fixation group demonstrated fewer wound complications (21.74% vs. 35.29%, P = 0.4947) and a significantly lower need for secondary surgery for implant removal (26.09% vs. 35.29%, P = 0.0001) compared to the minimally invasive locking plate group (Table 2). The relative risk (RR) of requiring implant removal was 0.74, indicating that patients treated with screw fixation were approximately 26% less likely to undergo implant removal than those treated with plate fixation.

Although a formal cost-effectiveness analysis was not conducted, basic economic parameters were evaluated. In brief, the screw fixation group exhibited shorter operative times and fewer reoperations for implant removal, whereas hospital stay durations were similar between groups. These findings imply potential cost advantages of screw fixation in selected fracture patterns, especially less comminuted types. However, a comprehensive cost-benefit evaluation, including indirect costs and reoperation risk, remains necessary in future studies.

Comparison and significance of radiological images

Although the screw fixation group showed slightly greater reduction loss than the minimally invasive plate group, this difference was not statistically significant (Table 3). Calcaneal widening after fracture may cause peroneal tendon impingement [27, 28], but no cases of lateral impingement were observed in either group. A study by Kir et al. [16] revealed that mini-plate fixation is superior to cannulated screw fixation in maintaining reduction, especially in the restoration of calcaneal widening. In this study, both groups showed significant improvement after reduction, and the MIP group demonstrated slightly better reduction maintenance than the SW group (reduction loss: 2.21 ± 1.17 to 2.75 ± 1.02 mm, P = 0.1282), although the difference was not significant. This may reflect the greater rigidity of plate fixation compared with screw fixation for the calcaneus.

Previous studies involving the two surgical methods

Generally, our imaging results suggest that the minimally invasive locking plate may provide better reduction maintenance than screw fixation in DIACFs in terms of the calcaneal height, length, width, and Böhler’s angle, although no significant differences were observed. Previous comparative studies and systematic reviews have investigated the optimal fixation method for DIACFs via the sinus tarsi approach. Feng et al. [29] conducted a prospective randomized controlled trial comparing percutaneous cannulated screw fixation with calcium sulfate cement grafting versus minimally invasive plate fixation. They found that while both methods achieved similar functional outcomes, plate fixation provided better maintenance of reduction and subtalar congruity in more comminuted fractures. Similarly, a study by Sato et al. [30] retrospectively reviewed 118 DIACFs cases treated via the STA at 12 months postoperatively and revealed that the mean correction loss of Böhler’s angle was 5.6 ± 5.3° in the screw group and 2.6 ± 2.7° in the locking plate group. The difference was statistically significant. In contrast, a study by Pitts et al. [31] revealed no significant difference in the restoration of Böhler’s angle between the two methods. Furthermore, a study by Guo et al. [17] analyzed 165 patients and revealed that less invasive plate fixation versus screw fixation via the STA contributed to a comparable quality of reduction after at least 2 years of follow-up. A study by Peng et al. [32] retrospectively reviewed 40 patients and reported equivalent reduction results between percutaneous screw fixation and open plate fixation group. These findings are broadly consistent with our results, which showed comparable functional and radiological outcomes but also indicated that plate fixation may better maintain reduction in severe comminuted DIACFs.

Additionally, Zhao et al. [33] conducted a recent systematic review and meta-analysis of 728 patients across seven studies comparing screw fixation and plate fixation via the sinus tarsi approach. They found no significant difference in functional outcomes, Böhler’s angle, or reoperation rates between the two methods, although plate fixation showed slightly better maintenance of Böhler’s angle, lower fixation failure, and less intra-articular step-off in complex fractures. These findings align with our results, which showed comparable clinical and radiological outcomes but suggested that plate fixation may better maintain reduction in more comminuted fractures. Overall, these results support the use of screw fixation for simpler fracture patterns and plate fixation for complex DIACFs when using the STA.

In brief, the treatment options of DIACFs are still under debate, and the choice of surgical method generally depended on surgeon preference, familiarity, and cost factors. While screw fixation remains suitable for simpler fracture patterns and may offer potential cost savings [17], robust economic data are still needed to support this assumption. However, for severe comminuted fractures with medial or lateral wall blowout, plate fixation may be preferable to ensure greater stability [30, 33].

Postoperative complications

Wound complications and even infection are still concerns related to DIACFs, especially in patients with comorbidities such as a high body mass index (BMI), diabetes mellitus and smoking [34]. The STA could provide fewer wound complication rates to the extensile lateral approach (ELA) [6, 7]. Li et al. [35] reported a relatively high risk of wound complications when open reduction and internal fixation via the sinus tarsi approach were performed within 6 days of injury. In our study, the average waiting time to surgery was 5.4 days (5.12 ± 2.91 days in the MIP group and 5.61 ± 2.90 days in the SW group, P = 0.6005). However, we performed surgery via the STA until soft tissue swelling subsided and the wrinkle sign of the lesion foot appeared; thus, the incidence of wound complications was not relatively high (3/40 = 7.5%) compared with other reports [36, 37].

With respect to sural nerve injury, transient or persistent numbness and partial or complete loss of sensation of the affected foot were noted during follow-up. Overall, there were three cases of sural nerve injury in the MIP group compared with only one case in the SW group. There was no significant difference between the two groups. The results implied that minimally invasive plate fixation may be associated with more frequent soft tissue complications. Thus, irritation to the sural nerve, even further injury during the surgical process of applying the locking plate, should be avoided.

Regarding subtalar joint arthritis, this complication could be attributed to imperfect reduction of the subtalar joint or high damage to the cartilage at the time of injury, which may persist even when radiographic anatomic reduction appears satisfactory [3, 38]. In this study, a few patients demonstrated radiographic signs of subtalar joint arthritis during follow-up, although the small sample size limited further subgroup analysis. To minimize the risk of subtalar degeneration, accurate restoration of the posterior facet congruity, careful handling of the cartilage during reduction, and appropriate fixation are crucial. In symptomatic cases where conservative management fails, subtalar arthrodesis remains an option to relieve pain and restore hindfoot function. Overall, most complications were mild and resolved with conservative treatment, highlighting the overall safety of the STA approach in treating DIACFs.

Clinical implications and biomechanical considerations

Screw fixation relies on the principle of interfragmentary compression using cannulated screws. It provides adequate initial stability but typically offer lower resistance to shear and torsional forces, especially in osteoporotic bone. Furthermore, screw fixation depends heavily on bone quality, and over time, may exhibit loss of reduction or screw loosening under cyclical loading. In contrast, plate fixation provides a biomechanically superior construct. Plates distribute mechanical loads more evenly along the lateral wall of the calcaneus and offer enhanced resistance to multidirectional forces. This makes plate fixation especially suitable for comminuted fractures, cases with lateral wall blowout, or in patients with poor bone quality.

However, some literature reported that screw fixation achieves similarly with plate fixation biomechanically [39, 40]. Furthermore, a systemic study by Dingemans et al. [41] revealed that most the investigated methods of fixation of calcaneal fractures seemed to be biomechanically sufficient.

Based on our findings and existing evidence, we suggest that for DIACFs treated via the STA, conservative management may be reasonable for non-displaced or minimally displaced fractures. For simple displaced fractures (Sanders type II), percutaneous screw fixation may be preferred in younger patients with good bone quality to reduce soft tissue disruption and cost. In more complex fractures (Sanders type III or IV) with comminution, lateral wall blowout, or poor bone quality, minimally invasive plate fixation may offer greater stability and better reduction maintenance. For severe soft tissue compromise, temporary external fixation or staged minimally invasive methods should be considered if immediate internal fixation is not possible.

While no established Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) values exist for DIACFs, we used effect size analysis (Cohen’s d) to complement statistical significance and better reflect clinical relevance. For example, the Cohen’s d of 0.70 for surgical time suggests a moderate-to-large difference that may impact operating room efficiency (Supplementary Table S1, S2). In addition, comparisons with published AOFAS benchmarks in ankle and foot surgery [42] suggest both groups achieved satisfactory functional recovery. Future studies should explore patient-reported MCID anchors and consensus thresholds to enhance interpretability.

In summary, while screw fixation may offer a cost-effective [17] and less invasive option for simple DIACFs in patients with good bone stock, plate fixation may be preferable in more complex cases where greater biomechanical stability and better reduction maintenance are desirable. Ultimately, the choice of surgical method should be individualized based on the patient’s fracture morphology, bone quality, and soft tissue condition.

Although our findings are generally consistent with previous studies and meta-analyses, this study adds practical evidence by specifically examining the STA in an Asian population with over five years of follow-up. Our analysis also highlights practical details such as implant removal rates, wound complications, and functional recovery in real-world settings, providing data that may help guide treatment choices for DIACFs. However, it should be noted that long-term issues, such as post-traumatic subtalar arthritis, chronic hindfoot pain, or progressive loss of reduction, may still occur beyond this medium-term period. Therefore, these results should be interpreted with caution when considering long-term outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the small sample size, especially for Sanders IV fractures, may limit statistical power to detect subtle differences between groups. Second, radiological assessments relied on plain radiographs rather than computed tomography (CT), which may reduce measurement precision for articular incongruity. Although preoperative CT scans were routinely performed for Sanders classification, postoperative CT was not routinely obtained. This limitation is common among similar studies, which typically assess postoperative alignment using standard radiographs. Future studies may benefit from incorporating postoperative CT imaging in selected cases to enhance measurement accuracy. Third, although outcome assessors were independent, complete blinding was not feasible due to visible differences between fixation methods, which may introduce observer bias, particularly for subjective outcomes like pain scores. Variable follow-up durations could also affect functional outcome comparability. Fourth, the retrospective design and surgeon preference in choosing fixation methods may introduce selection bias. Fifth, while all surgeries were performed by senior orthopedic trauma surgeons with comparable experience levels, formal control of inter-surgeon variability was not implemented. Subtle differences in intraoperative techniques or decision-making may have influenced outcomes. Future studies should consider surgeon-stratified analyses or adopt standardized surgical protocols to address this issue. Sixth, without routine dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) scans, bone quality was judged clinically and radiographically, which may have caused misclassification. Seventh, no formal economic analysis was performed, so the cost-effectiveness of each technique remains unclear. Finally, the medium-term follow-up may not fully capture late complications such as subtalar joint arthritis. Larger prospective studies with appropriate sample size calculations, long-term follow-up periods, and detailed cost analysis are warranted to validate these findings and guide clinical decision-making.

Conclusions

This study suggests that when using the sinus tarsi approach for displaced intra-articular calcaneal fractures (DIACFs), there was no significant difference in functional and radiological outcomes between minimally invasive locking plate fixation and screw fixation. The screw fixation group showed a trend toward shorter surgical time and a lower implant removal rate in our study. These findings indicate that both surgical methods may be considered reliable options for treating DIACFs when performed with appropriate technique; however, further large-scale prospective studies are warranted to confirm these results.
