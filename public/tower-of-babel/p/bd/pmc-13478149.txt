Neuronal dynamics, timing, and flow of sensory and choice-related information in auditory-prefrontal circuitry
Authors: Franco Giarrocco, Bruno B. Averbeck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Auditory decision-making requires transforming sensory inputs into meaningful choices by integrating external stimuli with internal cognitive processes. Classical hierarchical models propose sequential processing, where primary sensory areas relay sensory information to higher-order regions that compute decision-related processes. However, whether this sequential hierarchy fully captures cortical dynamics remains unclear. Here, we analyzed simultaneous neuronal recordings from primary auditory cortex (A1) and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (PFC) in two male macaque monkeys performing a spatial auditory decision-making task. Our analyses revealed a clear temporal hierarchy: sensory information was unidirectionally transferred from A1 to PFC, while decision-related information exhibited a bidirectional transfer, first from PFC to A1, and crucially back from A1 to PFC. These findings extend classical hierarchical assumptions, demonstrating a recurrent loop between A1 and PFC during decision-making, and offer a dynamic perspective on the integration of sensory and cognitive processes in the cerebral cortex.

Introduction

Auditory decision-making enables animals to interpret and respond adaptively to acoustic stimuli in their environment. From detecting predators and prey to navigating social vocalizations, the transformation of auditory inputs into appropriate behaviors is crucial for survival. Investigating the neural basis of how sensory signals become decisions is thus critical, particularly because disruptions in auditory processing contribute to various neurological disorders1–3. Central to this understanding is determining the extent to which sensory and higher-order cortical regions cooperate to achieve auditory-guided decisions.

Classic hierarchical models of cortical functions assumed that decision-making primarily occurred in higher-order association areas, particularly the prefrontal cortex, following initial sensory processing in primary sensory areas4–6. Consistent with this model, previous studies demonstrated the involvement of the prefrontal cortex in auditory-based decisions7–9, as well as performance deficits following prefrontal cortex inactivation10.

However, accumulating evidence challenges classic hierarchical models. Whereas early studies described auditory cortex functions as primarily involved in basic sensory analysis, such as discriminating sound identity and spatial location11–13, increasing evidence suggests a more integrated role for auditory cortical areas in cognitive processes. It has become evident that neuronal activity in the auditory cortex, including A1, extends beyond basic sensory encoding to represent cognitive functions traditionally associated with higher-order cortical areas. These include memory14–19, stimulus-response associations and reward signaling20–22, auditory categorization and task switching,23–25, as well as decision-making processes26–30. These cognitive representations often occur concurrently with prefrontal representations, suggesting parallel processing across cortical hierarchies18,25,30–32.

Importantly, previous work showed that decision-making-related neuronal activity within auditory cortex is not merely correlational but also causally linked to auditory-based decisions27. Together, these observations raise critical questions about the nature of communication between auditory and prefrontal regions. One hypothesis is that auditory cognitive processes arise through dynamic interactions involving feedforward sensory inputs from the auditory cortex integrated with reciprocal top-down feedback from PFC31,32. This hypothesis aligns with anatomical evidence of reciprocal connectivity between auditory and prefrontal areas33–35, yet direct neurophysiological characterization of these dynamic interactions remains absent.

Here, we analyze simultaneously recorded neuronal activity from A1 and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (PFC) while two macaque monkeys engaged in an auditory decision-making task. In this task, monkeys were presented with an auditory stimulus (cue) to either their left or right side, followed by a second auditory stimulus (target) presented on the same or opposite side of the cue. To obtain a reward, monkeys were required to make a response only if the locations of the two stimuli matched. We found that neural population activity in PFC and A1 dynamically represents both sensory (cue location) and decision-making (monkeys’ choice) variables, and that these representations are stronger in A1 than PFC. We show that cue-related information initially appears in A1 and is unidirectionally relayed to PFC (~130 ms after cue onset), supporting the role of A1 in early sensory processing. Crucially, we show a bidirectional exchange of information between A1 and PFC related to the monkeys’ choice, challenging the notion of strictly top-down control during decision-making. Choice-related signals first emerge in PFC and subsequently transfer to A1 (~340 ms after target onset). However, A1 does not passively receive choice-related information but relays it back to PFC. These results provide evidence that A1 and PFC engage in a bidirectional exchange of information during auditory decision-making. These findings extend classical hierarchical models, suggesting that auditory decision-making is mediated by a dynamic, bidirectional loop between primary sensory and higher-order prefrontal regions.

Results

Two rhesus monkeys performed an auditory decision-making task (Fig. 1a). Each trial began with the monkey holding a bar for a variable duration, followed by the presentation of an auditory stimulus (cue) to either the left or right side. This was followed by a delay period, during which white noise was played. After an unpredictable delay, a second auditory stimulus (target) was presented, in addition to the white noise, on either the same side (match trial) or the opposite side (non-match trial) as the cue. In match trials, monkeys had to release the bar within 700 ms after target presentation to receive the reward. In non-match trials, monkeys had to continue holding the bar for a variable period, after which a third stimulus was always presented on the same side as the cue. To receive the reward in non-match trials, monkeys had to release the bar following the third stimulus. Therefore, the probability of receiving the reward was the same for match and non-match trials. Behaviorally, both monkeys showed high performance in the task with accuracy level around 80% or above (Fig. 1b). The average response time following the target presentation on match trials was 564 ms (SEM = 10 ms) for monkey 1 and 601.4 ms (SEM = 25.1 ms) for monkey 2, while the response time following the second target on non-match trials was 314.7 ms (SEM = 13.2 ms) for monkey 1 and 251.1 ms (SEM = 6.3 ms) for monkey 2 (Fig. 1c). We analyzed the activity of 1199 neurons recorded across PFC and A1 over 37 sessions (monkey 1: 12 sessions, PFC n = 160, A1 n = 215; monkey 2: 25 sessions, PFC n = 465, A1 n = 359; Fig. 1d). In each session, neurons were simultaneously recorded from both areas, with an average of 15.5 neurons (SEM = 0.73) in A1 and 16.9 neurons (SEM = 1.01) in PFC (Fig. 1e).Fig. 1Task design, behavioral performance, and neuronal recordings.a Schematic of the auditory decision-making task. Sensory and choice coding in A1 and PFC neurons

We began our analysis by examining how activity in A1 and PFC reflected task-relevant factors. In both areas, single neurons showed mixed task-related modulation, with firing rate varying according to the cue side and whether the target was a match or a non-match (Fig. 2a). Single-unit examples illustrate the temporal framing of the task and the heterogeneity of responses we observed across neurons. To quantify cue-location and choice coding across neurons, and to assess how different delays affected this coding, we fit a linear regression of activity on cue side and the monkey’s choice for each neuron and delay duration (Fig. 2b). This regression provides a common metric for summarizing coding across analysis windows and areas. In both A1 and PFC, we identified neurons selective for cue, choice, or both, and we observed that the fraction of coding neurons did not vary with delay duration (Fig. 2c, chi-square test, monkey 1: A1 χ²(6) = 0.05, p = 1.0; PFC χ²(6) = 0.00, p = 1.0; monkey 2: A1 χ²(6) = 0.05, p = 1.0; PFC χ²(6) = 0.01, p = 1.0). We also investigated the extent to which errors arose from incorrect cue or target processing. During error trials, single-neuron activity aligned to cue onset showed modulations that closely resembled those observed during correct trials, suggesting that cue-related encoding was largely preserved during errors (Fig. 2d). In contrast, neuronal responses diverged between correct and error trials when activity was aligned to the target onset (Fig. 2e). To quantify this, we computed, for each neuron, the difference in firing rate between cue-left and cue-right conditions during the 150 ms following cue onset, separately for correct and error trials. Comparing the magnitude of these differences did not reveal any significant effect of performance on cue-related selectivity (mean firing difference ± SEM: monkey 1, A1: correct = 0.103 ± 0.015, errors = 0.110 ± 0.018, t(214) = 0.75, p = 0.457; PFC: correct = 0.104 ± 0.012, errors = 0.096 ± 0.015, t(159) = 0.60, p = 0.547; monkey 2, A1: correct = .086 ± 0.011, errors = 0.079 ± 0.013, t(358) = 0.98, p = 0.328; PFC: correct = 0.031 ± 0.010, errors = 0.021 ± 0.012, t(464) = 1.42, p = 0.156). We repeated the same computation within the 500–1000 ms window following target onset to assess whether target-related selectivity was reduced in error trials and found significant differences (mean firing difference ± SEM, Two-sided paired t-test: monkey 1, A1: correct = 0.224 ± 0.022, errors = −0.09 ± 0.017, t(214) = 9.03, p = 1.03 × 10−16; PFC: correct = 0.161 ± 0.021, errors = −0.101 ± 0.013, t(159) = 9.191, p = 2.087 × 10−16; monkey 2, A1: correct = 0.253 ± 0.019, errors = −0.114 ± 0.011, t(358) = 13.687, p = 1.371 × 10−34; PFC: correct = 0.022 ± 0.008, errors = −0.051 ± 0.007, t(464) = 5.47, p = 7.371 × 10−8). Population dynamics of sensory and choice representations

Building on the single-neuron analyses, we next examined how cue and choice representations unfold at the population level using principal component analysis (PCA). For this analysis, we pooled neurons from all sessions and treated them as a single population. This allowed us to capture the overall, task-related patterns of neural activity for each area.

We applied PCA to the average activity of single neurons, computed in 100 ms sliding windows advanced in 25 ms steps, during the four possible trial conditions (cue left, cue right, match, and non-match) and three delay durations. Neuronal trajectories revealed distinct patterns of activity across task conditions. In both areas and monkeys, trajectories initially overlapped during the baseline holding period but significantly diverged depending on cue location. Specifically, trajectories followed distinct paths based on whether the cue was presented on the left or right, regardless of match or non-match condition. Left-cue trials evolved along a similar trajectory, while right-cue trials followed a different direction in the PC space. We quantified these differences by computing the Euclidean distance over time between each pair of trajectories (Fig. 3c). Distances significantly increased following cue onset, except for trajectories corresponding to the same cue location, which followed similar paths.

After target onset (i.e., the second stimulus), trajectories further separated according to match/non-match conditions. In match trials, they shifted to a distinct region of PC space, while in non-match trials, they remained relatively close to their earlier position. Correspondingly, distances between match and non-match trajectories significantly increased post-target.

These results indicate that neural population activity in both PFC and A1 dynamically tracks task-relevant features. Following target onset, neuronal population activity reorganized according to trial type. The divergence of trajectories based on cue location reflects the first sensory encoding in both PFC and A1, while the later reorganization following target onset indicates a transition toward choice-related processing.

To further characterize the neural dynamics, we extended the PCA to include error trials. The single-unit analyses indicate that errors were not due to incorrect cue processing, and that neuronal activity was not modulated based on the delay duration. Therefore, we analyzed the neuronal activity aligned to the target presentation (−200 to +800 ms), pooling trials across the three delay durations. We applied PCA to four trial conditions defined by match/non-match category and behavioral outcome: match correct (Mc), match error (Me), non-match correct (NMc), and non-match error (NMe), and subsequently projected neuronal activity onto the first three PCs (Fig. 4a, b). We then computed the Euclidean distance over time between all pairs of conditions (Fig. 4c, d). This analysis revealed that errors in match and non-match trials had different neural signatures. The distance between match correct and match error trials (Mc-Me, same stimulus, different outcome) diverged at similar times in A1 and PFC, suggesting that failing to detect a match is reflected broadly and simultaneously across both areas. Consistent with this, the distance between non-match correct and match error trials (NMc vs Me) remained near baseline throughout, indicating that miss trials were neurally indistinguishable from correct holding. By contrast, all distances involving erroneous match reports on non-match trials (NMc vs NMe, Mc vs NMe, and Me vs NMe) diverged earlier in A1 than PFC, indicating that A1 is the first area in which these incorrect responses become distinguishable from other conditions. Decoding of cue side and choice from neuronal activity

The previous analysis examined how neuronal population activity in PFC and A1 evolves throughout the task, revealing a shift from an initial cue-related representation to a later choice-related representation. Here, we measured the strength and the onset time of these representations. We used a linear SVM classifier to predict cue location and the monkeys’ choice in two separate analyses. We performed the decoding using a 100 ms sliding window advanced in 25 ms increments from −500 ms to 1000 ms relative to stimulus onset (either the cue for cue side decoding or the target for choice decoding), with analyses conducted at the single-session level using simultaneously recorded neurons.

In both monkeys we were able to decode the cue side above chance level, with increased decoding performance in A1 compared to PFC (peak of accuracy ± SEM, Two-sided paired t-test: monkey 1, A1 = 0.826 ± 0.044, PFC = 0.698 ± 039, t(11) = 3.6, p = 0.004; monkey 2, A1 = 0.748 ± 0.029, PFC = 0.603 ± 0.015, t(24) = 4.32, p < 0.001) (Fig. 5a). We examined decoding latencies relative to cue onset. For each time bin after cue presentation, we compared the decoding performance in A1 and PFC to their respective baseline (the mean decoding performance during the 500 ms preceding cue onset, approximately 0.5 accuracy). We defined the decoding latency relative to the cue side as the first bin in which performance exceeded baseline with statistical significance. In both monkeys we observed earlier decoding of cue side in A1 compared to PFC (Fig. 5b. decoding latency (ms) ± SEM, Two-sided paired t-test: monkey 1, A1 = 25 ± 0 ms, PFC = 70.83 ± 2.81 ms, t(11) = 16.31, p = 4.69 × 10−9; monkey 2, A1 = 25 ± 0 ms, PFC = 87 ± 2.55 ms, t(24) = 24.32, p = 2.033 × 10−18). We were also able to decode the monkeys’ choice following the target presentation (Fig. 5c). (Note that this analysis included correct and error trials, so reflects actual choice, not just the cue x target interaction.) Again, we found increased decoding performance in A1 compared to PFC (peak of accuracy ± SEM, Two-sided paired t-test: monkey 1, A1 = 0.79 ± 0.017, PFC = 0.69 ± 0.02, t(11) = 4.68, p < 0.001; monkey 2, A1 = 0.75 ± 0.021, PFC = 0.66 ± 0.013, t(24) = 3.62, p = 0.001). Thus, although both cue side and choice were represented in A1 and PFC, these representations were stronger in A1. Importantly, their onset times differed between regions: cue-related information emerged earlier in A1, whereas choice-related information emerged earlier in PFC.

Sensory-related flow of information between A1 and PFC

Having established that information related to cue side and the monkey’s choice emerged with distinct latencies in A1 and PFC, we next sought to quantify the extent to which this information was transferred between the two areas using ensembles of simultaneously recorded neurons. We began by examining the flow of cue-related information. For each area, we identified a 1D population axis capturing cue-related variance and then measured how activity along this axis in one region predicted subsequent activity in the other. Discussion

A central goal in systems neuroscience is to understand how distributed brain areas coordinate to give rise to behavior and cognition. Although it is widely recognized that cognitive functions arise from computations occurring across interconnected cortical areas, only a few studies have directly measured how task-relevant information dynamically propagates and integrates among regions during behavior36–42. These studies have predominantly focused on visually-guided tasks, examining the flow of information between cortical areas or between cortical and subcortical structures. In contrast, auditory processing, in particular during decision-making, has remained relatively understudied, and direct neurophysiological characterization of inter-area interactions has been lacking.

In the present study, we addressed this by quantifying the extent to which sensory- and decision-related neuronal information is represented and transferred between A1 and PFC during auditory decision-making. We analyzed simultaneous neuronal recordings from A1 and PFC in macaque monkeys performing a spatial auditory decision-making task. In this task, monkeys indicated whether two consecutive auditory stimuli originated from the same or different spatial locations.

Auditory cortical connectivity in primates exhibits a ventral–dorsal organization analogous to the “what” and “where” pathways in the visual system35,43–47. The ventral auditory stream, projecting from anterior auditory cortex to ventrolateral PFC, primarily supports the processing of auditory object identity, such as vocalizations and communication signals48–50. Conversely, the dorsal stream, connecting posterior auditory cortex to dorsal prefrontal areas, supports auditory spatial processing and sensorimotor integration based on sound location34,51,52. Therefore, our task design and recordings explicitly targeted the dorsal auditory stream, making it particularly suitable for studying spatial decision-making processes.

Our results revealed that neuronal populations in both A1 and PFC dynamically transition from sensory to decision-related processing according to specific phases of the task. These findings align with prior observations of sensory and cognitive representations in these areas during task performance8,9,23,30,31,53, and parallel evidence from the somatosensory domain showing correlates of decision-making in sensory cortex54,55. However, we observed that these representations were stronger in A1 than in PFC (although this effect could be partially driven by differences in firing rate between the areas), complementing existing evidence of an A1 causal contribution to auditory-based decisions27.

Furthermore, the timing of these representations differed between areas: sensory signals appeared earlier in A1, whereas decision-related signals first emerged in PFC and subsequently in A1. This temporal pattern aligns with auditory categorization studies in ferrets, where sensory signals in auditory cortex preceded categorical representations emerging first in prefrontal cortex31.

In the present study, we found that sensory signals were unidirectionally relayed from A1 to PFC, while choice-related signals originating in PFC were first transmitted to A1 and then retransmitted back to PFC. During the cue period, processing likely operates in a feedforward mode (A1 to PFC), as the task primarily requires extracting and encoding spatial information rather than comparing or selecting. The reverse pathway (PFC to A1) may remain functionally silent when no top-down modulation is needed. This unidirectional flow of sensory information may therefore reflect our task structure, where the cue provides clear, unambiguous sensory evidence and no expectation or bias needs to be imposed on auditory processing. In this context, the PFC may focus on encoding and maintaining spatial information without the need to modulate sensory representations in A1. This suggests that the top-down signals from PFC to A1 do not re-encode or relay sensory features but instead reflect task-relevant decision-related states or internal goals. This view is supported by findings from the visual domain, where the prefrontal cortex has been shown to transmit rule-dependent category signals to posterior parietal cortex, consistent with a top-down control mechanism that adapts downstream computations to behavioral goals36.

By contrast, during the target and decision phases, the system integrates stored and incoming information to generate a choice. Under these conditions, reciprocal interactions emerge. This observation aligns with top-down modulation within cortical hierarchies56. In this framework, higher-order areas such as PFC exert top-down influence on lower-order sensory cortices. These feedback signals are thought to ‘edit’ sensory input, emphasizing its behavioral relevance rather than its raw physical features. Within this model, PFC-to-A1 signaling could enhance the representation of task-relevant information, for example by biasing perception toward an expected target location, which in our task corresponded to the location of the first cue. Our data provide direct neurophysiological evidence for such feedback information transfer in the auditory system. One possibility is that A1 returns a sharpened sensory signal that PFC integrates into the ongoing decision process. Alternatively, the bidirectional exchange may reflect a recurrent process in which choice-related signals are iteratively refined through interactions between the two areas. This aligns with models of hierarchical inference in which different cortical levels exchange information recursively to refine perception and guide behavior57,58. While hierarchical inference emphasizes bidirectional processing in sensory hierarchies, primarily within the visual system, and focused on perceptual inference, our findings suggest that similar computational principles can apply to interactions between sensory and cognitive areas. Specifically, the observation that A1 sends decision-related signals back to PFC reinforces the hypothesis that primary auditory cortex contributes to the formation of choice, rather than simply representing external stimuli. This extends the hierarchical inference framework beyond perceptual refinement to include active sensory-cognitive loops central to decision-making, highlighting a distributed architecture where early sensory cortices iteratively shape behaviorally relevant signals.

Furthermore, it is possible that the bidirectional interactions reflect cognitive processes such as working memory or sustained attention that are engaged only by the specific demands of this task. The observed bidirectional exchange could also partly reflect the existing anatomical reciprocal connectivity between these areas rather than a computationally necessary process. However, we note that a purely epiphenomenal account, in which bidirectional flow simply reflects activity propagating through existing anatomical connections, is difficult to reconcile with the task-dependent reconfiguration of information flow. Indeed, during the cue period both areas are active, yet the flow remains unidirectional, suggesting that the emergence of bidirectional exchange during the choice period reflects a functional shift rather than passive anatomical coupling. Nevertheless, the correlational nature of our data precludes direct causal inference. Distinguishing between these accounts will require future studies employing causal manipulations such as targeted inactivation during specific task phases. Additionally, implementing tasks that directly manipulate attentional or working memory demands could help determine under which conditions the recurrent dynamics between auditory and prefrontal cortex emerge.

Previous studies suggested a role for A1 in linking sensory input to motor behavior22,30,59. This raises the possibility that the late return signal to PFC observed in our data may reflect not only refined inference, but also the preparation or stabilization of an impending behavioral response. However, our paradigm does not allow us to dissociate decision-making from the motor response, and thus the observed A1-to-PFC signal could contain information related to both processes.

Unlike visual cortical areas, which predominantly exhibit feedforward hierarchical organization, auditory cortex demonstrates more integrative, bidirectional interactions with prefrontal cortex25,32,60–63, possibly reflecting the unique cortico-cortical auditory pathways that primates evolved as auditory cortical areas expanded and formed extensive connections with prefrontal regions46.

It is important to note that our recordings did not include additional cortical areas that play a role in auditory processing, in particular the parietal cortex64–66. However, we note that our analytical approach revealed asymmetric and task-specific patterns of directed communication consistent with the known monosynaptic anatomical connectivity between auditory and prefrontal cortex. Furthermore, the timing of information flow between these regions is compatible with conduction and synaptic delays for monosynaptic connections67, and to our knowledge, there is no cortical or subcortical structure that projects monosynaptically to both A1 and PFC with the appropriate timing to account for the observed bidirectional, task-dependent flow of information.

We also note that lesions of auditory cortex in monkeys produce hearing loss68,69, underscoring its crucial role as the principal cortical bottleneck for auditory input ascending from the thalamus. Together, these observations support the interpretation that the dynamic A1-PFC interactions we report reflect genuine cortico-cortical communication, rather than shared inputs from an upstream source.

Subcortical structures such as the thalamus or basal ganglia70–72 may also play a modulatory role in this recurrent cortical loop, a possibility that remains to be tested. Lastly, it is worth noting that a cognitive component of the task involves working memory73,74. Consistent with this, cue information remained decodable during the delay period, suggesting that memory traces of the cue were preserved and remained available in both A1 and PFC.

In conclusion, our results provide evidence of dynamic, recurrent interactions between auditory and prefrontal cortex during decision-making, challenging strictly classical hierarchical models and highlighting the possibility that cortical function may depend on flexible, interactive networks capable of dynamically integrating sensory and cognitive signals. These results also suggest that computational models incorporating reciprocal interactions between primary sensory and prefrontal areas could better capture decision dynamics than strictly feedforward architectures.

Methods

The present study is based on a subset of the dataset already published in a previous study30, which also provides detailed information on the experimental setup, neurophysiological recordings, and exact recording locations. All analysis were performed using MATLAB (MathWorks Inc.). Unless otherwise stated, all values presented in the figures and main text are reported as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). The SEM was calculated by dividing the standard deviation by the square root of the sample size.

Subjects

Two 9-year-old male monkeys (Macaca mulatta) were used as subjects in this study. For the duration of the study, the monkeys were placed on water control and earned their fluid through their performance on the task on testing days. They were socially pair-housed to promote natural interactions. All experimental procedures adhered to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were reviewed and approved by the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) Animal Care and Use Committee.

Task design and stimuli

Monkeys were trained to perform an auditory decision-making task while seated in a primate chair inside a dark, acoustically isolated room. Task execution was controlled by custom software (System 3: OpenWorkbench and OpenDeveloper, Tucker-Davis Technologies), which regulated multi-speaker sound delivery and recorded both bar-press responses and eye movements. Eye movements were tracked using the ViewPoint EyeTracker system (Arrington Research) at a sampling rate of 1 kHz. The monkey faced a 19-inch LCD monitor positioned 40 cm from the eyes. Auditory stimuli were presented via speakers positioned 10 cm to the left and right side of the monkey’s head. Juice rewards were dispensed through a solenoid-controlled delivery system (Crist Instrument).

The task consisted of two trial types, match and non-match trials. At the start of each trial, the monkey was required to press a bar and fixate on a central fixation point. After a variable delay of 2.1 to 2.4 s, a 50-ms, 4 kHz square-wave auditory stimulus (the cue) (70 dB) was played from either the left or right speaker. After a variable and unpredictable delay of 1000, 1300, or 1800 ms following the cue onset, a 300-ms, 1 kHz second auditory stimulus (the target) was played from either the left or right speaker. Different delay durations were introduced to prevent the monkeys from temporally predicting target onset, which could have otherwise influenced motor preparation and anticipatory activity. Target tones were played at different dB levels for the two monkeys to achieve comparable behavioral performance, with levels adjusted based on individual performance. For monkey 1, target tones ranged from 27 to 45 dB, with most tones falling between 29 and 36 dB. For monkey 2, target tones ranged from 16 to 40 dB, with most tones between 17 and 24 dB. Further, white noise (40 dB) was played on both the left and right sides from 500 ms following the cue presentation until the monkey released the bar.

In match trials, the target was played on the same side as the initial cue, and the monkey had to release the bar within 700 ms to obtain the reward. Releasing the bar more than 700 ms after the target presentation resulted in an error trial and the reward was not delivered. In non-match trials, the target was played on the opposite side from the cue, and the monkey was required to continue holding the bar. Releasing the bar after the target in non-match trials was considered an error, and the monkey was not rewarded. After an additional delay of either 800 or 1000 ms, a second 1 kHz target was always presented on the same side as the original cue during non-match trials. To receive the reward, the monkey had to release the bar within 700 ms after this second target. Thus, match and non-match trials had the same reward probability, and therefore the monkeys could not rely on differences in expected reward but had to use the auditory stimuli to guide their responses. Incorrect choices resulted in a prolonged timeout period before the next trial began. The use of square waves, which contain odd harmonics, ensured broadband spectral stimulation that engaged large portions of the auditory cortex more effectively than pure tones. Initial cue location was blocked, meaning that left-cue and right-cue trials were presented in separate blocks. Further, monkeys were required to maintain visual fixation throughout the trials. We measured the monkeys’ accuracy in the task as the proportion of correct choices in match and non-match trials. Response time was defined as the interval between target presentation and bar release.

Neurophysiological recordings

Before data collection, monkeys underwent two separate surgical procedures for the implantation of a titanium headpost for head stabilization and a custom-designed 45 × 24 mm recording chamber implanted on the skull. The chamber was aligned along the anterior-posterior axis to allow for vertical grid-based electrode penetrations targeting the left PFC and the auditory cortex. PFC recordings primarily included area 46/8Ad in the dorsal bank of the principal sulcus with a distance of at least 1 mm from the arcuate sulcus. Auditory cortex recordings were focused on A1, with minor coverage of adjacent belt territories. All electrode penetrations followed a dorsoventral trajectory, that was confirmed using a T1-weighted MRI scan. Neuronal activity was recorded using 16- or 24-channel laminar “V-Trodes” (Plexon) with contact spacing of 200–300 µm. For the A1 recordings, sharpened guide tubes were inserted 10–15 mm above the target area to ensure accurate placement. Electrodes were inserted via NAN microdrives (NAN Instruments) and allowed to stabilize for at least 1 h before recording. Neural signals were acquired using a 64-channel Tucker-Davis Technologies (TDT) data acquisition system. Spike signals were amplified, bandpass-filtered (0.3–8 kHz), and digitized at ~24.4 kHz. Online spike sorting was performed in real-time using window discrimination, with digitized waveforms and event timestamps stored for offline sorting using Offline Sorter (version 3.3.5, Plexon). We performed our analysis including both single- and multi-unit activity as their response patterns were similar.

Population dynamics of cue and choice representations

We analyzed data from 37 sessions (12 from Monkey 1 and 25 from Monkey 2) in which simultaneous recordings were obtained from A1 and PFC. To investigate how neuronal activity in these areas reflected task conditions, including the initial cue location and match/non-match conditions, we applied dimensionality reduction using principal component analysis (PCA).

For each neuron, we computed the firing rate aligned to cue onset within a 100-ms sliding window, advanced in 25-ms increments, and z-scored across all trials within the session. We then calculated the averaged neuronal activity over time for each neuron during the four trial types (cue-left, cue-right, match, and non-match), separately for the three delay periods. This led to 12 (4 trial types × 3 delays) time series of averaged activity for each neuron.

To perform PCA, we constructed a neural activity matrix n (neurons) × m (12 time series) separately for A1 and PFC. We applied PCA to this matrix, and neuronal activity was subsequently projected onto the first three PCs that explained most of the variance. We then represented neuronal activity during each trial type and delay condition as a trajectory in PC space, allowing us to capture how neuronal activity evolved throughout the task.

To quantify the differences between neuronal trajectories across trial types, we computed the Euclidean distance over time between each pair of trajectories in PC space. To assess statistical significance, we compared the Euclidean distance at each time point to a baseline, defined as the mean Euclidean distance across all pairs of trajectories during the 500 ms preceding cue onset. Distances exceeding the baseline by more than three standard deviations (SD) were considered significantly different. We repeated the same analysis including error trials with the activity aligned to the target onset. For this analysis, we pooled trials across delays and compared four conditions: match correct, match error, non-match correct, non-match error.

Decoding analysis

We used a support vector machine (SVM) classifier to separately predict the initial cue location and the monkeys’ choice. Decoding was performed in 100-ms time windows, advanced in 25-ms steps, using neuronal activity that was z-scored across all trials within a session. Neural activity was time-locked to either cue onset (for cue location decoding) or target onset (for choice decoding). Note that although z-scoring reduced baseline activity differences across areas, differences in firing rate between A1 and PFC could still affect the relative decoding strength observed in the two areas.

To evaluate classifier performance, we implemented a tenfold cross-validation procedure. Decoding accuracy was computed at the single-session level and then averaged across sessions separately for each monkey. To determine decoding latencies, we appended decoding accuracy over time across all sessions and performed a two-sided paired t-test between the average accuracy during the baseline period (500 ms before cue or target onset) and each time bin within 1000 ms after. The first time bin that showed a significant difference was defined as the decoding latency. To estimate the mean decoding latency ± SEM, we repeated this analysis n times, where n equaled the number of sessions, using a leave-one-session-out procedure.

This procedure generated residual activity over time, ε, that excluded within-area temporal dependencies while preserving neuronal activity modulations driven by interactions with the other area. To validate this approach, we applied an additional autoregressive model to the residuals and examined the regression coefficients across lags for each session and area. Coefficients at non-zero lags (e.g., ±1) were near zero, indicating that residual activity was no longer temporally autocorrelated and that using a single-lag model was sufficient to isolate across-area influences.

Finally, we quantified the flow of information related to task variables between A1 and PFC by performing cross-area predictions using the residual activity from the autoregressive model. Here a1a1 is the target area and a2a2 is the source area (each representing either A1 or PFC), while ii represents the lagged bin number in the source areas ahead of time t used for the prediction. This analysis was conducted in both directions, testing whether activity in PFC at time t could be predicted from past A1 activity and vice versa. We computed the coefficient of determination R2 over time to estimate the direction, latency, and strength of information flow between A1 and PFC. To determine the latency of information flow, we created a matrix of R² values, with rows corresponding to sessions and columns to time bins. We next performed two-sided paired t-tests comparing the average R² during the baseline period (500 ms before cue or target onset) to each time bin within 1000 ms after. The first time bin that showed a significant difference was defined as the information flow latency. To estimate the mean information flow latency ± SEM, we repeated this analysis n times, where n equaled the number of sessions, using a leave-one-session-out procedure. To examine differences in the flow of information latency, we applied a two-way ANOVA with one level of interaction, in which factors included the direction of the flow of information (A1 to PFC vs. PFC to A1) and the total number of bins used for the cross-area prediction model. The latency of the peak of flow of information was calculated as the time bin associated with the maximum R2 value.

Note that for all single-session analyzes of sensory information, we focused on the location of the cue. Although the target also carried spatial information, target-side decoding was weaker than cue-side decoding, particularly in monkey 2. This difference likely reflects the lower target intensity used in monkey 2 to match behavioral performance across animals. (see also “Task design and stimuli” section). Because the cue was presented at a consistent and higher intensity in both monkeys and was not reflective of ongoing decision and motor processes, it provided a more robust and comparable measure of sensory encoding across sessions.
