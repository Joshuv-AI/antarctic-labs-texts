GAS6-AS1 drives bladder cancer progression by increasing MMP7 expression in a ceRNA- and RBP-dependent manner
Authors: Xinyi Zhou, Linmei Xiao, Fanyu Meng, Fei Zuo, Weili Wu, Gongyu Li, Fei Han, Guohui Peng, Hua Shen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•GAS6-AS1 is highly expressed in bladder cancer.•GAS6-AS1 could promote the carcinogenesis of gastric cancer.•GAS6-AS1 promotes the expression of MMP7 by interacting with miR-367-3p and PRC1.

Introduction

Bladder cancer (BCa) affects individuals of all genders and ethnicities, ranking among the top 10 most prevalent malignancies worldwide [1]. In recent years, attributed to a variety of complex factors such as environmental and genetic factors, the incidence of BCa has surged sharply, standing as the second most common malignant tumor of the genitourinary system, with about 5.49 million new confirmed cases and 2 million deaths worldwide annually [2]. Urinary bladder cancer (UBC) constitutes the majority of BCa cases and is classified into four grades based on pathological assessment: papilloma, papillary urothelial neoplasm of low malignant potential (PUNLMP), low grade, and high grade [3]. Current treatment strategies primarily involve surgical excision, often supplemented with chemotherapy and immunotherapy, which are associated with high costs and recurrence risks [4]. Advancements in biomedicine and technology have led to the emergence of molecular-targeted therapies, which hold promise in delaying tumor invasion into the muscle and curbing further migration and invasion of tumor cells [5,6].

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are transcripts exceeding 200 nucleotides in length that typically lack protein-coding capabilities [7]. They are known to modulate the expression of various mRNAs, including oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes, by acting as microRNA (miRNA) sponges, thus functioning as competitive endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs). Moreover, lncRNAs interact with RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) to regulate downstream genes, thereby influencing key pathways. Additionally, lncRNAs can serve as molecular bridges, binding to DNA and impacting transcription processes [7]. Distributed widely across the nucleus, cytoplasm, and various organelles, lncRNAs collaborate with diverse biological macromolecules. Mounting evidence suggests that lncRNAs exhibit aberrant expression in various cancers, influencing tumor progression by modulating malignant biological behaviors in UBC cells.

Methods

Tissue samples and cell culture

With the patients' informed consent, we collected 70 UBC tissue samples and their adjacent normal tissues between April 2017 and April 2022 in the Nanjing BenQ hospital. We then obtained baseline data from medical records and determined survival through annual telephone follow-up. All patients were confirmed not to have received chemoradiotherapy and had no history of other malignancies.

All cell lines we used in this study were purchased from the Cell Center of Shanghai Institutes for Biological Sciences. Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM H-21 4.5 g/litre glucose) (Wisent, Canada) was applied to cell culture. Foetal bovine serum (FBS; Wisent, Canada) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin were added to the media. All cell lines were cultured in a controlled environment with 5% CO2 at a temperature of 37℃, maintaining optimal humidity levels.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

We extracted total RNA and performed qRT-PCR following the protocol as our previous description [8]. The primers used are listed in the supplementary material.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) assay

Cy3 and fluorescein amidite (FAM) were used to label GAS6-AS1 probes and miR-367-3p probes, respectively. The FISH assay was performed by the use of a FISH kit (RiboBio) to detect different probe signals in cells and tissues according to the protocol. 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) was used to stain nuclei.

RNA pull-down assay

We manufactured the probe-coated beads by incubating a biotin-labeled GAS6-AS1 probe with streptavidin magnetic beads (RioBio, China). After incubating T24 cell lysates with probe-coated beads overnight, the beads underwent a thorough washing process to obtain the pulled-down complexes. The extracted miRNA was subsequently detected using qRT-PCR, while the proteins were subjected to analysis through mass spectrometry and silver staining techniques.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay

In line with the descriptions of the Magna RIP Kit (Millipore, USA), we incubated several antibodies, including anti-IgG, anti-AGO2, anti-PRC1, or anti-MMP7 with magnetic beads, respectively. Then, the complexes were mixed with the cell lysates overnight and the bound RNAs were detected by qRT-PCR after elution.

Dual-luciferase reporter gene assay

Wild as well as mutant GAS6-AS1 and SLC12A5 sequences were inserted into the pmiR-RB-Report ™ vector (RiboBio, China). Following the synthesis of the wild-type APC promoter and its mutants, they were cloned into the PGL3-basic luciferase reporter plasmid (Realgene, China). After co-transfecting the cells with different mimics or vectors, we detected the luciferase activities with Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, USA).

Western blotting

The whole steps of Western blotting were introduced in our previous research [8]. The antibodies used in this research were listed here: anti-MMP7, anti-SLC12A5, anti-Ki67, anti-PRC1, and anti-APC (Abcam).

Functional experiments

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) assay, colony formation assay, wound healing assay, and transwell assays were conducted as described previously [8]. Flow cytometric analysis was employed to ascertain the distribution of various phases of the cell cycle according to the protocol of the Cell Cycle Analysis Kit (Multisciences, China).

Animal experiments

Upon receiving the endorsement from the Animal Ethics Committee of Nanjing Medical University (2310049), we purchased four-week-old male BALB/c mice and injected 0.1 ml of preprocessed cell suspensions subcutaneously into the right groin. Tumor growth was evaluated as (length × width[2])/2 every two days. After two weeks, we sacrificed the mice and peeled off the tumors, followed by the calculation of tumor weight and further immunohistochemistry.

Results

Characteristics of upregulated GAS6-AS1 in UBC

Intersection analysis between the TCGA database and GSE27448 identified four lncRNAs significantly upregulated in UBC (Fig. 1A). Subsequent examination in 16 UBC and adjacent normal tissues revealed a significant upregulation of GAS6-AS1 in UBC (Fig. 1B). This finding was further confirmed with a larger sample size of 80 patients, showing remarkable statistical differences between tumorous and normal tissues (Fig. 1C). Consistent results were observed in paired tissues from the TCGA database (Fig. 1D). The association of GAS6-AS1 with clinicopathological features was illustrated via a forest plot, indicating a positive correlation between GAS6-AS1 expression and muscle invasion and advanced grade, partially aligning with TCGA database findings (Fig. 1E-F). GAS6-AS1 functions as a sponge of miR-367-3p

LncRNAs were known to function by sponging miRNAs through the ages. Anchored in the lack of similar research about GAS6-AS1 in UBC, the AnnoLnc2 as well as lncRNASNP2 databases were used in unison to identify the potential miRNA associated with GAS6-AS1, and miR-367-3p has been pinpointed as a key player in this process (Fig. 4A). Followed by the construction of biotin-labeled probes and the validation of the efficiencies, the RNA pull-down assay was performed, which showed that miR-367-3p could be captured by the GAS6-AS1 probe, and vice versa (Fig. 4B-C). miR-367-3p was predicted to interact with GAS6-AS1 as a RISC component through its binding to Argonaute-2 (AGO2). As concluded from the RIP assay, the anti-AGO2 antibody could pull down both GAS6-AS1 and miR-367-3p (Fig. 4D). We also found thatGAS6-AS1 and miR-367-3p colocalized in the cytoplasms of T24 and 5637 cells via FISH assays (Fig. 4E). Moreover, FISH assays conducted on the tissues unveiled a heightened expression of GAS6-AS1 in the tumorous region of UBC tissues, whereas miR-367-3p was predominantly localized in the adjacent normal tissue. (Fig. 4F). For the exploration of the specific binding region, the luciferase assay was carried out and the results confirmed the prediction (Fig. 4G), supported by structural predictions via AnnoLnc2 (Fig. 4H). PRC1 was reported to function as a DNA-binding protein and usually inhibits the expression of the downstream gene. We analyzed the online databases together with differentially-expressed genes (DEGs) and prognosis-related genes (PRGs) of UBC to seek the potential gene (Fig. 7E). Among the eight potential genes, APC was a credible biomarker widely used in colorectal cancer. We performed the ChIP assay and dual luciferase reporter gene assay and obtained positive results. Moreover, the expression of GAS6-AS1 correlated positively with the occupancy of PRC1 in the APC promoter and negatively with the activity of the APC promoter (Fig. 7F-G). The binding sites were analyzed by the LongTarget website according to Hoogsteen interactions and two potential triplex-forming oligonucleotides (TFOs) within GAS6-AS1 and the corresponding triple target sites (TrTs) in the APC promoter were forecast (Fig. 7H). A triplex-capture assay was conducted to validate the prediction, unveiling that TFO1, as opposed to TFO2, interacts with the APC promoter through TrTs1 and establishes a complex triplex formation within the regulatory region of the APC gene (Fig. 7I-J). Therefore, the TFO sequences were inserted into the pcDNA3.1 vector and subsequently transfected into the T24 cells. It was observed that the expression of APC exhibited a negative correlation with that of GAS6-AS1. Additionally, overexpression of TFO1, but not TFO2, could reverse the increase in APC expression resulting from knocking down GAS6-AS1, suggesting the critical role of TFO1 as well (Fig. 7K). Considering that MMP7 is associated with both SLC12A5 and APC [[10], [11], [12]], we examined the regulatory effects of GAS6-AS1 on MMP7 in different ways at the protein level (Fig. 7L).

GAS6-AS1 drives UBC progression by upregulating MMP7

On the basis of our aforementioned findings, it was concluded that GAS6-AS1 could not only upregulate MMP7 by sponging miR-367-3p, but also bind with PRC1 and then downregulate the expression of APC. However, APC was the critical inhibitor of the Wnt pathway, suggesting that GAS6-AS1 could activate the Wnt pathway. Interestingly, MMP7 was also a part of the Wnt pathway and regulated by APC. Therefore, we performed rescue experiments to verify the association of GAS6-AS1 with MMP7. Functional experiments in vitro showed that GAS6-AS1 exerted significant influence on the proliferation, migration, invasion, and cell cycle could be retarded by MMP7 (Fig. 8A-E). Discussion

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) orchestrate complex mechanisms across various diseases, often modulating chromatin accessibility via their influence on histone-modifying enzymes and RNA polymerases [13]. LincRNAs, arising from intergenic regions, regulate the expression of nearby genes and distant genomic sequences, while ncRNAs transcribed from ultra-conserved regions (UCRs) have been shown to partially bind to miRNAs [14,15]. Amidst the translation process into clinical treatments, numerous RNA therapies, including antisense oligonucleotide (ASO), short hairpin RNA (shRNA), and CRISPR-Cas9-based gene editing, are in clinical development phase II or III. Notably, therapies targeting non-coding RNAs primarily focus on miRNAs, with no clinically proven lncRNA-based therapy established. Leveraging the diverse mechanisms of lncRNAs, potential approaches to address this issue include introducing artificial mimics of corresponding miRNAs and developing small molecule compounds as interaction blockers and structural elements to target ncRNAs [16]. Another promising avenue is exploring the application of natural antisense transcripts (NAT), specifically, ASO targeting NAT, termed "antagoNAT" [17].

In this study, we identified GAS6-AS1, highly expressed in urinary bladder cancer (UBC), through database analysis. We confirmed its role in regulating MMP7 at the post-transcriptional level by competitively binding miR-367-3p. Concurrently, GAS6-AS1 suppressed APC expression by interacting with RBPs, consequently promoting MMP9 expression downstream of the Wnt pathway. These findings aim to identify new biomarkers and offer insights into interfering with GAS6-AS1 function by analyzing pathway composition.

According to the results, several molecules constitute the downstream regulatory network of GAS6-AS1. It has been reported that miR-367-3p played a role in multiple tumors, forming a feedback loop with CEBPA in glioblastoma, influencing the efficacy of targeted therapy in hepatocellular carcinoma and cisplatin resistance in bladder cancer by modulating RAB23 [[18], [19], [20]]. Unlike miR-367-3p, PRC1 binds GAS6-AS1 as a protein. PRC1 is generally considered to have E3 ubiquitin ligase activity and specifically mediate H2AK118ub and H2AK119ub to exert pro-cancer effects in a variety of tumors [21]. PRC2, a member of the Polycomb repression complex, is thought to bind to a variety of non-coding RNAs and form RNA-DNA triplex structures with the DNA promoter regions of p21 and CDKN2B [22,23]. However, a similar mechanism associated with PRC1 has not yet been reported. As the identified downstream molecule of PRC1, APC is considered to be a key negative regulator of the Wnt signaling pathway and has implications for colorectal cancer diagnosis and treatment [24,25].

As one of the key proteins in the matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) family, MMP7 is a key regulator of cell-cell and cell-extracellular matrix communication. It is believed to possess the capacity for hydrolyzing a diverse array of structural and signaling proteins, thereby facilitating processes intricately linked to tumorigenesis. These include but are not limited to extracellular matrix reorganization, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, cellular migration, angiogenesis, and immune modulation. It has been widely reported to promote tumor progression in a variety of ways in multiple tumors, including gastric and colon cancers [26,27].

This study mainly applied online databases for analysis, and the lack of verification by sequencing in the organizations may be the main reason why our study was criticized and questioned. In addition, this study has several limitations which cannot be ignored. First of all, MMP9 is regarded as a key downstream molecule of GAS6-AS1 in this study. According to our analysis, GAS6-AS1 seems to play multiple roles by influencing many molecules downstream of Wnt pathway through APC, and we only explored part of the role of GAS6-AS1 based on the function of MMP9. Second, although we have spent a lot of time exploring the downstream molecules of GAS6-AS1, the lack of understanding of its potential upstream regulators may have caused us to miss some opportunities to block the cancer-promoting function of GAS6-AS1. Then, MMP7 is a well-known invasion-related protein, and we regret that we did not construct the corresponding tumor metastasis model in animal experiments. Finally, clinical correlation analysis showed that GAS6-AS1 seems to be related to the blood type of patients, and blood type has been found to be associated with the risk of developing a variety of tumors, which warrants further investigation [28,29].

To sum up, our research offers new evidence supporting the assumption that GAS6-AS1 is correlated with tumor progression and poor prognosis in UBC. Specifically, we unveil the dual role of GAS6-AS1 in promoting MMP7 expression. Within the nucleus, GAS6-AS1 orchestrates the recruitment of PRC1 to the APC promoter, forming an RNA‐DNA triplex that suppresses APC gene transcription. In the cytoplasm, GAS6-AS1 disturbs the binding of miR-367-3p to the SLC12A5 3′UTR to facilitate its expression (Fig. 9).
