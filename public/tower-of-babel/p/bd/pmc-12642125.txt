Flushing away disease: A one health perspective on sanitation, pathogen transmission, and rural health in China
Authors: Shilong Zhao, Junqiao Ma, Wenjing Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImproved sanitation is a cornerstone public health intervention, fundamentally integrating human, animal, and environmental health. China's Toilet Revolution marked a nationwide initiative to upgrade rural sanitation, yet evidence on its integrated health effects remains scare. Drawing on provincial panel data and China Health and Nutrition Survey (CHNS), this study applies two-way fixed effect and staggered Difference-in Differences (DID) from a one health perspective to assess the reform's impacts. Our results indicate that the expansion of sanitary toilets significantly disrupted environmental pathogen transmission, reducing the incidence of fecal–oral diseases such as bacillary dysentery and infectious diarrhea. The reform also lowered risks of zoonotic infections—including brucellosis, echinococcosis, and leptospirosis—by interrupting pathogen cycles between humans, animals, and the environment. At the household level, this reform generated substantial health benefits. Specifically, the probability of illness fell by 6.7 % in the past four weeks, and healthcare expenditure declined by 17.7 %. However, these benefits were not distributed equitably, with men and higher-income households benefiting more than women and lower-income groups. Collectively, this study demonstrates that while rural sanitation upgrades are a powerful one health intervention, their health benefits are maximized and sustained only through equitable and cross-sectoral collaboration.Keywords: Health impact, One health, Toilet revolution, Disease, Sanitation infrastructures

Introduction

Improved sanitation is a cornerstone of public health and a practical expression of the one health framework, which posits that human, animal, and environmental health are fundamentally interconnected [1,2]. The framework formalizes how health risks in one domain can generate negative spillovers into others as pathogens and pollutants rarely adhere to environmental or species boundaries [3]. Inadequate sanitation serves as a prime example of such cross-domain transmission [3]. Specifically, the improper disposal of human excreta contaminates soil and water system, creating environmental pathogen reservoirs that facilitate disease transmission [4,5], thereby generating negative health externalities across species and degrading ecosystem stability [1,4,[6], [7], [8]]. Indeed, this environmental pathway is a major driver of zoonotic disease, which accounts for more than half of human pathogens [1]. For example, infections like leptospirosis and gastrointestinal illnesses thrive, particularly in areas where fecal contamination persists [4,9]. Consequently, sanitation interventions that break fecal–oral transmission and reduce environmental pathogen loads not only directly safeguard human health but also disrupt broader infection cycles that involve animals and ecosystems. From a one health perspective, sanitation improvement is not just a human service but a cross-cutting public good that yields positive externalities across species and environments.

To standardize the measurement of global sanitation progress, the WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme (JMP) established the Sanitation Ladder, a five-tier framework ranging from open defecation to safely managed services [10]. At the bottom are unimproved facilities, such as pit latrines without slabs, which fail to separate waste from human contact. At the top, safely managed sanitation requires not only a private, improved facility but also safe treatment and disposal of waste, either on-site or off-site. Within this framework, China's Toilet Revolution stands out as a national campaign focused not only on sanitation coverage but also on toilet quality [9,[11], [12], [13], [14]]. Instead of merely reducing open defecation, the reform aimed to move rural households up the sanitation ladder by replacing unimproved or limited options with basic or safely managed systems [13,15,16]. This reform objective was operationalized through the Hygienic Specification for Rural Household Latrine, which defines a sanitary toilet as one that securely contains and treats waste through sealed, impermeable structures and safe disposal methods. These technical standards align closely with the JMP's criteria for basic, and often safely managed, sanitation [17], making a shift from infrastructure quantity to the quality of service required to protect public health and the environment.

For decades, rural China has lagged far behind its cities in sanitation and health outcomes [18,19]. In 1993, a merely 7.5 % of rural households had access to sanitary toilets, a deficit that contributed to the high prevalence of fecal-oral diseases and parasitic infections, such as ascariasis and hookworm [15]. This reveals a pronounced urban–rural gap in public health infrastructure, underscoring the need for targeted improvements. A turning point came in 2004, when the central government initiated the first national program providing direct fiscal support for rural toilet construction [13,15]. Between 2004 and 2008, nearly 1.3 billion RMB were funded the construction of about 4.4 million sanitary toilets in rural areas [13,15]. This substantial, state-led investment defines the 2004–2015 period as a key window for evaluating the policy's effects. The Chinese experience during this period—marked by rapid economic growth, rising inequality, and large-scale infrastructure investment—offers valuable lessons for other low- and middle-income countries [9,20]. Therefore, assessing the health and economic impacts of this large-scale sanitation program provides an important case study on the effectiveness of state-led public health engineering.

The link between sanitation and better health is well established in the existing studies. Earlier work provided the first systematic account of China's Toilet Revolution, tracing its goals, progress, and obstacles such as limited funding, uneven regional implementation, and low public awareness [15]. Subsequent research examined the economic impacts on off-farm employment, household welfare, and the needs of older populations [11,13,[21], [22], [23]]. Despite these contributions, the following gaps remain unsolved. First, while most studies focus on human health outcomes, few have empirically assessed sanitation's broader impacts across human, animal, and environmental domains within a one health perspective. The possibility that a human-centered intervention could reduce zoonotic disease risk remains largely unexplored. Second, there is little rigorous, quasi-experimental evidence on whether the benefits of large-scale sanitation reforms are equitably shared. It is still unclear if health gains extend equally across socioeconomic and demographic groups, or if vulnerable populations are disproportionately left behind.

This study addresses three core questions. First, to what extent did China's Toilet Revolution reduce pathogen transmission across environment, animals, and humans under one health? Second, what were the program's causal effects on human health outcomes and the associated economic burden of disease? Third, how were health gains distributed across different groups? Using a rigorous econometric design with large-scale longitudinal data, the paper moves beyond documenting general benefits of sanitation by providing robust quantitative evidence on both the broad effectiveness and the equity implications of one of the world's largest sanitation programs, and offering a clear case for applying one health principles to public policy.

Material and methods

Data

To evaluate the health effects of China's rural Toilet Revolution, this study combines macro- and micro-level panel data. At the provincial level, we use panel data for 31 provinces from 2004 to 2015. Information on the incidence of legally notifiable infectious diseases—including fecal-oral diseases such as bacillary dysentery and typhoid fever, as well as zoonoses such as brucellosis and echinococcosis—was drawn from the National Population and Health Science Data Sharing Platform. Provincial socioeconomic and healthcare indicators were obtained from the China Health Statistical Yearbook and the China Statistical Yearbook.

At the micro level, this study draws on the China Health and Nutrition Survey (CHNS), a long-term project jointly conducted by the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill and the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention. Launched in 1989, the CHNS follows roughly 7200 households and 30,000 individuals using multistage, random cluster sampling. By 2015, the survey covered 15 provinces that together represented nearly half of China's population, according to the 2010 census [24]. The CHNS provides rich longitudinal data on household health, nutrition, healthcare use, insurance, income, and demographics [25]. For this analysis, we use five survey waves—2004, 2006, 2009, 2011, and 2015—that align with the rollout of the centrally funded Rural Toilet Revolution. The baseline year of 2004 corresponds to the central government's first allocation of fiscal support for rural sanitation. Earlier waves were excluded to avoid bias from two major shocks: the 2003 SARS outbreak and the introduction of the New Rural Cooperative Medical Scheme, both of which reshaped healthcare access and health-seeking behavior.

Outcome variables. At the macro level, two groups of outcomes were considered. First, to track environmental contamination pathways, we used incidence rates of four major fecal–oral diseases: bacillary dysentery, typhoid fever, other infectious diarrheal diseases, and amoebic dysentery. Second, to assess zoonotic risks, we included incidence rates of brucellosis, echinococcosis, and leptospirosis. At the micro level, three sets of health outcomes were used. The first is a binary measure of recent illness, based on self-reported sickness in the past four weeks. The second captures healthcare use, with indicators for formal medical care (e.g., hospitals, doctors), informal care (e.g., village medics), and self-medication. The third measures the economic burden of illness, including log-transformed medical expenditure and medical spending as a share of household income.

Explanatory and control variables. At the macro level, the key explanatory variable is the provincial coverage rate of rural sanitary toilets (%). At the micro level, the treatment variable is a binary indicator: it equals 1 for a village in a given year if the Toilet Revolution had been implemented there by that year, and 0 otherwise. This variable reflects community-level exposure to the policy. As shown in Fig. 1, adoption rates in the CHNS sample closely match national trends, reaching 77.9 % by 2015, in line with the official figure of 78.5 % [15] (see Fig. 2). The analysis also controls for a rich set of covariates. At the individual level, controls include age, age squared, gender, education, and health insurance coverage. At the household level, they include per capita income, poverty status, and household size. To address potential endogeneity, the model further incorporates interactions between year fixed effects and baseline village characteristics measured in 2004, such as illness rates, per capita income, number of households, share of migrant workers, and proximity to development zones, open cities, special economic zones, navigable rivers, and bus stops.

Results

Interrupting environmental pathogen pathways

The Toilet Revolution improves health primarily by interrupting the transmission of pathogens from human excreta to the environment. By safely containing and managing waste, sanitary toilets reduce the contamination of soil and water—key vectors for fecal-oral diseases. Provincial panel analysis provides quantitative support for this effect. As shown in Table 3, higher rural sanitary toilet coverage is significantly associated with lower incidence of several fecal-oral transmitted diseases.

Specifically, a one-percentage-point increase in sanitary toilet coverage corresponds to a decline of 1.186 cases of Class A and B infectious diseases per 100,000 people. The effect is especially evident for fecal-related illnesses: bacillary dysentery decreased by 0.233 cases, typhoid fever by 0.025, amoebic dysentery by 0.007, and other infectious diarrheal diseases by 1.663 cases per 100,000 people. These findings confirm that by reducing the environmental pathogen burden, the Toilet Revolution disrupts the “feces–environment–human” transmission chain and lowers rural populations' exposure to enteric bacteria and parasites.

Mitigating zoonotic disease transmission risk

Beyond reducing human-specific diseases, the Toilet Revolution also generated important spillover benefits by lowering the risk of zoonotic transmission. Many zoonoses rely on contaminated soil or water as transmission media; improved sanitation disrupts these pathways by cleaning shared human–animal environments.

Empirical analysis confirms this cross-domain effect. As shown in Table 4, rural sanitary toilet coverage is significantly and negatively correlated with the incidence of three major zoonotic diseases. Each percentage-point increase in coverage corresponds to decreases of 0.013 cases of brucellosis, 0.060 cases of echinococcosis, and 0.032 cases of leptospirosis per 100,000 people. These results highlight that a human-centered sanitation intervention can simultaneously strengthen animal and environmental health. By shrinking the environmental pathogen reservoir, sanitary toilets reduce opportunities for spillover and exemplify the one health principle: actions in one domain can deliver protective benefits across others.

Improving human health and reducing economic burdens

The success of the reform is ultimately reflected in its impact on the health and well-being of rural residents. Micro-level analysis using CHNS data shows that reductions in environmental and zoonotic risks translated into tangible benefits for individuals and households.

Table 5 reports the baseline results of the Toilet Revolution's effects on illness incidence, healthcare demand, and medical expenditure. First, the reform significantly improved health outcomes: the probability of falling ill within the past four weeks declined by 6.7 %, representing a reduction of over 20 % relative to the control group mean (0.067/0.307). This substantial improvement underscores the health gains from enhanced sanitation. Second, the reform reduced healthcare demand. Columns (2)–(5) indicate that utilization of formal healthcare services fell by 5.3 % (a 22 % decline relative to the baseline rate of 0.234), while informal care and self-treatment decreased by 3.4 % and 2.1 %, respectively. These patterns suggest a shift in healthcare-seeking behavior induced by improved sanitation. Third, the reform eased the economic burden of healthcare. Columns (6)–(10) show that total medical expenditure fell by 17.7 %, and the share of household income devoted to healthcare dropped by 1 %, mainly due to lower spending on formal medical services. Overall, the evidence demonstrates that better sanitation can substantially reduce illness incidence, lower healthcare demand, and alleviate household healthcare costs, thereby delivering significant public health benefits.

Inequitable health gains

Although the Toilet Revolution generated substantial overall health benefits, these improvements were unevenly distributed. A heterogeneity analysis (Table 6) highlights pronounced disparities by gender and socioeconomic status. The health gains were significantly larger for men than for women: males experienced greater reductions in both the likelihood of illness and the probability of seeking medical treatment. Similarly, the benefits accrued disproportionately to wealthier households. Non-poor families exhibited meaningful declines in sickness and medical expenditures, whereas the effects for poor households were small and statistically insignificant. Higher-income groups also saw larger reductions in healthcare utilization relative to their low-income counterparts.

Robustness checks

Based on the above empirical strategies, we conducted several robustness checks to validate our main findings. Fig. 3 presents the event-study estimates, which test the parallel-trends assumption underlying the DID framework. The results show no significant pre-treatment differences in health outcomes between treated and control villages, while post-reform estimates are consistently negative and statistically significant, confirming the baseline results. Fig. 4 reports the placebo test, which uses 1000 simulations with randomly assigned reform years while preserving the original rollout pattern. The placebo estimates are centered around zero, whereas the actual coefficients lie in the tails of the distribution, suggesting that the observed effects are unlikely to be due to random chance. Table 7 summarizes additional robustness analyses. Columns (1)–(3) demonstrate that the results remain robust when (1) re-estimating models with propensity score matching (PSM), (2) excluding villages that implemented reforms before 2004, and (3) applying the Callaway and Sant'Anna [26] estimator to account for treatment effect heterogeneity. In all cases, the estimated impacts of the Toilet Revolution on illness incidence, medical service use, and expenditures remain negative and statistically significant. Discussion

This study provides robust evidence that China's Toilet Revolution functioned as a comprehensive one health intervention. Compared with the existing that focus impacts on human health, this study explores how sanitation improvements generated interconnected benefits across human, animal, and environmental domains. The installation of sanitary toilets reduced fecal contamination in soil and water, thereby lowering direct human exposure to pathogens and disrupting zoonotic transmission cycles. These environmental and animal health gains translated into measurable improvements in human health outcomes and a reduced disease burden [27]. This case illustrates the one health principle in action: an infrastructure intervention targeted at people created positive externalities for the environment and animals, which in turn amplified health gains. Compared with the findings that India's Total Sanitation Campaign emphasized eliminating open defecation [5,9,14,28], we figure out that China's emphasis on safely managed sanitation appears to have produced broader cross-sectoral benefits.

Another contribution of this study is the finding that these benefits were not equitably distributed. Gender disparities reflect entrenched socio-behavioral roles:, women in rural areas are more exposed to contaminated environments due to responsibilities such as caregiving, household chores, and water collection [29,30]. As a result, their relative health gains from sanitation improvements were smaller than those of men. Similarly, income disparities highlight the importance of complementary resources. A sanitary toilet is necessary but insufficient; higher-income households were more able to purchase soap, cleaning agents, and safe water, which enhanced the benefits of improved sanitation [31,32]. Poorer households, who are in shortage of these resources, can only achieve partial health gains. These inequities show that infrastructure alone cannot close health gaps; it must be paired with social policies that ensure equitable access to the resources needed for effective use.

The main strength of this study is its use of large-scale longitudinal data combined with a quasi-experimental design to estimate the effects of a major national policy. This approach provides a more robust and generalizable assessment of the Toilet Revolution's impacts than smaller case studies or cross-sectional analyses. Nonetheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, micro-level health outcomes rely on self-reported illness from the CHNS, which may be affected by recall and social desirability biases. While such measures are widely used in population health research and strongly predictive of mortality, the consistency between self-reported outcomes and macro-level infectious disease data strengthens confidence in the findings. Second, the treatment variable is measured at the village level, obscuring household-level variation in adoption, use, and maintenance quality. The estimates thus reflect community-level intent-to-treat effects rather than household-level usage. Third, environmental and animal health impacts are inferred from disease incidence data rather than direct pathogen measurements in water, soil, or animal reservoirs. These limitations highlight clear directions for future research. Future studies should incorporate more recent data to assess long-term health impacts, employ objective indicators such as clinical or biomarker data, and conduct integrated one health surveillance combining human, environmental, and animal samples. Such efforts would enable direct mapping of pathogen transmission pathways and a more comprehensive evaluation of how sanitation interventions reshape the broader ecosystem of risk.

Conclusions

This study provides robust evidence that improving rural sanitation is a highly effective public health investment with benefits extending across human, animal, and environmental health. Using a rigorous quasi-experimental design to evaluate one of the world's largest sanitation initiatives, China's Toilet Revolution, the analysis shows significant reductions in environmentally transmitted and zoonotic diseases, leading to better health outcomes and lower economic burdens. These results validate the one health principle: interventions targeting human health can also generate broad ecosystem benefits. However, the gains were uneven. Women and low-income households benefited less, highlighting the need to embed health equity in policy design. Infrastructure alone cannot guarantee equal outcomes; social and economic conditions shape who ultimately benefits.

Based on these insights, this study proposes the following recommendations to inform policy design. First, sanitation programs should adopt a one health framework—going beyond toilet construction to integrate safe sludge treatment, animal waste management, and hygiene education. To sustain impact, governments can foster cross-sector collaboration across public health, veterinary, agricultural, and environmental agencies. Second, equity measures should be considered such as subsidies for hygiene products for low-income families, gender-sensitive facility design, and tailored health education campaigns, to better cover the vulnerable groups. Third, investment in one health surveillance systems is essential to monitor pathogens across humans, animals, and the environment, enabling more effective prevention and response to health threats. By embracing an integrated and equitable one health approach, sanitation investments can not only reduce disease but also build more resilient and inclusive societies.
