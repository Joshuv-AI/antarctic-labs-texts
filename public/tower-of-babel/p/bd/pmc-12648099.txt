HECTD1-mediated SFTPB overexpression in fibrotic lung induced by silica
Authors: Mengling Chen, Shencun Fang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Alveolar epithelial cells type 2 are lung progenitor and secretory cells capable of secreting four surfactant proteins, namely surfactant protein A (SFTPA), surfactant protein B (SFTPB), surfactant protein C (SFTPC), and surfactant protein D (SFTPD). Among these proteins, SFTPB not only participates in the composition of surfactant proteins but also has important immune functions. In occupational dust exposure, dust entering the bronchi and alveoli can come into direct contact and stimulate AT2. To investigate the expression of SFTPB in fibrotic lungs, we constructed a mouse pulmonary fibrosis model using silica and detected the spatial expression of SFTPB protein and SFTPB mRNA in lung sections. Herein, we observed an increase in SFTPB production from AT2 in SiO2-exposed mice without a corresponding increase in mRNA levels in the lung sections. Pulmonary fibrosis continued to progress in mice on day 112 after modeling. During the progression of pulmonary fibrosis, the protein level of SFTPB was always higher than that of the control mice, accompanied by increased HECT domain E3 ubiquitin protein ligase 1 (HECTD1) and ubiquitin (Ub). HECTD1 knockdown can partially reverse the increase in SFTPB in AT2 induced by silica. Therefore, we concluded that HECTD1 mediated SFTPB overexpression in silica-induced fibrotic lungs.

Introduction

AT2 cells are a group of heterogeneous cells with the ability to proliferate, differentiate, and secrete. The apoptosis and dysfunction of AT2 cells are associated with various lung diseases. Lung fibrosis is driven by AT2 apoptosis. In old animals, mitochondrial respiration becomes abnormal, and mitochondrial rupture and swelling, as well as an increased number of spinal fractures, lead to excessive production of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species, resulting in cell apoptosis [[1], [2], [3]]. Knocking down the Nrf2 antioxidant gene in mice can abate AT2 cell proliferation and increase sensitivity to bleomycin-induced lung fibrosis [4,5]. Another important function of AT2 cells is their secretion of a surfactant to maintain lung compliance. AT2 cells secrete four surfactants, namely SFTPA, SFTPB, SFTPC, and SFTPD, anomalous processing and distribution of which can lead to spontaneous pulmonary fibrosis [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]].

As one of the four surfactants, SFTPB not only maintains lung homeostasis but also participates in various pathological processes of lung diseases. The upregulation of SFTPB can alleviate acute lung injury induced by the knockout of nicotinamide phosphoryltransferase (Nampt) in lung epithelial cells [13]. In addition, clinical studies have shown that pro-SFTPB in plasma can be used for the diagnosis of non-small-cell lung cancer [14]. Surfactant proteins are also associated with congenital immune diseases. Genetic defects of surfactant proteins can contribute to changes in lung function and congenital immune function in newborns, resulting in respiratory disorders, interstitial lung disease, and cystic fibrosis (CF) [15]. The single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) of a single SFTPB gene is associated with CF, and the SNP-SNP interaction between SFTPB and other surfactant protein genes is associated with moderate or severe CF, leading to lung disease in patients with CF.

Studies have shown that the stability of surfactant proteins is associated with the E3 ubiquitin ligase subunit FBXL2 and ubiquitin proteasomes [16,17]. HECT domain E3 ubiquitin protein ligase 1 (HECTD1) belongs to the HECT domain family of E3 ubiquitin ligases. E2 ubiquitin ligases provide ubiquitin to HECTD1, which forms a thioester bond with ubiquitin through its Cys active site’ HECTD1 and then connects ubiquitin to the target protein or the end of an extended polyubiquitin chain. Most studies on HECTD1 involve embryonic development, hematopoietic stem cell regeneration, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition of cancer cells [[18], [19], [20]].

In the present study, we investigated changes in SFTPB protein levels in AT2 cells exposed to SiO2, as well as the relationship between SFTPB and HECTD1.

Materials and methods

Ethics statements

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Welfare Committee of Southeast University (Approval No. 20190121002). We used as few animals as possible while still meeting the experimental requirements. After completing the experiment, the animals were euthanized, and their carcasses were disposed of appropriately. All animal experiments complied with the rules of animal experimental ethics and were subject to supervision and inspection by the Animal Experimental Ethics Committee.

Animals

Male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Hangzhou Ziyuan Experimental Animal Company (Hangzhou, China). Mice aged 4–5 weeks and weighing 20–22 g were used in the experiment. Mice were raised under a constant temperature (22 °C ± 2 °C) and humidity environment (40 % ± 10 %), alternating day and night for 12 h, with free access to food and water. Mice were raised in a stable environment for 1 week to ensure a stable health status before use.

Cell culture

MLE-12 and BEAS-2B cells were cultured in DMEM containing 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin in an incubator under conditions of a humidified 5 % CO2 atmosphere at 37 °C.

HECTD1 was knocked down using clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/CRISPR-associated protein 9 (Cas9) technology

BEAS-2B and MLE-12 cells were transfected with CRISPR/Cas9 plasmids to knock down HECTD1 expression. According to the manufacturer's protocol, MLE-12 or BEAS-2B cells were seeded at 1 × 105 cells per well in a 24-well plate and used for transfection. The prepared cells were added with 200 μL of DMEM medium and then the transfection system, which was composed of A (10 μL DMEM: 1.5 μL transfection reagent) and B (10 μL DMEM: 0.2 μg plasmid). After 24 h of transfection, the transfected cells were continued to culture with DMEM complete culture medium for 24 h and then used in subsequent experiments.

Single-cell sequencing

The single-cell sequencing experiments are described in our previously published article [21]. Lung tissues were harvested from mice and washed three times with pre-cooled phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Tissues were then minced into approximately 10 mm fragments and dissociated into single-cell suspensions using a Lung Tissue Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-095-927, Germany) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The resulting cell suspension was loaded onto a Chromium Single Cell Controller (10x Genomics) to generate single-cell gel bead-in-emulsion (GEM) libraries. Cells were captured and lysed within GEMs, allowing for barcoded reverse transcription of released RNA. Following reverse transcription and cDNA synthesis, amplification was performed and cDNA quality was assessed. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) libraries were constructed using the Single-Cell 5′ Library and Gel Bead Kit (1000165, 10x Genomics) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. Sequencing was carried out on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

Spatial transcriptomics

The spatial transcriptomics experiments can be found in our previously published article [21]. Lung tissue specimens harvested near the hilar region were sectioned horizontally and trimmed prior to embedding in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound for cryopreservation. Frozen sections of 10 μm thickness were mounted onto Gene Expression (GEX) arrays for spatial analysis. Sections were fixed with methanol and subsequently stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Spatial gene expression profiling, including tissue permeabilization and reverse transcription, was performed using the Visium Spatial Gene Expression Slides & Reagents Kit (10x Genomics, PN-1000184). Permeabilization duration was adjusted based on the extent of fibrosis observed at each disease stage. Reverse transcription, cDNA synthesis, and amplification were conducted following kit specifications, using the Visium Spatial Library Construction Kit (10x Genomics, PN-1000184). Final sequencing libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform according to the manufacturer's protocols.

Pulmonary function test

Mouse lung function testing was conducted using a forced-maneuver system (EMMS, Hants, UK) as described previously. In brief, the mice were anesthetized, and a tracheal tube was inserted into the trachea. The mouse was then placed on the platform of the lung function instrument. Then, the tracheal tube was connected to a lung function, and the volume recorder was closed. After all of the preparations, the lung function indicators were tested according to the set program. Each mouse was tested at least three times in duplicate, and one reliable result was selected for statistical analysis.

Western blot

Western blot analysis was used to detect protein levels in cells or lung tissues. RIPA lysis solution was added and incubated for 30 min on ice to lyse the tissue or cells, followed by centrifuging at 4 °C at 12,000 rpm and obtaining the supernatant protein. After detecting the concentration of protein samples, the same amount of protein from each sample was taken for SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis, and then the proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes. The PVDF membranes were then sealed with 5 % skim milk (diluted in TBST buffer) at room temperature for 1 h. Next, the membranes were incubated with the primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. The next day, after three washes with TBST, the membranes were incubated with rabbit anti-goat IgG secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h, followed by three washes with TBST. Finally, the signal on the membrane was detected using a chemiluminescence detection system. The intensity of protein bands was quantified using ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence staining was performed using a previously described method [21]. Immunofluorescence staining was performed on cryosections of lung tissue. Sections were first rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by permeabilization with 0.3 % Triton X-100 for 10 min. Nonspecific binding sites were blocked by incubation with 1 % bovine serum albumin (BSA) at room temperature for 1 h. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (anti-proSP-B/mature SP-B, Abcam; anti-HECTD1, Santa Cruz Biotechnology). The next day, sections were washed three times with PBST (PBS containing 0.1 % Tween-20) and incubated with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Unbound secondary antibodies were removed by washing with PBST, and sections were mounted with DAPI-containing mounting medium. Fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope.

H&E and Sirius red staining

H&E and Sirius red staining were performed, as previously described [21].

Co-immunoprecipitation

MLE-12 cells were cultured in vitro and treated with or without silica for 48 h. The cells were then harvested, and cellular proteins were extracted. Agarose and SFTPB antibodies were added to equal amounts of protein in each experimental group, and NP-40 lysate was added to form the same volume system. The above system was then incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by centrifugation, and the precipitate was obtained. Next, the precipitate was washed repeatedly and boiled at 100 °C for 5 min with the same volume of protein lysate and protein loading buffer added. Finally, pro-SFTPB and K48-Ub were detected using Western blot analysis.

Statistical analyses

GraphPad Prism 8 Software was used for statistical analysis. Student's t-test was used to determine differences between the two groups. Differences between two groups were considered significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Pulmonary fibrosis progressed after intratracheal instillation of SiO2 suspension

Based on our previous research, on the 56th day after intratracheal instillation of SiO2 suspension, computed tomography (CT) revealed that all model mice had entered the pulmonary fibrosis stage [21]. We began to evaluate the persistence of pulmonary fibrosis caused by silica on the 56th day and continued to assess the progression of lung fibrosis on the 84th and 112nd day after modeling. Mice instilled with normal saline (NS) were used as a control. CT scan of the mouse chest revealed fibrous high-density shadows in the lungs of the model mouse. On days 84 and 112, the high-density shadows became denser (Fig. 1A). From a general observation of lung tissue, on the 56th day of modeling, the lungs were swollen and larger, with gray-white nodules visible on the lung surface. Furthermore, the lungs were hard to the touch and felt gravelly when cut. On the 84th day of modeling, the lungs of SiO2-84d mice were even bigger than those of NS-84d mice. The gray-white nodules in the lungs of SiO2-84d mice became more difficult to observe than those of SiO2-56d mice (Fig. 1A). On the 112nd day of modeling, collagen fibers had undergone aging and contraction, resulting in extremely dense silicotic nodules, indicating that pulmonary fibrosis was entering its final stage (Fig. 1A). Compared with the control mice administered NS, the organ coefficients of the lungs in the model mice increased significantly, with the highest increase reaching 0.028 on day 84. The lung function indicators Cchord and FVC, reflecting lung compliance, showed a decrease on the 56th day and a significant decrease on the 84th day of modeling (Fig. 1B). The indicators—i.e., volume expired in the first 75 ms of fast expiration (FEV75), inspiratory capacity (IC), and mean mid expiratory flow (MMEF), all of which are related to small airway ventilation—appeared to significantly decrease on the 56th day of modeling (Fig. 1B), indicating that the ventilation capacity of the small airway was damaged in the early stages of lung fibrosis. In contrast, total lung capacity (TLC), which is often caused by restrictive ventilation disorders, was not significantly affected on the 56th and 84th days of modeling, but showed a decrease on the 112nd day. This phenomenon proved again that the fibrous scar had undergone aging and contraction, leading to a decrease in pulmonary diastolic function on the 112nd day of modeling.

To evaluate the progression of lung fibrosis in mice on the 56th, 84th, and 112nd days of modeling at the histological level, mouse lung tissues were obtained to make frozen sections and stained further. Sirius red staining showed that the collagen in the alveolar wall of NS mice was pink with uniform thickness and staining and that the alveolar cavity was irregularly shaped. Until the 112nd day of observation, there were no significant changes in the lung tissue of NS mice. On the 56th day of modeling, bright red fibrous nodules appeared in the lesion area; on the 84th day, fibrotic areas were more extensive; and on the 112nd day, the fibrous lesions in the lung tissue appeared as clumps, becoming extremely dense (Fig. 2A). The overall condition of the lung tissue can be divided into three types according to the degree of tissue fibrosis: residual normal alveoli, alveoli of thicken walls, and dense fibrotic nodules (Fig. 2A). The formation of dense fibrotic nodules is the result of collagen aging and is also the mechanism underlying the decrease in TLC. The H&E staining showed that the alveolar structure of NS mice was intact, composed of single-layer cells, with clear visible tracheal and vascular structures. On the 56th day of modeling, silicon nodules were formed (Fig. 2B). By this time, most of the pulmonary alveoli still maintained their normal structure, while inflammatory cells and protein accumulated in the diseased alveolar cavity. On the 84th day, the alveolar structure was further damaged, and the remaining normal alveoli were further reduced (Fig. 2B). On the 112nd day, the diseased lung tissue was characterized by dense fibrotic nodules, with few normal alveoli remaining (Fig. 2B). The staining results indicated that collagen deposition began on the 56th day of modeling and that pulmonary fibrosis developed progressively from the 56th to 112nd day.Fig. 2(A) Images of Sirius red staining of mouse lung tissues on the 56th, 84th, and 112nd days after modeling. To investigate the protein level of SFTPB during the progression of pulmonary fibrosis, we next obtained the lung tissues of mice on the 56th, 84th, and 112nd days of modeling and extracted tissue protein from the lung tissue homogenate. Changes in HECTD1 protein levels during the progression of pulmonary fibrosis

Previous studies have shown that the ubiquitin-proteasome system is an important pathway for maintaining lung surfactant protein levels [23], through which proteasome inhibitors can inhibit the expression of SFTPA, SFTPB, and SFTPC in lung epithelial cell lines in vitro. E3 ubiquitin ligase HECTD1 belongs to the family of HECTs (homologous to E6AP C terminals) and can form thioester bonds with ubiquitin, which is carried by E2, mediating substrate ubiquitination. HECTD1 has previously been reported to be involved in the development of lung diseases [24,25]. It has been proven that HECTD1 can release eIF6 factors from the large ribosomal subunit (60S) through the ubiquitination pathway, promoting the assembly of the 60S and 40S subunits into the 80S ribosome, thereby ensuring smooth protein translation. Therefore, HECTD1 deficiency can regulate protein expression at the post-transcriptional level [19]. To investigate the relationship between SFTPB and HECTD1 in fibrotic lung, we next detected the protein levels of Ub and HECTD1 in lung tissue. Western blot analysis showed that compared with the control mice administered NS, the levels of HECTD1 proteins in the lungs of the model mice were significantly increased on the 56th day (P < 0.01) (Fig. 5A–D), accompanied by an increase Ub. Using the same methods, we detected HECTD1 and Ub protein levels in mouse lungs on the 84th and 112nd days of modeling, as well as the co-expression of HECTD1 and SFTPB. Increase in SFTPB protein levels in the lung epithelium during stimulation with SiO2 in vitro

The SFTPB and SFTPC protein levels were detected at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h after the addition of SiO2 suspension (50 μg/ml) (Fig. 7A). Western blot analysis showed that, in in vitro cultured cell lines, the surfactant protein SFTPB mainly existed as pro-mature SFTPB (the precursor of SFTPB). After 24 h of treatment with SiO2, the expression of pro-mature SFTPB in MLE-12 significantly increased, lasting until 72 h (Fig. 7A and B). Under the same stimulation conditions, although SFTPC protein levels showed an upward trend, the increase was not statistically significant (Fig. 7C). The CCK-8 assay showed that cell viability significantly increased after 12 h of SiO2 stimulation and reached its peak at 24 h (Fig. 7D). The above in vitro experiments indicate that SiO2 can induce an increase in pro-mature SFTPB in pulmonary epithelial cells, which is consistent with the increase in SFTPB in the mouse lung treated with intratracheal instillation of SiO2 suspension. Studies have shown that SFTPB levels are highly susceptible to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) induced by sepsis and that its gene mutations are associated with interstitial lung disease and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease in children. In addition, pro-mature SFTPB is significantly elevated in the peripheral blood of patients with lung cancer and can therefore be used as a potential screening indicator for lung cancer. In the mouse model of pulmonary fibrosis in this study, the significant increase in SFTPB persisted until the 112nd day of modeling and was accompanied by an increase in HECTD1. Next, we further investigated the relationship between HECTD1 and SFTPB.

HECTD1 knockdown affected pro-mature SFTPB protein levels in lung epithelial cells

To investigate the effect of SiO2 on HECTD1 protein expression in the lung epithelium, MLE-12 and BEAS-2B cells were cultured in vitro and stimulated with SiO2. The expression of SFTPB is closely related to ubiquitin-proteasome activity [26]. Spatial transcriptomics showed no increase in SFTPB mRNA levels in fibrotic lung tissue, whereas proteomics and Western blot analysis showed a significant increase in SFTPB protein levels. Therefore, we speculated that HECTD1, as an E3 ubiquitin ligase, may regulate the expression of SFTPB at the post-transcriptional level. To verify this hypothesis, we knocked down the HECTD1 protein levels in MLE-12 and BEAS-2B cells using CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing technology and treated them with SiO2. Western blot analysis indicated that the downregulation of HECTD1 in MLE-12 cells partially reversed the increase in pro-mature SFTPB induced by SiO2 (Fig. 9D–F). The downregulation of HECTD1 in BEAS-2B cells significantly reversed the increase in pro-mature SFTPB induced by SiO2 (Fig. 9G–I). Based on these findings, we conclude that the increase in HECTD1 mediated the upregulation of pro-mature SFTPB, resulting in a large deposition of SFTPB in the lung ECM.

HECTD1, a member of the HECT family, mediates the ubiquitination of protein substrates. To explore whether the processing of pro-SFTPB is related to ubiquitination, we detected pro-SFTPB protein binding to Ub through co-immunoprecipitation. MLE-12 was cultured in vitro and treated with silica for 48 h. SFTPB antibody was incubated with the obtained cellular proteins, through which pro-SFTPBs of different molecular weights and the proteins bound to them can be captured. Western blot analysis showed that pro-SFTPB bound to K48-Ub weighted between 40 and 55 kD and was increased under silica treatment for 48 h (Fig. 10A–E). These results suggest that the processing and maturation of pro-SFTPB are closely related to ubiquitination. Discussion

The mouse model of pulmonary fibrosis was established through intratracheal instillation of SiO2 suspension, and pulmonary fibrosis was evaluated from the perspectives of CT, lung function, and histopathology until the 112nd day of modeling. From the 56th to 112nd days of modeling, CT showed that high-density shadows tended to have a progressive trend of aggravation and that lung function indicators continued to deteriorate, indicating that the model mice suffered progressive irreversible pulmonary fibrosis. Among the detected indicators, the lung organ coefficient of the model mice was persistently higher than that of the control mice, but its value decreased on the 112nd day of modeling, which was supposed to be a result of shrinkage of the pulmonary fibrosis scar. H&E staining and Sirius red staining confirmed that the lung tissue of mice collected on the 112nd day of modeling showed aging of fibrotic nodules, demonstrating that pulmonary fibrosis had entered the end stage.

In this study we established a silica-induced mouse model of pulmonary fibrosis, which recapitulates key features of occupational silicosis and coal workers' pneumoconiosis, thereby modeling pulmonary fibrosis triggered by particulate foreign bodies. This stimulus is distinct from other etiologies such as soluble toxins or infectious agents, as demonstrated by differences in histopathological characteristics and fibrotic progression. Silica-induced lesions typically persist beyond six months and exhibit limited spontaneous resolution [27], whereas bleomycin-induced lung injury generally stabilizes or partially regresses within 6–8 weeks [28], limiting its suitability for modeling drug-related non-resolving fibrotic conditions. The current model utilizes acute high-dose exposure via a single intratracheal instillation of silica suspension, differing from chronic, low-dose inhalation exposures encountered in occupational environments. Consequently, the total dose in this model may substantially exceed real-world long-term dust exposure levels, leading to discrepancies in the temporal development and spatial distribution of fibrosis compared with human silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis [29].

The functional disorder of AT2 cells is one of reasons for lung fibrosis, of which the abnormal proliferation, apoptosis, aging, and can eventually lead to the activation of fibroblasts, ultimately resulting in the deposition of lung collagen fibers [[30], [31], [32]]. Consistent with the findings of Haschek et al. [33], we found that SiO2 stimulated AT2 cells in vitro, resulting in an increase in cell viability and proliferation. Interestingly, AT2 cells secreted a large amount of STTPB in our mouse pulmonary fibrosis model until the 112nd day. It has been shown that an SNP of Sftpb is associated with secondary lung lesions in patients with moderate and severe cystic fibrosis [34]. In addition, the SFTPB 1580C (rs1130866) variant has been identified as a risk factor for lung diseases such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), COPD, and ARDS [15,35,36]. In our mouse model, Cchord and FVC decreased significantly on day 56, and other lung function indicators, including IC, MMEF, TLC, and FEV75, decreased successively on days 84 and 112, indicating the end stage of pulmonary fibrosis. During the progression of pulmonary fibrosis until the end stage, the level of SFTPB protein detected in model mice was continuously higher than that detected in NS mice; SFTPB protein was deposited in the lung ECM.

Regarding the role of SFTPB in pulmonary fibrosis, we propose two conjectures here. First, the continuous increase in SFTPB is a compensatory beneficial response of AT2 with the decline in lung function, which is to combat the increase in lung tension caused by pulmonary fibrosis; thus, hindering its secretion may lead to a more rapid decline in lung function in this pathological state. Second, although SFTPB, as a surfactant protein, has the effect of reducing lung surface tension, it is oversecreted and abnormally deposited in the lung interstitium in this pathological state, so it does not play a role in improving lung compliance but does lead to thickening of respiratory membranes and impaired gas exchange. The construction of a pulmonary fibrosis model using Sftpb knockout mice and the detection of relevant pulmonary function indicators will help to clarify the role of SFTPB. In a study of 210 patients with contiguous primary Sjögren syndrome, patients with severe glandular involvement, hypergammaglobulinemia, leukopenia, extraglandular manifestations, and positive anti-Ro/La antibodies had higher SFTPD levels, and a specific association between SFTPD levels and lung involvement may be pathophysiologically important [37]. In the previous study, we observed an increase in IgA expression in the pulmonary ECM of the fibrotic lung, and whether there is an association between IgA and SFTPB may be worthy of further investigation.

Studies have shown that Ub mediates Sftpb transcription by regulating thyroid transcription factor (NKX2-1) [26], promoting SFTPB protein production. In our mouse model of lung fibrosis, we found that the continuous increase in SFTPB was accompanied by an increase in K48-Ub. Pro-SFTPB can exist in several molecular weights at different processing stages, including 42, 23–25, and 9–10 kDa [38,39]. The whole membrane image of Western blot analysis (Fig. 10) showed that the molecular weights of pro-SFTPBs we detected were 55–70 kDa, around 55 kDa, and 55–40 kDa. The molecular weight of pro-SFTPB bound to K48-Ub was approximately 55 kDa by co-immunoprecipitation, suggesting that ubiquitination plays an important role in the early processing of pro-SFTPB at approximately 55 kDa. Moreover, in vitro experiments showed that HECTD1 knockdown inhibited the production of pro-SFTPB protein. Several clinical studies have reported that pro-SFTPB is elevated in the plasma of patients with lung cancer [40,41], and lung type 2 epithelial cells, as the only cell source of pro-SFTPB, have become the key research cells. HECTD1 plays an important role in physiological and pathological processes, such as placental development, lipid transport, and colitis [[42], [43], [44]]. Homozygous mutations in Hectd1 can lead to defects in cardiac development, characterized by ascending aorta hypoplasia, atresia, semilunar valve dysplasia, and ventricular septal defect [45]. Hectd1-deficient hematopoietic stem cells exhibit significant deficiencies in transplantation capacity, ex vivo maintenance, and decreased protein synthesis and growth rates under stressful conditions. Mechanistically, HECTD1 ubiquitinates and degrades the assembly factor ZNF622 of the ribosomal 60S subunit, and the loss of Hectd1 leads to the accumulation of ZNF622 and the anti-association factor eIF6 on 60S, resulting in a defect in the 60S/40S connection [19]. During the progressive exacerbation of pulmonary fibrosis, we detected persistent increases in the expression of HECTD1 and Ub and the presence of cellular colocalization of HECTD1 and SFTPB, suggesting that HECTD1 plays a positive role in the SiO2-induced increase in SFTPB. Spatial transcriptome sequencing showed no significant change in the mRNA level of Sftpb in fibrotic lung tissue, suggesting that the increase in SFTPB expression may be regulated by non-transcriptional factors. Furthermore, HECTD1 knockdown partially reversed the silica-induced increase in SFTPB.

Our study demonstrated for the first time that the E3 ubiquitin ligase HECTD1 is involved in the production of pro-SFTPB protein in AT2 cells, providing a potential control target for the study of the dysfunction of AT2 cell secretion of SFTPB in clinical practice.
