Structural transitions and molecular interactions of zein induced by ethanol and extrusion
Authors: Yingying Zhang, Chang Wu, Xin Fang, Lei Xu, Huishan Shen, Mengkun Song, Hua Zhang, Guangzhong Luan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEthanol improves the zein network in gluten-free dough, but the molecular mechanisms underlying this improvement remain incompletely elucidated. This study investigated ethanol plasticization (0–50 %, v/v) on commercial zein (C-zein) and extrusion-modified zein (E-zein, 140 °C). Both self-assembled into spherical microparticles merging into membranes at 50 % ethanol. Ethanol increased the porous structure and reduced the glass transition temperature (Tg) of zein, whereas extrusion showed the opposite trend. Analysis showed ethanol bonded to zein primarily through hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, and van der Waals forces. This binding induced the partial unfolding of zein molecules, thereby exposing additional hydrophobic residues and hydrogen-bonding sites, facilitating the formation of zein networks predominantly driven by non-covalent forces. Plasticization with 10 % ethanol above 40 °C is recommended to optimize E-zein functionality. These findings provide molecular-level insights into the interactions (hydrophobic, hydrogen-bonding, and van der Waals) between zein and ethanol and guide strategies for improving gluten-free zein foods.Keywords: Zein, Extrusion, Ethanol, Interaction, Molecular docking

Introduction

Zein can be used as a structural protein in starch-based doughs to confer cohesion and viscoelasticity. However, the properties of doughs and the quality of the products prepared with zein and starch alone were not satisfactory (Berta et al., 2019; Schober et al., 2008; Zhang et al., 2024; Zhang, Xu, Zhang, Hu, & Luan, 2022). For example, phase separation occurred in the preparation of zein-starch doughs using Mixograph (Sly et al., 2014), in addition to breads prepared with zein-starch, which were dense in structure, hard, and crumbly in texture (Berta et al., 2019). These undesirable phenomena are primarily attributed to the high molecular rigidity of zein, which leads to poor compatibility with starch granules in the dough system. Therefore, it is necessary to enhance the molecular flexibility of zein to improve its functionality in dough systems.

Ethanol has emerged as a promising plasticizer to improve the molecular flexibility of zein (Akin et al., 2019; Mattice & Marangoni, 2020; Smith et al., 2017; Yu et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2024; Zhang, Gao, Zhang, Dong, et al., 2022). Studies indicated that ethanol plasticization produced softer, smoother, and more extensible zein networks and zein-starch/flour doughs, while also increasing bread specific volume (Akin et al., 2019; Smith et al., 2017). Our prior research confirmed that adding 10 % (v/v) ethanol enhanced the smoothness and extensibility of zein-starch dough. We demonstrated that this improvement resulted from ethanol-induced formation of a finer, more uniformly distributed fibrous zein network within the dough matrix, which enhanced starch-protein compatibility and overall dough structure (Zhang et al., 2024). Furthermore, unlike alternative plasticizers such as acetic acid (King et al., 2016; Oguntoyinbo et al., 2018; Sly et al., 2014; Sun et al., 2022; Zhang, Gao, Zhang, Dong, et al., 2022; Zhang, Wang, et al., 2023), ethanol volatilizes more readily during cooking and imparts no acidic odor or taste. Beyond its plasticizing function, ethanol also exhibits antimicrobial properties that can enhance the microbial safety of food products. Similar to other novel processing technologies, such as electric fields (Xue et al., 2025), the efficacy of ethanol as a solvent-based antimicrobial treatment depends on the food matrix. Overall, as the primary component of alcoholic beverages and a natural by-product of yeast fermentation, food-grade ethanol holds greater promise for application in staple foods.

Although studies indicate that ethanol improves zein functionality and dough properties (Akin et al., 2019; Smith et al., 2017), the underlying molecular mechanisms remain poorly explored. Investigating the interaction between ethanol and zein could elucidate how ethanol modifies zein structure and function, thereby enhancing zein-starch dough performance. Due to zein's optimal solubility within the ethanol concentration range of 60 %–90 % (Keshanidokht et al., 2022), current research on zein-ethanol interaction has primarily focused on aqueous systems containing 50 %–90 % (v/v) ethanol, investigating aspects such as solution rheological properties, self-assembly behavior, and conformational transitions of zein (Nonthanum, Lee and Padua, 2012, Nonthanum, Lee and Padua, 2013; Wang & Padua, 2010; Yu et al., 2020). However, ethanol concentrations used in gluten-free doughs were much lower, ranging from 2.5 % to 15 % (v/v) (Zhang, Xu, Zhang, Hu, & Luan, 2022), where zein was insoluble but may undergo changes in secondary structure or protein interactions due to the altered solvent polarity (Smith et al., 2014). Furthermore, given reports that extrusion modification of zein benefits the elasticity of gluten-free doughs, we employed 10 % (v/v) ethanol to plasticize extruded zein in an attempt to enhance its functionality in dough systems further (Zhang et al., 2024). In practice, however, ethanol provided limited functional improvement to extruded zein. Extruded zein shows higher rigidity; therefore, it requires stronger plasticization. Thus, investigating the plasticizing effect of ethanol concentrations exceeding 10 % (v/v) on extruded zein is imperative.

Current research on zein-ethanol interactions has largely focused on aqueous systems containing 50 %–90 % (v/v) ethanol, while structural changes in zein below 50 % (v/v) ethanol remain poorly understood. Building on a simulation of low-concentration ethanol (2.5 %–15 %, v/v) in gluten-free dough systems, this study systematically investigated the molecular interactions and structural transitions of zein across a broader ethanol concentration range of up to 50 % (v/v). Specifically, ethanol ranging from 0 % to 50 % (v/v) was used to plasticize zein and zein extrudate (with pure water as the control). The dissolved and the undissolved proteins were characterized to obtain insights into the structural changes of zein and zein-ethanol interactions under poorly soluble conditions. The work aimed to reveal the reasons for improving of zein-starch dough properties by ethanol at the molecular interactions level. As well as to elucidate the plasticizing effect of ethanol on extrusion-modified zein to obtain an equilibrium between extrusion modification and ethanol plasticization. The results will provide a theoretical basis for improving the functionality of zein in gluten-free doughs.

Materials and methods

Materials

Commercial zein (C-zein, 93.7 % protein, db) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Co. (St Louis, MO, USA). Premixed Protein Marker (Low) was purchased from Takara (Japan). Coomassie Blue R250 and β-mercaptoethanol were produced by Sigma-Aldrich Co. (St Louis, MO, USA). Potassium bromide, spectral grade, was purchased from Kemiou Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China). Ethanol was produced by Shanghai Colon Chemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Other chemicals were all analytical grade.

Zein extrusion modification

Zein was modified using a twin-screw extruder (Eurolab 16, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) with a screw diameter of 16 mm, a length-to-diameter (L/D) ratio of 25, and a die diameter of 3 mm. Prior to extrusion, the moisture content of the zein was adjusted to 22 % (dry basis) and equilibrated at 4 °C for at least 12 h. The extrusion process was carried out with the barrel temperature zones set at 30, 50, 80, 100, 130, and 140 °C, respectively. The screw speed was maintained at 350 rpm, and the feed rate was 0.5 kg/h. During operation, the die pressure fluctuated between 30 and 40 bar. The extruded zein (E-zein) was dried in an oven at 30 °C, ground, and passed through a 100 mesh sieve.

Zein treated with different concentrations of aqueous ethanol

Zein (5.0 g) was dispersed in 37.5 mL of aqueous ethanol (0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 30, 40, and 50 % v/v) and shaken at 40 °C (above zein's glass transition temperature) for 24 h. Samples were left for 1 h, and the visual appearance was observed. The supernatant was collected, and its turbidity was assessed by measuring optical density at 600 nm using a Spark™ microplate reader (Tecan Austria GmbH, Austria) (Luo et al., 2015). The undissolved zein fraction was collected and lyophilized at −50 °C for 48 h in a freeze-drier (FD5-3, Gold-Sim Technology Group, China) for further analysis.

SDS-PAGE analysis

The protein molecular distribution of the dissolved zein was evaluated by SDS-PAGE (Electrophoresis apparatus DYY-6C, Beijing Liuyi Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) (Zhang, Qin, et al., 2023; Zhang, Wang, et al., 2023). Gels were stained with Coomassie Blue R-250, and the gel image was captured by the Bio-Rad System Gel Doc XR+ (Biorad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Morphology of the dissolved zein evaporation-induced self-assembly (EISA)

The self-assembly behavior of dissolved proteins in various aqueous ethanol was characterized by SEM. A 10 μL aliquot of the supernatant was dropped onto a silicon wafer and air-dried at ambient temperature for 12 h. The wafer was then mounted on conductive adhesive, sputter-coated with gold, and imaged using a field emission scanning electron microscope (FEI Nova Nano SEM-450, USA) operating at 10 kV (Zhang, Xu, Zhang, Li, et al., 2022).

Microstructure measurement of the undissolved zein

The undissolved zein was lyophilized and stuck on the conductive adhesive, and then observed with a scanning electron microscope (FEI Nova Nano SEM-450, UAS) at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV.

Glass transition temperature (Tg) determination of the undissolved zein

Zein was dispersed in various aqueous ethanol solutions. The undissolved fraction was recovered by centrifugation, then air-dried until reaching a moisture content of approximately 10.0 % (w/w). The dried zein (10 mg) was weighed into an aluminum pan and sealed. Samples were equilibrated at 20 °C for 2 min, then heated to 120 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min using an empty pan as a reference. Sample's Tg was calculated from the heat flow curve. Each sample was measured in triplicate.

Surface hydrophobicity (H0) determination of the undissolved zein

The H0 of the undissolved zein was determined using a bromophenol blue (BPB) binding assay based on Zhao et al. (2022) with modifications. Briefly, the protein sample (40 mg) was dispersed in 1.0 mL of 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and shaken for 10 min. Subsequently, 50 μL of BPB solution (1 mg/mL) was added. The mixture was shaken for 10 min at ambient temperature and centrifuged at 7000×g for 10 min. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 595 nm. Buffer without protein was used as a blank. Intermolecular forces determination of the undissolved zein

Changes in zein intermolecular forces induced by aqueous ethanol were evaluated based on sample solubility in specific solvents, following the method described by Yang et al. (2020) with slight modifications. The undissolved zein fraction was dispersed in the following denaturant solutions: S1 (Ionic Bonds): 0.6 M NaCl; S2 (Hydrogen Bonds): 0.6 M NaCl + 1.5 M Urea; S3 (Hydrophobic Interactions): 0.6 M NaCl + 8 M Urea; S4 (Disulfide Bonds): 0.6 M NaCl + 8 M Urea + 0.5 M β-Me. The results are expressed as the proportion of protein solubilized in each fraction relative to the total protein in samples. All analyses were conducted in at least triplicate.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) determination of the undissolved zein

Changes in protein secondary structure induced by aqueous ethanol were analyzed using a Vertex70 FTIR spectrometer (Bruker Optik, Ettlingen, Germany). Background spectra were acquired under identical conditions without protein. The amide I region (1700–1600 cm−1) was subjected to Fourier self-deconvolution of its second derivative spectrum using OMNIC software (Thermo Scientific, Madison, WI, USA) and PeakFit v4.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Molecular docking simulation

The molecular docking technique was used to gain further insight into the interaction between zein and ethanol. Commercial zein is basically composed of the α-subunit, which has two protein bands of 19 and 22 kDa, respectively. The structures of 19 kDa (ID: A0A317YFM5) and 22 kDa (ID: P04699) of α-zein were downloaded from the UniProt protein database (https://www.uniprot.org/). The 3D structure of ethanol was downloaded from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The docking process referred to the method of Liu et al. (2022), and the best result was further analyzed using Discovery Studio Visualizer v25.1.0.2.

Statistical analysis

SPSS statistical software (Version 20.0, IBM, Armonk, New York, USA) was used. Shapiro-Wilk and Levene tests were conducted to determine the normality and homogeneity of the variable data, respectively. Differences between data that met both normality and homoscedasticity were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Duncan's test (p < 0.05). Otherwise, differences between their variables were analyzed using a nonparametric test (Kruskal-Wallis H), p < 0.05. Results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Results and discussion

The appearance and solution turbidity

A series of zein dispersions of varying ethanol contents was prepared to evaluate the solubility of zeins at 40 °C. Zein exhibited a distinct glass transition, indicated by adhesion to container walls and agglomeration (Fig. 1A). The agglomerations of C-zein disappeared when the ethanol reached 50 % (v/v). E-zein agglomerated at low ethanol contents (0–20 %, v/v), with agglomerates dissolving at higher contents. Turbidity measurements of zein-ethanol solutions were conducted to further assess protein solubilization across ethanol contents (Fig. 1B). The turbidity of aqueous ethanol with C-zein and E-zein increased significantly with increasing ethanol contents, which was highly likely due to more proteins migrating from the solid phase to the liquid phase. This finding aligned with the study by Keshanidokht et al. (2022), which showed that ethanol content influenced the solubility of zein. Furthermore, both C-zein and E-zein demonstrated low solubility in 0–30 % (v/v) ethanol, moderate solubility in 40 % (v/v) ethanol, and substantially higher solubility in 50 % (v/v) ethanol, indicating that extrusion modification did not alter zein's solubility response trends to ethanol. At 50 % (v/v) ethanol, C-zein exhibited higher solubility than E-zein, attributable to physicochemical changes induced by extrusion. Overall, these results demonstrated that extrusion modification altered zein's solubility, dispersion behavior, and glass transition characteristics. Additionally, the formation of protein aggregates linked by covalent or non-covalent interactions can also cause an increase in the turbidity (Liu et al., 2020). So, both reducing and non-reducing electrophoresis were employed to evaluate zein molecular weight distribution.

Visual appearance of zein dispersed in 0–50 % (v/v) of aqueous ethanol (A) and the optical density of samples at 600 nm (B).

Molecular weight of dissolved proteins

SDS-PAGE was performed to analyze the molecular weight characteristics of the solubilized zein (Fig. 2). The protein abundance of C-zein was increased when the ethanol content reached 40 % (v/v), and two typical α-subunit bands appeared, which were consistent with the reported molecular weights around 19 kDa and 22 kDa (Nonthanum et al., 2012; Zhang, Xu, Zhang, Li, et al., 2022). The abundance of α-subunit bands increased dramatically at 50 % (v/v) ethanol, aligning with turbidity results (Fig. 1B), which confirmed that the increased turbidity in Fig. 1B is primarily due to the increased zein solubility. Additionally, a distinct protein band was observed below the 14.3 kDa. Given that δ-zein has a molecular weight of around 10 kDa and constitutes 1 %–5 % of total zein, this band likely corresponds to δ-zein. Compared to the β- and γ-subunits, the amino acid composition of the δ-subunit is closer to that of the α-subunit (Nonthanum et al., 2012), which results in a difficult separation of the α-zein and δ-zein. Similar to C-zein, E-zein showed a dramatic increase in α-subunit band abundance at 50 % (v/v) ethanol, though at a lower relative intensity than C-zein. This indicated reduced solubility of the extruded protein, consistent with the turbidity results (Fig. 1B). In addition, bands of high molecular weight were observed in proteins dissolved in 50 % (v/v) ethanol, which may be oligomers formed by protein subunits (Nonthanum et al., 2012). In summary, the α-subunit of zein began to dissolve when ethanol content reached 40 % (v/v), and extrusion modification at 140 °C reduced α-subunit solubility but did not alter its molecular weight.

Morphology of evaporative self-assembly of dissolved proteins

While C-zein self-assembly has been extensively studied in high-concentration ethanol systems, its behavior below 50 % (v/v) ethanol remains less explored. Previous studies reported C-zein's insolubility in low-concentration ethanol (Erickson et al., 2020; Smith et al., 2014); however, this study confirmed partial dissolution even in 5 % (v/v) aqueous ethanol. This raises questions about potential differences in self-assembly mechanisms between zein dissolved in low-concentration ethanol versus traditional 50 %–95 % ethanol systems. In addition, it is worth analyzing whether the effect of extrusion modification on the physicochemical properties of zein involves its self-assembly ability. Therefore, we examined the microscopic morphology of C-zein and E-zein following solvent evaporation in 0–50 % (v/v) aqueous ethanol (Fig. 3).

SEM images of soluble protein self-assembly in 0–50 % (v/v) aqueous ethanol. (A) and (B) represent commercial and extruded zein.

During solvent evaporation, ethanol evaporates faster, and the polarity of the solvent gradually increases. As a relatively hydrophobic protein, zein forms hydrophobic junctions that drive self-assembly (Wang & Padua, 2010). In 0–30 % (v/v) ethanol, the self-assembled structures of both C-zein and E-zein consisted predominantly of salt crystals, with no observable spherical protein microparticles. Although turbidity and electrophoretic data indicated limited dissolution in this range, protein self-assembly was likely obscured by salt crystallization. At 40 % (v/v) ethanol, salt crystals were virtually absent, replaced by protein microspheres throughout the field of view. Increasing ethanol to 50 % (v/v) reduced microsphere size and induced a tendency toward particle fusion. Patel and Velikov (2014) suggested that high ethanol content would induce zein self-assembly to form a smaller particle size, while a high concentration of zein would lead to an increase in self-assembly particle size. Ethanol content and zein concentration are the main factors affecting protein self-assembly (Patel & Velikov, 2014; Wang & Padua, 2010).

Scholars have given different theories about the self-assembly behavior of zein. Yu et al. (2020) claimed that α-zein first self-assembled into microspheres based on the polymerization of nuclei, then the microspheres adhered to each other to form bead fibers, followed by the formation of nanofibers in the final stage; the process was accompanied by a structural transition from the disordered to the ordered. Wang and Padua (2010) argued that, as the evaporation of ethanol increased the solvent water content and hydrophilicity, the microspheres grew via the hydrophobic association, and the microspheres adsorbed on the central nucleus and self-assembled layer by layer on the nucleus. Dense spheres were obtained at higher concentrations of zein, and with a further increase in zein concentration, the microspheres gradually collapsed and fused into a membrane. The self-assembly process of C-zein and E-zein did not show the adsorption and aggregation of two or more microspheres, which seems to be more in line with the theory of microsphere collapse into a membrane by Wang and Padua (2010). Collectively, extrusion modification exhibited minimal impact on the evaporative self-assembly behavior of C-zein.

Microstructure of undissolved proteins

Studies have shown that zein-starch dough containing ethanol exhibits softer, smoother, and more extensible properties (Akin et al., 2019; Smith et al., 2017). As reported by Li et al. (2012) and Mattice and Marangoni (2020), the protein structure of zein partially unfolded in response to ethanol plasticization. Surface solvent accessibility and water hydration of zein may be accordingly altered. Hydration alterations are associated with the surface and internal structure of the samples. The effect of ethanol plasticization on the microstructure of the undissolved zein was analyzed, as shown in Fig. 4.

SEM images of zein plasticized by 0–50 % (v/v) aqueous ethanol. (A) and (B) represent commercial and extruded zein.

Both pure water and ethanol-water systems induced a honeycomb structure in C-zein, with ethanol plasticization yielding more pronounced porosity than water plasticization. Ethanol-induced porosity increased zein's contact surface area, which will correspondingly facilitate hydration and accelerate the glass transition. This might be the reason for the significant improvement of zein-starch dough properties by ethanol. Besides, the porous structure of zein was similar to that of freeze-dried wet gluten reported in the literature (Han et al., 2020), suggesting C-zein's potential to fulfill structural roles analogous to gluten in gluten-free dough systems. While E-zein similarly underwent glass transition, evidenced by blocky aggregation in 0–20 % (v/v) ethanol. E-zein's structural porosity was less pronounced than that of C-zein, which implied that ethanol had a limited plasticizing effect on E-zein. E-zein formed fibrous clusters at higher ethanol contents (30 %–50 %, v/v) that increased protein-solvent interfacial area, thereby facilitating hydration. Overall, extrusion modification and ethanol content jointly modulate the structural properties of zein: ethanol plasticization enhances protein structure porosity, while extrusion diminishes it.

Glass transition temperature of undissolved proteins

The ability of zein to form viscoelastic networks is dependent on mixing temperature, as zein would not form viscoelastic protein networks at temperatures below its glass transition temperature (Tg). As zein's Tg is above ambient conditions, making it hard and unworkable at ambient temperature. Extensive evidence indicates that Tg is moisture-dependent, as water plasticization can significantly reduce the zein's Tg (Jeong et al., 2017; Mattice & Marangoni, 2020). Additionally, ethanol showed a stronger plasticizing effect on zein than water (Mattice & Marangoni, 2020; Zhang, Gao, Zhang, Dong, et al., 2022).

The Tg of C-zein and E-zein plasticized with varying contents of aqueous ethanol was determined (Fig. 5A). When plasticized with 5 % (v/v) ethanol, C-zein exhibited a Tg of 67.6 °C, which was not significantly different from that observed with pure water. As the ethanol content increased to 10 %–20 % (v/v), the Tg of C-zein decreased markedly to approximately 46.8 °C. Further increases in ethanol content resulted in a gradual stabilization of C-zein's Tg. This plateau effect at higher ethanol concentrations aligned with the findings of Taylor et al. (2013), who reported that the Tg of zein stabilized around 20 °C under fully hydrated conditions. The Tg of E-zein plasticized with pure water was about 74.2 °C, higher than that of C-zein (67.6 °C). A significant decrease in Tg was observed for E-zein at 5 %–10 % (v/v) ethanol, with no significant differences detected across the10 %–40 % (v/v) ethanol range. At 50 % (v/v) ethanol, the Tg dropped to approximately 48.5 °C, still higher than the corresponding value for C-zein (∼46.4 °C). These results indicated that extrusion modification increased the zein's Tg to some extent. Given that increasing the ethanol content from 10 % to 40 % (v/v) did not further reduce the Tg of E-zein, the use of 10 % (v/v) ethanol is considered appropriate for plasticizing E-zein in the dough system from both a cost-saving perspective and practical application in doughs.

Glass transition temperature (A) and surface hydrophobicity (B) of zein after plasticization by aqueous ethanol with content of 0, 5 %, 10 %, 15 %, 20 %, 30 %, 40 %, and 50 % (v/v). Different letters of the same sample indicate significant differences (p < 0.05) according to one-way ANOVA and Duncan's test.

Moreover, as shown in Fig. 4B, E-zein exhibited a relatively dense structure compared to the porous morphology of ethanol-plasticized C-zein. This structural compactness likely hinders the penetration of ethanol and water into the protein matrix, thereby limiting hydration and restricting the glass transition. Therefore, more intensive plasticization conditions, such as a hydration temperature above 40 °C combined with 10 % (v/v) ethanol, may be necessary to fully realize the functional potential of E-zein in practical applications.

Hydrophobicity of undissolved proteins

BPB, similar to fluorescent probes, can bind to the hydrophobic sites of proteins (Chelh et al., 2006). By comparing the absorbance of the BPB solution before and after reacting with the protein, the quantity of bound BPB and free BPB can be inferred, with more bound BPB indicating a higher H0 of zein. The H0 determination results of zein after ethanol-plasticization are shown in Fig. 5B. As the ethanol contents increased (0–15 %, v/v), the H0 of both C-zein and E-zein gradually rose, suggesting partial unfolding of zein proteins in aqueous ethanol and the consequent exposure of initially buried hydrophobic regions, thus increasing the measured H0 of the proteins. However, when ethanol content reached 20 % (v/v), the H0 of C-zein began to decline slightly, and decreased further at 30 % (v/v) ethanol. It then increased again within the 40–50 % (v/v) ethanol range. In contrast, E-zein exhibited a different behavior: its H0 continued to increase gradually with ethanol content, peaking at 50 % (v/v) ethanol. This divergence in H0 trends between C-zein and E-zein may be attributed to differences in sample particle size. As shown in the microstructure of undissolved proteins in Fig. 4, C-zein plasticized with 30 %–50 % ethanol showed an overall larger particle size compared to E-zein. From the dispersion state of the two proteins in ethanol solution (Fig. 1), it can be observed that in 30 %–50 % (v/v) aqueous ethanol, C-zein underwent glass transition to form a viscoelastic mass (transitioning from a glassy state to a highly elastic state). The undissolved proteins, after freeze-drying, are not dispersed into small particles like E-zein but instead form large protein clumps. BPB binding to proteins occurs on the protein surface. For the same protein mass, smaller particles provide a larger total surface area, allowing more hydrophobic sites to be exposed and available for BPB binding. In contrast, larger protein particles have a smaller total surface area, and many internal hydrophobic sites remain embedded and inaccessible to BPB. Therefore, the decrease in specific surface area of the C-zein caused a decrease in bound BPB.

In addition, the H0 of C-zein plasticized with 0–20 % (v/v) ethanol exceeded that of the corresponding E-zein. During extrusion, zein transformed from solid to molten under high temperature, pressure, and shear forces. In this process, the hydrophobic groups of proteins originally buried internally were exposed to the surface, theoretically increasing hydrophobicity (Zhang, Kang, Cheng, Cui, & Abd El-Aty, 2022). However, extrusion modification increased the structural density of zein. The contact surface between zein and BPB decreased, resulting in smaller measured H0 values. Nonetheless, this did not affect the trend in the effect of ethanol content on the surface hydrophobicity of proteins.

Hydrophobicity was reported to be crucial for the viscoelasticity of zein (Smith et al., 2014). The increase in H0 of zein induced by ethanol facilitated protein-protein interactions and the formation of a viscoelastic protein network. In summary, extrusion modification and ethanol content jointly affected the H0 of zein. Ethanol induced the exposure of zein hydrophobic groups and increased the H0 of zein with the increase of ethanol content, which favored the formation of a viscoelastic protein network through hydrophobic interactions.

Intermolecular forces of undissolved proteins

It is widely accepted that zein aggregates through non-covalent interactions, primarily hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding, to form viscoelastic protein networks (Erickson et al., 2021; Nonthanum et al., 2012; Schober et al., 2011; Smith, Bean, Selling, Sessa and Aramouni, 2014, Smith, Bean, Selling, Sessa and Aramouni, 2017). Intermolecular forces within undissolved zein proteins were characterized (Fig. 6). Ionic and hydrogen bonds played minor roles in both C-zein and E-zein. In contrast, hydrophobic interactions constituted the primary force stabilizing their protein structures, followed by disulfide bonds. This is related to the unique amino acid composition of zein. Zein contains a high proportion of nonpolar, hydrophobic amino acids such as leucine, alanine, and proline, while polar amino acids constitute a smaller proportion. Consequently, hydrophobic interactions play a dominant role in zein, whereas hydrogen bonds remain relatively weak. Ionic interactions occur between amino acid side chains with opposite net charges. Due to the scarcity of ionizable charged residues, ionic bonds contribute minimally to zein's structural stability (Nonthanum et al., 2012; Smith et al., 2014; Zhang, Xu, Zhang, Hu, & Luan, 2022).

As described in section 3.6, the determination results of C-zein were influenced by the formation of protein clumps. Therefore, the intermolecular forces of E-zein were more objective. Following plasticization with 30 %–50 % (v/v) ethanol, E-zein exhibited enhanced ionic bonds, hydrogen bonds, and hydrophobic interactions. This strengthening was likely related to ethanol-induced formation of fibrous clusters (Fig. 4), which increased the protein-solvent interfacial area, thereby improving solubility and hydration capacity. Overall, ethanol plasticization enhanced non-covalent interactions of zein, predominantly hydrophobic, but also hydrogen and ionic bonds, with enhancement correlating to ethanol contents. These findings corresponded to surface hydrophobicity results (Fig. 5B), wherein ethanol-mediated exposure of buried hydrophobic groups strengthened hydrophobic interactions. Next, how does ethanol interact with zein? Further exploration is needed.

Secondary structure of proteins

Secondary structural changes in C-zein and E-zein following aqueous ethanol plasticization are presented in Fig. 7. Relative proportions of secondary structures were determined by de-convoluting the amide I region and calculating second derivatives (Fig. 7B and Fig. 7D). The 1610–1630 cm−1 and 1630–1640 cm−1 regions represents intermolecular and intramolecular β-sheet structures, also known as low-frequency β-sheet structures; the 1640–1650 cm−1 and 1650–1660 cm−1 regions correspond to random coil and α-helix structures; the region between 1660 and 1675 cm−1 represents β-turn structures; and the 1675–1700 cm−1 region indicates high-frequency β-sheet structures (Chen et al., 2021; Jeong et al., 2017; Zhang, Gao, Zhang, Dong, et al., 2022).

Secondary structure of zeins plasticized by various aqueous ethanol. (A) and (C) represent ATR-FTIR spectra of C-zein and E-zein, respectively. (B) and (D) represent the secondary structure proportion of zeins plasticized by ethanol at contents of 0, 5 %, 10 %, 15 %, 20 %, 30 %, 40 %, and 50 % (v/v), respectively. (E) and (F) represent the proportions of ordered conformations (β-sheet and α-helix) and disordered conformations (random coil and β-turn) in C-zein and E-zein, respectively. Different letters of the same secondary structure indicate significant differences (p < 0.05) according to one-way ANOVA and Duncan's test.

C-zein and E-zein exhibited similar ethanol-dependent structural trends. Extrusion modification did not alter the trend of protein secondary structure changes with ethanol content. Increasing ethanol content initially elevated intermolecular β-sheet structure, followed by a reduction, while intramolecular β-sheet structure progressively decreased. β-sheet structure and α-helix structure are regarded as ordered conformations with relatively high stability, while β-turn structure and random coil structure are disordered conformations with relatively poor stability (Choi & Ma, 2007). The ordered and disordered conformations were statistically summed and shown in Fig. 7E and Fig. 7F. With the increase of ethanol content, both zeins showed a trend of gradually decreasing ordered conformations and increasing disordered conformations. Ordered conformations govern proteins with rigidity, while disordered conformations endow proteins with flexibility. Ethanol plasticization increased protein molecular flexibility, consistent with the protein unfolding and increased H0 (Fig. 5B). The increased flexibility can make hydrophobic residues and hydrogen bonding sites more exposed and able to participate in binding. This aligned with strengthened non-covalent protein interactions (Fig. 6), ultimately promoting the development of zein network driven by non-covalent interactions and producing softer, more extensible zein-starch dough.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking simulation efficiently investigates interactions and binding energies between small molecules and target macromolecules. In this study, the 19 kDa and 22 kDa subunits of α-zein, primary components of zein, served as receptor macromolecules, with ethanol as the ligand (Fig. 8A). Docking results revealed ethanol insertion into zein's active sites. Specifically, the 19 kDa subunit formed a complex with ethanol through Alkyl hydrophobic and Pi-alkyl hydrophobic interactions involving the amino acid residue TYR82 and LEU78, respectively, as well as a hydrogen bond interaction with amino acid residue MET79 (Fig. 8B1). Other interactions, such as van der Waals, were also formed in the complex. Similarly, the 22 kDa subunit engaged ethanol via Alkyl hydrophobic, Pi-alkyl hydrophobic, and van der Waals interactions (Fig. 8B2). These findings demonstrated that ethanol bonded to the active sites of zein proteins through hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and van der Waals forces.

Notably, in Fig. 8A and B, GLN (polar, uncharged), TYR (polar, uncharged), and LEU (nonpolar, hydrophobic) residues are commonly involved in zein-ethanol interactions. The binding of ethanol to LEU and TYR via hydrophobic interactions suggests that ethanol may penetrate and disrupt the hydrophobic core of zein, which is typically responsible for maintaining structural compactness. This disruption likely destabilized the native protein packing, facilitating partial unfolding and increasing molecular flexibility. Simultaneously, the van der Waals interactions with GLN and LEU indicated broad, non-specific binding that further contributed to structural loosening without inducing electrostatic repulsion or specific directional constraints. The participation of polar uncharged residues (GLN and TYR) signified that ethanol engaged both hydrophobic and polar regions, promoting a more extensive structural rearrangement than would be possible through hydrophobic interactions alone. Consequently, the combined effect of these interactions reduced structural rigidity, exposed additional hydrophobic patches and hydrogen bonding sites (Fig. 5 and Fig. 6), and enhanced the mobility of zein chains (Fig. 7). This ultimately supported the formation of a more flexible and extensible protein network in the dough system.

Conclusion

Zein, both in its commercial (C-zein) and extruded (E-zein) forms, was plasticized with 0–50 % (v/v) ethanol to investigate the structural transitions and molecular interactions of zein induced by ethanol and extrusion. C-zein and E-zein formed spherical microparticles after self-assembly. Ethanol enhanced the zein's structural porosity, hydration, and molecular flexibility while reducing the glass transition temperature (Tg). Conversely, extrusion modification induced structural densification of zein, decreased solubility and solvent accessibility, limited hydration, and increased Tg. Molecular docking revealed that ethanol binds to zein's active sites via hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, and van der Waals forces, resulting in the increased flexibility of zein molecules. This enhanced flexibility is expected to strengthen non-covalent protein interactions of zein and promote zein network development. Stronger plasticizing environments are recommended to stimulate the structural potential of E-zein, such as temperatures above 40 °C combined with 10 % (v/v) ethanol.

These findings elucidate the zein-ethanol interaction mechanism and provide a theoretical foundation for optimizing zein functionality in gluten-free foods. Building on the observed enhancement in molecular flexibility and non-covalent interactions induced by ethanol plasticization, future studies should focus on the following aspects: Establishing the correlation between zein's structural flexibility (e.g., Tg reduction, hydrophobic exposure) and macroscopic dough rheology; Verifying optimal processing conditions to maximize E-zein functionality; Quantify ethanol residues in various products (bread, pasta, steamed buns) prepared from ethanol-plasticized zein-starch dough.
