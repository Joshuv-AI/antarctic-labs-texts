False-positive PSMA uptake from urinary reflux into a seminal vesicle cyst in renal agenesis
Authors: Chee Han Ting, Dalveer Singh, John Yaxley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProstate-specific membrane antigen positron emission tomography is increasingly used in the evaluation of suspected prostate cancer, but radiotracer activity may occur in benign or congenital conditions and lead to false-positive interpretation. Although urinary tracer activity is often considered in patients with prior prostate or urinary tract surgery, congenital genitourinary anomalies can produce a similar imaging pitfall in patients without previous surgical intervention. We describe a seventy-three-year-old man with an elevated prostate-specific antigen level, no history of prior prostate or urinary tract surgery, and an indeterminate lesion on prostate magnetic resonance imaging who demonstrated focal tracer activity near the right seminal vesicle on prostate-specific membrane antigen positron emission tomographic imaging. Additional post-void imaging showed radiotracer accumulation within the right seminal vesicle region, and correlation with magnetic resonance imaging suggested reflux of urine into a cystic seminal vesicle remnant containing a small calculus. Further assessment revealed absence of the right kidney, consistent with a congenital renal tract anomaly linking the seminal vesicle abnormality and urinary reflux. Biopsy demonstrated benign prostatic tissue, confirming reflux of urine rather than malignancy as the cause of radiotracer uptake. This case highlights a rare interpretative pitfall in a patient without prior prostate surgery and emphasises that awareness of congenital anomalies involving the seminal vesicles can improve diagnostic confidence and reduce unnecessary biopsy in future cases.Keywords: PSMA, PET, Urinary reflux, Seminal vesicle cyst, Renal agenesis, False-positive imaging

Introduction

Prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) positron emission tomography (PET) and computed tomography (CT) is increasingly used in the diagnosis and assessment of prostate cancer. When combined with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), PSMA-PET improves sensitivity and negative predictive value compared with MRI alone, particularly for clinically significant disease [1,2]. It also has an emerging role in further evaluating indeterminate MRI findings such as PI-RADS 3 lesions, where it may help reduce unnecessary biopsies [3,4].

MRI remains the principal modality for local prostate cancer assessment, providing detailed anatomical and functional evaluation through the PI-RADS framework [3,4]. PSMA-PET has more recently become an important complementary technique, with several studies demonstrating diagnostic performance comparable to MRI and added value when the 2 modalities are integrated. MRI relies on morphological features and diffusion characteristics to estimate the likelihood of malignancy, whereas PSMA-PET identifies tumor by detecting focal radiotracer uptake above background activity [1,5]. As most prostate cancers arise in the peripheral zone, combining anatomical and molecular information can enhance detection. Efforts to standardize PSMA-PET interpretation and characterize intraprostatic uptake patterns, such as in the PRIMARY score, further support its expanding diagnostic role [1]. With growing use of PSMA-PET in diagnosis, staging, and investigation of biochemical recurrence, recognizing its advantages and potential pitfalls is essential.

Importantly, urinary tracer-related pitfalls are often considered in the context of prior prostate or urinary tract surgery, but may also occur in patients without previous surgical intervention, including those with congenital genitourinary anomalies. This report describes a 73-year-old man with no history of prior prostate or urinary tract surgery, renal agenesis, and a PI-RADS 3 lesion on MRI who demonstrated focal PSMA uptake concerning for prostate cancer, which was ultimately found to be benign and due to urine reflux into a seminal vesicle cyst. The case highlights an important interpretative pitfall as PSMA-PET use increases, and reinforces the association between renal agenesis and seminal vesicle cyst formation.

Case presentation

A 73-year-old man with a prostate-specific antigen (PSA) level of 13 ng/mL underwent initial radiological assessment with multiparametric MRI (Fig. 1). This demonstrated an 8 mm focus of diffusion restriction with corresponding apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) suppression in the periurethral left transition zone at the base-to-mid gland level. The finding was scored as PI-RADS 3. The right seminal vesicle appeared markedly hypointense on both T1-weighted and T2-weighted sequences, while the left seminal vesicle was unremarkable. The MRI appearance was initially indeterminate, as calcification within the seminal vesicle is uncommon, and MRI signal characteristics alone were insufficient to confidently distinguish a calculus from other causes of low signal within a cystic lesion without supplementary imaging. No pelvic lymphadenopathy was identified. Given the elevated PSA and indeterminate MRI findings, a diagnostic PSMA PET/CT was performed to further characterize the abnormality, to identify an avid lesion concordant with the MRI abnormality, and to exclude any other suspicious focus suitable for targeted biopsy.

(C, D) Coronal T2-weighted images show the right seminal vesicle appearing abnormal compared with the left, being T2 hypointense with a peripheral T2 cystic rim. T2 hypointensity was not initially attributed to the presence of a large calculus as was proven on subsequent hybrid PSMA PET/CT with contrast-enhanced CT urography, as this would be a rare abnormality. Punctate foci of prostate parenchyma calcification can be seen in the prostate at signal voids on MRI but such a large isolated dense calculus would be rare in clinical practice. Ipsilateral right renal agenesis (not included in the routine prostate MRI field of view) was identified on later PSMA PET/CT.

PET/CT was performed using F-18 DCFPyL for PSMA imaging. A dose of 244 MBq was administered intravenously, and imaging commenced 90 minutes later. Low-dose, non-diagnostic computed tomography (CT) and PET were acquired from the skull vertex to the knees for attenuation correction and anatomical localization. A diagnostic, contrast-enhanced CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis was subsequently obtained.

PSMA-PET/CT demonstrated intense radiotracer accumulation (SUVmax 12) near the right prostatic base, immediately anterior to a lobulated partly calcific focus (Fig. 2). Unlike typical intraprostatic malignancy, which demonstrates focal uptake centered within the prostate parenchyma, the uptake in this case was located adjacent to the prostate along the expected course of the seminal vesicle, an atypical distribution for true intraprostatic disease. However, given the atypical findings, a malignant lesion was still considered possible, and biopsy was advised. No additional abnormal PSMA-avid uptake was seen within the prostate, and there was no evidence of PSMA-avid lymphadenopathy or metastatic disease. It was also noted that the patient’s right seminal vesicle was possibly absent in association with an absent right kidney. On further assessment, post-void imaging demonstrated increased tracer activity centered on the region of the right seminal vesicle rather than within the prostate. This raised suspicion that the tracer was accumulating due to urinary reflux into a seminal vesicle cyst adjacent to the calcific focus. Further review by another radiologist and correlation with the prior MRI supported the possibility of a seminal vesicle cyst in the setting of ipsilateral renal agenesis, given the absence of any history of nephrectomy.

(B, C, D) Series of axial images in the same plane of post-void CT urogram, hybrid fused PSMA PET/CT and PET-only images showing intense radiotracer accumulation at the right seminal vesicle (arrows), adjacent to the prostate base. (E) Whole-body maximum intensity projection (right anterior oblique) demonstrating the PSMA-avid focus at the right seminal vesicle/prostate base (arrow) and ipsilateral renal agenesis.

The case was discussed with the patient’s urologist, and biopsy was undertaken given the atypical imaging appearances. Prostate biopsy, including targeted cores from the MRI and PET regions of interest, demonstrated benign tissue. The PSA remained stable on review. Taken together, the appearances were consistent with urinary reflux into a seminal vesicle remnant containing a small calculus in the context of ipsilateral renal agenesis. The patient was managed conservatively with ongoing clinical and biochemical follow-up.

Discussion

We describe a patient with elevated PSA and atypical imaging findings in whom a peri-prostatic uptake pattern on PSMA-PET prompted further investigation. PSMA-PET has emerged as a highly sensitive imaging modality in the evaluation of prostate cancer, but its expanding clinical use has also revealed several potential sources of false-positive uptake. This case demonstrates one such pitfall, in which urinary reflux into a seminal vesicle cyst associated with renal agenesis mimicked prostate malignancy on PSMA-PET.

PSMA-PET detects tumor by demonstrating focal radiotracer uptake above background activity. PSMA is a cell-surface protein highly expressed in prostate cancer, but it is also present in several normal tissues and in a range of benign and malignant conditions [5]. Physiologic biodistribution is seen in the kidneys, salivary and lacrimal glands, liver, spleen, and bowel [6]. Radiotracer is excreted renally, so marked activity is commonly seen in the ureters and bladder [7]. Familiarity with physiologic distribution, expected sites of accumulation and the non-malignant processes that may show uptake is essential to avoid misinterpretation and minimize false-positive findings.

Knowledge of regions with physiologic PSMA uptake that lie close to the prostate and pelvic lymph nodes is essential for accurate interpretation [8]. Increasing recognition has also been given to urinary reflux into the ejaculatory ducts or seminal vesicles as a source of false-positive uptake. Several case reports describe increased PSMA activity in anatomical variants and post-surgical states that allow tracer-containing urine to enter the seminal vesicles. Many patients undergoing PSMA-PET have a history of prostatic or urinary tract surgery, and false-positive uptake within the seminal vesicles has been reported after transurethral resection of the prostate and prostatic enucleation [[9], [10], [11]]. Interpreting radiologists should therefore review surgical history carefully. Notably, our report appears to be the first to demonstrate PSMA uptake in the absence of prostatic surgery, highlighting that benign activity can also occur in patients with congenital anomalies.

Optimizing scan technique and protocols remains essential for reducing false-positive interpretations. Many centers acquire images from the pelvis towards the head to minimize bladder-related artefact [5,7]. Intravenous frusemide can reduce ureteric and bladder tracer activity and has been shown to improve reader confidence in interpreting PSMA-PET/CT [12]. Atypical uptake patterns are increasingly being clarified through post-void imaging or CT urography, both of which help localize radiotracer activity to contrast-enhanced urine or demonstrate reflux into the seminal vesicles [7,11,13]. Integration of CT urography improves diagnostic confidence in distinguishing ureteric excretion from metastatic uptake, reclassifying equivocal foci in a significant proportion of patients [14]. Post-void imaging has also proved valuable in attributing radiotracer activity to urinary reflux, including in patients with prior transurethral surgery and in this case involving a congenital renal tract anomaly [9]. In practice, CT urography and post-void imaging should be considered when focal PSMA uptake adjacent to the prostate or seminal vesicles is atypical for intraprostatic malignancy or raises suspicion for urinary tracer reflux.

Combined MRI and PSMA-PET assessment for prostate cancer is increasingly used in cases of indeterminate MRI findings. This case reported a PI-RADS 3 lesion in the left transition zone, indicating that further evaluation was required for diagnostic clarity [3,4]. While MRI alone could not fully resolve the significance of the findings, PSMA-PET provided complementary information that strengthened lesion characterization. The abnormal appearance of the right seminal vesicle on MRI was present, but its importance was not noted until subsequent PSMA-PET/CT demonstrated this to represent a calcified focus within the seminal vesicle [15]. This, together with the absent right kidney, raised concern for an underlying congenital renal tract anomaly. The finding further highlights that signal-void areas on MRI should not be dismissed without considering calcification or other structural causes [15]. In addition, the MRI protocol limited to the prostate and pelvis did not include the abdomen, so the absent kidney was not identified without whole-body imaging or clinical correlation.

Understanding the embryological origin of the seminal vesicles helps explain why this patient developed a cystic remnant capable of filling with urine and mimicking tumor uptake on PSMA-PET. The kidneys and seminal vesicles share a developmental pathway through the mesonephric duct system [16,17]. Disruption of this pathway may result in renal agenesis and associated abnormalities of the ipsilateral seminal vesicle, including hypoplasia or cystic seminal vesicle remnant formation [[17], [18], [19]]. These remnants may retain communication with the ejaculatory duct or incorporate an ectopic ureteric insertion, creating a conduit for urinary reflux [20]. Refluxed urine may stagnate, leading to debris accumulation, dilation and, in rare cases, stone formation, as reported in seminal vesicle cysts associated with Zinner syndrome [21,22]. From an imaging perspective, this constellation of findings explains how congenital renal tract anomalies can produce focal PSMA uptake adjacent to the prostate that may mimic malignancy. In this case, the absent kidney was not identified on the initial prostate MRI, so the abnormal seminal vesicle signal could not be confidently attributed to a congenital anomaly. The PET/CT findings, incorporating imaging of the entire urogenital tract, therefore supported an underlying developmental variant rather than malignant disease.

Multimodal imaging resolved the diagnostic uncertainty in this case. MRI demonstrated an indeterminate abnormality of the right seminal vesicle but could not confidently characterize its nature. PSMA-PET identified focal uptake adjacent to the prostate and demonstrated absence of the ipsilateral kidney, while subsequent CT with post-void imaging localized tracer activity to the seminal vesicle, supporting urinary reflux into a congenital seminal vesicle remnant rather than malignancy. Despite the imaging findings favoring a benign process, biopsy was performed to ensure diagnostic certainty because of the unusual nature of the presentation. This case highlights several learning points. Firstly, when an abnormal seminal vesicle is identified, renal agenesis should be considered and correlation with other imaging or clinical history is essential. Secondly, calcification should be included in the differential when encountering a signal-void or low-signal focus on prostate MRI. Thirdly, radiologists must remain aware of the influence of urinary activity and radiotracer excretion on PSMA-PET interpretation and apply appropriate techniques to differentiate these effects from true disease. This includes training technologists to recognize abnormalities that may require modified scanning protocols such as post-void imaging. The overall aim is to strengthen diagnostic confidence so that biopsy may be avoided in future cases.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates an important interpretative pitfall on PSMA-PET imaging, in which atypical periprostatic tracer uptake in a patient without prior prostate or urinary tract surgery was caused by urinary reflux into a congenital seminal vesicle remnant rather than malignancy. A hybrid approach to imaging, assessment of the entire urogenital tract, being aware of congenital anomalies and use of post-void imaging can improve diagnostic confidence. Our case also shows a rare finding of calculus formation within a seminal vesicle remnant, which on MRI alone was difficult to interpret without hybrid PET/CT. Interpreting radiologists should be alert to PSMA uptake that is adjacent to, rather than centered within, the prostate and tracks along the expected course of the seminal vesicles, particularly when accompanied by calcification or absence of the ipsilateral kidney, as this pattern may indicate a non-neoplastic cause.
