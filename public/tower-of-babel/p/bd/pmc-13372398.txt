The Efficacy of Food‐Grade Substances to Protect Dietary Glutamine From Ruminal Degradation
Authors: Lillian P Carlyon, Tianna R Boyce, Leesa‐Joy Dunstan, Bruno R C Alves, Kiro Petrovski, Mariana Caetano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study was conducted to investigate the use of four, low cost, food‐grade substances to protect glutamine from microbial degradation in vitro, and subsequently validate their efficacy in vivo. Two in vitro trials were conducted using 250 mL bottles replicated in triplicate to test beeswax‐, paraffin wax‐, zein‐, and rice bran wax‐encapsulated glutamine (GlnFS1, GlnFS2, GlnFS3 and GlnFS4, respectively) compared to control (blank) and negative controls (GlnC, FS1, FS2, FS3 and FS4). Ammonium analysis was conducted to evaluate the degradation of glutamine at 30, 60, 90, 120, 180, 240, 300, 360, 420 and 480 min post inoculation. To further test the efficacy of the two most protective food‐grade substances in vivo, 16 Merino wethers (initial liveweight 53.75 ± 4.25 kg; 12 months old) were allocated to one of the treatments: control (n = 3), GlnC (n = 3), GlnFS1 (n = 5), and GlnFS2 (n = 5). Treatments were administered via oral syringe and bloods were taken via a jugular catheter every hour over 12 h post supplementation. Blood parameters including blood gases and plasma amino acid analyses were performed. The in vitro trial data were analysed using REML and area under the curve by R Software. Mixed models by SAS Software were used to analyse the in vivo data. All food‐grade substances protected glutamine from microbial degradation, with the highest protection observed for GlnFS1 and GlnFS2 (35.7% and 26.4%, respectively) compared to the GlnC in the in vitro trials. In the in vivo trial, an interaction between treatment and timepoint was observed for blood sodium (p = 0.004). The blood pH of GlnFS1 and GlnFS2 were higher than control and GlnC (p = 0.039). Alanine (p = 0.049), glutamine (p = 0.015), and histidine (p < 0.001) were elevated in GlnC compared to other treatment groups (control, GlnFS1, and GlnFS2). A higher concentration of plasma glutamine was detected in the blood of GlnC wethers with no significant difference from the control group. Future studies must evaluate the bioavailability of GlnFS1 and GlnFS2 in vivo.Keywords: amino acids, glutamine, rumen, sheep

Introduction

Ruminants have a unique digestive system, having the ability to maximise the utilisation of different sources of nitrogen from their feed. The rumen microorganisms convert the available protein sources (non‐protein nitrogen and rumen‐degradable protein) into ammonia, peptides, and amino acids (AAs) to be directly absorbed through the rumen wall or used as metabolisable protein (Millen et al. 2016). Importantly, AAs are the vital building blocks of proteins and play a role as precursors of hormones, nucleic acids, and lipids, and can function as neurotransmitters (Albrecht et al. 2010; Cruzat et al. 2018). There has been recent interest in protecting the AAs lysine, methionine (first limiting AAs), and histidine from microbial degradation in the rumen due to their essentiality (Räisänen et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2022; Van Den Bossche et al. 2023). The development of rumen‐protected AAs can be challenging due to their differing properties, including denaturation points, hydrophilicity, and polarity, therefore it is not a uniform process. The protective material must not interact with the AAs to change their chemical structure, and it should be able to withstand microbial degradation in order to bypass the rumen and release the AAs into the small intestine to be absorbed. Waxes have been successfully used in numerous studies as encapsulating materials (De Raphélis‐Soissan et al. 2017; Carvalho et al. 2019; Inô et al. 2024) and specifically beeswax, paraffin wax, and rice bran wax will be further explored in this current study. Despite there being many AAs that can enhance production, several publications have demonstrated glutamine's beneficial effects on immune function, intestinal health, and foetal development during pregnancy. Glutamine is largely involved in the immune system and has a positive, linear relationship with the regulation of pro‐inflammatory cytokines TNF‐α and IL‐1 (Wang et al. 2018), as these cytokines enhance the activation of B cells, natural killer cells, neutrophils, and T cells (Wallace and Keast 1992; Wells et al. 1999). Before glutamine can be utilised throughout the body, 66% of the absorbed glutamine is metabolised by enterocytes (Wu 1998), which have a large capacity for glutamine catabolism from digested and plasma‐derived glutamine (Wu et al. 2022). Intestinal enterocytes utilise glutamine for cell growth and maintenance as this AA is essential for epidermal growth factor‐stimulated intestinal cell proliferation (Ko et al. 1993; Rhoads et al. 1997). Glutamine is vital in late gestation as it can cross the placenta and is converted to glutamate in the foetal liver, which is then oxidised for the energy required for the growth and development of the foetus (Moores et al. 1994; Vaughn et al. 1995; Gao et al. 2009). Unprotected glutamine is converted to microbial protein or quickly catabolised to ammonia and glutamate in the rumen (Gilbreath et al. 2019), therefore when glutamine requirements increase, it needs to bypass the rumen (Caroprese et al. 2012; Nemati et al. 2018). To avoid rumen fermentation, glutamine requires protection to be absorbed and utilised as it has numerous benefits, and the potential for application into various aspects of livestock production, therefore, it is the focus of this study.

Ultimately, the current project aimed to test the efficacy of four food‐grade substances (beeswax, paraffin wax, zein, and rice bran wax) to protect glutamine from ruminal degradation, in both in vitro and in vivo trials. It was expected that all food‐grade substances tested would reduce the availability of glutamine to the rumen microbes, thus reduce the degradation rate. The first two trials tested the degradation rate of glutamine protected by four different substances in an in vitro setting. To measure their bioavailability, the two substances with the highest protection efficacy were further tested in vivo. It was hypothesised that food‐grade substances could be used to prevent microbial degradation of glutamine in vitro and the plasma glutamine levels of sheep would increase post supplementation in vivo.

Materials and Methods

Animal Ethics

This trial and associated procedures were approved by The University of Adelaide Animal Ethics Committee (approval number S‐2023‐042) and was conducted in accordance with the Australian code of Practice for the Care and Use of Animals for Scientific Purposes (2013).

In Vitro Trials

The glutamine (Sigma Aldrich Pty Ltd, St Louis, Missouri USA) was rumen protected using four food‐grade substances: beeswax, paraffin wax, zein, and rice bran wax (FS1, FS2, FS3 and FS4, respectively). The protocols considered labour intensity and preparation time for the manufacturer in order to obtain a feasible and cost‐effective manufacturing system, in a small‐scale setting.

All substances were used at a 2:1 ratio of substance: glutamine. Two grams of beeswax, paraffin wax and rice bran wax were each weighed in separate beakers and melted in a water bath at 65°C. One gram of glutamine powder was weighed, added and stirred into each beaker of wax until fully dissolved to form a homogenous matrix. The mixtures were left to cool at room temperature and were broken up into approximately 5 mm x 5 mm pieces. One gram of zein, 0.5 g of glutamine powder, and 5 mL of 85% ethanol solution were added to a beaker and stirred with a spatula. The stir bar was placed into the beaker and put onto a stirrer plate (temperature = 60°C; speed = 200 rpm). Once the mixture was thoroughly mixed, speed was increased to 400 rpm for 2 min. The mixture was poured onto aluminium trays, dried in the oven at 45°C for 20 min, and crumbled into approximately 1 cm x 1 cm pieces.

Two identical trials (day 1 and day 2) were conducted on separate days to evaluate the efficacy of the protocol used to encapsulate glutamine.

Rumen fluid was collected from one fistulated, non‐lactating, Jersey cow that had ad libitum access to clean drinking water and barley hay (Table 1). The rumen contents were removed and directly transferred into a pre‐warmed thermos at 39°C before being strained through two layers of cheesecloth into a 2 L bottle that was purged with carbon dioxide. To ascertain the quality of the inoculum, the pH of the rumen fluid was measured prior to the trials (6.49 ± 0.15; mean ± s.d.).

The in vitro methodology was adapted from Goering and Van Soest (1970) where the current analysis was conducted with 250 mL Schott bottles instead of 125 mL Pyrex Erlenmeyer flasks, consequently the ratios of solutions, samples and inoculum were adjusted accordingly. In summary, the incubated bottles contained a control (blank), negative controls (GlnC, FS1, FS2, FS3, and FS4), and the food‐grade substance‐encapsulated glutamine (GlnFS1, GlnFS2, GlnFS3, and GlnFS4) replicated in triplicate. The final buffer solution (80 mL) was added to each glass bottle and placed in a shaking orbital incubator at 39°C and 120 oscillations per minute. The composition of the final buffer solution included 2 g of trypticase, 400 mL of distilled water, 0.1 mL of micromineral solution, and once dissolved, 200 mL of buffer solution, 200 mL of micromineral solution, and 1 mL of resazurin solution were added (Goering and Van Soest 1970). To remove oxygen from the buffer solution and the head space, 2 mL of reducing solution was added in each bottle and carbon dioxide was purged into the head space. The reducing solution contained 0.625 g of cysteine hydrochloride, 4 mL of 1 N sodium hydroxide, 0.625 g of disodium sulphide nonahydrate, and distilled water to adjust to 100 mL volume. Once the buffer solution colour changed from blue to colourless, indicating all oxygen was removed, 20 mL of rumen inoculum was added. The in vitro trial was conducted over a total of 480 min and sampling was conducted at 10 timepoints: 30, 60, 90, 120, 180, 240, 300, 360, 420 and 480 min. To stop the fermentation at different timepoints, two millilitres of solution from each bottle was transferred into 20 µL of 6 N HCl solution in microtubes. All samples were stored at –20°C prior to ammonium determination.

Ammonium Analysis

Ammonia nitrogen levels from all in vitro samples were indirectly measured using a colourimetric assay for ammonium determination (ammonium determination 11732; International Standard ISO 1997). Once thawed, samples were centrifuged at 845 × g‐force for 5 min. The supernatant was then mixed with MilliQ water to achieve a 10 times dilution (100 µL sample + 900 µL MilliQ water). To determine the ammonia nitrogen concentration in each sample, the ammonia‐salicylate method was used. In summary, this involved a three‐step method where first, ammonia was converted to monochloramine. The monochloramine reacted with salicylate to form 5‐aminosalicylate. Nitroferricyanide then catalysed the oxidation of 5‐amniosalicylate to form indosalicylate, a blue‐coloured compound. Excess nitroprusside produced a yellow colour, therefore in combination, a green colour was formed and absorbed the light at 650 nm using the Multiskan plate reader (xMark™ Microplate Absorbance Spectrophotometer; South Granville, NSW, Australia). The ammonium standard solution (1000 mg/L ammonium; Mettler Toledo, Schwerzenbach, Switzerland) was used to develop a standard curve for this assay. The standard curve was used to calculate the ammonium concentration considering the dilution factor used in the laboratory. The average ammonium concentration for the three bottles in each group was calculated by subtracting the blank ammonium concentration and FS1/FS2/FS3/FS4 controls from their respective treatments in vitro.

In Vivo Trial

Two days prior to the commencement of the trial, 16 Merino wethers (initial liveweight 53.75 ± 4.25 kg; 12 months old) were placed in the Livestock Research Centre, at Adelaide University, Roseworthy Campus. The wethers were randomly allocated to one of the treatment groups: control (no substances administered; n = 3), negative control (10 g of glutamine dissolved in 200 mL of water; n = 3), the FS1‐protected glutamine (20 g FS1 coating 10 g glutamine; n = 5), and the FS2‐protected glutamine (20 g FS2 coating 10 g glutamine; n = 5), blocked by initial liveweight and body condition score. Wethers were placed in individual pens (1 m2) with ad libitum access to fresh drinking water and oaten hay (Table 2). Indwelling jugular catheters were placed 1 day prior to the administration of treatments for blood collection. On supplementation day, baseline blood samples were taken prior to intravenously administering 0.6 mL Diazepam per sheep. After 20 min, animals received their treatment via oral syringe feeding. All blood samples (10 mL) were collected in lithium heparin tubes every hour for 12 h after supplementation. Whole blood from three animals per treatment at timepoints 4, 8, and 12 h were analysed for pH, blood partial pressure of carbon dioxide (pCO2), partial pressure of oxygen (pO2), bicarbonate (HCO3), base excess (BE), oxygen saturation (sO2), total carbon dioxide content (TCO2), potassium (K), initial ionised calcium (iCa), glucose (Glu), haematocrit (Hct), and haemoglobin (Hb) using an i‐Stat Alinity analyser and CG8+ cartridges (i‐Stat Alinity, Abbott Park, Illinois, USA). All blood samples were centrifuged at 1,400 × g‐force for 10 min. The plasma was transferred to 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes and frozen at ‐80°C prior to amino acid analysis.

Amino Acid Analysis

The plasma samples for baseline (0), 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 h timepoints were analysed for free amino acid concentrations using the method from Agilent Technologies ‘Automation of Sample Derivatization Using the Agilent 1260 Infinity II Prime LC System for Amino Acid Analysis' in the AdvanceBio Amino Acid Analysis by Lucas Willmann. The Agilent 1260 liquid chromatography system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) consisted of a universal interface box (G1390B), a UV‐VIS diode array detector (G4212B), a thermostatted column compartment (G1316A), a multisampler (G7167A), and a quaternary pump (G1311B) with a flow rate of 1.200 mL/min. The reversed‐phase, high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) involved preparing mobile phase A (10 mM Na2HPO4 and 10 mM Na2B4O7 pH 8.2) and mobile phase B (Acetonitrile:methanol:water (45:45:10, v:v:v)), along with transferring the derivatization agents to the autosampler containers. These agents included a borate buffer 0.4 N in water, pH 10.2 (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), the FMOC reagent (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), and the OPA reagent (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Calibration was conducted using the amino acid standard (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), supplemented with asparagine, glutamine, and tryptophan (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and prepared in 0.1 N HCl. Samples were calibrated on six levels, 50, 100, 200, 400, 800, and 1600 µM and the internal standards norvaline and sarcosine at 500 µL. Nanosep 10 K omega filter tubes (Pall Corporation, Port Washington, NY, USA) were rinsed with 0.5 mL MilliQ water (17,949 × g‐force for 5 min). Once blood plasma samples were defrosted at room temperature, 90 µL was mixed with 10 µL of internal standard and this solution was transferred to the Nanosep tubes and filtered (centrifuged at 4ᵒC at 17,949 × g‐force for 15 min). From the filtered solution, 20 µL was transferred to HPLC sample tubes to be analysed. Instrument control, peak area calculation, and peak integration were measured with Agilent Chemstation software (Santa Clara, CA, USA). Amino acid concentrations of alanine, arginine, glutamine, glutamic acid, glycine, histidine, leucine, lysine, serine, threonine, and valine were determined, excluding aspartic acid, asparagine, tyrosine, cystine, methionine, tryptophane, phenylalanine, and proline due to unreliable concentrations lower than 50 µM.

Statistical Analysis

In Vitro Trials and Ammonium Analysis

The in vitro data were analysed using the statistical software R v 4.4.1 (R Core Team 2020). The packages biometryassist v 1.2.1 (Nielsen et al. 2024), ASReml‐R (The Vsni Team 2023), and tidyverse v 2.0.0 (Wickham et al. 2019) were used. The data were checked for normality using Shapiro‐Wilk for each trial; for skewed data (trial 1), data were log‐transformed before statistical analysis to meet assumptions, and then back‐transformed means were presented. For ammonium concentration a MIXED model was fitted, where treatment (n = 5), time of incubation (n = 10) and the interaction between treatment and time of incubation were fitted as fixed effects.

Area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to summarise the integrated response of ammonium concentration over time for each experimental unit. For each treatment × replicate × trial combination, observations were ordered by time and AUC was computed using the trapezoidal rule.

AUC values were analysed using linear mixed models fitted by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) in ASReml. Treatment was included as a fixed effect, while trial and replicate nested within trial were fitted as random effects. Model assumptions were assessed using residual diagnostics. Fixed effects were tested using Wald statistics with appropriate denominator degrees of freedom. Treatment least‐squares means were estimated from the fitted model, and pairwise comparisons were performed using Tukey's adjustment to control the family‐wise error rate. Data were considered significant with a p‐value ≤ 0.05.

In Vivo Trial and Amino Acid Analysis

Data were analysed in general mixed model using the MIXED procedure of SAS (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). For blood analysis (amino acid concentrations and blood gases), the treatment groups (control, GlnC, GlnFS1 and GlnFS2), timepoint, and the interaction between treatment groups and timepoint were used as fixed effects. The location of the pens and rows were included as random effects, and wether was the experimental unit. The repeated function of SAS was included in the model to evaluate different timepoints, where wether was the subject. Treatment means were determined using the LSMEANS statement. Tests for fixed effects with a probability ≤ 0.05 were declared significant.

In Vivo Trial

Amino Acid Analysis

No interactions between treatment and timepoint were observed for blood plasma amino acid concentrations tested except for isoleucine (Figure 1). Timepoints 0 and 2 h showed no difference between treatments. The GlnC was statistically higher than GlnFS1 at 4 (p = 0.041) and 10 h (p = 0.045) and GlnC was also higher than GlnFS2 at 6 h (p = 0.030). At 8 h, GlnC differed from GlnFS1 (p = 0.001) and GlnFS2 (p = 0.029), while the control differed from GlnFS1 only (p = 0.025). The control group was higher than GlnFS1 at 12 h (p = 0.050).

The interaction between treatments and timepoints for the mean ± SEM of plasma isoleucine concentration during the in vivo trial conducted with Merino wethers fed control, glutamine control (GlnC), glutamine encapsulated with food‐grade substance 1 (GlnFS1) and glutamine encapsulated with food‐grade substance 2 (GlnFS2) at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 h (p = 0.024).

There was a difference in plasma concentration between treatment groups for glutamine (p = 0.015), alanine (p = 0.049) and histidine (p < 0.001; Table 6). The highest plasma glutamine (Figure 2a) and alanine concentrations were observed in GlnC animals, followed by control, GlnFS2 and GlnFS1; with no difference between GlnC and control or between GlnFS1 and GlnSF2. The highest plasma histidine concentration was also observed in GlnC which was different from the control (p = 0.008), GlnFS1 (p < 0.001) and GlnFS2 (p < 0.001). No differences between treatment groups were detected for plasma arginine, glutamic acid, glycine, leucine, lysine, serine, threonine, or valine concentrations.

Discussion

The in vitro trial was conducted to determine the two most efficacious food‐grade substances, out of four tested, in protecting glutamine from rumen microbial degradation. To determine their ability to avoid microbial degradation and allow for intestinal glutamine absorption over 12 h, these substances were used for validation of their application in the in vivo trial.

In Vitro Study

Glutamine without microbial protection is a form of rumen degradable protein, due to the microbial conversion of glutamine into ammonia via the deamination pathway (Bach et al. 2005), therefore, it was expected that the GlnC would have a steady release of ammonia, as reported previously in other studies (Gilbreath et al. 2019; Suh et al. 2022; Zhu et al. 2022). Protected glutamine treatments were tested to provide a form of rumen undegradable dietary protein, ideally resulting in less microbial ammonia production and more bioavailable glutamine. The melting emulsification technique used to prepare GlnFS1 and GlnFS2 proved to be successful, as these treatments offered the best protection of glutamine by producing the lowest ammonium concentration and thus highest protection percentages. Beeswax has been used successfully as an encapsulation material in other studies (Carvalho et al. 2019; Feitosa et al. 2024; Inô et al. 2024). Beeswax contains a complex mix of wax esters, hydrocarbons and free fatty acids, in comparison to rice bran wax which mainly contains wax esters (Dimakopoulou‐Papazoglou et al. 2025), therefore beeswax is more difficult to hydrolyse and could explain why it offered a higher protection than rice bran wax. Similarly, paraffin wax offered a higher protection efficacy than rice bran wax, potentially because it is composed of a mixture of long‐chain saturated hydrocarbons and is very difficult to break down (National Center For Biotechnology Information 2025). Previous studies have used zein as an encapsulation material for lysine (Blaine 2011; Yoshimaru et al. 2000) by using the spray‐dry method of encapsulation. Perhaps the encapsulation method of zein in this current study is not as effective, hence its lower protection percentage compared to beeswax and paraffin wax. There are very few glutamine supplements to compare with the protective efficacy of GlnFS1, which overall provided 35.7% microbial protection and GlnFS2 which provided 26.4%. From the literature, two products that revealed the protective efficacy of glutamine against ruminal degradation had 74.7% protection (Wansheng Biological Co. Ltd., Zhongwei, Ningxia, China used by Wang et al. 2022) and 40.0% protection (Ascor Chimici, Capocolle di Bertinoro, Italy used by Caroprese et al. 2013). However, neither are currently commercially available, therefore there is an opportunity for the development of a rumen‐protected glutamine supplement that is low cost and easily manufactured.

Further In Vitro Validation

The microbial population within the rumen is influenced by the ruminant's diet (Wang et al. 2017). A forage diet compared to a diet higher in concentrates will result in a different microbial population breaking down and metabolising different feeds, for example, fibre and starch, respectively (Cherdthong et al. 2010; Metzler‐Zebeli et al. 2013; Grilli et al. 2016). Concurrently, the rumen pH also fluctuates from neutral to more acidic ranges as the concentrate feed increases (from pH 7 to 5.5; Gaafar et al. 2009; Anantasook et al. 2013; Millen et al. 2016). The pH of the rumen fluid used in both in vitro trials was 6.49 on average, consistent with the barley hay diet rich in fibre (NDF = 53.2% of DM) offered to the fistulated cows. Since the fistulated cow was fed a forage‐based diet, it was expected that a higher population of microbes with optimal activity within this pH range were predominant in both in vitro trials, and affected the glutamine degradation. Therefore, it would be beneficial to evaluate the degradation of rumen protected glutamine in vitro using rumen fluid collected from a fistulated cow fed a high concentrate diet and another fed a forage diet similar to this study. Furthermore, despite the rumen fluid being collected from the same cow, at the same time of day and undergoing the same in vitro methodology, samples collected from trial 1 had an overall larger ammonium concentration than those collected in trial 2, for all treatment groups. There are potential limitations to this in vitro study that must be acknowledged. The site of rumen fluid collection within the rumen can alter the microbial populations; since another study reported more cellulose‐digesting bacteria close to the fibre mat compared to the liquid phase (Muetzel et al. 2001; Klevenhusen et al. 2017). The collection process was identical between trial 1 and 2, however the proportion of fibre mat and liquid phase rumen fluid was not taken into consideration prior to the study. Therefore, differences in rumen contents may have contributed to the differing ammonium concentrations between trials.

In Vivo Study

Amino Acid Analysis

The plasma glutamine concentration of the control group was not different from the other treatment groups (GlnC. GlnFS1 and GlnFS2), potentially due to stress. The intensive activity of taking blood every hour and people moving in and around the pens over 12 h may have caused the sheep to stress. Anxiety or stress has been shown to disrupt the glutamine‐glutamate cycling in the brain, causing a build‐up of glutamine in blood plasma in dogs (Puurunen et al. 2018). However, a study conducted in rats showed the opposite result, where blood glutamine concentration decreased in stressed rats (Dadmarz et al. 1998). The impact of stress on blood glutamine concentration may vary among different species.

From the data, GlnC had the highest plasma glutamine concentration. This was a surprising result as glutamine in this treatment group was not rumen‐protected. However, it did have a different physical form compared to the other treatments. The GlnFS1 and GlnFS2 groups were crumb‐sized pellets compared to GlnC which was dissolved in water, therefore had a liquid form. This difference in form could increase the passage rate through the rumen, compared to solid form, due to the small particle size (Dufreneix et al. 2019). Due to high concentration and increased passage rate, some glutamine could be undegraded reaching the small intestine for absorption into the blood at a faster rate than other treatment groups.

Once glutamine is transported into the enterocytes, it is metabolised or transported into the blood to be utilised throughout the body. Glutamine is catabolised by the enzyme glutaminase, producing glutamate and ammonia from which the glutamate undergoes transamination to produce alanine (Newsholme et al. 2003). The current study found an increase in plasma alanine concentration associated with the GlnC group, coinciding with other studies infusing glutamine into the blood in rats and sheep (Moundras et al. 1993; Odongo et al. 2009); therefore, this alanine synthesis in the small intestine is a potential explanation for the finding of higher glutamine blood concentration in the GlnC group wethers. Another site for glutamine catabolism is the liver, as it contains both glutaminase and glutamine synthetase enzymes for the catabolism and production of glutamine, respectfully (Lobley et al. 2001). Oral supplementation of glutamine has been shown to increase glutamine liver concentration in rats (Macedo Rogero et al. 2004; Cruzat and Tirapegui 2009) which has been found to increase plasma urea nitrogen concentration (Moundras et al. 1993; Doepel et al. 2007). This is reasonable as ammonia and glutamine are important substrates for the synthesis of urea in the urea cycle (Watford et al. 2002). However, the differences of the digestive tract between rats and sheep should be taken into consideration when discussing glutamine metabolism.

Plasma histidine concentration also increased in wethers fed GlnC, suggesting that an increase in plasma glutamine may affect the metabolism or transport of histidine. Glutamine is a strong inhibitor of histidine in the N transporter system in hepatocytes (Giovanna Leonardi et al. 1988), therefore this speculation could elucidate the increase in plasma histidine (Holecek 2011). Due to glutamine impacting these AA concentrations, it was questioned whether glutamine may out‐compete other AAs that have not been deemed significant. Other studies found concentrations of glycine, serine, and threonine were depressed in the liver (Moundras et al. 1993) and plasma (Holecek 2011) following glutamine supplementation.

Isoleucine is part of the branched‐chain amino acids, along with leucine and valine; they play a vital role in protein synthesis, glucose metabolism and are precursors for alanine, glutamate, and glutamine (Nie et al. 2018). The effect of glutamine administration on isoleucine plasma concentrations can be variable as the current study and other studies found an increase (Shewchuk et al. 1997; Yang et al. 2018), while there have also been reports of a decrease in plasma concentration (Holeček et al. 2000; Holecek 2011). Undoubtably, increasing plasma glutamine concentration has flow‐on effects for many AAs in the mammalian body.

Blood Parameters

All blood parameter means were within the normal ranges for sheep throughout the trial, for the three timepoints measured except for iCa (Reece et al. 2015; Constable et al. 2016; Fails and Magee 2018). However, it was observed that the treatment groups GlnFS1 and GlnFS2 had higher blood pH compared to the control and GlnC groups. Despite the increase in blood glutamine concentration, the average blood pH for the GlnC group remained similar to the control group, therefore the blood pH did not change with an increase of plasma glutamine concentration in this study. This finding agrees with another study which injected glutamine directly into the jugular vein of sheep (Odongo et al. 2009). It is difficult to determine why the treatment groups GlnFS1 and GlnFS2 had higher blood pH parameters when additional dietary and environmental factors were controlled throughout the trial.

Another interesting result was the lower blood sodium levels observed in the GlnC group compared to the control, GlnFS1 and GlnFS2 groups. The transport of glutamine through the enterocyte membranes is driven by Na+/H+ gradients. These Na+ dependent, glutamine transporters in the intestines consist of ASCT2, B0AT1, and SNAT 3/5/7 (system N transporter; Oppedisano et al. 2007; Saha et al. 2012; Pochini et al. 2014), where Na+ moves in the same direction as glutamine. The intestinal luminal epithelium is electronegative, therefore Na+ is acquired from the blood and diffuses passively into the intestinal lumen to then be used to transport glutamine into enterocytes (Smith and Morton 2010; Butts et al. 2019). Enterocytes utilise two‐thirds of the absorbed glutamine, therefore there could be more glutamine going into the intestinal cells than into the blood, justifying the decrease in blood sodium. In addition, it demonstrates that the levels of absorbed glutamine reaching the bloodstream can be low, due to the high metabolism of glutamine in the enterocytes.

Future In Vivo Validation

Considering time limitations of this pilot study, further refinement is appropriate. An extended duration is essential to measure particle size, assess the in vitro bioavailability of the supplements and establish the flow rate of digesta. Particle size and density affects the mean retention time in the gastrointestinal tract of ruminants (Okine and Mathison 1991; Clauss et al. 2011; Dufreneix et al. 2019), therefore measuring this variable will have interesting implications in vivo in relation to passage rate and the overall bioavailability of glutamine supplementation. In the current study, particle size and density of GlnC, GlnFS1, GlnFS2, GlnFS3 and GlnFS4 were not measured, however it would be beneficial to measure both variables for all supplements, several times when prepared on different days. Consideration of such factors are crucial due to their influence on degradation rates of feeds. A larger particle size commonly results in longer rumen retention times, but a high density will result in a shorter retention time (Kaske et al. 1992; Rodríguez‐Prado et al. 2004).

It would be beneficial to conduct a laboratory simulation of the abomasum‐intestinal release of the rumen‐protected glutamine to help determine its bioavailability in vitro and in situ (Zhang et al. 2022) before the in vivo trial. The in vivo study did not find a difference between treatment groups (GlnC, GlnFS1 and GlnFS2) and the control, potentially due to various limitations such as short trial length, the lack of supplement particle size measurements, establishment of a constant digesta and passage rate in the tract, and faecal and urine output measurements to determine the bioavailability of supplemented glutamine. Considering previous studies that investigated rumen protected amino acids, animals were fed for a minimum of 21 days prior to sample collection (Chen et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2022). The length of the current in vivo trial may have not allowed enough time for the rumen‐protected glutamine to pass through the digestive tract, therefore a longer supplementation period would be required to first establish the flow rate of digesta and supplement through the tract (21 days), then metabolic crates can be utilised to evaluate nitrogen utilisation, where blood, faecal matter and urine samples can also be sampled (7 days; Kaske et al. 1992). However, a high cost would be involved to conduct such trials. These samples would be important to evaluate the bioavailability of the supplements. As stated earlier, particle size of the supplements should be measured during the manufacturing process to further investigate if it affects the degradation rate of glutamine. From the results of this study, glutamine could potentially be supplemented in liquid form, yet its stability in solution needs to be assessed as the pH, solvent, and storage conditions (i.e., light, oxygen exposure, temperature) would all affect its availability (Khan and Elia 1991). Glutamine has been found to be more stable at a neutral pH (Arii et al. 1999) and at cold temperatures ≥ 4ᵒC (Sigma‐Aldrich 2023), even so, the solvent properties will also need to be considered. It should be reiterated that the plasma glutamine concentration of sheep in the GlnC did not significantly differ from the control. A longer supplementation study (as mentioned earlier) is required to draw conclusions regarding the most effective treatment group for delivering bioavailable glutamine in sheep.

Conclusion

In summary, the GlnFS1 and GlnFS2 supplements provided glutamine protection from microbial degradation during 480 min of incubation in vitro. The plasma glutamine concentration of sheep in the three treatment groups (GlnC, GlnFS1 and GlnFS2) did not differ from the control within the 12 h post supplementation in vivo. The results of the trials provided insight into the use of four food‐grade substances to protect glutamine from ruminal degradation in vitro and have elucidated the aspects requiring refinement for future in vivo studies.
