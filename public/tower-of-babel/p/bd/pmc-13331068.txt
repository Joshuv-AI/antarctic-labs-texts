Changes in the use of basic obstetric care in sub-Saharan Africa: evidence from demographic and health surveys using an Oaxaca-Blinder approach
Authors: Hervé Bassinga, Douba Nabié, Oumy Ndiaye, Ouattara Karim, Matè Alonyenyo Labité, Daniel Mwanga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe widespread mobilisation to improve maternal health over recent decades has led to increased prenatal consultations and assisted deliveries, resulting in a significant reduction in maternal mortality in many sub-Saharan African countries. This paper examines the progress made in basic obstetric care in four sub-Saharan African countries (Burkina Faso, Côte d’Ivoire, Ghana and Senegal), and investigates the sources of this progress to better inform future policy actions that will help achieve the Sustainable Development Goal 3 targets related to maternal and child health.MethodsWe used data from two Demographic and Health Surveys (DHSs) conducted in each of the four countries in the early 2010s and 2020s by their respective national statistical offices. Specifically, we relied on the 2010 and 2021 DHS for Burkina Faso, the 2011 and 2021 DHS for Côte d’Ivoire, the 2014 and 2022 DHS for Ghana and the 2010 and 2023 DHS for Senegal. We first estimated coverage rates for various components of maternity care using descriptive statistics with CIs to assess significant differences. Associations were examined using a multivariate decomposition analysis based on the Oaxaca-Blinder method adapted for binary outcomes.ResultsThe results show a substantial increase in coverage of antenatal care (ANC) and postnatal care services over the past decade in all four countries. While progress in ANC4+ coverage is evident, the situation becomes more complex when considering more comprehensive quality indicators such as ANC8+ and its individual components. Decomposition results suggest that the factors driving progress are largely not fully captured by conventional socio-economic variables, pointing instead to changes in women’s health-seeking behaviour and broader improvements in health system performance.ConclusionThis study highlights a marked gap between the high frequency of antenatal consultations and the quality of services delivered, particularly with respect to ANC8+ coverage and the full set of ANC components. Sustaining and deepening progress will require greater emphasis on improving service quality and expanding behaviour-change communication strategies tailored to local contexts, alongside continued health system strengthening.Keywords: Africa, Health systems, Maternal health, Obstetrics, Health Services Accessibility

Introduction

One of the defining features of obstetric care is its dual focus on preventive interventions (such as antenatal and late postpartum care) and curative care or obstetric care related to the management of pregnancy, childbirth and the immediate postpartum period.1 According to the WHO, despite investments in maternal health systems, an estimated 287 000 women died in 2020 from pregnancy-related causes—approximately one death every 2 min.2 Of these, around 70% (or 202 000) occurred in sub-Saharan Africa, and 16% (approximately 47 000) in South Asia, together accounting for 87% of global maternal deaths.2 The vast majority of these deaths are preventable, as they are often linked to limited access to high quality maternal health services, particularly during childbirth.3 This persistent situation (since the postcolonial era) highlights structural inequalities and weaknesses in health systems serving pregnant women.4

Maternal mortality did not receive sustained global attention until the mid-1980s, when a small group of committed activists and professionals began to mobilise around the issue. A major turning point came with the Safe Motherhood Conference held in Nairobi in 1987, which helped establish maternal health as a global public health priority.5 Since then, maternal health has featured prominently in international forums, including the International Conference on Population and Development and the United Nations Millennium Development Summit. These discussions reflected growing recognition that, although pregnancy inherently carries risks, many of these risks can be substantially reduced with appropriate care.6 7

Another important development was the commitment of African States through the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), in particular SDG 3, the Global Strategy for Women’s, Children’s and Adolescents’ Health (2016–2030), Agenda 2063, among others. These commitments are reflected in the implementation of political strategies aimed at improving maternal and child health in different countries and free care for pregnant women and children under five. These efforts have yielded tangible results, with increasing antenatal care (ANC) and assisted deliveries alongside notable declines in maternal mortality. However, despite these substantial gains made over the last 15 years, maternal mortality remains unacceptably high in many countries in the region. The SDGs target of reducing maternal mortality to 70 deaths or fewer per 100 000 live births by 2030 remains largely out of reach for many sub-Saharan African countries. According to data from the Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) Programme, in Burkina Faso, the pregnancy-related mortality ratio declined from 440 deaths per 100 000 births in 1998 to 232 deaths per 100 000 births in 2021. In Côte d’Ivoire, the ratio stood at 457 in 2021, compared with 597 in 1994. Senegal has experienced a sharp decline as well, although the level remains far below expectations. The pregnancy-related mortality ratio fell from 498 in 1992 to 154 in 2023. In these three countries, the maternal mortality ratio per 100 000 births is respectively 198 for Burkina Faso in 2021, 385 in 2021 for Côte d’Ivoire and 153 in 2023 for Senegal.8 Recent DHS data also indicate improved access to ANC in several West African countries, while highlighting persistent challenges, particularly with respect to completion of ANC schedules and skilled attendance at delivery. In Burkina Faso, 91.7% of pregnant women had at least one antenatal consultation with a skilled health professional, yet only 72% completed the recommended four consultations.9 In Côte d’Ivoire, 92.5% of women attended at least one antenatal consultation with skilled personnel, while 51.2% achieved the recommended number of consultations.10 With regard to childbirth, 80.1% of deliveries in Burkina Faso and 77.6% in Côte d’Ivoire were attended by qualified personnel, representing a significant improvement over the previous years. Despite this progress, rates remain critical, particularly in rural areas, where persistent inequalities in access to care continue to contribute to high maternal and infant mortality.

Although maternal health in sub-Saharan Africa has been widely studied, the drivers of recent improvements in obstetric care utilisation remain understudied. Existing research highlights key determinants costs, service availability, quality of care and geographical accessibility as key determinants of care utilisation.11 12 Other studies emphasise the influence of social and cultural factors, including women’s education, autonomy in decision-making and gender norms.13 14 Social capital and community networks also play a significant role in shaping health-seeking behaviours, as do policy interventions such as free healthcare, which have yielded mixed effects across contexts, notably in Côte d’Ivoire and Burkina Faso.15,17 Finally, despite the well-established importance of prenatal care in preventing maternal and neonatal complications,18 19 the relationship between consultation frequency and improved health outcomes remains complex and highly context-dependent.20

Despite this body of research, relatively few studies have examined the mechanisms underlying changes in the use of basic obstetric care. This study addresses this gap by analysing five key components: ANC coverage (≥8 visits and ≥4 visits for comparability), early initiation of ANC, coverage of essential ANC components (including blood pressure measurement, laboratory testing, iron/folic acid supplementation, tetanus protection and antiparasitic treatment), and postnatal care (PNC) for mothers and newborns within 2 days of delivery. By examining these indicators, the study aims to better understand the factors driving changes in service coverage and to highlight context-specific dynamics across four West African countries. It also identifies key determinants of improved access to basic obstetric care in Burkina Faso, Côte d’Ivoire, Ghana and Senegal, with implications for designing equity-oriented health policies.

Structure of analysis population by country and survey year

DHS surveys are nationally representative cross-sectional household surveys based on a two-stage stratified sampling design.9 In the first stage, clusters (enumeration areas) are selected with probability proportional to size. In the second stage, households are systematically selected within each cluster. All women aged 15–49 residing in selected households are eligible for interview.

The analytical sample was restricted to the most recent live birth within the 2 years preceding each survey to minimise recall bias and ensure comparability of maternal health indicators. The unit of analysis is the live birth. Sample sizes range from 2329 births in Ghana (2014) to 5860 births in Burkina Faso (2010).

All analyses accounted for the complex survey design of DHS data. Sampling weights (v005/1000000) were applied to adjust for unequal probabilities of selection and non-response. Stratification and clustering were accounted for using the variables v023 and v021, respectively. Data were declared using the svyset command in Stata, and all descriptive and regression analyses were conducted using survey-adjusted procedures.

Analyses were conducted separately for each country and survey year. As such, no additional reweighting was performed across countries or survey rounds, as each DHS survey is designed to be independently nationally representative.

Variables and outcomes of interest

Five variables of interest were selected for this analysis because of their importance to basic obstetric care. First, we have ANC coverage (eight or more visits), early initiation of ANC services and coverage of the full set of ANC services components. According to current WHO standards, a pregnant woman should attend at least eight ANC visits, with the first occurring within the first 12 weeks of pregnancy. However, to ensure comparability with earlier DHS rounds, which were based on the previous standard of at least four ANCs to comply with the previous data used in the study for which the current standard was at least four ANCs. The coverage of all components of ANC services includes: (1) blood pressure measurement, (2) blood sampling for various tests (HIV test, hepatitis, etc), (3) urine sampling, (4) iron and folic acid supplementation, (5) protection against neonatal tetanus and (6) taking medication against intestinal parasites during pregnancy. However, certain components, introduced from DHS8 onwards, have not been considered, to allow the comparability of this indicator over time. Finally, after delivery, the variable used is the postnatal consultation coverage of the mother and newborn within 2 days of delivery. All these indicators were constructed in accordance with the DHS statistics guide.22

The control variables included in this study are recognised in the literature as determinants on the use of prenatal care during pregnancy and PNC after delivery. These variables were grouped into three categories: demographic, socio-economic and cultural factors. Demographic factors included place of residence, marital status, parity, mother’s age at childbirth and the sex of the household head. Socio-economic factors included household wealth, educational attainment and exposure to media. Cultural factors included the woman’s religion and her level of decision-making autonomy.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Statistical analysis

We primarily used bivariate descriptive statistics to examine associations between each dependent variable and the independent variables. In addition to point estimates, we estimated 95% CIs to determine significant differences between the various levels of each variable as well as the statistical significance of change over time. To identify the mechanisms of change in the use of obstetric care, we used the Oaxaca–Blinder multivariate decomposition method.23 This method quantifies the contributions of the different analysis variables to differences in mean predictions between two groups on the basis of multivariate regression models. Specifically, the technique leverages the coefficients derived from a regression model to estimate the proportion of statistical differences (either mean or proportion) between the two groups that can be attributed to disparities in population characteristics, as well as the proportion attributable to behavioural or political performance differences.23,25 Additionally, this technique can be applied in a longitudinal framework to decompose changes in a phenomenon into components associated with performance effect and a component linked to changes in the composition of the population studied (composition effect).23,25 We applied this model to each of the variables of interest for each of the four countries examined in this study. This arrangement is called twofold decomposition in that it splits the average difference into two components.23 27 The E component refers to the part of the differential linked to disparities in characteristics, generally referred to as the explained effect or the characteristics effect. The C component refers to the part of the differential attributable to differencess in the coefficients, usually called the unexplained component or coefficient effect. In this equation, group A is considered to be the control group. Thus, E reflects a counterfactual comparison of the difference in results from the point of view of group A, that is, the difference expected if group B were given the coefficients of group A. Thus, the coefficients remain identical for both groups; only the characteristics vary. Component C reflects a counterfactual comparison of the results from the point of view of group B, that is, the difference expected if the characteristics of group B were applied to group A. In the latter case, the two groups have the same characteristics and different coefficients.

By setting the coefficients of the composition component (E) to Group A levels, we estimate the contribution to the differential that would have occurred if the effects associated with the characteristics had been set to Group A values. By setting the characteristics to group B levels in component C, we assess the contribution to the differential that is due to the difference in effects. This difference in effects is also known as the unexplained difference, since the differences in observable characteristics do not allow us to account for it.28 It is often attributable to a difference in performance between the two groups, understood in the sense of changes in demographic and health behaviours and in the efficiency of the health sector. This difference in coefficients, like the composition effect, may of course be influenced by characteristics not taken into account by the model (One of the limitations of the decomposition method used here is that it does not provide an R2 type indicator to measure the share of variance explained by the decomposition).

Results

Descriptive results

Online supplemental appendix 1 presents the trends in ANC and PNC coverage in Burkina Faso. Overall ANC coverage increased significantly between 2010 and 2021. The coverage for antenatal consultations with four or more visits (ANC4+) more than doubled, rising from 32.5% in 2010 to 72.1% in 2021. This increase is particularly notable among the disadvantaged socio-economic groups (rural areas, lower standard of living, no level of education) who had the lowest coverage rates in 2010. The results show that the ANC8+ coverage remains low, ranging from 0.5% in 2010 to only 0.9% in 2021. ANC visits during the first trimester of pregnancy increased from 40% in 2010 to 53% in 2021. Burkina Faso also recorded substantial progress in the coverage of the full set of ANC components, which rose from 15.3% to 38.9% between 2010 and 2021. PNC coverage for mothers and newborns more than doubled, increasing from 25.4% to 69.5%. These increases reflect consistent improvement in early ANC initiation, coverage of all ANC components and PNC consultations across each socio-economic category.

The analysis of ANC4+ coverage in Ivory Coast, presented in online supplemental appendix 2, indicates a significant increase between 2011 and 2021. The ANC4+ coverage rate increased from 42.8% to 56.3% over this period. This progress reflects substantial increase in ANC+ coverage in most socio-economic categories, except for women from households with a high standard of living, those with secondary education or those with moderate decision-making autonomy, among whom ANC+ coverage remained stable and high between 2011 and 2021. The use of a minimum of eight ANCs remains limited. ANC8+ coverage increased from 3.1% in 2011 to only 3.7% in 2021, a non-significant increase at the 5% threshold. Early initiation of ANC increased significantly, from 27.8% in 2011 to 39.1% in 2021 (online supplemental appendix 2). Coverage of all ANC components also improved nationally rising from 21.2% to 34.2% between 2011 and 2021. In the same sense, a significant improvement was observed across most socio-economic groups, except for women from households with a higher standard of living or those with a secondary level of education or higher, among whom coverage remained stable and high between 2011 and 2021. Finally, coverage in PNC has seen a substantial increase, from 30.8% in 2011 to 66.3% in 2021. This progression reflects a generalised improvement in PNC coverage across all socio-economic categories.

In Ghana, the results show that coverage remained high and relatively stable between 2014 and 2022 (see online supplemental appendix 3). This ANC4+ coverage rate increased from 85.6% in 2014 to 87.8% in 2022. However, this slight increase is not statistically significant over the period. Although ANC4+ coverage is satisfactory in Ghana, the standard of eight ANC visits, including one during the first trimester of pregnancy, remains a major challenge. The ANC8+ coverage remains relatively low, although increasing, from 29.1% in 2014 to only 38.6% in 2022. The use of ANC during the first trimester remained stable over the same period (increased from 61.6% to 62.5% statistically insignificant). The results also highlight significant progress in the coverage of all ANC components between 2014 and 2022, increasing from 29% to 44.5%. Finally, postnatal consultation coverage for mothers and newborns also rose substantially, from 21.5% in 2014 to 81.2% in 2022. This increase reflects consistent improvements across all socio-economic categories.

In Senegal, the ANC4+ coverage increased from 48.4% in 2010 to 68.4% in 2023 following substantial gains across most socio-economic categories. The main exception were women from households with a high standard of living and those with secondary education or higher, among whom ANC4+ coverage remained consistently high and stable between 2010 and 2023. The results of ANC8+coverage and early ANC use show that the level of ANC8+ use remains low, although a slight increase has been observed (2.1% in 2010 to 6.6% in 2023). In contrast, early initiation of ANC increased significantly, rising from 58.3% to 70.5% between 2010 and 2023. Coverage of the full set of ANC components also improved markedly over the period, increasing from 13.8% to 29.5%. Finally, PNC coverage for mothers and newborns rose substantially, from 39.3% in 2010 to 73.6% in 2023 (see online supplemental appendix 4).

Overall, the findings suggest that unmeasured factors—particularly changes in behaviour and health system performance—made a strong and positive contribution to the increase in ANC and PNC coverage between 2010 and 2021. Specifically, behavioural or performance-related changes account for approximately 98.2% of the increase in ANC4+ coverage, while the contribution of changes in population structure is not statistically significant. Examining individual contributions within the performance effect, women in union contributed the most (29.1%), followed by women living in rural areas (14.7%) and those with two or three children (5.4%). In contrast, women with high decision-making autonomy (−3.8%), those from wealthier households (−3.4%) and those with secondary or higher education (−1.95%) slightly reduced the overall increase. However, these negative contributions remain relatively small. For early ANC initiation, 93.8% of the increase is explained by the performance effect and 6.2% by the composition effect. Positive contributions from the composition effect include women with secondary or higher education (4.6%), those from wealthier households (1.8%) and those living in female-headed households (1.3%). Conversely, women in union contributed negatively (−1.04%). Within the performance effect, women with moderate autonomy contributed positively (22.7%), while women from wealthier households (−14.4%), those exposed to media (−28.5%) and Christian women (−10.5%) constituted an obstacle to the increase in the use of early ANC. Regarding the increase in coverage of all components of ANC services, the multivariate decomposition analysis reveals that it is explained 99.2% by the behavioural or performance effect. The share due to changes in population structure is not significant at the 5% threshold. Women living in rural areas (23.8%), with moderate (14%) or high (15.1%) decision-making autonomy and with two or three children (8.6%) contributed most positively to this increase through the performance or behavioural effect. Conversely, women in union were a barrier to this increase with a relative contribution of −41.5%. Finally, observed changes in women’s behaviour explain approximately 100% of the increase in PNC coverage. The effect of changes in population structure on the increase in PNC coverage was not significant. Women exposed to media (18.2%) and those living in rural areas (12.2%) contributed positively, while women in union again slowed progress (−24.3%).

Côte d’Ivoire

ANC4+ coverage increased by 13.6% between 2011 and 2021, driven by a performance effect of 80.2%, compared with a composition effect of 19.9%. In other words, behavioural changes helped explain 80.2% of the increase, while changes in population structure accounted for 19.9% of this increase. Women with secondary education or higher (6%), those who were neither Christian nor Muslim (5%) and those living in rural areas (3.4%) contributed positively to the increase, while women with high decision-making autonomy (−4.7%) and those with primary education (−3.8%) slowed this increase through the composition effect. Through the performance effect, women with high autonomy contributed the most (17.1%) to the increase while women from rural areas slowed this increase with a relative contribution of −33.2%. The increase in the use of early ANC (11.3%) is explained 81.6% by the behaviour or performance effect and 18.4% by the composition effect. Women with secondary education or higher (5.6%) and those with at least six children (5%) contributed the most, through the composition effect, to the increase in this coverage while women with high autonomy slowed this increase with a relative contribution of −3.4%. Through the performance effect, women with four or five children and those from low-income households contributed the most to the increase with shares of 25.5% and 22%, respectively. Regarding the increase in coverage of all components of ANC services (13%), the multivariate decomposition reveals that it is explained 90% by the behavioural or performance effect against 10% due to changes in the population structure. Women living in rural areas (37.9%), women living in low (12.5%), medium (10.6%) and high (13.2%) standard of living households contributed positively to this increase through the performance or behavioural effect. Conversely, women in union slowed this increase with a relative contribution of −54.92%. Finally, changes in women’s behaviour explain approximately 99.4% of the increase in PNC coverage. Media exposure (14.4%) and women’s empowerment (10.2%) were the main positive contributors, while women aged 20–34 at childbirth contributed negatively (−22.5%) (table 3).

Discussion

The findings of this study show a substantial increase in the coverage of ANC and PNC services over the last decade across the four countries examined. This progress reflects a broad improvement in service utilisation across most socio-economic groups, except among the most educated women and those from wealthier households, where coverage levels were already high and remained largely stable. As a result, the greatest gains were observed among socio-economically disadvantaged populations. The decomposition analysis further indicates that the primary drivers of these improvements are factors not fully captured by the analytical models, notably changes in women’s health-seeking behaviour and improvements in health system performance. In contrast, changes in population composition played a relatively limited role in explaining the observed progress. These findings confirm trends already described in the literature, according to which interventions focused on improving access, quality of prenatal care and behaviour change through communication can produce significant positive effects on the use of services.29

Burkina Faso is a particularly notable case. The results show that disadvantaged groups in Burkina Faso, especially women who are rural, poor and less educated, experienced greater improvements compared with other countries. This trend can be partly attributed to the introduction in 2016 of a free healthcare policy for pregnant women and children under 5, which reduced financial barriers to accessing services.30 This initiative, by removing direct costs, has promoted greater equity, as also suggested by the work of Richard et al on the impact of pro-poor health policies in West Africa.31 However, it is noted that in rural areas, less educated women have benefited more from equity policies, which tends to reduce inequalities, as highlighted by Ameur et al.32 In Ghana, despite initially high levels of ANC and PNC utilisation, progress over time was more limited. Rather than indicating saturation, this pattern may reflect persistent non-financial barriers, such as transportation costs and lost income, as highlighted by previous studies.33 Moreover, the perceived quality of care, which strongly influences demand, remains a concern, particularly in public facilities.34 In Senegal, the positive impact of media exposure is particularly salient. The effectiveness of community campaigns, such as the ‘Badiene Gokh’ programme, which mobilises female community leaders to promote maternal health, could explain this dynamic.35 36 Furthermore, the national policy of subsidising deliveries and emergency obstetric and neonatal care has helped to improve access, although hidden costs may persist.37,39

While progress in ANC4+ coverage is real, the situation is much more mixed when analysing the full use of quality components of ANC, including ANC8+, laboratory tests, tetanus vaccination and iron supplementation. This inadequacy is worrying because a high frequency of consultations without quality interventions does not guarantee substantial health gains.40 41 Thus, in all four countries, achieving universal coverage remains insufficient for these critical components. The results corroborate the hypothesis that improved access (frequency of visits) is not always accompanied by an improvement in the actual quality of services.40,42 Although less decisive than the behavioural effect, the structural effect also plays an important role, notably through three key dimensions: media exposure, women’s decision-making autonomy and residence in an urban or rural area. In Senegal, high media exposure has made it possible to effectively disseminate messages promoting maternal care, contributing to better informing women about the importance of early and quality ANC.43 Similarly, in Côte d’Ivoire, the increase in the level of education and empowerment of women, particularly in urban areas, contributes to the improvement of coverage indicators. In Ghana, despite a relatively well-developed health insurance system, progress remains modest, partly because the structural effect (education, empowerment) has not evolved as quickly and rural barriers persist.44 In Burkina Faso, the structural effect is less pronounced, notably because policy interventions (such as free healthcare) targeted the entire population, regardless of social characteristics. However, it is noted that rural, poorly educated women from disadvantaged regions benefited more from equity policies, which tend to reduce inequalities, as highlighted by Ameur et al (2020).32

At the individual level, women’s education remains a major and cross-cutting determinant of obstetric care utilisation. The positive relationship between education and service use is well established in the literature11 and is confirmed in this study, although its magnitude varies by country. Media exposure also plays a critical role in shaping attitudes and social norms related to maternal health.45 In contrast, the effect of women’s decision-making autonomy appears more nuanced. While moderate levels of autonomy appear favourable in some contexts (eg, Burkina Faso, Senegal), higher levels of autonomy may, paradoxically, reduce the use of obstetric care in others (eg, Ghana, Côte d’Ivoire). This apparent paradox can be interpreted in light of the analysis by Bloom et al46 according to which autonomy, when not supported by a favourable environment (quality of services, respect for patients, trust in the health system), can lead to an increased distance from formal services. However, this interpretation warrants further nuance in the present context. The attenuation of progress in the group of women with high autonomy in these two countries could be explained by the achievement of a saturation level in terms of obstetric care in this group, the rates having not changed considerably over the last decade

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. By using data from four countries across two time periods, this study provides a valuable opportunity to examine changes in access to basic obstetric care over time and to compare these trends across diverse contexts. The analysis also benefits from the use of decomposition methods, which allow for a more detailed understanding of the mechanisms underlying these changes.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the DHS data offer substantial advantages, they are subject to limitations inherent to retrospective, cross-sectional, single-round surveys, including selection bias and omissions. Indeed, only survivors aged 15 to 49 who had not migrated at the time of the survey were asked about their last live births in the 2 years prior to the survey. However, if the number of children whose mothers are not alive or have emigrated is significant, or if they have different rates of basic obstetric care use than children whose mothers are interviewed, this would lead to significant bias in the estimation of changes. Second, DHS surveys rely on respondents’ recall to reconstruct relatively old events that may have occurred sometime before the interview, which may include some level of recall bias. This can lead to risks of omission, imprecision and concordance in the dating of events. However, according to Rutstein et al, the methodological limitations inherent in birth history and the risks of errors or inaccuracies in data collection generally result in a very small margin of error in the measurement of recent events.47 In addition, some essential variables, such as the spouse’s educational level and occupation, were not included in the analysis because of high rates of non-response.

With regard to the Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition, although the method is robust, it relies on assumptions of linearity and additive effects, which can oversimplify the complex interactions among socio-economic, behavioural and policy-related factors. In addition, unobserved variables, such as healthcare availability, contextual cultural changes in health-seeking behaviour or the presence of community-based health initiatives, were not captured in the models. The omission of these factors may have led to an overestimation of the contribution attributed to ‘performance effects’.

Third, differences in survey timing and contextual conditions across countries may affect comparability. For example, in Ghana, the baseline year is 2014, whereas the other countries have 2010 or 2011 as their baseline. In contrast, the final survey years differ only slightly from one country to another (2021, 2022 and 2023). Although these differences in baseline are relatively limited, they may nevertheless generate differential changes, with gains being able to be driven in one or 2 years of implementing a development policy. The case of Burkina Faso illustrates the importance of contextual differences, as data collection in 2021 took place during a period marked by an insecurity crisis. Such circumstances may have affected both sampling procedures and health system functioning in ways that differ from those in the other countries analysed. The same is true of the socio-economic and cultural contexts that differ between these four countries, which also motivated an isolated analysis by country. Consequently, comparisons across countries should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion and recommendation

Overall, the findings show a significant increase in ANC4+ coverage across all four countries, with particularly strong progress in Burkina Faso and Senegal, and more modest gains in Ghana and Côte d’Ivoire. However, a closer examination of ANC quality reveals persistently low adherence to the WHO-recommended standard of eight visits (ANC8+) and to the full package of essential interventions, including laboratory tests, tetanus vaccination, iron supplementation and HIV screening. Burkina Faso provides a particularly instructive case. The results show that women from disadvantaged backgrounds (those who are poor, rural and less educated) experienced more rapid improvements in access to care. This could be attributed to the free maternal and child healthcare policy introduced in 2016, which aimed to remove financial barriers to accessing health services. While this policy appears to have reduced financial barriers and expanded coverage, its impact on the quality of care remains limited, which may explain the low utilisation of comprehensive care components. These findings highlight that improving coverage without ensuring quality can hinder gains in maternal and newborn health.

From a policy perspective, the results highlight the need to strengthen interventions targeting vulnerable populations, particularly women who are poor, rural and less educated. This includes investing in girls’ education, promoting women’s empowerment in a context-sensitive manner and expanding media-based health promotion strategies tailored to local contexts. Above all, improving the quality of care must be a central priority to ensure the effectiveness and sustainability of maternal health intervention.
