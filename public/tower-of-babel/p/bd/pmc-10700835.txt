Incorporation of internal iliac arteries as target vessels during physician-modified fenestrated branched endovascular repair of pararenal abdominal aortic aneurysm with concomitant bilateral short common iliac artery aneurysms
Authors: Michelle Manesh, Herbert I. James, Alyssa Pyun, Young Hong, Jacquelyn Paige, Sukgu M. Han
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
This report describes an alternative endovascular approach to iliac branch devices for treatment of an abdominal aortic aneurysm with concomitant bilateral short common iliac aneurysms. The short distance between the renal arteries and internal iliac artery origins made the addition of distal iliac branch devices to the proximal fenestrated stent graft challenging. We elected to perform physician-modified fenestrated branched endovascular repair, using four fenestrations for the visceral and renal arteries and an additional two directional branches for the bilateral internal iliac arteries. The patient recovered uneventfully, and the 24-month follow-up imaging shows successful aneurysm exclusion with patent internal iliac artery branches.

Up to 40% of patients with an abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) have concomitant unilateral or bilateral iliac artery aneurysms.1 The initial approach to this anatomic scenario was extension of the iliac limb into the external iliac artery (EIA) combined with hypogastric embolization. The morbidity associated with unilateral or bilateral hypogastric occlusion is well described and includes buttock claudication in 1.6% to 56% of patients and erectile dysfunction in 17% of patients. More devastating complications such as spinal cord ischemia, bowel ischemia, and pelvic necrosis are seen in 1% of patients.1, 2, 3 The loss of collateral flow to the spinal cord is particularly ill-advised in patients undergoing repair of complex AAAs or thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysms in whom spinal cord coverage is significant. Several endovascular and hybrid techniques have been designed to preserve hypogastric perfusion, including iliac branched devices (IBDs), flared limbs, parallel graft techniques, and extra-anatomic bypasses.4

The IBD is well-described and most often used for internal iliac artery (IIA) preservation during endovascular repair of an infrarenal AAA with a concomitant common iliac artery (CIA) aneurysm. The use of an IBD, particularly in bilateral IIA preservation, is often limited by an insufficient distance from the lowest renal artery to the iliac bifurcation, frequently seen in patients with short CIAs.1 Bilateral IBD placement requires yet longer distances to accommodate the bridging iliac limb between the longer ipsilateral limb of the main body and the distal IBD.2 In the present, we describe an alternative endovascular technique of incorporating bilateral IIAs with additional side branches during physician-modified fenestrated branched endovascular aortic repair (PM-FBEVAR). The patient gave written informed consent for the report of his case details and imaging studies.

Case report

A 73-year-old man with a history of coronary artery bypass, hyperlipidemia, and hypertension presented with growth of a known AAA by 1 cm over 6 months. Imaging demonstrated a 5.9-cm juxtarenal aneurysm with bilateral, short, and aneurysmal CIAs (right CIA, 16.6 mm in maximum diameter by 19.0 mm in length; left CIA, 18.1 mm by 17.1 mm; Fig 1, A). Based on the patient's comorbidities, the patient was enrolled in a physician-sponsored investigational device exemption protocol (Impact of Frailty on Clinical Outcomes of Patients Treated for Thoracoabdominal and Complex Abdominal Aortic Aneurysms with Physician-Modified Fenestrated and Branched Stent Grafts; physician-sponsored investigational device exemption [PS-IDE], G200159; National Cancer Institute, APP-20-04282) for PM-FBEVAR. The objective of the PS-IDE study is to assess the technical success, major adverse events, and clinical outcomes when PM-FBEVAR is used to repair thoracoabdominal and complex aortic aneurysms in patients deemed at high risk for open repair. Procedure details

Physician modification

A single Zenith Alpha thoracic stent graft measuring 28 mm × 178 mm × 32 mm (Cook Medical) was modified to contain four fenestrations for visceral and renal artery incorporation and two outer branches for bilateral IIA incorporation (Fig 1, B). The IIA branches were preloaded in accordance with our previously described technique.5 In brief, back-table modification of the main body stent graft was performed under sterile technique. Fenestrations were created with ophthalmic cautery and reinforced with double layers of nitinol loops of an endovascular snare using locking running 5-0 Ethibond sutures (Ethicon, Johnson & Johnson MedTech). Side arm branch cuffs were created by sewing 1- to 2-cm segments of 8-mm self-expanding Viabahn covered stents (W.L. Gore & Associates) onto cauterized orifices using running 6-0 Gore-Tex sutures (W.L. Gore & Associates). Directional branches were then preloaded with 0.014-in. guidewire, and the modified device was resheathed using 2-0 silk ties and Silastic tape (Dow Chemical Co).

Implantation

Under general anesthesia, percutaneous bilateral femoral access and open exposure of the left brachial artery were performed. The descending aorta was accessed from the femoral approach. Angiography and cone beam fusion computed tomography angiography were performed to denote the origin of target vessels and facilitate subsequent steps. A 12F × 45-cm sheath was advanced to the descending thoracic aorta via brachial access. Through-and-through brachiofemoral access was established, and a 5F × 90-cm sheath was placed over this. Under fusion imaging guidance, the physician-modified fenestrated branched endograft was loaded and delivered from the left femoral artery to the abdominal aorta and partially deployed, with fenestrations aligning to their target vessels. The celiac artery, superior mesenteric artery, and bilateral renal arteries were sequentially catheterized via their fenestrations from the contralateral femoral approach. Next, the IIA branch cuffs were sequentially catheterized via through-and-through brachiofemoral access using the preloaded wires. The main body was then fully deployed, and the proximal seal zone was balloon molded. Bridging branch stents (10-mm × 15-cm followed by 10-mm × 10-cm self-expanding Viabahn stents) were placed into the IIA branch cuffs. A 3-cm seal zone into the bilateral IIAs was obtained (Fig 2). These were balloon molded across the branch cuffs and the entire length of the bridging stents. The visceral and renal arteries, with wires still place, were then stented and flared across each fenestration. Subsequently, a distal bifurcated component (Excluder endoprosthesis; W.L. Gore & Associates), measuring 31 mm × 14.5 mm × 13 cm, was deployed, sealing distally into the right EIA, followed by deployment of its contralateral limb, measuring 14.5 mm × 10 cm and sealing distally into the left EIA. All overlap zones were balloon molded with a Coda balloon. Completion angiography demonstrated excellent apposition of the graft with widely patent branches (Fig 3). The total procedure time was 223 minutes, including 36 minutes of fluoroscopy time using 150 mL of contrast. Outcome

Our patient had an uneventful postoperative course and was discharged home on postoperative day 2. At his most recent follow-up at 24 months, he remained asymptomatic with computed tomography angiography showing patency of the IIA branches, no endoleak, and aneurysm sac regression from 5.9 cm to 4.5 cm (Fig 4). The patient underwent angioplasty of the superior mesenteric artery and right renal artery stents at 18 months for in-stent restenosis noted on surveillance imaging. Discussion

Preservation of the IIA during complex EVAR has been most commonly reported using IBDs, which have demonstrated safety and efficacy in the treatment of unilateral and bilateral iliac aneurysms during EVAR.1, 2, 3 A recent meta-analysis included 36 studies and 1502 patients.1 The pooled technical success rate was 97%, and the 30-day mortality was 0.67%. At a mean follow-up of 16 months, reinterventions were required for EIA occlusion (2.6%), IIA occlusion (3.1%), and endoleak (13%), with type II endoleaks the most common (8.5%). Anatomic features limiting application of IBDs include excessive iliac tortuosity, calcification, angulation between the CIA and IIA, intraluminal CIA thrombus, and IIA stenosis. One of the most cited limitations is an insufficient distance from the renal artery to the iliac bifurcation, often seen in patients with short CIAs, such as patients of Asian descent.2,6, 7, 8, 9

Off-label techniques to overcome this limitation have been reported, facilitating IIA preservation during FBEVAR.7,8 Recent data suggest that >60% of IBDs are placed outside of their instructions for use.3,7 Friesen et al8 repaired bilateral IIA aneurysms via custom-made, bilateral, triple-branched IBDs to preserve both superior and inferior gluteal arteries, thereby treating all aneurysmal disease and decreasing the risk of pelvic ischemia. Wang et al9 recently described their single-center experience with surgeon-modified IBDs for IIA preservation in 15 patients with abnormal iliac anatomy. The outcomes from their study (mean follow-up time of 35.4 months) demonstrate this technique is comparable to that of commercially available IBDs in terms of procedural success, mortality, patency, and freedom from reintervention.9

We present an alternative endovascular approach for IIA preservation in patients with an AAA and concomitant bilateral CIA aneurysms. In general, the choice between fenestrations and branch cuffs in PM-FBEVAR depends on patient-specific anatomy, accounting for aortic size and angulation at the level of the target branches. In the present case, IIAs were targeted with directional branches, given their caudal positioning and lack of significant stenosis or angulation. Directional branches were placed below the visceral and renal fenestrations in a staggered fashion to facilitate sheathing of the device. By leveraging the preloaded wires from the left brachial approach (SCAPED [sequential catheterization amid progressive endograft deployment] technique10), each IIA was successfully catheterized and stented. Also, IIA catheterization was not difficult or excessively time-consuming. The total operative time and modification time were comparable to the average times at our institution for most PM-FBEVARs.

Furthermore, the addition of IBDs during FBEVAR comes with an increased number of component overlaps, with the inherent risk of failure modes, including endoleak, migration, and graft occlusion. The technique described in our report reduces the number of aortoiliac components from eight to four and reduces the component overlap zones from seven to five (Fig 5). Another consideration that lends favor to a simpler repair with fewer endograft components is the cost. In a recent multicenter retrospective review evaluating the use of the Gore iliac branch endoprosthesis (IBE) for treatment of bilateral CIA aneurysms, Maldonado et al2 found that, in fact, the cost could be prohibitive at most centers. A potential limitation in this technique is the long IIA bridging stents required, with the increased risk of kinking and occlusion. However, our technical success and mid-term follow-up data are promising. The long-term durability of this construct will be assessed as a part of the ongoing PS-IDE protocol.

Conclusions

Incorporation of bilateral IIAs with side arm branches during PM-FBEVAR offers a cost-effective, alternative, endovascular repair technique for complex AAAs with concomitant short CIA aneurysms.
