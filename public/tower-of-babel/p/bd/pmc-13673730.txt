Long-term clinical outcomes and complications after Open Reduction and Internal Fixation (ORIF) with locking plate for proximal humerus fractures: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Jacopo Conteduca, Elena Gasbarra, Igor Rausa, Giorgio Giannini, Luigi Valentino, Giuseppe Solarino, Luigi Meccariello, Giuseppe Rollo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOpen reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) using locking plates remains a widely used head-preserving treatment for displaced proximal humerus fractures in adults. While short-term outcomes are well documented, long-term functional performance, complication burden, and implant survivorship beyond 5-years remain poorly defined. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to synthesise long-term (≥ 5-years) functional outcomes, complication rates, and reoperation rates following locking plate ORIF for proximal humerus fractures.MethodsA protocol-registered (PROSPERO CRD420251156067) systematic review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines. PubMed, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library were searched for English-language randomized and non-randomized clinical studies reporting ≥ 5-year follow-up after ORIF with plate fixation for two-, three-, or four-part proximal humerus fractures in adults. Functional outcomes like Constant–Murley score (CMS), Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand Score (DASH), University of California Los Angeles Shoulder Score (UCLA), and Oxford Shoulder Score (OSS), complications, and reoperation rates were extracted. Random-effects meta-analyses were performed, with heterogeneity assessed using the I² statistic. Risk of bias was evaluated using RoB 2 and ROBINS-I, and certainty of evidence was graded using GRADE.ResultsTen studies met the inclusion criteria, with follow-up ranging from 5 to nearly 15 years. Pooled analyses demonstrated no statistically significant long-term differences in CMS, DASH, or UCLA scores, although the OSS favored ORIF (MD 5.00; 95% CI 1.31–8.69). Long-term complications were common, particularly AVN (5–23%), screw penetration (9–15%), stiffness, and glenohumeral osteoarthritis (5–16%). Meta-analysis suggested reduced risks of complications (RR 0.06), overall reoperation/secondary surgery (RR 0.34; 95% CI 0.13–0.87), implant removal (RR 0.34; 95% CI 0.17–0.67), and revision surgery (RR 0.17; 95% CI 0.07–0.45); however, these estimates should be interpreted cautiously because heterogeneity was substantial to extreme across outcomes. Overall certainty of evidence was very low.ConclusionLocking plate ORIF for proximal humerus fractures provides acceptable but highly variable long-term functional outcomes, with a considerable burden of late complications and secondary procedures. These findings highlight the importance of careful patient selection, meticulous surgical technique, and long-term follow-up. High-quality prospective studies with standardized outcome reporting are needed to better define the durability of ORIF in this challenging injury.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-026-10189-1.

Background

Proximal humerus fractures are among the most common fractures in adults, and their incidence has increased substantially with the aging population and the onset of osteoporosis [1, 2]. Among the various surgical approaches, open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) using locking plates has emerged as the most popular method, especially for displaced two-, three-, and four-part fractures [3, 4]. Locking plates are specifically engineered to offer angular stability, restore anatomy, and preserve the native humeral head, making ORIF a preferable option for both young and older patients [5]. However, despite the many benefits, ORIF is also the method most linked to a broad range of postoperative and delayed complications, such as avascular necrosis (AVN), varus collapse, screw penetration, non-union, hardware-related mechanical failure, and reoperation [6]. These complications naturally lead to questions about the long-term durability and functional survivorship of locking plate ORIF, especially since early radiographic healing does not necessarily correlate with long-term joint preservation and implant success.

Since ORIF remains the predominant method of head preservation for proximal humerus fractures in all age groups, the need for long-term follow-up arises [7]. The late complications of humeral head AVN, secondary glenohumeral osteoarthritis, loosening or migration of hardware, and conversion to arthroplasty usually occur many years after the initial surgery [8]. Therefore, the knowledge of long-term outcomes of ORIF is imperative for surgical planning and providing realistic functional expectations.

Recent literature has highlighted significant limitations. Kavuri et al. have synthesized complications related to locking plates, but included studies had a minimum of follow-up of only 12 months, without providing a combined long-term functional outcome or assessing the performance of the implants beyond the early postoperative period [9]. Similarly, the network meta-analysis by Zheng et al. has compared different treatment modalities for proximal humerus fractures, including ORIF, Intramedullary Nail, HA, and reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (RTSA), but the included randomized trials mainly reported short-term outcomes, with most follow-up periods limited to 24 months [10]. Moreover, existing literature on arthroplasty, such as the assessment of tuberosity healing after reverse shoulder arthroplasty, is limited to arthroplasty-related outcomes and geriatric patients, without considering the long-term prognosis of ORIF with locking plates [11]. Collectively, these studies underscore a persistent gap: to date, no systematic review has synthesized functional outcomes and late complications from studies reporting long-term follow-up (≥ 5-year) after ORIF with locking plate fixation for proximal humerus fractures.

Therefore, the aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to evaluate long-term (≥ 5-year) clinical outcomes, functional scores, and complication rates following ORIF with locking plates for proximal humerus fractures in adults. By synthesizing data exclusively from studies meeting this long-term follow-up threshold, this review addresses a key evidence gap and provides clinically meaningful insights into the durability and long-term prognosis of ORIF for proximal humerus fractures.

Methodology

Protocol and registration

This review followed a prospectively registered protocol in PROSPERO (CRD420251156067) and was conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines; a PRISMA flow diagram documents the screening pathway [12].

Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies included adult patients (≥ 18-years) with two‑, three‑, or four‑part proximal humerus fractures treated using ORIF with plate osteosynthesis, with a minimum follow‑up of 5-years and outcomes reported using validated functional scores such as Constant–Murley score (CMS), Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand Score (DASH), University of California Los Angeles Shoulder Score (UCLA), Oxford Shoulder Score (OSS) or American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons Score (ASES). Both randomized and non‑randomized clinical studies published in English were included. Studies were excluded if they involved paediatric populations, follow‑up < 5-years, non‑plate fixation methods (such as intramedullary nails, arthroplasty, conservative treatment, or pin fixation), non‑English publications, case reports, studies with < 10 patients, biomechanical studies, or fractures outside the specified classification (Table 1).

Information sources and search strategy

We searched PubMed, Scopus, and The Cochrane Library (CLIB) without date restrictions and limited to English; additional studies were identified by snowballing/forward citation and searching trial/study registers. Only published studies were sought. Full search strings are available in the Additional file 1.

Study selection

After duplicate removal, titles/abstracts and then full texts were screened independently by at least two reviewers, with disagreements resolved by discussion. The PRISMA 2020 diagram summarizes identification, screening, eligibility, and inclusion.

Data extraction

Data were extracted by one reviewer and verified by a second using a piloted extraction form aligned with the protocol (study identifiers, country, design, sample size; demographics; fracture classification; intervention details including surgical approach, augmentation and imaging; follow‑up duration/method; functional outcome instruments; complications; and reoperations). Authors were not contacted for additional information.

Risk of bias assessment

RoB 2 was used for randomized trials and ROBINS‑I for non‑randomized studies, with assessment performed by one reviewer and checked by a second [13, 14]. Any uncertainty was resolved by discussion or a third reviewer if needed.

Data synthesis and analysis

Narrative synthesis was planned when pooling was not feasible due to substantial variability in study design, outcome measures, populations, or comparator arms. RevMan 5.3 analysis software was used for conducting the meta-analysis (Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, Denmark) [15]. Dichotomous outcomes were pooled as risk ratios using the Mantel–Haenszel method. Continuous outcomes were pooled as mean differences using the inverse-variance method. The proportion of variability that attributes heterogeneity was assessed via Cochran’s Q-statistic and I2 statistics. The I² values were interpreted as follows: 0–25% indicated low heterogeneity, 25–50% moderate heterogeneity, 50–75% substantial heterogeneity, and values above 75% represented high or considerable heterogeneity. Random-effects models were applied because clinical and methodological heterogeneity was expected across studies. Funnel plots were employed for detection of publication bias, and bias is revealed if the plots are asymmetrical. The value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Certainty of evidence

Certainty of evidence for each outcome was rated using GRADE (risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, publication bias) with Summary‑of‑Findings tables planned [16].

Result

Study selection

The literature search identified 475 records through database searching and 3 additional records through manual searching. After removal of 54 duplicate records, 424 records underwent title and abstract screening, of which 283 were excluded for irrelevance. The remaining 141 full‑text reports were assessed for eligibility, leading to the exclusion of studies for several predefined reasons, including < 5‑year follow‑up (n = 96), non‑English language (n = 11), paediatric populations (n = 5), lack of reported outcomes (n = 7), plate‑synthesis‑only reports (n = 5), review articles (n = 3), biomechanical designs (n = 1), open fracture populations (n = 1), non-plate fixation (n = 1), and mixed intervention (n = 1). Ultimately, 10 studies met all inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the qualitative and quantitative synthesis (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included studies

The ten included studies varied in design, population profile, fracture type, and duration of follow‑up, contributing to substantial heterogeneity in outcome reporting. Most studies were retrospective or prospective cohort designs evaluating long‑term outcomes following locking‑plate ORIF, although one multicentre RCT compared ORIF with RTSA in elderly patients [17]. The studies were geographically diverse, representing Germany, Türkiye/Turkey, the USA, the UK, Switzerland, Norway, and China. Sample sizes ranged widely for example, Robinson et al. (2019) [18] included 368 patients, whereas Güven et al. (2024) [19] enrolled only 15 patients, resulting in differential statistical precision across studies. Across cohorts, patient demographics differed notably in age, comorbidity burden, and bone quality, affecting prognosis and complication risk. Several studies included predominantly older, osteoporotic populations such as Bahrs et al. (2015) [20], who reported up to 74% osteoporosis prevalence while others, such as Zhang et al. (2024) [21], targeted younger, non‑osteoporotic patients with high‑energy trauma patterns (Table 2).

Surgical characteristics and outcome assessment

The included studies predominantly reported ORIF using locking plates, most commonly PHILOS or other angular‑stable systems, as the primary surgical intervention for proximal humerus fractures [20, 26]. Variations of plate fixation were also reported, including minimally invasive plate osteosynthesis (MIPO) approaches and ORIF with iliac crest bone‑graft augmentation for selected three‑ and four‑part fractures [19, 23]. Two studies included alternative comparison groups—reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (RTSA) in elderly patients [17] and non‑operative management [24].

Fracture classifications also varied, encompassing Neer two‑, three‑, and four‑part fractures, as well as AO/OTA configurations, with surgical approaches predominantly deltopectoral or minimally invasive deltoid split [20, 23]. Follow‑up durations extended from 5-years to nearly 15-years, with several studies specifically reporting outcomes at ≥ 10‑year intervals [21, 22]. Functional outcome measures were heterogenous as well: although CMS were frequently reported, many studies used distinct tools (e.g., QuickDASH, OSS, UCLA, SSV), preventing pooling for multiple metrics but providing broad qualitative insight into shoulder recovery trajectories [22, 25, 26] (Table 3).

Long-term complications and reoperation

The long-term complications showed substantial variability in terms of incidence and severity. AVN and screw penetration were among the complications that were more frequently reported. The incidence of AVN varied from 5% to 23%, and it was lower in older osteoporotic patients [22, 26] and much higher in younger patients with non-osteoporotic fractures [21]. Screw penetration was reported in 9–15% of the locking plate group depending on the study [21, 22, 26].

Stiffness and adhesive capsulitis were common, with Robinson et al. (2019) [18] reporting stiffness in almost 24% of patients, often requiring arthrolysis, and Frima et al. (2019) [23] noting high rates of implant irritation necessitating elective hardware removal in over 50% of their patients. Degenerative glenohumeral osteoarthritis was another common late complication, though inconsistently defined across studies, with rates ranging from approximately 10% [20]to 16% [22] and 5% in the younger population of Zhang et al. (2024) [21]. Other complications, while less common, were still significant and included rotator cuff pathology, nerve injuries, and recurrent instability episodes [17, 23].

Reoperation rates varied across the included studies, with several cohorts reporting both elective and unplanned procedures. Elective implant removal was common in some series, occurring in over 50% of patients in the MIPO cohort [23] and in 40% of patients in a long-term PHILOS cohort [26]. Unplanned revision surgeries were less frequent, with reported rates ranging from 6% to 14% for complications such as fixation failure, screw penetration, infection, or AVN [20, 25, 26]. In a large retrospective series, arthrolysis with implant removal accounted for a substantial number of reoperations [18], while a comparative cohort reported 37.9% revision procedures in surgically treated patients [24] (Table 4).

Functional outcomes

This section summarizes the random-effects meta-analyses of functional outcomes. Where comparative data were available, the locking plate ORIF group was compared with the corresponding comparator group reported in each study, including RTSA or non-operative treatment when applicable (Constant-Murley, UCLA, DASH, and the Oxford Shoulder Score). Across the comparative functional outcome analyses, 344 patients were included in the locking plate ORIF groups and 259 patients in the comparator groups. The pooled mean difference for the Constant–Murley score favored the locking plate ORIF group numerically (MD = − 11.10); however, the difference was not statistically significant (95% CI − 37.22 to 15.02; p = 0.40). There was significant heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 99%), which indicates that there may be a large amount of variability in the studies for multiple reasons including differences in treatment, study populations, and follow-up durations (Figs. 2 and 3). Regarding the UCLA subgroup analysis, while the pooled mean difference was not statistically different between the groups (MD = 1.00; 95% CI=-0.25 to + 2.25; p = 0.12), (Figs. 4 and 5). While some individual studies reported better DASH scores in the locking plate ORIF group, the pooled mean difference was not statistically significant (MD = − 37.63; 95% CI − 110.31 to 35.06; p = 0.31), and the extreme heterogeneity (I² = 100%) suggests substantial between-study variability in functional outcomes (Figs. 6 and 7). The Oxford Shoulder Score was statistically significantly different in favour of the locking plate ORIF group (MD = 5.00; 95% CI = + 1.31 to + 8.69; p = 0.008), which demonstrates better shoulder-specific function (Figs. 8 and 9).

Overall reoperation / secondary surgery (%)

Overall reoperation was defined as any secondary surgical procedure reported after the index treatment, including both elective procedures, such as implant removal, and complication-driven procedures, such as revision fixation, infection surgery, or conversion arthroplasty. Because implant removal and revision surgery have different clinical implications, these outcomes were also analysed separately in "Implant removal" section and "Revision surgeries" section. Figures 10 and 11 summarize the pooled analysis of overall reoperation events. Where comparative data were available, reoperation rates were compared between the locking plate ORIF group and the corresponding comparator group reported in each study. The pooled risk ratio (RR) of 0.34 (95% CI 0.13–0.87), calculated using a random-effects model, suggested a lower risk of overall reoperation in the locking plate ORIF group compared with the corresponding comparator groups. The diamond at the bottom of the plot lies entirely to the left of the line representing no effect (RR = 1), suggesting a statistically significant pooled association favoring the locking plate ORIF group (Z = 2.24, p = 0.03). However, the amount of heterogeneity identified in the studies incorporated into the meta-analysis was very high with a I²=98%, Chi²=401.88, p < 0.00001 indicating a significant amount (variability) in the study results; likely due to variations in the study type, study population; or/and study interventions. The individual study effect estimates were also considerably different. Individual study estimates varied substantially, with some studies suggesting lower reoperation rates in the locking plate ORIF group and others showing little or no difference. Overall, the pooled estimate suggests a possible lower reoperation rate after locking plate ORIF; however, because of the very high heterogeneity, this finding should be interpreted cautiously and considered exploratory (Figs. 10 and 11).

Complications

The forest plot presents pooled analyses of postoperative complications, including secondary fracture displacement, humeral head AVN, non-union, screw penetration, and infection. Results show a statistically significant overall pooled summary with the locking plate ORIF group showing a total risk ratio (RR) of 0.06 and 95% confidence interval (95% CI): 0.04 to 0.10, suggesting lower pooled complication rates in the locking plate ORIF group where comparative data were available (Z = 11.17; p < 0.00001). Subgroup analyses suggested lower pooled risks for AVN, infection, non-union, and screw penetration; however, these estimates should be interpreted cautiously because outcome definitions and study designs varied across studies. However, secondary fracture displacement and humeral head AVN did not show a statistically significant result as their confidence intervals include 1. The overall degree of heterogeneity across the studies is high (I²=86%) with considerable variability noted in the subgroups of study populations, methodologies, and/or interventions, thus resulting in numerous results of different significance. Although the pooled estimate suggested lower complication rates in the locking plate ORIF group, the very high heterogeneity and variability in complication definitions substantially limit confidence in this result. Therefore, this finding should be considered exploratory (Figs. 12 and 13).

Implant removal

Implant removal was analysed separately from overall reoperation to distinguish elective or irritation-related hardware removal from complication-driven secondary procedures. Although the rounded pooled RR for implant removal was numerically similar to the overall reoperation estimate, this was a separate analysis including a different set of events and different heterogeneity statistics. The pooled estimate for implant removal was RR = 0.34 (95% CI 0.17–0.67; p = 0.002), with very high heterogeneity (I² = 95%).

The pooled estimate suggested a lower rate of implant removal in the locking plate ORIF group; however, the high heterogeneity indicates that this result should be interpreted cautiously. Individual study estimates varied, with some studies reporting lower implant-removal rates in the locking plate ORIF group, while others showed non-significant differences. The heterogeneity statistics indicated substantial variability among the included studies (Tau²=0.66; Chi²=98.40, df = 5, p < 0.00001; I²=95%). An I² value of 95% suggests very high heterogeneity, indicating considerable differences in study characteristics, methodologies, or populations across the included studies. Therefore, although the pooled effect was statistically significant, the results should be interpreted cautiously due to the substantial heterogeneity observed. (Figures 14 and 15)

Revision surgeries

Revision surgery was analysed as a secondary-procedure outcome rather than as a grouping variable. Where comparative data were available, revision-surgery events were compared between the locking plate ORIF group and the corresponding comparator group. Seven studies were included in this analysis, comprising 756 participants. The pooled analysis suggested a lower risk of revision surgery in the locking plate ORIF group (RR = 0.17; 95% CI 0.07–0.45; p = 0.0003). However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because revision-surgery definitions and comparator structures varied across studies. Individual study estimates generally favored the locking plate ORIF group, although the magnitude of effect varied substantially across studies.

The heterogeneity analysis revealed considerable variability among the included studies, with Tau² = 1.58, Chi² = 154.20, df = 6 (p < 0.00001), and I² = 96%. An I² value of 96% indicates extremely high heterogeneity, suggesting substantial differences in study populations, methodologies, interventions, or outcome measurements across studies. Although the pooled estimate favored the locking plate ORIF group, the result should be interpreted with caution because of the very high heterogeneity observed among the included studies (Figs. 16 and 17).

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis

Supplementary Table S1 in Additional file 2 presents the results of a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis examining the robustness of pooled estimates for functional outcomes, reoperation rates, and complications. Overall, the findings demonstrate that the pooled estimates remained relatively stable after sequential removal of individual studies, indicating that no single study substantially altered the overall results.

For the Constant–Murley score, the original pooled estimate was − 11.10 (95% CI: −37.22 to 15.02) with extremely high heterogeneity (I²=99%). Sequential exclusion of individual studies produced similar estimates ranging from − 17.16 to 1.74, while heterogeneity remained high (I²=77–99%). This suggests considerable variability among studies but indicates that the overall findings were not driven by any single study. Similarly, for the UCLA score, the pooled estimate was 1.00 (95% CI: −0.25 to 2.25), although heterogeneity could not be assessed due to the limited number of studies. Removal of individual studies altered the estimate slightly, indicating some influence of individual studies due to the limited number of datasets.

For the DASH score, the pooled estimate was − 37.63 (95% CI: −110.31 to 35.06; p = 0.31) with extreme heterogeneity (I² = 100%). This result was not statistically significant and was consistent with the DASH analysis reported in Sect. 3.5. Excluding specific studies, particularly Güven et al. (2024), reduced heterogeneity to 53%, respectively, suggesting these studies contributed notably to between-study variability. The Oxford Shoulder Score showed a pooled estimate of 5.00 (95% CI: 1.31 to 8.69), indicating a statistically significant improvement, although heterogeneity could not be assessed due to the limited number of studies.

For reoperation rate, the pooled estimate was 0.34 (95% CI: 0.13–0.87) with high heterogeneity (I²=98%). Removal of individual studies produced minimal changes in the pooled estimate, suggesting stable results. Among complications, pooled estimates indicated relatively low rates of infection (2%) and non-union (2%), while screw penetration (16%) showed no heterogeneity (I²=0%), indicating consistent reporting across studies. Overall, these findings suggest that the meta-analysis results are generally robust despite substantial heterogeneity in several outcomes.

Risk of bias

Risk-of-bias assessment was performed using RoB 2 for the randomized trial and ROBINS-I for all non-randomized studies. The single RCT (Fraser et al., 2024) showed some concerns with high risk of bias, mainly due to lack of blinding and high levels of missing outcome data at long-term follow-up [17]. All non-randomized studies were rated as serious risk of bias, largely because they were single-arm or unadjusted observational designs with substantial loss to follow-up, no control for confounding, and absence of pre-specified analysis plans. These limitations indicate that the overall certainty of evidence from observational studies is reduced (Supplementary Table S2 – Additional file 2).

GRADE certainty of outcome assessment

The GRADE assessment of outcomes in this review indicates that the overall certainty of evidence is very low across all evaluated domains, despite several statistically significant pooled effects. Functional outcomes demonstrated inconsistent results, with no significant differences observed for Constant–Murley, DASH, or UCLA scores, while only the Oxford Shoulder Score showed a modest improvement; however, these findings were limited by extreme heterogeneity (I² 99–100%) and variability in outcome measures. Similarly, although meta‑analysis suggested a significant reduction in complications (RR 0.06), overall reoperation/secondary surgery (RR 0.34; 95% CI 0.13–0.87), implant removal (RR 0.34; 95% CI 0.17–0.67), and revision surgery (RR 0.17; 95% CI 0.07–0.45); however, confidence in these estimates remains limited due to substantial to extreme heterogeneity (I² 86–98%), inconsistent definitions of outcomes, and variability in study populations and methodologies. Although the rounded point estimates for overall reoperation and implant removal were identical, these analyses represented distinct outcomes with different confidence intervals, event definitions, and heterogeneity estimates. All outcomes were downgraded due to serious risk of bias inherent to predominantly observational studies, inconsistency across results, indirectness arising from heterogeneous populations and outcome definitions, imprecision in effect estimates, and suspected publication bias. Consequently, while the direction of effect generally favours the intervention, the evidence base lacks robustness, and these findings should be interpreted with caution when informing clinical decision-making (Supplementary Table S3 – Additional file 2).

Discussion

Summary of findings

Across the included long-term studies (≥ 5 years follow-up), ORIF with locking plate fixation demonstrated acceptable but highly variable functional outcomes, with pooled analyses showing no statistically significant differences in Constant–Murley, UCLA, or DASH scores, while only the Oxford Shoulder Score indicated a modest but significant functional advantage. However, these findings were accompanied by extreme heterogeneity (I² ~99–100%), reflecting substantial variability in patient characteristics, fracture patterns, and surgical techniques. The burden of long-term complications remained considerable, with avascular necrosis, screw penetration, stiffness, and glenohumeral osteoarthritis being the most frequently reported adverse outcomes. Meta-analysis suggested a significant reduction in complications and reoperation rates in the locking plate ORIF groups, along with lower risks of implant removal and revision surgery; nevertheless, these results must be interpreted cautiously given the consistently high heterogeneity (I² 86–98%) and methodological limitations across studies. Reoperation rates varied widely, with relatively low rates of unplanned revision but notably high rates of elective implant removal in several cohorts. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the stability of pooled estimates, although heterogeneity persisted, and overall certainty of evidence was rated very low due to observational study designs, risk of bias, and inconsistent outcome definitions. Collectively, these findings suggest that while ORIF remains a viable head-preserving option with reasonable long-term survivorship, it is associated with a substantial complication and secondary intervention burden, underscoring the need for careful patient selection, optimized surgical technique, and standardized long-term outcome reporting.

Clinical implications

The findings of this review have several important implications for clinical practice. First, although ORIF with locking plates provides reasonable long-term functional outcomes, the wide variability across studies suggests that outcomes depend heavily on patient selection, particularly age, bone quality, and fracture complexity. Younger, non-osteoporotic patients demonstrated a higher risk of AVN, indicating that surgeons should weigh fixation strategies carefully in this group [27]. Second, the consistently reported rates of screw penetration, stiffness, and glenohumeral osteoarthritis highlight the need for meticulous intraoperative technique, especially restoration of medial support, precise screw length selection, and stable reduction to minimize avoidable complications [28, 29]. Third, the high frequency of elective hardware removal and stiffness-related procedures indicates that patients should be counselled about the realistic possibility of secondary interventions, even when initial healing is satisfactory [30]. Fourth, because functional improvement plateaued after a certain point in several cohorts, rehabilitation protocols may need to emphasize early mobilization balanced with fracture stability to optimize long-term shoulder function [31, 32]. Finally, the substantial heterogeneity and risk of bias across long-term studies reinforce that future clinical decision-making should prioritize individualized assessment and that further high-quality prospective research is needed to guide treatment choices in complex proximal humerus fractures.

Strength and limitations

There are several key strengths to this systematic review. It is the first to synthesize evidence exclusively from studies with long-term follow-up (≥ 5-years), allowing for a more precise evaluation of the longevity and survivorship of the locking plate ORIF than previous systematic reviews that focused on short-term results. This study draws on a variety of international cohorts, which helps to improve generalizability, and examines a wide variety of outcomes, including functional scores, complications, and reoperations, which helps to provide a complete picture of long-term performance. The methodological quality of the study is improved by registration on PROSPERO, PRISMA reporting, the use of validated tools such as RoB 2 and ROBINS-I to assess risk of bias, and the application of high-level statistical analysis techniques such as random-effects models, heterogeneity testing, and funnel plot analysis.

This review also has some limitations. The studies included show large heterogeneities in terms of fracture patterns, surgical methods, implants used, age groups of patients, outcome measures, and follow-up periods, making it difficult to interpret the results. The majority of studies included were observational single-arm studies, which had a high risk of bias due to confounding, lack of blinding, and incomplete follow-up. There were also variations in the reporting of outcomes, particularly complications like AVN, screw penetration, glenohumeral osteoarthritis, and stiffness, which were measured differently across the studies. Functional outcomes were also difficult to standardize because of the differences in the scoring systems used (CMS, DASH, QuickDASH, OSS, UCLA, and SSV). Additionally, there are asymmetries in the funnel plots, indicating possible publication biases. Some studies also used early-generation implants.

Future directions

Future studies should focus on conducting high-quality prospective studies and randomized controlled trials with consistent reporting of fracture patterns, surgical methods, and long-term results to address the significant heterogeneity found in this review. There is a need to establish common definitions of complications, particularly AVN, screw penetration, stiffness, and glenohumeral osteoarthritis, to allow for more accurate comparison among studies. Functional results should be assessed using standardized and validated scoring systems to improve data comparability and meta-analysis. Studies should also investigate patient-specific variables, such as age, bone quality, fracture severity, and comorbidities, to establish evidence-based selection criteria for ORIF versus other treatment options, including RTSA. Furthermore, future studies should concentrate on contemporary implant designs, biomechanical improvements, and methods that may potentially decrease complications, as many studies included in this review utilized first-generation implants. Longitudinal studies on rehabilitation methods, predictors of stiffness, and approaches to minimize secondary procedures, particularly implant removal, are also necessary. Finally, cost-effectiveness analyses and patient-reported outcomes studies are required to inform clinical and policy decisions in the management of complex proximal humerus fractures.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis suggests that ORIF with locking plates may provide generally acceptable long-term functional outcomes for proximal humerus fractures, although results appear variable across studies. While a modest functional advantage was observed in the OSS, pooled estimates for the CMS and DASH scores did not demonstrate clear differences.

Long-term complications, including avascular necrosis, screw penetration, stiffness, and glenohumeral osteoarthritis, were reported with variable frequency, and secondary procedures—particularly elective implant removal—were relatively common.

Overall, ORIF remains a widely used joint-preserving treatment option; however, outcomes likely depend on patient characteristics, fracture complexity, and surgical technique. Given the substantial heterogeneity and the very low certainty of evidence, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Further well-designed prospective studies using standardized outcome definitions are needed to better clarify the long-term effectiveness and durability of this approach.
