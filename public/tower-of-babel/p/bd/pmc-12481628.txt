Norwegian Anaesthesiologist's Perspectives on Perioperative Allergies and Penicillin Allergy Evaluations: A National Survey
Authors: Marie Bjørbak Alnæs, Anne Berit Guttormsen, Ingrid Terreehorst, Oddvar Oppegaard, Bård Reiakvam Kittang, Margrethe Aase Schaufel, Louise Savic, Torgeir Storaas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPenicillin allergy is a frequent challenge in perioperative antibiotic stewardship. Although 3%–10% of hospitalised patients declare a penicillin allergy, up to 95% of penicillin allergy labels are refuted through allergologic testing. Identifying incorrect penicillin allergy labels reduces the consumption of broad‐spectrum second‐line antibiotics, benefiting patients and society. Penicillin allergy evaluation is a recommended action for fighting antimicrobial resistance, and the procedure is under‐facilitated perioperatively. Anaesthesiologists are important stakeholders in perioperative antibiotic stewardship, but knowledge about their attitudes and potential barriers to adopting penicillin allergy evaluation is scarce.MethodsWe conducted a national, cross‐sectional online survey among 1667 members of the Norwegian Anaesthesiology Association. The study was informed by the British Drug Allergy Labels in Elective Surgical Patients anaesthesiologist survey. The survey examined knowledge about perioperative allergies and penicillin allergy evaluations. Quantitative data were analysed descriptively, and qualitative data were analysed using systematic text condensation.ResultsWe received 405 responses (24% response rate). Barriers to penicillin allergy evaluation included: (1) a perceived lack of knowledge, (2) time constraints and the absence of structured workflows, and (3) not feeling responsible for evaluating penicillin allergies. If respondents believed that the declared penicillin allergy was highly unlikely to represent a true allergy, 65% would administer penicillin. However, in patients de‐labelled by oral provocation testing with penicillin, 34% of the respondents would still not re‐expose the patient to penicillins. Senior clinicians expressed the lowest confidence in evaluating penicillin allergies, and respondents were mostly unaware of national penicillin allergy evaluation guidelines.DiscussionDistrust in penicillin allergy evaluation procedures is prevalent among Norwegian anaesthesiologists. Key barriers to the implementation of perioperative penicillin allergy evaluation include time constraints, insufficient knowledge and unclear clinical responsibilities. To improve commitment, targeted education, specialty‐specific guidelines, a simplified drug allergy warning system, and clearly defined roles are essential. We compared our results with results from the UK and found similar challenges. As our findings reflect challenges reported in other European countries, we call for a coordinated European initiative to enhance the implementation of perioperative penicillin allergy evaluations. Increasing anaesthesiologists' engagement in penicillin allergy evaluation can potentially improve perioperative outcomes and reduce antimicrobial resistance.Editorial CommentThis survey study assessed clinician opinions of penicillin allergy evaluation in one Scandinavian country. The authors present suggestions for improving the process for evaluation of suspected penicillin allergy as part of a general clinician responsibility for antibiotic treatment stewardship.

Introduction

Penicillin allergy is a major obstacle in perioperative antibiotic stewardship as it leads to increased use of broad‐spectrum alternatives and more costly treatment [1, 2, 3, 4]. Penicillin allergy is reported by 3%–10% of hospitalised patients, but up to 95% of these allergies are refuted when subjected to allergologic examination [5]. Hence, performing a structured penicillin allergy evaluation based on medical history and potentially removing incorrect allergy labels, termed penicillin allergy de‐labelling, is a recommended action for fighting antimicrobial resistance [6]. Several guidelines and tools for penicillin allergy evaluation have been published, all advocating a risk stratification‐based process, ranging from direct penicillin allergy de‐labelling or oral provocation testing in low‐risk cases to comprehensive testing, including skin testing and serum IgE measurements, for high‐risk cases [7, 8, 9].

The preoperative setting may be optimal for de‐labelling elective surgical patients, as it leads to immediate improvements in antibiotic stewardship and increased patient safety [10, 11]. Anaesthesiologists are strategically positioned to participate in perioperative penicillin allergy evaluations. The United Kingdom (UK) has guidelines for perioperative penicillin allergy evaluations, endorsed by the Royal College of Anaesthetists and the Association of Anaesthetists [12]. However, studies indicate that barriers persist, most notably anaesthesiologists' lack of confidence in allergologic testing [13, 14]. The Drug Allergy Labels in Elective Surgical patients (DALES) study made important discoveries about how anaesthesiologists in the UK manage perioperative allergies and patients with penicillin allergy labels [13].

In Norway, no comparable data exist regarding anaesthesiologists' perspectives on perioperative allergies and penicillin allergy evaluations. We have based our work on the UK DALES study and adapted it to explore Norwegian anaesthesiologists' attitudes, knowledge, and practices related to perioperative allergies and penicillin allergy evaluations, and to compare these findings with existing UK data. We aim to identify context‐specific facilitators and barriers to implementation of perioperative penicillin allergy evaluations. We hypothesise that Norwegian anaesthesiologists differ from their UK counterparts in their approach to penicillin allergy evaluations, and that these differences are shaped by national clinical, educational and systemic factors.

Methods

This anonymous, internet‐based, cross‐sectional survey was conducted using SurveyMonkey between September 2023 and 2024 [15]. The study was approved by the Regional Ethics Committee of Western Norway (REK‐West: 199210) with a waiver of written consent. The questionnaire was based on the DALES study [13] and modified to include questions about respondents' characteristics and professional experience (Appendix S1). It comprised 36 items covering demographics, general knowledge of anaesthetic allergy, penicillin allergy evaluations and responses to clinical case scenarios. All questions were aligned with Norwegian antibiotic guidelines [16]. The survey was initially developed in English by Louise Savic (L.S.), Marie Bjørbak Alnæs (M.B.A.), Ingrid Terreehorst (I.T.) and Anne Berit Guttormsen (A.B.G.), translated into Norwegian by Translated, and further refined by M.A. The Norwegian version (Appendix S2) was distributed to all 1667 members of the Norwegian Anaesthesiologists Association (NAF), representing 97% of anaesthesiologists practicing in Norway [17]. NAF members were invited to participate in the survey twice through their membership email and once through their workplace email. Additionally, flyers with a survey QR code were distributed at the annual NAF meeting. Duplicate responses were limited by device‐based submission restrictions. A minimum sample size of 313 was required to achieve a 95% confidence level with a ±5% margin of error [18].

Survey responses were analysed using SurveyMonkey and IBM‐SPSS Statistics version 29. Results were reported in accordance with the CROSS guidelines (Appendix S3) [19]. Descriptive statistics were applied to respondents' characteristics. Crosstabulation and chi‐squared tests were applied to examine categorical variables. Seven questions had the foreseen option of adding free‐text comments. These invited participants to elaborate on their rationale in decisions concerning the management of penicillin allergies. We also inquired about gaps they face in managing declared penicillin allergies and what practical support could enhance their approach.

Free‐text responses were analysed with systematic text condensation, a pragmatic, thematic analysis proceeding through the following four steps: (1) obtaining an overall impression by reading the complete material, (2) identifying units of meaning and preliminary coding, (3) condensing and abstracting meaning from the coded groups, (4) summarising and creating concepts and descriptions from the condensates [20].

Frequency tables were generated to describe the characteristics of the free‐text responses. The qualitative results are reported following the SRQR guidelines (Appendix S4) [21]. M.A. read, coded all free‐text responses, and performed the preliminary coding. M.A., Torgeir Storaas (T.S.) and Margrethe Aase Schaufel (M.A.S.) reviewed the full dataset with raw materials and refined coding. The coding log was maintained in Microsoft Word. Refined coding and results were discussed among all authors, with A.B.G. and L.S. providing anaesthetic contextual knowledge into the analysis. Inter‐coder variability and diverse opinions considering concept construction were resolved to agreement through discussion.

Results

A total of 405 out of 1667 potential participants responded to the survey, equalling a 24% response rate. Respondent descriptive characteristics are presented in Table 1 and are consistent with demographic data for Norwegian anaesthesiologists [17]. Response rates varied between 405 and 363 across survey items. Only 17% of respondents routinely discussed perioperative allergy with patients. Most anaesthesiologists demonstrated sound knowledge in distinguishing allergies from adverse reactions of another kind. However, 2% incorrectly considered anaphylaxis unlikely to be allergy related, and 18% would avoid administering propofol to patients with a soy allergy. Detailed results are presented in Table 2.

Regarding penicillin allergy, 65% would administer penicillin when allergy appeared unlikely, whereas 4% would do so even if they believed the penicillin allergy to be genuine. Overall, 34% remained hesitant to administer penicillin to patients previously labelled penicillin allergic, even after penicillin allergy de‐labelling via oral provocation testing. Among the most experienced anaesthesiologists, this hesitancy increased to 50%. Notably, 86% of those who trusted penicillin allergy de‐labelling via oral provocation testing also trusted penicillin allergy de‐labelling performed by a specialist without any testing. Only 15% of respondents reported that their hospital had penicillin allergy evaluation guidelines. When penicillin was administered uneventfully to a patient with a declared penicillin allergy, half of the respondents would update the hospital's allergy warning system, amend the anaesthetic chart, and inform the surgeon and/or patient. Only 14% would notify the patient's general practitioner (GP).

We identified three main barriers to the implementation of penicillin allergy evaluation:

Perceived Lack of Knowledge

Respondents frequently cited insufficient knowledge regarding penicillin allergy evaluation, allergic reactions and allergy testing. Although many expressed willingness to receive further training, few felt confident to manage or initiate evaluation of penicillin allergy. They recognised this knowledge gap as a significant barrier to effective antibiotic stewardship and patient safety and emphasised that education on allergies and penicillin allergy evaluation during their medical training had been insufficient. One respondent wrote:

I do not have any knowledge about allergologic testing, I do not know if it is safe, and I do not trust the result… (Respondent nr. 231)

Patient misconceptions, particularly the distinction between adverse effects and allergies, were also identified as a barrier. Improved communication strategies were highlighted as facilitators for better management of penicillin allergies.

Patients often do not understand the difference between adverse effects and allergy. I think better communication would reduce the number of false penicillin allergy labels… (Respondent nr.174)

Time Constraints and Absence of Structured Workflows

Respondents emphasised limited time to conduct penicillin allergy evaluation during routine practice and noted the absence of structured workflow for the procedure. Discussions about evaluating penicillin allergies often occur preoperatively or in the operating theatre, but in the absence of local guidelines, the task gets set aside. The multiple electronic systems that must be updated when correcting penicillin allergy labels were described as time‐consuming and burdensome.

It takes time and is not a priority. Often, we talk about it in operating theatre, but as there is no guideline towards how to do it, it is just forgotten, both the amending of charts and informing the patient. The systems we have today are a hassle, we need to update at least 4 systems if changing a drug allergy label, how are we going to have time or remember all of them… (Respondent nr. 352)

Respondents expressed openness to using guidelines and apps if they were endorsed by the government, the hospital trusts, and/or NAF, and integrated into the electronic health systems they already use. They clearly called for focused guidelines and simplified tools for updating drug allergy records and informing patients.

National guidelines that actually say what I have to do and accredited apps that easily update drug allergy warnings in real‐time and in all places at once would help me… (Respondent nr. 278)

Not Feeling Responsible for Penicillin Allergy Evaluation

In Norwegian hospitals, the surgeon mostly decides on the type of perioperative antibiotics. Several respondents stated that penicillin allergy evaluations should therefore be the responsibility of the surgeon or the patient's GP and did not feel responsible for any part of the evaluation of penicillin allergies. They stressed that anaesthesia is a treatment, not a diagnostic setting, and that anything disturbing the perioperative workflow is pushed aside.

It is too much of a hassle, this is the surgeon's or general practitioners' responsibility. Anaesthesia is treatment and not diagnostics… (Respondent nr 227)

However, not taking responsibility for evaluating penicillin allergies was recognised as a two‐way street. The respondents clearly recognised that the way they limited their role in the evaluation of potential drug allergies may lead to inadequate patient care. As one respondent so aptly stated:

Surgeons expect that the anaesthesiologists deal with the drug allergy information, and vice versa. We should agree on who in the treatment chain is actually responsible… (Respondent nr.91)

Our findings indicate distrust towards penicillin allergy evaluations. Knowledge among anaesthesiologists concerning penicillin allergy evaluations and de‐labelling seems limited. This highlights a gap between knowledge and what is expected to be performed in clinical practice. When procedures are not trusted, they are unlikely to be implemented, thereby depriving patients of the benefits of de‐labelling [22]. Our findings are consistent with the DALES results; however, unlike in the UK, where the convenience of prescribing alternative antibiotics was a key barrier for evaluating penicillin allergies, Norwegian anaesthesiologists did not cite this as a reason [13]. This may reflect the impact of Norway's mandatory antibiotic guidelines [16].

Knowledge on how to manage anaphylaxis and perioperative allergies is expected to be basic knowledge for Norwegian anaesthesiologists, and courses that provide anaphylaxis and perioperative allergy education are mandatory prior to board certification. However, our results suggest that this education is insufficient [23]. Respondents expressed a strong desire for additional training and reported feeling underprepared to evaluate penicillin allergies. The issue is twofold: Inadequate education may lead to both unnecessary penicillin avoidance and inappropriate penicillin re‐exposure, potentially endangering patients. The same results were found in the UK [13]. Notably, in our study, the most senior anaesthesiologists exhibited the lowest confidence in penicillin allergy evaluation, suggesting that outdated training without continued education contributes to the problem. Even respondents who demonstrated strong allergologic knowledge in the survey expressed uncertainty towards penicillin allergy evaluations. This finding underscores that knowledge alone is not enough to empower clinicians to perform penicillin allergy evaluations and aligns with results from other studies [14, 24].

Respondents reported ambiguity regarding responsibilities. They view penicillin allergy evaluations as the surgeon's responsibility while recognising that surgeons assume drug allergies fall under the anaesthesiologist's purview. Norwegian guidelines currently assign PAE to all physicians but do not delineate specific responsibilities, resulting in confusion and few physicians feeling accountable [25]. In contrast, UK guidelines clearly outline the anaesthetist's role in penicillin allergy evaluations [12]. Furthermore, fewer respondents in Norway than in the UK were aware that guidelines for penicillin allergy evaluation exist. Nevertheless, both British and Norwegian anaesthetists emphasised the importance of having guidelines endorsed by their hospital and colleagues. Our findings suggest that guidelines must clearly define responsibilities, outline the process, and provide instructions for updating drug allergy warnings. Equally important, these guidelines must be disseminated and easily accessible to clinicians.

Norwegian antibiotic guidelines mainly recommend first‐generation cephalosporins for surgical prophylaxis, and UK guidelines often recommend a second‐generation cephalosporin [16, 26]. These are all contraindicated in patients with a declared penicillin allergy in Scandinavia, the UK, and internationally [25, 26, 27, 28, 29]. Given the patient safety benefits of preoperative penicillin allergy evaluations, we anticipated greater willingness among anaesthesiologists to engage in the process [10, 11]. Emphasising the positive outcomes of penicillin allergy evaluation may help increase clinician participation, as a lack of belief in its benefits has been previously identified as a key barrier [14].

Working conditions appeared to hinder penicillin allergy evaluation. Respondents cited heavy workloads and time constraints as major barriers, expressing concern that penicillin allergy evaluations might disrupt surgical schedules. This was a common theme in both countries. Some also viewed anaesthesia as a treatment‐focused rather than a diagnostic discipline. This mismatch between responses and the national standards for anaesthesia and perioperative antibiotic stewardship may reflect physician fatigue due to increasing hospital workloads and/or anxiety towards performing penicillin allergy evaluation [23, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35]. The overall high anxiety reported in the survey surrounding penicillin allergy in the perioperative setting was unexpected, considering that anaesthesiologists are extensively trained in emergency medicine and routinely administer high‐risk medications [23, 36]. Anxiety was expressed differently in Norway than in the UK, but common themes were that the respondents were scared to make mistakes and scared about medicolegal consequences. Consistent with earlier studies, time pressure, insufficient knowledge, and anxiety are significant barriers to evaluating penicillin allergies [13, 14, 37, 38, 39]. These challenges cannot be addressed by individual physicians alone but must be tackled at a health system level.

Encouragingly, 65% of respondents indicated a willingness to administer penicillin when an allergy appeared unlikely, and only one respondent categorically opposed penicillin re‐administration following a negative penicillin allergy de‐labelling. This suggests progress compared to the DALES study, where only 40% felt confident. Norway's supportive medicolegal framework and growing awareness of the harms caused by incorrect penicillin allergy labels may be contributing to this change [16].

In the UK and Norway, few respondents reported informing the patients' GP about changes in their patients' penicillin allergy status, even though Norwegian respondents indicated that they see updating drug allergy warnings as being the GP's responsibility. Norwegian anaesthesiologists can communicate directly with GPs by adding the GP as a recipient to their electronic patient journal documentation, including the drug allergy warning module, but seldom do so as the surgeons oversee patient discharges. This points to a gap between perioperative and primary care. One contributing factor is the process of updating drug allergy warnings. Norway's national drug allergy warning system is linked in real time with hospital records, yet respondents still found updating records burdensome. Streamlining this workflow is essential to support broader adoption and sustainability of penicillin allergy evaluations.

When comparing results, one must remember that there are contextual differences between the countries, even when they are two Northern European high‐income countries with similar public funding and healthcare organisations. Anaesthesiologists' education and practice differ; antibiotic use and legislation differ. These factors might explain some of the differences in the results. Also, DALES was published in 2020; knowledge and practice in the UK might have changed.

Since the DALES study (2020), international support for non‐allergist led evaluation of penicillin allergies has grown, and awareness of the risk associated with false penicillin allergy labels has increased [7, 11, 12, 13, 25, 40, 41, 42]. Nevertheless, our results suggest that further efforts are needed for Norwegian anaesthesiologists to fully trust and engage in evaluating penicillin allergies.

Our study benefits from a mixed‐method design, which combines quantitative data with qualitative insights. The demographics of the respondents closely match national statistics, enhancing the generalisability of the findings. Also, as we could compare results from two countries, finding similar challenges and barriers in both places strengthens the validity of our results. However, the study has limitations. The response rate was low (24%), potentially introducing a selection bias, and response numbers declined over the course of the study, a common challenge in surveys and particularly in health care‐related research [43, 44]. It was nevertheless comparable to other anaesthesiologists' surveys [45]. To address the changing response rates throughout the survey, we provided response counts for each question (Table 2) and included raw data (Appendix S5). Another limitation is that we did not survey anaesthetic nurses, who in Norway often manage low‐risk patients and may represent an important but forgotten group in perioperative implementation of penicillin allergy evaluations [34, 46, 47].

Qualitative analysis was initially conducted by two allergologists experienced in penicillin allergy evaluation and a qualitative researcher without specific experience in evaluating penicillin allergies. This may have influenced interpretation, but anaesthesiologists were actively involved in the analysis, enriching contextual understanding and improving validity. Survey translation may introduce risks of misinterpretation, but the alignment between free‐text responses and preselected options, along with candid discussion of sensitive issues such as knowledge gaps and distrust, supports the study's internal validity. Furthermore, our findings align with UK data, suggesting relevance for further European countries.

Conclusion

We found prevalent distrust towards penicillin allergy evaluation procedures among Norwegian anaesthesiologists. Key barriers to perioperative evaluation of penicillin allergy include time constraints, insufficient training, and unclear clinical responsibilities. To improve uptake, targeted education, specialty‐specific guidelines, simplified drug allergy warning systems, and clearly defined clinical roles in the evaluation of penicillin allergies are essential. As our findings reflect challenges reported from other European countries, we call for a coordinated European initiative to enhance implementation of perioperative penicillin allergy evaluations. Increasing anaesthesiologists' engagement in penicillin allergy evaluation has the potential to improve perioperative outcomes and reduce antimicrobial resistance.
