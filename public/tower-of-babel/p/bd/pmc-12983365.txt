Parent Artery Occlusion for a Giant Serpentine Aneurysm of the Internal Carotid Artery under Electrophysiological Monitoring: A Case Report
Authors: Ryota Hagihara, Makoto Isozaki, Ayumi Akazawa, Ken Matsuda, Hidetaka Arishima, Kenichiro Kikuta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveGiant serpentine aneurysms (GSAs) are a rare subtype of giant intracranial aneurysms characterized by partial thrombosis and a tortuous serpentine vascular channel. We report a case of pediatric GSA of the right internal carotid artery (ICA) in which ischemic tolerance was assessed using balloon test occlusion (BTO) with motor-evoked potential (MEP)/somatosensory-evoked potential (SEP) monitoring under general anesthesia, followed by parent artery occlusion (PAO).Case PresentationA 9-year-old boy was referred to our hospital for decreased right visual acuity detected during a school screening. Imaging revealed a GSA of approximately 39 mm extending from the siphon to the terminus of the right ICA. Under general anesthesia, BTO was performed with concurrent MEP and SEP monitoring. A transient decrease in MEP occurred 18 min after occlusion; however, the waveform recovered immediately with +10% stimulation intensity, and no further MEP deterioration was observed during BTO (total 30 min). PAO of the right ICA was then performed using the double-catheter technique. Postoperatively, the patient developed mild transient left upper limb weakness, and MRI revealed luxury perfusion; both the conditions resolved within a few days without infarct extension. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 10 without any new neurological deficits.ConclusionThis case suggests that when awake BTO is difficult to perform in pediatric patients, BTO with electrophysiological monitoring under general anesthesia can be useful, while highlighting the limitations of MEP monitoring. As a clinical symptom–based assessment is impossible during BTO performed under general anesthesia, a multimodal assessment may further enhance safety when transient MEP deterioration occurs. At present, BTO performed under MEP monitoring should be interpreted as a supplementary assessment to be used only when other alternative methods are unavailable.

Introduction

Giant serpentine aneurysm (GSA) is a rare subtype of giant intracranial aneurysm defined by partial thrombosis and a serpentine vascular channel, accounting for <0.1% of all intracranial aneurysms. The treatment options include direct surgical clipping and resection with bypass, and more recently, endovascular parent artery occlusion (PAO) and flow diverter (FD) placement have been used. In PAO, the assessment of ischemic tolerance using balloon test occlusion (BTO) is crucial for mitigating ischemic complications. However, awake BTO is often difficult to perform in children. Herein, we report a pediatric case of a right internal carotid artery (ICA) GSA in which PAO was performed after BTO with motor-evoked potential (MEP)/somatosensory-evoked potential (SEP) monitoring under general anesthesia.

Case Presentation

A 9-year-old boy was referred to our hospital for decreased right visual acuity detected during screening. He was otherwise healthy with normal motor and cognitive development and no notable family history. Ophthalmological testing revealed a marked right visual loss (right eye: 0.15; left eye: 1.2) and a temporal visual field defect in the right eye. Head CT and MRI demonstrated marked dilation and tortuosity of the right ICA from the siphon to the terminus with calcification, along with enlargement of the carotid canal (Fig. 1A–1D). DSA revealed a maximum aneurysmal diameter of 39 mm. On the right ICA angiography, major branch vessels arising from the ICA, including the ophthalmic artery (OphA) and anterior choroidal artery (AChA), were not opacified (Fig. 1E–1H). A small saccular aneurysm was also present in the right posterior cerebral artery. No extracranial–intracranial anastomoses were identified.

Head CT (A, B) and MRI (C, D) demonstrated marked dilation with calcification from the petrous segment to the bifurcation of the right ICA. The carotid canal was also markedly enlarged (arrowhead). On the lateral view of right ICA angiography (E, F: arterial phase; G: capillary phase; H: venous phase), branches such as the ophthalmic artery and anterior choroidal artery arising from the right ICA could not be identified.

ICA, internal carotid artery

As the results of the vision test conducted 1 year earlier were normal, the symptoms were considered progressive, and surgery was indicated. As the patient could not cooperate during awake testing (DSA also required sedation), we planned to perform BTO under general anesthesia. If MEP/SEP amplitudes remained stable, PAO would be performed; if they decreased, bypass would precede PAO.

Endovascular procedure

Both femoral arteries were accessed using a 7-Fr sheath in the right femoral artery and a 4-Fr sheath in the left. After systemic heparinization, a 7-Fr Optimo EPD 90 cm (Tokai Medical Products, Aichi, Japan) was placed in the right common carotid artery (Fig. 2A). A 3.8-Fr Pinnacle Blue 27 125 cm (Tokai Medical Products) was navigated to the petrous ICA using a Synchro Select Standard Straight guidewire (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA), and BTO was performed (Fig. 2B and 2C). During left ICA angiography under BTO, the right A1 and M1 segments and the superior portion of the ICA aneurysm opacified via the anterior communicating artery (Acom). At 18 min after the occlusion, the left-hand MEP amplitude transiently decreased. However, the waveform improved immediately when the stimulation intensity was increased by 10%, and no further amplitude reduction was noted even after 10 min (Fig. 3). SEP remained stable; therefore, PAO was performed.

Initial right ICA angiography revealed a GSA with severe tortuosity (A: anteroposterior view). The balloon was inflated at the horizontal portion of the petrous ICA (arrowhead) for BTO (B, C). A working angle (D) was selected to visualize the A1 and M1 origins, allowing coil deployment within the GSA while sparing these branches (E). Subsequent left ICA angiography showed that, while the A1/M1 origins and adjacent GSA were opacified, retrograde flow into the GSA had disappeared (F). Additional coils were placed in the normal ICA proximal to the GSA (arrow) and at the cervical ICA (arrowhead) (G). Antegrade right ICA flow was completely arrested, and the ophthalmic artery (arrow), which was not opacified preoperatively, was visualized via the middle meningeal artery (arrowhead) (H: anteroposterior view; I: magnified lateral view). The right A1 and M1 were supplied via the anterior communicating artery (J).

The Optimo was repositioned distally in the cervical ICA. An Excelsior SL-10 Pre-Shaped 45° 150 cm (Stryker) and Headway 17 Pre-Shaped 45° 150 cm (Terumo, Tokyo, Japan) were advanced near the ICA bifurcation. Preserving the origins of A1 and M1 with a small safety margin, PAO was performed using a double-catheter technique with 18 coils (620 cm in total) (Fig. 2D and 2E). Left ICA angiography confirmed the loss of aneurysmal opacification up to the proximal coil mass (Fig. 2F). Tight packing of the petrous ICA (the normal segment proximal to the GSA) was then performed with 25 coils (282 cm in total), completely eliminating antegrade flow into the GSA. Finally, the cervical ICA origin was embolized using 19 coils (total, 177 cm) (Fig. 2G). On the right common carotid angiography after PAO, the OphA was faintly visualized retrogradely via the middle meningeal artery (MMA) (Fig. 2H and 2I). On the left ICA angiography, the right A1 segment, the superior portion of the aneurysm, and the right M1 segment were opacified through the Acom (Fig. 2J). MEP/SEP remained unchanged after PAO, and the procedure was completed (Fig. 3).

Postoperative course

On postoperative day 1, the patient developed mild weakness in the left upper limb. Additionally, MRI revealed a hyperintense lesion on diffusion-weighted imaging in the right frontoparietal watershed area (Fig. 4A), with a decrease in the apparent diffusion coefficient (Fig. 4B), indicating true ischemic injury rather than simple vesogenic edema. Hyperperfusion on arterial spin labeling (ASL) (Fig. 4C) was considered to indicate reactive post-ischemic hyperperfusion, commonly referred to as luxury perfusion. Aspirin 100 mg and a calcium channel blocker were temporarily administered. On postoperative day 8, the hyperperfusion on ASL improved without infarct extension (Fig. 4D), and the weakness subsided. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 10 with no new deficits. Ophthalmological examinations at 1 week and 4 months after PAO revealed no deterioration in visual acuity or visual field compared with the preoperative status.

Discussion

GSA is extremely rare, accounting for <0.1% of all intracranial aneurysms and 17.6% of giant aneurysms.1) It is typically ≥25 mm and is characterized by partial thrombosis and a serpentine channel.2,3) The pathogenesis remains unclear and the natural history unpredictable.2) The middle cerebral artery (MCA) and its branches are the most commonly affected sites (50%), followed by the ICA (13%) and anterior cerebral artery (<3%).1) Histologically, GSAs are often characterized by a 1–4-mm fibrous wall with calcified thrombus.1) Persistent inflow into a large, non-occluded aneurysm may promote flow stagnation, thrombus accumulation, enlargement, and tortuosity—potentially involving the Coandă effect.4) In a series of 39 GSAs, angiographic enlargement occurred in 12, suggesting repeated intraluminal bleeding with thrombus accumulation as a mechanism of growth.5) Rupture is relatively rare; most patients present with mass-effect symptoms such as headache, seizures, hemiparesis, cranial nerve palsies, and visual disturbance.1,6) In our patient, progressive visual impairment likely reflected gradual enlargement.

As nonoperative management may lead to ongoing enlargement and neurological decline, active intervention is recommended.3,4,7) Nevertheless, treatment—irrespective of whether endovascular or open—remains challenging and can result in neurological disorders or death.2) Traditional approaches include clipping and aneurysm resection. Recent endovascular options include PAO (using coils or liquid embolics) and FD placement, with encouraging results even in surgically difficult locations.8,9)

Surgical clipping and resection with bypass: A previous series reported a morbidity/mortality rate of approximately 30%–35% for clipping.10) Resection combined with bypass preserves the distal flow and can immediately relieve the mass effect; reconstruction with resection and bypass has been particularly useful for MCA GSAs.7,11) While aneurysm resection may be justified in cases where relief of the mass effect is expected to improve symptoms, no recent study has comprehensively evaluated the outcomes of aneurysm resection.

Parent artery occlusion: PAO is a deconstructive, minimally invasive treatment that arrests blood flow and promotes thrombosis.4) As PAO carries an ischemic risk, pre-procedural assessment of ischemic tolerance is essential.12) If no neurological deficits occur during temporary balloon occlusion in awake patients, definitive PAO can generally be performed safely. PAO is often considered the 1st-line treatment for GSA, with bypass reserved for patients without ischemic tolerance. Favorable outcomes with endovascular PAO have been described for GSAs of the anterior cerebral artery, posterior cerebral artery, vertebral artery, and ICA. Conversely, for GSAs arising from the MCA, PAO following superficial temporal artery–MCA bypass to ensure distal flow reconstruction has been effective.6) Furthermore, PAO preceded by intracranial revascularization via a radial artery graft from the internal maxillary artery has been described.13)

FD stent: FD alters the direction of blood flow while preserving the patency of the parent artery, thereby allowing the disappearance of the serpentine channel.1) By maintaining flow within the parent vessel and simultaneously restricting flow into the aneurysm, FDs promote intra-aneurysmal thrombosis and eventually lead to aneurysm occlusion. However, this process is not immediate. Moreover, long-term dual antiplatelet therapy is required.12) In a report of 13 aneurysms treated with FDs, postoperative complications occurred in 38.5% of the patients, including subarachnoid hemorrhage and transient or permanent ischemic symptoms. Furthermore, during a follow-up period of up to 5 years, the mortality rate was 33.3%, with causes of death including GSA rupture and delayed ICA occlusion.3)

In our case, FD was not selected because of the anticipated need for prolonged dual antiplatelet therapy. Aneurysm resection was not selected because retinal thinning had already occurred, making visual recovery from mass effect relief unlikely. Preoperative angiography did not reveal branches of the OphA or AChA arising from the right ICA, suggesting reduced blood supply to the typical vascular territories of these arteries. After PAO, the OphA became opacified via the right MMA, and no visual deterioration was observed. Furthermore, no cerebral infarction occurred in the AChA territory. In ICA GSAs, there have been no reports that clearly describe congenital absence of major vessels such as the OphA or AChA. However, these branches may not be visible on angiography; furthermore, a GSA may sequester contrast material, resulting in non-opacification, or may progress to functional occlusion.1)

During the GSA treatment, carefully assessing whether the distal brain parenchyma receives sufficient blood flow is essential. Accordingly, evaluating whether the parent artery can be sacrificed using DSA and BTO is pivotal in determining the treatment strategy. Since the introduction of BTO, the postoperative incidence of ischemia after ICA occlusion has decreased from 49% to 20%.14) In general, the absence of neurological symptoms during BTO, which lasts approximately 30 min, is considered indicative of adequate collateral circulation,7) although a false-negative rate as high as 22% has also been reported.15)

Electrophysiological neuromonitoring is useful for predicting the risk of ischemia because it reflects ischemia more immediately and precisely than the clinical responses. The mean time to a decrease in MEP amplitude has been reported to be 6 min during temporary ICA occlusion and 9 min during temporary MCA occlusion.16,17) Although Suzuki et al. reported durations of MEP deterioration ranging from 8 to 16 min in 4 patients with ICA aneurysms who developed postoperative hemiparesis,18) there is a lack of widely accepted cutoff.

As maintaining the patient in a still position during awake testing was difficult, owing to his age, we decided to perform BTO under general anesthesia with electrophysiological neuromonitoring. In this case, a decrease in the MEP amplitude was observed 18 min after ICA occlusion; however, the waveform obtained with a +10% stimulation intensity was comparable to that of the control. Regarding this MEP amplitude decrease, we considered the possibilities of reversible ischemia and a false-positive finding. MEPs often display substantial run-to-run variability and are also influenced by anesthetic depth and physiological factors.19) In particular, a previous study reported an intraoperative change rate in the motor threshold of MEPs of 4.4% ± 15.1% on the affected side.20) Based on the facts that the MEP waveform improved promptly within the range of stimulation intensity reported previously, recovery persisted thereafter until 30 min after BTO, the MEP decrease was limited to a single muscle, and no abnormality was observed in SEP, although borderline ischemic tolerance was possible, the findings did not indicate sustained ischemia, and therefore, we proceeded to PAO. Postoperatively, although the patient did not develop a permanent neurological sequelae, a cerebral infarction occurred in the watershed area accompanied by transient incomplete left hemiparesis. We considered it acceptable to continue BTO while adjusting the stimulation intensity within the range of ordinary threshold fluctuations reported previously; however, in light of the clinical course of this case, judging ischemic tolerance based solely on the post-adjustment MEP waveform entails a certain risk. No clear criteria have been established for a significant threshold of MEP amplitude reduction or for an acceptable increase or decrease in stimulation intensity. In the future, while examining similar cases in which MEP deterioration occurs, if the examination environment allows, it may be meaningful to consider additional perfusion assessments under BTO, such as CT perfusion and SPECT.

Conclusion

GSA is exceptionally rare and does not require an established treatment protocol. Therefore, PAO represents a key therapeutic option. Although BTO and MEP monitoring contribute to improving the safety of treatment, their limitations should be recognized. Even if MEP deterioration is transient, when borderline ischemic tolerance is suspected, additional cerebral blood flow assessment under BTO or a staged strategy such as combining bypass surgery may have to be considered. At present, BTO performed under MEP/SEP monitoring should be interpreted as a supplementary assessment to be used only when other alternative methods are unavailable.
