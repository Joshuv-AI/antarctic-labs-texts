Concomitant Transthyretin Amyloid Cardiomyopathy in Patients With Multiple Myeloma
Authors: Mohamed Ibrahim, Rashid Alhusain, J. Christopher Ray, Melissa A. Lyle, Juan C. Leoni Moreno, Nabeel Aslam, Jose N. Nativi Nicolau, Grace Lin, Taimur Sher, Awais Malik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiac amyloidosis is a restrictive cardiomyopathy caused by myocardial deposition of insoluble amyloid fibrils. The 2 most frequent types are transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM) and light-chain amyloid cardiomyopathy (AL-CM). AL-CM is a type of plasma cell dyscrasia characterized by overproduction of light chains by a plasma cell clone. Some plasma cell dyscrasias, such as monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance, are common in patients with ATTR-CM; however, the coexistence of multiple myeloma and ATTR is rarely described in the literature.Case SummaryWe describe 2 patients with confirmed plasma cell disorders: one with smoldering multiple myeloma and another with multiple myeloma receiving chemotherapy. Both exhibited cardiac imaging findings of infiltrative cardiomyopathy and were initially suspected to have AL-CM due to concomitant multiple myeloma. However, after bone marrow biopsy and fat pad aspiration did not show evidence of amyloidosis, an endomyocardial biopsy was performed that led to the diagnosis of ATTR-CM.DiscussionThese cases underscore the necessity of tissue confirmation when evaluating cardiac amyloidosis in the context of plasma cell dyscrasias. Adhering to guideline-directed diagnostic pathways is essential to distinguish ATTR-CM from AL-CM, as misclassification may result in delayed or inappropriate therapy.Take-Home MessagesATTR-CM can occur in patients with multiple myeloma. Endomyocardial biopsy is essential to accurately distinguish ATTR-CM from AL-CM in the presence of plasma cell dyscrasia and to ensure appropriate and timely treatment.

Transthyretin amyloidosis (ATTR) and light-chain amyloidosis (AL) are the 2 principal forms of cardiac amyloidosis, both with overlapping presentations and imaging findings but distinct pathophysiologies, prognoses, and treatments.1 ATTR cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM) can be hereditary due to a pathogenic mutation in the transthyretin (TTR) protein which predisposes it to amyloidogenesis or wild-type due to age-related changes in the stability of the TTR protein. On the other hand, AL-cardiomyopathy (AL-CM) is a plasma cell dyscrasia where a plasma cell clone overproduces a specific type of light chain that misfolds into amyloid fibrils.1 Both types of cardiac amyloidosis lead to progressive myocardial deposition of insoluble amyloid fibrils, ultimately resulting in restrictive cardiomyopathy.Take-Home Messages•Transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy can occur in patients with multiple myeloma.•Endomyocardial biopsy is essential to accurately distinguish transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy from light-chain amyloid cardiomyopathy in the presence of plasma cell dyscrasia and to ensure appropriate and timely treatment.

Endomyocardial biopsy is essential to accurately distinguish transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy from light-chain amyloid cardiomyopathy in the presence of plasma cell dyscrasia and to ensure appropriate and timely treatment.

Due to similar clinical and imaging presentations of both amyloid cardiomyopathies, the crucial first step in the diagnostic approach to suspected cardiac amyloidosis is to rule out plasma cell dyscrasia. Plasma cell dyscrasia can encompass a range of conditions including monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS), smoldering multiple myeloma, and multiple myeloma. Due to the high prevalence of MGUS and ATTR-CM, especially wild-type, in adults >70 years of age, it is not uncommon for these disorders to coexist.1

Although noninvasive diagnosis of ATTR-CM using cardiac amyloid radionuclide imaging (CARI) has played a pivotal role in the growth of the field, it has limited specificity for ATTR-CM in the presence of plasma cell dyscrasia. Over 20% of patients with AL-CM can have abnormal CARI, necessitating an endomyocardial biopsy with proteomic analysis to confirm the type of amyloidosis and start appropriate treatment.2

Just like MGUS can coexist with ATTR-CM due to high prevalence of both conditions in the elderly population, the presence of concomitant multiple myeloma and AL-CM is also common.3 However, concurrent presentation of ATTR-CM and multiple myeloma is rare, and only a few such reports exist in the literature.4,5

In this case series, we report 2 illustrative cases of ATTR-CM: one in the setting of smoldering multiple myeloma, and another in a patient with multiple myeloma receiving chemotherapy. These cases underscore the necessity of biopsy for definitive diagnosis in patients with multiple myeloma and suspected cardiac amyloidosis and highlight the clinical and diagnostic challenges encountered in this complex intersection of hematologic and cardiac diseases.1,6,7

Case 1

A 77-year-old female with a history of IgG kappa multiple myeloma was referred to the Cardiac Amyloid Clinic for evaluation of suspected amyloid cardiomyopathy. Her past medical history included bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome 10 years ago post release surgery, De Quervain's tenosynovitis, and coronary artery calcifications.

The patient presented with headaches, with imaging revealing calvarial and spinal lytic lesions that were noted to be hypermetabolic on positron emission tomography scan. Laboratory studies revealed an IgG kappa monoclonal spike on serum protein electrophoresis and immunofixation. A serum free light chain (FLC) assay showed a kappa FLC of 308.56 mg/L, lambda FLC of 5.31 mg/L, and a markedly abnormal kappa/lambda FLC ratio of 58.11. Bone marrow biopsy demonstrated 10% to 20% kappa-restricted plasma cells with negative Congo red staining for amyloidosis. She was diagnosed with multiple myeloma and initiated on chemotherapy with daratumumab, lenalidomide, bortezomib, and dexamethasone, later paused after 3 cycles in the setting of heart failure. The patient experienced progressive dyspnea, orthopnea, and peripheral edema with a concomitant rise in N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide from a baseline of 1,171 pg/mL to 3,868 pg/mL, and high-sensitivity troponin T of 34 ng/L (normal ≤10 ng/L). Electrocardiogram revealed a normal sinus rhythm with a left-axis deviation and delayed R wave progression. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed markedly increased concentric wall thickness measuring 18 to 19 mm, grade 2 diastolic dysfunction, and an abnormal averaged left ventricular global longitudinal strain of −15% with an apical sparing pattern (Figures 1A and 2B). The presence of multiple myeloma and imaging findings of cardiac amyloidosis led to suspicion of AL-CM. Due to a negative Congo red stain on bone marrow biopsy, a fat pad aspirate was performed. It was positive for amyloid deposits by Congo red staining, but mass spectrometry typing could not be performed due to insufficient sample size. A repeat aspirate was Congo red negative. Suspected cardiac amyloidosis with negative hematologic evaluation and remote history of bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome raised suspicion for ATTR-CM. An endomyocardial biopsy was recommended; however, the patient was hesitant to proceed. In that case, CARI with technetium-99m-labeled 3,3-diphosphono-1,2-propanodicarboxylic acid was performed to rule out amyloid cardiomyopathy, with the understanding that an abnormal scan would necessitate an endomyocardial biopsy due to its limited specificity for ATTR-CM in the presence of plasma cell dyscrasia. After CARI demonstrated a heart-to-contralateral lung ratio of 1.82 at 3 hours on planar imaging (exceeding the diagnostic threshold of 1.3 at 3 hours) along with myocardial radiotracer uptake on single-photon emission computed tomography/computed tomography images (Figures 1E and 2F), an endomyocardial biopsy was performed. It revealed amorphous, eosinophilic, glassy deposits in a chunky manner in between the myocytes as well as Congo red–positive amyloid deposits (Figure 1G). Laser microdissection with mass spectrometry confirmed ATTR-CM. TTR gene testing was negative for mutations, establishing the diagnosis of wild-type ATTR-CM. After an informed discussion about commercially available disease-modifying therapies for ATTR-CM, she opted to proceed with acoramidis. She was also prescribed empagliflozin 10 mg daily for chronic heart failure with preserved ejection fraction in the setting of amyloid cardiomyopathy. The patient had an excellent response to multiple myeloma treatment, achieving very good partial hematologic response with a reduction in the FLC ratio to 2.12 after 3 cycles of chemotherapy. Due to side effects of shortness of breath after 3 cycles and a subsequent diagnosis of COVID-19, she transitioned to daratumumab and bortezomib. She then completed 8 additional cycles, followed by maintenance therapy with daratumumab.

Case 2

A 73-year-old physically active male with a medical history of hypertension, dyslipidemia, obstructive sleep apnea, remote history of bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome, and smoldering IgG lambda multiple myeloma was referred for evaluation of suspected cardiac amyloidosis following exertional intolerance over several months. He remained highly active, participating in recreational baseball, but noticed reduced stamina while running bases. He denied orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, lower-extremity swelling, or syncope.

Four years prior, the patient was diagnosed with smoldering multiple myeloma while undergoing evaluation of an incidental monoclonal gammopathy. Bone marrow biopsy showed a 20% to 30% cellularity of a lambda-restricted plasma cell neoplasm with mildly increased overall cellularity. Congo red staining was not performed at the time. Cytogenetics revealed a mosaic karyotype and multiple chromosomal abnormalities. Initial serum protein electrophoresis and immunofixation showed an IgG lambda monoclonal protein, and serum FLC analysis revealed an elevated lambda FLC of 19.7 mg/L, kappa FLC of 1.07 mg/L, and an abnormal kappa/lambda FLC ratio of 0.05. These findings were consistent with low-intermediate-risk smoldering myeloma, for which he was monitored without treatment.

Four years later, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (Figures 2C and 3D) obtained for exertional symptoms revealed moderate basal septal hypertrophy measuring 16 mm and diffuse subendocardial and mid-myocardial late gadolinium enhancement, particularly in the basal segments, with abnormal myocardial nulling pattern and atrial enhancement, suggestive of an infiltrative cardiomyopathy such as cardiac amyloidosis. Electrocardiogram revealed sinus bradycardia with a right-axis deviation and low-voltage QRS. A transthoracic echocardiogram confirmed preserved left ventricular systolic function with an ejection fraction of 62%, with an abnormal global longitudinal strain of −16%, and an apical sparing pattern (Figure 2B). Diagnostic Pathway for Suspected Amyloid Cardiomyopathy

Once amyloid cardiomyopathy is suspected based on suggestive clinical, electrocardiographic, and/or cardiac imaging features, the first step is to rule out plasma cell dyscrasia. It includes testing for serum free light chain ratio and serum/urine immunofixation with electrophoresis. It is important to use the estimated glomerular filtration rate–adjusted cutoffs (table) for free light chain ratio in patients with kidney disease. If plasma cell dyscrasia is ruled out, then the patient has possible transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM). So, the next step is to obtain radionuclide imaging, which if positive confirms the diagnosis of ATTR-CM. At this point, it is important to test for pathogenic TTR variant and start ATTR-CM-directed therapies for example transthyretin stabilizer or silencer. In cases where plasma cell dyscrasia is uncovered or it is previously known, biopsy along with hematologic consultation is recommended. Typically, the first step is to obtain bone marrow and fat pad aspirate. When cardiac involvement is suspected, endomyocardial biopsy should be obtained. If biopsy shows amyloid deposits, it is very important to obtain mass spectrometry protein sequencing to determine the exact type of amyloidosis and start type-directed therapies. Standard heart failure therapies, as tolerated, should be initiated.

On laboratory evaluation, repeat monoclonal protein studies confirmed persistent monoclonal IgG lambda. Lambda FLC were elevated at 27.3 mg/L, and kappa FLC were at 1.13 mg/L (kappa/lambda FLC ratio: 0.04). High-sensitivity troponin T was elevated at 27 ng/L, and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide was elevated at 589 pg/mL. Fat pad aspirate was Congo red negative. An endomyocardial biopsy was Congo red positive, and mass spectrometry confirmed TTR amyloid deposition. TTR gene analysis did not reveal any pathogenic mutations. Taken together, these findings confirmed wild-type ATTR-CM in a patient with concurrent smoldering multiple myeloma. The patient was subsequently counseled regarding disease-specific treatment options for wild-type ATTR-CM and opted to proceed with vutrisiran.

Discussion

This case series reports 2 cases of wild-type ATTR-CM, with one patient undergoing treatment for multiple myeloma, and the other being followed for smoldering myeloma. It highlights several important teaching points, including the following. 1) ATTR-CM can present with concomitant multiple myeloma and should be on the list of differential diagnoses along with AL-CM when amyloid cardiomyopathy is suspected. 2) Endomyocardial biopsy is required to make the diagnosis of ATTR-CM for cases of suspected cardiac amyloidosis with plasma cell dyscrasia. 3) Diagnosis of ATTR-CM in multiple myeloma patients is crucial to ensure appropriate treatment targeting ATTR.

There are several clues that raised suspicion for ATTR-CM in these cases. The first indication was the remote history of bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome. Although bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome is recognized as a manifestation in both AL and ATTR, it is much more prevalent in ATTR patients.8 Furthermore, according to Milandri et al,8 the prevalence of carpal tunnel syndrome in AL patients is similar to that in the general population. Second, AL is a more aggressive process than the more indolent presentation of ATTR. Therefore, the remote history of bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome was more suggestive of ATTR, as carpal tunnel syndrome often predates the diagnosis of ATTR-CM by more than 5 years.8

Left ventricular wall thickness is also an important finding which can be potentially useful in differentiation of AL-CM and ATTR-CM. While ATTR-CM is a purely infiltrative cardiomyopathy, light chains in AL-CM not only infiltrate the myocardial wall but also exert cardiotoxic effects. Due to these important pathophysiologic differences, wall thickness is sometimes only mildly increased or normal in patients with AL-CM as reported by Nagy et al.9 In our case series, both patients had markedly increased left ventricular wall thickness, which along with remote history of bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome and negative hematologic evaluation for amyloidosis was slightly more suspicious for ATTR-CM over AL-CM.

While left ventricular wall thickness and a remote history of bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome can help distinguish between AL and ATTR, an endomyocardial biopsy remains necessary when cardiac amyloidosis is suspected and concomitant plasma cell dyscrasia is present. This is because CARI can lead to false-positive results in the presence of AL-CM. Therefore, while negative CARI can rule out ATTR-CM in the presence of plasma cell dyscrasia, a positive scan is not able to distinguish between AL-CM and ATTR-CM and necessitates an endomyocardial biopsy for definitive diagnosis.

Early recognition and treatment are critical, as disease-modifying therapies can reduce mortality and hospitalizations and are most effective when initiated in earlier stages of the disease.10 These cases underscore the importance of a multidisciplinary approach, integrating hematology and cardiology expertise, to ensure accurate diagnosis and optimal management in this complex patient population.

Conclusions

This case series reports 2 rare cases of wild-type ATTR-CM, with coexisting multiple myeloma in one case and smoldering myeloma in the other case. It highlights the potential differences in clinical and imaging presentation of AL vs ATTR and underscores the importance of endomyocardial biopsy with proteomic analysis in confirming the diagnosis. In addition, a multidisciplinary approach that incorporates hematologic and cardiac expertise with cardiac imaging and histological analysis is essential to confirm accurate diagnosis and ensure appropriate treatment.
