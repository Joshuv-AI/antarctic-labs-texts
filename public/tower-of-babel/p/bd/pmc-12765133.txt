The seasonality of respiratory syncytial virus and its associations with meteorological factors in Hong Kong, 2014–2023
Authors: Qian Xiong, Zhuyao Rong, Jinjun Ran, Xiang Ren, Li Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRespiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a common respiratory virus that causes respiratory diseases in all ages and causes severe diseases mainly among children and older adults. Understanding the seasonal pattern of RSV and its influencing factors is crucial for policy makers to optimize the timing of RSV immunization strategies. We explored the RSV seasonality and its associations with meteorological factors in Hong Kong from 2014 to 2023.MethodsWe collected weekly RSV surveillance data in Hong Kong between 2014 and 2023 from Centre for Health Protection (CHP) of Hong Kong. Three different approaches were applied to explore the RSV seasonality including the 50th percentile method, the average annual percentage (AAP) method, and the 2 % threshold method. A generalized additive model was used to explore the associations between RSV positive rate and meteorological factors.ResultsDuring 10-year study period, 44,722 specimens were positive for RSV with a positive rate of 2.81 % in Hong Kong. The RSV epidemic varied year-on-year, with annual and biannual epidemics observed. The three approaches identified similar RSV epidemic in Hong Kong, a total of 9 RSV epidemics were found. The RSV epidemics mainly occurred from mid-February to late September, with an average duration of approximately 6 months during the study years. We found that mean temperature was positively associated with RSV positive rate, while mean dew point temperature showed a negative association.ConclusionsRSV epidemic varied year-on-year in Hong Kong, the 2 % threshold is recommended as an alert threshold for RSV epidemics. A One Health perspective might enhance future prevention strategies by incorporating broader environmental and intersectoral factors.

Introducion

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is one of the main causes of acute lower respiratory infection (ALRI), and ALRI is one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in young children aged <5 years, especially in infants aged under 1 year [1,2]. Shi et al. estimated that there were 3.2 million RSV-associated hospitalizations in children aged <5 years, especially among infants aged less than 6 months [3]. And RSV can cause a substantial disease burden in adults, especially in older adults aged ≥65 years [4]. There were 336,000 RSV-associated hospitalizations in older adults globally [1]. Early-life infection with RSV has been well documented to increase the risk of recurrent wheezing and asthma [5], and reinfection with RSV can occur throughout life.

RSV epidemics show varied seasonal patterns in different areas and different years around the world. In temperate areas, RSV usually has an annual epidemic and peaks during winter months, while for subtropical and tropical areas, RSV seasonality varied from annual epidemic and semi-annual epidemic to circulating all year round [6]. The RSV seasonality could vary by year, timely surveillance would be necessary for guiding healthcare and preventing strategies and policies. Meteorological factors might affect the RSV seasonality, such as temperature, humidity, rainfall, etc. Several studies reported that in temperate and subtropical areas, higher RSV activity is correlated with lower temperature and higher relative humidity [7]. However, the relationship between RSV activity and meteorological factors, such as humidity and temperature, varies considerably in subtropical and tropical regions [8]. Although a few studies have explored the associations, the available results remain inconsistent [[9], [10], [11]]. And the pandemic of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) which was caused by the novel severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) also could lead to a decline in most seasonal respiratory viruses, including RSV [12].

The One Health framework is a collaborative, multisectoral, and transdisciplinary approach that recognizes the interdependence of human, animal, and environmental health [13]. It has become increasingly important in understanding and managing infectious diseases, particularly those influenced by ecological changes and cross-species interactions [14]. While RSV is primarily a human pathogen, emerging evidence suggests the existence of animal reservoirs or parallels in other species. In addition, environmental changes (e.g., driven by urbanization, land use modification, and ecosystem disruption) might influence the transmission and seasonality of respiratory pathogens, including RSV [15]. Therefore, investigating how meteorological factors influence RSV seasonality is an essential step toward identifying ecological drivers and informing more integrated prevention strategies.

Hong Kong is a subtropical area and its RSV surveillance has been conducted for several years. RSV caused diseases burden in Hong Kong, particularly among infants aged <6 months and old adults aged ≥65 years [16]. The seasonality of RSV varied by geographical locations and latitude [9], especially in subtropical and tropical areas. However, the seasonality of RSV and relationships between RSV activity and meteorological factors remain unclear in Hong Kong. Additionally, RSV antibody products targeting young children (Nirsevimab and Palivizumab) [17], pregnant women between 32 and 36 weeks of pregnancy [18], and older adults aged more than 60 years are now available [19]. Therefore, understanding RSV seasonality is crucial for timely and effective implementation of the immunization program. This study aims to provide an overview of RSV seasonality in Hong Kong using surveillance data from 2014 to 2023; and to explore the associations between RSV positive rate and meteorological factors.

Methods

Data sources

Weekly data on RSV detections from 2014 through 2023 were derived from Centre for Health Protection (CHP) of Hong Kong, including total number of test specimen and the number of specimens testing positive for RSV. The information is based on RSV test from clinical specimens, which was received from hospitals and outpatient clinics for sentinel surveillance routinely by the Public Health Laboratory Services of Department of Health of Hong Kong during the designated week. In this study, two RSV proxies were used to represent RSV activity in the population for exploring RSV seasonality: i) the weekly number of specimens testing positive for RSV (here we used “RSVNumber” to indicate the weekly number of specimens testing positive for RSV for simplicity; 4-week moving average method was used to mitigate the variation of RSVnumber; Supplementary Text I and Supplementary Figs. S1-S3); ii) weekly RSV positive rate (i.e. ratio between the number of specimens testing positive for RSV and the total number of test specimens, “RSVrate” was used to indicate the ratio) (Fig. 1) [20]. Identification of RSV epidemics

The data from 2020 to 2022 were excluded to avoid the influence of COVID-19 pandemic [21]. We used three approaches to the RSV proxies mentioned above to identify the RSV epidemics.i)The 50th percentile methodThe 50th percentile of all the non-zero 4-week moving average of RSVnumber during the study period was defined as the epidemic threshold. The onset and end of RSV epidemic were defined as the first and the last 2 consecutive weeks when the RSV proxy exceeded the defined epidemic, respectively. RSV epidemics were defined as periods of at least 4 or more consecutive weeks during which exceeded the epidemic threshold, with a maximum of 2-week interruptions with the RSV proxy fell below the defined epidemic threshold.ii)The annual average percentage (AAP) methodThe AAP method was used to obtain the RSV epidemic here [22], which was calculated by the following formula:AAPi=ni∑1xni×100where i denotes week number, n denotes the number of RSV detections, x is the total number of weeks in a epidemic, firstly, 4-week moving average of RSVnumber were sorted in descending order, the epidemic weeks were defined as the weeks accounting for the sum of 75 % of all RSV detections in the defined epidemic, such, the onset and offset the epidemic was defined as the first and last weeks of longest consecutive epidemic weeks with the maximum of 2-week interruptions in an epidemic.iii)The 2 % threshold methodThe epidemic threshold was defined as the 2 % of RSVrate [23]. The onset and end of RSV epidemic was defined as the first and last two consecutive weeks when RSVrate exceeded 2 % with the maximum of 2-week gaps in an epidemic.

The 2 % threshold method

The epidemic threshold was defined as the 2 % of RSVrate [23]. The onset and end of RSV epidemic was defined as the first and last two consecutive weeks when RSVrate exceeded 2 % with the maximum of 2-week gaps in an epidemic.

For each epidemic, we determined four characters, including: i) the onset and ending week of the RSV epidemic; ii) the peak week of the epidemic (the week with the highest RSV proxy in the epidemic); iii) the duration of RSV epidemic, which was defined as the number of weeks from the onset week to the ending week; iv) the captured percentage of RSVnumber in a year.

For each approach, we present the results of the most suitable proxy and approach combination in the main analysis based on the following criteria: i) the average captured percentage of RSVnumber during the defined epidemics exceeded 70 %; ii) we selected the methods with the lowest standard deviations for both the duration of RSV epidemic and the captured percentage of RSVnumber.

Associations between meteorological factors and RSV

We explored the associations between RSVrate and the meteorological factors (i.e. mean ambient temperature, mean dew point temperature, relative humidity, mean pressure, rainfall volume, and mean amount of cloud) by using generalized additive models (GAM), assuming an additive relationship between RSV positive rate and meteorological factors (Supplementary Text II). The COVID-19 pandemic period, from 30 January 2020 to 22 December 2022, was excluded from the main analysis due to substantial changes in public health interventions and viral transmission dynamics during this period and the inclusion of this period would lead to relatively worse model fit (Supplementary Text III and Supplementary Figs. S4-S5). To account for potential shifts in population susceptibility or immunity before and after the COVID-19 pandemic, we included an indicator variable of pre-pandemic period. Including pre-pandemic variable allowed indirect adjustment for changes in population immunity that might influence RSV dynamics.

We used different time lags for the associations between meteorological factors and RSV activity (i.e., 0-week, 1-week, 2-week, and 3-week lags) and compared model performance using the Akaike information criterion (AIC) and R-squared statistics. The optimal lag time was selected based on these goodness-of-fit measures (Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Fig. S6).

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted sensitivity analyses to examine the robustness of the identified RSV epidemics in Hong Kong by using different approaches to different RSV proxies: i) alternative thresholds of 2.5 %, 3 %, and 7 % instead of 2 % were used; ii) the 50th percentile method and AAP method were applied to identify the RSV epidemic based on all the non-zero RSVnumber during the study period [24]; iii) the 50th percentile method and AAP method were applied to identify the RSV epidemic based on all the non-zero weekly RSV+ counts (weekly RSV+ counts were estimated using a proxy method; details of the calculation are provided in the Supplementary Text I) during the study period.

All statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

During 10-year study period from 2014 to 2023, 44,722 specimens were tested positive, with the proportion of test positive for RSV being 2.81 %. The pattern of RSV detection rates varied over time, annual and biannual RSV epidemics were found in Hong Kong (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Figs. S1-S3). The peak of RSV positive rate increased from 7.29 % in 2014 to 8.34 % in 2023 (Supplementary Fig. S4). Annual laboratory-confirmed RSV positive rate increased from 3.13 % in 2014 to 3.52 % in 2023. The RSV positive rate was stable with a median of 3.13 % (range: 2.11 %, 3.52 %) excluding the COVID-19 pandemic period (Supplementary Fig. S7). However, the RSV positive rate stayed at a relatively lower level among people aged <18 years during the COVID-19 pandemic, with a median of 1.54 % (Range: 0.67 %, 2.00 %). We found that the RSV positive rate experienced a substantial increase following the COVID-19 pandemic, the RSV positive rate in 2023 also increased substantially by around 67.10 % compared to 2019 (i.e. before the COVID-19 pandemic), with the RSV positive rate of 3.52 % in 2023 and 2.11 % in 2019, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S7).

According to the 50th percentile method, we found annual RSV epidemics in 2014, 2015, 2016, 2018, and 2023, while biannual epidemics occurred in 2017 and 2019. The start of the RSV epidemic varied from early January to late May, while the end of the epidemic varied from early February to late October (Table 1 and Fig. 2). The peak week was identified in early April, and the epidemic lasted approximately 26 weeks (i.e. around 6 months), capturing an average of 71.95 % of the RSV cases. The AAP method found a similar RSV epidemic to the 50th percentile method, identifying comparable onset and ending time. The length of epidemic was identified as 28 weeks (i.e. around 6 months) with the AAP method, capturing around 76.21 % of RSV cases during defined epidemic (Table 1 and Fig. 2). The peak week was also in April. The 2 % threshold method identified a much longer epidemic (i.e. 33 weeks, around 7.5 months) with the same annual and biannual RSV epidemics as the other two methods. The 2 % threshold method identified the RSV epidemic started from mid-January to mid-October, capturing around 81.25 % of RSV cases during the defined epidemic (Table 1 and Fig. 2). Applying the 2.5 %, 3 %, and 7 % thresholds yielded RSV epidemic periods capturing 74.08 %, 62.71 %, and 36.92 % of total RSV detections, respectively (Supplementary Table S2). We compared these methods by evaluating the consistency of the RSV epidemic characteristics across the study years, including the duration of epidemic and the captured percentage of annual RSV detections (Table 1). The epidemics identified by AAP method were closely aligned with the 50th percentile method, showing an RSV epidemic started from mid-February to late September (Table 1 and Fig. 2). The 2 % threshold method identified a longer RSV epidemic of about 7.5 months compared to 6 months identified by the other two methods (i.e. about 6 months). The AAP method captured the most stable RSV epidemic, lasting for around 6 months with a standard deviation of 5.32 weeks. Additionally, the AAP method had the most stable capture percentage of RSV detections in different study years, capturing an annual average of 76.21 % of RSV cases with a standard deviation of 3.30 %, compared to the other two methods.

Overall, the start, the end, the peak, and the duration of an epidemic varied by year in Hong Kong. In addition, both annual epidemics and biannual epidemics were identified in different years. Generally, the onset and offset weeks for RSV epidemic varied by year with a wide range. The median duration of RSV epidemic was found to be around 6 months per year.

To assess the possible driving factors on RSV epidemics, we used the GAM with a 3-week lag between meteorological factors and RSV activity, selected based on model fit criteria (Supplementary Text IV and Supplementary Table S1). Given the substantial impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on healthcare systems, public behavior, and disease transmission dynamics, we excluded the data of the COVID-19 pandemic period (Supplementary Figs. S4-S5) and incorporated the pre-pandemic variable to account for changes in population immunity between the pre- and post-pandemic periods in our primary model. We found that RSV activity in large increased with mean temperature, while mean dew point temperature was in large negatively associated with RSV activity (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table S3). It seemed that the associations of RSV activity with other meteorological factors were statistically non-significant. Discussion

We reported an overview of RSV seasonality in Hong Kong from 2014 to 2023 using three different approaches. The RSV epidemic was summarized as occurring annually from mid-February to late September during study years. We identified a total of 9 epidemics during the study period. The mean duration of the RSV epidemic was approximately 6 months. We found that the AAP method might be a suitable approach to identify the RSV epidemic in Hong Kong with a stable capture percentage of 76.21 % ± 3.30 %. All three methods identified similar RSV epidemic in Hong Kong, each capturing more than 70 % of annual RSV cases within approximately 6 months.

The AAP method was recognized as the most stable method, but it was only applicable post-epidemic and could not be utilized for real-time surveillance. While the AAP method was useful for summarizing the RSV epidemic retrospectively, which could provide valuable insights into RSV dynamics and impacts. The 2 % threshold method has been shown to identify a longer RSV epidemic, characterized by both an earlier onset and a later ending time of the RSV epidemics compared to the other two methods accessed. Alternative thresholds (i.e. 2.5 %, 3 %, and 7 %) were examined in sensitivity analyses. We found that while all tested thresholds yielded consistent seasonal patterns and peak timing, the 2 % threshold provided an appropriate balance, capturing a highest proportion of annual RSV cases while enabling timely detection of epidemic onset. Thus, the 2 % threshold could serve as a real-time alert level in future, as it allowed for the timely detection of the onset of the RSV epidemic. Such early identification was crucial for the strategic implementation of prevention measures (e.g. monoclonal antibody, maternal vaccine etc.) among high-risk groups. Future research could focus on further validating the efficacy of the 2 % threshold method in Hong Kong and exploring its potential integration with other real-time surveillance tools.

The dynamics of RSV epidemic varied by year within the same area, RSV could be annual or biannual epidemics in Hong Kong, which is a subtropical area. Unlike the temperate regions, where the onset and ending of the RSV epidemic are typically consistent, such as, the epidemic onset in mid-October and lasting for 31–33 weeks in Beijing and the United States [25], respectively. Additionally, the annual average duration of RSV epidemic in Hong Kong was approximately 6 months, with a range extending from 5 to 8 months. This duration was slightly longer compared to temperate regions, where RSV epidemic typically spans 5–6 months [25].

The COVID-19 pandemic had significantly alerted the circulation patterns of many respiratory viruses worldwide, including RSV [12]. We found that RSV positive rate stayed at a low level among people aged <18 years during the COVID-19 pandemic in Hong Kong (Supplementary Fig. S7). This trend was consistent with the other countries, including the United States [26], Canada [27], Australia [28], England [29], Europe [12], etc. Such uniform reductions in RSV transmission during the COVID-19 pandemic could be attributed to: i) widespread implementation of non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) [21], such as social distancing, wearing masks [30], enhanced hygiene practices etc.; ii) “zero-COVID” policy in Hong Kong during the pandemic period, including travel restrictions, stay at home orders, lockdown, school closures etc., those reduced not only the transmission of SARS-CoV-2, but also impacted the transmission dynamics of other respiratory pathogens, including RSV [31]. Furthermore, RSV positive rate experienced a substantial increase following COVID-19 pandemic (Supplementary Fig. S7), characterized by a relative increase in the number of RSV detections and positive rate. This phenomenon could be partly explained by the concept of “immunity debt” [31], which means COVID-19 pandemic could cause the lack of immune stimulation for RSV, because of the reduced circulation or vaccine uptake during the pandemic periods. Consequently, as these individuals lacked prior immune exposure to RSV, the population-level herd immunity had declined, setting the stage for more severe epidemics than those observed in the pre-pandemic year [32]. Another contributing factor could be the possible evolutions of RSV virus following the pandemic [33]. Additionally, immune dysregulations following SARS-CoV-2 infection might play a role [34].

RSV seasonality varied by year and by location, which largely depended on the geographical locations, meteorological factors, and other possible factors. It was important to explore RSV seasonality in different areas for future prevention strategies implementation. The positive association between RSV activity and mean temperature in Hong Kong likely reflected a combination of environmental, behavioural, and biological mechanisms. Warm conditions could enhance RSV stability in the environment, increasing its survival in aerosols and on surfaces [11]. In addition, elevated temperature might impair mucociliary clearance in the upper respiratory tract, weakening host defenses and increasing susceptibility [35]. The findings were aligned with previous research conducted in Hong Kong [6]. The summer conditions in Hong Kong (i.e. characterized by high temperature, high humidity, and frequent rainfall) might tend to drive people indoors in more crowded conditions, indirectly increasing the risk of RSV transmission through altered social behaviours [36]. A positive association between temperature and RSV activity was also found in other regions, such as Philippines, Baguio City, Malaysia [10], and Singapore [37], underscoring the need to consider local climate conditions when designing RSV surveillance and prevention strategies. Notably, dew point temperature was negatively associated with RSV activity, but the association varied across different areas, with some regions showing positive associations while others reporting negative associations [38]. Such variability was likely due to differences in local climate, population density, and behavioural patterns [39]. It was important to note that limited studies had investigated these associations, highlighting the need for further research.

Furthermore, climate change is increasingly recognized as a driver of shifts in infectious disease patterns, including respiratory viruses such as RSV [40]. Changes in global temperatures might affect RSV seasonality, geographic spread, and transmission efficiency [41]. Moreover, ecosystem disruptions and human encroachment on wildlife habitats might increase the risk of novel respiratory viruses emerging from animal reservoirs, as seen with other zoonotic pathogens [42]. In addition to meteorological factors, variables such as air quality, urban density, and ecological changes might influence pathogen emergence, amplification, and transmission. For example, dense housing and limited ventilation in urban areas might increase close-contact transmission of RSV [43]. Furthermore, exposure to air pollutants was associated with heightened vulnerability to viral infections [44]. Integrating additional ecological data (e.g. air quality indices, land-use patterns, and biodiversity metrics) in future research could offer a more comprehensive understanding of RSV seasonality and support more targeted public health interventions within a One Health framework (Fig. 4). Additionally, human–animal interfaces, including agricultural practices, animal husbandry, and wildlife trade, may contribute to the emergence or evolution of respiratory viruses, even those like RSV currently considered to have low zoonotic potential. These interactions could facilitate viral adaptation and cross-species transmission [45]. In line with the One Health approach, which emphasizes the interconnectedness of human, animal, and environmental health, future RSV surveillance and research would benefit from integrating data across these domains (Fig. 4). We found that RSV activity was higher in the post-pandemic period compared to the pre-pandemic period, with notable shifts in intensity of epidemics (Supplementary Fig. S7). These changes were likely driven by a complex interplay of factors, including reduced or disrupted RSV circulation resulting from stringent public health interventions implemented during the COVID-19 pandemic [46], altered population-level immunity due to decreased exposure (i.e., “immunity gap”) [[47], [48], [49], [50]], and changes in behavior such as mask-wearing, physical distancing, and school closures [51,52]. The resurgence of RSV activity following the relaxation of NPIs might reflect a temporary increase in susceptibility, particularly among young children who missed early-life exposure [50]. Such variability underscored the importance of flexible and real-time surveillance systems capable of detecting unexpected shifts in RSV activity.

Our study had several limitations. First, the data on laboratory-confirmed RSV infection were derived from CHP, the detailed characteristics (e.g. the demographic and basic information of the specimen providers, including age, sex, lifestyles, and immune status) and specific specimen information was not available for further analysis. In addition, the pattern of RSV detections number / RSV positive rate might not accurately represent the true trends of RSV activity, as these detections could include co-infections with other viruses rather than being solely RSV-associated infections. Furthermore, our study was limited by the absence of animal and ecosystem health data. Future research should aim to incorporate environmental and veterinary surveillance data, which could enhance the applicability of findings within a One Health framework. Additionally, improving the age diversity of research participants and refining methodologies for respiratory sample collection and RSV detection were recommended to ensure more accurate, representative, and robust epidemiological insights.

In conclusion, our study revealed notable year-to-year variation in RSV epidemics in Hong Kong, identifying 9 distinct outbreaks typically beginning in mid-February and ending by late September. A 2 % threshold may serve as an effective alert level for early detection. Mean temperature was positively associated with RSV activity, while mean dew point was negatively association with RSV activity. These findings underscore the importance of ongoing surveillance to inform timely public health responses, particularly the optimal timing of immunization programs for high-risk populations. In addition, the dynamic influence of multiple meteorological factors should be considered to optimize timing for vaccination campaigns, public advisories, and resource allocation. From a One Health perspective, the influence of environmental conditions on RSV dynamics highlights the need for integrated approaches. We recommend strengthening intersectoral collaboration among public health, veterinary, and environmental agencies to enhance surveillance systems and better prepare for future respiratory virus threats.
