Effect of stellate ganglion block on perioperative psychological symptoms and recovery after total hip arthroplasty for osteonecrosis of the femoral head in non-elderly patients: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Xiaomin Fan, Ziqi Huang, Xia Li, Jing Li, Baojia Lin, Xinjian Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe incidence of osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) in non-elderly patients is increasing. Psychological distress, including anxiety and depression related to both the disease itself and total hip arthroplasty (THA), may adversely affect postoperative outcomes. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of stellate ganglion block (SGB) on perioperative psychological symptoms and postoperative recovery in patients undergoing THA for ONFH.MethodsA total of 94 patients undergoing THA were enrolled in this study and randomly divided into the stellate ganglion block group (SGB group) and the control group (CON group). Patients in the SGB group received ultrasound-guided unilateral stellate ganglion block before surgery with an injection of 4 mL of 0.8% lidocaine; patients in the CON group underwent the same procedure, with an equal volume of normal saline replacing lidocaine. On postoperative days 1 and 4, the degree of anxiety and depression, pain intensity, sleep quality, recovery status, and occurrence of complications were evaluated in all patients.ResultsCompared with the CON group, patients receiving SGB treatment showed favorable trends in GAD-7 scores and improved postoperative recovery quality, as reflected by the primary mixed-effects analysis (P < 0.05) and significantly higher QoR-15 scores. However, additional ANCOVA adjusting for baseline GAD-7 scores did not confirm a significant between-group difference in GAD-7 outcomes. The observed GAD-7 effect was therefore not robust to baseline adjustment. Although HADS scores tended to be lower in the SGB group, the difference did not reach statistical significance. SGB did not significantly affect pain intensity (VAS score) or the incidence of adverse events, including hypotension, dizziness, nausea and vomiting, and nightmares (all P > 0.05).ConclusionAmong non-elderly patients undergoing THA, SGB was associated with some favorable trends in perioperative psychological symptoms and postoperative recovery quality. However, the evidence was inconsistent across different psychological assessment scales and sensitive to baseline adjustment. Therefore, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive.Trial registrationThis study was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry. (ChiCTR2400089542) on 2024/09/10.

Background

The prevalence of anxiety and depressive symptoms in the general population is estimated to be approximately 4%-7% and 3%-6% [1, 2], respectively. However, substantially higher rates have been reported among patients undergoing total hip arthroplasty (THA), with postoperative anxiety and depression affecting up to 20.4% and 21.8% of Chinese patients [3], respectively. Osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH), a common indication for THA in non-elderly patients, is characterized by progressive structural destruction and collapse of the femoral head resulting from ischemia. The disease is frequently accompanied by chronic hip or groin pain, impaired mobility, and even loss of working capacity. Beyond its physical consequences, ONFH can impose a considerable psychological burden on affected individuals [4, 5]. This burden is particularly pronounced among young and middle-aged patients, who often face concerns regarding disease progression, functional limitations, employment, and changes in family and social roles. Consequently, these patients may be more vulnerable to anxiety, depression, and other psychological disturbances, which can further compromise postoperative recovery and quality of life and impose additional burdens on both families and society [6–8].

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) represents the most effective treatment for end-stage ONFH, with surgical techniques now well-established. Current research priorities have shifted toward promoting rapid recovery in these patients. Studies have reported that the incidence of perioperative anxiety and depression exceeds 50% among THA patients [9, 10], which not only directly affects treatment compliance but also significantly impacts anesthesia, postoperative pain, complications, and long-term rehabilitation [9, 11–13]. The combination of these dual factors renders non-elderly patients the most susceptible group to anxiety and depression in this surgical population, warranting serious attention from researchers. Implementing appropriate interventions to improve anxiety and depression in this patient population helps achieve favorable therapeutic outcomes, fulfills core requirements for rapid recovery, facilitates patients’ return to normal social functioning, and reduces healthcare resource consumption.

Stellate ganglion block (SGB) modulates sympathetic tone, improves cerebral blood flow perfusion, and regulates neuroimmunity by blocking the cervical sympathetic ganglia, demonstrating potential clinical value in alleviating anxiety and depression [14–17]. For instance, studies have shown that SGB can effectively reduce symptoms of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and depression by restoring autonomic nervous system balance and enhancing regional cerebral blood flow. Its clinical utility in alleviating perioperative anxiety and depression among non-elderly patients undergoing total hip arthroplasty (THA) is therefore promising. Accordingly, this study implemented preoperative SGB in THA patients and comparatively analyzed its effects on postoperative anxiety and depression, pain severity, sleep quality, recovery status, and related complications in this demographic, thereby evaluating its application value within the enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) pathway for this patient population.

Methods

This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Consolidated. Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) 2010 guidelines.

Participants

Participant recruitment commenced in July 2024 and was completed in July 2025.

Inclusion criteria: ①Age 18–65 years, because osteonecrosis of the femoral head commonly affects relatively younger adult populations, patients older than 65 years were excluded to minimize the potential influence of advanced age-related comorbidities, cognitive decline, and altered perioperative psychological responses on study outcomes; ②Diagnosed with osteonecrosis of the femoral head and scheduled for elective total hip arthroplasty; ③Clear consciousness, no psychiatric disorders, and able to complete scale assessments independently and accurately; ④ASA physical status I–III; ⑤ Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) [18] score > 7 or a Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) [19] score > 4. These thresholds were selected to identify patients with at least mild psychological distress who might be susceptible to perioperative anxiety and depressive symptoms. Exclusion criteria: ①Severe language or hearing impairment, intellectual disability, difficulty in follow-up, or poor compliance; ②Known allergy to lidocaine; ③Significant cardiovascular disease, including uncontrolled hypertension, aneurysm, severe arrhythmia, or heart failure; ④Severe respiratory conditions, such as acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, pharyngeal or laryngeal mass, or severe respiratory tract infection; ⑤Long-term use of sedatives, anxiolytics, antidepressants, or antipsychotic medications that could interfere with perioperative psychological evaluation; ⑥Contraindications to stellate ganglion block, including coagulation disorders, local skin infection, glaucoma, or thyroid enlargement.

Randomisation and intervention

All patients were screened 1–2 days before surgery. Eligible patients provided written informed consent after being fully informed of the study objectives, procedures, and potential risks. This study was designed as a randomized, single-blind controlled trial. Patients were randomly assigned to the stellate ganglion block group (SGB group) or the control group (CON group) using a random number table. Preoperative anxiety and depression levels were assessed using the GAD-7 and HADS.

On the day of surgery, patients in the SGB group received ultrasound-guided unilateral stellate ganglion block in the pre-anesthesia room by an experienced anesthesiologist, with 4 ml of 0.8% lidocaine injected; the CON group received an equivalent volume of normal saline. After excluding contraindications to neuraxial anesthesia, all patients underwent combined spinal-epidural anesthesia with invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring.

Upon entering the operating room, all patients received intravenous midazolam 0.05 mg/kg for sedation. Intraoperative adjunctive medications, vasoactive agents, or antiemetics were administered at the discretion of the anesthesiologist according to clinical condition. Perioperative vital signs were closely monitored, with mean arterial pressure (MAP) maintained at ≥ 65 mmHg or within 25% of baseline. Intraoperative systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), MAP, heart rate (HR), and oxygen saturation (SpO2) were continuously recorded. Fluid management was individually adjusted based on hemodynamic status.

Postoperatively, all patients received patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) with a regimen of sufentanil 2 µg/kg, flurbiprofen axetil 3 mg/kg, and tropisetron 8 mg diluted to 100 ml, at a background infusion rate of 2 ml/h. Intramuscular tramadol was administered as rescue analgesia within 48 h after surgery according to patient request, with additional injections given if pain control remained inadequate. Perioperative adverse events were prospectively monitored and systematically recorded by investigators blinded to group allocation. Recorded adverse events included hypotension, dizziness, nausea and vomiting as well as nightmares. Adverse events were assessed intraoperatively and throughout the postoperative observation period until hospital discharge. All adverse events were managed according to standard clinical protocols and resolved with routine clinical management. No severe procedure-related complications occurred during the study period.

Outcomes

The primary outcome measures were the longitudinal changes in HADS and GAD-7 scores during the perioperative period. HADS was used to evaluate perioperative anxiety and depressive symptoms during hospitalization. Assessments were performed preoperatively, on postoperative day (POD) 1, and POD 4. The HADS consists of 14 items divided into two 7-item subscales assessing anxiety (HADS-A) and depression (HADS-D). Each item is scored on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 3, resulting in subscale scores ranging from 0 to 21. Scores of 0–7 indicate no clinically significant symptoms, 8–10 indicate borderline symptoms, and 11–21 indicate clinically significant anxiety or depressive symptoms. The GAD-7 scale was used to assess the severity of anxiety symptoms, with evaluations conducted preoperatively and POD 4. The scale consists of 7 items assessing core anxiety-related symptoms, including nervousness, inability to control worrying, excessive worrying, difficulty relaxing, restlessness, irritability, and fear that something awful may happen. Each item is rated from 0 to 3, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating more severe anxiety symptoms. According to the original GAD-7 classification, scores of 0–4, 5–9, 10–14, and 15–21 represent minimal, mild, moderate, and severe anxiety symptoms, respectively.

Secondary outcomes included the Athens Insomnia Scale [20](AIS), the Quality of Recovery-15 [21](QoR-15) scale, the Ramsay Sedation Scale (RSS) [22] and the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) for pain assessment. (1) Postoperative insomnia was assessed using the AIS on postoperative days 1 and 4. This 8-item scale comprises five items evaluating nocturnal sleep disturbances (e.g., difficulty initiating sleep, nighttime awakenings) and three items assessing daytime dysfunction (e.g., mood changes, reduced energy). Each item is scored from 0 to 3 (0 = no problem, 3 = serious problem), with total scores ranging from 0 to 24; a cutoff of ≥ 6 indicates clinically relevant insomnia. (2) Quality of postoperative recovery was assessed on POD 4 using the QoR-15. This scale evaluates five domains-pain, physical comfort, physical independence, psychological support, and emotional state-to provide a comprehensive assessment of overall recovery. (3) Intraoperative sedation was assessed using the Ramsay Sedation Scale (RSS) 30 min after the start of surgery [23], when spinal anesthesia and intraoperative conditions had become relatively stable. The RSS is a 6-point scale ranging from 1 (anxious or agitated) to 6 (no response to stimulation), with higher scores indicating deeper levels of sedation. (4) Pain intensity was assessed on POD 1 using the (VAS).

Blindings

Participant blinding was achieved by using visually identical local anesthetic solutions and a standardized injection procedure. The SGB was performed by an experienced anesthesiologist who was not involved in outcome assessment, thereby minimizing procedural bias. To reduce assessment bias, investigators responsible for collecting patient-reported outcome measures-including the HADS, GAD-7, AIS, VAS, and QoR-15 were blinded to group allocation. In addition, the statistician performing the final data analysis remained blinded until the completion of the analysis.

Sample size

Sample size was calculated using PASS 15.0, with the HADS and GAD-7 scores on POD 1 as the primary outcome. The sample size calculation was primarily based on HADS scores on POD 1 because HADS had been more frequently used in our preliminary clinical observations and pilot data collection. A pilot study (10 patients per group) showed a mean difference of 1.8 and a standard deviation of 4. With a two-sided α of 0.05 and power (1-β) of 0.90, 45 patients per group were required. Considering a 10% dropout rate, the final sample size was set at 53 per group.

Statistical methods

Data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Continuous variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed data were presented as median (interquartile range). Between-group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables, including intraoperative body movement and perioperative adverse events, were analyzed using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. Heart rate and oxygen saturation before and after block were compared using ANCOVA adjusted for baseline values to reduce the influence of interindividual variability. Intraoperative sedation scores, assessed using the RSS, were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test because of their ordinal distribution. Linear mixed-effects models were used to analyze HADS, AIS, and GAD-7 scores, evaluating between-group differences and longitudinal changes over time. The model included group, time, and group-by-time interaction as fixed effects to assess the intervention effect of SGB, with a random intercept for each patient to account for within-subject correlation and individual variability.

Results

Participant flow and recruitment

Figure 1 illustrates the flow of participants throughout the trial. A total of 105 patients were initially assessed for eligibility. Among them, 11 patients were excluded: 2 due to inability to cooperate with the study procedures, 6 because of missing primary outcome data (HADS or GAD-7 scores), and 3 owing to surgery cancellation, resulting in an overall dropout rate of 10.4%. Ultimately, 94 patients were included in the final analysis, with 47 patients allocated to each group.

Baseline characteristics were generally comparable between the two groups, with no significant differences in age, BMI, sex, ASA classification, comorbidities, preoperative HADS scores, or preoperative GAD-7 scores (all P > 0.05). Notably, preoperative HADS and GAD-7 scores were numerically higher in the SGB group; however, these differences did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.060 and P = 0.074, respectively). Discussion

In recent years, the proportion of non-elderly ONFH patients experiencing perioperative anxiety and depression during THA has been continuously rising. The increasing psychological burden on patients has inevitably led to increased analgesic requirements, delayed postoperative recovery, intensified medical resource consumption, and prolonged hospitalization. More importantly, it has reduced patients’ cooperation with surgery and compliance with postoperative rehabilitation therapy, thereby adversely affecting postoperative outcomes and contributing to substantial individual and societal burdens.

Perioperative anxiety and depression are multifactorial conditions involving neuropsychological stress induced by chronic pain and functional impairment, dysregulation of the sympathetic nervous system and neurotransmitters, inflammatory activation, surgical and hospitalization-related stress, as well as age-specific social and psychological pressures, including concerns about disease progression and treatment outcomes [24–27]. Effective management of perioperative anxiety and depression has therefore become an important component of ERAS pathways. In the present study, preoperative single-shot SGB was associated with favorable changes in GAD-7 scores and improved QoR-15 scores in non-elderly ONFH patients undergoing THA, suggesting a potential benefit in perioperative psychological symptoms and postoperative recovery. However, the mixed-effects model analysis did not demonstrate a significant group-by-time interaction for HADS scores. Moreover, although a significant group-by-time interaction was observed for GAD-7 scores, additional ANCOVA adjusting for baseline GAD-7 scores did not confirm a significant between-group effect. Therefore, the psychological benefits of SGB should be interpreted cautiously and warrant further confirmation in larger prospective studies.

The primary function of SGB is to regulate autonomic nervous function. Through mechanisms such as inhibiting excessive sympathetic nervous excitement, regulating neurotransmitter levels, and improving cerebral blood flow perfusion, it positively affects emotional states [9, 28–31]. A retrospective study involving 42 patients with anxiety, depression, and insomnia who received ultrasound-guided SGB combined with cervical and temporal myofascial trigger point (MTrPs) acupuncture showed that after 4 weeks of treatment, patients’ Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA), and Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD) scores were significantly decreased [32]. Another study on patients with generalized anxiety disorder found that SGB could significantly improve anxiety symptoms and sleep quality by regulating NE, 5-HT, and neuropeptide Y (NPY) levels [33]. A randomized controlled study on military populations showed that after SGB treatment, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and anxiety scores were improved [34]. Thus, SGB has demonstrated potential therapeutic benefits in various non-surgical populations. To our knowledge, this is the first randomized controlled study to explore the role of SGB in non-elderly ONFH patients undergoing THA. Our findings suggest that SGB may contribute to favorable perioperative psychological symptoms and improved postoperative recovery quality, supporting its potential as an adjunctive component of ERAS protocols in this population. Nevertheless, further studies are required to confirm its effects on perioperative anxiety and depression.

According to existing reports, the foundation of SGB’s emotional regulation lies not only in its classical sympathetic inhibitory effects but also in its multi-node intervention on the psycho-neuro-immune axis: on one hand, by blocking the efferent pathway of the cervical sympathetic trunk, it reduces locus coeruleus-noradrenergic system activity, alleviating prefrontal cortex functional inhibition and amygdala hyperactivation; on the other hand, through vagal afferent feedback, it regulates hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis reactivity and inhibits NF-κB pathway-mediated pro-inflammatory cytokine release, breaking the vicious cycle of “pain-anxiety-sympathetic hyperactivity-inflammatory activation,” and simultaneously improving emotional experience, pain perception, and immune homeostasis [35–38]. Notably, the above regulatory network is particularly active and more plastic in non-elderly patients; their sympathetic-adrenal medullary axis has a lower response threshold, and their neuroendocrine compensatory mechanisms have not yet fully degenerated, making them more sensitive to SGB-mediated autonomic rebalancing interventions. Meanwhile, patients in this age group have relatively intact prefrontal cortex function and stronger cognitive restructuring capabilities, which facilitates the positive synergy between psychological intervention effects and neuromodulation effects, thereby achieving synchronized advancement of physiological and psychological rehabilitation under the ERAS framework. These mechanisms may partly explain how SGB contributes to favorable perioperative psychological symptoms and enhanced postoperative recovery within the ERAS framework for non-elderly ONFH patients undergoing THA.

Although SGB may provide perioperative psychological benefits, the observed effects in this study were primarily limited to the early postoperative period and were more evident in GAD-7 outcomes than in the longitudinal trajectories of HADS scores. This discrepancy may reflect differences in scale sensitivity and assessment domains, as GAD-7 focuses specifically on anxiety symptoms and may be more responsive to short-term perioperative emotional changes. However, although a significant group-by-time interaction was observed for GAD-7 scores, this finding was not maintained after adjustment for baseline differences using ANCOVA. In addition, higher perioperative Ramsay Sedation Scale scores in the SGB group may have influenced the reporting of self-assessed psychological symptoms. Although the psychological questionnaires were administered on postoperative days 1 and 4, and any direct sedative effects would be expected to diminish over time, a potential influence of perioperative sedation on early patient-reported outcomes cannot be completely excluded. Therefore, the anxiety-related findings should be interpreted cautiously, as regression to the mean, residual baseline imbalance, or the influence of perioperative sedation may have contributed to the observed effect. Further studies with larger sample sizes and more balanced baseline psychological characteristics are warranted to confirm these findings.

While SGB did not demonstrate statistically significant longitudinal improvements in HADS or AIS scores, substantial interindividual variability in these outcomes may have reduced the ability to detect treatment effects. Importantly, the sample size was powered based on HADS scores, which did not show significant improvement. Therefore, the positive findings observed for GAD-7 should be considered exploratory, and the study may have been underpowered to detect small but clinically meaningful effects on HADS or other psychological domains. Nevertheless, the absence of significant longitudinal improvement in HADS remains an important limitation. In addition, because the current study included only THA patients with preoperative psychological distress, the applicability of SGB to patients without clinically relevant anxiety or depressive symptoms remains uncertain. Larger prospective multicenter studies involving broader arthroplasty populations are warranted to confirm these findings and further define the clinical role of SGB in perioperative psychological management.

At the same time, SGB remains a relatively invasive cervical sympathetic block procedure, and its potential clinical benefits should be balanced against procedural invasiveness and safety considerations. Potential complications include vascular puncture, hematoma, local anesthetic toxicity, recurrent laryngeal nerve block, pneumothorax, and transient Horner syndrome. However, young and middle-aged patients generally have clearer cervical anatomical structures, better tissue elasticity, and greater physiological reserve, which may provide a wider procedural safety margin. In the present study, all patients underwent ultrasound-guided SGB via the anterior cervical approach, with continuous monitoring of blood pressure, heart rate, and SpO2 throughout the perioperative period to maximize procedural safety. No significant hemodynamic instability was observed. Furthermore, although the incidences of adverse events, including dizziness, nausea, vomiting, and nightmares, did not differ significantly between groups, a lower numerical incidence was observed in the SGB group.

Overall, this study suggests that SGB may be associated with improved postoperative recovery quality in non-elderly patients undergoing THA for ONFH. While some favorable trends in perioperative psychological symptoms were observed, the evidence was inconsistent across psychological assessment scales and sensitive to baseline adjustment. Accordingly, the psychological findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Further adequately powered multicenter studies are needed to confirm these findings and better define the role of SGB within ERAS protocols.
