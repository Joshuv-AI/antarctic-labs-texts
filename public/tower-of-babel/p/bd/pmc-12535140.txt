Prevalence of developmental oral anomalies among school children in Port Harcourt: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Efetobo Victor Orikpete, Bamidele Babatope Osagbemiro, Grace Onyenashia Alade, Ayamma Udo Umanah, Stanley Chukwuemeka Raymond, Joycelyn Odegua Eigbobo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe development of the mouth, face, teeth and related structures is a highly complex process; genetic and/or environmental disturbances during this period can lead to various developmental anomalies, which may present as variation in number, colour, shape or size of the affected structures. The aim of this study was to determine the prevalence of developmental anomalies among school children in Port Harcourt, Rivers State.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study carried out within Port Harcourt metropolis, Obio-Akpor local government area of Rivers state. A total of 5 schools (3 primary schools and 2 secondary schools) were selected by multistage sampling method. An interviewer administered questionnaire was used to elicit information, following which oral screening/examination was performed for all the study participants. Data collected were entered into a spreadsheet and analyzed using the SPSS package, version 27 (IBM SPSS, Chicago, USA).ResultsA total of 1802 children; 790 (43.9%) males and 1012 (56.1%) females participated in this study and had a mean age of 10.01 ± 3.67 years. Developmental anomalies of the teeth, tongue and jaws were identified in 181 individuals (10.0% of the study population). A total of 199 anomalies were recorded (11.04%), as some individuals presented with more than one anomaly. Hypoplasia was the most frequent anomaly, with a prevalence of 39% among children aged 6-12years and 26% among children aged ≥ 13years; and this difference was significant (p = 0.002). Microdontia (p = 0.002) and exostoses(p = 0.004) similarly showed statistically significant differences across the age groups. Fluorosis (37%) and macrodontia (26%) were also more among children aged 6–12 years. The distribution of these anomalies across gender and different socioeconomic status showed no statistical significance.ConclusionThe most common anomaly was hypoplasia followed by fluorosis while macrognathia and enamel pearl ranked the lowest. However, overall prevalence of developmental anomalies in this study is low compared to other studies.

Introduction

The development of the teeth and orofacial structures is a highly complex process and as such various anomalies can arise from alterations in the normal process of development. Such anomalies could be congenital, developmental or acquired, and could affect the teeth, jawbones or the oral soft tissues. The exact aetiology of most anomalies that occur in the orofacial region is unknown; however, a multifactorial cause is most widely accepted, involving interplay of both genetic and environmental factors [1–3]. There are different types of dental anomalies, which can be categorized into anomalies involving the number, size, shape, position or structure of the teeth [2]. Anomalies of the jawbones include agnathia, macrognathia, micrognathia, tori and exostoses [4], and anomalies of the oral soft tissues which include fissured tongue, hairy tongue, ankyloglossia [5].

The occurrence of developmental anomalies varies from one geographical region to another. In an Italian study [6], dental anomalies had a prevalence of 20.9% among the studied population. Studies from Saudi Arabia, have reported a prevalence ranging from 2.6% to 45.1%. Other authors have reported a prevalence of 36.7% [7] and 28% [8] in an Indian and Mexican population respectively. The studies reported on dental anomalies in Nigeria show the prevalence that ranged from 4.2% to 28.1% [9–14]. Similarly, the most frequent dental anomalies have also varied among the studies, depending on the study design and population.

While the pattern of dental anomalies has been frequently reported on by many authors, there is paucity of similar studies reporting on the prevalence and distribution of anomalies arising from the jawbones and the oral soft tissues. Furthermore, in Nigeria, many of the studies on dental anomalies have been hospital-based [10, 12, 14], and these may not give the best representation since dental anomalies are often asymptomatic, and it has been reported previously that Nigerians typically seek oral healthcare mostly because of pain. Furthermore, most of the studies have been from the South-West zone, with only one previous study in the South-South zone [12], a region with an estimated population of 26 million people.

The aim of this study, therefore, was to describe the prevalence of developmental anomalies affecting the teeth, jawbones and oral soft tissues among children and adolescents in Port Harcourt, a major city in Nigeria and the most densely populated in the South-South geo-political zone.

Materials and methods

This was a cross-sectional study carried out within Port Harcourt metropolis, Obio-Akpor local government area of Rivers state, Nigeria. Permission was sought from the State Universal Basic Education Board. Ethical Approval for the study was obtained from the Health Research and Ethics committee of the University of Port Harcourt Teaching Hospital, before commencement of the study. The study protocol, including the consent procedure, was reviewed and approved by the Health Research and Ethics Committee (Approval No: UPTH/ADM/90/S.II/VOL.XI/1948). Considering the minimal-risk and non-interventional nature of the study, and logistical challenges of obtaining written parental consent, parents were informed through the school head, who obtained verbal consent on their behalf. In addition, all children were provided with age-appropriate explanations of the study and gave verbal assent before participation.

A total of five government schools (three primary schools and two secondary schools) were selected by multistage sampling, and all children who met the inclusion criteria were included in the study. Pre-nursery and nursery school children in the selected primary schools were also included in the study. Exclusion criteria for the study were (1) those with limited mouth opening (2) children presenting with acute febrile illness requiring medical care and (3) those for whom consent could not be obtained.

An interviewer-administered questionnaire was then used to elicit information on age, gender, level of school education, and parental level of education. Oral examination was performed for all the study participants by trained dentists, and all developmental disorders affecting the oral hard- and soft-tissues were recorded. Examinations were done in the school hall using natural light and a wooden spatula. Examinations were performed by 15 dentists (6 consultants, 9 resident doctors). Prior to data collection all examiners attended a standardized training session that utilized clinical photographs to describe the anomalies. Inter-examiner calibration was performed on a pilot set of 50 children; inter-examiner agreement for the presence of any dental anomaly was quantified using Cohen’s kappa. Intra-examiner reliability was assessed by re-examining 20 participants after 7 days. Cases that were ambiguous were adjudicated by a senior consultant. Participants were categorized into three age groups based on the dentition: 0–5 years (primary dentition stage), 6–12 years (mixed dentition stage) and ≥ 13 years (permanent dentition stage). All eligible participants had an oral examination while seated, using natural light. The diagnosis of oral hard and soft tissue anomaly was made exclusively via clinical examination. Anomalies such as hypodontia and supernumerary teeth were diagnosed by counting of the teeth. Macrodontia and microdontia were diagnosed as gross deviations in tooth size that are easily discernible by clinical judgment. The anomalies were:

The anomalies assessed were then categorized as: anomalies of the teeth (Hypoplasia, Fluorosis, Macrodontia, Microdontia, Tetracycline staining, Supernumerary teeth, Hypodontia, Talon’s cusp, Double teeth, Macrognathia, Enamel pearl), Anomalies of the jawbones (Tori/Exostoses, Macrognathia, Micrognathia), and anomalies of the oral soft tissues (Fissured tongue).

Statistical analysis

Data collected were entered into a spreadsheet and analyzed using the Statistical Product and Service Solution, SPSS package, version 27 (IBM SPSS, Chicago, USA). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Associations between categorical variables (e.g., presence of anomalies, gender, anomaly type) were tested using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. P values < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 1802 children participated in this study. Their ages ranged from 1 year to 19 years, with a mean of 10.01 ± 3.67 years. There were 790 (43.8%) males and 1012 (56.2%) females. Inter-examiner agreement on the pilot sample (n = 50) demonstrated substantial reliability, with a kappa coefficient of κ = 0.79 (95% CI: 0.76–0.82) for the presence and classification of anomalies. Intra-examiner reliability, assessed through repeat examinations, yielded kappa values ranging from κ = 0.82 to 0.88, indicating very good agreement. A total of 181 children (10.0%) had at least one dental anomaly. Of these, the majority (162; 89.5%) had a single anomaly, while 19 children (10.5%) had more than one. Altogether, 199 anomalies were recorded, since some individuals contributed more than one anomaly, with an anomaly-based prevalence of 11.04% (Figure 1). Thirteen different types of disorders were recorded. Discussion

This study investigated the prevalence and pattern of developmental anomalies affecting the teeth, jaws, and tongue among school children in Port Harcourt. The overall prevalence of these anomalies was 11.04%, which is within the range reported in similar Nigerian studies (4.2% to 28.1%) [9–14], and lower than values reported in some international studies, whereas prevalence reached up to 36.7% in India [7] and 28% in Mexico [8]. The differences in prevalence seen in the various studies may be attributed to study design and population; for example, while some of these studies were based on hospital records [10, 12, 15], others were either household surveys [9, 13] or school surveys [11]. Notably, anomalies of the teeth were the most common, comprising 9.1% of all participants, while jaw and tongue anomalies accounted for only a small fraction (0.4% and 0.2%, respectively).

The majority of affected children (89.5%) presented with a single anomaly, while only 10.5% had multiple anomalies. This pattern is consistent with findings from similar studies in Nigeria and elsewhere. Temilola et al. [9] reported that 89.5% of children had single anomalies, while Yassin et al. [15] and Folayan et al. [13] found that 74.6% and 77.3% of anomalies, respectively, occurred as isolated findings. In fact, in their study, Popoola et al. [11] did not even encounter any case of multiple dental anomalies in the same individual, further emphasizing the rarity of such occurrences. The low occurrence of multiple anomalies may reflect the sporadic nature of the aetiological factors and/or the limited duration of exposure to these agents [9]. On the other hand, the occurrence of multiple anomalies is indicative of greater systemic or environmental influences during critical periods of oro-dental development [16].

With respect to age distribution, anomalies were rare in children under 6 years of age, but more frequently detected in those aged 6–12 years (mixed dentition stage) and ≥ 13 years (permanent dentition stage). This pattern may be attributed to increased visibility of developmental anomalies as more permanent teeth erupt and as children age. This finding supports earlier reports that this transitional stage is a sensitive window during which developmental disturbances are more likely to be clinically apparent, due to overlapping biological changes and increased visibility of tooth structures [3]. Anomalies were relatively rare in the primary dentition stage (0–5 years), likely due to reduced exposure duration to potential environmental insults and the fewer number of teeth present at that stage [17].

The gender distribution of anomalies in this study showed a slight female predominance (6.33% in females versus 4.72% in males), though the difference was not statistically significant. This aligns with some previous studies, which have also reported a higher prevalence of anomalies among females [9, 18], while others have shown male predominance [14, 19]. It is therefore likely that gender does not play a strong or consistent role in the development of these anomalies.

Among the types of anomalies identified, enamel hypoplasia was the most frequent, affecting 3.66% of all children examined. The higher prevalence of hypoplasia could be attributed to several factors such as systemic illnesses during the first years of life, nutritional deficiencies, or exposure to certain medications during tooth formation. In Nigeria, unsupervised and widespread use of over-the-counter medications in children is common [20] and may play a significant role in disrupting amelogenesis. When these disturbances occur, especially in early childhood, they can potentially disrupt the process of amelogenesis, resulting in hypoplastic defects [1, 21]. Moreover, the multifactorial aetiology of hypoplasia, which includes both prenatal and postnatal influences, increases its likelihood of occurrence across various age groups.

Fluorosis was the second most common anomaly, with a prevalence of 2.89%, and was more prevalent in children aged 6–12 years. This can be explained by excessive fluoride exposure during the critical periods of enamel development. In the Port Harcourt area, children may be exposed to fluoride from both drinking water and ingestion of fluoridated toothpaste. Notably, Ideriah et al. [22], reported that many toothpastes marketed in Port Harcourt exceeded the recommended fluoride content, containing fluoride levels more than the national safety recommendations. Furthermore, the common practice of toothpaste ingestion by young children, especially in the absence of supervised brushing, could significantly increase systemic fluoride intake. Some previous studies have reported high fluoride levels in water sources in Nigeria partly due to the arid climate and presence of granite rocks in some areas [23–26]. The WHO recommends that fluoride concentrations in drinking water should be between 0.5 and 1.0 mg/L. However, the fluoride concentrations in water sources in some parts of Nigeria exceeds this level, with a range from 0.03 to 6.7 ppm [24]. A previous study assessing the ground water quality in Emohua LGA in Rivers State however did not specifically measure the fluoride concentration [27]. While specific data on Port Harcourt and other parts of Rivers State is limited, the presence of elevated fluoride levels in other parts of Nigeria suggests a potential risk. Given the geological similarities across regions, it is plausible that communities in and around Port Harcourt can be affected by high fluoride levels in water sources. High fluoride concentrations in drinking water could impact children residing in such communities, highlighting a regional risk of fluoride overexposure from environmental sources [23].

Limitation

This study employed only clinical examination, without the use of radiographic imaging, and may have underrepresented those anomalies that required radiographic examination to confirm their diagnosis. However, in line with global best practices and safety guidelines, it would have been unethical to expose the participants to potentially harmful ionizing radiation solely for research purpose. Excluding children with acute systemic illness may have slightly biased the prevalence estimates if such illnesses were associated with different patterns of dental anomalies. However, the exclusion was primarily to ensure child safety and welfare.

Conclusion

Overall, while the prevalence of developmental anomalies in this study is relatively low, the prominence of preventable conditions such as fluorosis and enamel hypoplasia call for increased public health awareness, supervised oral hygiene practices in children, and further investigation into environmental fluoride exposure in the region.
