Parameter influence on the mechanical properties of FFF printed PLA/copper for EV lightweight dashboard analysis
Authors: Yaw Chong Tak, A Kottasamy, K Kadirgama, W S W Harun, M Samykano, L Samylingam, Navid Aslfattahi, Chee Kuang Kok, Arunkumar, Johnny Koh Siaw Paw
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the influence of key 3D printing parameters, specifically layer height and printing speed, on the mechanical properties of PLA/Copper composite materials used for lightweight electric vehicle dashboards. By evaluating the tensile, flexural, compression, and impact properties, this research aims to identify optimal printing conditions that enhance the performance of FFF-printed PLA/Copper parts for automotive applications. Two PLA/Copper composite brands, Gizmodorks and Colorfabb, with varying copper content, were analyzed to understand how these parameters affect mechanical strength, stiffness, and resilience. This study offers valuable insights into optimizing FFF printing settings to achieve desired mechanical properties for sustainable and high-performance automotive components.Keywords: Tensile properties, Flexural properties, Compression properties, Impact properties, Response surface methodology, PLA/CopperSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

The term “additive manufacturing,” or “AM,” refers to a process by which three-dimensional structures are constructed by the deposition of materials, usually in layers, under computer command1,2. To create the desired object, the material is deposited one layer at a time during the procedure. Before constructing the finished product, additive manufacturing also makes it easier to test and evaluate designs3. Additive manufacturing (AM) relies on the fusion of materials to create three-dimensional items from digital 3D model data, typically in a layer-by-layer fashion instead of using subtractive techniques for production4,5. Additive manufacturing (AM) encompasses a diverse range of technologies that enable the layer-by-layer fabrication of three-dimensional objects from digital models. These technologies are commonly classified based on the methods used to deposit and bond materials, with the primary categories being material extrusion, powder bed fusion, vat photopolymerization, binder jetting, directed energy deposition, sheet lamination, and material jetting.

Material extrusion, one of the main AM categories, involves the deposition of material through a nozzle or orifice, which moves in specific patterns to build up each layer of the object6. Within the material extrusion category, Fused Filament Fabrication (FFF), sometimes also referred to as Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM), is the most widely used technique. FFF operates by heating a thermoplastic filament until it softens, then extruding it through a heated nozzle onto a build platform. The extruded material cools and solidifies, bonding with the layer below to form a cohesive structure7. This process is repeated layer by layer until the entire part is constructed. The material extrusion category is particularly popular in industries such as automotive, aerospace, and consumer products due to its relative affordability, ease of use, and capability for rapid prototyping8. FFF specifically is well-suited for producing complex geometries, lightweight structures, and parts with varying mechanical properties through the use of composite materials, such as PLA reinforced with copper particles9. This makes FFF an attractive option for applications requiring customizability and material efficiency, like lightweight automotive components10. The advantages of additive manufacturing include the ability to create complex shapes with little to no tooling, to have fewer inventory needs, to use fewer raw materials, to run the machine without human supervision, and to manufacture unique creations from scratch4. Material extrusion, specifically Fused Filament Fabrication (FFF), is popular for its simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and ability to work with a wide range of thermoplastics and composite materials. FFF is particularly advantageous in applications requiring rapid prototyping and functional testing, as it enables designers to create durable, lightweight parts with relatively low material waste. However, FFF has limitations in surface finish and dimensional accuracy compared to other AM techniques, making it less suitable for high-precision applications.

Powder bed fusion (PBF), which includes methods like selective laser sintering (SLS) and selective laser melting (SLM), uses a laser to fuse powdered materials, typically metals or high-performance polymers, layer by layer11. This method offers excellent mechanical properties and is suitable for producing complex, high-strength parts for aerospace, automotive, and medical implants12. The drawbacks of PBF include higher equipment costs, longer build times, and the need for specialized safety measures when working with metal powders13. Vat photopolymerization, such as stereolithography (SLA), involves curing liquid photopolymers layer by layer using a light source, typically a laser or UV light14. SLA provides high-resolution parts with smooth surface finishes, making it ideal for applications in the dental, jewelry, and medical industries15. However, SLA parts are often brittle and not suitable for functional testing or high-stress applications, as they tend to degrade over time, particularly under UV exposure. Binder jetting and directed energy deposition (DED) are additional AM techniques used in niche applications16. Binder jetting is frequently employed in the creation of sand molds, ceramics, and metal parts, although it generally requires post-processing to achieve full density17. DED, on the other hand, uses an energy source to melt material as it is deposited, making it useful for repairing or adding material to existing parts, particularly in the aerospace and defense sectors18. These varied AM methods highlight the versatility of additive manufacturing, with each technique tailored to specific material types, desired properties, and industry requirements. Within this landscape, FFF stands out for applications that benefit from rapid prototyping, customization, and cost efficiency, particularly in sectors like automotive, consumer products, and educational prototyping.

There are several types of materials used for FFF printing in the industrial sector. At the moment, thermosetting polymer materials, such as epoxy resins, and thermoplastic polymer materials, such as polycarbonates (PC), acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS), polylactic acid (PLA), and polypropylene (PP) are typically used11,12. Due to its lightweight, economical, and versatile processing capabilities, polymer filaments have been widely used as effective filaments in the 3D printing industry19,20. However, due to insufficient strength and low functionality, polymers are less suitable for 3d printing21. To encounter this problem, a new composite filament has been developed for the FFF technique where adding reinforcements like particles, fibers, or nanomaterials showed improved structural and functional characteristics of polymer composites22. The quality of the products via the FFF technique highly depends on the correct selection of printing parameters. The printing parameters can affect the efficiency of the production and the mechanical properties of the product23. Commonly utilized parameters in FFF printing include infill pattern, infill density, raster width, raster angle, layer thickness, printing speed, air gap, operating temperature, and build orientation24. The qualities and behavior of the FFF printed specimen were determined by the input settings and type of material used25. The main challenges of FFF additive manufacturing are detecting internal faults and evaluating the quality of parts, despite its capability to produce intricate geometries26. Composite materials have been created by combining two materials to enhance mechanical and material qualities, aiming to improve quality27. Combining reinforcing particles with composite materials and printing them might lead to different flaws, as stated by Kalsoom et al.28. Defects in the material include warping, geometrical distortion, voids, porosities in the matrix and filament, and inadequate fiber matrix bonding29–32.

One limitation of FFF compared to traditional production is the insufficient research connecting printing settings with mechanical behavior33. The research was conducted using specific variations of printing conditions and particular mechanical tests to assess the mechanical properties, focusing on PLA/composites for this study. This paper aims to determine the optimal printing parameters to enhance the mechanical qualities of PLA/Copper for an electric vehicle lightweight dashboard. Two types of PLA/Copper filament brands, Gizmodorks, and Colorfabb, were utilized in this study and analyzed by adjusting the printing parameters. This study examines how layer height and printing speed impact the mechanical qualities, including tensile strength, compression resistance, bending strength, and impact resistance, of objects produced via 3D printing. Statistical models are created using experimental data and response surface methods. This study is the first to examine how layer height and printing speed impact the mechanical properties of FFF 3D printing through response surface methods. Analyzed in this study is a sophisticated polymer-matrix composite (Cu-PLA) with changes in layer height and printing speed. The confidence level is 95% with a significance threshold of α = 0.05. A P value below 0.05 indicates a significant effect by the parameter. This research identifies important characteristics and offers models for mechanical test properties, as well as optimizes parameters for specific mechanical performance goals. Mathematical models for tensile, compression, bending, and impact properties were constructed using the response surface method, focusing on adjusting layer height and printing speed to anticipate certain mechanical properties. XRD and FTIR analysis were conducted to confirm the existence of Cu particles in PLA, determine the types of compounds present, and analyze the crystalline structures of the specimens.

Materials and methods

The material utilized in this study is PLA-Copper, which is extruded utilizing the hot ends of a 3D printer by entering the appropriate command. Copper has found extensive use in a large variety of applications due to its outstanding characteristics, including malleability, strong corrosion resistance, and great electrical and thermal conductivities. Two types of brands are analyzed for this study for PLA/Cu which is Gizmodorks and Colorfabb brands. The specifications of PLA-Copper filament by respective manufacturers are shown in Table 1. For this research, RAISE 3D N2 PLUS was chosen for our 3d printing study. The Raise3D series features a solid, all-metal design that enables accurate and fluid movement while 3D printing. The selection of printing parameters is critical in FFF, as variations in layer height, printing speed, infill pattern, and density can significantly impact the mechanical properties of printed parts. For this study, layer height and printing speed were selected as the primary variables due to their substantial influence on interlayer bonding and structural performance, particularly in tensile and flexural strength. Previous studies23,24 indicate that lower layer heights generally improve bonding between layers, enhancing strength, while slower printing speeds allow for better adhesion by providing adequate cooling time between deposited layers.

Infill pattern and density are indeed additional factors that affect mechanical properties, including stiffness and durability. However, this study chose to use a consistent concentric infill pattern with 50% density across all samples to maintain uniformity in infill structure and focus on the effects of layer height and printing speed. According to Sheoran and Kumar24, standardizing parameters such as infill patterns is a common practice in additive manufacturing studies to minimize variability and isolate the effects of specific variables under investigation. The concentric infill pattern was chosen for this study because it provides a balance between structural stability and material efficiency, which is critical for lightweight applications such as electric vehicle dashboards. As highlighted by Rodríguez-Panes et al.25, concentric infill patterns exhibit uniform stress distribution, making them suitable for parts subjected to mechanical loading. Furthermore, maintaining a consistent infill pattern and density (50%) across all samples ensures that the observed differences in mechanical properties can be attributed primarily to the selected layer height and printing speed, as well as the material composition, without the confounding influence of varying infill geometries. This approach aligns with established practices in 3D printing research to enhance the reliability and reproducibility of experimental results. Future studies could further investigate the combined impact of infill patterns, density, and other factors to develop a more comprehensive understanding of their role in FFF-printed composite materials. While infill patterns can influence mechanical properties, a consistent pattern is selected to reduce variability and focus on the primary effects of layer height and printing speed on mechanical strength and stiffness. This approach allows for a more controlled investigation of these two critical parameters in FFF-printed PLA/Copper composites.

Printing process of the specimen

For this research, Solidworks software is utilized to design 3D models that need to be printed which are tensile specimens, compression specimens, bending specimens, and impact specimens for the experiment. The dimension of the test specimen is based on the ASTM standards concerning the specific mechanical performance test. The designed file is stored in .STL format (stereolithography). Once the model has been created, slicing is done using specialized software such as ideaMaker. A 3D model is converted into a set of instructions for 3D printers through the process of slicing. After that, the file is saved in G-code format using ideaMaker for 3d printing. In FFF printing, the choice of printing parameters is crucial, as it directly affects both production efficiency and the mechanical properties of the printed parts. For this study, we focused on layer height and printing speed, as these parameters are known to significantly impact the interlayer bonding and structural integrity of FFF-printed parts. These two parameters were held constant across both filaments (Gizmodorks and Colorfabb) to facilitate a controlled comparison of their mechanical properties. By standardizing layer height and printing speed, we aimed to isolate the influence of the different filament compositions on mechanical performance, ensuring that observed differences in tensile, flexural, compression, and impact properties could be attributed primarily to the material characteristics of the filaments rather than variations in process parameters.

This study’s findings demonstrate that layer height and printing speed significantly influence the mechanical properties of PLA/Copper composites in FFF printing, with observed impacts on tensile strength, flexural strength, and compression strength. Similar to findings by Rouhi Moghanlou et al.65, this study showed that reducing the layer height increased the ultimate tensile strength (UTS) and Young’s Modulus, which can be attributed to improved interlayer bonding at lower layer heights. This trend aligns with the work of Another Pulipaka et al.66, who found that reduced layer height promotes stronger layer adhesion, resulting in stiffer and more resilient structures. However, the results also indicate that an excessively low layer height does not linearly increase strength, likely due to the increased thermal stress associated with prolonged cooling periods during printing, as suggested by Kuznetsov et al.67. In terms of printing speed, our findings indicate that slower speeds yield higher tensile and compressive strengths, which concurs with studies by Brion et al.68, who found that slower printing speeds allow for better layer fusion and minimize warping or distortion. This observation supports the hypothesis that increased cooling time between layers enhances bonding, but at high speeds, rapid deposition can lead to incomplete fusion, which reduces strength. The presence of copper particles in our PLA/Copper composite also plays a role in enhancing stiffness, as metallic reinforcement generally improves rigidity, as reported by Sakthi Balan and Aravind Raj69. However, compared to studies on pure PLA, this study’s composite showed reduced flexibility, possibly due to the copper’s influence on limiting molecular mobility within the PLA matrix. These findings provide valuable insights into optimizing FFF parameters for composite materials. They also highlight the nuanced interplay between material properties and process parameters, supporting the conclusion that careful optimization of layer height and speed is essential to achieving desired mechanical outcomes in FFF-printed composites. This study contributes to the existing body of knowledge by providing parameter-specific guidelines for PLA/Copper composites, reinforcing and extending the findings of previous research.

Conclusion

This study investigated the influence of layer height and printing speed on the mechanical properties of PLA/Copper composite parts fabricated using FFF for automotive applications. The major findings reveal that lower layer heights and slower printing speeds generally enhance mechanical performance, with reduced layer height contributing to improved tensile strength and Young’s Modulus due to better layer adhesion. Conversely, faster printing speeds were found to compromise strength, likely due to reduced interlayer bonding. Additionally, the copper reinforcement within the PLA matrix contributed to increased stiffness, although it slightly reduced flexibility compared to pure PLA. These insights offer valuable guidance for optimizing FFF parameters to achieve the desired balance of strength and durability in lightweight automotive components. Future research could explore the effects of other FFF parameters, such as infill density and pattern, and investigate the use of alternative composite materials to further enhance mechanical properties. Additionally, examining the long-term durability and environmental stability of PLA/Copper composites under different operational conditions would be beneficial, particularly for applications in extreme temperature or high-stress environments. Advanced modeling techniques could also be used to predict mechanical performance based on varied printing parameters, offering a more comprehensive understanding of FFF composites for industrial applications.
