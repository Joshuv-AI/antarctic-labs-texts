Impact of Clonal Hematopoiesis of Indeterminate Potential on Treatment Response and Tumor Microenvironment in Breast Cancer Patients Undergoing Neoadjuvant Chemotherapy
Authors: Muyuan Yang, Gengshen Yin, Yifan Yu, Dihui Zhang, Xiaoying Wang, Xingchen Zhou, Xinyu Liu, Shuya Huang, Ying Shan, Xianfang Liu, Fei Wang, Juandong Wang, Yang Song, Yang Wu, Dong-Xu Liu, Zhigang Yu, Yujuan Xiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) is associated with systemic inflammation and solid tumor progression, but its impact on breast cancer therapy remains unclear. This study investigated whether CHIP influences neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) response and shapes the tumor microenvironment (TME) in breast cancer. We analyzed tumor sequencing data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), focusing on mutations in CHIP-driver genes within a variant allele frequency range of 2–33.3%, a signature indicative of tumor-infiltrating clonal hematopoiesis (TI-CH). Patients harboring these TI-CH-associated mutations exhibited significantly worse overall and disease-free survival. In a retrospective cohort of 44 NAC-treated patients, CHIP mutations were detected in 15.9% of patients, most frequently in DNMT3A and NOTCH3. CHIP positivity was significantly associated with extensive residual disease (residual cancer burden, RCB III; P = 0.027) and chemotherapy resistance. CHIP-positive patients also showed elevated post-treatment markers of renal stress (creatinine) and hypercoagulability (D-dimer). Transcriptomic analysis revealed an immunosuppressive TME in CHIP-positive tumors after NAC, characterized by reduced CD8 + T cell infiltration and dysregulation of immune pathways including antigen processing and presentation, T cell receptor signaling, and primary immunodeficiency. Notably, CHIP-positive tumors also carried a higher tumor mutational burden, suggesting a state of “immune ignorance” where defective myeloid-driven immunity overrides neoantigen abundance. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that CHIP promotes an immunosuppressive TME and systemic inflammation, both linked to chemotherapy resistance and poor outcomes in breast cancer. Our results identify CHIP as a potential biomarker for risk stratification and a therapeutic target to overcome therapy resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10911-026-09603-0.

Introduction

Clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) is a premalignant condition characterized by somatic mutations that drive clonal expansion in the absence of cytopenias or a definitive hematologic malignancy [1–3]. Initially described in the context of myeloid neoplasms, CHIP is now recognized as a clinically significant state that also elevates the risk of developing solid tumors [4]. This is particularly relevant in breast cancer, as CHIP has been detected in up to 40% of patients with solid malignancies [5]. Critically, these circulating CHIP-mutant hematopoietic clones can infiltrate the tumor microenvironment (TME), a phenomenon termed tumor-infiltrating clonal hematopoiesis (TI-CH), where they may directly influence local biology and treatment response [6]. This raises concerns about its potential impact on a patient population routinely exposed to chemotherapy.

Beyond its role in cancer development, CHIP is associated with poorer prognosis in oncology patients. Its presence has been correlated with reduced overall survival (OS), an effect primarily attributed to an increased risk of cardiovascular mortality [7]. This survival disadvantage appears to be exacerbated in CHIP carriers undergoing chemotherapy [8, 9]. However, the specific impact of CHIP on breast cancer outcomes remains unresolved. Despite numerous investigations, no conclusive associations have been established [10–12], highlighting a critical gap in our understanding and the need for dedicated studies.

Emerging evidence has begun to delineate the complex dynamics of CHIP in patients receiving systemic therapy for breast cancer. Approximately 15% of patients present with CHIP prior to neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) or endocrine therapy, yet it is seldom detected following treatment completion [4]. Intriguingly, chemotherapy itself appears to act as a selective pressure, promoting the emergence of new clones, particularly those with variant allele frequencies (VAF) ≥ 0.005 [4]. Mechanistically, CHIP mutations are increasingly implicated in systemic immune dysfunction. They drive the overexpression of inflammatory genes in innate immune cells, creating a pro-inflammatory state that may accelerate comorbidities and potentially influence cancer progression [13]. Supporting this, studies in calcific aortic valve disease have demonstrated that CHIP disrupts key immune signaling pathways, including IL-15 and B-cell receptor signaling [14], offering mechanistic clues for how CHIP might remodel the TME. Nevertheless, the specific consequences of CHIP, and by extension, TI-CH, on the anti-tumor immune response and its temporal evolution throughout breast cancer treatment are still largely unexplored.

The NAC setting provides an ideal model to address these questions. As a standard of care for early-stage breast cancer, NAC offers the dual advantage of enabling in vivo assessment of chemotherapy sensitivity while providing a window to observe molecular and cellular changes within the TME before and after treatment [15–19].

To bridge this knowledge gap, we designed a study that integrates public tumor sequencing data with a meticulously curated retrospective cohort of breast cancer patients treated with NAC. We collected paired peripheral blood and tumor specimens pre- and post-treatment, employing whole-exome sequencing (WES) and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to comprehensively characterize CHIP prevalence, mutational profiles, and concomitant immune signatures. Our primary objective was to determine the influence of CHIP on NAC response, subsequent clinical outcomes, and the state of the TME. By integrating multidimensional molecular data with clinical parameters, this work aims to elucidate the role of CHIP in breast cancer therapy and identify potential biomarkers for future patient stratification and monitoring strategies.

Materials and Methods

Public Breast Cancer Tumor Sequencing Database and Survival Analysis

Somatic mutation data (derived from tumor tissue sequencing) and clinical data of breast tumors were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) via cBioPortal. To enrich for mutations likely arising from TI-CH, we applied a focused filtering strategy. We analyzed nonsynonymous mutations in established CHIP driver genes (DNMT3A, ASXL1, TET2, TP53, RAD21, PPM1D, SF3A1, NOTCH3) [20, 21]. Only mutations with a VAF between 2% and 33.3% were retained for analysis. This VAF range aims to exclude low-confidence sequencing artifacts (VAF < 2%) and high-frequency somatic mutations more characteristic of tumor epithelial driver events (VAF > 33.3%), thereby increasing the probability that the analyzed variants represent infiltrating hematopoietic clones [22]. Patients were stratified as mutation-positive if they harbored at least one such qualifying variant. OS was defined as the time from diagnosis to death from any cause, and disease-free survival (DFS) as the time from diagnosis to the first recurrence, metastasis, or death. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed to compare OS and DFS between mutation-positive and mutation-negative groups. The log-rank test was used to assess differences between groups, and hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated using Cox proportional hazards regression models.

Patient Cohort

A total of 44 patients diagnosed with primary breast cancer who received NAC at The Second Qilu Hospital of Shandong University between June 2013 and August 2019 were included in this study. All patients received standard NAC regimens based on anthracyclines and/or taxanes. Detailed information regarding this patient cohort, including comprehensive clinical and pathological characteristics, has been reported previously [23].

Sample Collection

Pre- and post-treatment tumor tissues were collected via ultrasound-guided core needle biopsy and during definitive surgery, respectively. To ensure sample quality, representativeness, and diagnostic accuracy, a standardized protocol was implemented for all tumor tissue specimens. For each pre-treatment biopsy, at least two tissue cores were obtained under real-time ultrasound guidance. One core was immediately formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE) for standard histopathological assessment. The remaining fresh core(s) were either snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen or preserved in RNAlater solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 4 °C overnight before long-term storage at − 80 °C for nucleic acid extraction. The presence of invasive breast carcinoma in the pre-treatment FFPE core was confirmed by blinded review of H&E slides by two independent breast pathologists; only cases with a concordant diagnosis were included in the genomic analyses. For post-treatment surgical specimens, a portion of the resected tumor tissue was similarly processed for FFPE-based pathological confirmation of residual invasive disease, while a separate fresh-frozen aliquot was preserved for sequencing.

Prior to DNA/RNA extraction from the fresh-frozen tissue, mirror H&E-stained slides were reviewed to guide macro-dissection and enrich for tumor-rich areas. Furthermore, the tumor purity (the proportion of tumor nuclei) for each pre-treatment biopsy sample was estimated computationally from the WES data reported previously [23], using the ABSOLUTE algorithm, which infers purity based on somatic copy-number and allelic composition [24, 25]. Samples with an ABSOLUTE-inferred tumor purity of ≥ 0.15 were retained for downstream analysis. The median tumor purity of the included pre-treatment biopsies was 0.24 (range: 0.16–0.55).

Peripheral blood was sampled at three timepoints: before treatment initiation (baseline), after the second cycle of NAC, and after the final cycle.

Clinical Data and Pathological Analyses

Clinical data, including age at diagnosis, menopausal status, tumor histology, tumor size, lymph node status, and histological grade, were collected. The expression of Ki-67, estrogen receptor (ER), and progesterone receptor (PR) was evaluated by immunohistochemistry (IHC). Ki-67 high expression was defined as staining in at least 30% of tumor cells [26], whereas ER and PR positivity was defined as staining in at least 1% of tumor cells [27]. Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) status was assessed using both IHC and in situ hybridization (ISH). A positive HER2 status was defined as an IHC score of 3+, i.e., corresponding to more than 10% of tumor cells exhibiting intense, complete, and uniform membrane staining, or a positive ISH result. Tumor and lymph node staging prior to NAC was determined by physical examination and ultrasound, with independent assessments conducted by at least two clinicians and radiologists. Post-treatment residual tumor burden was evaluated using both the residual cancer burden (RCB) grading system and the Miller-Payne (MP) histological grading system. The RCB is calculated according to the classification proposed by Symmans et al. [28] and categorized into four groups: RCB 0 indicates pathologic complete response (pCR), RCB I represents minimal residual disease (RCB index ≤ 1.36), RCB II indicates moderate residual disease (1.36 < RCB index ≤ 3.28), and RCB III representes extensive residual disease (RCB index > 3.28) [29, 30]. The MP grading system classified histological response into five levels: level 1 indicates no tumor cell reduction; level 2 represents < 30% reduction; level 3 indicates a 30–90% reduction; level 4 signifies more than 90% reduction; and level 5 indicates the absence of invasive tumor cells, with only ductal carcinoma in situ remaining [31].

DNA and RNA Sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples using a QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) and used for WES, as described previously [23]. Briefly, DNA sequencing libraries were prepared using a KAPA Library Quantification Kit (KAPA Biosystems, South Africa) and a SureSelectXT Human All Exon V6 kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Validated DNA libraries were analyzed with paired-end runs on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) by CapitalBio (Beijing, China).

Total RNA was isolated from fresh-frozen tissues collected before and after NAC using TRIzol reagents (Tiangen, Beijing, China) and used for RNA-seq, as described previously [23]. Briefly, RNA-seq libraries were prepared using a TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) and sequenced using paired-end runs on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) by the CapitalBio.

WES Data Processing and CHIP Identification

Raw WES data were processed using fastp (v0.20.0) for quality control, and reads were aligned to the GRCh38 human reference genome using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (v0.6.1). Mutation annotation format (MAF) files were generated and processed using Sentieon (v202010). The mean sequencing depth for blood samples was approximately 100×. CHIP was suspected if somatic variants were identified in CHIP-associated genes (DNMT3A, ASXL1, TET2, TP53, RAD21, PPM1D, SF3A1, NOTCH3) [20, 21]. Variants with a VAF ≥ 2% were considered CHIP mutations, whereas variants with a VAF > 33.3% were excluded from further analysis, as they were considered potential germline variants.

RNA-Seq Data Analysis

Raw RNA-seq data were filtered with standard pipelines, and reads that did not meet the analysis criteria were removed using fastp, as previously reported [23]. Gene expression levels were quantified as transcripts per million (TPM), and differential gene expression between subgroups was analyzed using the “limma” R package. Immune cell composition within tumor tissues was estimated using CIBERSORT with the LM22 signature matrix to quantify 22 immune cell subsets, including major populations such as cytotoxic T cells, helper T cell subsets, regulatory T cells (Tregs), and polarized macrophages (M1/M2) [32]. Functional annotation and pathway enrichment analyses of differentially expressed genes were performed using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID, https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov/).

Assessment of Peripheral Blood Counts, D-Dimer and Creatinine

Peripheral blood was collected into ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)-coated tubes for complete blood count (CBC), sodium citrate tubes for coagulation assays, and plain tubes for biochemical measurements. All samples were stored at room temperature in the dark and processed within 4–6 h to preserve leukocyte morphological integrity and ensure counting accuracy. CBC and differential analysis were performed using a fully automated hematology analyzer to quantify absolute counts of white blood cell, neutrophil, lymphocyte, monocyte, red blood cell, and platelet. For coagulation and biochemical parameters, sodium citrate and plain tubes were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min to obtain plasma and serum, respectively. Plasma D-dimer levels were measured via latex turbidimetry on an automated blood coagulation analyzer (Werfen), and serum creatinine levels were determined using the sarcosine oxidase method on an automated biochemical analyzer; all assays employed the manufacturers’ corresponding reagents and quality control products. The following reference ranges were considered normal: white blood cell count, 3.5–9.5 × 109/L; neutrophil count, 1.8–6.3 × 109/L; lymphocyte count, 1.1–3.2 × 109/L; monocyte count, 0.1–0.6 × 109/L; red blood cell count, 3.8–5.1 × 1012/L; platelet count, 100–350 × 109/L; D-dimer, ≤ 500 ng/mL; and creatinine, 53–115 µmol/L.

Statistical Analyses

Continuous variables were compared using the Student’s t-test, the Wilcoxon test, or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), depending on data distribution. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s chi-square test, Continuity correction chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. A P value of < 0.05 or a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R package version 4.2.0 (https://cran.r-project.org/) and SPSS version 23.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Association Between CHIP-Associated Gene Mutations and Survival Prognosis in Breast Cancer

CHIP has been linked to systemic inflammation, impaired immune surveillance, and adverse outcomes in several cancer types, but its prognostic impact in breast cancer remains unclear. To explore the potential impact of clonal hematopoiesis-derived cells within the TME, we performed a targeted analysis of tumor sequencing data from the TCGA breast cancer cohort, focusing on CHIP-driver gene mutations filtered for a TI-CH-compatible VAF range (2–33.3%). Patients harboring such TI-CH-signature mutations exhibited significantly poorer outcomes compared with those without such mutations. Specifically, both OS and DFS were reduced in the mutation-positive group (Fig. 1A, P = 0.003; Fig. 1B, P = 0.049). These findings suggest that the presence of molecular alterations compatible with TI-CH may be associated with inferior prognosis in breast cancer, providing a rationale for our subsequent direct investigation of blood-defined CHIP and its impact on treatment response.

Identification of CHIP Patients Underwent NAC

To investigate the relationship between CHIP and NAC efficacy in breast cancer, we conducted a retrospective cohort study of 44 patients who received standardized anthracycline/taxane-based NAC [23]. Tumor tissue samples collected before and after treatment were subjected to RNA-seq, while pre-treatment peripheral blood samples were analyzed using WES (Fig. 2A). We successfully obtained WES data from all 44 pre-treatment blood samples. RNA-seq data was acquired from all 44 pre-treatment tumor samples and from 41 post-treatment samples, as three patients had no residual cancer cells following NAC (Fig. 2B). The baseline clinicopathological characteristics of the cohort are summarized in Table 1. Based on WES analysis of pre-treatment blood, seven patients (15.9%) were identified as CHIP-positive (Table 1). A total of 13 mutations were identified across eight CHIP-related genes (Fig. 3A, B). The most frequently mutated genes were DNMT3A (3/13) and NOTCH3 (3/13), with single-nucleotide substitutions such as G > A, C > T and A > G being the most common (Fig. 3C).

Association analyses demonstrated that CHIP status was not significantly correlated with any of the clinicopathological parameters examined, such as tumor size, lymph node metastasis, clinical stage, Ki-67 expression, or receptor status (ER, PR, HER2) (Table 1). This finding is supported by the consistency between the molecular subtype distribution in our cohort and that of a previously published population [4] (Supplementary Table SI), indicating that our cohort is representative and that CHIP is not linked to a specific breast cancer subtype.

CHIP is Associated with Reduced Response to NAC as Measured by the RCB Index

We next examined whether CHIP status influenced pathological response to NAC. The MP grading system has traditionally been used to assess the pCR of patients to NAC [33–36], and CHIP has previously been linked to increased breast cancer risk [37] as well as altered therapeutic responses [38]. In our cohort, although the association between CHIP status and MP grading did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.363), a trend toward reduced treatment sensitivity was observed among CHIP-positive patients. Following all cycles of NAC, their MP grading distribution was as follows: four achieved MP2, two MP3, and one MP5—corresponding to a response rate of 14.3%, compared to 24.3% in CHIP-negative patients.

To further evaluate treatment response, we employed the RCB system, which has been demonstrated to provide a more accurate assessment of pathological response [39]. Using this method, six of seven CHIP-positive patients (85.7%) were classified as RCB III, and only one patient (14.3%) achieved RCB 0. In contrast, among the 37 CHIP-negative patients, 14 (37.8%) were RCB III, 20 (54.1%) were RCB I–II, and three (8.1%) were RCB 0 (Table 1; Fig. 4A). Statistical analysis revealed a significant association between CHIP status and poor NAC response based on RCB grading (P = 0.027), with 85.7% of CHIP-positive patients remaining RCB III compared to only 37.8% of CHIP-negative patients. Notably, although one CHIP-positive patient achieved RCB 0, this individual subsequently developed metastasis, underscoring the adverse prognostic impact of CHIP. Together, these findings demonstrate that CHIP is significantly associated with diminished responsiveness to NAC.

CHIP is Linked to Reduced Kidney Function Following NAC

CHIP has been shown to be associated with kidney function [40]. We thus evaluated systemic biomarkers to explore whether CHIP status also correlated with treatment-associated kidney impairment. Both serum creatinine and D-dimer levels help assess renal function [41, 42]. We measured their serum levels during NAC at three clinically meaningful timepoints: pre-NAC (baseline), after two cycles (early exposure), and after completion of NAC (cumulative exposure). While NAC did not increase D-dimer levels in CHIP-negative patients, a significant rise was observed in CHIP-positive patients (P = 0.014) (Fig. 4B). For serum creatinine, no overall effect of NAC was observed within each of the CHIP-positive and negative groups; however, between-group comparisons revealed significant differences. After NAC, CHIP-positive patients exhibited higher serum levels of both D-dimer and creatinine compared with CHIP-negative patients. Specifically, after completion of NAC, D-dimer levels were significantly elevated in CHIP-positive patients (P = 0.046) (Fig. 4B), and creatinine levels were higher in CHIP-positive patients after both two (P = 0.030) and all (P = 0.008) cycles (Fig. 4C). Therefore, CHIP status was associated with higher post-NAC levels of D-dimer and creatinine. Because the baseline levels of D-dimer and creatinine did not differ significantly between CHIP-positive and CHIP-negative patients prior to NAC, the observed differences emerged as a consequence of treatment. Collectively, these results suggest that CHIP is linked to impaired kidney function following therapy.

CHIP Status is Not Associated with Alterations in Peripheral Blood Counts

To assess whether CHIP status correlates with baseline or post-treatment hematologic parameters, we compared complete blood count parameters between CHIP-positive and CHIP-negative patients at diagnosis (pre-NAC) and after treatment completion (post-NAC). No significant differences were observed between CHIP-negative and CHIP-positive patients in white blood cell, neutrophil, lymphocyte, monocyte, red blood cell, or platelet counts at either time point. Furthermore, NAC did not induce significant changes in these counts within either patient group (all P > 0.05; Supplementary Figure S1). This is consistent with the typically asymptomatic hematologic presentation of CHIP [2].

CHIP Attenuates NAC-Induced Immune Infiltration

NAC is known to remodel the TME, with important consequences for patient survival and therapeutic response [43, 44]. Using CIBERSORT with the LM22 signature matrix, we performed a comprehensive deconvolution of 22 immune cell subsets within the TME examined immune cell composition before and after NAC. At baseline, no significant differences in immune infiltration were observed between CHIP-positive and CHIP-negative patients (Fig. 5A). Following completion of NAC, clear divergences emerged. CHIP-positive tumors exhibited significantly reduced infiltration of CD8 + T cells compared with CHIP-negative tumors (P = 0.042; Fig. 5B). This profiling revealed concomitant changes in related populations, including a non-significant decrease in Tregs (P = 0.053; Fig. 5B) and variations in macrophage subsets. These findings reinforce that CHIP is associated with a post-NAC TME characterized by impaired cytotoxic T cell infiltration, consistent with our earlier observation that CHIP was associated with higher RCB index.

CHIP-Positive Tumors Exhibit Higher Tumor Mutational Burden

To investigate the relationship between CHIP status and tumor immunogenicity, we estimated the tumor mutational burden (TMB) from pre-NAC tumor WES data using the maftools R package, as previously reported [23]. Consistent with prior evidence linking CHIP to elevated genomic instability [45], the CHIP-positive group exhibited significantly higher TMB compared with the CHIP-negative (P < 0.01; Supplementary Figure S2). However, elevated TMB was not associated with expected signatures of immune activation.

Pathway Enrichment Gene Expression in CHIP-Positive Versus CHIP-Negative Tumors After NAC

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the attenuated immune response in CHIP-positive patients, we performed KEGG pathway enrichment and differential gene expression analyses on post-NAC RNA-seq data. In RCB III tumors, multiple immune-related pathways were significantly dysregulated in CHIP-positive patients, including the primary immunodeficiency pathway, T cell receptor signaling, and antigen processing and presentation (FDR < 0.01; Fig. 6A).

Further gene-level analyses revealed broad suppression of cytotoxic immune effectors in CHIP-positive tumors. For example, the primary immunodeficiency pathway gene CD8B was significantly downregulated (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 6B). Within the TCR signaling pathway, multiple critical genes including CD3G, CD8A, RASGRP1, CD8B, GRAP2, and CD3E, were significantly suppressed (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 6C). Similarly, several genes in the antigen processing and presentation pathway (KLRC2, CD8A, CD8B, and HLA-F) were significantly downregulated (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 6D). In contrast, pathways unrelated to immune activity, such as neuroactive ligand–receptor interaction, did not show gene-level differences (data not shown).

Together, these results demonstrate that CHIP profoundly disrupts immune signaling and TME remodeling following NAC. The downregulation of key T cell-related pathways provides a mechanistic basis for the reduced CD8 + T cells infiltration observed in CHIP-positive patients and may explain their impaired therapeutic response.

Discussion

In this combined analysis of a public dataset and a clinical NAC cohort, we demonstrate that CHIP represents a clinically significant biomarker in breast cancer, associated with resistance to NAC and impaired survival outcomes. Patients harboring CHIP mutations exhibited significantly worse OS and DFS in the TCGA cohort. Clinically, in our NAC cohort, CHIP-positive patients were markedly more likely to exhibit extensive residual disease (RCB III), indicating primary chemotherapy resistance. Beyond tumor response, CHIP was linked to impaired renal function during treatment, as evidenced by elevated D-dimer and creatinine levels post-chemotherapy. Critically, CHIP-positive tumors failed to mount an effective antitumor immune response after NAC, showing significantly reduced CD8 + T cell infiltration and broad downregulation of key immune activation pathways. These findings position CHIP as a readily detectable biomarker that could help identify patients at high risk for treatment failure, enabling more personalized therapeutic strategies and enhanced monitoring for chemotherapy-related toxicity.

The detection of clonal hematopoiesis mutations in tumor sequencing has gained increasing attention for its potential role in solid tumor progression and treatment response, particularly in the context of TI-CH. CHIP clones in the blood can systemically infiltrate the TME. When high-sensitivity sequencing is used to analyze tumor tissues, these infiltrating mutant immune cells can be detected, forming what is known as a ‘tumor-derived’ signal [6]. Our TCGA analysis was designed to capture this TI-CH signal by focusing on CHIP-driver gene mutations within a specific VAF range (2–33.3%) to enrich for likely hematopoietic origin. Consistent with the TI-CH paradigm, breast cancer patients harboring these mutations experienced worse outcomes. This clinical association is recapitulated and mechanistically explained by a TET2-deficient CHIP mouse model: CHIP-bearing mice developed more aggressive primary tumors, spontaneous lung metastases, and shorter distant metastasis-free survival [38], an effect attributed to TET2-loss-driven inflammatory responses [46].

Our findings, viewed through the TI-CH framework, support an integrative model where CHIP-mutant hematopoietic clones systemically seed tissues, driving pathology through chronic, aberrant inflammation. The infiltration of these mutant cells into the TME directly reprograms local immunity. As noted, TET2-deficient myeloid cells hyperactivate the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to IL-1β-driven immunosuppression [46]. DNMT3A and other CHIP mutations similarly contribute to an immunosuppressive TME, in part by promoting the expansion of granulocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells (G-MDSCs) via elevated IL-6 secretion, which potently suppresses CD8 + T cell cytotoxicity [47]. This myeloid-driven immunosuppression was evident in our data as a post-NAC TME depleted of both cytotoxic CD8 + T cells and Tregs. This pattern is consistent with a broad T cell-poor or ‘immune desert’ phenotype, where the principal defect is a failure in T cell recruitment and activation, rather than active suppression by abundant Tregs. This aligns with our transcriptomic evidence of downregulated antigen presentation and T cell signaling pathways, suggesting immunosuppression is primarily driven by the altered myeloid compartment and the systemic inflammatory milieu, which together create a microenvironment inhospitable to T cell infiltration and function.

Notably, we found that CHIP-positive tumors exhibited a higher TMB. This presents a paradox: high TMB is typically associated with immunogenicity and response to immune checkpoint therapy [48]. Our data resolve this paradox by suggesting that CHIP induces a state of immune ignorance, a phenomenon in which antigenic tumors evade immune recognition not through tolerance or deletion of T cells, but through failure of antigen delivery to secondary lymphoid organs and consequent absence of T cell priming. This mechanism was first established in sarcomas, where solid tumor pieces grew progressively despite expression of a strong viral antigen, strictly correlating with failure of tumor cells to reach draining lymph nodes and absence of primed cytotoxic T cells [49]. Subsequent work has extended this paradigm to head and neck squamous cell carcinomas, in which nuclear β‑catenin activation is significantly associated with a non‑inflamed, immune‑ignorant phenotype and low CD8 + T cell infiltration [50]. More broadly, breast, colon, and prostate cancers have been categorized as primary immune‑ignorant tumors, with low response rates to single‑agent immune checkpoint inhibition attributed to oncogenic pathway‑driven immune ignorance rather than active immunosuppression [51].

Here, we show that in CHIP‑positive breast cancers, immune ignorance arises through a distinct mechanistic route: despite abundant neoantigens, the CHIP-mutant hematopoietic system fails to mount an effective response due to defects in antigen presentation (downregulated antigen processing pathways) and T cell priming (downregulated TCR signaling), coupled with active suppression by the altered myeloid compartment. This model explains how high TMB and an immunosuppressive TME can coexist in CHIP-associated breast cancer. Beyond the tumor, the same principle of mutant cell infiltration applies to other organs, explaining systemic comorbidities. Infiltration of CHIP-mutant macrophages into the kidney parenchyma promotes inflammation and interstitial fibrosis, exacerbating baseline renal vulnerability and potentially amplifying chemotherapy-induced stress [52]. Concurrently, the CHIP-associated pro-inflammatory and pro-thrombotic state (evidenced by elevated D-dimer) may further compromise renal and systemic vascular health [53, 54]. Thus, CHIP appears to act as a systemic “inflammatory amplifier,” coordinately driving immunosuppression within the TME and end-organ damage, which collectively contribute to poorer treatment responses and adverse clinical outcomes.

However, the association between CHIP and breast cancer prognosis remains controversial, with some studies reporting inconsistent results [10–12]. These discrepancies may partly reflect differences in cohort size, follow-up duration, and methodological approaches to defining CHIP (e.g., blood vs. tumor sequencing). Public databases typically include larger populations with longer observation periods, thereby providing greater statistical power. By contrast, our NAC cohort comprised only 44 patients, which precluded robust survival analysis. Taken together, current evidence suggests that breast cancer patients with CHIP may be predisposed to less favorable outcomes, although further validation in larger, well-characterized cohorts is warranted.

In our cohort, CHIP mutations were identified in 15.9% of patients, a prevalence rate consistent with previous reports [55]. CHIP has been detected in approximately 25% of patients across various solid tumors, with even higher rates (∼30%) observed in lymphoma patients following stem cell transplantation [56]. In breast cancer, reported CHIP prevalence ranges from 15% to 44% [4, 10], though it can be as low as 2.7% in younger patients [11].

Among the mutated genes in our cohort, DNMT3A was the most frequently altered CHIP-associated gene in NAC patients. DNMT3A mutations are among the most common drivers of clonal hematopoiesis, conferring proliferative advantages to hematopoietic stem cells through epigenetic dysregulation. Previous studies have linked DNMT3A-mutant CHIP to poor clinical outcomes [57], and elevated serum interferon-γ levels [58], suggesting a role in systemic inflammation. Notably, DNMT3A is also among the most frequently mutated genes in both inflammatory bowel disease and hematologic malignancies [59, 60], further supporting a potential biological link between chronic inflammation and leukemogenesis. These findings highlight the importance of further investigating the role of DNMT3A mutations in the context of NAC-treated breast cancer.

Few studies have directly examined the relationship between CHIP and treatment response in breast cancer, making our findings particularly relevant. We assessed this association using the RCB system, a validated and standardized method for evaluating residual tumor burden following NAC. CHIP-positive patients were significantly more likely to be classified as RCB III, a category strongly predictive of poor prognosis in breast cancer [28, 61]. These findings suggest that CHIP may contribute to reduced treatment sensitivity in breast cancer.

Beyond treatment response, CHIP has also been associated with impaired renal function. Previous studies have shown that CHIP is associated with increased susceptibility to acute kidney injury (AKI), delayed recovery, and progressive renal decline [40]. Mechanistically, CHIP contributes to accelerated renal functional decline via macrophage infiltration into the kidney parenchyma and consequent induction of interstitial fibrosis [62]. In our cohort, although creatinine levels remained within normal range, CHIP-positive patients showed a rising trend compared with CHIP-negative patients, suggesting a potential predisposition to subclinical renal impairment during chemotherapy.

We also observed elevated D-dimer levels in CHIP-positive patients after NAC. D-dimer, a marker of hemostasis and fibrinolysis, is widely used to identify hypercoagulable states [63]. CHIP has been previously linked to a proinflammatory milieu, which may promote coagulation activation [64–66]. Our findings are consistent with this mechanism, indicating that CHIP-associated inflammation may elevate D-dimer during treatment. These observations raise the possibility that routine monitoring of D-dimer in CHIP-positive patients could help guide timely interventions to reduce thrombotic risk during NAC.

The immune landscape was also markedly altered in CHIP-positive patients. After NAC, these patients displayed reduced CD8 + T cell infiltration, reflecting a more immunosuppressive TME. Previous studies suggest that CHIP-related mutations disrupt T cell polarization, amplifying inflammatory responses via T helper cells and Tregs [67]. Tet2-deficient mice exhibit suppressed CD8 + T cell cytotoxicity and accelerated tumor progression [47]. These observations highlight the potential of therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring CD8 + T cell function or targeting the suppressive myeloid compartment to improve outcomes in CHIP-positive patients.

Transcriptomic profiling further revealed broad downregulation of immune-related pathways in CHIP-positive tumors classified as RCB-III after NAC. The changed pathways included primary immunodeficiency, TCR signaling, and antigen processing and presentation. Dysregulation of the primary immunodeficiency pathway has been linked to cancer initiation, progression, and therapy resistance [68, 69]. Similarly, the TCR signaling pathway, which is central to T cell activation, proliferation, and effector functions, is more frequently upregulated in patients who respond favorably to therapy [70, 71]. The antigen processing and presentation pathway is equally critical for immune recognition and clearance of tumor cells; its impairment fosters immune evasion, therapeutic resistance, and poor outcomes, while its activation is associated with better prognosis [72–74]. Together, the suppression of these pathways in CHIP-positive patients provides mechanistic insight into their diminished chemotherapy sensitivity and supports the rationale for exploring combinatorial strategies that integrate immunotherapy with NAC.

Our findings should be interpreted in light of certain limitations: (1) The modest cohort size may limit the generalizability of the results as well as the statistical power. Future validation in larger, multicenter cohorts, using matched tumor–blood paired samples subjected to deep sequencing and coupled with extended clinical follow-up, is required to substantiate the observed associations; (2) Our analysis of the post-NAC TME focused on patients with residual disease (RCB I-III). A direct comparison of the immune landscape between patients achieving pCR (RCB 0) and those with extensive residual disease (RCB III) could not be performed due to the low number of pCR cases in our cohort (n = 4): three patients had no residual cancer cells following NAC, and only one having analyzable post-treatment tissue. This is a relevant distinction, as emerging evidence suggests RCB III is characterized by T cell exhaustion and dysfunctional immunity, whereas RCB 0 reflects a state of successful immune activation [75–77]; (3) Our TCGA analysis, while employing a focused VAF/gene filter to enrich for TI-CH signals, cannot definitively confirm the hematopoietic origin of mutations without matched normal sequencing; this is an inherent constraint of such public dataset analyses; (4) The retrospective design and absence of prospectively collected serum samples precluded direct measurement of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, IL-8, IL-1β, and VEGF. This is an important limitation, as TET2- and DNMT3A-mutant CHIP has been mechanistically linked to elevated IL-6 and IL-1β signaling, which contributes to cardiovascular pathology and can promote a tumor-permissive microenvironment [78–80]. While our findings are consistent with this CHIP-associated inflammatory axis, we cannot definitively attribute the observed systemic effects to these specific mediators. Future prospective cohorts should incorporate standardized serum biomarker panels to directly correlate CHIP mutational status with systemic inflammatory profiles and to dissect the cytokine networks involved; and (5) While we identified marked differences in immune and signaling pathways, the study did not include functional experiments to establish causality. Subsequent mechanistic studies are warranted to evaluate potential therapeutic strategies that target CHIP-associated immune dysregulation.

In summary, our integrated analysis establishes that CHIP is associated with poorer prognosis and diminished response to NAC in breast cancer. CHIP-positive patients exhibited higher residual tumor burden, elevated systemic biomarkers of renal impairment and hypercoagulability, an immunosuppressive TME with reduced CD8 + T cell infiltration, and broad downregulation of key immune activation pathways. These multi-level alterations suggest that CHIP contributes to chemotherapy resistance by coordinately reshaping systemic physiology and antitumor immunity. Although further validation in larger prospective studies is needed, our work supports the potential of CHIP as a clinically informative biomarker for risk stratification and therapy monitoring in breast cancer. Future research should prioritize the development of targeted approaches, including immunomodulatory strategies aimed at the CHIP-mutant myeloid compartment, to overcome CHIP-mediated treatment resistance and improve clinical outcomes.
