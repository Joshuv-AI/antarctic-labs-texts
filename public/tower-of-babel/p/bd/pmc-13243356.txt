Manifestations and Influencing Factors of Posttraumatic Growth Among Ovarian Cancer Patients Undergoing Chemotherapy: A Qualitative Exploration
Authors: Fangyu Ai, Yihua Ni, Kanfei Yao, Weili Wu, Li Yang, Lulu Shi, Jianfen Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to explore the manifestations of posttraumatic growth (PTG) and its influencing factors among ovarian cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy.MethodsA descriptive qualitative study was employed. Between May 2023 and June 2024, 12 patients undergoing chemotherapy for ovarian cancer were recruited from a tertiary hospital in Hangzhou, China. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews and analyzed using conventional content analysis.ResultsThree major themes and nine subthemes emerged: (1) Manifestations of PTG, including adopting healthy lifestyles, enhancing self-management awareness, maintaining a positive psychological state, and developing harmonious interpersonal relationships; (2) Facilitators of PTG, including medical resource accessibility, family support, and an optimistic personality; (3) Barriers to PTG, including family burden and conflict, and negative illness perception.ConclusionPTG among ovarian cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy manifests as an integrated process of behavioral, psychological, and social adaptation. Family support and illness perception may modulate the degree of PTG. Future research should adopt multi-center and longitudinal designs to validate and extend these findings.Keywords: ovarian cancer, chemotherapy, posttraumatic growth, factors influencing, qualitative study

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OC) is the eighth most common cause of cancer and cancer death in women worldwide.1 According to data from the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), new OC cases account for 1.6% (324,000) of all new female cancer cases globally, while OC-related deaths represent 2.1% (206,000) of all female cancer deaths.2 Due to the absence of recognizable early symptoms and effective screening methods, most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage.3 Surgery combined with platinum-based chemotherapy remains the standard treatment.1,4 Although chemotherapy has improved treatment outcomes and reduced mortality and recurrence rates, it induces adverse effects such as nausea, vomiting, bone pain, and hair loss, which impose substantial physiological, psychological, and social burdens.5,6 Consequently, supporting OC patients in coping adaptively with chemotherapy-related challenges and promoting positive psychological adjustment has become an important clinical concern.

With the establishment of the bio-psycho-social medical model, the goals of cancer care have expanded beyond prolonging survival to include psychological adjustment, functional rehabilitation, and quality of life improvement.7 In this context, increasing attention has been paid to how patients can achieve positive psychological transformation when facing illness-related adversity. The theory of posttraumatic growth (PTG) provides an important explanatory perspective, suggesting that individuals may experience positive psychological changes through their struggle with traumatic events or situations, including enhanced personal strength, improved interpersonal relationships, and a renewed appreciation of the value of life.8,9 Importantly, PTG is not an automatic outcome of trauma; rather, it emerges from the ongoing effort to make sense of and adapt to the illness experience. This framework provides a theoretical basis for examining how patients achieve positive psychological adaptation.

While PTG has been widely documented across various cancer populations, its exploration in OC patients remains limited. Previous qualitative studies10 have primarily focused on psychological adaptation following diagnosis, rather than during active chemotherapy. Chemotherapy involves repeated treatment cycles and cumulative side effects, which may give rise to unique PTG manifestations. Additionally, some studies11 have examined PTG among caregivers of OC patients during chemotherapy, indirectly suggesting that this phase may represent an important window for the occurrence of PTG. However, positive growth observed in caregivers cannot be assumed to reflect or substitute for patients’ own psychological adaptation. During chemotherapy, patients face ongoing challenges, including concerns about disease progression, uncertainty regarding treatment outcomes, and disruptions to social roles, which may further exacerbate psychological distress.12 Therefore, exploring the manifestations and influencing factors of PTG during this specific treatment phase is crucial for gaining a nuanced understanding of how OC patients achieve positive psychological adaptation.

Method

Study Design

A descriptive qualitative design was employed in this research. This approach prioritizes direct, comprehensive descriptions of experiences over theoretical interpretation,13 making it well-suited for exploring how OC patients perceive and manage the challenges associated with both their illness and chemotherapy.

Participants

Purposive sampling was used to recruit OC patients receiving chemotherapy at a tertiary hospital in Hangzhou, China, between May 2023 and June 2024. To maximize information variability, the sample included both patients who exhibited signs of positive psychological adaptation in clinical care and those who, despite struggling, continued with treatment.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged≥18 years; (2) diagnosed with OC through histopathology ≥3 months and currently undergoing chemotherapy; (3) able to communicate verbally and express personal emotions; (4) self-reported positive psychological changes during chemotherapy (assessed through a brief conversation with the researchers); and (5) willing to participate and providing informed consent. The exclusion criteria included: (1) patients with other life-threatening or severe mental illnesses; and (2) those who had experienced a major traumatic life event (eg, recent bereavement) within the past month.

The sample size was determined according to the principle of data saturation. No new information emerged during the formal interviews with the first 10 patients. To confirm that saturation had been achieved, two additional patients were interviewed, resulting in a total sample of 12 participants. Development of the Interview Outline

The interview outline was developed based on the PTG function model14 and a comprehensive review of relevant literature. Two patients meeting the inclusion criteria participated in a pilot interview (these patients were not included in the final analysis). Following a group discussion by the research team, the final interview outline was established. The specific questions included: (1) How did you feel when you were first diagnosed with OC? (2) What was your reaction upon learning that chemotherapy would be required? (3) What was the most difficult experience during chemotherapy, and how did you cope with it? (4) What positive changes have you noticed in yourself during the chemotherapy process? (5) From the moment you learned about your cancer to your active engagement in treatment, what has supported you through this journey?

Procedure

All interviews were conducted by the first author, who was not involved in the clinical care of the participants and had received systematic training in qualitative research. Before each session, the schedule was confirmed with the participant. Interviews took place in a private, comfortable health education room to promote open and honest discussion. Prior to starting, participants received a thorough explanation of the study’s purpose and significance, and were informed of the anonymity and independence of the interview process prior to consent. Interviews lasted 30 to 40 minutes, during which the investigator took comprehensive notes and audio-recorded the sessions. Participants were encouraged to express their thoughts and emotions freely using their own words, and technical terminology was avoided throughout. The investigator paid close attention to both verbal and non-verbal cues, such as body language and emotional fluctuations. Follow-up questions were posed to clarify responses and ensure comprehensive data collection. Appropriate responses to participants’ remarks helped maintain rapport, allowing for a more in-depth exploration of their experiences.

Data Analysis

Within 24 hours of each interview, the audio recordings were transcribed and field notes organized. Data management was supported by NVivo 11.0 software. A conventional content analysis approach was adopted.15 The analytical process involved the following steps: (1) Repeatedly reviewing the recordings and transcripts to develop a comprehensive understanding of the material; (2) Identifying key words and significant segments in the transcripts, refining their core meanings, and completing open coding; (3) Grouping similar or related codes into themes and sub-themes; (4) Defining and coding the categories, and identifying corresponding examples from the text. All transcripts underwent rigorous quality checks, including detailed reviews, to ensure completeness prior to analysis. To enhance methodological rigor, two researchers independently implemented a dual coding process. Any inconsistencies in data organization and analysis were resolved through team discussions, after which the final themes were refined and confirmed.

Ethics Approval

This study strictly followed the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Approval ID: 2023–0221). All participants signed a paper-based informed consent form.

Manifestations and Factors Influencing PTG

Theme 1: Manifestations of PTG

When recounting their illness experiences, all participants described initial reactions of shock, fear, and a sense of life disruption upon diagnosis. However, as they navigated the chemotherapy journey, they gradually identified meaningful changes in themselves. These changes were not abstract realizations but concrete shifts in daily life, self-perception, and social interactions.

Adopting Healthy Lifestyles—“Nothing Is More Important Than Being Healthy”

Many patients engaged in a process of reflection regarding the potential causes of their disease. The majority attributed the onset of OC to previous unhealthy lifestyles, such as staying up late, irregular eating, or poor dietary habits. This reflection prompted concrete behavioral changes.

P2: “In the past, I often played mahjong until two or three in the morning. Now, no matter who calls me to play in the middle of the night, I won’t go. Nothing is more important than being healthy.”

P6: “The biggest change is definitely that my three meals are much more regular now. I also avoid takeout and raw pickled seafood as much as possible. It’s my way of telling myself I still care about living.”

For some patients, these lifestyle changes extended beyond themselves to their families. P4 shared: “I used to cook whatever was convenient. Now I’ve learned about nutrition and cook healthy meals for my whole family. In a way, this illness made us all healthier.”

Enhancing Self-Management Awareness—“Now I Know What Helps and What Doesn’t”

As treatment progressed, patients gradually moved from passive endurance to active participation in their care. This shift was often prompted by challenging experiences that taught them the importance of self-management.

P1: “During my first cycle, I experienced constipation but didn’t think much of it. By the fourth day, my abdomen was so distended that I had to go to the hospital. The doctor explained that these symptoms were related to chemotherapy. That period felt truly miserable, but it taught me to pay attention to my body. Now I track my symptoms and manage them proactively.”

P9: “At first, I just endured the nausea and fatigue passively. But later, I started keeping a diary to record my reactions after each session. Now I know that drinking plenty of water and taking short walks right after chemotherapy can really help alleviate the discomfort.” This enhanced self-management awareness gave patients a sense of control. P9 added: “It’s small, but it makes me feel like I’m not just a passive recipient of treatment. I’m part of the team.”

Maintaining a Positive Psychological State—“Being Positive Doesn’t Mean Being Happy All the Time”

The shift in mindset was the most frequently mentioned manifestation of PTG. Patients described various strategies for maintaining a positive outlook, ranging from cognitive reframing to behavioral engagement.

P3: “Since it had already happened, I figured there was no use dwelling on it. I just tell myself every day to live well and take things as they come. If you can learn to take things lightly, you won’t feel so miserable.”

P8: “When I first learned I had this disease, I felt like my whole world had collapsed. After all, I was only 29 years old. I kept thinking that I hadn’t done anything wrong, yet I was going to die. I felt a deep sense of unfairness, but also completely powerless. But after visiting multiple hospitals and hearing the same message, I realized I had no choice but to face it. That acceptance, hard as it was, became the foundation of my strength.”

Some patients found that staying engaged in meaningful activities helped sustain their psychological state. P6: “For the first few days after chemotherapy, I felt completely drained and unable to do anything. During that time, my husband noticed that I was just staying at home all day with nothing to do but brood, so he got me involved in gardening with him to distract me. Now, I’ve taken over all his plants. I’ve found that keeping myself occupied really helps. Whenever I have free time, I tend to my plants. It’s definitely better than staying at home alone with nothing to do but think.”

Notably, patients emphasized that maintaining a positive state did not mean denying negative emotions. P1 reflected: “I still have bad days. But I’ve learned that being positive doesn’t mean being happy all the time—it means not letting the bad days define me.”

Developing Harmonious Interpersonal Relationships—“It’s Not Pity—It’s Understanding”

Patients described a complex process of navigating relationships during chemotherapy. Initially, many withdrew from social interactions due to embarrassment, fear of burdening others, or physical limitations. Over time, however, some rebuilt connections in ways that felt more authentic and emotionally sustaining.

P7: “After learning about my illness, many relatives and friends I hadn’t seen for years traveled long distances to visit me. It was such a rare opportunity for us to sit together and chat. It really made me happy.”

Others found that illness provided an opportunity to heal strained family relationships. P3 shared: “I had been estranged from my sister for years over some trivial family matter. After I got sick, she came to the hospital without a word. We didn’t talk about the past—we just sat there. That silence said more than any apology could. Now we call each other every week. I lost a lot because of this disease, but I also got my sister back.”

A particularly meaningful development was the formation of peer support networks within the hospital. P11: “I used to avoid people because I didn’t want them to see me like this. But then a nurse introduced me to another patient, and we started talking. Now I’ve come to know most of the patients on this ward. We ask each other how we’re doing, when our next chemotherapy appointments are, and sometimes we go for walks together. It feels really good—like I’m not alone in this.”

Theme 2: Facilitators of PTG

Patients identified a range of factors that supported their ability to cope and grow during chemotherapy. These factors operated at both external and internal levels.

Medical Resource Accessibility

Access to expert care and timely treatment not only improved physical outcomes but also instilled hope and trust in the treatment process. Patients often contrasted their experience with imagined or observed alternatives, deepening their appreciation.

P1: “ I feel very fortunate to have encountered such skilled doctors here. Although I had to undergo chemotherapy before surgery and felt like I was in really bad shape, now I can feel myself gradually recovering. Watching those cancer markers decrease makes me feel that everything has been worthwhile.”

P4: “Before my first chemotherapy, I was terrified—I had no idea what to expect. But the nurse sat down with me and explained everything: what each drug was for, what side effects might happen, and how to manage them at home. She even gave me a printed guide with when to call for help. I felt like I was in safe hands.”

Family Support

During the struggle against the disease, the care, support, and companionship of family members are indispensable. Patients described how the presence of family members gave them strength to keep going.

P6: “Although I basically live alone now, my parents come from our hometown every time I’m hospitalized for chemotherapy. After each session, they stay at my place for a few days, cleaning and cooking every day. Having them around means I don’t have to worry about household matters. It saves me a lot of energy and gives me peace of mind.”

P10: “Actually, I didn’t want to continue treatment at first. But my daughter kept urging me, saying that if this disease can be treated, I should go for it. When she said she wanted me to be around for more years, my initial reluctance began to waver. I realized if undergoing surgery and chemotherapy could bring a turnaround, perhaps it would be worth it.”

Optimistic Personality

An optimistic personality facilitates positive psychological transitions and contributes to the development of PTG in OC patients undergoing chemotherapy.

P1 reflected on how her personality shaped her experience: “I’ve always been an easygoing person. During my first few rounds of chemotherapy, I shared a room with another patient who barely spoke. She just lay there, completely overwhelmed by the whole thing. I understood—I had days like that too. But most days, even when I felt unwell, I would still chat with the nurses or joke with the visitors next door. It made me feel better, more like myself.” She added: “Some people think being positive means pretending everything is fine. That’s not it. It’s about remembering that cancer is part of my life, but it’s not all of it.”

For some patients, optimism was not an inherent trait but a gradually cultivated mindset. P4: “ In the beginning, after each chemotherapy session, I would lie alone in bed, refusing to talk to anyone, just staring at the ceiling with tears in my eyes. Then one day, a nurse said to me, ‘You can’t control the cancer, but you can control how you respond to it.’ That really stuck with me. After that, I started making small changes—greeting my roommate, saying thank you to the nurses for their care. It felt forced at first, but gradually it became part of my routine. I’m not a naturally optimistic person, but choosing to face things positively does make each round of chemotherapy a little more bearable.”

Theme 3: Barriers to PTG

Even among individuals who reported PTG, moments of despair, guilt, or uncertainty were common, indicating that growth and distress can occur simultaneously. These coexisting negative emotions did not completely prevent PTG but sometimes served as a catalyst for self-reflection and subsequent growth.

Family Burden and Conflict

Paradoxically, family members who provided crucial support also sometimes became sources of stress. Financial strain, caregiving responsibilities, and family conflicts intersected with the chemotherapy experience, increasing patients’ psychological burden.

P6: “I keep telling them they don’t need to keep coming back and forth like this—I can manage the hospitalizations and chemotherapy on my own. But they insist on coming anyway. Sometimes, seeing them do this actually makes me feel even more pressured. Now they’re using their retirement savings to pay for my treatment. If it ends with them having to bury their own child, then what’s the point of all this treatment?”

P10: “My daughter accompanies me to every medical appointment and hospitalization for chemotherapy. But watching her rush around—finishing work, cleaning her own home, then coming to the hospital to see me before hurrying back—it really pains me. I always feel like I’ve become her biggest burden.”

Negative Illness Perception

Due to factors such as age, cancer stage, tumor gene expression, and sensitivity to chemotherapy agents, illness perceptions and survival outcomes vary among OC patients.16,17 Patients who held fatalistic views about cancer often described their experiences with a sense of hopelessness and expressed passive resignation.

P5: “I’m old. What’s the point of all this? Everyone says this disease can’t be cured. After nearly a year, I’m not cured—instead, I feel increasingly weak. I only came for chemotherapy because my son dragged me here.”

P12: “I’ve seen too many people die from this. I don’t believe chemotherapy will save me. I just wait.”

P8 described her struggle: “Everyone tells me to stay positive, to fight. But I look in the mirror and I don’t recognize myself. I’m tired all the time. Nothing tastes good anymore. I’m supposed to be in the best years of my life, and instead I’m attached to an IV pole. What exactly am I fighting for?”

Discussion

PTG Manifests as an Integrated Process of Behavioral, Psychological, and Social Reconstruction

Our findings suggest that PTG among OC patients undergoing chemotherapy is not a single outcome but an integrated process encompassing four interconnected dimensions: adopting healthy lifestyles, enhancing self-management awareness, maintaining a positive psychological state, and developing harmonious interpersonal relationships.

Maintaining a positive psychological state emerged as the core manifestation that enables and sustains growth in other dimensions. Patients who successfully reframed their mindset were more likely to engage in healthy behaviors, actively manage their symptoms, and rebuild social connections.18

Adopting healthy lifestyles represented a concrete expression of positive adaptation. Most patients attributed the onset of OC to their previous unhealthy living habits, and thus took the initiative to correct maladaptive behaviors such as staying up late or having irregular meals. This behavioral change not only served as a self-protective strategy to cope with the disease but also evolved into a long-term health management pattern following the traumatic experience, improving patients’ physical tolerance to chemotherapy and creating favorable conditions for disease recovery.19

Enhancing self-management awareness was a key step in shifting patients from passive recipients to active participants in the chemotherapy process. By recording chemotherapy-induced side effects, communicating regularly with medical staff and proactively acquiring disease-related health knowledge, patients gradually mastered scientific methods to cope with chemotherapy-related symptoms. This process not only alleviated physical discomfort but also enhanced patients’ sense of control over their disease, further consolidating their positive psychological state.20

At the social level, developing harmonious interpersonal relationships provided essential emotional and practical support. Reconnecting with relatives and friends alleviated the loneliness and alienation brought by the disease, while peer support networks within the hospital enabled patients to share experiences and foster mutual encouragement—a mechanism particularly vital for psychological adaptation in cancer populations.21,22

The Dual Role of Family Support in PTG Development

Family support is widely recognized as a crucial facilitator of PTG.23,24 During chemotherapy, care, companionship, and material assistance from family members help patients manage practical difficulties and provide emotional comfort, effectively alleviating negative emotions such as anxiety and depression.25 However, this study highlights the inherent duality of family support. The financial toxicity associated with OC treatment26 and the care burden placed on family members can generate a strong sense of self-perceived burden in patients.27 Many participants reported guilt over consuming family savings or burdening loved ones, and some even considered giving up treatment due to this guilt. This tension may attenuate the benefits of family support, thereby reducing the psychological resources available for growth.28

The dual impact of family support is further influenced by the cultural context. In China, where family interdependence is highly valued, an individual’s sense of worth is closely tied to their ability to contribute to the family.29 However, chemotherapy can disrupt this role, shifting patients from giver to receiver and creating a role conflict. Whether a young patient observes her parents spend their retirement savings on her treatment or an older patient sees her daughter exhausted from caregiving, the underlying conflict is the same: patients’ perceived inability to fulfill culturally prescribed family roles intensifies feelings of guilt. Notably, when experienced at manageable levels, guilt sometimes motivated adaptive behaviors, such as enhanced self-management and healthier lifestyle adjustments.30 In summary, family support influences PTG not by entirely preventing it, but through patients’ perceived burden, which modulates the degree of PTG during chemotherapy.

Illness Perception as a Critical Moderator of PTG

Beyond the objective realities of cancer stage and treatment response, how patients interpret their illness profoundly shapes their psychological adaptation and capacity for PTG. Influenced by factors such as advanced age, late-stage cancer, poor chemotherapy tolerance, and pervasive social rumors, some patients internalized the fatalistic view that “OC is incurable” and “chemotherapy is ineffective.” This cognitive framework trapped them in a state of despair, rendering them passive recipients of treatment rather than active agents in their own recovery.

According to the Common-Sense Model of Self-regulation,31 illness perception is not merely a reflection of clinical reality but a cognitive construction that mediates the relationship between disease and adaptation. Patients who perceive their illness as uncontrollable or life-threatening are more likely to adopt avoidant coping strategies—such as withdrawing from social connections, disengaging from self-management, and relinquishing hope. This passive resignation not only compromises treatment adherence but also limits the potential for PTG.32 Importantly, the impact of illness perception on PTG can operate independently of objective clinical indicators. Two patients with identical prognoses may experience vastly different psychological trajectories depending on how they interpret their situation. This suggests that, although healthcare professionals may not be able to alter the disease trajectory, they can influence patients’ illness perceptions and coping responses, thereby creating conditions conducive to PTG.

Clinical Implications

Drawing on the most novel finding of this study—that family support can paradoxically generate guilt and modulate PTG—clinical interventions may prioritize addressing patients’ self-perceived burden. Excessive guilt may attenuate the benefits of family support, whereas manageable guilt can promote adaptive behaviors. Interventions may include providing patients with sufficient time and space to express their feelings, guiding family members to understand patients’ perspectives, and implementing family-centered strategies, such as organizing family meetings, providing training in positive communication, and leveraging family strengths to alleviate self-blame and guilt.33

Another key area is addressing negative illness perceptions as a modifiable cognitive barrier. Excessive negative perceptions can increase psychological burden and reduce patients engagement with treatment, thereby hindering PTG. Interventions may include structured health education, peer experience sharing, and symptom management supported by digital platforms, which collectively may enhance patients’ understanding, confidence, and proactive engagement in treatment.34

Limitations

There are several limitations of this study. First, the cross-sectional design captures PTG at a single time point, precluding insights into its dynamic trajectory over the course of treatment. Second, the single-center sample from a tertiary hospital may limit the transferability of findings to other settings. Third, this study included only patients who self-reported psychological changes during chemotherapy, which may have excluded patients with potential PTG who lacked awareness or willingness to report it, as well as those without any PTG. However, this design choice was made to ensure that participants had experienced PTG, allowing for an in-depth exploration of its manifestations and influencing factors, rather than representing a methodological flaw. Consequently, the “barriers” identified in this study should be more accurately understood as factors that moderate the degree of PTG rather than as determinants that completely prevent its occurrence.

Conclusion

This qualitative study provides an exploration of PTG among OC patients undergoing chemotherapy, highlighting its multidimensional manifestations and key influencing factors. The findings indicate that PTG manifests as an integrated process of behavioral, psychological, and social adaptation, with family support and illness perception potentially modulating its extent. Given the single-center, cross-sectional design, these results should be interpreted as exploratory; future research should employ multi-center and longitudinal designs to validate and extend these findings.
