The evolution of real-world evidence in lung cancer: a bibliometric analysis from 2009 to 2024
Authors: Ke-Ke Liu, Meng-Ke Wang, Ting-Ting Hou, Wei-Jing Gong, San-Lan Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeReal-world evidence (RWE) plays an increasingly important role in complementing randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in lung cancer research. This study aims to map the knowledge structure, identify research trends, and highlight future directions of real-world studies (RWS) in lung cancer through bibliometric analysis.MethodsWe retrieved publications on RWS in lung cancer from the Web of Science Core Collection (WOSCC) spanning 2009 to 2024. Bibliometric analyses were performed using CiteSpace, VOSviewer, and the R package Bibliometrix to examine publication trends, collaborative networks, co-citation patterns, and keyword bursts.ResultsA total of 2,137 articles were included. Annual publications increased markedly after 2017, accounting for 97.52% of the total. China contributed the most publications (n = 660), while the USA exhibited the highest centrality in international collaboration. Keyword burst detection identified “combination therapy,” “KRAS mutation,” and “deep learning” as the most current research frontiers.ConclusionsThis analysis underscores the pivotal role of RWE in complementing RCTs and validating therapeutic strategies in oncology. Current frontiers include combination therapy, KRAS mutations, and deep learning, indicating a shift toward precision oncology and advanced data analytics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-04794-8.

Introduction

Lung cancer is a leading malignant cancer with high global incidence and mortality, posing a severe threat to human health [1]. The epidemiological landscape is particularly concerning in China, where lung cancer tops the list in both morbidity and mortality, with a troubling trend towards younger onset ages, thereby imposing substantial societal and economic burdens [2]. The therapeutic arsenal for lung cancer has expanded significantly to include surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy.

The cornerstone of therapeutic evidence remains the randomized controlled trial (RCT). However, RCTs have inherent limitations, including stringent eligibility criteria, homogeneous patient populations, and controlled settings, which can restrict the generalizability of their findings to the broader, more diverse patient population seen in real-world clinics [3]. In this context, real-world studies (RWS), which collect data from routine clinical practice, have emerged as a vital complement to RCTs. RWS provides evidence on effectiveness, safety, and treatment patterns in heterogeneous patient groups, including the elderly and those with comorbidities often excluded from trials [4, 5].

Despite advancements in targeted therapies and immunotherapy that have improved survival outcomes [6, 7], the ongoing integration of these innovations into routine clinical care for diverse real‑world populations requires continuous generation and synthesis of real-world evidence (RWE). Bibliometric analysis provides a systematic method to map the production landscape of RWE – including which patient populations, interventions, and outcomes are most frequently studied, and which remain underexplored. By identifying knowledge clusters and research gaps, it offers an evidence-based roadmap for prioritizing future real-world investigations and informing policy decisions [8]. Tools like VOSviewer [9], CiteSpace [10, 11], and the R package “Bibliometrix” [12], enable the visualization of collaborations, identification of research hotspots, and tracking of thematic evolution [13]. VOSviewer [9] is a Java-based bibliometric application that can perform co-occurrence, co-citation, coupling analysis, clustering, and burst detection analyses on literature in a research field, presenting the results in a visual format through maps. CiteSpace [10], a visualization tool for scientific knowledge mapping developed by Dr. Chaomei Chen at Drexel University in the United States, is based on Java. It can generate author and institution collaboration maps, keyword co-occurrence maps, and dynamically identify co-occurrence clusters, and research hotspots, making it suitable for multidisciplinary research. Bibliometrix [12] is an R-based bibliometric software developed by a team from the University of Naples Federico II in Italy. It can conduct visual analyses of literature information exported from databases such as Web of Science. The combination of these three tools enables a comprehensive analysis of the development direction and history of a field.

While previous bibliometric studies have explored areas like nanotherapeutics [14], immunotherapy [15], and microenvironment [16] in lung cancer, a dedicated, comprehensive analysis of the RWS landscape is absent. This study fills this gap by conducting a systematic bibliometric analysis of RWS in lung cancer from 2009 to 2024. Our objectives are to: (1) quantify the growth trajectory and global contributions in this field; (2) map the collaborative networks among researchers, institutions, and countries; (3) identify the foundational and emerging research themes; and (4) discuss future directions for RWS in lung cancer research.

Method

Database and search strategy

Literature data were retrieved from the Web of Science Core Collection (WOSCC). We selected WOSCC because it provides comprehensive indexing of high‑impact biomedical journals, complete and structured citation data, and full compatibility with the three bibliometric tools used in this study (CiteSpace, VOSviewer, Bibliometrix). WOSCC has long been regarded as the gold standard database for bibliometric analysis due to its rigorous journal selection process, broad coverage of scientific disciplines, and unique citation network structure that enables accurate co‑citation, coupling, and burst detection analyses—capabilities that are less developed or inconsistently structured in other databases such as Scopus or PubMed. While using a single database may omit some regional or non‑English publications, this approach ensures analytical consistency and reproducibility—a well‑accepted practice in bibliometric research. The search strategy was designed to capture all relevant RWS on lung cancer. We defined a RWS as any non‑interventional, observational, or retrospective/prospective investigation that uses data collected during routine clinical practice (e.g., electronic medical records, claims databases, disease registries, or patient charts) to evaluate treatment patterns, effectiveness, safety, or biomarkers in lung cancer patients. The insights derived from such studies are termed RWE. The timespan was set from January 1, 2009, to December 31, 2024. This period was selected to comprehensively cover the major development phase of RWE in oncology, beginning with early regulatory frameworks and extending through the widespread clinical adoption of immunotherapy and targeted agents. The final literature search was performed on June 6, 2025. Only original articles published in English were included. Research letters that contain original research data were also eligible and were classified as articles. Editorial material, meetings, abstracts, reviews and Editorial or correspondence letters without original data were excluded. Two reviewers (K.L., M.W.) independently screened titles and abstracts; full‑text review was performed when the study design was unclear. Disagreements were resolved by consensus or by consulting a third reviewer (W.G.).

Data analysis

This study employed WPS (Version 12.1.0.20784) to conduct an analysis of the external characteristics of the indentified publications, such as annual number of publications and frequency.

VOSviewer (Version 1.6.20) was employed to construct and visualize co-authorship, co-occurrence, and co-citation networks for authors, countries, institutions, journals, and keywords. In the generated maps, node size represents publication volume or co-occurrence frequency, connecting lines denote collaborative relationships, and line thickness reflects collaboration strength [9]. In the keyword density view, node brightness correlates with keyword frequency; higher brightness signifies greater frequency and research prominence.To ensure the visualization focuses on core contributors and improves the clarity and stability of the network structure, minimum document thresholds were set case-specifically: a threshold of 9 was applied for authors and institutions, while a threshold of 1 was used for countries and 5 for journals. Normalization methods (association strength) and layout parameters (attraction and repulsion forces) were adjusted to achieve uniform node distribution and reduce label overlap.

CiteSpace (Version 6.3.R1) was applied for keyword co-occurrence, clustering, burst detection, and timeline visualization analyses. Parameters were set as follows: time span 2009–2024 with 1-year slices; link strength calculated using the Cosine algorithm within slices to reduce the influence of absolute co‑occurrence frequencies and to better reflect the relative strength of associations between nodes; selection criteria based on the g‑index with a scaling factor k = 10, which optimizes the trade‑off between including highly cited items and controlling network complexity; additionally, Top N = 50 and Top N% = 10.0% were applied per time slice—a standard configuration for longitudinal burst detection. Network pruning employed “Pathfinder” and “Pruning sliced networks”; these methods retain the most salient connections while eliminating redundant edges, a well‑established practice in CiteSpace‑based bibliometric studies to improve visual interpretability without distorting the underlying network structure. In keyword co-occurrence graphs, node size reflects keyword frequency, colors denote publication years, lines indicate collaborations, and line thickness represents collaboration intensity [11, 17]. Centrality assessed node importance, with higher centrality indicating greater influence and collaboration; centrality ≥ 0.10 denotes significant influence. Keyword clustering for keywords and cited references used the log‑likelihood ratio (LLR) algorithm, which produces semantically more coherent and readable cluster labels than other methods (e.g., latent semantic indexing or mutual information) [17]. A modularity (Q) value > 0.3 indicates significant clustering [18], and a mean silhouette (S) value > 0.7 suggests high cluster consistency [19].

Bibliometrix was used to analyze international collaboration networks among authors’ countries. Original literature data were processed in the R environment (Version 4.4.2). Data cleaning utilized grep commands and regular expressions. The Bibliometrix package extracted corresponding authors’ nationality information to generate country collaboration maps.

Core authors were identified based on Price’s Law, which posits that half of the publications on a single research topic should originate from a core group of highly productive authors. This core group’s size approximates the square root of the total number of authors [20]. The defining formula is: N = 0.749*(ηmax)1/2, where ηmax represents the publication count of the most prolific author. This formula has been validated across multiple scientific disciplines and provides an objective, replicable threshold for identifying core authors. Authors with publications exceeding N were classified as core authors.

Results

Annual publication output and growth trend

A total of 2,137 articles on RWS in lung cancer were identified from the WOSCC for the period 2009–2024. The detailed screening and selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1. The growth trajectory can be divided into two phases: a nascent phase (2009–2016) with low and stable output, and a rapid growth phase (2017–2024). Publications in the latter phase constituted 97.52% of the total, with a peak of 530 articles in 2024 (Fig. 2). This inflection point coincides with the formalization of regulatory frameworks for RWE and the widespread clinical translation of immune checkpoint inhibitors, which substantially expanded the need for post‑marketing effectiveness and safety studies.

A total of 13,395 authors contributed to the field. Applying a minimum publication threshold (T) of 9, VOSviewer generated a co-authorship network of 129 authors, forming 5 distinct clusters (Fig. 3A). WANG, YAN from China was the most prolific author (31 articles), followed by HAO, XUEZHI (28 articles) and CHOUAID, CHRISTOS from France (28 articles) (Table 1). The high productivity of Chinese investigators reflects the country’s massive patient pool and sustained national investment in precision oncology research (e.g., National Key R&D Program), which has incentivized large‑scale real‑world data (RWD) collection and outcomes research. According to Price’s Law (N = 4.17), 542 core authors (publications ≥ 5) were identified, contributing to 63.50% of all publications, indicating a mature and collaborative research community. The overlay visualization (Fig. 3B) highlights emerging active research teams led by HU, XIAOHAN and CHIHARA, YUSUKE.

National and international collaboration landscape

86 countries/regions contributed to the research with T set at 1. China produced the most publications (n = 660, 30.9%), followed by the USA (n = 586, 27.4%) and Japan (n = 210, 9.8%) (Table 2). China’s leading output is closely associated with the National Natural Science Foundation’s sustained funding for lung cancer epidemiology, health services research, and the rapid expansion of hospital‑based electronic medical record systems. However, the USA held the highest total link strength (TLS = 623), indicating it is the most central hub in the global collaboration network (Fig. 4, Supplemental Fig. 1). This centrality reflects the USA’s long‑standing coordinating role in multinational clinical trials (e.g., ECOG‑ACRIN, SWOG) and its mature infrastructure for RWD research, including SEER‑Medicare, Flatiron Health, and ASCO’s CancerLinQ. European countries, including Germany, England, and France, also showed strong collaborative ties. China’s high output but relatively lower TLS (272, ranked 7th) suggests a potential for greater international integration, partly attributable to language barriers, historically domestic‑oriented research evaluation systems, and limited participation in global trial networks until recent years.

Analysis of core journals and knowledge base

Publications were spread across 341 journals. 82 journals met the threshold and formed 4 clusters with T set at 5. LUNG CANCER was the leading journal (141 articles), followed by FRONTIERS IN ONCOLOGY (114 articles) (Table 4; Fig. 6A). The prominence of LUNG CANCER reflects its dedicated focus on RWS, rapid peer‑review process, and willingness to publish observational research that is often under‑represented in general medical journals. The journal co-citation network, comprising 5,231 sources, identified JOURNAL OF CLINICAL ONCOLOGY (5,036 citations), JOURNAL OF THORACIC ONCOLOGY(4,462 citations), and NEW ENGLAND JOURNAL OF MEDICINE (4,418 citations) as the primary knowledge foundations (Fig. 6C). These high‑impact journals serve as the main conduits for pivotal clinical trials and authoritative practice guidelines, confirming that the intellectual base of RWE in lung cancer remains firmly anchored in gold‑standard randomized evidence.

Other high-frequency keywords included “immunotherapy,” “epidermal growth factor receptor,” and “targeted therapy” (Fig. 7B).

Cluster analysis identified 10 coherent themes (Q = 0.5964, S = 0.8844), including #0 non-small cell lung cancer, #1 immune checkpoint inhibitor, and #4 real world study (Fig. 7C). The timeline view (Fig. 7E) illustrates the thematic evolution, from early focus on EGFR and brain metastasis to the recent dominance of immunotherapy and RWE survival analysis.

Burst detection revealed the frontier topics (Fig. 7D). “Treatment patterns” was the strongest burst (3.35, 2015–2020). The most active current bursts (2022–2024) are “combination therapy,” “real-world study evidence,” “KRAS mutation,” and “deep learning”. These frontiers directly mirror contemporary clinical challenges: the urgent need to optimize immunotherapy combinations to overcome resistance, the recent FDA approvals of KRASG12C inhibitors (sotorasib, adagrasib) and the consequent demand for post‑marketing effectiveness and resistance data, and the digital transformation of oncology data science through artificial intelligence capable of extracting insights from unstructured medical images and electronic health records.

Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive bibliometric overview of the global RWS landscape in lung cancer over the past decade and a half. The exponential growth in publications, particularly post-2017, aligns with the clinical adoption of immunotherapy and the increasing recognition of RWE’s value by regulators, clinicians, and payers.

The geographical analysis reveals a dominant contribution from China, reflecting substantial national investment in cancer research and the vast clinical data resources available. The USA’s position as the central collaborative hub underscores its long-standing leadership in oncology research and its ability to forge international partnerships. Japan’s institutional leadership, exemplified by the National Cancer Center, can be attributed to its universal health coverage system, which facilitates long‑term, population‑wide data linkage, and the proactive role of the National Cancer Center in promoting RWD utilization since the early 2010s. The journal landscape reveals a persistent “evidence hierarchy” bias: while specialized journals such as LUNG CANCER have become major outlets for RWS, the most co‑cited sources remain elite general medical and clinical oncology journals that predominantly publish randomized trials. This suggests that RWS are still primarily positioned as complementary, rather than equal, forms of evidence. Dedicated platforms that recognize the methodological rigor of observational research are essential to further elevate the credibility and visibility of RWE. The active participation of pharmaceutical companies in the top institutions list highlights the critical role of industry-sponsored RWS in post-marketing surveillance and outcomes research [22]. However, as acknowledged in the limitations, this prominence may also reflect differential funding capacities and publication incentives, and should not be equated solely with scientific leadership.

The intellectual structure and evolution of the field, as mapped by keywords and co-cited references, tell a compelling story. The field has matured from describing basic treatment patterns and outcomes in NSCLC to rigorously evaluating the real-world effectiveness of targeted therapies (e.g., EGFR TKIs) and immunotherapies (e.g., PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors). The emergence of “combination therapy” and “KRAS mutation” as bursting keywords marks a new phase. After establishing the efficacy of single-agent therapies, research is now focused on optimizing combination strategies and tackling previously “undruggable” targets like KRAS with novel agents, with RWS playing a crucial role in validating these approaches in diverse populations [23, 24]. RWD on KRASG12C inhibitors (e.g., sotorasib, adagrasib) are already being used by clinicians to assess effectiveness in patients typically excluded from trials (e.g., those with brain metastases, poor performance status), and by payers and health technology assessment bodies to inform coverage decisions and managed entry agreements. Similarly, RWS on immunotherapy combinations are helping to identify which patient subgroups derive the greatest benefit and to characterize rare but serious adverse events that may not emerge in pre‑approval trials.

Perhaps the most forward-looking trend is the burst of “deep learning”. This signifies a paradigm shift from traditional statistical analyses of RWD towards the application of artificial intelligence. Deep learning models can unlock insights from complex, unstructured RWD (e.g., medical images, pathology reports, genomic sequences), facilitating more precise prognostication, prediction of treatment response, and identification of novel biomarkers [25, 26]. Nevertheless, the translation of these computational advances into routine clinical practice faces substantial hurdles, including the need for interoperable data standards, large annotated datasets, prospective validation, and regulatory frameworks for algorithm transparency and ethical oversight. Recognizing this, agencies such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration have recently issued draft guidance on artificial intelligence (AI) ‑ based software as a medical device, and RWE is expected to play a central role in the post‑market surveillance and continuous performance monitoring of such algorithms.

This study has limitations. First, exclusive reliance on WOSCC may under-represent studies published in regional journals or non‑English languages, potentially biasing the geographic distribution of our results toward high‑income countries and English‑speaking regions, and underestimating contributions from researchers in non‑English speaking countries. Second, the inclusion of only English‑language articles may introduce language bias, which could skew authorship and institutional rankings toward English‑proficient nations. Third, the prominence of industry‑sponsored studies among highly productive institutions (e.g., AstraZeneca, Merck & Co., Bristol Myers Squibb) may reflect differences in research funding and publication incentives rather than purely scientific productivity. Therefore, institutional leadership patterns should be interpreted with this caveat in mind. Fourth, our analysis did not adjust for self‑citations, which may inflate the perceived citation impact and co‑citation centrality of certain authors, institutions, or journals. This potential bias should be considered when interpreting author influence and journal co‑citation rankings. Fifth, bibliometric algorithms—particularly those used for clustering (LLR) and burst detection—are inherently sensitive to parameter choices (e.g., threshold values, pruning methods, time‑slice settings). Although we followed widely adopted practices and reported all parameters transparently, different configurations might yield slightly different thematic groupings or burst patterns. Future studies should conduct sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of these findings. Future studies should integrate multi‑source data (e.g., Scopus, PubMed Central, regional databases) and employ sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of the findings.

The future of RWS in lung cancer is bright but requires concerted effort. There is a need for greater standardization in data collection and methodology to enhance the reliability and comparability of RWE [27]. Finally, as AI models become more prevalent, developing frameworks for their transparent and ethical validation using RWD will be paramount [28].

Conclusion

This bibliometric analysis delineates the dynamic evolution of RWS in lung cancer, highlighting its growth from a niche area to a central component of oncological evidence generation. The field is characterized by strong global contributions, increasingly sophisticated research questions, and the adoption of advanced analytical technologies. The current hotspots—combination immunotherapy, KRAS-targeted therapy, and deep learning—herald a future where RWE is seamlessly integrated with cutting-edge science to guide personalized patient care and improve outcomes in lung cancer. Bridging the gap between bibliometric insights and clinical adoption will require sustained multi‑stakeholder collaboration among researchers, regulators, payers, and professional societies.
