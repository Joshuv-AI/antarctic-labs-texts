Investigating biotypes and neural characteristics among anxiety, depression, and comorbidity via a novel noisy label learning method
Authors: Yuhui Du, Wei Huang, Yulong Wang, Vince D Calhoun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnxiety and depression share many symptoms, frequently leading to diagnostic uncertainty. They are also the most common co-occurring psychiatric disorders. Standard clinical evaluations typically diagnose each condition categorically rather than assessing their relative severity when both are present. Prior imaging studies largely concentrated on delineating similarities and distinctions between the two.MethodsTo address this gap, we propose a novel noisy label learning method to investigate the biotypes and relevant neural characteristics among anxiety, depression, and their comorbidity. Our approach constructs a robust classification model to address potential diagnostic confusion among anxiety, depression, and comorbidity using a noisy label learning strategy, and employs the trained model to identify the anxiety and depression biotypes, as well as comorbidity biotypes characterized by differing degrees of anxiety and depression predominance.ResultsUsing brain functional network connectivity (FNC) from 502 depression patients, 245 anxiety patients, 177 comorbid patients with both anxiety and depression, and 500 healthy controls, we not only identify four distinct biotypes, but also reveal neural linkages between comorbid biotypes and depression and anxiety biotypes. Compared to the depression biotype (Biotype 1), the anxiety biotype (Biotype 2), and the comorbid biotype with an anxiety predominance (Biotype 3–2) demonstrate comparable alterations across seven FNCs, mainly including the connections between the cognitive control and visual domains. Relative to the anxiety biotype (Biotype 2), the depression biotype (Biotype 1), and the comorbid biotype with a depression predominance (Biotype 3–1) exhibit similar connectivity profiles across 16 FNCs, primarily involving connections within the cognitive control domain, as well as between the sensorimotor and visual domains. Furthermore, biotypes are characterized by distinct patterns of behavioral symptoms that are consistent with their underlying neural relationships.ConclusionsIn summary, we propose a neuroimage-based model that addresses the diagnostic ambiguity between anxiety and depression and derive data-driven subtypes for promoting the precise diagnosis.Keywords: anxiety, depression, comorbidity, brain functional network connectivity, noisy label learning

Introduction

Mental disorders are increasingly becoming one of the major challenges to human health and social well-being worldwide (Wittchen et al., 2011). Among them, depression and anxiety, as the two most common mental disorders, have caused a huge burden on society and families (Chisholm et al., 2016; Trautmann et al., 2016). Anxiety is more prominently characterized by excessive worry about future uncertainties and increased vigilance, while depression is marked by persistent low mood and significant loss of interest or pleasure. The clinical manifestations of anxiety and depression also overlap to a high degree (Zbozinek et al., 2012; Kalin, 2020). Specifically, both disorders can present with sleep disturbances, fatigue, decreased concentration, and decision-making difficulties, which makes them easily confused in clinical practice. More challenging is the fact that the comorbidity rate of these two disorders is significantly higher than that of other combinations of mental disorders (Brown et al., 2001; Kessler et al., 2005). This high comorbidity poses significant challenges for clinical diagnosis and treatment. Notably, current clinical practices often classify comorbid patients as a distinct category without considering the relative severity of the two disorders for targeted intervention, leading to reduced therapeutic efficacy compared to non-comorbid patients (Pollack, 2005). Hence, utilizing objective neuroimaging data is crucial for elucidating the mechanisms underlying the comorbidity of depression and anxiety and, importantly, for quantitatively evaluating the relative severity of anxiety or depression in patients.

In addition to examining anxiety (Langhammer et al., 2025) or depression (Suo et al., 2025a) independently using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) techniques, most neuroimaging studies on the two disorders (LeDoux and Pine, 2016) have focused on investigating the similarities and differences between them. For instance, analyses of brain structural MRI (sMRI) measures such as cortical thickness, surface area, and volume indicate that depression and anxiety share thinning in the left orbitofrontal cortex, while also exhibiting distinct neurobiological characteristics (Maggioni et al., 2019). One study conducted diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) meta-analysis to identify shared and disorder-specific white-matter alterations across major depressive disorder, anxiety disorder, and post-traumatic stress disorder, revealing common fractional anisotropy reductions in the corpus callosum alongside distinct microstructural signatures for each disorder (Guo et al., 2025). Additionally, researchers have analyzed resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data to examine low-frequency signal amplitude and functional connectivity in a set of a priori regions, revealing that these metrics varied with both diagnostic group and symptom severity (Oathes et al., 2015). Collectively, these findings suggest that psychopathology does not align neatly with discrete diagnostic categories, supporting a transdiagnostic view of the neurobiological mechanisms underlying anxiety and depression rather than a strictly categorical model. Moreover, although some progress has been made in studying depression and anxiety from an imaging perspective, research on the comorbidity of anxiety and depression is still very limited (Sindermann et al., 2021). Previous studies typically regarded comorbid patients as a homogeneous group, overlooking their inherent heterogeneity. This “one-size-fits-all” approach to analyzing comorbidity masks the potential differences within the comorbid population and fails to explain the underlying neural mechanism. Indeed, most existing imaging studies on anxiety and depression have been conducted under the assumption that clinical diagnostic labels are accurate, without considering the possible misclassification caused by overlapping clinical symptoms. Although a recent study by Tozzi et al. (2024) proposed circuit-based biotypes of depression and anxiety using hierarchical clustering on regional connectivity features, the framework was primarily based on traditional feature extraction and clustering techniques, which may be less effective in modeling the complex and nonlinear structure of neuroimaging data underlying psychiatric heterogeneity. Therefore, how to employ objective neuroimaging to account for the confusion among anxiety, depression, and comorbidity remains a significant challenge.

Currently, the combination of neuroimaging and deep learning techniques for exploring mental disorders is increasingly popular. Deep learning offers distinct advantages for analyzing large-scale, complex, and high-dimensional brain imaging data by enabling automatic feature learning. This capability allows for accurate discrimination among different mental disorders (Suo et al., 2025b), effective differentiation between patients and healthy controls (HCs), and patient clustering for biotype identification (Wang et al., 2026). However, most studies on depression and anxiety disorders rely on clinical diagnostic labels when constructing models (Zafar et al., 2024), assuming that these labels are highly accurate. In actual clinical practice, however, the clinical diagnosis is easily confused, leading to noisy labels, which makes traditional supervised deep learning models struggle to cope with such complex situations. Encouragingly, deep learning strategies focusing on learning with noisy labels have been proven to significantly enhance model performance and generalization ability in the presence of label noise (Song et al., 2022), providing a powerful tool for building more reliable disease classification models. Despite this progress, although many methods have been proposed for biotype identification, noisy label learning strategies have rarely been explored for this goal. For example, HYDRA is a supervised method that relies on labels from HCs to achieve hard subtyping of patients via multiple discriminant hyperplanes (Varol et al., 2017). Non-negative matrix factorization is an unsupervised approach that enables purely data-driven soft subtyping by decomposing the original feature space (Chen et al., 2020). Smile-GAN, as an unsupervised generative method, provides continuous subtyping for patients by learning latent representations directly from data (Yang et al., 2021). Recently, our study (Du et al., 2025a) demonstrated that noisy label learning has strong potential to address inaccuracies in psychiatric disorder labels arising from the subjectivity of clinical diagnosis. Specifically, we proposed a mutualistic multi-network noisy label learning (MMNNLL) method that leverages multiple deep neural networks (DNNs) to learn from noisy labeled data by maximizing cross-network consistency in identifying and exploiting both clean and noisy labels. This approach not only enables the construction of more robust classification models but also facilitates the identification of potential disease biotypes. However, to date, no prior work has leveraged noisy label learning to disentangle the overlap between anxiety and depression or to quantify their severity in patients.

To address this gap, this paper presents a novel neuroimaging-based approach for addressing diagnostic ambiguity among anxiety, depression, and their comorbidity using noisy label learning, while simultaneously deriving distinct biotypes defined by unique neural characteristics. Unlike purely unsupervised methods, healthy population–supervised approaches, and our previous noisy label learning method MMNNLL, this study employed a new noisy label learning framework that incorporates semi-supervised learning principles by progressively leveraging unreliable samples under the guidance of more reliable ones. Our work not only enables the construction of robust deep learning models despite potential inaccuracies in the diagnosis of anxiety, depression, and their comorbidity, but also automatically yields data-driven biotypes that reflect varying degrees of anxiety and depression predominance. Leveraging brain functional network connectivity (FNC) features from a large cohort of ~1500 participants spanning anxiety patients, depressive patients, comorbid cases (with both anxiety and depression), and HCs, we identify refined biotypes for anxiety and depression, as well as two distinct comorbid biotypes that differ in their relative predominance. Subsequently, we characterize the neural substrates of these biotypes by examining their functional connectivity differences and their alterations relative to HCs. These findings offer insights into both biotype-shared and biotype-specific features and advance our understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying comorbid anxiety and depression. We further demonstrate how their symptom patterns relate to the underlying neural differences. In summary, our method addresses a key gap in neuroimaging-based evaluation of diagnostic confusion and comorbidity between anxiety and depression, providing a promising foundation for more personalized and precise diagnostic and treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Data

We utilized resting-state fMRI data from 502 patients with major depressive disorder, 245 patients with anxiety disorder, and 177 comorbid patients from the UK Biobank project (Miller et al., 2016), and employed the FNC features in the study. Specifically, group assignments were based on the UK Biobank mental health-related field 41270. These FNC features included in the field ID 25753 were downloaded directly from the UK Biobank repository and were generated using the standardized and publicly available UK Biobank brain imaging preprocessing pipeline (Alfaro-Almagro et al., 2018). The data were processed using group independent component analysis, resulting in 55 brain functional networks and their corresponding time courses for each subject. The Supplementary Fig. S1 and Table S1 include the information of those 55 group-level functional networks. For each individual subject, partial correlations were computed between the time courses of every pair of functional networks. Following Fisher’s r-to-z transformation, a 55 × 55 FNC matrix representing inter-network connectivity was obtained. Due to the symmetry of the matrix, the upper triangular 1485 elements of the FNC matrix were extracted as the FNC features for each subject. Notably, no thresholding was applied to the FNC matrices, and all 1485 connections were retained for subsequent analyses. This standardized pipeline and the derived FNC metrics have been extensively validated and employed in numerous large-scale studies, confirming their reliability for population neuroscience research (Smith et al., 2020; Di Biase et al., 2023; Du et al., 2025b).

Additionally, we used resting-state fMRI data from 500 HCs from the UK Biobank project as the control group and obtained the corresponding FNC features. The HCs were selected to minimize between-group differences in gender and age relative to the depression, anxiety, and comorbid patient groups. As included in Table 1, Chi-square tests for gender and analysis of variance for age confirmed that there were no significant differences among the groups, ensuring a reliable analysis of the neural substrates among them.

Methods

Based on the FNC features obtained, we enhanced a powerful noisy label learning method called Promix (Xiao et al., 2023) to construct a robust deep learning model for identifying biotypes across depression, anxiety, and their comorbidity. During training, the model leveraged its advanced noisy label learning capability to mitigate confounding effects inherent in clinical diagnostic labels. Once trained, the model can assess the severity of both anxiety and depression in new patients, enabling the delineation of four distinct biotypes, each characterized by varying degrees of anxiety or depression predominance. Our method consists of the following four steps: (i) We performed the soft labeling by converting the original hard label of each sample into a two-dimensional soft label reflecting the initial predominance inferred from prior clinical information. (ii) The deep learning model was pre-trained using the training set samples, enabling it to acquire preliminary feature-learning and classification abilities. (iii) The pre-trained model was used to filter the training set data according to certain criteria, distinguishing between samples with clean labels and those with noisy labels. After the screening, a semi-supervised learning framework was employed, using different learning strategies for samples with clean labels vs. those with noisy labels to ensure optimal performance. (iv) After training, the robust model processed each new sample and assigned it to the depression biotype (Biotype 1), the anxiety biotype (Biotype 2), the comorbid biotype with an anxiety predominance (Biotype 3–2), or the comorbid biotype with a depression predominance (Biotype 3–1) based on the prediction output.

Initial soft labeling

The data from the three categories were first processed by applying soft labeling. To reflect the initial predominance indicated by prior clinical information, the original hard label of the ith sample was mapped to a two-dimensional soft label yi = [yD, yA]. For the depression and anxiety patient groups, the labels were transformed into soft labels [1.0, 0.0] and [0.0, 1.0], respectively; comorbid patients received a balanced soft label [0.5, 0.5] representing dual-disease activation. The soft labels of these patients were taken as input for the subsequent model training.

After generating the soft labels, a 5-fold cross-validation framework was implemented for model training and prediction on new data. In particular, the entire dataset was split into five folds with a 4:1 train-test ratio, maintaining the class proportions in each subset to reflect the distribution of the full dataset. Models were trained exclusively on the training set across five runs and evaluated on the held-out test fold.

Pre-training the model

As shown in Fig. 1, the training set was first used to pre-train the model, ensuring that the model started the formal training with an initial degree of stability and reliability. The model consisted of a backbone network (a four-layer multilayer perceptron) and two independent output layers (A and B). During the pre-training phase, only the backbone and the output layer A were activated for learning. For each training sample, features extracted by the backbone network were passed solely to the output layer A to produce probability distribution. In the pre-training process, the loss function called Lwarmup was computed by comparing the model’s predicted distribution with the soft labels, and this loss was then used for backpropagation to update the model. Formulated by (1)–(3), Lwarmup consisted of two parts: the standard cross-entropy Lx and a regularization penalty Lreg defined as the negative entropy of the output distribution. Here, yi is the soft label of the ith sample, pi is the predicted probability distribution of output layer A for the same sample, and C is the sample number.

Pre-training framework.

Robust model construction based on noisy label learning

Based on the initial model obtained from pre-training, the training set data were filtered, as shown in Fig. 2 for the overall screening process. The screening process was divided into two stages: class-balanced small-loss screening (CSS) and high-confidence matching-based cleaning screening (MHCS). Initially, the CSS was applied to the training set samples to obtain preliminary clean-labeled samples and the remaining samples. Subsequently, the remaining samples after CSS were further screened using MHCS. The clean-labeled samples obtained from MHCS were then combined with the preliminary clean-labeled samples from CSS. The remaining samples after this combined screening were regarded as samples with noisy labels.

Framework for identifying and separating clean vs. noisy labeled samples.

First, the basic CSS screening was conducted. Based on the probability distribution output by output layer A of the model, the losses for depression and anxiety samples were calculated separately for each category Lx. Subsequently, the samples of depression and anxiety were sorted and screened separately according to their losses in ascending order. For each category, a certain proportion of samples with small losses were selected as clean samples. Here, n represents the total number of all samples, C denotes the number of categories, and R indicates the selection ratio. In the initial screening using class-balanced small-loss, we only screened the samples of anxiety and depression to ensure that the model prioritizes the establishment of the core distinguishing features of depression and anxiety. This significantly alleviated the risk of feature extraction contamination in the early training stage due to the more complex imaging features of comorbid samples. Compared to comorbid samples, non-comorbid samples have more heterogeneous features. We aim to learn more distinctive features from non-comorbid samples so that the model can better output and analyze comorbid samples.

Subsequently, to extract as many clean-labeled samples as possible, a secondary screening based on high-confidence matching was conducted on the remaining samples after the initial CSS. This secondary screening involved all three types of samples, including selecting a certain proportion of comorbid samples as clean samples for learning, with the expectation that the model would be able to distinguish between common and heterogeneous features. Specifically, high-confidence matching was based on the model’s confidence in its predictions for each sample, determining whether the confidence was greater than a given threshold and whether the model’s prediction matched the given label. Samples that met these criteria were merged into the clean set. Unlike traditional confidence value calculations, we employed different methods for calculating the confidence values of different types of samples in accordance with the research objective of this paper. Specifically, for depression and anxiety samples, the probability values corresponding to their respective dimensions in the model’s output layer were directly used as the basis for confidence assessment. For comorbid samples, a novel confidence measure was introduced. The confidence level of dual-pathology co-activation was quantified by calculating the Manhattan distance between the prediction vector and the ideal comorbid activation state.

After the screening, different learning strategies were applied to the clean samples and the remaining samples, as shown in Fig. 3 for the semi-supervised learning process of the model. For clean samples, output layer A generated the probability distribution pi, based on which the cross-entropy loss was computed, likewise, output layer B generated and computed its own loss , with each performing backpropagation separately.

Semi-supervised learning framework for leveraging labeled and unlabeled data.

For the remaining samples, which were treated as unlabeled data, we used the primary output A to generate pseudo-labels. Pseudo-labels were derived by processing the probability distribution from output A through a sharpen function (Berthelot et al., 2019). This function enhanced the certainty of the distribution by forcing it toward a one-hot form, with its temperature parameter T set to the commonly used value of 0.1. Then, we calculated Lx between the output of output layer B and the pseudo-labels to obtain LuB, and performed backpropagation for output layer B. This part of the loss is weighted by a factor λu, which linearly increased from 0 to 0.6 over a period lasting twice the total number of epochs allocated for pre-training.

Identifying depression and anxiety biotypes as well as the comorbidity biotypes with distinct disorder predominance

Following model training on the 4-fold training subsets and subsequent application to the corresponding held-out test set in each cross-validation run, patients were assigned to one of four categories: Biotype 1, Biotype 2, Biotype 3–1, and Biotype 3–2. The determination was made based on the model’s two-dimensional probability output y = [yD, yA] obtained through the softmax function. If yD exceeded a predefined threshold of 0.65, the sample was assigned to Biotype 1, which represents the depression biotype. If yA exceeded the same threshold, it was assigned to Biotype 2, which corresponds to the anxiety biotype. For samples where both probabilities were below 0.65, further classification was performed: if yD > yA, the sample was labeled as Biotype 3–1, indicating a comorbid biotype with a stronger depression predominance; otherwise, it was labeled as Biotype 3–2 representing a comorbid biotype with a stronger anxiety predominance.

Parameter setting

To determine the optimal hyperparameters, including the learning rate and batch size, we employed a grid search method based on inner 5-fold cross-validation within each training data set. The specific calculation procedure was as follows: (i) After training the model on the inner training data within the inner 5-fold cross-validation framework, three clustering indices were computed to examine the quality of the resulting biotypes on the corresponding inner test data. The three clustering indices include the Silhouette index (Rousseeuw, 1987), the Davies–Bouldin (DB) index (Davies and Bouldin, 2009), and the Calinski–Harabasz (CH) index (Caliński and Harabasz, 1974). (ii) The DB index was inverted to align its direction with the Silhouette and CH indices (larger value indicates better clustering performance). All three indices were subjected to min–max normalization. (iii) The arithmetic mean of the three normalized indices was computed and used as the final composite clustering metric. (iv) The hyperparameter combination that achieved the highest averaged composite metric value across inner five runs was selected as the optimal configuration for model retraining on the whole training data (within the outer 5-fold cross-validation).

In ProMix, the CSS strategy introduces a balance parameter to control the ratio of selecting samples with small training losses. In this study, this parameter was conservatively fixed at the default value of 0.5 as per the original implementation. This setting implies that only the 50% of samples with the smallest loss values in each selection round are considered reliable. From a theoretical perspective on noise robustness, a dataset with over 50% label noise is typically regarded as exceedingly poor in quality and generally unsuitable for model training. Furthermore, the original authors reported that this default setting exhibits empirical robustness across diverse datasets. Accordingly, following the original ProMix framework, we adopted this default configuration without additional tuning.

Evaluation

Evaluating the model effectiveness and reliability

We comprehensively evaluated the effectiveness and reliability of the models produced by our method. First, we demonstrated the loss curves during the training processes to validate the model convergence. We then applied t-SNE to visualize the extracted features together with the predicted classes of the remaining test data for each model. Since our framework adopted a 5-fold cross-validation, all results were reported separately for each of the five models, each trained on a different training set.

Revealing functional changes of biotypes relative to HCs as well as functional changes of original diagnostic classes relative to HCs

To further characterize the neurobiological signatures of the identified biotypes, we examined their FNC alterations relative to HCs. For each FNC, a two-sample t-test was performed to compare HC and each biotype, including Biotype 1, Biotype 2, Biotype 3–1, and Biotype 3–2. Bonferroni (BFN) correction at P < 0.05 was then applied to identify significant FNC differences. Similarly, we assessed FNC alterations for the original diagnostic classes i.e. depression, anxiety, and comorbidity, relative to HCs. Comparisons between HCs and each patient group were conducted using two-sample t-tests on FNCs under a consistent multiple-comparison correction threshold.

Elucidating functional differences across identified biotypes and original diagnostic classes

To validate the biological relevance of the identified biotypes, we examined group differences among the depression biotype (Biotype 1), the anxiety biotype (Biotype 2), the comorbid biotype with depression predominance (Biotype 3–1), and the comorbid biotype with anxiety predominance (Biotype 3–2). Specifically, we conducted two-sample t-tests to compare FNCs between Biotype 1 and Biotype 2, between Biotype 1 and Biotype 3–2, and between Biotype 3–1 and Biotype 2. For completeness, we also examined the original diagnostic categories by performing two-sample t-tests to evaluate FNC differences between depression and anxiety, between depression and comorbidity, and between anxiety and comorbidity. Because none of these results survived multiple comparison correction, we summarized the identified FNCs at the threshold of P < 0.001.

Furthermore, we systematically evaluated the FC changes that overlapped between Biotype 2 and Biotype 3–2 relative to Biotype 1, based on the FNCs showing group differences in both Biotype 1 vs. Biotype 2 and Biotype 1 vs. Biotype 3–2 comparisons. For each of these FNCs, we presented the mean FC strengths for the three groups (i.e. Biotype 1, Biotype 2, Biotype 3–2). In addition, we investigated the FC patterns shared by Biotype 1 and Biotype 3–1 when contrasted with Biotype 2. This analysis relied on the FNCs exhibiting significant differences in Biotype 1 vs. Biotype 2 and Biotype 3–1 vs. Biotype 2 comparisons. The results from the targeted pairwise comparisons indicated significant differences in functional connectivity across the biotypes, suggesting that the subtyping method possesses good discriminative validity, which is consistent with our expectation. For these FNCs, we similarly demonstrated the mean FC strengths for Biotype 1, Biotype 2, and Biotype 3–1.

Evaluation of clinical symptoms associated with the biotypes

We further analyzed the clinical symptoms of participants belonging to each biotype, aiming to characterize their distinct clinical profiles and to understand how these symptom patterns relate to the underlying neural differences. We employed the individual-level information from the Composite International Diagnostic Interview–Short Form (8-item) (CIDI-SF-8) and Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) (Kessler et al., 1998; Spitzer et al., 2006; Davis et al., 2020) questionnaires to conduct a comparative analysis. The CIDI-SF-8 is widely used in large-scale population surveys and research contexts to identify individuals who are likely to meet the criteria for major depression. The GAD-7 has demonstrated strong reliability and validity and is commonly used to identify individuals with possible generalized anxiety disorder. Based on these instruments, a total depression score was computed for each participant by averaging the eight CIDI-SF-8 items, and a total anxiety score was derived by averaging the seven GAD-7 items. Subsequently, the composite depression score was compared across Biotype 1, Biotype 3–1, and Biotype 3–2, while the composite anxiety score was compared across Biotype 2, Biotype 3–1, and Biotype 3–2.

Results

The model effectiveness and reliability

Fig. 4a shows the loss curves for the five models, each built on a separate training dataset. Overall, the curves exhibited an initial brief increase followed by a steady decline, indicating good convergence. The trend and speed of the convergence were consistent across different runs. Fig. 4b displays the t-SNE projection results of the test dataset across the five runs in the 5-fold cross-validation. In each run, the results consistently revealed that Biotype 1 and Biotype 2 were well separated, while Biotype 3–1 clustered closely with Biotype 1, and Biotype 3–2 clustered closely with Biotype 2. This structure further supports that the anxiety biotype and the depression biotype are distinct from each other, whereas comorbid individuals with depression predominance resemble the depression biotype, and those with anxiety predominance resemble the anxiety biotype.

Functional differences between HC and biotypes are more evident than that between HC and original disorder classes

Fig 5a shows group differences obtained from two-sample t-tests comparing HCs with each disorder group, and Fig. 5b and c presents the corresponding results after BFN correction at P < 0.05. Three FNCs showed significant differences between HCs and the depression group, four FNCs differed significantly between HCs and the anxiety group, and no significant FNC differences were observed between HCs and the comorbidity group. Among those FNCs, only the FNC between the left inferior parietal lobule and the right middle frontal gyrus was evident for both the HC vs. depression and HC vs. anxiety comparisons, indicating a single, overlapped neural change in the original categories. Fig. 5d–f demonstrate the group differences between HCs and each biotype, including both before and after correction. There are 3, 19, 2, and 2 FNCs that showed significant differences between HCs and Biotype 1, between HCs and Biotype 2, between HCs and Biotype 3–1, and between HCs and Biotype 3–2, respectively. Biotypes exhibited more numerous and larger functional brain alterations relative to HCs than those observed in the original diagnostic categories. Importantly, the identified Biotype 1 and Biotype 2 shared no common alterations, supporting a clearer biological significance. In contrast, Biotype 3–1 and Biotype 1 shared similar alteration in one FNC between left superior medial frontal gyrus and left paracentral lobule, implying that the pathological features of depression and comorbidity with a tendency toward depression are associated with specific functions related to self-awareness and motor control. Multiple resting-state fMRI studies have demonstrated that, compared with HCs, patients with depression exhibit significant functional connectivity alterations, with the medial prefrontal cortex being one of the most prominently affected nodes (Yang et al., 2021). These connectivity abnormalities are closely associated with impaired emotion regulation, increased self-reflection, and heightened negative self-focus, and are considered key neurobiological features of depression. Some studies have observed reduced functional connectivity between the left paracentral lobule and motor- and visual-related regions in patients with depression, suggesting a potential role of this region in the functional network abnormalities associated with depression (Luo et al., 2022). Interestingly, Biotype 3–2 and Biotype 2 shared similar alterations in two FNCs: one between the right crus I of the cerebellum and the left inferior temporal gyrus, and another between the left inferior parietal lobule and the right middle frontal gyrus. This pattern suggests that anxiety and anxiety-predominant comorbidity are associated with impaired emotional regulation, attentional control, and multisensory integration. Previous neuroimaging studies support the involvement of cerebellar–temporal and frontoparietal networks in anxiety-related psychopathology. The cerebellar Crus I, through its extensive connections with prefrontal, temporal, and parietal cortices, plays an important role in emotional regulation and higher-order cognitive processing, and its functional connectivity has been shown to be altered in anxiety disorder (Yuan et al., 2017; Schmahmann, 2019). The inferior temporal gyrus is involved in visual and multisensory processing as well as affective perception, with abnormal connectivity reported in anxiety-related conditions (Liao et al., 2010). In addition, the inferior parietal lobule and middle frontal gyrus are key components of frontoparietal control networks supporting attentional control and executive function, and functional disruptions within these circuits are consistently observed in anxiety disorders (Etkin et al., 2009; Sylvester et al., 2012).

Biotypes exhibit greater functional differentiation than the original diagnostic classes

Fig. 6 illustrates the group differences obtained from two-sample t-tests across the original disorder classes and across the biotypes. Specifically, Fig. 6a displays the group differences for depression vs. anxiety, depression vs. comorbidity, and anxiety vs. comorbidity, while the results surviving P < 0.001 are shown in Fig. 6b. It is seen that only one FNC showed a significant difference between depression and anxiety, four FNCs differed significantly between depression and comorbidity, and one FNC differed significantly between anxiety and comorbidity. Fig. 6c presents the group differences between Biotype 1 and Biotype 2, between Biotype 1 and Biotype 3–2, and between Biotype 3–1 and Biotype 2. A total of 63, 9, and 24 FNCs survived the P < 0.001 threshold for the comparisons of Biotype 1 vs. Biotype 2, Biotype 1 vs. Biotype 3–2, and Biotype 3–1 vs. Biotype 2, respectively. These results indicate that the biotypes exhibited a greater number of FNC differences compared with those observed in the original diagnostic categories. More interestingly, the differences between Biotype 1 and Biotype 3–2, as well as between Biotype 3–1 and Biotype 2, partially resembled Biotype 1 vs. Biotype 2 differences in specific FNCs, reflecting the neural mechanism of our identified depression, anxiety, and comorbidity biotypes.

Moreover, Fig. 7 summarizes the unique neural characteristics of different biotypes. For the seven FNCs that differed between Biotype 1 and both Biotype 2 and Biotype 3–2 (Fig. 7a), Biotype 2 and Biotype 3–2 generally exhibited similar FC strengths that were distinct from Biotype 1, indicating shared functional alterations relative to Biotype 1. The divergent FNCs predominantly involved networks related to cognitive control, visual processing, sensorimotor function, and cerebellar regions. An examination of the specific connections, as detailed in Table 2, reveals that the observed alterations centered on interactions between the cognitive control network and visual/sensorimotor regions (e.g. IC14-IC9, IC30-IC5), as well as within the cognitive control network itself (IC26-IC32). This pattern underscores a divergent functional integration in Biotype 1, particularly in circuits supporting visuospatial processing and executive functions. Furthermore, the identified FNCs included key cerebellar connections, such as those linking auditory and cerebellar regions (IC3-IC24) and cerebellar with sensorimotor areas (IC58-IC7), which are implicated in sensorimotor coordination and prediction. The presence of a within-network sensorimotor connection (IC7-IC28) further highlights alterations in primary motor execution pathways. Collectively, this specific connectivity profile suggests that Biotype 1 is characterized by a unique configuration of large-scale networks, especially involving cognitive-visual integration and cerebellar-motor loops, which distinguishes it from the other two biotypes.

Comorbidity biotypes exhibit greater symptom severity

As shown in Fig. 8, clinical scores on the CIDI-SF-8 were higher in both Biotype 1 and Biotype 3–1 compared to Biotype 3–2, with Biotype 3–1 exhibiting the most severe depressive symptoms. Conversely, on the GAD-7, Biotype 3–1 scored significantly lower than both Biotype 2 and Biotype 3–2, which showed elevated anxiety symptoms. These patterns suggest that Biotype 1 and Biotype 3–1 are more closely associated with depressive symptomatology, whereas Biotype 2 and Biotype 3–2 align more strongly with anxiety-related features. Notably, Biotype 3–1 showed the highest depression score and Biotype 3–2 the highest anxiety score, indicating that the comorbid subtypes (depression–anxiety) may represent more severe or distinct clinical profiles compared to the predominantly single-domain subtypes.

Discussion and conclusion

Anxiety and depression are among the most prevalent and disabling mental disorders worldwide. Their substantial symptomatic overlap and high comorbidity frequently complicate diagnosis in clinical settings (Brown et al., 2001; Kessler et al., 2005; Wittchen et al., 2011; Zbozinek et al., 2012; Chisholm et al., 2016). While existing neuroimaging studies have advanced a transdiagnostic understanding by comparing these disorders (Oathes et al., 2015; LeDoux and Pine, 2016; Maggioni et al., 2019; Sindermann et al., 2021; Guo et al., 2025), they largely treated comorbid patients as a homogeneous group (Pollack, 2005), overlooking their inherent heterogeneity and the potential misclassification arising from overlapping symptoms. To address these limitations, this study proposed a novel noisy label learning framework on resting-state fMRI data from a large cohort to account for diagnostic uncertainty and derive data-driven biotypes. We identified not only distinct anxiety and depression biotypes but also two separable comorbid biotypes differentiated by their relative disorder predominance, thereby elucidating shared and unique neural substrates and linking them to specific clinical profiles.

The constructed model under the noisy label learning strategy demonstrated strong validity and robustness, supported by two key pieces of evidence from the 5-fold cross-validation. First, the loss curves for all five independently trained models showed a consistent and steady decline, indicating stable and successful convergence across all training runs. Second and more significantly, the t-SNE visualizations of the test data revealed a highly reproducible and interpretable clustering pattern in each fold: Biotype 1 (depression biotype) and Biotype 2 (anxiety biotype) are distinctly separated, while Biotype 3–1 (comorbidity–depression predominance) reliably clustered with Biotype 1, and Biotype 3–2 (comorbidity–anxiety predominance) clusters with Biotype 2. This consistent geometric structure in the learned feature space strongly validated the model’s ability to not only distinguish core depression and anxiety biotypes but also meaningfully align comorbid individuals with their predominant symptomatic profile, thereby confirming the underlying neural and phenotypic relationships proposed by the study.

The neural significance of our identified biotypes was underscored by their ability to reveal a far more detailed and biologically coherent map of functional brain alterations than the original diagnostic categories. Notably, the biotyping framework uncovered substantially more numerous and pronounced FNC differences both relative to HCs and in between-group comparisons. Our findings resonate with recent circuit-based biotyping study (Tozzi et al., 2024) in depression and anxiety, which has demonstrated that neurobiologically grounded subtypes provide substantially greater explanatory power than conventional diagnostic categories. In contrast to this study based on conventional feature extraction and clustering, our work leverages an advanced noisy label learning framework to more effectively capture complex and heterogeneous neuroimaging patterns. Extending this line of work, our results show that biotyping based on FNC yields a more fine-grained and biologically coherent map of brain alterations, revealing numerous and robust connectivity differences both relative to HCs and across biotypes. Notably, the refined depression and anxiety biotypes exhibited completely non-overlapping FNC alterations, indicating a clear neural dissociation that is obscured under traditional diagnostic frameworks. The pattern of shared and distinct FNCs such as the seven connections differentiating the depression biotype from both anxiety-related biotypes, and the sixteen connections differentiating the anxiety biotype from both depression-related biotypes primarily involved key networks for cognitive control, sensorimotor processing, visual integration, and cerebellar function. Importantly, the comorbid biotypes were not neurobiologically homogeneous; instead, they showed structured alignment with their respective core depression and anxiety spectra at the circuit level, thereby providing a mechanistic substrate for the observed clinical heterogeneity and a robust neural basis for precise subtyping. Specifically, the depression-predominant comorbidity biotype shared connectivity alterations with the depression biotype in circuits implicated in self-referential processing and motor control, whereas the anxiety-predominant comorbidity biotype shared alterations with the anxiety biotype in circuits supporting emotional regulation, attentional control, and multisensory integration. This pattern of shared and distinct network alterations suggests that comorbidity reflects extensions along core neurobiological dimensions rather than a simple additive mixture of disorders, further supporting the neurobiological validity of the identified biotypes. Together, these findings provide a mechanistic account for the marked clinical heterogeneity observed in comorbid depression and anxiety and establish a robust neural foundation for biologically informed subtyping and precision intervention.

The analysis of symptom severity across the identified biotypes revealed a distinct and clinically meaningful pattern of depression and anxiety features. Our findings support that Biotype 1 and Biotype 3–1 were characterized by more prominent depressive symptomatology than Biotype 3–2, as evidenced by their significantly higher scores on the CIDI-SF-8 for depression. Conversely, Biotype 2 and Biotype 3–2 were associated with more severe anxiety symptoms than Biotype 3–1, reflected in their elevated scores on the GAD-7. This clear separation supports the validity of the biotyping approach in distinguishing between symptom-driven phenotypic clusters, even within a transdiagnostic framework. A particularly salient finding is the differential severity observed within the comorbid biotypes. Biotype 3–1, representing a comorbidity–depression profile, demonstrated the highest level of depressive symptom severity. In contrast, Biotype 3–2, representing a comorbidity–anxiety profile, exhibited the highest level of anxiety symptom severity. This suggests that when comorbidity is present, the clinical presentation is not simply an additive mix of moderate symptoms but may be dominated by a primary symptomatic dimension of exceptional severity, consistent to previous studies (Dong et al., 2020; Kalin, 2020). The overall greater symptom severity in these comorbid biotypes (Biotype 3–1 and Biotype 3–2) compared to the “pure” depression (Biotype 1) and anxiety (Biotype 2) biotypes underscores the substantial clinical burden associated with comorbid conditions and highlights them as high-priority targets for intervention. Consistent with our findings, recent work (Tozzi et al., 2024) using personalized brain circuit scores has identified clinically meaningful biotypes that cut across depression and anxiety, demonstrating that comorbid biotypes are characterized by more severe symptom profiles, distinct circuit-level dysfunctions, and differential treatment responses, thereby highlighting their elevated clinical burden and relevance for stratified intervention.

Overall, given the complexity in depression and anxiety, our study proposed a deep learning-based noisy label learning method that identified neurobiologically coherent biotypes with clearer functional boundaries than conventional diagnostic categories. Our approach is potential for disentangling the psychiatric comorbidity, paving the way for more precise disease subtyping and personalized intervention strategies. Future work will aim to validate this framework in larger and more diverse cohorts, extend its applicability to additional psychiatric disorders, and further refine the model into a clinically actionable tool to support diagnostic decision-making and stratified treatment planning.
