Study on the Cyclic Impact Mechanical Characteristics of Coal under Confining Pressure
Authors: He Yongliang, Sun Liying, Xing Xuegang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoal damage accumulation and strength deterioration caused by mining-induced disturbances in deep mines are among the factors influencing the occurrence of dynamic disasters such as rock bursts. To study the mechanical deformation and failure characteristics of coal masses under both cyclic impact and confining pressure, SHPB experiments were performed to systematically analyze the behavior of coal samples under 1, 2, and 3 cycles at impact pressures of 0.25, 0.30, 0.35, 0.40, and 0.45 MPa. To study the influence of pressure and impact frequency on the dynamic mechanical failure of coal samples, a weakening effect model of coal samples under confining pressure was established, revealing the dynamic mechanical characteristics and failure mechanism of coal samples under different impact pressures and impact frequencies. The confining pressure SHPB results reveal that the number of cycles and impact pressure are inversely proportional to the peak stress and are proportional to the degree of weakening. The peak stress weakening coefficient of the coal samples under the different impact pressures ranged from 28.5 to 73.2%, and a linear weakening relationship with the number of cycles was obtained. The coal exhibited an end effect-controlled Y-shaped failure mode under both confining pressure and dynamic loading. This study provides an experimental reference for preventing the energy absorption and erosion of weak structures around rock bursts and improving the stability of supporting structures.

Introduction

The coal occurrence conditions in China are complex.1 As the mining depth of coal mines has continued to increase, coal mining has increased in depth, and more than 90% of coal is mined underground.2 With increasing coal mining depth, coal and rock samples experience a complex mechanical environment of “three highs and one disturbance”3,4 (high geostress, high geotemperature, high water pressure, and strong dynamic disturbances such as blasting and mechanical excavation). Under the coupling and superposition of high static loads and strong dynamic loads deep underground, a coal–rock mass is in a three-dimensional combined dynamic and static stress-bearing state. Cracks in coal–rock bodies propagate and develop, damage to coal bodies continues to worsen, and dynamic disasters such as rock bursts,5 roof cavings,6 large roadway deformations,7 and coal and gas outbursts8 are becoming increasingly severe in mechanically complex deep environments. The above disasters and influencing factors seriously threaten the safe and efficient mining of coal mines. After the original stresses of coal and rock are redistributed due to initial mining activities, the influence of constantly changing mining activities on geostresses is much greater than that on the original geostresses, leading to coal and rock deformation and cracking instability. Changes in cracks and fissures in coal are affected by the stress caused by mining.9,10 To maintain the stability of roadways and working face roofs, monitoring and predicting the deterioration of coal damage under dynamic loading and determining the dynamic mechanical failure characteristics and damage evolution process of coal samples under different impact pressures and impact cycles under confining pressure are necessary. One of the main areas of research on the prevention and control of roadway impact dynamic disasters and roof impact accidents is the dynamic failure law of coal samples and the influence of impact frequency on coal body cracks. Studying the dynamic failure patterns and processes of coal samples under different impact pressures and impact cycles under confining pressure plays a very important role in preventing rock bursts and stabilizing roadway support.

Many experts and scholars have conducted extensive research on the dynamic and static mechanical characteristics, failure patterns, and crack evolution characteristics of coal samples, obtained the mechanical laws of coal samples under different conditions, and achieved fruitful results.11−14

The mechanical properties of coal vary with changes in the stress state and loading conditions.26−29 Different impact pressures under confining pressure generate different strain rates, which significantly affect the dynamic strength and deformation characteristics of coal samples. The SHPB test objects were mostly rock, concrete, coal and other materials. There have been few studies on the dynamic mechanical properties of a coal mass under cyclic impact. In this study, with a Φ75 mm SHPB test system, impact compression tests were conducted at different strain rates on coal samples under the conditions of imposed radial and confining pressures on the coal body, and the effects of the confining pressure and different impact pressures on the stresses in the coal samples were analyzed. The stress–strain curves, branch stresses and strains, failure modes, dynamic compressive strengths, and relationships between different impact pressures (different impact velocities) and different impact frequencies were obtained. This study is highly important for studying the mechanical properties and failure modes of complex coal samples under impact conditions.

Experimental Procedures

Coal Sample Preparation

A large complete coal sample from the Changcun Coal Mine of Henan Dayou Energy Co., Ltd., was used as the base material. The coal sample was cored perpendicular to the bedding planes, cut and ground to obtain cylindrical coal samples with a diameter of 50 mm and a height of 50 mm. The bulk coal without gangue bands and obvious cracks was chosen as the raw material, as shown in Figure 1. In accordance with international rock mechanics test codes, to ensure that the unevenness of the two end surfaces of a sample was less than 0.05 mm and that the nonparallelism of the two end surfaces was greater than 0.02 mm, after each coal sample was prepared, the distance between the two ends of the sample was checked before the mass, density and other physical parameters were measured.30,31 To reduce the effect of sample dispersion on the test results, the wave velocity of the samples was measured via a nonmetallic ultrasonic detector, and coal samples with obvious damage and a wave velocity deviation greater than 10% were excluded. SHPB Experimental System

An SHPB experimental system with confining pressure was used to carry out repeated impact dynamic loading experiments under different impact velocities to investigate the relationships between the dynamic strength, peak strain and macroscopic fracture morphology of a coal sample and the impact velocity and confining pressure of the test setup. The test system included a loading system, a velocity measuring system, a compression bar system and a data acquisition system.32,33 The loading system included a high-pressure nitrogen cylinder, an air pressure control valve, a high-pressure gas chamber, and an emission chamber. The speed measurement system included a speed measurement circuit, a parallel light source, and a timer. The compression rod system was composed of an impact rod, an incident rod, a transmission rod, and an energy-absorbing device (Figure 2). The data acquisition system was composed of strain gauges, a junction bridge box, dynamic strain gauges, an oscillometric recorder, etc.34,35

SHPB experimental system.

Propagation Theory of Stress Waves

In the SHPB setup, the cyclic impact crushing test of coal is achieved through the following process: Driven by high-pressure gas, the incident bar generates an incident pulse upon collision. By controlling the impact pressure and the number of cycles, the SHPB impact crushing test is conducted. By varying the number of cycles on the specimen, the crushing characteristics of the coal sample under different impact cycles are obtained. When the incident wave reaches both ends of the specimen, the stress wave is repeatedly reflected and transmitted, causing the specimen to deform at high speed. The stress wave is transmitted through the specimen to the transmission bar, and all signals are collected by strain gauges on the incident bar and transmission bar via the signal acquisition system. After the test, the mechanical properties curve and corresponding parameters of the specimen can be obtained by further calculating the stress wave signals in the incident and transmission bars.

Testing Method

On the basis of geostress tests in coal mines and considering the experimental results and actual conditions, the experiment was performed on a control group under both confining pressure and dynamic loading with a confining pressure of 10 MPa and axial pressure of 12 MPa. The impact air pressure was adjusted to impact the incident rod at different speeds. Impact loads with increasing intensities were applied to the coal samples. The impact pressures were set to 0.25, 0.30, 0.35, 0.40, and 0.45 MPa, and each impact pressure was applied 3 to 4 times. The preparations before the test included the following steps: (1) The equipment was checked to ensure that it was in good condition before installing the coal sample. (2) Confining pressure dynamic loading test: The confining pressure was applied to the sample, and the confining pressure was increased by increasing the oil pressure in the confining pressure device, which was convenient and simple. (3) The data were recorded, and the mechanical properties of the coal samples were analyzed. Figure 4 shows the test process.

Experimental Results

Stress–Strain Curves

For the stress–strain curves discussed here, the abscissa represents the dynamic strain, which is obtained from the electrical signals collected by the strain gauges and processed by eqs 1 and 3; the ordinate represents the dynamic stress. The sample was first loaded with 12 MPa axial compression and 10 MPa confining pressure prestress, and then a quasistatic load was applied. Afterward, the dynamic strain gauge was rebalanced. Therefore, the collected strains did not include the compressive deformation of the sample under prestress and were the dynamic strains rather than the total strains of the sample.37

A typical stress–strain curve under both confining pressure and dynamic loading is divided into four stages,38,39 as shown in Figure 5. The OA stage is the first stage, i.e., the linear elastic stage or coal sample compaction stage, in which the coal sample does not fail under the initial impact; instead, the pore structure of the coal sample is compacted under impact, and the stress and strain increase linearly. The AB stage is the second stage, i.e., the elastic stage, in which the slope of the stress–strain curve is a straight line and the coal sample experiences elastic deformation and failure due to compaction. The BC stage is the third stage, i.e., the yield stage. As the load continues to increase, new cracks appear in the coal samples, and plastic changes increase significantly; the cracks further propagate and extend under the action of dynamic loading. The coal samples begin to fail completely in this stage. The CD stage is the fourth stage, i.e., the complete failure stage, in which the cracks in the coal samples further develop under impact, and the strength of the coal sample decreases after reaching the peak stress.

Typical stress–strain curve under dynamic loading with confining pressure.

Under the same number of cycles, as the impact pressure increases, the stress gradually decreases. When the impact pressure is the same, the stress gradually decreases as the number of impacts increases. When the number of cycles was 2, the peak stresses were 19.4, 20.2, 19.3, 17.6, and 14.9 MPa when the impact pressures were 0.25, 0.30, 0.35, 0.40, and 0.45 MPa, respectively. When the impact pressure was less than 0.35 MPa, the peak stress fluctuated more, and there was greater dispersion. When the impact pressure was greater than 0.35 MPa, the peak stress gradually decreased with increasing impact pressure. Taking the impact pressure at 0.35 MPa as an example, when the number of cycles was 1, 2, and 3, the peak stresses were 24.5, 19.3, and 14.0 MPa, respectively.

Stress–strain curves of coal samples under dynamic loading with respect to the confining pressure.

Figure 6 shows that when the coal sample was subjected to impact loading, there was no damage to the coal sample, and the coal sample had high impact resistance.40,41 Therefore, the stress–strain curve is a straight line. As the impact cycles increased, the primary pores and cracks in the coal sample compacted, and the stress gradually increased. As the impact continues to develop, the cracks in the coal sample expand under the action of impact kinetic energy, absorbing some of the impact energy. The stress continues to increase, and the coal sample enters the failure stage. As impact cycles continued, the cracks inside the coal sample completely developed, and the coal sample reached complete failure. Under impact, the internal cracks of the coal sample compact and close in a very short time. Owing to the high impact energy, the cracks formed inside further expand, causing complete damage to the coal sample.

Peak Stress

To analyze the changes in the peak stress of coal samples under different impact pressures and impacts, the peak stresses of different impact pressures and impacts were analyzed,42,43 as shown in Figures 7 and 8. Figure 7 shows that there is a relationship between the peak stress and the number of impact cycles. As the number of cycles increased, the peak stress gradually decreased, and the strength of the coal sample gradually decreased. This phenomenon indicates that as the number of impacts increased, cracks in the coal sample gradually developed. As the impact pressure increased, the difference in peak stress between the first and third impacts increased, indicating that the greater the impact pressure is, the greater the number of cycles, and the more severe the damage to the coal sample. Figure 8 shows that during the first cycle, as the impact pressure increases, the strain rate gradually increases, and the peak stress gradually increases. The stress increases with increasing strain rate, which is consistent with the results of a single coal–rock SHPB experiment. Under the action of the second cycles, as the impact pressure increased, the peak stress first increased and then decreased; when the peak stress decreased from an impact stress of 0.25–0.30 MPa, fewer cracks formed in the coal seam sample, and no large cracks formed. When the impact pressure was increased to 0.35 MPa, the number of cracks in the coal increased, and the peak stress decreased. Under the action of the third cycles, as the impact pressure increased, the peak stress gradually decreased, and the number of cracks in the coal body increased; notably, when the impact pressure increased between 0.35 and 0.45 MPa, the stress decreased more quickly. The decreasing trend of the peak stress reflects the decreasing rate of change in the stability of the sample and the number of cracks in the sample.

Strain Rate Effect

The average strain rate of impact dynamic load experiments is an important reference standard for coal sample deformation.44,45 The change in impact stress is a form of experimental variation, and the strain rate is the main influencing factor on the performance of coal samples. The impact pressure is directly proportional to the strain rate, as shown in Figure 9, which shows the relationship between the peak shadow flow and the strain rate. Under the action of the first cycle, the peak stress of the coal sample increased as the strain rate increased, and the ability of the coal sample to withstand external impact gradually increased; under the action of the second and third cycles, the peak stress of the coal sample gradually decreased as the strain rate increased. When the strain rate was low, the ability of the coal sample to resist external impact gradually decreased, and the degree of damage to the coal sample increased. The coal sample has an approximately linear relationship between the peak stress and strain rate, which can be fitted by the following equation: σ = aε1 + b.

Relationship between the elastic modulus and number of cycles.

Considering the porous brittle–plastic material properties of raw coal, the causes of the strain hardening phenomenon in three-dimensional dynamic impact and the evolution pattern of the deformation modulus in the first loading stage in cyclic impact studies were investigated. The deformation modulus in the first loading stage represents the ability of a sample to undergo elastic deformation during a single impact cycle. Since coal is a typical porous cemented body, the compaction of pores and cracks not only improves the impact resistance ability of coal but also increases its ability to undergo elastic deformation. However, as the number of cycles increases, the internal damage of the sample gradually develops, and the corresponding ability to undergo elastic deformation also decreases, which leads to an overall decreasing trend of the deformation modulus in the first loading stage. In addition, the sharp decrease in the elastic modulus in the middle and late stages of cyclic impact is also consistent with the rapid decrease in the cyclic impact strength in the late stage.

Failure Characteristics

The degree of fracture of the coal samples under the action of impact loading was relatively high; the fractured surfaces of the coal sample fragments exhibited rectangular, cone-shaped, and wedge-shaped cross sections, mainly with massive or granular morphologies.48,49 The coal sample did not undergo severe deformation or damage under impact loading, the integrity of the coal sample was high, relatively few macroscopic cracks formed at the surface, and only a small amount of microcracks and granular debris fell off. Three-dimensional static prestress can improve the ability of a coal sample to resist deformation and failure, decreasing its likelihood of failure. This also explains the deformation and failure of the coal mass and roadway-surrounding rocks in a downhole working face. Under the influence of mining, the surrounding coal body gradually changes from three-directional loading to two-way or unidirectional loading.50,51 Coal near excavations has stress unloading surfaces, and in the absence of three-dimensional stress constraints, the coal body is prone to deform and fail toward the stress unloading surface under dynamic load disturbance (Figures 12 and 13).

Failure modes of coal samples under dynamic loading with confining pressure.

Failure mechanism of the coal samples.

Under the action of confining pressure and dynamic loading, the samples experience interface friction and end effects. Under the action of a dynamic load, the lateral deformation is limited, and tension cracking failure does not occur. The stress wave is reflected and transmitted at the microcracks, and the stress wave acts on the coal sample. Failure occurs at the contact point. Stress waves are reflected at the inclined cracks of coal samples, and the reflected waves propagate along the cracks. The strength factor of the crack tip ultimately reaches the fracture toughness, and the cracks propagate to trigger macroscopic failure.52−54 Thus, under the action of confining pressure and dynamic loading, the coal samples exhibit an approximately Y-shaped failure mode.

Under triaxial compression, the failure mode of a coal sample is single shear failure, which is limited by the confining pressure. The confining pressure process is equivalent to imposing a tensile stress on the coal sample in the radial direction, which weakens the limitation of the confining pressure on the development of fractures in the coal sample. However, since the confining pressure does not decrease to zero, the confining pressure still has certain restrictions on the coal sample. Therefore, coal samples under confining pressure exhibit failure modes ranging from single shear failure to dominant shear failure with some splitting failure. This failure morphology reflects conditions between uniaxial and conventional triaxial compression.

Discussion

Under the influence of mining activities, the structure of the coal–rock body changes, which leads to the redistribution of the original rock stresses and stress zoning.55,56 If the coal–rock mass in the stress concentration area has certain physical properties (e.g., impact tendency), its stress reaches a critical magnitude for failure; then, the coal–rock body experiences instability and failure, and the stress decreases, possibly resulting in the occurrence of rock bursts in the coal mine. Structural factors, physical factors, stress factors and energy factors strongly affect the formation of rock bursts. These four factors influence and constrain each other, and under their combined action and certain conditions, a rock burst will occur.

There is a static equilibrium stress field in the rock surrounding the deep roadways of a coal mine. The stress waves generated by mining act on the rock surrounding the roadway to generate a dynamic stress field, and the dynamic load and the static load are superimposed.57 (1) When there is a high static stress field in the roadway, under the action of a large-energy stress wave, when the superposed load exceeds the limit load that induces failure of the rock surrounding the roadway, the surrounding rock fails, and the stress wave is instantaneous and impactful. The roadway is very likely to suffer sudden instability, resulting in an impact disaster. (2) When the stress wave energy is low, the rock surrounding the roadway may not undergo macroscopic failure, but the cumulative fatigue damage to the roadway can increase. The propagation of stress waves can be divided into longitudinal waves and shear waves, where longitudinal waves have compressive and tensile effects and shear waves have shearing effects.

Dynamic disturbance can aggravate coal damage and failure, and the larger the original axial static load is, the lower the confining pressure, the larger the dynamic disturbance amplitude, the longer the wavelength, the more severe the coal damage under the disturbance, and the faster the decrease in the compressive strength and elastic energy storage limit. During the underground mining process, the surrounding rock or coal pillars of the roadway accumulate a large amount of elastic strain energy under the action of a high load. Under the influence of mining disturbance or blasting vibration, damage to high-stress coal accumulates and expands, the compressive strength decreases, the limit of elastic energy storage decreases, and the elastic energy originally stored in the coal is released instantaneously, resulting in dynamic disasters such as rock bursts. The larger the original vertical stress (axial pressure) of the coal body is, the lower the horizontal stress (confining pressure), the larger the mining disturbance amplitude and the longer the wavelength, the more elastic the strain energy released by the coal mass induced by the dynamic disturbance, and the greater the probability of ground shock occurring.

The stress environment of an underground rock mass can be considered a function of three-dimensional space; therefore, the elastic strain energy is also a function of three-dimensional space. The potential rock burst point can be determined from the formula, and preventive measures can be taken accordingly. To prevent induced rock bursts, the elastic energy released from coal pillars induced by a dynamic disturbance can be reduced. First, the ability of coal pillars to resist disturbance can increase, the support of roadways can increase, the horizontal supporting force of coal pillars can increase, and the elasticity of coal pillars can improve. On the other hand, to reduce the elastic energy accumulated in the coal, pressure relief holes can be designed for coal pillars to release the elastic energy in the coal pillars in advance.

The mechanical properties and failure evolution law of coal samples under different pressures and impact cycles provide a theoretical basis for the support of rock burst roadways. The greater the impact energy and the greater the number of impacts that occur under the confining pressure of the rock burst roadway are, the more severe the damage to the rock surrounding the roadway, and the worse the support effect of the roadway. The study of the dynamic evolution law of confining pressure coal samples under different impact pressures and number of impacts provides basic theoretical support for rock burst roadways. Owing to the existence of cracks that can absorb impact energy, the larger the soft area formed by cracks in the unsupported area of the roadway is, the more energy is absorbed. Therefore, the absorption of impact energy after coal sample failure provides a reference for energy absorption in different areas of rock burst roadways.

Conclusions

(1)Impact compression tests were conducted on coal samples at different strain rates via a 75 mm SHPB testing system under radial and confining pressure conditions. The results revealed that the number of cycles and the impact pressure were inversely proportional to the peak stress and directly proportional to the degree of weakening. The peak stress weakening coefficient of coal samples under different impact pressures and numbers of cycles ranges from 28.5 to 73.2%, showing a linear weakening relationship with the number of cycles.
