Activation of orexin neurons changes reward-based decision-making strategies
Authors: Hiroyuki Mizoguchi, Kentaro Katahira, Ayumu Inutsuka, Ryosuke Kaneko, Daisuke Ono, Kazuhiro Hada, Yuki Murata, Shuji Yasuike, Madoka Isobe, Mizuki Kusaba, Yutao Dong, Haruna Iida, Kazuya Fukumoto, Yuchio Yanagawa, Akihiro Yamanaka, Kiyofumi Yamada
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOrexin neurons regulate physiological functions, including not only energy homeostasis and wakefulness but also motivated behaviors. These neurons play important roles in linking metabolic requirements to such behaviors. However, it is unknown whether these neurons are involved in reward-based choice behavior. We recently developed a transgenic rat line that expresses Cre recombinase exclusively in orexin neurons. Here, we examined the roles of orexin neurons in reward choice behavior under conditions of uncertainty in a gambling test in rats and analyzed the changes in choice behavior when orexin neurons were manipulated using chemogenetic approaches. In the gambling test, chemogenetic activation of orexin neurons resulted in risky arm choice. Positive, but not negative, reward-prediction error contributed to reward-based risky choice when orexin neurons were activated. These risky choice behaviors were canceled by suvorexant, an orexin receptor antagonist. In addition, suvorexant administered alone led to conservative choice behavior. The computational approach revealed that orexin/orexin neurons modulate the learning process and motivational value of rewards. Thus, our data suggest that orexin/orexin neurons affect the processing of reward-prediction error and alter strategy in reward-based decision-making.

Impaired decision-making is a symptom of several neuropsychiatric diseases. Patients with narcolepsy and cataplexy who demonstrated loss of orexin neurons also exhibit impairment in decision-making performance. However, it is unclear whether these findings were the compensatory result of disrupted orexin function or whether orexin neurons themselves are involved in decision-making. Here, we demonstrated that rats with activated orexin neurons chose a high-risk/high-reward option more frequently: activation of orexin neurons increased the response to large positive reward-prediction error: suvorexant treatment led to conservative choice behavior. This is the study to report that orexin/orexin neurons are involved in changes in choice strategy in reward-based decision-making.

Introduction

Orexin is composed of two neuropeptides, orexin A and orexin B (1, 2). Orexin A acts on orexin 1 receptor (OX1R) and orexin 2 receptor (OX2R). Although orexin B acts on both receptors, it has two to three orders of magnitude lower affinities and in vitro potency against OX1R compared with orexin A (3). Orexin neurons originate in the hypothalamus and project to various brain regions, and the expression patterns of orexin receptors differ between regions (1). Early studies demonstrated that orexin neurons regulate physiological functions, including energy homeostasis and wakefulness. Orexin neurons are also involved in natural reward-seeking behaviors. Activation of orexin neurons increases locomotion, eating, and drinking (4), and orexin neuron activity is increased during eating and decreased after eating (5). Food intake is promoted by orexin signaling, whereas activities are reduced during the sensorimotor act of eating (6). Thus, eating is thought to be driven in part by an instinctive drive to decrease enhanced orexin signaling in the brain (5). Moreover, orexin neurons are thought to play important roles in linking metabolic needs to the reward system. Orexin neurons modulate monoaminergic neurons (7), and vice versa; these two types of neurons are mutually involved in the regulation of neural activity (1). It was recently reported that the orexin system is activated in response to acute stress and is involved in fighting and escape behaviors (2, 8).

Impaired decision-making is a symptom of several neuropsychiatric diseases, such as depression, schizophrenia, Parkinson's disease, and drug dependence (9–12). Impairment of decision-making in neuropsychiatric disorders is associated with an inability to make profitable long-term decisions that incorporate expectations of future outcomes (13). Notably, patients with anorexia, bulimia, and obesity were found to show decision-making deficits on the Iowa gambling task, indicating that the reward system is impaired in compulsive food-related disorders (14). Orexin neurons were increased in addicted patients who exhibit impaired decision-making (15). Patients with narcolepsy and cataplexy who demonstrated loss of orexin neurons exhibited selectively reduced Iowa gambling test performance and may opt for choices with higher immediate emotional valence, regardless of higher future punishment, to compensate for their reduced reactivity to emotional stimuli (16). Moreover, OX1R in the anterior cingulate and orbitofrontal cortices regulates cost and benefit decision-making in rats, indicating that orexin neurons play a crucial role in evaluating and paying the cost of acquiring greater rewards (17). These reports suggest that orexin neurons are implicated in mental health disorders and that they may be involved in reward-based choice strategy.

We previously established a gambling test in rodents that can sensitively measure aspects of decision-making, such as whether or not to perform high-risk/high-return (H-H) actions (18–20). In this context, the term “risk” is not used in the sense of financial risk, which corresponds to the variance of outcomes but rather in its everyday meaning as the possibility of something bad happening. In the present study, we developed orexin (orx)-Cre recombinase (Cre) BAC transgenic (Tg) rats, and investigated whether orexin neurons were associated with reward-based decision-making strategies in rats using this gambling test and chemogenetic approaches.

Results

Activation of orexin neurons induces risk-seeking strategy in decision-making

In this study, we used chemogenetics to manipulate the activity of orexin neurons in orexin-Cre BAC Tg rats. We injected adeno-associated virus (AAV) that expressed hM3Dq-mCherry in the presence of Cre into the hypothalamus of orexin-Cre BAC Tg rats (Fig. 1A), and mCherry-positive cells were identified (Fig. 1B). Approximately 79.16 ± 3.69% of confirmed orexin neurons expressed hM3Dq-mCherry, while 94.47 ± 1.65% of confirmed hM3Dq-mCherry–expressing cells were orexin positive (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, we assessed Cre expression in the hypothalamus and surrounding areas. As shown in Fig. S1, ∼91.77% of confirmed orexin neurons expressed Cre, whereas 99.16% of confirmed Cre-expressing cells were positive for orexin. Cre expression was not detected in the surrounding areas. CNO treatment increased c-Fos expression in mCherry-positive neurons in hM3Dq-overexpressing rats (control, 7.44 ± 3.78%; CNO, 78.96 ± 5.99%), indicating activation of these neurons (Fig. 1D). Then, we confirmed the possibility that the function of orexin neurons was altered in this rat strain.

We next examined the effect of orexin neuron activation on decision-making in rats. Orexin-Cre BAC Tg rats overexpressing mutant hM3Dq in the hypothalamus were subjected to the daily gambling test for 14 days; each test was performed 30 min after i.p. injection of either CNO or vehicle (control group). At the end of the 14 days, control orexin-Cre BAC Tg rats preferred the L-L arm choice to the H-H arm choice. CNO injection resulted in an increased frequency of the H-H arm choice and a decreased frequency of the L-L arm choice (Fig. 1F), but there was no change in the number of entries into the empty arms (Fig. 1G).

Subsequently, we analyzed the effect of large positive and negative reward-prediction errors (RPEs) on the subsequent arm-choice behavior. Win–stay behavior was assessed in the trial immediately after the animal encountered a large reward (seven food pellets) in the H-H arm. Lose–shift behavior was analyzed in the trial immediately after the animal encountered a quinine pellet in the L-L arm. When a rat encountered a large reward in the H-H arm (large RPE: the outcome was better than expected), its subsequent choice was counted as a win–stay if it revisited the H-H arm in the next trial; otherwise, the choice was determined as a win–shift. Alternatively, when a rat encountered the quinine pellet in the L-L arm, its subsequent choice was scored as a lose–shift (negative RPE: the outcome was worse than expected) if it switched to the H-H arm; otherwise, the choice was scored as a lose–stay. There was no difference in the frequency of the lose–shift behavior in CNO-treated rats relative to vehicle-treated rats (Fig. 1I), whereas the frequency of the win–stay behavior was significantly increased in response to CNO treatment (Fig. 1H). These findings suggested that orexin neurons played a critical role in arm choice in the gambling test used in this study.

Figure S2 shows tdTomato expression in orexin-positive cells in double Tg rats. Double Tg rats were obtained by crossbreeding homogeneous orexin-Cre BAC Tg rats with heterogeneous reporter rats. As mentioned above, the presence of Cre recombinase activity in cells indicates the expression of tdTomato. Of 13 rat pups, six showed clear tdTomato expression in orexin neurons, while seven showed no clear tdTomato signal in the hypothalamus. Thus, ∼46% of rats exhibited clear tdTomato expression, although it was unknown whether this low rate of expression was due to issues in orexin-Cre BAC Tg rats or reporter rats. We observed tdTomato expression in 73.69 ± 5.35% of orexin-positive cells, showing that Cre recombinase exerts enzymatic activity in almost all of these neurons (n = 6).

Suvorexant induces risk-aversive strategy in decision-making

To examine the effect of orexin receptor antagonists on decision-making, we injected naive rats with suvorexant, a nonselective orexin receptor antagonist.

Suvorexant was extracted from Belsomra using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS), according to previous studies (21–24). We performed three extractions and confirmed the high purity each time (Fig. S3). We first examined the pharmacological effects of a single treatment on locomotion (Fig. S4). Suvorexant treatment doses of 10 and 30 mg/kg reduced locomotor activity in naive rats and significantly reduced locomotor activity for 60 min after treatment.

Subsequently, we examined the effect of suvorexant on decision-making. Naive rats were subjected to the daily gambling test for 14 days. Each test was performed 30 min after intraperitoneal injection of either suvorexant or vehicle (control group). At the end of the 14 days, control rats preferred the L-L arm choice to the H-H arm choice. Suvorexant injection resulted in decreased frequency of the H-H arm choice and an increased frequency of the L-L arm choice (Fig. 2A), with no change in the number of entries into the empty arms (Fig. 2B).

Subsequently, we analyzed the effect of large positive and negative RPEs on subsequent arm-choice behaviors. There was no difference in the frequency of lose–shift behavior in suvorexant-treated rats compared with that in vehicle-treated rats (Fig. 2D), whereas the frequency of win–stay behavior was significantly reduced in response to suvorexant treatment with a dose of 10 mg/kg (Fig. 2C). These findings suggest that endogenous orexin release plays a critical role in reward-based choice behaviors.

Risk-seeking strategy in orexin neuron-activated rats were blocked by suvorexant pretreatment

Finally, we investigated whether suvorexant treatment inhibited CNO-evoked risk-seeking behavior in orexin-Cre BAC Tg rats overexpressing mutant hM3Dq in the hypothalamus. As expected, suvorexant pretreatment (dose: 30 mg/kg) decreased the frequency of the H-H arm choice and increased that of the L-L arm choice (Fig. 3A), with no change in the number of entries into the empty arms (Fig. 3B). Moreover, there was no difference in the frequency of lose–shift behavior in suvorexant-treated orexin-Cre BAC Tg rats compared with that in vehicle-treated rats (Fig. 3D), whereas the frequency of win–stay behavior was significantly reduced in response to suvorexant treatment (Fig. 3C).

Rats with activated orexin neurons show alteration of the learning process and assign higher motivational values to large rewards

There are several possible reasons why the CNO-treated orexin-Cre BAC Tg rats preferred the H-H arm to the L-L arm. Because the frequency of the H-H arm choice in the CNO-treated group did not exceed 33% (the level of chance), two trivial explanations are valid: first, the activation of orexin neurons may have merely impaired or retarded the learning process; second, this activation may simply have made the choice more random. A third, nontrivial explanation is that the activation of these neurons altered the relative motivational values associated with a small reward, a large reward, and quinine. To determine which explanation best explained the data, we applied reinforcement learning models to the rats’ behavioral data and estimated the process underlying the trial-by-trial learning dynamics according to previous studies (18, 25). If the first possibility were correct, the learning rate α, which determines the magnitude of the value update resulting from a single trial, would differ between CNO-treated and control rats. If the second possibility were correct, the inverse temperature parameter β, which determines the randomness of choice, would differ between the two groups. Finally, if the third possibility were correct, the motivational value parameters for the large reward κLR (relative to quinine and small reward) would differ between the two groups.

We calculated the estimated parameters for each group, as shown in Fig. 4. We observed significant differences between groups in regard to the motivational value parameters for the large reward (Fig. 4C). The differences in the motivational value for the large reward best explain the difference in the behavior of vehicle- and CNO-treated orexin-Cre BAC Tg rats (Fig. 4D). In addition, the learning rate (Fig. 4A) and inverse temperature (Fig. 4B) did not differ between the two groups. Regarding the parameters depicted in Fig. 2, we observed significant differences between the learning rate and motivational value parameters for the large reward between the groups (Fig. 4E and G). Regarding the parameters shown in Fig. 3, we observed significant differences in the learning rate and motivational value parameters for the large reward between the groups (Fig. 4I and K). The differences in these parameters best explain the difference in behavior, as illustrated in Figs. 2 and 3 (Fig. 4H and L). These results support the conclusions that orexin/orexin neurons alter the balance of motivational values and learning rate, instead of merely impairing choice behaviors.

Discussion

Orexin neurons are involved in reward circuits and decision-making in humans. Patients with narcolepsy and cataplexy preferred risky choices in a decision-making task under ambiguous conditions, regardless of whether or not they were taking psychostimulants (26). In another study involving a decision-making task, patients with narcolepsy type 1, which is characterized by orexin deficiency, exhibited reduced ability to learn to avoid losses but had normal positive reinforcement learning that was not suggestive of dopamine deficiency (27). These phenomena are associated with sleepiness. In a visuomotor game–like task during fMRI scanning, patients with narcolepsy and cataplexy showed abnormal activity in reward brain circuits; dopaminergic activity in the ventral midbrain (ventral tegmental area) was not modulated during high-reward expectancy, and ventral striatum activity was reduced during winning, while abnormal activity in the amygdala and dorsal striatum was increased after positive outcomes (28). However, it is unclear whether these findings were the compensatory result of disrupted orexin function, or whether orexin neurons themselves are involved in decision-making. In this study, suvorexant treatment decreased the frequency of H-H choices and increased that of L-L choices in the rodent gambling test. The frequency of win–stay behavior, but not that of lose–shift behavior, was reduced. Moreover, cell-specific excitatory manipulation of rat orexin neurons using chemogenetics increased the frequency of H-H choices and decreased that of L-L choices in the rodent gambling test. The frequency of win–stay behavior, but not lose–shift behavior, was increased, indicating that chemogenetic activation of orexin neurons leads to an increased response to large positive RPE. These behaviors were inhibited by suvorexant treatment. This is the study to report that orexin neurons control decision-making strategy in an H-H situation.

The orexin system acts as a critical link between peripheral energy balance and the components of the central nervous system that regulate homeostatic feeding behavior (1, 29, 30). Orexin treatment increases both consumption of and motivation for palatable food; in particular, orexin A increases reward-taking in a progressive ratio test and stimulates binge-like intake of various palatable foods (31). The orexin system is readily activated in response to high-reward salience and conditioned stimuli that elicit expectation of a food reward (32). Relapse to food seeking is associated with orexin neuron activity (33). Interestingly, Mehr et al. (30) hypothesized that as a strategy to combat drowsiness during the daytime or to cause behavioral entrainment, inappropriately high orexin system function results in hyperarousal and sleep dysregulation, which in turn promotes an increased risk of binge eating due to weakened inhibitory control. Thus, dysfunction of orexin neurons is involved in the development of a hypermotivated state for food, which in turn increases the proclivity to binge eat. Our data also showed that overactivation of orexin neurons led rats to prefer high-risk reward-based choice behavior (Fig. 1F), and these are the first results to show that orexin neuron activity influences decision-making. We also believe that this abnormal decision-making or changes in choice strategy may lead to overeating or binge eating if orexin neurons are activated in humans, but further research on this issue is needed.

Interestingly, we found that win–stay behavior related to a large positive RPE was increased, whereas lose–shift behavior was not (Fig. 1H and I). This result is different from our previous data; methamphetamine-treated rats showed impairment in both positive and negative RPEs (18), and when nicotine treatment ameliorated impaired decision-making in methamphetamine-treated rats, only increased lose–shift behavior was decreased (19). Thus, this result is one of the characteristics of the change in decision-making strategies caused by activation of orexin neurons, and only a positive RPE may contribute to reward-based risky choice, even if the rats with activated orexin neurons exhibit the same risky choice behavior as rats with methamphetamine addiction. In fact, orexin neuron activity is altered in food-taking behavior. Therefore, if rats encounter a large reward and orexin neurons are activated, the rats may expect it again. Activated orexin neurons may increase reward sensitivity, leading to risk-taking behavior in rats. Moreover, orexin neurons also release co-transmitters such as glutamate and dynorphin. Therefore, although orexin neurons were activated, it is possible that transmitters other than orexin modulate decision-making strategies. However, high-risk reward-based choice behaviors evoked by the chemogenetic approach were inhibited by suvorexant. Thus, our data demonstrated that orexin is important for decision-making strategies.

Further studies should be performed using an orexin antagonist in naive rats to confirm our hypothesis. Suvorexant is a nonselective orexin receptor antagonist used clinically as a sleep aid. Our results showed that suvorexant treatment induced a risk-averse choice strategy, although no dose-dependent effects were observed between 10 and 30 mg/kg. Furthermore, win–stay behaviors were significantly decreased by suvorexant at a dose of 10 mg/kg but not 30 mg/kg. These results suggest that released endogenous orexin is involved in reward-based risk- and benefit-choice behaviors under normal conditions and states wherein orexin neurons are forcibly activated. Several studies have demonstrated that patients with narcolepsy and cataplexy prefer risky choices (16, 26). However, suvorexant treatment led to a preference for low-risk choices. Thus, there may be a compensatory result of disrupted orexin function in human studies; however, further research on this issue is needed. Moreover, to date, many studies have demonstrated that orexin antagonists affect food and water intake and expenditure; however, the effect is almost acute. In the present study, we investigated the effects of subacute and chronic treatments. To the best of our knowledge, in rat experiments, this is the first study to examine the long-term effects of reward-based decision-making. In many clinical settings, patients use orexin receptor antagonists as long-term sleep medications. Thus, it may be necessary to be careful of psychological and mental changes to rewards associated with the long-term use of this drug, taking the dosage and half-life into consideration.

Orexin neurons project to various brain regions. Orexin is implicated in reinforcement- and reward-related processes through its actions on ventral tegmental area dopaminergic neurons via OX1R (34). Nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurons are sensitive to changes in action-outcome contingency, creating Pavlovian conditioned stimuli with circuit-defined motivational properties (35). This indicates that orexin neuronal activation modulates dopaminergic neurons, resulting in changes in decision-making strategies. Moreover, insular orexin transmission is associated with nicotine abuse (36). We previously demonstrated that the insular cortex is an important region in risk-seeking behavior (18), suggesting that orexin neural pathways may be important in decision-making strategies. Future studies should examine the brain regions that receive orexin neuron projections and determine their involvement in risky choices.

Our previous study (18) used the same behavioral paradigm to examine the parameters underlying choice behavior by computational modeling, using reinforcement learning to determine the reason for the arm-choice behavior in animal model rats of addiction. However, the sample size was not sufficiently large to examine group differences using the current standard method of computational model fitting (individual-level parameter estimation). Therefore, we did not perform such an analysis; however, for comparison with previous studies, we performed the same analysis as in previous studies, ignoring individual differences within groups for reference (Fig. 4). Thus, dysfunction of orexin neurons, including disruption (neuronal inhibition and overactivity), affects learning and motivational processes and may alter strategy in reward-based choice behavior. Model simulations using the estimated parameters showed a difference in arm selection between the groups (Fig. 4D, H, and L). Further improvements to this model are required.

It is possible that the activation of orexin neurons does not alter responses to large rewards, but rather modulates sensitivity to outcome uncertainty (e.g. risk in the financial sense). However, in the gambling task in this study, both the H-H and L-L arms were associated with a 12.5% probability of a rare event (large reward and quinine pellet, respectively); thus, there was no difference in uncertainty in that regard. Therefore, we believe that this possibility can be ruled out. However, there was a difference in the variance of outcomes between the two arms when the number of pellets was also taken into account, and the difference in sensitivity to risk in the financial sense cannot be completely eliminated. This is a subject for future research. Moreover, orexinergic modulations may change the perceived value of each option because all groups strongly preferred the L-L arm to the H-H arm. In CNO treatment, the value of a high reward may increase (Fig. 4C and K), leading to a smaller difference in expected value between options and a subsequent slower development of preference for the L-L arm (37, 38). In the present study, there were differences in the learning rate of suvorexant-treated rats (Fig. 4E). The rats do change strategy but potentially not through altering risk preference. Further experiments will be needed to clarify role of orexin/orexin neurons in choice behavior.

Finally, as there is no evidence regarding the stability of the hM3Dq transgene across generations of the orexin-Cre BAC Tg rats used for breeding, there remain concerns about unstable transgene expression in later generations of these rats. However, AAV-induced mCherry expression was clearly seen in Figs. 1B, C and S5. Moreover, Cre expression was also clearly observed (Fig. S1). Thus, we believe there should be no problems with the expression or specificity of Cre in adulthood. Nonetheless, further studies examining these points are needed.

In conclusion, endogenous orexin was involved in reward-based decision-making strategies. Orexin-Cre BAC Tg rats were also useful for examining the role of orexin in a difficult and complex behavioral task. Activated orexin neurons affected the processing of RPE and may alter strategy in reward-based choice behavior. To confirm this possibility, further studies should be performed.

Methods

Animals

Experiments in this study used male orexin-Cre BAC Tg Long-Evans homogeneous and heterogeneous rats generated by the Comprehensive Brain Science Network. Cre was expressed in orexin neurons under the mouse orexin promotor. LE-Tg(Gt(ROSA)26SorCAG-tdTomato)24Jfhy rats were provided by the National BioResource Project for the Rat in Japan (39). Naive Long-Evans rats aged >8 weeks (Clea Inc., Tokyo, Japan) were used. The rats were socially housed in plastic cages, and kept in a regulated environment (23 ± 1°C; 50 ± 5% humidity) with a 12-h light–dark cycle (lights on at 8:00 AM). Food (CE-2, CLEA Japan) and tap water were available ad libitum. The weight of almost rat was >300 g at the time of surgery to perform virus injection, and each rat was approximately over 11 weeks old at the time of behavioral experiments. All experiments were performed in accordance with the Guidelines for Animal Experiments of Nagoya University, the Guiding Principles for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (approved by the Japanese Pharmacological Society), and the United States National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Nagoya University (Permit Number: R230008-004, M25108-001). One day before starting behavioral experiments, rats underwent food restriction and their weight was kept at ∼80–90% of their ad libitum weight.

Construction of Orx-Cre BAC

This study used a BAC clone (RP24-185L22) from a C57BL/6J mouse genomic BAC library (BACPAC Resource Center, Oakland, CA, USA), spanning from ∼74 kb upstream of the Orx initiation codon to ∼66 kb downstream of the Orx termination codon (total size ∼142 kb). NLS-Cre cDNA with a rabbit beta globin polyadenylation (polyA) signal was inserted into the Orx BAC clone using homologous recombination in Escherichia coli EL250 (40). The primer sequences used in the following modifications are shown in Table S1: Orx5-F, Orx5Cre-1R, Cre665R, Orx3-FP, Orx3-RX. The 5' homology arm with NLS-Cre cDNA was PCR amplified using primer pairs of Orx5-F and Orx5Cre-1R, and then Orx5-F and Cre665R. The 3' homology arm was PCR amplified using primer pairs of Orx3-FP and Orx3-RX. Next, the recombination cassette was assembled with the 5′ homology arm, NLS-Cre, polyA signal, neomycin resistance cassette, and the 3′ homology arm using standard molecular biology techniques. Through lambda red protein–mediated homologous recombination in E. coli EL250, the above elements flanked by the homologous fragments were inserted into the BAC clone at the desired location. The Neo cassette was excised by arabinose-induced FLP expression, thereby leaving only a single residual FRT site. The integrity of modification was verified by DNA sequencing.

Generation of BAC transgenic (Tg) rats

Recombined BACs were linearized and then gel purified. The purified BAC fragments were microinjected into pronuclei of Long-Evans rat oocytes according to the procedure described by Takahashi et al.(41). The BAC Tg founders were identified by PCR in this study (Table S1). One founder rat was selected and was crossed with Long-Evans rats to produce rat pups. Afterward, the male and female heterogeneous rats were bred with each other to create male and female homogeneous rats, as confirmed by Southern blotting, and these rats were maintained and then used in experiments. Now that 25 and 26 generations have passed, 37 male and 38 female rats are born at the same rate.

Surgery to perform viral infection and drug treatment

The mCherry-tagged hM3Dq receptor transgene was expressed under the control of the cytomegalovirus promoter using AAV vectors. AAV vectors were produced using the AAV Helper-Free System (Agilent Technologies) and purified according to a previously published method (42). Rats were subcutaneously given three types of mixed anesthetic agents (medetomidine, 0.15 mg/kg; midazolam, 2 mg/kg; butorphanol, 2.5 mg/kg), and then placed into a stereotaxic frame under 1–2% isoflurane. According to the rat brain atlas (43), orexin-Cre BAC Tg rats were bilaterally injected with 0.5 or 1.0 μL of AAV-CAG-FLEX-hM3Dq-mCherry (Ser9; 2.64 × 1011, 2.6 × 1013, 5.95 × 1012 particles/mL) or AAV-hSyn-FLEX-hM3Dq-mCherry (DJ; 6 × 1012 particles/mL) or AAV-CAG-FLEX-mCherry (Ser9; 8.59 × 1012 particles/mL) into the hypothalamus (AP −3.0; ML +1.0, +2.0; DV −7.5) with a glass micropipette at a flow rate of 0.25 μL/min. The injector was left in place for an additional 15 min to minimize diffusion up the injector tract. At the end of each surgery, rats were treated with atipamezole (0.75 mg/kg).

Clozapine-N-oxide (CNO, Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved in 10% dimethyl sulfoxide in saline. Thirty minutes before the gambling test on each day, rats were intraperitoneally given CNO at a dose of 0.5 mg/kg.

As per previous studies (21–24), suvorexant was extracted from Belsomra using LC–MS/MS, as described below. Suvorexant was suspended in 0.5% carboxymethyl cellulose in saline. Rats were administered 10 or 30 mg/kg suvorexant intraperitoneally 30 min before the gambling test to examine the effects of suvorexant alone in the gambling test (Fig. 2). Thirty minutes before CNO treatment each day, the rats were administered 30 mg/kg suvorexant to examine the effects of CNO (Fig. 3).

Extraction of suvorexant from Belsomra

All solvents were obtained from commercial suppliers and used without further purification. Thin-layer chromatography was performed, and the compounds were visualized under UV light (254 nm). Purification of suvorexant was performed using Biotage Isolera One (Biotage, Uppsala, Sweden) equipped with Biotage Sfär Silica D columns. Belsomra tablets were suspended in water (400 mL) and stirred for 10 min at 50 °C. Ethyl acetate (400 mL) was added to the resulting suspension. The resulting mixture was filtered through a celite pad, and the filtrate was extracted three times with ethyl acetate (200 mL). Thereafter, the organic phase was dried over MgSO4 and concentrated under reduced pressure. The residue was purified by Biotage Isolera One, and suvorexant was isolated. The purity of the extracted suvorexant was confirmed using LC–MS/MS (see “LC–MS/MS analysis” section).

LC–MS/MS analysis

Qualitative and quantitative determination of suvorexant by LC–MS/MS was performed as previously described with slight modifications (21–24). Suvorexant was determined in the positive ion mode using an LC system (Nexera 2; Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) connected to a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (LC–MS 8040; Shimadzu) with an electrospray ion source. LC separation of suvorexant and estazolam-d5, an internal control, was performed using a C18 column (2.1 × 50 mm, 3-μm particle size; GL Sciences, Tokyo, Japan) maintained at 35 °C. Separation was performed using gradient elution with mobile phases A (0.1% formic acid in water) and B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile; MPB). The gradient program was run as follows: 40% MPB (linear gradient, v/v) at 0 min, 40−90% MPB at 4 min, 90% MPB at 2 min, and 90−40% MPB at 2 min at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min, with a run time of 8 min for each 3-μL injection volume. The multiple reaction monitoring mode was applied for the detection of suvorexant and estazolam-d5 by monitoring the transitions: m/z 451.4 to 186.1 for suvorexant and m/z 300.25 to 272.1 for estazolam-d5. The purified suvorexant was dissolved in MPB and diluted to 100 ng/mL, followed by the addition of estazolam-d5 to 500 ng/mL. The detection times were 4.223 and 2.063 min for suvorexant and estazolam-d5, respectively.

Gambling test using a radial arm maze

We used the same apparatus as that described in our previous reports (18, 19). The apparatus consisted of two start arms and four choice arms (two baited arms and two empty arms) on an elevated six-arm Plexiglas radial maze (Brainscience Idea, Osaka, Japan) located in a sound-attenuated testing room illuminated at 40 lux (Fig. 1E). The other two arms of the original eight-arm maze were removed from the apparatus. The entrances from the start arms to the central platform could be blocked with black guillotine doors (30-cm high and 11-cm wide).

According to previous methods (18, 19), prior to starting the task, the rats were habituated to the apparatus through a 20-min free exploration trial. This trial was conducted once per day for 8 days, during which 45 mg of banana-flavored food pellets (F0159, Bioserv, Frenchtown, NJ, USA) was placed in the food cups of all four choice arms. The rats used in these trials were mildly food restricted (three meals of ∼3 g solid feed [∼9 g, CE-2] per day), and the food restriction was maintained through the end of the gambling test. In the decision-making task, the reward was 45 mg of banana-flavored food pellets, and after negative outcomes the rats were given 45 mg of quinine-coated food pellets that were unpalatable but not inedible. Rats could obtain either rewards or quinine pellets in the baited arms (see below), but no food pellets were placed in the empty arms. Rats were randomly placed in one of the two start arms, and they learned to enter the central platform and then choose one of the four choice arms when the guillotine door was opened. When a rat obtained a food pellet during a trial, a researcher returned it to one of the start arms before the next trial. When a rat entered an empty arm, it was returned to the start arm, and the trial was continued until the animal entered a baited arm and received either a reward or a quinine pellet. Rats performed 16 trials per day, and sessions were repeated for 14 days.

In the decision-making task, rats were trained to choose one of four choice arms (one low-risk/low-return [L-L] arm, one H-H arm, and two empty arms). Under the standard conditions, choice of the L-L arm resulted in frequent (14/16 trials; reward probability: 87.5%) small rewards (one banana-flavored food pellet; reward value: 45 mg) with infrequent (2/16 trials) negative outcomes (one quinine-coated food pellet). Choice of the H-H arm resulted in infrequent (2/16 trials, reward probability: 12.5%) large rewards (seven banana-flavored food pellets; reward value: 315 mg) with frequent (14/16 trials) negative outcomes (one quinine pellet). Spatial locations of the L-L arm, H-H arm, two empty arms, and two start arms were fixed among animals throughout the experiments. The rewards were provided randomly at a rate of two trials with rewards out of the total of 16 trials in each day. In the L-L arm, the negative outcome was a quinine pellet, and in the H-H arm, a large reward was provided. Accordingly, there was no difference in the total reward value (630 mg) between the L-L and H-H arms. For three-choice task analysis, H-H or L-L arm choice (%) was expressed as follows: arm choice (%) = ([{total number of either arm choice}/{16 trials + total number of entries into empty arms}] × 100).

Win–stay/lose–shift behavior

According to our previous report (18, 19), win–stay behavior was measured in the trial that followed immediately after the animal encountered the large reward (seven food pellets) in the H-H arm, whereas lose–shift behavior was analyzed in the trial that followed immediately after the animal encountered the quinine pellet in the L-L arm. When a rat encountered the large reward in the H-H arm (large positive RPE), its subsequent choice was counted as a win–stay if it revisited the H-H arm in the next trial, and otherwise as a win–shift. Alternatively, when a rat encountered the quinine pellet in the L-L arm, its subsequent choice was scored as a lose–shift (negative RPE) if it switched to the H-H arm, and otherwise as a lose–stay. Win–stay or lose–shift behavior was expressed as follows:

H-H arm choice (%) for win–stay behavior = ([total number of win–stay behaviors/total number of win–stay + win–shift behaviors] × 100), considering all win–stay and win–shift behaviors during the 14-day training session (16 trials/day × 14 days = 224 trials).

H-H arm choice (%) for lose–shift behavior = ([total number of lose–shift behaviors/total number of lose–shift + lose–stay behaviors] × 100), considering all lose–shift and lose–stay behaviors during the 14-day training session.

If a rat never encountered the large reward in the H-H arm or the quinine pellet in the L-L arm, its behavior was omitted from the data.

Locomotor activity

Locomotor activity was measured as previously described with minor modifications. Naive Long-Evans rats aged >8 weeks (Clea Inc.) were randomly divided into three groups. Each rat was placed in an open field (W: 60 cm, D: 60 cm, H: 35 cm) under normal light conditions (40 lx or less). To determine the effects of the vehicle treatment and suvorexant at doses of 10 or 30 mg/kg on locomotor activity, measurements were taken immediately after injection for 120 min in each mouse. Locomotor activity was measured using an infrared sensor (ABC system; Brain Science Idea, Osaka, Japan).

Immunohistochemistry

Animals were perfused with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS. The brains were cryoprotected in 20–30% sucrose overnight, frozen, and sectioned at 40- or 50-μm thickness. Sections were incubated with primary antibody goat anti-orexin A (1:500 or 1,000, sc-8070, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) or rabbit anti-orexin A (1:2,000, Phoenix Pharmaceuticals, Belmont, CA, USA), rabbit anti-DsRed (1:1,000, Cat# 632496, TaKaRa Bio USA), goat anti-mCherry (1:1,000, AB0040, OriGene Technologies, Inc., MD, USA), rabbit anti-c-Fos (1:1,000, sc-253, Santa Cruz Biotechnology; 1:1,000, ab190289, abcam), and mouse anti-Cre (1:1,000, MAB3120, Millipore, Burlinton, MA, USA) followed by the appropriate secondary antibody, specifically donkey anti-goat or anti-rabbit immunoglobulin IgG (1:500 or 1,000, Alexa Fluor 488) or anti-rabbit or anti-mouse IgG (1:500 or 1,000, Alexa Fluor 546 or 594). tdTomato expression

Double Tg rats were obtained by crossbreeding homogeneous orexin-Cre BAC Tg rats with heterogeneous LE-Tg(Gt(ROSA)26SorCAG-tdTomato)24Jfhy rats (39). If Cre recombinase exerted enzymatic activity in cells, tdTomato was expressed in Cre-positive cells. The brains of all rat pups were examined. Data were obtained using rats expressing tdTomato in orexin neurons. If tdTomato was not expressed at all, the rats were excluded from the analysis.

Statistical analyses

All data are expressed as means ± SE. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 7.0. Statistical significance was determined using the Mann–Whitney U test for comparisons of two groups, Student's t test, or ANOVA for repeated two-way ANOVA, with significance established at the P < 0.05 level.

The maximum likelihood approach was used to estimate the reinforcement learning model parameters from the rat choice data, and the results are shown in Fig. 4. To determine which parameters differed between subject groups, we adopted the procedures described below. First, we constructed a model set for all combinations of three key parameters of interest: the learning rate, inverse temperature, and motivational value parameter for the large reward. For two-group comparisons, each parameter was either assumed to be shared across groups or allowed to differ between them, resulting in two options per parameter. In the case of three groups, we considered five configurations for each parameter: (i) shared across all groups, (ii) different across all groups, and (iii–v) shared between two of the three groups (i.e. three possible group pairings).

Afterward, we performed a fixed-effects analysis, in which a single parameter set was estimated for each model across all subjects within each group. Model fitting was conducted by minimizing the negative log-likelihood using the solnp function from the R package rsolnp. Model comparison was based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC), and the model with the smallest AIC was selected as the best. If the selected best model allowed for group differences in a parameter, we formally tested the significance of that difference using a likelihood ratio test. Specifically, we compared the best model against a reduced model, in which the parameter was constrained to be equal across groups, using the likelihood ratio statistic and evaluating significance at the P < 0.05 level under the null hypothesis that any improvement in likelihood was due to chance alone.
