Motor sequences resist automatization as attentional demands increase with sequence learning
Authors: Stephan Frederic Dahm, Veit Kraft, Markus Martini, Daniel Krause, Joachim Hermsdoerfer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutomatization of motor sequences involves a shift from effortful, attention-dependent control to fluent performance through practice. This study examined how learning cues and instructions influence the development of sequence-specific automatization using a modified serial reaction time task with temporally spaced stimuli. Participants practiced a training sequence across ten sessions under four conditions varying in instruction (intentional vs. incidental) and visual cues (present vs. absent). Performance was assessed before and after training under single- and dual-task conditions for trained and control sequences. Results demonstrated robust sequence learning, with faster reaction times for trained than control sequences. Intentional instruction enhanced learning, particularly with visual cues. Contrary to expectations, dual-task costs decreased for the control sequence but increased for the trained sequence. Sequence-specific dual-task costs were associated with superior learning and explicit knowledge, suggesting that deeper sequence encoding may prolong attentional demands despite extensive practice.Subject terms: Mathematics and computing, Neuroscience, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Thoughts and actions often unfold in a spatial-temporal sequence. For instance, activities like driving a car or playing piano involve events that are organized in time and space which require single events to be completed in a specific order to form a whole skill. Thus, learning the correct order of motor sequences and their automatization with practice is relevant for many motor skills. While learning is slow and effortful at first, with practice, motor behavior becomes more automated, freeing up limited attention resources and making room for processing additional information. Motor automaticity refers to the degree to which a person performs movements with minimal reliance on limited attention resources, such as working memory1. Freeing these resources during motor skill learning is important to ensure a high level of motor performance, despite interfering attention-demanding secondary task processes (e.g., sign reading while driving the car; communicating while playing an instrument). Taken together, these examples illustrate why the study of how motor skills become automated (and the change of attentional demands during sequence learning) is central for understanding skilled performance. In the following sections, we therefore outline theoretical models of automatization, discuss how external cues may shape these processes, and investigated motor automatization under controlled conditions using the serial reaction time task (SRTT) for examining intentional and incidental motor learning.

In the context of motor learning, there are changes in the relevant cognitive representations for the control of motor skills. Generally, motor control in the early stages of learning relies heavily on explicit (external) information, with a high demand on attention for motor planning and execution. In contrast, motor control in the later stages of learning relies on less explicit representations and demands less attention2–4. More specifically, in the context of motor sequence learning where a fixed sequence of motor elements is learned over repetitive trials, the Parallel-Neural-Network-Model5 postulates that motor learning involves two parallel systems. It suggests that learners use explicit knowledge, such as a spatially coded sequence representation, in the early stages of motor learning. In this stage, external visual stimuli guide sequence learning and learners respond to each stimulus individually (reacting mode)6. Over time, a basic spatial representation of the sequence is formed, enabling anticipation of upcoming stimuli and execution of the sequence in an associative mode6. In these early stages, a fast-learning, attention-dependent spatial system, establishes movement sequence representations based on a spatial code that is independent of the body parts involved7. Parallel to the spatial system, a slow-learning motor system is established, representing movement sequences based on an effector-dependent motor code7,8. Following substantial practice, the motor system5 controls the task with minimal reliance on working memory. During automatization of a motor sequence, single independent sequential elements can be bound into related motor chunks (chunking mode)6, especially in later stages of the automatization process. This late stage of motor learning refers to the ‘automatic execution stage’ 3, and the underlying execution process as ‘motor automatization’ 4. The automatization of motor behavior is typically assessed by comparing performance under dual-task versus single-task conditions9,10. The basic assumption is that dual-task costs decrease over time.

Several modulating factors of motor learning have been identified that can influence the transition from attention-dependent to automatic sequence execution—the extent to which learning is influenced by anticipatory cues and the awareness of the learned motor sequence. In motor learning, the presence of external cues during the learning process, such as visual aids, written instructions, or notational guidance, can significantly influence the encoding and retention of information11. While these cues can facilitate initial understanding and performance, they can paradoxically hinder learning mechanisms necessary for long-term retention and automatization12,13. This phenomenon is particularly evident in domains such as music education. For example, when musicians rely on reading sheet music when practicing, they may become dependent on visual input. This can hinder the development of anticipatory motor planning and auditory imagery, both of which are crucial for automatizing motor performance12. In contrast, memorizing a piece and practicing without visual cues encourages the engagement of cognitive processes such as anticipation, mental rehearsal, and sensorimotor integration13. These processes strengthen memory traces and support the transition from effortful execution to automaticity. Thus, while external cues can support learning in the initial stages, their ongoing presence may hinder or decelerate the development of more integrated and therefore more efficient motor representations that underlie expert performance. While such cue-based effects have been demonstrated in domains with a high relevance of skilled movement like music12,14 and sports15,16, the mechanisms by which motoric sequence knowledge emerges can differ depending on whether learning occurs intentionally, with the knowledge of the underlying motor sequence structure or incidentally, without this knowledge.

To study how people learn motor sequences, researchers frequently use the SRTT6,17,18. In this task, individuals respond to a series of stimuli (e.g., a circle appearing at different positions on the screen) by pressing predefined keys that correspond to the positions at which the stimulus can appear17. Usually unknown to participants, the stimulus order follows a predefined complex (spatial) pattern. Participants react to the stimuli several hundred times. Studies have shown that, over time and with increasing practice, response times decrease. However, when the underlying pattern is removed by a new sequence or altered by deviating from the original sequence order, response times increase again. This indicates that knowledge about the original sequence has been built18. Since this learning often occurs unintentionally and without conscious awareness of the underlying sequence pattern, it has been suggested that the SRTT relies at least partially on incidental (implicit) learning, rather than intentional (explicit) learning18,19. However, participants may also learn the sequence deliberately, either by being given explicit instructions that an underlying pattern exists or by increased awareness of the underlying pattern with practice20. Therefore, the SRTT is suited to studying both intentional (explicit) and incidental (implicit) motor sequence learning. By varying the instructions, participants can learn the SRTT either with (explicit) or without (implicit) awareness that the stimulus follows a repetitive spatial sequence. Therefore, the nature of the instruction, the level of awareness and degree of automation can influence motor learning21,22. Sequence-specific automatization in the SRTT is often investigated by comparing a dual-task condition with a single-task condition. In the dual-task condition, a secondary task (e.g., tone counting) is conducted in parallel to the SRTT, while in the single-task condition, only the SRTT is performed. With increased practice and thus automatization of the sequence structure, reaction times decrease, indicating that automatization frees up cognitive resources, which in turn reduces dual task costs.

Despite extensive research using the SRTT, evidence for sequence-specific automatization—operationalized as selective reductions in dual-task costs for practiced sequences—remains inconsistent. While several studies indicate that practice can reduce dual-task costs, this “dual-task practice advantage” is restricted to very specific task combinations (e.g., when both tasks are practiced together) and does not result from practicing a sequence in isolation. Moreover, context-specific reductions of dual-task costs do not indicate automatization, but rather an improvement of integrated processing of two specific tasks23. In many paradigms, including SRTT variants that encourage rapid, self-paced responding, freed-up attentional capacity is difficult to observe because timing parameters limit opportunities to reallocate processing resources24,25. Other studies show that predictability or explicit sequence knowledge can produce improvements that are task-specific (i.e., benefits for the predictable task itself) and do not automatically transfer to concurrent, independent tasks26–28. Furthermore, long, intensive practice does not invariably eliminate dual-task costs; in certain paradigms, dual-task interference can persist despite extensive training29. Finally, models of sequence execution emphasize that explicit/associative representations can actually increase the reliance on higher-order control (e.g., during associative-mode execution or when execution rates are low). This may counteract reduction in dual-task costs for long or complex sequences6,30. Taken together, these findings highlight three methodological issues in existing SRTT studies: (a) many protocols allow for excessively rapid self-paced responses, which limits the reallocation of resources; (b) practicing only the primary task may lead to task-specific improvements that do not generalize to concurrent tasks; and (c) the emergence of explicit sequence representations, especially for long sequences, may increase rather than decrease attentional demands. These considerations motivated the current task design, which imposes a minimum response–stimulus interval, contrasts a highly practiced sequence against a structurally matched control sequence, and manipulates instruction and anticipatory cues. This allowed us to test whether sequence-specific reductions in dual-task costs emerge under conditions that favor resource reallocation.

The extent to which sequence-specific automatization can be observed in motor learning tasks may critically depend on methodological parameters, particularly the temporal constraints imposed by the task. The nature of the task—specifically, whether it emphasizes speed versus precision—may determine whether such resource reallocation is possible. In tasks where participants are instructed to perform as quickly as possible, we can assume that cognitive resources remain heavily engaged due to the demands of maximal performance, limiting the observable effects of automatization. By contrast, tasks that prioritize precise timing or continuous control over speed may free up cognitive resources as performance becomes more fluent, thereby enabling task switching or automatization to circumvent dual-task costs. This aligns with theories of cognitive control31,32, which suggest that task switching, as opposed to simultaneous processing, becomes viable only when sufficient temporal gaps are available for cognitive operations. Consequently, it is more likely to see the benefits of automatization or multitasking in precision tasks, where freed-up processing capacity can be redirected.

Given the importance of task demands when investigating skill acquisition and automatization, we adapted the SRTT in the present study. Firstly, previous studies20,33, administered the SRTT without an experimenter-imposed response–stimulus interval, meaning each stimulus appeared immediately after the response. Under these conditions of continuous, self-paced rapid responding, sequence-specific reductions in dual-task costs were not observed. Therefore, we added a timing constraint with a minimum response-stimulus interval of 400 ms to delay the next stimulus (and its response) in case an answer was made in under 400 ms. It was assumed that this would free up cognitive resources at later stages of learning, allowing to perform a secondary task without performance increments of the primary task. Secondly, in order to determine whether automatization develops selectively for the learned sequence rather than reflecting general skill improvements, a highly practiced sequence was contrasted with a structurally matched but unpracticed control sequence. Thirdly, we included both single-task and dual-task conditions in the Pretest and Posttest, enabling us to examine whether practice reduces dual-task costs specifically for the practiced sequence. Finally, by assessing both explicit and implicit sequence knowledge through recognition and generation tests, we could determine whether any observed dual-task effects were due to the emergence of explicit sequence knowledge. If sequence practice results in a reduction of dual-task costs for the practiced sequence (i.e., dual task costs in practiced sequence < dual task costs in control sequence), this would provide direct evidence that sequence practice shifts control during sequence execution from attention-demanding, capacity-limited processes to more proceduralized, resource-efficient modes of control. Such a result would support both classic stage models of automatization, which describe the transition from attention-dependent to autonomous stages, and contemporary accounts that posit a shift in the locus of control from frontoparietal working-memory systems to procedural motor circuits4,5. This would also validate the prediction that anticipatory control yields representations that no longer require continuous high demand of working memory processes, making sequence execution less prone to interference from a concurrent tone-counting task3,6. Finally, selective reductions in dual-task costs following single-task practice would challenge dual-task theories by suggesting that, under certain conditions, practice can reduce overlap in the response selection—a pattern that has been formalized in recent dual-task and sequence frameworks6,34.

We expected that extensive practice of a fixed sequence would reduce its reliance on attention-demanding control processes, rendering a practiced sequence less affected by a secondary task than an unpracticed control sequence3,26,35–37. Therefore, we expected dual-task costs to decrease more strongly in the practiced sequence than in the control sequence.

Correlations

Finally, we exploratively correlated the above-mentioned measures with each other. A performance measure of RTs was calculated by subtracting RTs of the Control Sequence from RTs of the Practice Sequence in Single-Task blocks of the Posttest. This index of sequence learning correlated with Free Generation Performance (response: r = 0.3; visual: r = 0.27) and Recognition Performance (response: r = 0.4; visual: r = 0.28, Note that for N = 87 the critical r = 0.21). Further, the increase in dual-task costs from Pretest to Posttest in the Practice Sequence correlated with the index of sequence learning (r = 0.76), Free Generation Performance (response: r = 0.35; visual: r = 0.32) and Recognition Performance (response: r = 0.22; visual: r = 0.21). However, the decrease in dual-task costs from Pretest to Posttest in the Control Sequence did not correlate with these sequence learning measures (−0.1 < r < 0.1).

Discussion

The aim of the present study was to investigate automatization in the SRTT controlling for task instruction (intentional or incidental learning), availability of anticipatory cues (with or without) and the presence of dual-task (single-task or dual-task). Participants showed sequence-specific learning across practice trials and conditions. After practice and contrary to our assumptions, we found a sequence-unspecific reduction in dual-task costs (indicating automatization) in the control sequence, but not in the practice sequence. In fact, dual-task costs in the practice sequence increased from Pretest to Posttest.

In the SRTT, RTs decreased from Pretest to Posttest in both the practice and the control sequences. These sequence-unspecific learning effects7,33,38, could be due to improved stimulus-response coupling39–41, or potentially due to testing effects42.

The present results replicate previous findings, showing that intentional learning has an advantage over incidental learning in the SRTT20,43. Sequence-specific learning, as measured by the RT differences between the practice and control sequences, was greater under intentional learning conditions than under incidental learning conditions. Consistent with this, free recall and recognition performance were higher under intentional than incidental learning conditions. These differences in recall and recognition performance were even more pronounced in the visual mode condition than in the response mode condition. Therefore, providing explicit visual sequence information in advance to sequence learning, enhances the learning process. However, this was not the case for anticipatory cues provided during practice—response times did not differ significantly when sequences were learnt with or without anticipatory cues. With adding cues, we intended to modulate anticipation and thus automatization of the sequence performance12,13. Though apart from some results indicating that anticipatory cues were actually detrimental for developing an explicit visual representation of the sequence, we did not find any influence on dual-task costs. This pattern contrasts with prior findings demonstrating that anticipation supported by extrinsic cues results in faster and more accurate performance than anticipation relying solely on intrinsic information44. From a theoretical perspective, such advantages may reflect a transient facilitation arising from externally provided predictive information rather than the formation of robust internal anticipatory models. Accordingly, performance may be enhanced as long as external cues remain available44, but becomes compromised when these cues are removed at test, as in the present study.

In the Pretest, RTs were longer under dual-task conditions than single-task conditions, reflecting the anticipated dual-task costs. This finding generally supports the view, that motor sequence learning and sequence production rely heavily on attention-dependent resources (i.e., spatial-system5; reacting mode and associative mode6). Consistent with this, response times in the SRTT increased when participants switched between the SRTT and the tone counting task, which required them to update a running count of the target tone.

In the Posttest, we found that dual-tasking significantly reduced RTs in the practice sequence, but had no effect on performance in the control sequence. Our finding that dual-task costs decreased from Pretest to Posttest in the control sequence is consistent with previous studies20,33 and are indicative of the attentional demands required for the motor task. However, this finding was contrary to our assumption that dual-task costs would decrease with practice. We expected that a higher degree of response automatization would free-up attentional resources to process tone counting. Consequently, we expected that automatization would reduce dual-task costs to the extent that tone counting can be performed in parallel with the SRTT28. Numerous studies have demonstrated that dual-task practice reduces dual-task costs24,29,34,45. This reduction in dual-task costs through practice seems to require practicing the combination of two tasks24,46. It has been suggested that these reductions in dual-task costs after dual-task practice, might not be due to automatization, but rather to optimized, processing of a specific dual-task combination or a specific temporal switching pattern of attentional demands (task integration)23. In our study, participants practiced the SRTT exclusively under single-task conditions, without combining it with the tone counting task during practice. Moreover, the tone counting task was externally paced, making it unlikely to be susceptible to changes in dual-task costs based on task switching performance. We suggest that increased practice leads to a more coherent and integrated memory representation, i.e., associations are formed between successive response features, stimulus features, or response-to-stimulus compounds across trials. This integrated representation of the sequence requires attentional resources to be maintained in working memory. This process is susceptible to interference when a secondary task relies on the same resources (e.g., maintaining information in working memory). Conversely, for the control sequence, it is not necessary to maintain the sequence representation, rather, processing occurs at the levels of stimulus encoding, response selection, and response execution47. According to the stages postulated by Verwey et al.6, attentional demands increase in the transition from the reacting mode to the associative mode. In the current setting, sequence learning may still be in progress in the associative mode, with progression to the automatic mode requiring even more extensive practice. Earlier research revealed lower dual-task costs in practiced sequences with six elements when complexity was comparably low36, but not with 12 elements when complexity was rather high35, while both studies used a continuous execution of the sequence comparable to the current study. From this perspective, it seems reasonable to assume that sequence complexity may moderate sequence-specific automatization effects, with shorter sequences being automatized after relatively little practice.

Interestingly, the demand for attentional control, as indicated by dual-task costs, increased for the practice sequence. This might refer to an increase of attentional control in the associative mode as compared to the reacting mode6, which is plausible given that sequence production based on explicit internal sequence representations (i.e., associative mode) requires more attentional resources than reacting to external stimulus information (i.e., reacting mode) in the initial phase of sequence learning. As the reactive mode develops into the associative mode, associations are formed between successive response features, stimulus features or response-to-stimulus compounds, thus establishing an explicit representation. This explicit representation requires more attentional resources for processing during stimulus encoding, response selection and response execution47 than a low-practiced (control) sequence does. According to this view, which aligns with the Triarchic Theory of Learning3, automaticity will develop after this temporary increase in attentional demands and dual-task costs. To the best of our knowledge, the current dataset is unique in showing an increase in dual-task costs for sequence production. However, the positive correlations between the increase in dual-task costs and the dependent measures of sequence learning and sequence representation suggest that establishing explicit sequence knowledge requires greater attentional control.

At this point, it remains unclear why the increase in dual-task costs was not moderated by the type of instruction (intentional versus incidental). One potential explanation is that the level of awareness developed during practice depends not only on the instructions received beforehand, but also on the individual strategies employed, and the awareness gained by participants during the task. For instance, some participants in the intentional group might have relied primarily on implicit processes despite being aware of the underlying sequence. Additionally, participants in the incidental group may have become aware of the underlying sequence, thereby engaging in processes related to explicit sequence learning22,48. This notion is supported by the exploratory correlational analysis, which indicates an association between explicit knowledge of the sequence on the final day of learning and resulting dual-task costs in SRTT responses. Nevertheless, it should be noted that the results of the generation and the recognition tasks showed that more explicit knowledge was generated under intentional than incidental learning conditions.

Rather than observing sequence-specific automatization, all groups automatized sequence-unspecific motor elements. In our study, this was indicated by a reduction in dual-task costs from Pretest to Posttest for the control sequence. This raises the question of why we found no task-switching costs in the less well-trained control sequence, indicating a parallel processing mode. In the dual-task condition, attentional resources appear to be shared when the sequence is less well trained, enabling both reacting to the visual stimuli of SRTT and the counting of tones to be carried out in parallel, albeit at a slower rate. This is indicated by the higher RTs in the single- and dual-task control conditions compared to the practice sequence49. One possible explanation is that participants may have automatized the stimulus-response coupling by strengthening the associations between stimuli and their corresponding responses33.

We assumed that controlling for a minimal response-stimulus interval in the SRTT would facilitate sequence-specific automatization. The idea was that maintaining a minimal response-stimulus interval would free-up cognitive resources to perform a secondary task, particularly in the practice sequence, where responses are given earlier than in the control sequence. Though this was not the case. Our findings showed that practicing a motor sequence over multiple days leads to sequence-specific learning. However, after practice, when the motor sequence had to be performed in parallel to a secondary tone counting task, dual-task costs increased rather than decreased. So why are sequence-specific movement representations not automatized, and why do dual-task costs increase in the SRTT? One possible explanation is that the explicit representation of a complex motor sequence develops with practice, leading to an increased demand for higher-order cognitive control that cannot be overcome with extensive practice.

Methods

Participants

All participants were between 19 and 35 years old. The data sets of six participants were excluded from analysis due to extremely high error rates in the SRTT (>50%), which indicates non-compliance with the task (guessing behavior). Primary task: serial reaction time task

In the SRTT, participants were prompted to respond as quickly as possible to a sequence of visual stimuli presented in one of four horizontally aligned boxes on the screen18. Responses were made using the ‘d’, ‘f’, ‘j’, and ‘k’ keys, with the index and middle fingers of each hand resting on these keys. Each block began with four empty boxes (2.5 × 2.5 cm) presented in the center of the screen. After 500 ms, one box turned blue, indicating the corresponding response. If a response was incorrect, the target remained on-screen until a correct response was provided. The task included two distinct twelve-element sequences: sequence A (j-f-j-d-k-f-k-d-f-d-j-k) and sequence B (d-f-j-d-j-f-k-j-k-d-k-f). Half of the participants practiced sequence A. The other half practiced sequence B. The unpracticed sequence was used as a Control Sequence to measure sequence learning. The sequences were structurally matched with each element appearing an equal number of times, avoiding consecutive repeats of the same element, and each transition between elements occurring at the same frequency, which eliminated the possibility of first-order learning18. The maximum overlap of consecutive elements between the sequences was limited to triplets (e.g., d-k-f). Figure 5 illustrates one of the sequences. Each block contained 120 trials, corresponding to ten repetitions of the twelve-element sequence. The starting point within each block was randomly selected. Random blocks were constructed with the same constraints as the fixed sequences, ensuring equal element frequency and the absence of consecutive repetitions. The timing procedure differed from that used in the previous study by Dahm and Krause20. Here, we implemented a minimum response-stimulus interval of 400 ms. Hence, if the correct response occurred within 400 ms, the next stimulus appeared 400 ms after the previous one. If the correct response took longer than 400 ms, the next stimulus followed immediately after the response. This procedure ensured that responses could not exceed a maximum of 150 responses per minute.

Depiction of the instructions.

Experimental sequences were shown to participants in the explicit sequence learning groups with A internal anticipation and B external anticipation.

Secondary task: tone counting

In sessions involving the Tone Counting Task (TCT)51, a short tone discrimination task was performed first to check and adjust participants’ individual sound settings. The Tone Discrimination Task involved a 100 ms target tone (440 Hz) and a 100 ms distractor tone (698 Hz), which were first presented along with the instructions. Then, one of the tones was presented again (random order). Participants could adjust the loudness of the tones according to their needs and setting. In case the volume was appropriate, they indicated whether it was a target tone or a distractor tone. The discrimination task ended if four tones were consecutively identified correctly.

In the TCT51, participants were instructed to count the number of target tones in each block and report the total amount of target tones at the end of the block. Target tones, distractor tones, or no-tone events were presented on each tone-incident. Each block consisted of twenty tone-incidents with target tones, distractor tones, or no-tones randomly assigned under the constraint that each type appeared at least four and most twelve times per block. In single TCT blocks, the interval between tone incidents varied randomly between 2000 and 4000 ms.

To provoke dual-task costs in RTs of the SRTT, participants were asked to prioritize tone counting in dual-task blocks. In dual-task blocks, each twelve-element SRTT sequence contained exactly two tone-incidents, presented simultaneously with the onset of the visual stimulus51. At least two SRTT responses occurred between tone incidents. At the end of each block, participants indicated the number of target tones by clicking on response boxes labeled 1 to 12.

Task and procedure

The experiment was conducted on participants’ personal laptops in an out-of-the lab setting52 using OpenSesame 3.3.1453. The experiment file can be accessed at: https://osf.io/j28ps. Each of the eleven sessions lasted ~15 min. At the beginning of each session, participants were instructed to sit at a table in a quiet environment. Sessions were spaced roughly 24 h apart, with at least one night of sleep between each session.

There were ten days of practice between Pretest and Posttest, each involving ten blocks of 120 responses (1000 twelve-element sequence repetitions in total). Participants in the Incidental Instruction Groups were not informed that stimuli in the SRTT follow a spatial pattern. Participants in the Intentional Instruction Groups were informed about the spatial pattern. Prior to each practice session, all twelve elements were presented graphically (see Fig. 5), and participants were asked to memorize the sequence for at least three minutes. In the groups without anticipatory cues, the subsequent response-relevant stimulus (s + 1) could not be foreseeable from the stimulus material (s). In the groups with anticipatory cues the subsequent response-relevant stimulus (s + 1) was already predicted in the stimulus material (see Fig. 5B). To maintain salience of the actual target, which was a box filled with dark blue, the cue was presented with a light blue bounding of the box. After each block, participants received feedback on the mean response time for correct responses in that block. This feedback was intended to motivate participants to respond as fast and correct as possible54.

Session 1 started with ten Familiarization blocks, each containing 120 random responses. Note that a previous study20 included only one familiarization block. Here, we intended to reduce sequence-unspecific learning effects (e.g., stimulus-response coupling) between Pretest and Posttest by allowing for such learning already before the Pretest. Familiarization was followed by the Pretest. In the Pretest, single task blocks and dual-task blocks were performed with the Practice and the Control Sequence. Each block was performed twice in a counterbalanced order.

In Session 11, participants completed the Posttest, which was similar to the Pretest but included four additional tasks. First, to assess motor representations, participants undertook a Free Generation Test. In this test, they were asked to generate as many responses of the practiced sequence as possible. For this, they made twelve keyboard responses while viewing empty boxes. Next, to evaluate their visuospatial representations, they completed a Free Generation Test of the visuospatial stimuli, selecting the order of stimuli through twelve mouse clicks on one of four possible options. The third task aimed to test motor recognition and involved participants rating the likelihood that a previously completed block of 24 responses matched the Practice Sequence on a scale from 1 (very unlikely) to 9 (very likely). Finally, to assess visuospatial recognition, participants rated the likelihood that a visually presented sequence matched the Practice Sequence on a scale from 1 (very unlikely) to 9 (very likely). Both recognition tasks were conducted for the Practice Sequence as well as for the Control Sequences. The sequence order in the recognition tasks was randomized.

Data analysis

The error rate in Pre- and Posttests was M ± SD = 2.4 ± 2.3%. Response time (RT) was defined as the time between stimulus onset and the correct response. For the RT analysis, the first twelve responses in each test block were removed (Pretest-, Posttest-, and Practice blocks). The median of the remaining 108 correct responses was then averaged from the identical blocks. To assess recall performance in the Free Generation Tests, we calculated the number of triplets that aligned with the Practice Sequence and the Control Sequence55. In this context, a score of 12 represents a complete match, while a score of 0 indicates no sequence match. Additionally, we analyzed ratings for visuospatial and motor sequence recognition. To do this, we calculated a difference score between the ratings in the Control Sequence and Practice Sequence.

The dependent variables were analyzed using mixed analyses of variance (ANOVA). If Mauchly’s test indicated that the assumption of sphericity was violated, Greenhouse-Geisser corrected degrees of freedom and p-values were reported. Further comparisons were conducted using t-tests with Holm-adjusted pairwise comparisons. Where appropriate, we report minimum (pmin) or maximum (pmax) statistical values. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. For explorative analyses, we calculated Pearson correlations between implicit and explicit measures of sequence learning and changes in dual-task costs.
