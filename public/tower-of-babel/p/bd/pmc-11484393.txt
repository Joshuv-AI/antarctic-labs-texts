The predictive value of stress-induced hyperglycemia parameters for delayed healing after tibial fracture post-surgery
Authors: Xiang Ji, Dunxu Zhao, Zetuan Xin, Hai Feng, Zhiyong Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe objective of this retrospective cohort study was to investigate the predictive value of stress-induced hyperglycemia parameters for delayed healing after tibial fracture post-surgery.MethodsA cohort of 108 participants who underwent surgical intervention for tibial fractures caused by trauma was included in this retrospective study. Data collected from electronic medical records encompassed demographic characteristics, bone healing assessments, stress-induced hyperglycemia parameters, inflammatory markers, and stress-related hormones. Comparative analyses, correlation analyses, univariate logistic regression analyses, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were conducted to assess the predictive value of the studied parameters.ResultsThe delayed healing group exhibited higher levels of fasting blood glucose, postprandial glucose, and HbA1c, as well as elevated levels of inflammatory markers and stress-related hormones compared to the normal healing group. Correlation analysis and logistic regression demonstrated positive associations between stress-induced hyperglycemia parameters, inflammatory markers, stress-related hormones, and delayed union of tibial fractures (R2: 0.183 ~ 0.403;OR: 1.091 ~ 16.332). ROC curve analysis revealed high area under the curve (AUC = 0.911) values for stress-induced hyperglycemia parameters, indicating their potential as predictive markers for delayed healing. Multivariate regression analysis further substantiated the predictive capability of stress-induced hyperglycemia parameters.ConclusionThe study findings highlight the complex interplay between stress-induced hyperglycemia, inflammatory response, and bone healing outcomes in patients undergoing surgical intervention for tibial fractures. The identification of stress-induced hyperglycemia parameters as potential predictive markers for delayed healing after tibial fracture surgery offers insights for risk assessment and patient management, emphasizing the need for comprehensive understanding of these factors to optimize postoperative recovery in orthopedic patients.Keywords: Stress-induced hyperglycemia, Delayed healing, Tibial fracture, Predictive

Introduction

Tibial fractures, including diaphyseal, plateau, and distal fractures, account for 4.0%, 1.7%, and 1.0% of all adult skeletal fractures, respectively [1]. These fractures are associated with severe soft tissue injuries, posing significant treatment challenges and an increased risk of long-term complications such as post-traumatic arthritis [2]. Tibial fractures are common and can occur due to various reasons, including high-energy trauma such as motor vehicle accidents, sports injuries, and direct blows to the leg, as well as low-energy injuries like falls or twisting of the leg [3]. These fractures can lead to significant morbidity and impaired quality of life for the affected individuals [4]. The tibia, being one of the weight-bearing bones of the lower extremity, plays a crucial role in mobility and stability, and any impairment in its function due to fracture can have a substantial impact on the individual’s daily activities [5]. Surgery is the primary approach in clinical practice for the treatment of tibial fractures [6]. It aims to maintain the stability of the tibia in patients by open reduction and internal fixation or open reduction and external fixation, restoring its alignment, position, and associated functions [7]. Post-surgical complications in tibial fracture patients, including delayed healing, can have a significant impact on patient outcomes [8].

Typically, tibial fracture patients experience callus formation around 12 weeks post-surgery, with clinical healing achieved in approximately 70 to 80 days [9–11]. However, clinical studies have revealed that even among patients with similar fracture severity, there are variations in the speed of fracture healing [12–14]. Reports indicate that the incidence of delayed or non-union fracture healing post-surgery in some tibial fracture patients ranges from 5–20% [15–17].

Stress-induced hyperglycemia refers to the transient increase in blood glucose levels in response to the physiological stress of surgery, trauma, or critical illness [18, 19]. This phenomenon is particularly relevant in the context of orthopedic surgery, as it has been associated with various adverse postoperative outcomes, including impaired wound healing, increased risk of surgical site infections, and prolonged hospital stays [20, 21]. Several studies have demonstrated the detrimental effects of hyperglycemia on wound healing and tissue repair, highlighting its potential role as a predictive factor for delayed healing after orthopedic procedures, such as tibial fracture surgery [22, 23]. The impact of stress-induced hyperglycemia on postoperative outcomes is of particular concern in orthopedic patients, as optimal bone healing and restoration of function are paramount to achieving successful surgical outcomes in this patient population [24, 25].

Given the growing body of evidence implicating stress-induced hyperglycemia in orthopedic surgery outcomes, there is a compelling need to further elucidate the specific parameters of hyperglycemia that may serve as predictive markers for delayed healing after tibial fracture surgery. Understanding the intricate interplay between hyperglycemia and delayed healing at a molecular, cellular, and clinical level is essential for developing targeted interventions aimed at mitigating the adverse effects of hyperglycemia and optimizing the postoperative recovery of orthopedic surgery patients.

By investigating the predictive value of stress-induced hyperglycemia parameters in the context of delayed healing after tibial fracture surgery, this study aims to assess their potential as predictive markers and to improve the clinical management of patients undergoing tibial fracture surgery.

Materials and methods

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

Patients with a confirmed diagnosis of tibial fractures based on clinical and radiological assessments [1]; Patients who underwent surgery at our hospital for tibial fractures caused by trauma; Availability of complete medical records, including demographic information, bone healing outcomes, stress-induced hyperglycemia parameters, inflammatory markers, and stress-related hormones; Adults aged 18 years and above.

Exclusion criteria

Patients with pre-existing diabetes mellitus or a documented history of diabetes diagnosis prior to the tibial fracture; Patients with known endocrine disorders or conditions that could significantly impact bone healing, such as hyperthyroidism or Cushing’s syndrome; Patients with a history of chronic inflammatory conditions, autoimmune diseases, or malignancies that could affect bone healing and inflammatory marker levels; Patients with a history of conditions known to impact stress-related hormone levels, such as pheochromocytoma or adrenal insufficiency; Patients with a history of previous surgeries in the affected area, regardless of the nature of the surgery; Patients with a history of long-term corticosteroid therapy or other medications known to affect bone metabolism and healing; Patients with significant comorbidities that could influence bone healing and glycemic control, such as chronic kidney disease or chronic liver disease.

Study design and participants

A retrospective cohort study was conducted to assess the predictive value of stress-induced hyperglycemia parameters for delayed healing after tibial fracture post-surgery. The study aimed to analyze the association between stress-induced hyperglycemia parameters, bone healing outcomes, and inflammatory markers in patients with tibial fractures who underwent surgical intervention. The study included 108 participants who were divided into two groups based on their healing outcomes: a normal healing group (n = 51) and a delayed healing group (n = 57). The definition of normal union and delayed union was made by examination 80 days after fracture surgery [26, 27]. During the X-ray examination process, typically, anteroposterior and lateral X-rays are taken [28]. If there is any obstruction from internal fixation, oblique X-rays should also be obtained [29]. Typical radiographic features of nonunion include discontinuity of callus trabeculae, gap at the fracture site, sclerosis at the fracture site, medullary canal obliteration, fracture end resorption, osteoporosis, loosening or breakage of internal fixation, and pseudoarthrosis formation [30, 31].

Data collection

Data collection was performed by reviewing electronic medical records, including demographic characteristics (age, gender, BMI, smoking status, presence of diabetes, hypertension, fracture type, and surgery time), bone healing assessment (X-ray Union Score, bone mineral density, and callus formation), stress-induced hyperglycemia parameters (fasting blood glucose, postprandial glucose, and HbA1c), inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α), and stress-related hormones (cortisol, epinephrine, and norepinephrine).

Outcome measures

The primary outcome measure was to evaluate the predictive value of stress-induced hyperglycemia parameters for delayed healing after tibial fracture post-surgery. Secondary outcome measures included the assessment of bone healing outcomes, inflammatory markers, and stress-related hormones in relation to delayed healing.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic characteristics and outcome variables. Continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations, and categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Comparative analyses between the normal healing and delayed healing groups were conducted using independent t-tests, chi-square tests, and correlation analyses. Univariate logistic regression analyses were performed to assess the predictive value of stress-induced hyperglycemia parameters and other indicators for delayed union. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was utilized to evaluate the predictive performance of the studied parameters. Additionally, a KNN multivariate linear regression analysis was deployed to determine the predictive capability of stress-induced hyperglycemia parameters in combination.

Results

Demographic characteristics

The study included 108 participants, 51 in the normal healing group and 57 in the delayed healing group (Table 1). The mean age of the normal healing group was (35.25 ± 5.64) years, while the delayed healing group had a mean age of (36.58 ± 6.22) years, with no statistically significant difference observed between the two groups (t = 1.159, p = 0.249). Gender distribution was similar between the groups, with 23 males and 28 females in the normal healing group, and 27 males and 30 females in the delayed healing group (χ2 = 0.002, p = 0.966). No significant differences were found in BMI, smoking status, hypertension, open/closed fracture, comminuted/non-comminuted fracture, and surgery time between the two groups (p > 0.05). There was no significant difference between the two groups, which laid a foundation for the subsequent study of stress hyperglycemia parameters on delayed union of tibial fractures caused by trauma.

Bone Healing

Firstly, this study conducted an assessment of bone healing. The results demonstrated that there were statistically significant differences between the normal healing group and the delayed healing group (Table 2). The normal healing group exhibited a higher X-ray Union Score (8.94 ± 1.55) compared to the delayed healing group (7.96 ± 1.88), demonstrating a significant t-value of 2.979 and a p-value of 0.004. Similarly, bone mineral density was higher in the normal healing group (0.81 ± 0.06) compared to the delayed healing group (0.77 ± 0.09), with a t-value of 2.852 and a p-value of 0.005. Callus formation, measured in square centimeters, was also greater in the normal healing group (5.62 ± 1.25) than in the delayed healing group (4.84 ± 1.56), showing a significant t-value of 2.865 and a p-value of 0.005.

Stress-Induced Hyperglycemia parameters

Next, this study compared markers of stress hyperglycemia in the normal healing group with those in the delayed healing group (Fig. 1). Fasting blood glucose levels were significantly higher in the delayed healing group (110.87 ± 15.65 mg/dL) compared to the normal healing group (94.55 ± 10.28 mg/dL) with a t-value of 6.465 and p < 0.001. Similarly, postprandial glucose levels were significantly elevated in the delayed healing group (140.28 ± 20.54 mg/dL) compared to the normal healing group (120.51 ± 15.64 mg/dL) with a t-value of 5.803 and p < 0.001. Additionally, HbA1c levels were also significantly higher in the delayed healing group (6.14 ± 0.67%) compared to the normal healing group (5.23 ± 0.45%), demonstrating a t-value of 8.288 and p < 0.001.

Inflammatory markers and stress-related hormones

To assess stress-related inflammatory markers and hormones in the normal healing and delayed healing groups, the following studies were performed (Table 3). C-reactive protein levels were significantly higher in the delayed healing group (5.08 ± 2.55 mg/L) than in the normal healing group (3.57 ± 2.45 mg/L), with a t-value of 3.14 and p = 0.002. Similarly, interleukin-6 levels were elevated in the delayed healing group (16.76 ± 5.24 pg/mL) compared to the normal healing group (12.41 ± 6.55 pg/mL), with a t-value of 3.783 and p < 0.001. Tumor necrosis factor-α levels also showed a significant increase in the delayed healing group (10.65 ± 4.87 pg/mL) compared to the normal healing group (8.49 ± 3.76 pg/mL), with a t-value of 2.598 and p = 0.011. Moreover, stress-related hormones cortisol, epinephrine, and norepinephrine exhibited significant elevations in the delayed healing group (cortisol: 25.54 ± 7.29 µg/dL, epinephrine: 50.39 ± 15.47 pg/mL, norepinephrine: 60.36 ± 21.67 pg/mL) compared to the normal healing group (cortisol: 15.02 ± 5.37 µg/dL, epinephrine: 34.33 ± 11.35 pg/mL, norepinephrine: 42.58 ± 15.21 pg/mL), with respective t-values of 8.598, 6.195, and 4.975, and all with p < 0.001.

Correlation analysis

The correlation analysis of various indicators for delayed union of tibial fractures caused by trauma revealed several significant findings (Table 4). X-ray Union Score, Bone Mineral Density, and Callus Formation showed negative correlations with delayed union, with respective R2 values of 0.076, 0.069, and 0.07, all with p-values less than 0.01. Conversely, Fasting Blood Glucose, Postprandial Glucose, HbA1c, C-reactive Protein, Interleukin-6, Cortisol, Epinephrine, and Norepinephrine exhibited positive correlations with delayed union, with strong R-squared values ranging from 0.183 to 0.403, all with p-values less than 0.001.

ROC

Based on the results presented in Table 6, various indicators were evaluated for their predictive value in delayed union of tibial fractures caused by trauma. The X-ray Union Score demonstrated a sensitivity of 0.386, specificity of 0.922, with an AUC of 0.649 and a Youden index of 0.308. Bone Mineral Density exhibited a sensitivity of 0.614, specificity of 0.608, with an AUC of 0.636 and a Youden index of 0.222. Furthermore, Callus Formation (cm²) showed a sensitivity of 0.632, specificity of 0.686, with an AUC of 0.647 and a Youden index of 0.318. Fasting Blood Glucose, Postprandial Glucose, and HbA1c demonstrated sensitivities of 0.614, 0.561, and 0.772 respectively, with specificities of 0.922, 0.922, and 0.863, and AUCs of 0.81, 0.784, and 0.868, along with Youden indices of 0.536, 0.483, and 0.635. Moreover, C-reactive Protein, Interleukin-6, Tumor Necrosis Factor-α, Cortisol, Epinephrine, and Norepinephrine displayed varying sensitivities, specificities, AUCs, and Youden indices. These findings provide valuable insights into the predictive capabilities of the evaluated indicators for delayed union of tibial fractures, thereby contributing to the potential development of effective diagnostic and prognostic approaches in this clinical context. Finally, this study performed a knn multivariate linear regression analysis on the combination of three stress-induced hyperglycemia parameters - fasting blood glucose, postprandial glucose, and HbA1c - in relation to delayed union of tibial fractures caused by trauma (Fig. 2). The results revealed an AUC value of 0.911.

Discussion

The present study aimed to investigate the predictive value of stress-induced hyperglycemia parameters for delayed healing after tibial fracture post-surgery. Our findings have revealed valuable insights into the complex interplay between stress-induced hyperglycemia, inflammatory markers, stress-related hormones, and bone healing outcomes in patients undergoing surgical intervention for tibial fractures caused by trauma. Notably, one of the key findings was the significant elevation of stress-induced hyperglycemia parameters, including fasting blood glucose, postprandial glucose, and HbA1c, in the delayed healing group compared to the normal healing group, highlighting their potential as predictive markers for delayed healing.

In addition, assessment of bone healing outcomes, including X-ray Union Score, bone mineral density, and callus formation, demonstrated significant differences between the normal healing and delayed healing groups. The normal healing group exhibited superior X-ray Union Scores, bone density, and callus formation, highlighting the correlation between these indicators and favorable healing outcomes. Similar results were obtained in the study by Schubert I et al. [30]. These results underscore the critical role of bone healing parameters in gauging the progression of recovery following tibial fracture surgery and lay the groundwork for investigating the influence of stress-induced hyperglycemia on delayed healing.

Importantly, our study revealed that stress-induced hyperglycemia parameters, including fasting blood glucose, postprandial glucose, and HbA1c levels, were significantly elevated in the delayed healing group compared to the normal healing group compared to the normal healing group. This observation underscores the potential impact of stress-induced hyperglycemia on delayed union after tibial fracture surgery and emphasizes the importance of glycemic control in this patient population. Previous research has highlighted the detrimental effects of hyperglycemia on bone healing and surgical outcomes [32–34]. Hyperglycemia has been shown to impair wound healing and increase the risk of complications, such as surgical-site infections and delayed bone union [32, 35]. These findings suggest that elevated glucose levels may serve as predictive markers for delayed healing, shedding light on the complex relationship between hyperglycemia and the healing process.

Furthermore, our investigation into stress-related inflammatory markers and hormones revealed significant elevations in the delayed healing group, pointing to the presence of a stress-induced inflammatory response that may further influence the delayed healing process. The increased levels of cortisol, epinephrine, norepinephrine, C-reactive protein, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α in the delayed healing group underscore the multifaceted nature of the physiological stress response following tibial fracture surgery. The study by Laggner R et al. also pointed out that C-reactive protein has a physiological stress response to fracture healing after surgery [36]. These findings highlight the potential impact of stress-induced hyperglycemia and the associated inflammatory response on delayed healing, reinforcing the need for a comprehensive understanding of the intricate interplay between these factors to optimize postoperative recovery in orthopedic patients.

The predictive value of the evaluated indicators was further elucidated through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, which demonstrated the sensitivities, specificities, and area under the curve (AUC) values for each parameter. Notably, stress-induced hyperglycemia parameters, including fasting blood glucose, postprandial glucose, and HbA1c, exhibited high AUC values, underscoring their potential as valuable predictors of delayed union after tibial fracture surgery. The multivariate regression analysis further substantiated the predictive capability of stress-induced hyperglycemia parameters, emphasizing their collective influence on the risk assessment of delayed healing in this patient population.

Our study’s findings have several implications for clinical practice and future research in the field of orthopedic surgery. Firstly, the identification of stress-induced hyperglycemia parameters as potential predictive markers for delayed healing after tibial fracture surgery offers a novel avenue for risk assessment and patient management. Clinicians may consider incorporating targeted glycemic control strategies in the perioperative care of tibial fracture patients to mitigate the risk of delayed healing and improve postoperative outcomes. Additionally, the association between stress-induced hyperglycemia and delayed union underscores the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in the management of orthopedic patients, involving collaboration between orthopedic surgeons, endocrinologists, and anesthesiologists to optimize perioperative glycemic control and its potential impact on bone healing.

This study bears several strengths, including a well-defined study cohort, comprehensive data collection encompassing demographic characteristics, bone healing assessments, stress-induced hyperglycemia parameters, inflammatory markers, and stress-related hormones, as well as robust statistical analyses to elucidate the predictive value of the studied parameters. However, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The retrospective nature of the study may have introduced inherent biases, and the single-center design may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future research endeavors should aim to conduct prospective, multicenter studies to validate and further refine the predictive value of stress-induced hyperglycemia parameters for delayed healing after tibial fracture surgery. Additionally, the inclusion of a broader array of clinical and laboratory parameters, such as nutritional status, immune function, and genetic factors, could enrich our understanding of the complex interplay of factors influencing delayed healing in this patient population. Finally, the bone healing assessment was primarily based on X-ray examinations rather than more precise imaging techniques such as CT scans, which would allow for more accurate measurements of bone healing and the percentage of bone healing achieved.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our investigation revealed that stress-induced hyperglycemia parameters, such as fasting blood glucose, postprandial glucose, and HbA1c, were significantly elevated in the delayed healing group compared to the normal healing group. These findings highlight the potential of these parameters as predictive markers for delayed healing after tibial fracture surgery, emphasizing the importance of glycemic control in improving clinical outcomes.
