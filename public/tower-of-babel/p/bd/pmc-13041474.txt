Background

Amaurosis fugax is a classic clinical presentation of carotid arterial embolism. Amaurosis results from ischemia to the retinal tissue, which receives blood supply from the ipsilateral internal carotid, ophthalmic artery (OA), central retinal artery as well as branch vessels in a proximal to distal distribution [1–3]. Clinically, a patient with amaurosis fugax will describe “a shade/curtain” in vision that comes down for a short period of time, although in the literature, the presentation often takes on a broader definition as any transient monocular vision loss [4, 5] Amaurosis fugax is vision threatening and an ophthalmological emergency, but if the underlying etiology is self-limiting or treated, there may be no long-term observable defect [6]. Stenoses isolated within the distal arteries supplying the retina (OA and more distal branches) as a cause of transient visual loss are likely underdiagnosed, since the evaluation of vascular causes are typically focused on large vessel disease (carotid arteries) resulting in embolic events, not localized abnormalities of the OA. Isolated stenosis of the OA may also be underrecognized by clinicians, as its discussion appears limited to case reports within the literature [7–13].

Our case report describes a young and otherwise healthy female patient without typical risk factors who presented with features suggestive of amaurosis fugax related to OA stenosis. This report is important, as it may encourage physicians to include imaging analysis of the OA, particularly when other etiologies of amaurosis are not found. Identification of localized OA disease may then lead to appropriate therapy and prevent further episodes of visual loss, even in atypical patient populations, as it has in our case.

Methods

We report a patient presenting with amaurosis fugax and imaging evidence of OA stenosis. Clinical data collected include age, race, sex, ophthalmic and neuro-radiologic examination findings, and treatment approach and outcomes. Radiologic imaging was independently reviewed by a neuroradiologist and radiology resident (authors EJR and BCS respectively). We performed a structured search in PubMed using Boolean combinations of “amaurosis fugax,” “stenosis,” “ophthalmic artery,” and “vision loss” in the PubMed database. Articles from all dates were included in the search and were limited to the English language. Approximately 550 articles were populated. Titles and abstracts of these articles were reviewed, aiming to find cases in which monocular vision loss was attributed to ophthalmic artery disease. Studies were excluded when the etiology of amaurosis was deemed more attributable to more classical etiologies of amaurosis, including embolic events, or carotid artery disease, as these entities are well understood and not pertinent to the suspected pathophysiology in our case. Of the 550 articles, approximately 25 pertinent articles fit the above criteria and were completely analyzed. Of the 25 reports analyzed, seven were included in our literature review, as their conclusions were reasonably attributed to stenoses of the OA, and were supported by imaging confirmation. Age, gender, history of trauma, treatment approach, and clinical outcomes were recorded when possible. The study was exempt from approval from the Northwestern University Institutional Review Board because it is a case series of fewer than 5 patients.

Case report

A 39-year-old female presented with four distinct episodes of transient, painless monocular vision loss with increasing frequency. The patient described these episodes as “blurring to black out” of her temporal visual field in the right eye. She could not recall if the vision loss extended past midline. The first episode of vision loss occurred 15 years before seeking medical care, with similar events occurring 4 years and 6 months before the fourth and final episode, which finally prompted the patient to seek medical evaluation. The patient urgently saw her primary care physician, who ordered a screening magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain and an urgent evaluation with neuro-ophthalmology. MRI of the brain with and without contrast was obtained within weeks and was read as unremarkable without evidence of ischemia, white matter changes related to migraines, or evidence of active demyelination. The patient underwent evaluation in neuro-ophthalmology clinic three days following the MRI by a board-certified neuro-ophthalmologist (author SS). A comprehensive ophthalmologic examination was completed, including visual acuity and color vision testing, visual field testing, intraocular pressure measurements, and dilated fundoscopic examination at the slit lamp. Ophthalmic imaging was obtained, including optical coherence tomography and color fundus photos (Figs. 1 and 2). Examination and testing were within normal limits during asymptomatic evaluation. She declined additional associated symptoms, including headaches, positional involvement, psychosocial contributors, or any other association per the recollection of the patient when queried. Modifiable risk factors for vascular disease were assessed at the time of evaluation, though given the age and good overall health of the patient, labs were not obtained at the time of evaluation. Given the negative screening MRI, the leading clinical considerations included ischemic events related to amaurosis fugax versus complex migraine. The latter diagnosis was considered in the setting of visual disturbances that lasted on the order of 10–20 min (with vision loss related to amaurosis typically spanning seconds to minutes). The patient also self-reported a personal history of migraine headaches, though the patient noted an absence of headaches at the time of visual symptoms (which does not exclude the diagnosis). A magnetic resonance angiogram (MRA) of the brain and neck arterial vasculature was ordered and obtained within two weeks of the neuro-ophthalmologic evaluation to evaluate for vascular etiologies of the patient’s symptoms. The MRA demonstrated a short segment of severe stenosis of the right OA spanning approximately 3 mm in length (Figs. 3a and 3b) and (Figs. 4a, and 4b). Given the imaging finding and otherwise non-specific symptomology, the patient was started on empiric treatment with Aspirin 81 mg daily with close clinical follow-up. Imaging follow-up with MRA was also obtained one year later, redemonstrating the same imaging finding. To date, the symptoms have not recurred following treatment, now demonstrating over three years of durability.

Discussion

Our case report describes a young and otherwise healthy female patient without typical risk factors who presented with four distinct episodes of amaurosis fugax with increasing frequency. Workup with MRA demonstrated a 3 mm long severe stenosis of the ipsilateral OA, confirmed on multiple MR pulse sequences and redemonstration of the findings on a one-year follow-up imaging with MRA. The patient has been on empiric daily aspirin 81 mg to date, for over 3 years without recurrence of symptoms.

Stenosis of the OA and its distal branch vessels is a relatively unknown and underdiagnosed etiology of amaurosis. In review of the literature, only seven examples of amaurosis could be reasonably attributed to narrowing of the OA with associated imaging confirmation(Tables 1 and 2) [7–13]. These case reports are further validated by resolution of symptoms when treated with anti-platelet, dual-anti-platelet and/or anti-coagulation [7–12]. The effectiveness of anti-platelet therapy in this entity is not surprising given its efficacy elsewhere in narrowed similar sized arteries in other areas of the body [13–15]. Interestingly, in a single report, following non-durability of an empiric fibrinolytic intervention and antiplatelet therapy for 2 months, angioplasty of a severe OA stenosis was performed, with subsequent resolution of symptoms, suggesting a possible role for angioplasty in selected refractory cases [13]. This case is particularly unique, as the patient represents the youngest patient (39 years old) to be included in the cohort of related case reports by over 10 years of age (49–74 years of age) [7–13]. The patient had no identifiable risk factors typically associated with amaurosis or atherosclerotic related stenosis, including the absence of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, obesity or medications that could produce clotting, such as estrogen containing contraceptives in a patient of her age, among others. Suspected mechanisms of vision disturbance in our patient, although unproven, could be congenital narrowing, transient narrowing of the stenotic segment of the OA related to retinal artery vasospasm, reversible cerebral vasoconstriction, or microvascular angina analogies [15]. Regardless of the specific entity underlying the observed OA narrowing, the patient continues to be asymptomatic to date on daily aspirin.

This case highlights the importance and the standard of care in using MRA and/or computed tomography angiography (CTA) for evaluation of arterial stenosis in patients presenting with amaurosis fugax as well as in cases of otherwise unexplained, unilateral, transient vision changes, even when the patient does not demonstrate typical risk factors [14]. MRA has proven to be both sensitive (greater than 88%) and specific (greater than 91%) in diagnosing significant stenosis in the head and neck as well as distal vasculature of similar luminal diameter to the OA elsewhere in the brain [2]. MRA is effective in evaluating carotid artery disease [16–18]. CTA may also be effective for evaluating the OA, although bone artifact as the artery passes through the optic canal may limit accuracy. CTA was deferred in this patient due to the reliable MRA findings and to avoid radiation exposure. Angiography may be reserved for refractory cases, as resolution of small vessels is better than MRA and CTA. Angiography remains the gold standard; however, it is often deferred, as it was in our case, due to its invasive nature and risk profile [19].

In this case, imaging findings of OA stenosis on non-contrast flow dependent time-of-flight MRA of the brain was further supported by persisting stenosis of the right OA on the non-flow dependent contrast enhanced portion of the MRA of the carotid arteries (Fig. 5). The stenosis was confirmed on two distinct MRA techniques (time-of- flight and contrast-enhanced), as well as temporally, as these images were not obtained concurrently but rather several minutes apart, limiting the chance for flow-related artifact to explain the narrowing, a common limitation of MRA [16]. Additionally, a follow-up MRA was obtained 1 year following the initial exam, with persisting OA stenosis in the same area of concern.

Given our patient’s overall healthy profile, treatments such as statins, dual-antiplatelet, anticoagulation, or even angioplasty, were not chosen, in favor of monotherapy with aspirin. As demonstrated in the literature review, monotherapy with aspirin has been utilized with success, and other additional treatment modalities could be considered if symptoms recurred. In our case, it is thought that the aspirin may inhibit transient platelet aggregation as blood flows through the narrowed vessel, though this is conjecture. Given the excellent and durable treatment response with empiric daily aspirin 81 mg without recurrence, transient retinal ischemia related to OA stenosis remains the leading diagnostic consideration in this case. In any case report, the findings and conclusions are limited due to including only a single patient, as well as lack of conventional transcatheter angiographic confirmation.

Conclusions

This report describes a 39-year-old healthy patient with multiple episodes of amaurosis related to short segment stenosis of the ipsilateral OA diagnosed on MRA. She was then treated with daily aspirin 81 mg without recurrence of symptoms for over three years. MRA and CTA remain the non-invasive gold standard for evaluation of carotid stenosis and sources of retinal emboli, and OA stenosis may be a rare but important and underdiagnosed etiology of amaurosis fugax which can be reliably evaluated on non-invasive vascular imaging. To our knowledge, this phenomenon has yet to be described in radiology literature with very few reports in ophthalmic and vascular-related journals. This case highlights the importance of evaluating patients with transient vision loss with high-resolution vascular imaging, including not only proximal arterial sources of retinal embolization, but also the OAs, and for referring physicians to consider asking the radiologist to specifically evaluate for this underrecognized entity. Recognition of this entity is important, even when the patient’s risk profile is not typical of amaurosis, as intervention may help prevent recurrence of symptoms as illustrated by this case.
