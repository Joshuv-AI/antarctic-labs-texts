Oncolytic virus hijacks GOT1 and pyrimidinosomes to fuel pyrimidine synthesis for replication in tumor cells
Authors: Ning Tang, Yabin Gong, Changrun Zhao, Yujia Niu, Lei Tan, Cuiping Song, Xusheng Qiu, Ying Liao, Shengqing Yu, Chan Ding, Shuhai Lin, Yingjie Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oncolytic viruses selectively infect and kill tumor cells, but the metabolic adaptations that support their replication remain incompletely understood. Here, using oncolytic Newcastle disease virus (NDV) as a model, we identify glutamic-oxaloacetic transaminase 1 (GOT1) as a key metabolic enzyme required for efficient viral replication through its dual role in de novo pyrimidine synthesis. In NDV-infected tumor cells, GOT1 promotes aspartate production through the malate-aspartate shuttle to support pyrimidine biosynthesis, while also maintaining NAD+/NADH homeostasis to activate the mTOR-S6K-CAD signaling axis and further enhance pyrimidine synthesis. These GOT1-dependent metabolic and signaling adaptations sustain pyrimidine biosynthesis and viral replication. In addition, NDV infection promotes pyrimidinosome assembly, and GOT1 functions as a pyrimidinosome-associated component. Together, these findings reveal a mechanism by which oncolytic NDV rewires host pyrimidine metabolism to support its replication and provide a rationale for metabolic modulation of oncolytic virotherapy.

Introduction

Viruses orchestrate intricate interactions with host cell metabolic networks, strategically rewiring cellular processes to meet the high demands of viral replication. This metabolic reconfiguration increases the availability of critical nutrients and metabolites, including nucleotides, amino acids, lipids, and energy (ATP), all of which are essential for supporting the viral life cycle. Key metabolic pathways are frequently hijacked by viruses including glucose and glutamine metabolism, fatty acid synthesis, nucleotide metabolism, and pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) [1,2,3]. Among these, nucleotides are particularly indispensable, as they are the building blocks of viral DNA or RNA, forming the core of viral genetic material and structure. Like cancer cells, viral infections exhibit an increased dependence on nucleotide synthesis to support their rapid replication [4,5]. Consequently, restricting nucleotide availability can effectively suppress viral proliferation by targeting the nucleotide synthesis pathway, highlighting potential therapeutic vulnerabilities [[6], [7], [8]]. Two key enzymes, inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase (IMPDH) and dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH), which are essential for the synthesis of purines and pyrimidines, respectively, have been extensively validated as broad-spectrum antiviral targets [[9], [10], [11]]. Unlike DNA viruses, RNA viruses incorporate uridine monophosphate (UMP), a nucleotide produced specifically by DHODH, into their genomes instead of thymidine monophosphate. This distinction suggests that RNA viruses may depend more heavily on the pyrimidine synthesis pathway [8]. The multifunctional enzyme Carbamoyl-phosphate synthetase 2, Aspartate transcarbamylase, Dihydroorotase (CAD) catalyzes the first three steps of de novo pyrimidine biosynthesis, forming dihydroorotate with a completed pyrimidine ring, which is then oxidized by DHODH to produce orotate, a precursor for pyrimidine nucleotides [12]. Both CAD and DHODH are recognized as rate-limiting enzymes in this pathway [13,14]. While DHODH has been widely validated as an antiviral target, the role of CAD and the intermediate metabolites involved in the early steps of pyrimidine synthesis during RNA virus infection has been less extensively studied.

The de novo pyrimidine biosynthesis pathway begins with CAD catalyzing the first three steps, utilizing glutamine, carbon dioxide (CO2), and aspartate to assemble the pyrimidine ring [12]. The primary regulatory mechanism of CAD is phosphorylation, particularly through the mTORC1 and MAPK signaling pathways, which link CAD activity to cellular growth and proliferation [15,16]. mTORC1 acts as a central regulator, integrating signals from nutrients, energy availability, growth factors, oxygen levels, and cellular stress to coordinate cellular growth and metabolism [17]. Under favorable conditions, mTORC1 phosphorylates CAD via S6 kinase 1 (S6K1), enhancing nucleotide production to support growth and division [15,18]. Aspartate, a key raw material in pyrimidine biosynthesis, uniquely contributes both carbon and nitrogen to the pyrimidine ring [19]. Its availability is tightly regulated and dependent on mitochondrial metabolism [20,21]. Notably, during infection, many viruses disrupt mitochondrial dynamics to facilitate their proliferation [22,23], which may render aspartate a metabolic bottleneck in the context of viral infections.

The malate-aspartate shuttle (MAS) facilitates the transfer of reducing equivalents (NADH) from the cytoplasm into mitochondria and works in tandem with transamination reactions to maintain aspartate availability. MAS is essential for aspartate utilization as it ensures that mitochondrial aspartate is transported to the cytoplasm, where it is required for biosynthetic processes [20,24]. The cytosolic glutamic-oxaloacetic transaminase (GOT) 1 and mitochondrial GOT2 are key players in MAS and aspartate synthesis. Mitochondrial GOT2 catalyzes the production of aspartate from mitochondrial oxaloacetate and glutamate, supplying aspartate for export to the cytoplasm to support nucleotide biosynthesis [20,24]. Additionally, aspartate can also be generated in the cytoplasm through a reversible reaction catalyzed by GOT1 [25]. GOT1 is a bidirectional enzyme that, under normal conditions, utilizes aspartate to support electron transfer into mitochondria. However, when the electron transport chain (ETC) is inhibited, GOT1 operates in reverse to produce aspartate in the cytosol, partially compensating for the reduced mitochondrial synthesis of aspartate [21]. Numerous viruses have been reported to disrupt mitochondrial dynamics, which is likely to impair ETC function and reduce aspartate production [26,27]. This raises the question of how aspartate is supplied under these conditions to meet the increased demand for purine and pyrimidine synthesis required for viral replication.

Newcastle disease virus (NDV) is a highly contagious and widespread avian virus that also acts as an oncolytic virus, selectively infecting tumor cells [28,29]. A very recent study developed an engineered NDV strain that safely lysed tumors in humans, achieving a 90% disease control rate in a clinical trial of 20 patients with refractory cancers and distant metastases, demonstrating its great potential as an oncolytic virus [30]. Our previous studies demonstrated that NDV replicates efficiently in various tumor cells, highlighting its ability to rapidly hijack host metabolic resources for replication [[31], [32], [33]]. Specifically, NDV disrupts mitochondrial dynamics and induces mitophagy to reprogram energy metabolism [34]. Furthermore, NDV manipulates the mitochondrial one-carbon pathway to support viral nucleotide synthesis [35]. Both tumor cells and virus-infected cells share a reliance on nucleotide synthesis to sustain their unrestrained proliferation. However, it remains unclear how oncolytic viruses reprogram nucleotide metabolism to hijack nucleotides from tumor cells for efficient replication. In this study, we used large-scale targeted metabolomics to confirm the critical role of pyrimidine synthesis in NDV replication. Notably, cytoplasmic GOT1 employs a "one-stone-for-two-birds" strategy, simultaneously regulating endogenous aspartate synthesis and phosphorylating CAD to enhance viral replication. Interestingly, NDV-induced pyrimidine biosynthesis appears to be orchestrated by the formation of the pyrimidinosome—a recently discovered multienzyme complex in which enzymes from different subcellular compartments collaborate to enable efficient pyrimidine biosynthesis.

Results

De novo pyrimidine synthesis is essential for NDV replication

NDV efficiently replicates in diverse cell types, demonstrating its ability to rapidly commandeer host metabolic pathways for its replication [[31], [32], [33]]. To identify differential metabolites and metabolic pathways that facilitate NDV replication in infected cells, we conducted large-scale targeted metabolomics using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) to analyze the cellular metabolome at various time points post-infection. Metabolic pathway enrichment analysis of these differential metabolites revealed significant enrichment in pathways related to purine metabolism, pyrimidine metabolism, and glutamate and aspartate metabolism, all of which are closely linked to nucleotide metabolism (Fig. 1A). Heat maps of metabolite abundance related to nucleotide synthesis at various time points following NDV infection showed significant alterations in pathways such as glycolysis, the PPP, one-carbon metabolism, and pyrimidine and purine nucleotide synthesis. Of note, pyrimidine metabolites, such as glutamine, aspartate, carbamoyl-phosphate, orotate and UMP, were rapidly consumed at the late stage of infection (Fig. 1B). [U-13C] glucose was further used to trace its incorporation into nucleotide synthesis. Labeled glucose isoforms were synthesized into R5P, which was then processed into downstream purine or pyrimidine nucleotides (Fig. 1C). To investigate whether NDV regulates pyrimidine and purine nucleotide metabolism to facilitate its replication, we used leflunomide (Lef), a DHODH inhibitor, to block the pyrimidine synthesis pathway, as well as AG2037, an inhibitor of phosphoribosylglycinamide formyltransferase (GART), which catalyzes the third step of de novo purine biosynthesis by converting glycinamide ribonucleotide (GAR) to formylglycinamide ribonucleotide (FGAR) using 10-formyltetrahydrofolate as the formyl donor [36]. We also used mycophenolic acid (MPA), an inhibitor of inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase (IMPDH), to block de novo guanine nucleotide biosynthesis [37] (Fig. 1E–F). The results showed that inhibition of the de novo pyrimidine synthesis pathway, but not purine synthesis, significantly impaired NDV replication (Fig. 1G). The observed consumption of glutamine, aspartate, and carbamoyl-phosphate following NDV infection led us to speculate about the potential involvement of the first three steps of the pyrimidine synthesis pathway. To explore this, NDV replication was demonstrated upon CAD knocked down. The results showed that CAD knockdown significantly reduced NDV viral nucleoprotein (NP) expression and viral titer, while supplementation with the downstream metabolite dihydroorotate rescued viral replication in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1H–I). To further assess whether this dependence on de novo pyrimidine biosynthesis extends to other oncolytic viruses, vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV), a well-characterized oncolytic virus [38,39], was examined in parallel. Similar to NDV, VSV replication was also sensitive to leflunomide-mediated inhibition of pyrimidine biosynthesis (Fig. S1A and S1B). Furthermore, CAD exhibited significant time-dependent phosphorylation following NDV infection (Fig. 1J–K). Collectively, these results demonstrate that NDV reprograms host nucleotide metabolism, with the de novo pyrimidine synthesis pathway playing a crucial role in viral replication.

NDV hijacks glutamine metabolism to support aspartate-driven pyrimidine synthesis

Aspartate, a non-essential amino acid obtained through intracellular conversion or extracellular uptake, serves as a critical substrate in the initial three steps of the de novo pyrimidine synthesis pathway (Fig. 2A). The potential role of aspartate in NDV replication was subsequently evaluated. NDV replication remained largely unaffected by the supplementation of varying doses of aspartate, suggesting that extracellular aspartate uptake does not influence virus replication (Fig. 2B). In contrast, treatment with aminooxyacetate (AOA), a GOT inhibitor that disrupts the MAS and impairs aspartate synthesis [40], effectively inhibited NDV replication without significantly affecting cell viability at the concentrations used in this study (Fig. 2C and Fig. S1C–D). Likewise, knockdown of either GOT1 or GOT2 led to a significant reduction in NDV replication (Fig. 2D). Importantly, supplementation with aspartate successfully restored NDV replication under both AOA treatment and siGOT1/2 conditions (Fig. 2C and E). Aspartate can be synthesized from glucose via intermediates such as acetyl-CoA or through pyruvate carboxylase (PC), or from glutamine, which converts into tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediates and is transported to the cytoplasm as oxaloacetate via the MAS [41]. To trace the source of aspartate during NDV infection, we conducted stable isotope tracing experiments by culturing NDV-infected and mock-infected A549 cells with [U-13C] Glucose or [U-13C] Glutamine (Fig. 2F). NDV infection increased glucose uptake, but pyruvate was primarily converted to lactate (Fig. 2G), reducing its mitochondrial transfer for acetyl-CoA production. NDV infection also significantly downregulated isotopologues of isocitrate [M+2], α-KG [M+2], fumarate [M+2], malate [M+2] (Fig. 2H), as well as aspartate [M+2] and [M+3] (Fig. 2I). Glutamine metabolism, divided into oxidative and reductive pathways, contributes to the TCA cycle and aspartate synthesis. Oxidative metabolism of [U-13C] glutamine generates aspartate [M+4] and citrate [M+4], while reductive carboxylation produces aspartate [M+3] and citrate [M+5] (Fig. 2F). As expected, NDV infection enhanced glutamine flux through the TCA cycle, replacing glucose as the primary carbon source (Fig. 2J). NDV also increased aspartate flux through oxidative and reductive carboxylation pathways (Fig. 2K). To investigate whether NDV hijacks aspartate for pyrimidine synthesis or as an anaplerotic substrate for the TCA cycle, we performed stable isotope tracing using [U-13C] aspartate (Fig. 2L). Interestingly, despite NDV replication being unaffected by exogenous aspartate deletion, we observed aspartate uptake post-infection. Additionally, increased 13C enrichment in malate and UMP isotopologues (Fig. 2M) indicated aspartate contributes to both the TCA cycle and pyrimidine synthesis. Consistent with isotope tracing, glucose supplementation under AOA treatment failed to rescue NDV replication due to the inability to convert glucose to aspartate, and glutamine supplementation also failed due to MAS disruption. Unexpectedly, pyruvate supplementation significantly rescued virus replication to a similar extent as aspartate supplementation as a positive control (Fig. 2N). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that NDV remodels the glutamine redox pathway to synthesize aspartate, supporting pyrimidine synthesis and viral replication.

Pyruvate restores NDV replication under AOA treatment by regulating the NAD+/NADH balance and pyrimidine biosynthesis

The finding that pyruvate supplementation successfully restored NDV replication under AOA treatment was unexpected, as isotopic tracing results clearly demonstrated that aspartate was derived from glutamine rather than glucose or its downstream product pyruvate during NDV infection (Fig. 2F–N). Next, we investigated the ‘aspartate-independent’ role of the MAS in NDV replication, focusing on its connection to pyruvate. Pyruvate plays a central metabolic role, either being decarboxylated to acetyl-CoA for entry into the TCA cycle and aspartate production, or serving as an electron acceptor by being converted to lactate to consume excess NADH [42]. These pathways are specifically inhibited by UK5099, which targets the mitochondrial pyruvate carrier (MPC), and GSK2837808A, which inhibits lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) (Fig. 3A). The results showed that treatment with GSK2837808A, but not UK5099, reversed the rescue effect of pyruvate supplementation on NDV replication (Fig. 3B–C). Consistently, GSK2837808A, but not UK5099, inhibited lactate production, confirming that GSK2837808A effectively blocks the conversion of pyruvate to lactate (Fig. 3D). To further investigate, we performed [U-13C] Pyruvate stable isotope-assisted metabolic labeling to evaluate the role of pyruvate in the presence or absence of AOA (Fig. 3E). As expected, pyruvate uptake (M+3) was significantly increased and predominantly converted to lactate (M+3) following AOA treatment (Fig. 3F). Moreover, although AOA treatment led to increased pyruvate levels, its contribution to aspartate synthesis, including pathways involving pyruvate carboxylase (M+3) and TCA cycle intermediates (M+2), was not enhanced (Fig. 3G). Instead, the percentage of unlabeled aspartate (M+0) was significantly elevated (data not shown). Correspondingly, pyruvate's contribution to TCA cycle intermediates was reduced in the AOA-treated groups (Fig. 3H). Metabolomic analysis further confirmed that AOA treatment reduced intracellular levels of pyrimidine-related metabolites. However, pyruvate supplementation increased lactate levels and, more importantly, restored the levels of pyrimidine intermediate metabolites, such as dihydroorotic acid and UDP (Fig. 3I). During the conversion of pyruvate to lactate, pyruvate functions as an electron acceptor, thereby regulating the intracellular NAD+/NADH ratio. As expected, during NDV infection, AOA treatment led to a decreased NAD+/NADH ratio, which was restored by the supplementation of pyruvate, but not aspartate (Fig. 3J). The NAD+/NADH system, beyond acting as a key electron carrier, plays a central role in cellular energy production, biosynthesis, and redox homeostasis. It regulates the AMPK and mTOR pathways via NAD+-dependent deacetylases, such as sirtuins, while AMPK and mTOR are known to control CAD phosphorylation [43]. Based on metabolomic data, we speculate that pyruvate regulates the NAD+/NADH ratio, which in turn influences CAD phosphorylation, thereby ensuring proper pyrimidine biosynthesis. Consistent with this hypothesis, AOA treatment significantly impaired NDV-induced CAD phosphorylation, which was restored by pyruvate supplementation but not by aspartate (Fig. 3K–L). NAD+ generation by the MAS promotes CAD phosphorylation and NDV replication through S6K activation

CAD-dependent de novo pyrimidine synthesis is regulated by mTORC1 through the phosphorylation of CAD-S1859 via S6K (pT389), with downstream S6 phosphorylation at S234/S236 serving as an additional marker [15,18]. Based on the results suggesting a potential link between the cytoplasmic NAD+/NADH ratio and CAD phosphorylation, we next investigated the downstream molecules of NAD+ involved in regulating CAD phosphorylation (Fig. 4A). We first demonstrated that NDV infection significantly increased the phosphorylation levels of S6 and CAD, while also upregulating the intracellular NAD+/NADH ratio in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 4B–D). In contrast, UV-inactivated NDV did not induce these changes (Fig. S2A–C), indicating that they are associated with replication-competent NDV infection. Consistent with the above results, AOA treatment significantly reduced CAD phosphorylation and the NAD+/NADH ratio, which was rescued by pyruvate supplementation but not by aspartate. Notably, the phosphorylation of S6K and S6 exhibited a similar pattern to that of CAD (Fig. 4E–G). To exclude a possible contribution of ROS, we supplemented AOA-treated cells with NAC. Although NAC effectively reduced AOA-induced ROS accumulation, it did not restore S6 or CAD phosphorylation or NDV replication, suggesting that ROS is not responsible for the inhibitory effect of AOA on this pathway (Fig. S2D–F). As expected, pyruvate and aspartate rescued viral NP expression and extracellular viral titers in AOA-treated cells (Fig. 4E and H). Similarly, during VSV infection, exogenous pyruvate and aspartate reversed AOA-mediated suppression of S6 and CAD phosphorylation, as well as viral replication (Fig. S1E). Furthermore, treatment with Torin 1, an mTORC1 inhibitor, significantly abolished the rescue effect of pyruvate supplementation on the phosphorylation of S6K, S6, and CAD, as well as NDV replication in the presence of AOA (Fig. 4I–L). Pyruvate, as a central metabolite, has multiple functions beyond serving as an electron acceptor. To investigate the precise role of the cytoplasmic NAD+/NADH ratio in regulating the phosphorylation of S6 and CAD, duroquinone, a synthetic organic compound that serves as an electron acceptor, was chosen for validation. Duroquinone relies on the intracellular reductase NQO1 to regenerate NAD+ from NADH, converting itself into reduced durohydroquinone in the process [44]. As expected, supplementation with duroquinone also rescues the phosphorylation of S6K, S6, and CAD, as well as the NAD+/NADH ratio and NDV replication in the presence of AOA, exhibiting the same phenotype as pyruvate (Fig. 4M–P). To further verify that the NAD+/NADH ratio mediates the S6 and CAD phosphorylation, we employed nicotinamide riboside (NR), which is sequentially converted to NAD+ by nicotinamide riboside kinase (NRK) and nicotinamide mononucleotide adenylyltransferase (NMNAT) [45]. The results showed that NR supplementation effectively increased the intracellular NAD+/NADH ratio, enhanced S6 and CAD phosphorylation, and rescued the impairment of viral replication caused by AOA treatment or GOT1 knockdown, consistent with the findings described above (Fig. S2G–J). GOT1 uniquely regulates CAD activity beyond aspartate supply during pyrimidine synthesis and NDV replication

Using AOA as a MAS inhibitor by simultaneously targeting GOT1 and GOT2, the above results demonstrated that the MAS plays a dual role in regulating both aspartate supply and CAD activity, contributing to de novo pyrimidine synthesis and virus replication (Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4). Additionally, GOT1 and GOT2, key components of MAS and aspartate synthesis, were shown to regulate aspartate supply during NDV infection (Fig. 2E). We next investigated whether GOT1 and GOT2 differ in their regulation of CAD activity. The results showed that while both pyruvate and aspartate supplementation restored viral replication (Fig. 5A and D), only pyruvate supplementation rescued S6 and CAD phosphorylation, as well as the NAD+/NADH ratio, in GOT1 knockdown cells (Fig. 5A–C). Moreover, these findings were further confirmed in the VSV infection model, in which exogenous pyruvate rescued the GOT1 knockdown-induced decreases in S6 and CAD phosphorylation and viral replication (Fig. S1F). This pyruvate-mediated rescue of S6 and CAD phosphorylation, the NAD+/NADH ratio, and virus replication was abolished by Torin 1 treatment (Fig. 5E–H). Similarly, supplementation with duroquinone restored S6 and CAD phosphorylation and the NAD+/NADH ratio in GOT1 knockdown cells (Fig. 5I–L), mirroring the phenotype observed with AOA treatment. In contrast, in GOT2 knockdown cells, pyruvate supplementation failed to rescue S6 and CAD phosphorylation, the NAD+/NADH ratio, or virus replication (Fig. 5M–P). NDV infection promotes the assembly of pyrimidinosome involving GOT1 and pyrimidine synthases

A recent study demonstrated that, compared to GOT2, GOT1 plays a more critical role in the conversion of glutamine to aspartate and pyrimidines in tumor cells. Notably, cytosolic GOT1, CAD, and UMPS assemble into a ‘pyrimidinosome’ by interacting with the outer mitochondrial membrane protein voltage-dependent anion-selective channel protein 3 (VDAC3) and the inner membrane-anchored DHODH [46]. Both virus-infected cells and cancer cells depend on metabolic rewiring to sustain their uncontrolled replication, including the reprogramming of nucleotide metabolism to hijack host resources [6]. Given our findings that GOT1 plays a bifunctional role in regulating aspartate supply and CAD activity during NDV infection, we explored whether these molecules assemble into a pyrimidinosome to facilitate pyrimidine synthesis in the context of viral replication. We transiently expressed GFP-GOT1 and mCherry-UMPS in H1299 cells, then infected them with NDV infected at various doses and time points. Immunofluorescence results showed that these proteins co-localized upon viral infection, whereas no co-localization was observed in the Control group or the UV-NDV group (Fig. 6A–B). Next, using Tom20 as a mitochondrial marker, we observed that NDV infection triggered the clustering of endogenous GOT1, UMPS, and DHODH, all of which colocalized with Tom20 (Fig. 6C). Furthermore, to confirm the colocalization of these proteins, fluorescently labeled plasmids expressing GOT1, UMPS, and DHODH were transfected into cells in pairs and analyzed for co-localization. The results showed punctate aggregation of GOT1, UMPS, and DHODH following NDV infection and, importantly, demonstrated colocalization between GOT1 and UMPS, GOT1 and DHODH, as well as DHODH and UMPS (Fig. 6D–F). Unfortunately, the immunofluorescent antibody and fluorescently labeled plasmids for CAD did not work effectively, preventing observation of colocalization between CAD and these proteins. To address this limitation, CO-IP experiments were performed. The results showed that endogenous GOT1 co-precipitated with CAD, UMPS, and DHODH, with interactions significantly enhanced by NDV infection (Fig. 6G). Similar results were observed when endogenous UMPS was used as the bait (Fig. 6H). Additionally, CO-IP demonstrated exogenous interactions between GOT1 and CAD, which were further strengthened following NDV infection (Fig. 6I–J). Previous studies have shown that the pyrimidinosome is driven by phase separation [41]. In our co-localization experiments, the fluorescence displayed a highly regular round-shaped pattern, suggesting the potential occurrence of phase separation. To verify this, we bleached the fluorescence of clustered GOT1, UMPS and DHODH, and observed a gradual loss of fluorescence without any recovery, suggesting virus-induced pyrimidinosome is probably not formed through phase separation (Fig. 6K). Pyrimidinosome formation as a cellular stress response to MAS blockage

NDV infection induces the formation of a pyrimidinosome composed of GOT1 and pyrimidine synthases, with GOT1 regulating CAD activity to promote pyrimidine synthesis. To better understand the function of the pyrimidinosome, particularly under conditions where the MAS was blocked, we examined its behavior. Unexpectedly, AOA treatment resulted in an increase in punctate pyrimidinosome signals during NDV infection. Interestingly, pyruvate supplementation caused these signals to aggregate into larger, plaque-like structures, a phenomenon reversed by Torin 1 treatment, which restored the structures to smaller punctate signals (Fig. 7A–B). These observations suggest that pyrimidinosome formation during viral infection may represent a cellular stress response triggered by limited resources or enzyme dysfunction in pyrimidine synthesis. To further explore these dynamics, we assessed pyrimidine synthesis in the presence of AOA, pyruvate, and Torin 1. Glutamine, which contributes to aspartate via the MAS to support pyrimidine synthesis (Fig. 2J–K), was tracked using [U-13C] glutamine labeling to monitor metabolic flux (Fig. 7C). As expected, AOA treatment reduced glutamine utilization for aspartate synthesis through both oxidative (M+4) and reductive (M+3) pathways, resulting in decreased incorporation of newly synthesized aspartate into downstream intermediates such as Carbamoyl-Aspartate (M+3/M+4), Orotate (M+3/M+4), and UMP (M+2/M+3). Pyruvate supplementation restored the levels of these intermediates, while Torin 1 treatment further suppressed their production (Fig. 7D–G). Taken together, these results reveal an inverse relationship between pyrimidinosome formation and pyrimidine synthesis under MAS blockage. Discussion

In this study, we investigated how oncolytic NDV reprograms host nucleotide metabolism to support its replication in tumor cells. Our results show that NDV induces a metabolic state that sustains de novo pyrimidine synthesis, which is required for efficient viral replication. Mechanistically, we identify GOT1 as a central regulator of this process by coordinating two complementary functions: promoting aspartate production through the malate-aspartate shuttle and maintaining NAD+/NADH homeostasis to support activation of the mTOR-S6K-CAD signaling axis. In addition, we find that NDV infection promotes pyrimidinosome assembly, with GOT1 functioning as a pyrimidinosome-associated component, suggesting an additional layer of metabolic organization during viral replication (Fig. 8). Together, these findings reveal a previously unrecognized mechanism by which oncolytic NDV rewires host pyrimidine metabolism and provide insight into how metabolic pathways can be leveraged to modulate oncolytic virotherapy.Fig. 8| Proposed model for the regulation of de novo pyrimidine synthesis by GOT1 during NDV infection. Cytoplasmic GOT1 serves as a central regulator of both endogenous aspartate synthesis and the mTOR-S6K-CAD signaling axis, thereby enhancing de novo pyrimidine synthesis and viral replication. The formation of pyrimidinosomes serve as a compensatory strategy to ensure sufficient pyrimidine production for viral needs.Fig. 8

Viral replication depends on a sufficient supply of nucleotides generated through de novo biosynthetic pathways. Consistent with previous reports showing broad antiviral effects of DHODH and IMPDH inhibition, including against influenza virus, HBV, HCV, EBOV, and SARS-CoV-2 [[9], [10], [11]]. Our targeted metabolomics analysis revealed significant enrichment of de novo nucleotide metabolism during NDV infection. Moreover, leflunomide markedly reduced the replication of NDV, as well as VSV, AIV and HSV-1 (data not shown), supporting pyrimidine biosynthesis as an important metabolic pathway for viral replication. In this study, we focused on the early steps of pyrimidine biosynthesis during NDV infection, particularly the roles of aspartate and CAD. In our experiments, low-dose exogenous aspartate did not significantly affect NDV replication under basal conditions, whereas our recent study showed that higher concentrations of extracellular aspartate (2-10 mM) effectively promoted NDV replication [47]. The differential effects of exogenous aspartate likely reflect its limited uptake under basal conditions, such that only higher extracellular concentrations produce measurable metabolic effects [[48], [49], [50]]. Beyond exogenous aspartate supply, we further examined the MAS, a metabolic pathway that can support endogenous aspartate production but is often overlooked in studies of virus-induced metabolic reprogramming. In this context, GOT1 emerged as a critical determinant of NDV replication. Notably, GOT2 knockdown significantly reduced GOT1 expression, whereas GOT1 knockdown did not affect GOT2, suggesting asymmetric regulatory crosstalk beyond their canonical metabolic roles. Similar cross-compartment regulation has been reported for SHMT1 and SHMT2 [51]. Together, these findings identify GOT1 as a key metabolic determinant of NDV replication and provide further insight into the metabolic regulation of oncolytic virus replication.

Despite the critical role of nucleotide metabolism in viral replication, how viruses reprogram host nucleotide metabolism remains incompletely understood. Different viruses appear to use distinct strategies to secure nucleotide supply. Human cytomegalovirus (HCMV) enhances de novo pyrimidine biosynthesis to support UDP-sugar production and viral protein glycosylation [52], whereas coxsackievirus B3 (CVB3) and encephalomyocarditis virus (EMCV) increase nucleotide metabolite levels mainly through nucleic acid degradation and salvage pathways [53]. In parallel, one-carbon metabolism has emerged as another important node in virus-induced metabolic remodeling, as shown for SARS-CoV-2 and, in our previous work, NDV through regulation of MTHFD2 [35,54]. Nucleotide metabolism is also important in transformation driven by oncogenic viruses such as Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) and Kaposi's sarcoma-associated herpesvirus (KSHV) [6,[55], [56], [57]]. In contrast, how oncolytic viruses reprogram tumor cell nucleotide metabolism remains largely unclear. Here, we provide evidence that NDV, as an oncolytic virus, reprograms nucleotide metabolism to support its replication in tumor cells. Given the shared biosynthetic demands of virus-infected and cancer cells [6], competition for metabolic resources, including nucleotides, may represent an important component of the interaction between oncolytic viruses and tumor cells. This possibility and its consequences for tumor cell fate warrant further investigation.

Emerging evidence suggests a link between nucleotide metabolism and innate antiviral immunity. For example, DHODH inhibitors such as DD264 and brequinar have been reported to enhance innate immune responses through transcription factors including IRF1 and IRF3, while simultaneously suppressing the replication of several RNA viruses, including Chikungunya virus (CHIKV), measles virus (MV), West Nile virus (WNV), and classical swine fever virus (CSFV) [55,56]. Similarly, during infection of hepatitis E virus (HEV), combined targeting of purine and pyrimidine synthesis shows potent antiviral activity that is accompanied by induction of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) [57]. These findings support the idea that perturbation of nucleotide metabolism can influence antiviral immune signaling, although the mechanisms underlying this crosstalk remain incompletely understood. Most previous studies have focused on canonical nucleotide synthesis enzymes, such as DHODH and IMPDH, when examining the relationship between nucleotide metabolism and innate immunity. By contrast, much less is known about whether metabolic pathways that support nucleotide biosynthesis indirectly, including aspartate-related pathways, also contribute to antiviral immune regulation. In this context, GOT1 is of potential interest. An in vivo study showed that GOT1 is dispensable for M2 macrophage differentiation and does not affect LPS-induced immune paralysis [58], suggesting that its immunological role may be context dependent. Whether GOT1 contributes to antiviral innate immune responses under virus infection therefore remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

In this study, we provide evidence for an association between NAD+ metabolism and mTOR-CAD signaling during NDV infection, which may contribute to the metabolic support of viral replication. We found that NDV infection increased the intracellular NAD+/NADH ratio and was accompanied by S6K activation, enhanced CAD phosphorylation, and increased pyrimidine synthesis. These observations suggest a potential connection between NAD+ homeostasis and mTOR-dependent regulation of CAD, extending previous studies that mainly link CAD phosphorylation to nutrient and energy signaling [15,18]. NAD metabolism has diverse regulatory functions beyond its classical role in redox balance [59]. However, the mechanism by which NAD+ may influence mTOR-CAD signaling remains unclear. One possible link is the sirtuin family, whose members are NAD+-dependent enzymes with broad roles in metabolic regulation [60]. In support of this possibility, NAD+ has been reported to modulate the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway through SIRT1 in experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis [61]. In addition, our previous work showed that NDV induces PINK1-PRKN-dependent mitophagy to promote SIRT3 degradation and metabolic reprogramming [34]. Further studies will be needed to determine whether sirtuins mediate the connection between NAD+ and mTOR-CAD signaling during NDV infection.

Recent studies have shown that de novo nucleotide biosynthesis can be organized through biomolecular assemblies such as purinosomes and pyrimidinosomes [46,62,63]. Pyrimidinosomes, which include enzymes such as CAD, UMPS, and DHODH, are thought to facilitate efficient pyrimidine production and to respond dynamically to changes in metabolic status [41]. In this study, we provide evidence that NDV infection is associated with the formation of pyrimidinosome, suggesting that spatial reorganization of pyrimidine synthesis may also occur during viral infection [64]. Notably, previous studies have shown that purinosome and pyrimidinosome assembly can increase under conditions of metabolic stress or nucleotide insufficiency [46,65,66], suggesting that these structures may also represent an adaptive response to limited biosynthetic resources. Consistent with this, AOA increased pyrimidinosome puncta during NDV infection despite reducing glutamine-derived aspartate and downstream pyrimidine synthesis, suggesting a compensatory response to precursor limitation rather than enhanced pyrimidine production. Interestingly, although NDV infection enhanced the interactions among key pyrimidine synthesis enzymes, the resulting structures showed little fluorescence recovery after photobleaching and were not fully consistent with classical liquid-like condensates, suggesting a distinct mode of enzyme organization during viral infection [[67], [68], [69]]. This difference may reflect the unique intracellular environment created by viral infection, which could favor a more stable or less dynamic higher-order assembly than the phase separation-driven structures reported under other conditions. Moreover, pyruvate treatment induced unusually large plaque-like puncta, indicating that these structures are highly responsive to metabolic perturbation. Together, these observations suggest that the formation and function of virus-induced pyrimidinosomes warrant further investigation.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that NDV enhances its replication by remodeling host pyrimidine metabolism through the dual roles of GOT1, while pyrimidinosome formation may serve as a compensatory response. These findings uncover an additional strategy by which an oncolytic virus exploits tumor cell metabolism to support its replication and suggest that GOT1 may represent a potential target for antiviral and antitumor intervention.

Following infection, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 10 min at room temperature. Following another three 5-min PBS washes, cells were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. After three 5-min PBS washes, cells were incubated with species-specific secondary antibodies. For exogenous immunofluorescence analysis, cells were seeded in 6-well plates containing glass coverslips and cultured overnight. Immunoblotting and immunoprecipitation. Immunoblotting and immunoprecipitation was performed as described previously [72]. Briefly, for immunoblotting, cell samples were washed with PBS to remove residual medium, then lysed at room temperature for 5 min using an appropriate amount of RIPA Lysis Buffer containing 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). The lysates were subsequently denatured and separated using sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). Protein levels were analyzed via immunoblotting and quantified with ImageJ software. For immunoprecipitation, HEK-293 cells, either transfected or non-transfected, were lysed in lysis buffer containing 1 mM PMSF and protease inhibitors. After centrifugation, lysates were incubated with antibody-conjugated magnetic beads overnight at 4 °C, washed, and eluted in SDS loading buffer at 100 °C. The eluted proteins were analyzed by immunoblotting.

Lactate and NAD+/NADH Measurements. Extracellular lactate concentrations were determined using the Lactate Assay kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) catalyzes the oxidation of lactate to pyruvate, reducing nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) to its reduced form (NADH). Phenazine methosulfate (PMS) transfers NADH to nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT), forming a purple formazan, which is quantified by measuring absorbance at 530 nm using a spectrophotometer.

Intracellular NAD+/NADH ratios were measured using the NAD+/NADH assay kit with WST-8, following the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were lysed, centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected. For NADH measurement, the supernatant was heat-treated at 60 °C for 30 min. NADH standards were prepared to generate a standard curve, and optical density (OD) values of NAD+ and NADH were measured separately using a microplate reader with ethanol dehydrogenase as the detection reagent.

Stable isotope labeled medium. For [U-13C] glucose, [U-13C] glutamine (2 mM) or [U-13C] pyruvate tracer experiments, A549 cells were either mock-infected or infected with NDV (MOI = 1). After viral adsorption, the medium was replaced with modified DMEM (D5030, Sigma-Aldrich), lacking glucose, glutamine, and pyruvate, but supplemented with [U-13C] glucose (12.5 mM), [U-13C] glutamine (2 mM), or [U-13C] pyruvate (5 mM), respectively, along with the other two non-labeled metabolites to ensure complete supplementation. Cells were maintained until defined infection time points, followed by mass spectrometry analysis of intracellular metabolites. (M+a) represents the mass shift of metabolites incorporating +a13C atoms from the [U-13C]-labeled tracers. For [U-13C] aspartate tracer experiments, a similar procedure was followed as described for the other tracers, except the medium was replaced with aspartate-free RPMI-1640 (custom medium, BasalMedia) supplemented with [U-13C] aspartate (150 μM).

Metabolite Extraction and LC-MS Analysis. The metabolite extraction and LC-MS analysis was performed as described previously [35]. Briefly, A549 cells were cultured overnight in 6 cm dishes to allow for adhesion. Viral infection and drug treatment were then applied to the respective experimental groups. Following viral adsorption, metabolomics samples were immediately replaced with 2% maintenance medium. For isotope tracing samples, cells were rinsed twice with PBS and switched to isotope-labeled medium for tracer culture, with samples collected at the specified time points.

Fluorescence Recovery After Photobleaching (FRAP). Cells were cultured on glass bottom culture dishes (NEST, 801001) and transfected with GFP-DHODH, mCherry-UMPS, or GFP-GOT1. Following 12h of NDV infection, FRAP analysis were performed using a confocal microscope (TCS-SP8, Leica, Shanghai, Germany). The initial fluorescence intensity was recorded, after which selected regions of interest (ROIs) were photobleached using maximum laser intensity at either 568 nm or 488 nm. Fluorescence recovery was monitored by acquiring images every 2.578s for a total duration of 5 min.

Statistical analyses. Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism. Results are expressed as means ± standard deviations. Statistical significance between two groups was determined using a two-tailed independent Student's t-test (P < 0.05). For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post-hoc test was applied. All experiments were repeated independently at least three times, with a significance threshold set at 0.05 for all statistical tests. ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, ns: not significant (P ≥ 0.05).
