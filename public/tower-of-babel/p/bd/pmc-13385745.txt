Elasticity-gated thermal plasticity via superheating-mediated nucleation and growth in polymer networks
Authors: Guye Wei, Jiageng Pan, Shunjie Mo, Jian Liao, Liang Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLiving systems encode environmental history into material structure, enabling adaptive response thresholds, a capability lacking in synthetic materials with fixed thermal transitions. We present a thermodynamic approach that programs critical transition temperatures in polymer networks via salt-dependent thermal plasticity. Conditioning poly(vinyl propional) hydrogels at a swelling temperature Ts in Hofmeister-modulated media embeds thermal and saline history into equilibrium water content, which then dictates superheating-mediated nucleation upon heating. Two synergistic mechanisms: temperature-dependent miscibility encodes memory via adaptive swelling, while network elasticity gates nucleation barriers, giving Tc = Ts + ΔT, where ΔT is set by thermal history and salt identity. Structural characterization reveals a hierarchical architecture of tight physical crosslinks and loose co-evolving domains, offering a universal design rule for history-responsive materials. We demonstrate reprogrammable freeze-exposure indicators for vaccine cold-chain monitoring to quantify sub-zero breach severity and duration. This work establishes intelligent matter capable of autonomous sensing, memory, and adaptive actuation.Subject terms: Gels and hydrogels, Polymers, Mechanical properties

Introduction

Living systems uniquely encode environmental history into their material structure to adaptively modulate response thresholds1–3—a capability conspicuously absent in synthetic materials. While marine organisms like corals can “remember” past temperature and saline conditions to shift their critical bleaching thresholds (Fig. 1A, B)3–8, synthetic analogues remain frozen in static behaviours. This disparity exposes a fundamental limitation: current materials cannot autonomously store environmental information and translate it into reprogrammable thermodynamic transitions.

Classical thermoresponsive hydrogels, such as poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAM), exhibit a transparency-to-opaque transition at a static Tc that is invariant to thermal history (Fig. 1C, D, Supplementary Movies 1–2, Supplementary Figs. 1–2)9–11. While recent advances have achieved thermal history-dependent responses, their design principles and modulated behaviours diverge markedly from authentic salt-dependent thermal plasticity (SDTP) traits (Supplementary Table 1). For instance, certain hydrogels can “learn” to respond to light alone after prior exposure to combined light–heat stimuli12–19. In parallel, memory-like behaviours have been demonstrated with programmable optical, volumetric or pH changes, which exhibit spontaneous forgetting and hysteretic phase transitions that persist across thermal cycles9,20–23. However, these systems rely on multiple external inputs (chemical, optical, or mechanical) or kinetic trapping or dissipation in metastable states. In stark contrast, biological SDTP achieves a thermodynamically shifted Tc through non-volatile, in-matter memory of past temperature and saline conditions, enabling autonomous sensing, remembering, and adaptation within a self-contained system 2,24,25.

Here we introduce a thermodynamic framework that resolves this fundamental gap by enabling non-volatile, reversible programming of Tc in polymer networks through environmental history alone. Figure 1E shows a phase diagram of a temperature (T) against composition (water fraction, φw) with an upper miscibility gap. In this diagram, the bi-nodal curve demarcates stable from metastable states, while the spinodal curve signals the onset of thermodynamic instability.. The critical temperature (Tc) is determined by the intersection of the system’s water content (φw) with these boundaries. Crucially, φw can be precisely modulated not only by temperature but also by specific ion effects according to the Hofmeister series, enabling salt-mediated modulation of Tc26. Guided by this phase diagram, our design employs saline-mediated thermal conditioning to traverse stable and metastable regions of the phase diagram, achieving programmable critical temperature dynamics that mirror coral thermal plasticity. The gel operates via an adaptive swelling equilibrium governed by two competing contributions: polymer–water mixing entropy and elastic network constriction. When swollen at a specific temperature (Ts) in either salting in, water or salting-out media, the gel equilibrates at a specific water content (φweq) that positions it near bi-nodal curve at Ts. Elastic forces from the cross-linked network then stabilise the homogeneous state until a thermal perturbation (ΔT > 0) raises the temperature to Tc = Ts +ΔT, triggering phase separation to produce water-rich domains (with a high-water fraction,φw″) and polymer-rich gel domains (with a low water fraction,φw′). The interphasic region scatters light to make gel whitening. By varying the temperature and salts in swelling media, a spectrum of φweq can be created to encode a distinct Tc.

The key innovation lies in decoupling information storage from actuation. During swelling at defined temperature Ts in Hofmeister-modulated media, the gel autonomously encodes history into its equilibrium water fraction (φweq). Upon heating, this stored information is read out through elasticity-constrained nucleation: the network’s elastic modulus (Ge)—which itself scales with Ts—creates a mechanical barrier that linearly shifts the critical super-saturation required for phase separation. This quantitative elasticity-gating mechanism provides predictive design rules unattainable in conventional systems, where phase separation thresholds are compositionally fixed.

As a demonstration of practical utility, we deploy SDTP-gels as reprogrammable freeze-exposure indicators for vaccine cold-chain monitoring, where machine learning-assisted grayscale analysis quantifies sub-zero breach severity and duration. However, the fundamental advance extends beyond this application: we establish universal criteria for thermodynamic plasticity that are generalizable across polymer chemistries, opening a predictive framework for engineering intelligent matter capable of autonomous environmental adaptation27.

Results

Fabrication and thermal plasticity of SDTP-gels

As depicted in Fig. 2A, SDTP-gel synthesis commenced with poly(vinyl propional) (PVP) bearing 50 mol% propanal functionalities (synthetic details in Supplementary Figs. 3–4, Supplementary Movie 3). Significantly, this elevated acetalization degree yields blocky propanal sequences28, which segregate into propanal-rich and hydroxyl-rich domains.

After dissolution and casting, the polymer was dried into freestanding sheets (Supplementary Figs. 5–6), which were then equilibrated in aqueous media at defined conditioning temperatures (Ts)—deionised water, salting-in (e.g., 1 M NaSCN), or salting-out (e.g., 1 M Na₂SO₄) solutions—to inscribe salt-dependent thermal memory. This swelling process produces transparent SDTP-gels. This transparent state remains unchanged for over 6 months (Supplementary Fig. 7A), demonstrating that kinetic stabilisation by the elastic network renders it effectively permanent on all practical experimental timescales.

The transparent SDTP-gels, upon heating at <1 K min⁻¹ from Ts to Tc = Ts + ΔT, undergo highly reversible opacity transitions (Fig. 2B) with ≤2 K hysteresis (Supplementary Fig. 8). Transmittance drops from 90% to 50% across this transition, and the switching persists over hundreds of thermal cycles (Supplementary Movie 4). Importantly, the phase transition behavior remains stable over numerous cycles and extended swelling periods, with negligible change observed (Supplementary Fig. 7B, Supplementary Fig. 9). The influence of salt concentration on SDTP behavior was also investigated (Supplementary Fig. 10). The results confirm that the ionic species determines the direction of modulation, while the concentration controls the magnitude. Importantly, in striking contrast to that classical process, the opaque state is stable for days with no observable coarsening (Supplementary Fig. 11, Supplementary Movie 5). As shown later, the essential ingredient leading to this unusual stability is the elasticity of the polymer matrix.

Importantly, ΔT scales positively with Ts across all media and intensifies with salting-out strength (Fig. 2B). For example, conditioning at Ts = 20 °C and 40 °C yields Tc values of 25 °C (ΔT = 5 K) and 47 °C (ΔT = 7 K) in water, 21 °C (ΔT = 1 K) and 43 °C (ΔT = 3 K) in NaSCN, and 40 °C (ΔT = 20 K) and 67 °C (ΔT = 27 K) in Na₂SO₄. The molecular weight and AD also significantly affect the SDTP behavior. As shown in Supplementary Figs. 12–13, lower AD or lower molecular weight leads to higher equilibrium water content and a lower Tc. Blending polymers of different molecular weights yields intermediate responses.

More intriguingly, Tc can be orthogonally reprogrammed via two pathways: re-equilibration at reduced temperature (Ts’ < Ts) or exposure to alternative saline compositions (Fig. 2C).

A gel conditioned at Ts = 50 °C (Tc = 56 °C) exhibits a reprogrammed Tc’ = 35 °C after re-swelling at Ts’ = 27 °C in water (Fig. 2CI). Memory updating also occurs through salt exchange alone: a gel initially equilibrated in water at Ts = 28 °C displays Tc values of 31 °C and 56 °C after re-swelling in NaSCN and Na₂SO₄, respectively (Fig. 2CII). Notably, the high-Tc state induced by Na₂SO₄ can be reverted to a low-Tc state by subsequent re-swelling in water or NaSCN, demonstrating full reversibility.

The SDTP-gel system replicates three features of biological thermal plasticity: autonomous memory encoding, dynamic threshold adaptation, and non-volatile re-programmability. Experimentally, the “training” is achieved by swelling a dried gel at a defined temperature Ts in a selected salt solution, which sets the equilibrium water content and network elasticity. The material’s thermoresponsive behavior directly reflects this training: upon heating, the gel undergoes its transparency-to-opacity transition at a new critical temperature Tc that scales linearly with Ts. Thermal history is thus autonomously stored, while Tc can be dynamically updated through retraining at a different Ts or in a different salt medium. Crucially, re-swelling at reduced Ts′ non-destructively resets the thermal memory, enabling sequential adaptation cycles. The thermodynamic basis of these SDTP traits can be further elucidated via analysis of the swollen network phase diagram.

Thermally adaptive swelling

The temperature-dependent equilibrium water content (φweq) of SDTP-gels in different aqueous media is plotted against the swelling temperature (Ts) in Fig. 3A (natural logarithmic form). The data were fitted to the empirical relationship: ln(φw)≈a−b×T. The fitted parameter b was found to be negative and nearly constant across all salt types as indicated in Fig. 3A. Interestingly, the φweq-Ts curves for SDTP-gels swollen in different salt media can be collapsed onto a single master curve by simply shifting Ts (Supplementary Fig. 14). This observation confirms that salting-in and salting-out effects functionally mimic low-temperature and high-temperature swelling for SDTP-gel preparation, respectively. In contrast, PNIPAM-based gels, whether chemically or physically cross-linked, fail to exhibit SDTP characteristics (Supplementary Figs. 1–2, Supplementary Movies 1–2). Their opacity transition occurs as soon as the conditioning temperature exceeds a fixed Tc.

While molecular-level distinctions between PNIPAM and PVP systems require further investigation, a pivotal discrepancy emerges: dry PVP sheets show pronounced Ts-dependent water uptake, whereas PNIPAM sheets do not. The contrasting behavior can be understood from their respective phase diagrams. In PNIPAM/water, the bi-nodal and spinodal curves are separated by an exceptionally narrow temperature gap (ca. 0.6 °C)29. Consequently, once the temperature exceeds the bi-nodal, phase separation occurs immediately, leaving no room for thermal history programming. In contrast, poly(vinyl acetal)-based systems such as PVP possess a broader metastable region due to the presence of acetal rings and residual hydroxyl groups, which alter the concentration dependence of the polymer–water interaction parameter. This broader window prevents phase separation during swelling, enabling the elastic-gated nucleation mechanism that underlies SDTP behavior.

The adaptive swelling of PVP originates from its amorphous network architecture. Unlike pristine polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), which exhibits a crystalline melting peak near 220 °C (Supplementary Fig. 15)30, the residual hydroxyl groups in PVP remain non-crystalline, preserving hydration capability. Water therefore acts as a marginal solvent for 50 mol% propanal-modified PVP at 23 °C, evidenced by a relative energy difference of 2–3 for PVP–water interactions (Supplementary Fig. 16 and Table 2)31. This moderate solvent quality drives network permeation through competing mechanisms: (i) dissociation of hydroxyl-rich segments, creating a loose network32; and (ii) association of propanal-rich side chains, forming tight domains33. The swelling process thereby quenches inhomogeneities into the matrix due to the density fluctuation34.

These frozen inhomogeneities can be quantitatively described as a hierarchical assembly of three structural components (Fig. 3B)35,36: (i) solid-like domains with dense, short-range order, characterised by a correlation length ξtight; (ii) a looser interstitial network (ξloose) interconnecting these domains, which approximates the mesh size; and (iii) microgel-like solid domains undergoing Brownian motion, described by a larger correlation length Ξ and shape factor F(R), with mean radius 〈R〉. Small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) patterns were fitted using a hierarchical model combining Gaussian and Ornstein-Zernike functions (Supplementary Fig. 17), yielding the structural parameters summarised in Supplementary Table 3. The relative volume fractions of the three components—floose, ftight, and fsolid—were obtained by averaging their scattering contributions across the full q-range.

To probe swelling-induced structural evolution, we compared dry PVP films with specimens hydrated in water at 15 °C, 28 °C, and 60 °C, and further assessed salt effects by swelling films in water, Na₂SO₄, or NaSCN solutions at 28 °C—conditions yielding distinct φweq values. As compiled in Fig. 3C, increasing φweq systematically reduces ξtight while expanding both ξloose and 〈R〉. For example, dry films exhibit ξtight = 2.1 nm, ξloose = 5.0 nm, and 〈R〉 = 26.2 nm, whereas swelling in 1 M NaSCN at 28 °C (φweq = 0.72) reduces ξtight to 0.8 nm and increases ξloose and 〈R〉 to 15.0 nm and 65.0 nm, respectively.

The contracted ξtight reflects intensified inter-chain association within dense domains as water drives aggregation of propanal-rich segments. Conversely, the enlarged ξloose and 〈R〉 signify that water disrupts hydrogen-bonded clusters among hydroxyl-rich regions, enabling network expansion and relaxation while reducing steric hindrance for propanal side-chain assembly into larger microgel domains. Notably, floose exceeds 0.90 in dry or poorly hydrated states but declines to ~0.60 upon full swelling, while the ftight rises to ~0.30—demonstrating that hydration redistributes polymer chains from expanded to condensed domains via hydrophobic association.

Collectively, these data reveal that salting-in media and lower temperatures potentiate swelling, augmenting both solid-like domain size and interstitial network correlation length. The consolidated tight network, stabilized by propanal packing and water-bridged hydroxyl clusters37–41, functions as quasi-permanent physical crosslinks, while the swollen loose regions provide entropic elasticity. This rubber state is corroborated by the Fickian diffusion behavior of SDTP-gel during swelling (Supplementary Fig. 18)42, where rapid chain relaxation outpaces water transport–a hallmark of entropy-driven elasticity in rubber networks stabilized by persistent crosslinks43.

Rheological characterization corroborates this hierarchical structural model. A frequency-independent rubbery plateau (G’ > G”, 0.1–100 rad/s, Supplementary Fig. 19), stabilized by the tight network serving as physical crosslinks, confirms gel elasticity. Within each medium, the equilibrium modulus Gₑ scales with swelling temperature as Gₑ ∝ υ × Ts (Fig. 3D). In water, for instance, Gₑ increases from 75 kPa at 13 °C to 300 kPa at 60 °C. The coefficient ν gauges thermal sensitivity and intensifies with salting-in strength, reflecting greater modulus responsiveness at higher hydration. Furthermore, we confirmed that the network remains rubbery under all experimental conditions by showing that Tg as a function of water content lies well below Ts (Supplementary Fig. 20), consistent with the rheological rubbery plateau in Supplementary Fig. 19. Intriguingly, at fixed Ts, salting-out media produce higher Gₑ than salting-in media: gels conditioned at 40 °C display Gₑ values of 63.9 kPa (NaSCN) < 118.8 kPa (water) <242.5 kPa (Na₂SO₄), consistent with Hofmeister-mediated mechanics26.

While this thermodynamic framework rationalises the adaptive swelling underpinning SDTP behavior, two pivotal questions persist: First, why do SDTP-gels remain transparent during swelling, unlike classical PNIPAM systems that undergo immediate phase separation? Second, what governs the observed Ts-dependent scaling of ΔT? Addressing these questions requires deeper examination of the phase separation dynamics in SDTP-gels.

Elasticity constrained phase separation

Phase separation underpins the opacity transition in SDTP-gels. We mapped this phenomenon by constructing demixing curves that correlate the water fraction (φα(Tc)) of opaque gels with their Tc across NaSCN, water, and Na₂SO₄ media (Figs. 4AI–4AIII). This demixing boundary (Tc vs. φα(Tc)) tracks the spinodal curve, while the swelling equilibrium line (Tsvs.φweqTs) approximates the bi-nodal limit, indicating that whitening occurs upon entering the metastable zone between these two thermodynamic boundaries (Supplementary Fig. 22).

The transition proceeds via a nucleation-and-growth (NG) mechanism, generating a characteristic sea-island morphology comprising isolated water-rich pores embedded in a continuous polymer matrix. Confocal imaging of opaque SDTP-gels confirms this architecture, revealing discrete water-rich domains (dark regions) within the polymer scaffold (red fluorescence) (Supplementary Movie 6, Fig. 4B). In elastic networks, pore dimensions formed during NG phase separation inversely correlate with equilibrium modulus Ge47,48. To quantify this effect, we used two-dimensional fast Fourier transform (FFT) analysis to determine average pore size and distribution. SDTP-gels with Ge spanning 10–70 kPa exhibit pore size reduction from 6.9 μm to 3.2 μm as water content decreases from 0.83 to 0.50 (Fig. 4C–4D). Further analysis reveals that the average pore diameter scales approximately as R∝Ge−0.33 (see Supplementary Fig. 23), consistent with the scaling law predicted for viscoelastic phase separation48. Correspondingly, FFT patterns (Fig. 4B insets) shift toward lower spatial frequencies as Ge decreases, indicating coarser, more periodic domain arrangements. Chord length analysis, which can effectively extract characteristic length for biphasic media with irregular geometry36, further independently validates this Ge–pore size correlation (Supplementary Fig. 24). The consistent inverse relationship confirms that elevated network elasticity raises nucleation barriers, thereby restricting the stable nucleation radius48–50.

Using this framework, we calculated the normalised nucleation barrier ΔGNS/kBTs as a function of radius r at 313.15 K for different swelling media and super-saturation-driving parameter△Tα (Fig. 5B). Effective nucleation requires surpassing a critical △Tα. In Na₂SO₄, △Tα≥ 5 K generates sufficient supersaturationto create a thermodynamic driving force that overcomes the barrier, enabling nucleation at sub-mesh scales (~10 nm). Conversely, at low undercooling (△Tα < 0.5 K in NaSCN), insufficient super-saturation cannot overcome the mechanical penalty, causing ΔGNS to increase monotonically with r. This eliminates a nucleation pathway and suppresses phase separation. Furthermore, the enormous elastic contribution shown in Fig. 5B raises the nucleation barrier ΔGNS by approximately three orders of magnitude compared to an unconstrained system. This reduces the nucleation rate J ∝ exp[-ΔGNS/kBT] exponentially, extending the predicted induction time τ (∼1/J) to values far exceeding practical timescales. This theoretical prediction of extreme kinetic stability is in full agreement with our experimental observation that the transparent state remains unchanged for over 6 months (Supplementary Fig. 7).

From the extremum condition ∂(ΔGNS)/∂r = 0, we derived the critical nucleus size r* and the minimum △Tα required for nucleation. The predicted relationship between preparation temperature Ts and required △Tα qualitatively matches experimental data (Fig. 5C). These results establish a quantitative framework linking preparation temperature to nucleation undercooling, demonstrating that medium-dependent super-saturation and network elasticity jointly control phase separation kinetics in responsive polymer gels.

Collectively, these theoretical analyses establish a foundation for the observed Ts-dependent ΔT scaling, demonstrating how swelling-medium-modulated super-saturation and network constraints jointly govern the nucleation thermodynamics in stimuli-responsive polymer networks.

During thermal conditioning at Ts, the gel resides in the stable regime (S ≤ 1). Heating to Tc = Ts +ΔT quenches the system into the unstable regime (φα > φα,min), satisfying n nwkBT ln S > Pc for bulk nucleation. Between Ts and Tc the gel passes through the metastable orange region, where elastic suppression prevents rapid nucleation but allows gradual solute redistribution. In the meanwhile, we note that the NG mechanism dominates under the relatively slow heating conditions employed in this study. However, if the gel were rapidly heated deep into the unstable region (i.e., quenched into the spinodal region), a bi-continuous morphology characteristic of spinodal decomposition would likely emerge, as recently demonstrated in other LCST-type polymer systems54. Exploring such rapid quenching conditions could provide additional insights into the elasticity-modulated phase separation dynamics and will be an interesting direction for future research.

This unified relationship demonstrates that network elasticity, not just thermodynamic incompatibility, quantitatively governs the phase separation landscape. The interplay between medium-modulated super-saturation and elastic constraints thus provides a predictive framework for the temperature-dependent swelling and demixing behavior in stimuli-responsive polymer networks. Leveraging this mechanistic understanding, we translated the reprogrammable nature of SDTP-gels into a practical tool for visualising freeze breaches in vaccine cold-chain logistics.

SDPT-gel as time-temperature Indicator for freeze-sensitive vaccines

Vaccines require strict temperature control (2–8 °C) during storage and distribution, with both overheating and freezing posing risks to efficacy55. While significant progress has been made on overheating indicators56,57, approximately 59% of vaccines procured by United Nations Children’s Fund are freeze-sensitive (as summarised in Supplementary Table 4) 27—a critical gap, as sub-zero exposure can irreversibly compromise these vaccines even after returning to safe temperatures (Fig. 6A).

Phase behavior is shown via temperature vs. water fraction (φw) curves. C. Transmittance change (ΔT550NM at 550 nm) during over-freezing: ΔT increases with subzero temperature (TOF = 0/ − 5/ − 10 °C) and duration (τOF), with distinct responses for different TOF as indicated. D. Standard “VALID” indicator images across TOF ( − 10 to 0 °C) and τOF (0–75 min), serving as a reference for (E). E. Workflow for quantifying over-freezing severity: real-time photos of the indicator are compared to standard images D via MATLAB; the Severity Index (S) and S-TOF-τOF pseudocolor map are calculated, and SRT (real-time S) is mapped to the curve. To address this unmet need, we demonstrate an over-freezing time-temperature indicator (OFTTI) based on SDTP-gels. The device operates by exposing a gel conditioned at a safe storage temperature (Ts, e.g., 5 °C) to freezing conditions (Ts’ < 0 °C) for a duration t (as illustrated in Fig. 6B). This low-temperature swelling re-programs the gel, lowering its critical demixing temperature (Tc’ < Ts). Upon returning to the safe temperature range, the gel’s opacity level visually reports both the severity and duration of the freezing event—effectively “memorizing” and “reporting” the cold-chain breach.

The SDTP-gel’s OFTTI functionality is experimentally validated (Fig. 6B, Supplementary Movie 7). A gel equilibrated at 5 °C records freezing events upon exposure to subzero temperatures TOF = 0, −5, −10 °C; saline media remain liquid) for durations τOF. Note that the applied saline (4 M NaSCN) solution does not freeze at the tested subzero temperatures (down to −10 °C; freezing point ≈ −40 °C, see Supplementary Fig. 25), allowing the necessary water transport for reprogramming.

Upon returning to safe temperatures, the gel exhibits opacity levels that report the freezing history. Quantitative readout is achieved by measuring transmission decay at 550 nm (ΔTtran), which scales nearly linearly with τOF, following ΔTtran = (0.38-0.038TOF)×τOF,, enabling reconstruction of both freezing temperature and duration from a single measurement (Fig. 6C).

We integrated this response into a vaccine failure indicator card (Fig. 6D) with clear “safe” to “potentially unsafe” boundaries. For practical deployment, we developed a MATLAB-based machine learning algorithm that automates freeze-exposure quantification (Fig. 6E, Supplementary Movie 8). A failure indicator card was generated by imaging SDTP-gels exposed to systematic combinations of freezing temperatures (TOF) and durations (τOF), then converting the resulting transmission images into grayscale maps where transparency correlates linearly with pixel intensity (Fig. 6F, see Supplementary Note V). This calibrated image array served as the training dataset for a supervised regression model that establishes the quantitative relationship between grayscale patterns and exposure parameters. Unknown samples that have experienced over-freezing and returned to safe temperatures are similarly imaged and converted to grayscale; the trained algorithm then analyzes their intensity distribution to predict both the minimum temperature and duration of freeze exposure. This automated, quantitative approach eliminates manual interpretation, accelerates breach response, and ensures reliable vaccine viability assessment.

Discussion

This work establishes a thermodynamic approach for engineering synthetic hydrogels with coral-inspired salt-dependent thermal plasticity (SDTP) by encoding environmental history directly into polymer network architecture. Through a synergistic design harnessing thermally adaptive swelling and elasticity-constrained phase separation, we demonstrate SDTP-gels that autonomously perceive temperature and saline conditions during formation, store this information non-volatilely in their equilibrium water content, and actuate history-dependent opacity transitions at programmable critical temperatures (Tc = Ts + ΔT). Unlike conventional hydrogels with fixed transition points or metastable memory systems, this platform achieves true thermodynamic plasticity via two coupled mechanisms: (1) temperature- and Hofmeister-modulated miscibility that governs adaptive swelling, and (2) network elasticity that quantitatively controls nucleation barriers and phase separation thresholds. Structural characterization by SAXS and rheology reveals a hierarchical architecture of tight physical crosslinks, loose interstitial networks, and microgel domains, whose evolution with swelling enables linear Tc tuning and reversible memory reprogramming. As a practical demonstration, we deploy these gels as over-freezing indicators for vaccine cold-chain monitoring, where freeze exposure history is visualised through opacity changes and quantified by machine learning.

Finally, we highlight that while validated using poly(vinyl propional) as a model system, the core principles of our thermodynamic framework—adaptive swelling governed by temperature-dependent miscibility, Hofmeister-mediated hydration, and elasticity-controlled nucleation—are chemically generalizable. We have extended these design rules to other polyvinyl acetals, such as poly(vinyl butyral) (PVB), as well as to alternative polymer systems (e.g., poly(2-(2-ethoxyethoxy)ethyl acrylate-co-acrylic acid)) beyond polyvinyl acetals (see Supplementary Note V and Supplementary Figs. 26–27, Supplementary Movie 9). These systems exhibit analogous thermal plasticity behavior, confirming that the SDTP phenomenon is not unique to PVP but represents a more general design principle across different polymer chemistries, whereas conventional systems such as PNIPAM exhibit a fixed transition temperature that is insensitive to swelling history.

This raises a key question: what is the physical origin of the thermal plasticity observed here, which is absent in conventional PNIPAM gels? We propose that our system satisfies three synergistic design principles that collectively enable this property. First, and in contrast to the narrow metastable window of typical PNIPAM, our polymer-solvent system must possess a sufficiently wide gap between its bi-nodal and spinodal curves. This thermodynamic stability is essential to prevent phase separation during the history-encoding swelling stage. Second, the network’s stiffness must be sensitively tuned by the very conditions (temperature and ionic environment) of that swelling, thereby translating history into a programmable mechanical barrier to phase separation. Third, kinetically, chain relaxation must outpace water diffusion to permit the network to adaptively reorganize during encoding. A complete molecular-level picture will be refined through future studies integrating theoretical modelling and multiscale in situ characterization.

Methods

To synthesis PVP, 30 g of PVA is fully dissolved in deionised water at 90 °C for 5 h to achieve a concentration of 5 wt.%, then cooled to room temperature for later use. Then, 15 g of NaBr and 9 mL of propanal are sequentially added and vigorously stirred for 30 min at a controlled temperature (T = 20 °C) to form a homogeneous solution. Then, 2 mL of concentrated H₂SO₄ was added to the PVA–butyraldehyde mixture to adjust the pH to 1.5, and the reaction was maintained for 2.5 h. In the termination step, NaOH solution is immediately added to halt the reaction. The resulting white particles were repeatedly washed with deionised water to remove any residual aldehyde. The purified PVP product was then collected by vacuum filtration and dried under dynamic vacuum at 80 °C for 2 days. SDTP-gels were prepared by swelling dry PVP film in different aqueous media at a given temperature. The preparation process of the SDTP hydrogel is as follows: PVP particles were added to anhydrous DMF and continuously stirred at 90 °C until fully dissolved. The resulting clear solution was then cast into a customized smooth glass mold. Finally, the mold was left undisturbed in a temperature-controlled, low-humidity environment for 3 days to obtain a dried PVP film. The obtained film was placed in a vacuum drying oven at 75 °C for 24 h to evaporate residual DMF. The detailed procedure can be found in Supplementary Note II.
