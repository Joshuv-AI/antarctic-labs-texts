Growth Performance and Intestinal Health of Triploid Rainbow Trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss) Through Bile Acid Supplementation in Low Fishmeal Diets: Insights From Metabolomics, Microbiota, and Gene Expression
Authors: Huamin Wang, Siyuan Liu, Zhenhua Ma, Shidi Wang, Shuze Zhang, Shaoxia Lu, Shicheng Han, Haibo Jiang, Gefeng Xu, Hongbai Liu, Chang’an Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This research aimed to explore the impact of bile acid (BA) supplementation in low fishmeal diets on growth performance, serum biochemical indices, tissue morphology, lipid metabolism, gene expression, and gut microbiota in triploid rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss, initial weight 15.97 ± 1.4 g). The experimental design involved a low fishmeal diet (10%), and four treatment groups with BA additions (0.05%, 0.10%, 0.15%, and 0.20%) to the basal diet. The findings indicated that the group receiving 0.10% BA (G3) exhibited an enhancement in final body weight (FBW), specific growth rate (SGR), weight gain rate (WGR), and condition factor (CF), significantly outperforming the control group (G1, p < 0.05). 0.10% BA addition significantly increased whole‐body crude protein and lipid content. (p < 0.05). Serum analysis showed a significant reduction in total bilirubin (TBIL) and triglycerides (TG), and an increase in BA and lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C) in the G3 group compared to G1 (p < 0.05). The 0.10% BA supplementation downregulated pro‐inflammatory gene expression, like il-1β, and upregulated lipid metabolism‐related genes, like scdb, in the intestinal tract of O. mykiss (p < 0.05). 16S high‐throughput sequencing identified key microbial groups in the intestine of O. mykiss, highlighting significant differences in microbial composition with BA supplementation. Metabolomic analysis revealed that BA addition altered the metabolic profile of O. mykiss, affecting pathways such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) catabolism, cysteine (Cys) and methionine (Met) metabolism, the sulfur relay system, arachidonic acid (AA) metabolism, and ovarian steroidogenesis. In summary, a 0.10% BA addition to the diet of O. mykiss significantly improved growth performance, mitigated intestinal inflammation in specimens fed low fishmeal diets, and promoted overall gut health and lipid metabolism.

1. Introduction

The sustained growth of aquaculture is crucial for global food security. Recent years have witnessed a rapid expansion in this sector, with projections from the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) of the United Nations indicating that ~53% of global seafood will be sourced from aquaculture by 2030 [1]. Despite this growth, the industry faces significant challenges, particularly in the realms of feed costs and fishmeal production. The availability and cost of sustainable feed are major concerns in aquaculture [2]. Feed expenses represent a substantial portion of the operational costs in sustainable aquaculture, typically ranging from 30% to 70% of total costs. Consequently, managing fishmeal expenses is vital for controlling overall feed costs, which in turn impacts the profitability of aquaculture ventures [3]. Historically, fishmeal has been the preferred dietary protein source in aquafeed production, owing to its rich composition of long‐chain omega‐3 fatty acids, vitamins, minerals, and a balanced profile of essential amino acids, all of which are fundamental for normal animal growth [4]. However, the rapid expansion of aquaculture has led to a global shortfall in fishmeal supply, thereby necessitating the exploration of alternative protein sources [5]. This emerging scenario underscores the urgency to identify and develop alternative feed protein sources as the production of fishmeal continues to decline annually.

Plant‐based protein feeds, due to their consistent yield and cost‐effectiveness, are frequently utilized as substitutes for fishmeal in aquafeeds. Examples include soybean meal, cottonseed meal, and rapeseed meal [6]. Soybean meal, in particular, is distinguished among plant proteins for its high protein content, palatability, and digestibility when compared to other options such as cottonseed, rapeseed, and peanut meals. Compared to animal‐derived proteins such as meat‐and‐bone meal and plasma protein powder, soybean meal offers advantages in terms of availability, resistance to oxidation and lipidation, and a higher safety profile. Consequently, soybean meal is extensively used in aquafeeds, with tens of millions of tonnes employed annually as a premium fishmeal alternative. Research indicates that substituting fishmeal with soybean meal at optimal levels does not markedly impede fish growth [7, 8]. Nonetheless, soybean meal harbors antinutritional factors (ANFs), including trypsin inhibitors, hemagglutinins, antigenic proteins, saponins, and phytates. These elements can reduce feed palatability and digestibility, potentially harming specific physiological functions and digestive tract tissues in aquatic species, thereby impacting their growth, immune response, and intestinal health [9]. ANFs have been documented to trigger inflammatory signaling in the digestive tract of various fish species, with some studies reporting soybean meal‐induced inflammatory responses in the distal intestinal mucosa of farmed fish [10]. Furthermore, trypsin inhibiting factor (TIF), commonly present in vegetable protein diets, can bind to trypsin, forming an irreversible complex that reduces protein digestibility. TIF also induces pancreatic hyperactivity, leading to increased intestinal trypsin activity. Trypsin contains high levels of sulfur‐containing amino acids. It can cause a chronic imbalance in amino acid metabolism in aquatic animals, eventually leading to hyperplasia and hypertrophy of the pancreas and other organs [11, 12]. These factors present significant challenges to the development of low fishmeal diets and the sustainable advancement of aquaculture. Rainbow trout fed low fishmeal diets containing soyabean meal exhibit poor growth and feed efficiency, whereas bile acid (BA) supplementation improves these parameters to the same level as fish fed dietary whole fishmeal [13].

BA, a crucial component of bile and a soluble byproduct of hepatic cholesterol catabolism, plays a significant role in the digestive system [14]. Transported to the biliary tract and secreted into the intestine, BAs are instrumental in dissolving dietary lipids, thereby facilitating their digestion and absorption. Subsequently, they are reabsorbed in the intestinal tract and returned to the liver via enterohepatic recycling [15]. In aquaculture, BAs are commonly utilized as feed additives due to their involvement in lipid and glucose metabolism [16, 17]. They regulate enterohepatic circulation and maintain homeostasis of triglycerides (TG), cholesterol, energy, and glucose [18].

BAs also function as signaling molecules, activating various nuclear and membrane receptors, including the farnesoid X receptor (FXR), G‐protein coupled receptor 5 (TGR5), vitamin D receptor (VDR), constitutive androstane receptor (CAR), and pregnane X receptor (PXR) [18]. Specifically, BA regulates lipid metabolism through FXR activation, with chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA), a primary BA, being a potent activator of FXR [19, 20]. The addition of BAs to aquafeeds has been shown to enhance the growth and gut health of species such as largemouth bass (Micropterus salmoides) and grass carp (Ctenopharyngodon idella) [21, 22]. The composition of the gut microbiome is vital for fish growth, and its stability and appropriate composition are crucial for fish health. Research has highlighted a significant interplay between BA metabolism and intestinal microflora [23–25]. Consequently, the role of BAs in aquaculture is increasingly recognized, with growing research and application in fish nutrition and health management [26].

The rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss), classified under the family Salmoniformes, Salmonidae, is globally recognized by the FAO as a prime candidate for aquaculture due to its rapid growth and high economic yield. O. mykiss ranks as the 17th most farmed aquaculture species globally and is the second most cultivated salmonid, following the Atlantic salmon (Salmo salar). In China, triploid rainbow trout dominate commercial farming due to their sterility and growth advantages, yet their response to low‐fishmeal diets remains understudied. Consequently, functional feed additives are increasingly incorporated into aquafeeds to enhance fish growth and organ health, thereby mitigating the negative effects of substituting fishmeal with alternative protein sources [27]. Given these challenges, this study investigates the potential of BAs to mitigate the adverse effects of low fishmeal diets in triploid O. mykiss, focusing on growth performance, digestive health, and gut microbiota composition.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Experimental Feed

“G1 to G5” correspond to BA levels of 0%–0.2%, as detailed in Table 1. The experimental basal diets consisted of soybean meal and fishmeal as the main protein sources, a 1:1 mixture of soybean oil and fish oil as the main lipid component, and flour as the main carbohydrate source. The preparation process involved initial grinding and sieving of all raw materials, followed by precise measurement according to the specified feed formulation recipe. The ingredients were blended using a step‐by‐step expansion method. After thoroughly mixing the micro components, bulk ingredients such as soybean meal and fishmeal were added and finally mixed with fish oil. Subsequently, an adequate amount of water was added to the homogenizer to thoroughly mix the feed, ensuring the correct dry humidity for granulation. The mixture was then pelletized into 1.5 mm feed pellets using a pelleting machine (HX‐200G; Guanghe Instruments, Guangzhou, China). These pellets were dried to ~10% moisture content in a ventilated oven at 60°C, cooled to ambient temperature, and then packaged in sealed bags. The packaged feed was stored in a refrigerator at −20°C for future use.

2.2. Fish Management

In this study, O. mykiss specimens were procured from Agrimarine Holdings Inc., Benxi, China. The initial cohort of juvenile rainbow trout, averaging a weight of ~15.97 ± 1.4 g, underwent a 14‐day acclimatization period in recirculating aquaculture systems. Subsequently, 300 specimens exhibiting robust health and uniform size were selected. These fish were allocated into five experimental groups, each differentiated by the gradient concentration of BA. Each group consisted of three replicates, with 20 fish per replicate. The control group received a low fishmeal basal diet, while the experimental groups were fed diets infused with varying levels of BAs.

The culture environment was meticulously controlled: tap water served as the water source; water temperature was maintained between 13.5 and 14.5°C; dissolved oxygen levels were kept above 6 mg/L; pH ranged from 6.5 to 6.8; concentrations of NO2-‐N and NH4+‐N were maintained below 0.02 and 0.20 mg/L, respectively. Feeding was based on the satiation level relative to the body weights of the fish. The photoperiod was set to 12 h of light per day. Daily water exchange was maintained at 25%, and the feeding trial lasted for 56 days, which was conducted at 8:00 a.m. and 4:00 p.m. The fish were weighed every 14 days to determine body weight and adjust meal amounts.

2.3. Sample Collection and Analysis

Upon completion of the 56‐day feeding trial, O. mykiss specimens were subjected to a 24‐h fasting period before sampling. The fish were anesthetized using MS‐222 at a concentration of 75 mg/L, followed by weighing. The count and weight of O. mykiss in each tank were recorded to calculate survival rate (SR), final body weight (FBW), weight gain rate (WGR), specific growth rate (SGR), and feed conversion rate (FCR). Blood samples were extracted from the caudal vein and centrifuged at 3500 × g for 10 min at 4°C to obtain the supernatant, which was then stored at −80°C for future biochemical assays. For body composition analysis, three whole fish from each tank were preserved at −20°C. Additionally, three samples each of midgut and liver tissues were collected from every tank and stored at −40°C for biochemical examinations. For gene expression analysis, three samples each of the proximal intestine and liver were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently stored at −80°C. Furthermore, the midgut and liver of three fish per tank were collected and immersed in Bouin’s fixative for histological examination of these organs.

The intestinal contents of O. mykiss were collected under sterile conditions, rapidly frozen with liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C. Based on growth performance parameters of O. mykiss in this study, the control group, G1, and the most significantly altered experimental group G3 were chosen for further analysis, with six replicates each. The intestinal contents of these groups were subjected to nontargeted metabolomic analysis and microbial community diversity assessment using Illumina MiSeq PE250 sequencing (Shanghai BIOZERON Co., Ltd.). For the purpose of this analysis, the control and experimental groups were designated as BA0 and BA1000, respectively.

2.4. Body Composition and Histological Analysis

Body composition (moisture, protein, lipid, and ash) of fish and feed was determined following AOAC protocols [28]. Moisture content was ascertained by drying the samples in an oven (Model 202‐3AB Electrothermal Constant Temperature Drying Oven, Tianjin Tester Instrument Co., Ltd.) at 65°C until a consistent weight was achieved. Crude protein analysis was performed using a protein analyzer (Elementar, Germany), employing the Dumas combustion method. Crude lipid content was determined through petroleum ether (analytically pure, boiling range class I) extraction. Ash content was assessed by incinerating the samples in a muffle furnace (KSW‐6–12 electric furnace temperature controller, Yuyao Jieda Temperature Instrument Factory) at 550°C for 4 h.

For histomorphological studies, ~1 cm sections of intestines and livers were immersed and fixed in prepared Bouin’s fixative for over 48 h. The samples were then dehydrated and cleared in graded alcohols and xylene, followed by embedding, sectioning, and staining with hematoxylin‐eosin (H. E.). The sections were then sealed with neutral gum and examined and photographed using a biomicroscope (HistoCore MULTICUT, Leica, Shanghai, China).

2.5. Serum Biochemical Parameters and Enzyme Activities

The levels of total bilirubin (TBIL), total BAs (TBAs), TG, and total cholesterol (THOL) in the samples were quantified using an automatic biochemical analyzer (MNCHIP, Celercare V5, China; Table S1).

Serum samples were analyzed for low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C, A113–1–1) and high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C, A112–1–1) utilizing a commercial assay kit provided by Nanjing Jianjian Bioengineering Institute (Table S1). For intestinal sample preparation, phosphate buffer saline (PBS, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co.,Ltd.) was added in a ratio of 1:9 (weight in grams to volume in milliliters), followed by mechanical homogenization under ice‐water bath conditions to ensure adequate cell disruption and extraction of intestinal samples. Subsequent analyses of relevant indices were conducted in accordance with the specifications of the kit provided by Nanjing Jianjian Bioengineering Institute.

2.6. RNA Extraction and Real‐Time Quantitative PCR

Primer design was conducted using Primer 3 Plus, adhering to established primer design principles, and validation was performed through the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). The base sequences of the primers are listed in Table 2. Primers for this experiment were synthesized by Jilin Kume Biotechnology Co. The melt curve analysis was performed to ensure primer specificity. The expression levels of the relevant genes were quantified using a real‐time fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument, and the expression of target genes was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method [29]. Total samples were homogenized under liquid nitrogen to preserve RNA integrity, and subsequently stored at −80°C by the guidelines provided by the assay kit. Total RNA was extracted from the powdered samples using TRIzol Reagent (Ambion, San Diego, CA, USA) and reverse transcribed into cDNA (RR047A; TaKaRa, Beijing, China). The concentration and purity of RNA were assessed using the UV colorimetric method (Nanodrop Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), with absorbance ratios (260/280 nm) of the samples ranging between 1.8 and 2.0. The internal reference gene β-actin was used to normalize cDNA loading. Each group included nine samples for RT–PCR analysis, with three technical replicates per sample. The methodology for real‐time quantitative PCR is detailed in Table S2 .

2.7. Intestinal Microbiota Bioinformatics

The methodology for Illumina PE250 sequencing encompassed several key steps: extraction of genomic DNA, PCR amplification of the V4‐V5 region of the bacteria 16S ribosomal RNA gene, fluorescence quantification, construction of the Illumina PE250 library, and subsequent Illumina PE250 sequencing. The paired‐end (PE) reads generated from Illumina PE250 sequencing were initially assembled based on their overlapping sequences, followed by a quality assessment. Postquality assessment, the reads were differentiated among samples, facilitating operational taxonomic unit (OTU) clustering analysis and taxonomic classification of species.

Utilizing the data from OTU clustering, comprehensive analyses were conducted, including multiple diversity indices of OTUs and assessment of sequencing depth. Additionally, the taxonomic data enabled statistical analyses of community structures across various taxonomic levels. Leveraging these analyses, extensive statistical and visual examinations of community structures and phylogenetic relationships were performed. The detailed workflow of this process is outlined in Table S3.

2.8. Untargeted Metabolomics Materials and Methods

Based on the growth performance data of O. mykiss, we selected samples from G1 to G3 for metabolomic analysis. This study employs ultra‐high performance liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (UHPLC–MS/MS, Vanquish UHPLC system, Thermo Fisher, Germany) technology for nontargeted metabolomics analysis. Q ExactiveTM HF mass spectrometer was operated in positive/negative polarity mode. The experimental methodology encompasses several critical stages: extraction of metabolites from samples, LC–MS/MS analysis, and subsequent data processing and interpretation. 2.9. Data Analysis

The growth performance parameters were calculated as follows:

WGR (%) = 100 × (W t−W 0)/W 0;

SR (%) = (Number of surviving fish / initial number of fish) × 100;

feed conversion ratio (FCR) = W f/(W t−W 0);

condition factor (CF) = 100 × W t/L t 3;

SGR (%/d) = 100 × (lnWt−lnW0)/ 56;

hepatosomatic index (HSI; %) = 100 × (Liver weight (g)/body weight (g));

viscerosomatic index (VSI; %) = 100 × (Viscera (g)/body weight (g));

where W 0 represents the initial body weight (g), W t represents the FBW (g), W f represents the feed intake (g), and L t represents the final body length (cm).

Statistical analyses were conducted utilizing SPSS 26.0 for Windows (SPSS, Chicago, USA). Prior to analysis, all data underwent tests for normality and homogeneity of variance using the chi‐square test. Data conforming to a normal distribution were then analyzed using one‐way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s multiple range test for post hoc comparisons. Statistical significance was established at p < 0.05, while a p of less than 0.01 was considered highly significant. Results are presented as mean ± standard error (S.E.). For the graphical representation of gene expression data, GraphPad Prism 8.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA) was employed.

3.3. Histomorphological Assessment

3.3.1. Intestinal Histomorphology

Table 5 indicates that the addition of BAs to the feed significantly enhanced the villi height and muscular layer thickness in the midgut of O. mykiss (p < 0.05). The length of the midgut villi in O. mykiss was significantly greater in the group with 0.10% BA addition. BA supplementation significantly enhanced villus height and muscular thickness in the midgut (p < 0.05), with the most pronounced effect observed in G3 (0.10% BA). Histological examination revealed well‐organized villi, intact microvilli, and increased goblet cell numbers in G2 and G3 (Figure 2).

4. Discussion

BAs, functioning as natural emulsifiers, enhance lipid contact with lipase, effectively emulsifying lipids into smaller droplets or cohesive particles. This process accelerates lipid metabolism in aquatic animals, thereby improving growth performance and mitigating the effects of high‐lipid diets on abnormal lipid deposition. BAs facilitate the absorption of lipids and lipid‐soluble vitamins, promoting fish growth [30]. Numerous studies have demonstrated the beneficial effects of dietary BAs on fish growth performance, leading to their widespread use as aquafeed additives. The growth‐promoting effects of BAs are influenced by several factors, including the optimal amount for different fish species and their impact on growth performance. For instance, in Channa argus, 1000 mg/kg of BAs enhanced growth, whereas 200 mg/kg reduced it [31]. In this study, a 0.10% BA addition in feed markedly improved the growth performance of O. mykiss. However, excessive BAs stimulate the gastrointestinal mucosal membrane of fish, reduce their feed intake and feed utilization rate, and ultimately lead to slowed growth rate and stagnant weight gain, as observed in Oreochromis niloticus, Otenopharyngodon idellus, and O. mykiss [17, 21, 32].

BAs act through various pathways, such as enhancing lipid utilization, regulating sugar and lipid metabolism, modulating the immune system, and improving antioxidant capacity [33]. The addition of BAs to feeds attenuates the negative effects of vegetable proteins on growth performance, feed utilization lipid digestion, and metabolism in Micropterus salmoides [14]. The addition of BAs to increase cholesterol and TBA in Scophthalmus maximus may be due to the upregulation of cholesterol 7 alpha‐hydroxylase (cyp7a1) expression, which improves cholesterol digestion and absorption and TBA synthesis. Supplementation with bovine BAs or synthetic bovine bile salts in O. mykiss feeds has been shown to prevent cholestasis and mitigate growth performance issues, higher FCRs, and intestinal and liver damage caused by ANFs in alternative protein sources like soybean meal [34].

The nutritional status of fish is reflected in the histomorphology of their digestive system, primarily the intestine and liver. Histomorphometric analysis is commonly used to assess fish nutrition [35]. The height of intestinal folds and flukes correlates positively with absorptive capacity [36]. Studies have shown that replacing more than 50% of fishmeal with soybean meal in Epinephelus lanceolatus diets significantly reduces villus height [37]. In O. mykiss and S. salar, diets supplemented with soy isoflavones led to hepatocellular atrophy and reduced vesicle accumulation, but taurocholate supplementation alleviated these effects [34]. In Pangasianodon hypothalamus, 1% and 1.25% BA supplementation significantly increased intestinal villi length [38]. Similarly, this study found that BA addition improved intestinal villus length and liver cell morphology in O. mykiss fed a low fishmeal diet. This may be because BAs activate fxr as a signaling factor, promoting intestinal cell proliferation, thereby increasing the height of intestinal villi, expanding the intestinal absorption area, and enhancing intestinal digestive capacity [39]. Sufficient BA facilitates fat digestion; however, excessive BA may disrupt the acid–base balance in the intestinal tract, compromise the mucosal protective barrier, and induce intestinal dysfunction [40].

BA may influence lipid metabolism by increasing lipid oxidation or energy expenditure in aquatic species [41]. BA supplementation in soybean meal‐based diets restored normal digestive processes in O. mykiss, improving growth rates and feed utilization [42]. TG concentration in serum tends to decrease with increasing dietary BA levels. This study observed significantly lower TG levels in the BA supplementation group compared to the control. Similar findings were reported in Oreochromis mossambicus and Channa argus [17, 43]. LDL transports cholesterol from the liver to peripheral tissues and blood, while HDL carries cholesterol back to the liver. Existing studies have demonstrated that the reduction in circulating TC resulted from reduced LDL‐C and increased HDL‐C. Decreased circulating LDL‐C is due to BA negatively regulating VLDL secretion via nuclear receptors FXR and SHP and by stabilizing LDL receptor mRNA and facilitating LDL uptake [44]. In this study, BA addition did not significantly affect serum cholesterol. However, LDL and HDL cholesterol levels increased with BA supplementation, indicating enhanced cholesterol metabolism in O. mykiss. Based on our current understanding, the mechanism by which BAs affect circulating HDL levels in animals remains unclear and calls for more research.

BA promotes lipid digestion and absorption and acts as a signaling molecules, activating fxr to regulate lipid metabolism. fxr, a nuclear receptor family member prevalent in animal liver and intestines, is key to BA metabolism homeostasis [45]. BAs regulate fish lipid metabolism primarily through the FXR‐SHP (atypical nuclear receptor small heterodimer) pathway [46]. srebp1 is pivotal in lipid synthesis. BA‐activated fxr upregulates shp, inhibiting srebp1 and downstream lipid synthesis genes like acetyl‐CoA synthetase 2 (accs2), reducing lipid synthesis [47]. In Cichlid macrocephalus, fxr activation lowered liver TG and THOL, while inhibition increased both. In O. mykiss, fxr regulates enterohepatic circulation; its downregulation decreased liver cholesterol, TG, and BAs [48]. fxr activation also induces pparα expression, participating in lipid metabolism and fatty acid β‐oxidation [18, 49, 50]. pparα activation increases mitochondrial carnitine palmitoyltransferase1 (cpti) and acox activity, accelerating β‐oxidation and regulating TG [51]. Nevertheless, the present study merely infers the role of this pathway in O. mykiss. It did not quantify the protein levels of fxr, shp, and srebp1, and thus direct activation remains unconfirmed.

Aquatic organisms maintain a balance between oxidation and antioxidants to prevent oxidative damage. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and lipid peroxidation protective factors ensure normal cellular metabolism [52]. BA supplementation significantly reduced MDA levels, aligning with clinical findings, where BA reduced ROS and increased glutathione levels [53]. CAT activity improved with different BA levels, possibly due to ROS activity releasing free radicals and leading to peroxidation, affecting metabolic processes and cell viability. BA supplementation in broilers improved antioxidant performance, a finding corroborated by this study in O. mykiss. This study found that BAs act as hepatic metabolic signaling factors, reducing liver damage from oxidative stress and inflammation by stimulating hepatocyte proliferation and participating in liver regeneration through receptor activation [54, 55]. Similarly, the optimal supplementation level of BAs has been shown to markedly boost liver antioxidant capacity in L. crocea and M. salmoides fed high‐lipid diets [41, 56]. The likely mechanism could be linked to the direct antioxidant properties of ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), which has been established as a potent scavenger of hydroxyl radicals in in vitro experiments [57].

Macrophages are one of the important immune cells in the intestinal tract. In Ctenopharyngodon idella, BA supplementation in a low‐protein, high‐lipid diet reduced inflammatory responses by downregulating pro‐inflammatory factors and upregulating anti‐inflammatory cytokines [21]. Similarly, this study found that BA supplementation in a low fishmeal diet downregulated pro‐inflammatory factors and upregulated anti‐inflammatory factors in O. mykiss intestines. BAs can activate the receptor gpbar1 to reduce the secretion of pro‐inflammatory factors tnf, il-6, and il-1β in M1 macrophages and promote the differentiation of M2 macrophages secreting the anti‐inflammatory factor il-10 in order to resist intestinal inflammation and enhance intestinal immunity [58]. Tight junctions, supported by F‐actin and myosin II, are crucial for barrier regulation. mlck activation increases paracellular permeability and is required for tight junction barrier regulation [59]. Moderate concentrations of BAs activate receptors on intestinal smooth muscle cells (G protein‐coupled receptors) to regulate mlck activity. This reduces degradation of tight junction proteins (occludin, zonula occludens‐1), thus maintaining epithelial barrier integrity and preventing pathogen or toxin translocation [40]. This study found that BA supplementation reduced mlck expression and upregulated zo-1, suggesting improved intestinal barrier health in O. mykiss.

The gut microbiota, a pivotal regulator of metabolic processes including energy balance, glucose metabolism, and lipid metabolism, facilitates digestion, nutrient absorption, and immune system activation, thereby playing an essential role in fish growth and health [60]. Prebiotic and probiotic feed additives modulate the fish microbiome and interact with the host immune system. Distinct from other vertebrates, the fish gut microbiome is characterized by the dominance of Proteobacteria and significant abundance of Clostridium [61, 62]. While multiple factors influence gut microbiota composition and function, host habitat constitutes the primary determinant of the fish gut microbial community, contrasting with other vertebrates where diet is considered the predominant factor [61]. In this study, Flavobacteriaceae exhibited significant abundance differences within BA0. Flavobacteriaceae represent a significant pathogenic risk to fish health, primarily responsible for flavobacteriosis in freshwater‐farmed salmonid trout, causing substantial disease‐related losses, particularly at juvenile stages [63]. Massilia is also frequently identified in infected fish [64]. A study investigating Aeromonas salmonicida achromogenes‐induced rapid microbiota alterations in O. mykiss revealed a significant increase in Oxalobacteraceae populations within the mucosal skin of rainbow trout 72 h postinfection [65].

Intestinal microorganisms regulate BA metabolism, with enzymes metabalized by gut microbes altering secondary BA composition. Gut microbes influence BA anabolism, affecting BA metabolic homeostasis and inducing diseases. Intestinal probiotics can improve pathological abnormalities, affecting glucose and lipid metabolism mediated by secondary BAs. BAs interact with microbes, explaining 37% of gut microbial distribution differences [66]. Intestinal microbes uncouple bound BAs, producing free and secondary BAs, such as converting cholic acid to deoxycholic acid and CDCA to lithocholic acid [67]. The experimental results of the study were comparable to those of the effect of BAs added to low fishmeal diets on the gut health of Penaeus vannamei [68]. Supplementing low fishmeal diets with BA boosted gut flora diversity and abundance, particularly elevating Verrucomicrobiae levels in the BA1000 group versus the BA0 group. Verrucomicrobiae bacteria metabolize primary BAs into secondary BAs [24]. The abundance of Verrucomicrobiae thickwill showed a trend of increasing with increasing BA addition. UDCA, a secondary BA, helps maintain intestinal health, stimulates the expression of BA‐associated receptors in intestinal epithelial cells, and effectively inhibits inflammatory responses. This may be because BA demonstrates antibacterial properties by disrupting the cell membrane structure of pathogenic bacteria (such as Vibrio, Aeromonas, and Edwardsiella), thereby compromising membrane integrity. This mechanism inhibits the proliferation of these harmful microorganisms and mitigates the risk of intestinal infection.

Recent studies have indicated that dietary changes can significantly alter the metabolic profiles of aquatic animals [69]. In this study, notable differences in metabolites were observed between the BA0 and BA1000 groups. Key metabolic pathways affected included the degradation of PAHs, Cys and Met metabolism, sulfur relay systems, AA metabolism, and ovarian steroidogenesis. However, the metabolic pathways and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites did not show significant changes.

Unmetabolized PAHs are toxic, and their active metabolites, such as epoxides and dihydrodiols, can bind to cellular proteins and DNA, potentially leading to mutations, developmental abnormalities, tumor formation, and cancer [70]. Enzymes from the cytochrome P450 (CYP) family, including CYP1A, CYP1B, CYP2C, and CYP2E, are involved in metabolizing PAHs into diol epoxides. PAH exposure induces the production of these cytochromes, which convert PAHs into mutagenic diol epoxides. This process involves PAH molecules binding to the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR), subsequently activating transcription factors that increase cytochrome production [71]. The gut microbiota plays a crucial role in the degradation of PAHs, breaking them down into simpler compounds, thus reducing their accumulation and toxicity.

Proteins and amino acids are crucial for the physiological condition, immune status, and growth performance of fish. Met and Cys are sulfur‐containing amino acids essential for protein structure and metabolism. Met metabolism involves transmethylation, remethylation, and transsulfuration processes [72]. Met is a limiting amino acid in fish diets that use high levels of non‐fishmeal protein sources. Both deficiencies and excesses of Met can inhibit fish growth, with varying responses observed in different fish species to increased dietary Met levels [33]. In fish, hepatic lipid accumulation results from dietary lipid uptake, de novo lipogenesis, and β‐oxidation. Met, as a methyl group donor, and its metabolites regulate lipid metabolism. Both excessive and deficient Met levels can trigger apoptotic pathways and reduce lipid transport and oxidative gene expression, leading to increased hepatic lipid deposition in banded catfish (Tachysurus fulvidraco) [73]. This may decrease lipid deposition and improve hepatic health. In the gut, certain bacteria participate in Cys and Met metabolism, producing sulfur metabolites like hydrogen sulfide, methyl mercaptan, and dimethyl sulfide. These metabolites have beneficial effects, such as regulating intestinal motility and improving permeability, but excessive amounts can have adverse health effects. Dysregulation of Cys and Met metabolism in gut microorganisms is linked to diseases like inflammatory bowel disease [74].

Lipid metabolism involves the conversion of lipids into lysophosphatidic acid, cannabinoids, prostaglandins, sphingosine 1‐phosphate, and steroid hormones [75]. Cholesterol, besides being present in the gastrointestinal tract and hepatobiliary system, is abundant in human follicles for sex steroid formation, detectable in follicular fluid in the presence of BAs [76]. AA, an essential unsaturated fatty acid in the omega‐6 family, is metabolized by cyclooxygenase (COX), lipoxygenase (LOX), and CYP enzymes, producing various biologically active mediators [77]. Mammalian studies suggest that BAs may influence the AA synthesis pathway [55]. Additionally, the type and quantity of gut microbes are related to AA metabolism. Certain probiotics and prebiotics can promote AA metabolism, reducing inflammatory responses. Thus, modulating the structure and metabolic activity of gut microorganisms may offer new approaches to disease prevention and treatment.

The enrichment of intestinal metabolic pathways in O. mykiss by BA may be a regulatory process that precisely matches its physiological characteristics. As a cold‐water carnivorous fish, O. mykiss relies on lipids for energy supply, requires efficient utilization of high‐quality proteins, and has a fragile intestinal barrier. BAs meet these core needs through the coordinated regulation of multiple pathways. First, they enrich the lipid metabolic pathway: by virtue of emulsification and activation of lipases, BAs promote fat degradation, converting over 80% of dietary lipids into energy to adapt to the energy demand of the cold‐water environment. Second, they enrich the amino acid metabolic pathway, with a particular focus on activating Met metabolism. By enhancing the activity of Met transaminase, BA facilitates the conversion of Met into methyl donors or its involvement in protein synthesis, thereby supporting muscle growth and immune requirements. Meanwhile, BA enriches the energy metabolic pathway, strengthening the TCA cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation to compensate for the insufficient enzyme activity under low temperatures. In response to environmental stress, they enrich PAHs metabolic pathway: by activating CYP enzymes, BA accelerates the degradation of PAHs and reduces toxic damage. Additionally, BA regulate the cholesterol metabolic pathway, promoting the absorption of dietary cholesterol while inhibiting excessive cholesterol synthesis in the liver, thus maintaining a balance between membrane construction and BA synthesis. Furthermore, BA enrichs the SCFA metabolic pathway through remodeling the intestinal microbiota, thereby repairing the intestinal barrier. These pathways work synergistically to ensure the growth, health, and environmental adaptability of rainbow trout, ultimately translating into improved aquaculture efficiency.

5. Conclusion

This study demonstrates that adding 0.10% BAs to low fishmeal diets significantly enhances the growth and health of O. mykiss and that excessive supplementation may have negative or diminishing returns. Biochemically, BA supplementation led to increased levels of crude protein and lipids, and altered lipid metabolism markers, including reduced TG and increased LDL cholesterol. Importantly, BA reduced the expression of pro‐inflammatory genes, suggesting a mitigating effect on intestinal inflammation. The study also revealed shifts in intestinal microbiota composition and metabolic pathways, highlighting the role of BA in promoting gut health and metabolic balance. These findings offer valuable insights for aquaculture nutrition, suggesting that BA supplementation could be a viable strategy to enhance the growth and well‐being of O. mykiss on low fishmeal diets.
