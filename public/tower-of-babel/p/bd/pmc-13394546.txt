Electrodeposition of Zn nanoparticles via nanoemulsion: a single-entity electrochemistry study
Authors: Ali Abdi, Leila Nazari, Yuming Zhao, Talia Jane Stockmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Steel continues to be a dominant construction component owing to its superior material and physicochemical properties. Corrosion/unwanted metal oxidation still plagues this sector leading to material failure. Zn remains a cornerstone coating material acting as a sacrificial anode; meanwhile, coating nanofunctionalization has been shown to enhance anticorrosion performance. Water-in-oil nanoemulsions were explored as confined nanoreactors for electrochemical functionalization using ultramicroelectrodes (UMEs) fabricated from carbon fiber, Pt, or steel wire, polished to form inlaid disc UMEs 7, 10, and 25 µm in diameter, respectively. Zn-containing nanoemulsions were stabilized by alkylphosphonium ionic liquids (ILs) with electrodeposition investigated via single-entity electrochemistry (SEE). Emulsions were prepared from ZnCl2/LiCl-containing aqueous droplets dispersed in α,α,α-trifluorotoluene (TFT) along with IL salts as surfactants composed of trihexyltetradecylphosphonium (P66614+) paired with different anions, including Br−, NTf2− (bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide), or TB− (tetrakis(pentafluorophenyl)borate). CTAB (cetyltrimethylammonium bromide) was also used as a surfactant but induced poorer emulsion stability compared to the ILs. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements showed noticeable changes in droplet size and stability when the IL type, water-to-oil ratio, salt concentration, and pH were changed. P66614Br performed better than P66614NTf2 and P66614TB, becoming the focus. Zn electrodeposition signal is complicated by the H2 evolution reaction (HER) occurring at similar potentials. The shape and frequency of SEE current transients recorded at −1.3 V with ZnCl2-emulsions were compared to those with electro-inactive MgCl2 to determine the relative contribution of HER to the current density. Integration of the i–t spikes allowed the charge transferred (Q) to be determined, from which the mean radius of water nanodroplets (rdrop) to be calculated. The rdrop at carbon fiber UMEs agreed well with DLS measurements, while Pt and steel UMEs demonstrated a 3× and 2× relative signal enhancement owing to their enhanced electrocatalytic activity towards HER. SEM analysis revealed the differences in these deposited structures, which are dependent on emulsion conditions and the substrate used. Overall, our studies show that nanoemulsion composition and droplet behaviour can strongly influence confined Zn electrodeposition. Mechanistic insights into the relationships among emulsion stability, stochastic electrochemical signals, and metal–deposition processes relevant to Zn-based corrosion protection systems are established.

Introduction

Steel remains a critical material for marine infrastructure such as oceangoing vessels, owing to its excellent mechanical properties and the abundance and low-cost of iron. However, its operational lifespan is limited by metallic oxidation/corrosion, which can lead to catastrophic material failure.1 Various corrosion mitigation strategies have been employed to address this challenge, including organic coatings, such as epoxies and other organic polymers,2–6 as well as metallic coatings7–9 often achieved through galvanization.1,10,11

Galvanization is the process of coating steel with zinc (Zn), which can be performed through several methods, most notably hot dip coating10 and electroplating.12 While hot-dipping requires the immersion of steel components in a molten Zn bath,10 electroplating involves the reduction of zinc salts (e.g., ZnSO4) from an electrolyte solution.12,13 The resulting Zn coating provides the underlying steel with physical/wear protection while simultaneously acting as a sacrificial anode. This sacrificial behavior is attributed to zinc's modest reduction potential (vs. RHE14), which facilitates the preferential oxidation of Zn over Fe and other alloying elements within the steel.15,16

Many effective Zn electrodeposition methods have been developed to enhance performance. Tan et al.17 employed pulsed electroplating, which offers distinct advantages over direct-current methods due to its ability to achieve higher current densities and promote faster nucleation. This approach enables control over the crystallization process and the resulting morphology of the Zn layer. Manzano et al.18 exploited this technique to grow ZnO films on a Au(111) substrate, achieving a diverse array of film morphologies, including platelets, planar films, flower-like structures, and columns. It is noteworthy that these morphologies were obtained without the use of additives, relying solely on pulsing the potential between anodic and cathodic regimes.

Recently, water-in-oil emulsions have emerged as an effective medium for the electrodeposition of metal nanoparticles (NPs). In this approach, water micro- or nanodroplets diffuse through the solution via Brownian motion, colliding randomly with the electrode surface.19 Hydrophobic surfactants are commonly applied in this method to minimize surface wetting, thereby maintaining the spherical shape of the droplet, which then functions as a discrete micro-/nanoreactor.19–22 Upon collision, the metal precursor salts are reduced to form nanoparticles, the size and shape of which are governed by the size of the nanoemulsion and the internal concentration of precursor salts20,23–25 Dick's group23 demonstrated that a water-in-oil emulsion could be utilized for electrogenerating Pt NPs on highly oriented pyrolytic graphite (HOPG) electrodes. In their study, stochastic collisions of water nanodroplets delivered the PtCl4− salt to the electrode surface, where reduction occurred within the confined geometry of the droplet to form NPs. Furthermore, the size and surface roughness of the NPs could also be tuned via the choice of surfactant and the applied reduction potential.23 This approach was subsequently extended to the synthesis of high- and medium-entropy alloys (HEAs or MEAs).25 Murakami et al.20,24 utilized this technique to electrodeposit CoNiCu MEAs by encapsulating the metal chloride salts within an emulsion stabilized by sodium bis(2-ethylhexyl)-sulfosuccinate as surfactant, using 1,2-dichloroethane (DCE) as the oil/continuous phase. Their work succeeded in generating a homogeneous layer on an Au substrate.

Similarly, Rossi et al.26 electrosynthesized Ag–Au bimetallic NPs via encapsulation of metal precursor salts within polystyrene-b-poly(2-vinylpyridine) reverse micelles, generating core-shell-like NPs on HOPG electrodes. The authors investigated the influence of applied reduction potential and a DCE/toluene continuous phase with the traditional n-butylammonium hexafluorophosphate (TBAPF6) or an imidazolium ionic liquid (IL) as supporting electrolyte on the final NP morphology. They also employed single entity electrochemistry (SEE) to characterize the electrodeposition process.26 Their findings underscore the necessity of comprehensively evaluating all parameters within the electrosynthetic strategy.

Single-entity electrochemistry (SEE) is a cutting-edge domain of electroanalytical chemistry and a special branch of nano-electrochemistry.27 Several seminal SEE studies were published in the early 2000's.19,28,29 Detection relies on the random collision of single entities (e.g., single molecule, nanoparticle, protein, nanodroplet) with an electroactive interface, which can be a solid/solution28–31 or immiscible liquid/liquid32–36 one. In this technique, the collision is recorded in the current–time (i–t) domain as a current transient or “spike”. The signals are typically triggered by the particles through one of the following mechanisms: (i) electrocatalytic amplification (ECA) of a heterogeneous electron transfer reaction; (ii) the oxidation or reduction of the particle, often leading to its dissolution; (iii) the particle blocking an ongoing, steady-state oxidation/reduction of a redox mediator.37–40 SEE can be used to probe the fusion events of nanoemulsions at immiscible liquid/liquid interfaces, which trigger simple ion transfer or release33,34,36,41–43 upon nanodroplet collision, as well as emulsion-encapsulated redox mediators whose redox chemistry is initiated upon impact.44–46

Regarding the electrodeposition of NPs from emulsions, commonly used substrates include HOPG,20,23,24,26 carbon fiber,47 and metals such as Au.18 These substrates have well-characterized surfaces and are therefore frequently cited as model surfaces in the literature. The electrodeposition properties of reverse micelles/nanoemulsions on steel surfaces remain an area of significant interest. In this work, we investigated the electrodeposition of Zn NPs onto a steel, Pt, or carbon fiber ultramicroelectrode (UME). The system utilized a ZnCl2 precursor salt encapsulated within a water-in-oil emulsion, the stability of which was examined using dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements. The droplets were stabilized by alkylphosphonium IL surfactants, and their performance was compared to a conventionally used surfactant, cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB).

Next, SEE was employed to characterize the electrodeposition process through monitoring the stochastic impacts of the nanoreactors towards the UME electroactive surface. Fig. 1A depicts the general SEE/emulsion strategy, while Fig. 1B shows a typical current spike recorded during a water droplet collision. Because the reduction of Zn2+ overlaps with the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER),13,48 a control emulsion was prepared in which ZnCl2 was replaced with MgCl2. Since the reduction potential of Mg2+ is significantly more negative than Zn2+ and HER, it can be considered to be essentially electro-inactive under these conditions. The total current density (jtotal) is a sum of the electrodeposition (jdeposit) and HER (jHER) heterogeneous reaction current densities, i.e.,1jtotal = jdeposit + jHERIn this way, the contribution of HER to the SEE current transient signal can be deconvoluted from the ratio of HER to Zn0 electrodeposition, allowing for the determination of the faradaic efficiency of the reaction.13,48

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time a steel UME has been employed to investigate SEE phenomenon with its response compared to that of traditional UME materials such as carbon fiber or Pt. Moreover, our results illustrate how SEE can be used to compare electrocatalytic performance between different electrode materials.

Experimental

Chemicals were used as received without further purification. Aqueous solutions were prepared using Milli-Q filtered ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ cm). Zinc chloride (ZnCl2, 98%) was purchased from Alfa-Aesar, and lithium chloride (LiCl, 99%) was obtained from AnalaR, BDH Chemicals Ltd. Lithium etherate tetrakis(pentafluorophenyl)borate (LiTB, >99%) was sourced from Boulder Scientific. Cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB, 97%), trihexyltetradecylphosphonium bromide (P66614Br, 95%), lithium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide (LiNTf2, 99%), magnesium chloride (MgCl2, 98%), chlorotrihexylsilane (95%), and α,α,α-trifluorotoluene (TFT, 99%) were procured from Sigma-Aldrich. Trihexyltetradecylphosphonium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide (P66614NTf2, 97%) was obtained from Strem Chemicals Inc.

Emulsions were generated by adding water (0.5%, 1.0%, or 20% v/v) to the oil phase containing the chemical species specified in Scheme 1, followed by sonication for ∼30 min. The emulsions were prepared in 1.8 mL vials, the interior of which was first silanized by using chlorotrihexylsilane for ∼20 min and subsequently rinsed with ultrapure water and TFT.

Components of the water-in-oil emulsions consisting of (A) aqueous and (B) α,α,α-trifluorotoluene (TFT) phases. The aqueous phase comprises x mM of CTAB (cetyltrimethylammonium bromide) and y mM of ZnCl2 or MgCl2, with LiCl added at a concentration equivalent to the divalent metal salt. The TFT phase contains z mM of P66614X. P66614X is an ionic liquid (IL) comprised of the trihexyltetradecylphosphonium cation paired with either Br−, TB (tetrakis(pentafluorophenyl)borate), or NTf2 (bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide) anion.

Ultramicroelectrodes (UME) were fabricated by placing carbon fiber (CF), Pt (99.9%), or AISI 302 stainless steel wire (Delta Scientific) into borosilicate glass capillaries (Sutter Instruments) with outer and inner diameters of 2.00 mm and 1.16 mm, respectively. The diameters of the carbon fiber (CF), Pt (99.9%), and AISI 302 stainless steel wires are 7.0, 10, and 25 µm, respectively. The fabrication process has been described in detail elsewhere.49,50 All UMEs were prepared such that the ratio of the outer glass radius (rg) to the inlaid disc electroactive radius (ra) exceeded 30 (i.e., Rg = rg/ra > 30). Fig. 2 shows micrographs of a carbon fiber UME tip captured using a trinocular microscope (90× magnification) equipped with an 18-megapixel CCD camera.

Micrographs of the top-down view (A) and side view (B) of a carbon fiber (CF) ultramicroelectrode (UME) with ra = 3.5 µm. Images were captured using an 18-megapixel CCD camera attached to a trinocular microscope at 90× magnification.

UMEs were polished using Al2O3 polishing pads with grit sizes from 0.5 to 30 µm (Buehler Canada Scientific). All electrochemical experiments were performed in a three-electrode configuration employing a PG-618-USB potentiostat (Heka Electroniks) equipped with a head-stage mounted inside a grounded Faraday cage. The UME served as the working electrode, while the Pt wire and Ag/AgCl wires were used as the counter and reference electrodes, respectively. Due to the use of TFT as the bulk solvent, the Ag/AgCl-coated wire functioned as a quasi-reference electrode.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements were performed using a Delsa Nano S (Beckman Coulter Inc.). Prior to each measurement, the emulsions were thoroughly mixed and then sonicated for approximately 30 min.

Results and discussion

The stability of emulsions was initially investigated using DLS at water-to-oil ratios of 0.5, 1.0, and 20% (v/v), with TFT as the oil phase. The composition and concentration of the surfactants and metal salts were varied according to the parameters detailed in Scheme 1. The conventional surfactant CTAB was compared against a suite of three tetraalkylphosphonium ILs, all featuring the trihexyltetradecylphosphonium (P66614+) cation paired with either Br−, bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide (NTf2−), or tetrakis(pentafluorophenyl)borate (TB−) anions. Initial attempts using untreated glass vials resulted in rapid coalescence of water droplets along the inner glass walls. This was mitigated by treating the interior surface of the vials with chlorotrihexylsilane for ∼20 min. The silanization process effectively suppressed the aggregation of large water droplets.

Fig. 3A and B show representative DLS curves recorded at 5 min intervals over a 30 min period for emulsions that contain 5 mM P66614Br, LiCl, and ZnCl2, with and without the addition of CTAB. In some cases, a bimodal distribution of water droplet sizes was observed; for example, the ‘as-prepared’ red solid curve in Fig. 3B (without CTAB) exhibits two distinct peaks. This distribution shifted to a unimodal one after 5 min of evolution. Similarly, a bimodal size distribution is evident in the solid black trace of Fig. 3A at the 10 min mark.

The error bars represent the half-width-at-half-maximum (HWHM) of the corresponding DLS distributions. Interestingly, the use of P66614Br in the absence of CTAB, combined with 5 mM of ZnCl2, yielded the smallest water droplet diameters (see Fig. 3C, E, and F). Initially, the droplet diameter was measured to be 0.61 µm; however, it rapidly decreased to ∼0.10 µm and remained stable for the subsequent 30 min. Emulsions generated with P66614NTf2 and P66614TB showed diameters greater than ∼1 µm, typically in the range of 5 to 25 µm. The mean droplet diameters for the P66614Br, P66614NTf2, and P66614TB ILs (without CTAB, but with [ZnCl2](aq) = 5 mM) were 0.18, 8.54, and 15.0 µm, respectively. Furthermore, maintaining a constant concentration of P66614Br in TFT (5 mM) while increasing the [ZnCl2] from 0.25 mM to 0.5 and 5 mM resulted in mean diameters of 0.34, 0.59, and 0.18 µm, respectively.

Fig. 4A–C show the photographs of the shake-flask vials containing 20% (v/v) W/O suspensions, captured before and after a 30 min equilibration period for the three ILs in the presence of CTAB. For the P66614NTf2 and P66614TB systems, phase separation was observed, manifesting either as a distinct aqueous phase on top of TFT or a mixed emulsion layer above a largely separate TFT phase. These observations suggest that the DLS measurements may not have fully captured this macroscopic phase separation; consequently, the bimodal distributions recorded in Fig. 3A and B likely resulted from coalescence of water droplets in this way. Fig. 4E and F depict photographs of 1.0% W/O emulsions containing 5 mM ZnCl2, which exhibit similar stability trends. P66614Br demonstrated the smallest droplet sizes and the greatest stability even in the absence of CTAB. It was decided to abandon the use of CTAB going forward.

The hydrophilicity of the IL anions follows the trend: Br− > NTf2− > TB−.51–58 The intermediate hydrophilicity of NTf2− was demonstrated by Quinn et al.56 and Stockmann et al.57 through ion transfer studies at an immiscible water|DCE (w|DCE) interface. It is likely that NTf2− acts as an effective phase-transfer catalyst and hence promotes droplet agglomeration. In contrast, Br− and TB− exhibit high hydrophilicity and hydrophobicity,55,58 respectively, at w|TFT interfaces. It is therefore unlikely for TB− to transfer from the TFT into the aqueous (w) phase. Furthermore, TB− is believed to insignificantly contribute to the stabilization of the emulsion given its weak coordinating ability.

Next, the effects of the total water content and [ZnCl2] on emulsion stability were investigated. The conditions of 1% W/O and 5 mM ZnCl2 yielded the smallest emulsion, which remained stable over a 30 min observation period (Fig. 3D and E). Lower ZnCl2 concentrations (0.25 and 0.5 mM) also produced stable water droplets with diameters below 1 µm. Consequently, based on these DLS data, the optimal conditions for achieving the smallest diameter and greatest stability were found to be 5 mM of ZnCl2(aq), 5 mM LiCl(aq), and 5 mM of P66614Br with 1.0% (v/v) W/O.

The diffusion coefficient (Ddrop) of the water droplets was determined using the Stokes–Einstein equation,39,592where rdrop is the radius of the water droplet, kB is the Boltzmann constant (1.381 × 10−23 J K−1), T is the absolute temperature (298.15 K), and ηTFT is the viscosity of TFT (0.5505 mPa s = 0.5505 × 10−3 J s m−3).60 Using this equation, Ddrop was calculated to be 4.51, 0.093, and 0.053 µm2 s−1 for the Br−, NTf2−, and TB− emulsions, respectively. These correspond to the droplet diameter trends shown in Fig. 3C. Similarly, for the emulsions without CTAB (Fig. 3C), increasing the [ZnCl2] from 0.25 mM to 0.50 and 5.0 mM resulted in Ddrop values of 2.35, 1.35, and 4.51 µm2 s, respectively.

The emulsions analyzed in Fig. 3 were created using ultrapure water with a pH ≈ 5.5. In Fig. 5, the DLS data are shown for emulsions in which the pH was carefully adjusted to 5 or 6 using HCl or LiOH solutions with either ZnCl2 or MgCl2 as electrolyte. Both salts resulted in bimodal emulsion size distributions with a small peak at 0.060–0.100 µm and a large peak at 0.6–1.0 µm. Fig. 5E depicts the diameter trend for the larger peak over 30 min, demonstrating that both electrolytes facilitate good stability with the droplet diameter starting at 0.6 µm and growing to nearly 1.0 µm. Since the diameters for the ZnCl2 and MgCl2 emulsions are similar, both were used in the SEE studies described in the following section.

Large current spikes were observed starting at −0.5 V for electrolytes. These spikes resemble stochastic impact events,39,49,50 where a sharp increase in current occurs followed by a prompt return to the baseline.

These observations were further investigated using chronoamperometry at the three different UMEs, as shown in Fig. 8. Magnified current transients for the Pt UME experiments at pH 5 and 6 are plotted in Fig. 9. A potentiostatic pulse was applied at −1.3 V vs. Ag/AgCl-quasi-RE and sustained for 180 s. This potential was chosen to ensure complete reduction of Zn2+, particularly due to the use of a quasi-RE. Between successive pulses, the UME was taken out of the solution and polished to remove any electrodeposited material. In the meantime, the emulsion solution was sonicated to ensure homogeneity. The root-mean-square (RMS) noise for each UME was determined using potentiostatic pulses in a 5 mM MgCl2 aqueous solution at −1.3 V (data not shown), following the procedure described previously.50 The average noise for the CF, Pt, and steel UMEs was approximately 0.80, 8.0, and 2.8 pA, respectively.

The observed spikes show profiles similar to other SEE responses,27,37–40,49 typically characterized by a sharp onset current followed by an exponential decay back to the baseline. In certain cases, however, the transient profiles are more symmetric, appearing to follow an exponential onset and decay. Such features may result from the emulsion droplets nearing the UME surface and entering the electron tunneling region.65,66 Stochastic impacts were observed for both MgCl2 and ZnCl2 electrolytes suggesting that the HER occurs on the surfaces of carbon fiber, Pt, and steel wires. The baseline is more stable when ZnCl2 is used (comparing the black and the red traces in Fig. 8), and a higher incidence of transients with larger peak current intensities (ip) is observed for the ZnCl2 system for all three UMEs. Further qualitative analysis of the i–t curves reveals a significantly higher frequency of impacts at the Pt UME, which is likely due to Pt's greater electrocatalytic activity than that of carbon fiber or steel.

Two additional assumptions were made: (i) only eqn (7) is applicable for determining the number of electrons transferred (n) during the HER, and (ii) that all Zn2+ was fully reduced within the droplet during each collision event in the ZnCl2 SEE experiments. To calculate rdrop from the SEE data, the average charge transferred determined from the Gaussian fitting of the ZnCl2 and MgCl2 histograms (Fig. 10) were applied to the following equation,12Qdeposit = Qtotal − QHERwhere Qtotal and QHER represent the mean charge transferred during the ZnCl2 and MgCl2 experiments, respectively, and Qdeposit is the charge utilized to electrodeposit Zn2+. In this approach, the charge transferred during the ZnCl2 electrolyte experiments is a sum of the Zn electrodeposition and HER signals. For the MgCl2 series, only H2 evolution is assumed to take place. This value was then applied to eqn (9) to calculate rdrop.

The results for the carbon fiber UME show the closest agreement with the DLS measurements followed by the steel UME. In contrast, the Pt UME shows the most significant discrepancy relative to the DLS data, with an almost 3× and 2× differences at pH 5 and 6, respectively. This disparity is likely due to the superior electrocatalytic activity of Pt toward the HER.

The simultaneous occurrence of Zn electrodeposition and the HER, along with the subsequent challenge of deconvoluting their respective electrocatalytic signals, has long been recognized as a critical issue.13,17,18,68,69 This overlap represents a primary technical bottleneck which considerably hinder the development of Zn-ion batteries.68 The HER consumes charge that would otherwise be directed toward Zn deposition, thereby lowering the overall charge storage efficiency. Furthermore, the accumulation of released H2 in the battery can cause device swelling and pose a significant explosive hazard. Recently, Rana et al.68 used electrochemical mass spectroscopy in conjunction with traditional electrochemical measurements to decouple these two processes. Since H2 is the sole product of the HER, this approach is highly effective at quantifying the current consumed by the HER. Similarly, the HER also reduces the overall faradaic efficiency during the electrodeposition of other base metals, such as Fe, Co, and Ni. While simultaneous HER can be advantageous for modifying the morphology of the metal deposit to generate foam-like structures so that the electroactive surface area (ESA) can be increased,70 it still poses a challenge to other applications. Moreover, if the goal of material design is aimed at making compact layers, this feature is undesirable.

Isolating these mechanisms and decoupling the two reactions is of great interest for applications in metallurgy and coatings. Kovács et al.13 developed an analytical model to describe the faradaic contributions of the HER and electrodeposition, using rotating disc electrodes (RDE). Kovács and our approach herein ostensibly compare electrochemical signals from electrolyte solutions with and without metal ions. This method, however, can be somewhat problematic, as the base metals deposited may enhance the HER, either through increasing the ESA or forming nanomaterials on the electrode surface. In this study, this issue is mitigated by comparing the effects across different UME materials.

Fig. 11 presents the SEM and EDX spectroscopic mapping of the UME tips after immersion in the 5 mM ZnCl2, 1.0% W/O, and 5 mM P8888Br as the surfactant in TFT (see specifications in Scheme 1) along with the application of a 180 s pulse at −1.3 V. The CF UME shows significantly less Zn deposition on its surface compared to Pt and steel (Fig. 11G–I), with Zn NPs appearing scattered across both the electroactive and glass surfaces. For the Pt and steel UMEs, Zn deposits are clustered on the electroactive surface, with only some Zn NPs on the surrounding glass insulator. This observation suggests that CF is a poor surface for the growth of continuous metal layers, whereas epitaxial growth regimes are common on metal surfaces like Pt and steel, which these results agree well with. It is also possible that Zn NPs formed at the CF UME surface, detach, and then diffuse into solution, subsequently adhering to the surrounding glass surface as evidenced in Fig. 11G.

Conclusions

In this study, single-entity electrochemistry (SEE) was utilized to investigate the electrodeposition of Zn NPs on UMEs. The UMEs were fabricated from three distinct materials: carbon fiber, Pt, and steel. Zn2+ ion was introduced inside a water nanodroplet suspended in a TFT continuous phase, forming a water-in-oil emulsion. These droplets function as nanoreactors for the confined electrodeposition of Zn NPs onto the UME surface. The total charge transferred (Q) during each stochastic impact was determined through peak integration of the transient signals, which was used to calculate rdrop. It was assumed only the HER took place during the MgCl2 electrolyte experiments, whereas during ZnCl2 experiments both Zn electrodeposition and H2 evolution contribute to the total charge transfer (i.e., Qdeposit = Qtotal − QHER).

Single-entity derived radii were compared to data obtained from DLS measurements. The calculated droplet radii were found to vary between the different UME material interfaces. Pt gave the highest calculated rdrop, followed by steel, while carbon fiber yielded the smallest. Carbon fiber showed the closest agreement with DLS measurements. Pt's results are consistent with the high HER electrocatalytic activity of Pt. Meanwhile, these data suggest that steel acts as an effective HER catalyst that synergizes with electrodeposited Zn. These results provide a more accurate semi-quantitative estimate of the relative contributions of electrodeposition and the HER.

The observation that electrogenerated Zn NPs do not adhere to the surface of the carbon fiber UME suggests interesting future avenues for NP synthesis. While the conventional objective is to form NPs on a surface and grow new layers for either coating or electrocatalysis applications, this phenomenon could be exploited as a strategy for electrogenerating NPs in solution without the need for secondary reducing agents.
