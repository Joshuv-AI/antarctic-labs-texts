Exploring how older adults perceived the impact of infectious diseases and vaccines on ageing: a cross-sectional study in France
Authors: Alexandra Addario, Gaëtan Gavazzi, Thomas Célarier, Boris Chapoton, Nathalie Barth, Bienvenu Bongue, Elisabeth Botelho-Nevers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis cross-sectional study examined perceptions of health and ageing, and their influence on vaccine acceptance among individuals aged 65 and older living at home in the Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes region of France.MethodsAnonymous online questionnaires were distributed to users of CARSAT Rhône-Alpes (N1 = 50,000) and Auvergne (N2 = 9,597). The 62-question survey covered vaccination perceptions, the impact of the Covid-19 pandemic, health concerns, preventive measures, health information sources, and vaccination history. Vaccine hesitancy was assessed using the 5 C model, categorizing respondents into the group ‘Opposed’, ‘Hesitant’, and ‘Very Favourable’ to vaccination based on vaccination history and reasons for non-vaccination.ResultsOut of 3,331 respondents, 2,416 questionnaires were analysable. Most participants identified stroke (43.4%), cancer (20.4%), and dementia (18%) as primary health concerns. Infectious diseases ranked lowest, with pneumonia (1.24%), Covid-19 (0.89%), influenza (0.43%), and shingles (0.11%) infrequently cited. The Hesitant group more frequently questioned vaccination recommendations (82%) compared to the Very Favourable group (74%). Opposed participants significantly doubted vaccine efficacy and its role in healthy aging, differing from Hesitant and Very Favourable participants (p < 0.001). Perceptions of vulnerability to infection and beliefs about the seriousness of vaccine-preventable diseases significantly differed among groups (p < 0.001), with higher agreement in the Very Favourable group. Health-preservation behaviours, like regular medical check-ups and vaccination, were more common among the Very Favourable group, with no correlation found between diet, physical activity, and vaccination status.ConclusionsThe worry of infectious diseases is low compared to fear of disability. Differences in perceptions of infection vulnerability and vaccine efficacy highlight the need for targeted communication to enhance vaccine acceptance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07086-6.

Background

The ageing global population has prompted the prioritisation of policies to promote healthy ageing, addressing challenges such as equitable access to healthcare and resources [1]. The goal is to help older people age in good health and dignity while contributing to society. However, despite the crucial importance of vaccination in safeguarding older adults health, it is often only viewed as a preventive measure against infectious diseases by public health policies [2] and the World Health Organization [1].

Older adults are particularly vulnerable to infectious diseases due to age-related immune decline [3]. Infectious diseases pose a major burden on the older adults, causing severe health issues due to age-related immune system weakening [4]. These diseases can lead to hospitalizations, organ damage, or death [5, 6], and exacerbate other medical conditions, increasing the risk of functional decline and loss of independence [7–9]. Infections can also cause both infectious complications like respiratory diseases [10] and non-infectious complications such as myocardial infarction [11–14]. Moreover, the financial burden of these diseases is substantial for both individuals and healthcare systems [15, 16]. Infections can significantly compromise physical, cognitive, and social well-being, thereby impeding the process of ageing well.

Vaccination programs are essential for promoting healthy ageing. Vaccines against influenza, COVID-19, pneumococcal infections, and shingles can reduce illness risk and severe complications among older adults. Vaccination is crucial for mitigating the severity and incidence of infections, indirectly protecting against non-infectious complications like cardiovascular events and dementia [17, 18]. For example, influenza vaccination prevents severe complications like pneumonia and respiratory failure [19], COVID-19 vaccination reduces severe illness and hospitalization [20], pneumococcal vaccines protect against meningitis [21], and shingles vaccination reduces the risk of post-herpetic neuralgia [22].

Despite these benefits, vaccination coverage among older adults remains low. Influenza Vaccine Coverage (VC) is below World Health Organization’s (WHO) 75% target for older adults [23], with 56.2% in France [24]. Pneumococcal vaccination rate are approximately 4.8% among seniors [25], while shingles vaccination is under 10%, in France [26]. Despite the wider administration of the COVID-19 vaccination due to the pandemic, significant hesitancy remain among the older adults population. Vaccine hesitancy (VH) defined by the WHO as the delay in accepting or refusing immunisations despite the availability of vaccination services [27], is has a negative impact on VC and was identified as a top global health threats [28]. In France, VH affects 35% of the older adults [29]. Unlike opposed vaccine movements, VH is not necessarily based on a structured opposition to vaccination; it is a spectrum ranging from complete refusal to complete acceptance of vaccines. The Health Belief Model (HBM), which emphasizes perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits and perceived barriers, has been widely used to understand vaccination behaviour among older adults [30]. Our study builds on this theoretical framework by examining how older adults perceive their vulnerability to infectious diseases in relation to their broader concerns about healthy ageing.”

This study examines the perceptions of older adults regarding vaccination, worries about their health, and successful ageing [31]. The study analyses representations of vaccination in relation to the process of healthy ageing, explores their perceptions of vaccination in connection with health-preserving actions, and investigates strategies to leverage the fears of older adults to encourage them to adopt vaccination.

Methods

Design and sample

A cross-sectional study was conducted among individuals aged over 65 and residing at home in the Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes region of France. An online, anonymised questionnaire was distributed via email addresses to the users of the Caisse d’Assurance Retraite et de Santé Au Travail (retirement and occupational health insurance funds, CARSAT) Rhône-Alpes (N1 = 50,000) on 21 September 2023, and to users in selected areas of CARSAT Auvergne (N2 = 9,597) on 16 October 2023. Due to technical constraints, CARSAT Auvergne could not distribute the questionnaire to all users; therefore, areas were purposively selected to ensure balanced representation of region. The initial contact was made via email with a reminder sent 15 days later.

Survey questionnaire

A questionnaire was developed to explore perceptions of vaccination, fears, sources of information, access capabilities, the validated 5 C questionnaire [32], vaccination history, and sociodemographic data among these older people. These questions were determined through the collaboration of university researchers. The digital questionnaire was accessible on the open-source survey software tool LimeSurvey (https://gerontopoleaura.limesurvey.net/158356?lang=fr). The anonymous questionnaire comprised 62 questions, encompassing demographic information. It included relevant inquiries regarding perceptions of vaccination and the impact of the Covid-19 pandemic on these perceptions. Additionally, it addressed health-related worries, preventive actions taken, and scenarios to assess various means of prevention. Participants were also asked about their sources of health information and their vaccination history. VH was evaluated utilising the 5 C model [32], with an additional dimensions (social conformism) from the extended 7 C model, resulting in six scores : Confidence, Complacency, Constraints, Calculation, Collective Responsibility, and Social Conformism. The higher the score, the greater the support for vaccination. The full 7C model was not used to maintain a reasonable questionnaire length for this elderly population. Responses were primarily recorded using a 5-point Likert scale or with options such as yes/no/don’t know. Participants were also requested to order 11 distinct illnesses based on the level of worry they evoke, from the most frightening to the least. Subsequently, a ranking was generated their reactions presented with prevention campaigns informing them about the potential benefits of vaccination in reducing the risk of specific health issues, such as stroke, heart attack, dementia, and falls with fractures.

This study was approved by an ethics committee of the Saint-Etienne University Hospital, “Commission recherche de Terre d’Ethique” (Terre d’Ethique no. : IRBN012022/CHUSTE). The study protocol conformed to the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

The respondents were divided into three groups of respondents: “Opposed”, “Hesitant” and “Very Favourable” according to their vaccination behaviour regarding Tetanus, Diphtheria, Poliomyelitis (TDP), influenza, Covid19, Pneumococcal, herpes zoster and reasons for missing certain vaccinations (see Table 5 in Supplementary Data). Given the lack of awareness surrounding the efficacy and necessity of pneumococcal and herpes zoster vaccinations, it was deemed that the absence of these vaccinations due to the general practitioner’s lack of knowledge or advice was not indicative of a Hesitant behaviour. Pearson’s Chi-squared tests, Fisher’s exact test or Kruskal-Wallis rank sum were performed to explore differences in participant responses among the Opposed, Hesitant and Very Favourable group. P-values of 0.05 or less were considered significant. To perform the correlation coefficient calculations, we initially transformed the qualitative variables into ordinal qualitative variables. Correlations were calculated using appropriate methods, such as Spearman’s correlation coefficient. P-values of 0.01 or less were considered significant and are shown in the figures. Participants’ questionnaires with missing values for one or more study variables were excluded only in the analyses involving those variables. Multinomial univariate logistic regressions were first performed in order to examine each variable individually without adjusting for the effects of others. To achieve this, the Likert scale variables were dichotomised into “Disagreement” (including responses from “Strongly Disagree” to “Neutral”) and “Agreement” (including responses from “Agree” to “Strongly Agree”). Odds ratios (OR) and their respective 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated. P-values of 0.05 or less were considered significant.

All statistics were two-tailed. Analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.2.

Results

A total of 3,331 individuals responded to the questionnaire (Fig. 1). Of these, 2,416 individuals provided comprehensive responses regarding their vaccine history, allowing for analysis. A complete response was available for 2,383/3,331 (71.5%) of them. Table 6 in Supplementary Data presents the basic demographic information of the respondents according to their vaccine groups (Opposed, Hesitant and Very Favourable). The respondents were mainly women (56.3%). The mean age of respondents was 70 years (quartiles: 67.0, 74.0). In terms of educational attainment 38.7% (n = 921) had completed high school. All respondents were retired, 38.9% (n = 926) held executives and higher intellectual professions, while 32.4% (n = 771) were employees. Among the participants, 21.1% (n = 703) were employed in healthcare or teaching. The distribution of residences was balanced between rural (49.6%) and urban (50.4%) areas. There were significant differences in the characteristics of population, including age, gender, departments of residence, educational level, and field of work between the attitudes towards vaccines (all p < 0.05). Overall vaccination coverage rates were as follows: TDP 91.28%, influenza 74.85%, COVID-19 90.17%, Pertussis 48.68%, Pneumococcal 23.10% and shingles 3.59% (Table 1). Educational level and socio-professional category were not significantly associated with vaccination attitudes (Supplementary Table 7).

Attitudes and perceptions regarding vaccination, health, and aging by vaccination attitude

Worries regarding health and ageing of persons over 65 years of age

Concerns of respondents related to ageing are presented in Table 2. Stroke, cancer and dementia are the 3 most frequent concern reported among respondents, with each respectively reported as the primary worry in 43.4%, 20.4% and 18%. In contrast, preventable vaccine infections were ranked at the end of the classification, as shown in Table 2. This classification was not significantly different among the three groups of vaccine attitude (Tables 8 and Table 10 in Supplementary Data). When queried about their concerns regarding pathological ageing and disability, respondents indicated that their primary concerns were the development of Alzheimer’s-type disorders (33.2%), entering a long-term care facility (21.9%), and the need for assistance with daily activities (20.4%) (Table 3). Representations of vaccination and healthy ageing

Table 1 outlines vaccination-related attitudes and behaviours for all participants and across Opposed, Hesitant, and Very Favourable groups. Of note is the observation that the Hesitant group (82%) more frequently questioned vaccination recommendations compared to the Very Favourable group (74%). A significant proportion of respondents in the Very Favourable group (96%) agreed with the statement “vaccination protects me against infectious diseases,” while a smaller proportion of respondents in the Hesitant group (85%) and the Opposed group (21%) expressed this view (p < 0.001). With regard to the proposition that vaccination facilitates healthy ageing, 78% of respondents in the Very Favourable group expressed agreement, 56% of those in the group that was Hesitant group and 3% of those in the Opposed group (p < 0.001). Furthermore, the data demonstrate that 6.1% of the Opposed group, 55% of the Hesitant group, and 74% of the Very Favourable group concur with the perception of vulnerability to infection associated with their pathologies (p < 0.001). In contrast, respectively, 9.1%, 37% and 54% agreed with the perception of vulnerability to infection linked to age (p < 0.001).

Table 4 indicates that the belief in the protective role of vaccination against infectious diseases is positively associated with being classified as Very Favourable (OR = 46.8, 95% CI [21.2, 104]) and, to a lesser extent, with being classified as Hesitant (OR = 20.2, 95% CI [12.0, 34.1]). Similarly, belief in the contribution of vaccination to healthy ageing is positively associated with being classified as Very Favourable (OR = 109, 95%CI [48.3, 244]) and, to a lesser extent, Hesitant (OR = 30.1, 95%CI [15.9, 56.9]). In addition, perceived vulnerability due to age is associated with being classified as Very Favourable (OR = 14.9, 95%CI [8.19, 27.2]) and, to a lesser extent, as Hesitant (OR = 5.83, 95%CI [3.48, 9.78]). Furthermore, the perception of vulnerability to disease is associated with being classified as Very Favourable (OR = 10.3 95%CI [5.78, 18.4]) and, to a lesser extent, Hesitant (OR = 4.83 95%CI [2.91, 7.99]). Conversely, the perception of vaccine-preventable diseases as insignificant was inversely associated with both Very Favourable (OR = 0.02, 95% CI [0.01, 0.05]) and Hesitant (OR = 0.08, 95% CI [0.04, 0.15]) attitudes.

Actions implemented to ensure healthy-ageing

Table 1 reveals notable differences among the three groups regarding health-preserving behaviours, including healthy eating, vaccination, and regular medical check-ups (p < 0.001). The Very Favourable group exhibited higher engagement in these actions compared to the Hesitant group. A multinomial univariate logistic model (Table 4) indicates that the perception of vaccination as a healthy action is positively associated with being classified as Very Favourable (OR = 471, 95% CI: 109; 2,041), and with being Hesitant (OR = 73.9, 95%CI: 18.0, 303). The perception of regular medical follow-up as a healthy practice is positively associated with being classified as Very Favourable (OR = 14.6 95%CI: 7.97, 26.9) and, as Hesitant (OR = 7.42 95% CI: 4.49, 12.3).

Discussion

In our study, we observed that older individuals expressed significant concerns regarding the decline of their physical and cognitive abilities, while showing very little apprehension towards infectious diseases. This low concern about infectious diseases is presented among all respondents, whatever their attitude regarding vaccines. Furthermore, our findings indicated a correlation between the acceptance of vaccination and the perception of its efficacy in combating infectious diseases. However, the perception of ageing and how vaccination may promote healthy-ageing is significantly less frequent among Hesitant vaccine group. Concerning preventive behaviours, dietary and exercise habits are generally accepted by all senior, whereas vaccination and regular medical check-ups are associated with a favourable vaccine attitude. Finally, our findings suggest that communicating the benefits of vaccination for maintaining physical and cognitive capacities could serve as an effective motivator for vaccination among older adults.

Impact of perception of disease vulnerability on vaccination acceptance

In our study, we observed prevalent worries among older adults concerning cardiovascular accidents, cancer, and dementia. These age-related diseases significantly impact morbidity and mortality [31]. Cardiovascular events, such as strokes and heart attacks, are leading causes of disability and death in older adults [33]. Cancer incidence increases with age, exposing older adults to higher risks of various malignancies [34]. Additionally, dementia, including Alzheimer’s disease, significantly affects cognitive function and autonomy [35]. Despite the impact of chronic conditions, infectious diseases critically affect older adults due to compromised immune systems [36], leading to increased hospitalisations and associated risks such as hospital-acquired infections, reduced mobility, and psychological strain from extended hospital stays [36]. Each episode of hospitalisation can result in a decline in physical [37] and cognitive functions [38]. Infections also increase the risk of cardiovascular events such as strokes [17] and falls [39], impairing quality of life and independence. Consequently, infections can significantly impair the quality of life of older adults. Our results demonstrated that older adults often underestimate the burden of infectious diseases. This low concern may reflect a generational perspective, as today’s older adults witnessed the success of antibiotics and vaccines in controlling once deadly infectious diseases, shifting health concerns toward chronic conditions [40, 41]. Older adults may also perceive infections as benign conditions primarily affecting younger population [42]. These hypotheses warrant further exploration, particularly through cross-cultural comparative studies. Moreover, a substantial proportion of Very Favourable respondents had questioned vaccine effectiveness, while some Opposed acknowledged vaccination’s protective role. This question was contextualised in the COVID-19 pandemic setting which may partly explain these finding. This paradox likely reflects differing attitudes between general vaccination acceptance and specific vaccines, with COVID-19 vaccination crystallizing rejection more than traditional vaccine like influenza and TDP [43]. Furthermore, Opposed individuals may recognize vaccination efficacy for others while not feeling personally concerned, perceiving themselves as healthy enough not to require vaccination [44].

Furthermore, older adults worry about losing their autonomy, particularly regarding physical and cognitive abilities [45]. Our findings indicate a gradient in the perception of vaccination as a strategy for healthy ageing, with individuals who are Very Favourable more likely to view vaccination as beneficial for ageing compared to those who are Hesitant. Nevertheless, vaccination significantly reduces the risk of cardiovascular accidents [17, 46], which is particularly beneficial for older adults. Moreover, the participants in our study expressed their worry of dementia or vaccination has been associated with a reduced risk of dementia [47], indicating its potential role in preserving cognitive function. Thus, vaccination promotes healthy aging by preventing infectious diseases and preserving physical and cognitive capabilities crucial for independence.

Participants who perceived themselves as vulnerable due to their age or health conditions were more inclined to support vaccination, aligning with research by Gidengil et al. [48]. High-risk adults’ perceptions of severe infectious diseases positively influence their vaccination behaviours. Older adults perceiving higher risks often see vaccination as crucial, increasing urgency and adherence to schedules [49]. The perception of the risk of infectious diseases, shaped by personal experiences, and communication from healthcare providers, influences vaccination intention.

Interactions between preventive health behaviours and vaccination attitudes

The present study highlights the importance of individual perceptions of vaccination in maintaining health. The results of the analysis reveal positive associations between the perception of vaccination and regular medical consultations as a means of preserving health, as well as 5 C scores, indicating that individuals who were favourable to a medicalised approach to preserve their health were also more inclined to adopt a on favourable attitude towards vaccination. This finding is consistent with previous studies that have also highlighted the importance of medical consultations in promoting vaccination [50]. Furthermore, our findings indicate that the perception of physical activity and dietary hygiene as means of preserving health is not associated with uptake of different vaccinations. Previous studies have analysed this association and their results are contradictory, particularly for physical activity [51–54].

Moreover, with regard to regular medical consultations, there appears to be a distinction between two categories: those who engage in all preventive measures with a medicalised approach and those who prioritise behaviours such as diet and exercise but may be less inclined towards vaccination, representing a non-medicalised approach. It is also important to consider the role of alternative medicines, such as homeopathy or chiropractic, is also crucial. These practices are often favoured by individuals with a holistic view of health, yet they may also harbour mistrust towards conventional medical interventions, including vaccination [55]. While promoting overall health can foster acceptance of vaccination, it is vital to recognise and understand the factors contributing to vaccine reluctance, including individual preferences for alternative medicine.

Key Strengths and Limitations

We cannot eliminate selection bias in this study, as the specific sample from the Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes region may limit generalisability. However, this region represents over 12% of the French population and shares similar demographic characteristics with national data (64). Our study reveals a high VC rate but also significant hesitancy. Self-reported vaccination may introduce response bias. The larger proportion of ‘Hesitant’ IPs compared to previous studies likely reflects stricter criteria for influenza (annual) and Covid-19 (at least one booster) vaccinations, aligning with vaccine hesitancy definitions (65) and current recommendations (66). Conducted post-pandemic, when vaccine reluctance was high (67), it is plausible hesitancy has increased, as seen in the declining influenza VC rate among over-65s and low Covid-19 booster uptake (68). Lastly, using an online questionnaire excludes those less familiar with digital tools, potentially limiting the sample. The response rate (5.5%) was low but consistent with web-based surveys in older populations, likely due to digital divide, email fatigue, and questionnaire length [56]. The online distribution method may have introduced selection bias, overrepresenting digitally literate, younger (mean age 70 years), and higher-educated individuals, thus limiting generalizability to the oldest-old (≥ 80 years) and lower-educated populations. Future research should include stratified analyses by age groups to determine whether perceptions differ between young-old (65–74 years) and oldest-old (≥ 75 years) populations.

Conclusion

This study highlights the significance of addressing perceptions of vulnerability among older adults to enhance vaccination acceptance. It is crucial to contextualize the risk and impact of infectious diseases in comparison to other conditions with a relatively lower impact at this age. Communicating the protective role of vaccines on non-infectious events, which are primary concerns for older adults, is equally important. Perceptions of vulnerability to health risks associated with aging and existing health conditions are key determinants of vaccination acceptance. Healthcare providers and public health authorities should consider these perceptions when designing communication strategies and interventions for older adults. Future research should investigate behaviours and the impact of adapted communication, evaluating longitudinal studies to track changes over time, and include stratified analyses by age groups to determine whether perceptions differ between young-old (65–74 years) and oldest-old (≥ 75 years) populations. Comparative studies across different regions can also provide valuable insights. By addressing concerns related to vulnerability and emphasizing the importance of vaccination for non-infectious disease prevention, we can promote greater acceptance and uptake of vaccines among older adults, ultimately reducing the burden of vaccine-preventable diseases in this population. Future research should include stratified analyses by age groups to determine whether perceptions differ between young-old (65–74 years) and oldest-old (≥ 75 years) populations.
