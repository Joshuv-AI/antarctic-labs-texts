Dark triad personality traits and perfectionism in Peruvian university students: a network analysis
Authors: Cristian Ramos-Vera, Dennis Calle, Royer Olivera-Cercado, Susan M. Oblitas-Guerrero, Giovanna Larraín Távara, Jacksaint Saintila
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough studies on the dark triad and perfectionism have been conducted in different regions, most rely on correlational or regression approaches and rarely explore these traits using psychological network analysis. Furthermore, few investigations have examined the role of sociodemographic variables, such as gender and age, in shaping these associations.ObjectiveIn this study, the relationship between the Dark Triad and perfectionism in Peruvian university students was analyzed by means of psychological network analysis including variables such as gender and age.MethodsThe sample consisted of 515 university students between 20 and 30 years of age (56.7% male). In this study, the Dark Triad Short Scale and the Multidimensional Brief Perfectionism Scale were used.ResultsThe results of the study revealed a positive association between Machiavellianism and perfectionistic strivings and concerns. In addition, psychopathy was found to be associated with perfectionistic concerns, whereas narcissism showed a relationship with perfectionistic strivings. Likewise, a stronger association was observed between Machiavellianism and men, while perfectionistic strivings were directly correlated with age. Finally, psychopathy was found to be the most central dimension in the network, showing a strong interconnection with narcissism and perfectionistic concerns.ConclusionThese results provide further insight into the complex interaction between aversive traits and goal-oriented traits, both adaptive and maladaptive, in university students.

Introduction

Dark triad traits

In recent decades, research has been conducted on taxonomic models of personality that go beyond traditional approaches, such as the Big Five model, and focus on more detrimental or aversive personality dimensions toward others. The dark triad, originally proposed by Paulhus and Williams [1], refers to three aversive personality traits: Machiavellianism, subclinical psychopathy, and subclinical narcissism. In recent years, these traits have been examined as patterns of antagonistic behavior in university students [2]. Furthermore, it has been suggested that these traits can have a significant impact on an individual’s career success or detriment [3]. In addition, these tendencies have also been found to be associated with various negative consequences, such as academic fraud, cyberbullying, and superficial interpersonal relationships with low empathy [4–6].

Machiavellian individuals are characterized by having an opportunistic and manipulative social orientation, as well as exhibiting strategic and amoral behavior for personal gain [7]. On the other hand, those individuals who present high levels of psychopathy are characterized by lack of self-control, insensitivity, impulsivity, and superficial and instrumental relationships [8]. As for grandiose narcissists, they are distinguished by their egocentric and superiority attitudes, their constant search for attention, and their belief of having more rights than others [9].

Perfectionism

In addition to the Dark Triad, there are other personality traits that can be adaptive or maladaptive, such as perfectionism, which has been extensively studied in various age groups, including university students [10]. This variable usually measures two dimensions: perfectionistic strivings (PS) and perfectionistic concerns (PC). PS involves setting high personal standards and striving to achieve them, motivated by achievement and excellence. On the other hand, PC are characterized by intense self-criticism and excessive preoccupation with negative evaluations from others when one’s own expectations are not met [11]. Perfectionism characterized by high standards can motivate students to strive for and achieve superior performance (Stoeber et al., 2006). However, other research suggests that students with perfectionistic concerns may experience symptoms of stress, anxiety, and depression, which limits their ability to enjoy university life and participate fully in activities [11–13].

Dark triad traits and perfectionism

Previous research studies have examined the relationships between Dark Triad traits and perfectionism. Significant associations have been identified between Machiavellianism and both types of perfectionism [14–16]. In the university setting, these associations can lead those individuals seeking to achieve academic and professional excellence to use unethical strategies to achieve their goals, such as cheating on exams and manipulating their peers [17]. In addition, it has been observed that those individuals with a higher degree of these personality traits may assume leadership roles in group projects with the goal of taking advantage of or sabotaging the work of other group members, all to highlight their own importance [18]. On the other hand, some traits of demanding perfectionism may also have a negative impact on certain Machiavellian students, who may be very self-critical when they fail to achieve an expected social reputation [16].

In addition, it has been observed in previous research that individuals with high levels of perfectionism and psychopathy tend to exhibit unethical and dishonest behaviors to achieve their goals. In the academic context, it has been reported that these individuals may show a rebellious and uninhibited attitude by plagiarizing or taking advantage of the work of others to succeed in their courses and may even harass others to achieve their goals without showing any remorse [19]. However, research has been found that some university students with psychopathic tendencies may report a feeling that they do not deserve respect and have a negative self-image if they do not achieve positive results in their academic activities [15]. This suggests that these individuals are also emotionally influenced by the consequences of not achieving their desires and may act impulsively to gain benefits at any cost in their interpersonal relationships and academic performance [15].

Significant evidence has been found for a positive relationship between perfectionistic striving and grandiose narcissism. A meta-analysis that examined 33 studies supports this association [20]. People with narcissistic traits may show an obsession with receiving admiration and recognition from others to feel special, which can lead them to strive for perfection in their work and personal achievements [21]. This dynamic drives them to set goals that surpass those of others and to impose high academic and social demands to gain recognition and admiration. The obsessive pursuit of a successful image by narcissistic individuals allows them to satisfy their need for attention and provides them with justification for imposing their desires on others.

The DSM-5’s alternative model for personality disorders (AMPD) includes rigid perfectionism as a maladaptive trait relevant to obsessive-compulsive personality disorder (OCPD). However, some researchers [22] have proposed that perfectionistic traits should also be considered when assessing narcissistic personality disorder (NPD). A study by Hemmati et al. [23] investigated this proposal, finding that perfectionistic traits significantly improved the construct validity of OCPD but not NPD. In other words, while perfectionism is a helpful indicator in diagnosing OCPD, it does not appear to be a significant factor in diagnosing NPD.

Sociodemographic variables, dark triad traits, and perfectionism

On the other hand, several studies have investigated the relevance of sociodemographic variables, such as gender, in relation to dark traits and perfectionism. In general, it has been observed that men tend to exhibit significantly higher levels of narcissism, machiavellianism, and psychopathy compared to women [24–26]. Although correlations have been found between dark traits and sex, it is important to note that these correlations often vary in magnitude. This may be attributed to a combination of biological, cultural, and social role factors underlying the manifestation of these traits [24, 25]. In relation to perfectionism, there are studies that suggest a higher prevalence of perfectionistic dimensions in women, although research has also been reported in which no significant sex differences were found [27–29]. These contradictory results highlight the need to address this research gap and conduct more studies that examine the relationship between perfectionism and gender more broadly and accurately [28].

Previous studies indicate that young adults tend to show higher levels of Machiavellianism and psychopathy traits compared to other ages [30–32]. These findings suggest a possible transition to more mature behaviors as people age [31, 32]. Likewise, it has been observed that the relationship between perfectionism and age is also important. This is consistent with the results of a meta-analysis that analyzed 164 studies, which found an increased frequency of perfectionistic striving and preoccupation in university students in recent decades. These findings suggest that sociocultural factors, as well as the increased use of social networks to compare oneself with others, could be influencing this trend [10].

In addition, it is relevant to address how the variables associated with individual differences may be interrelated. Personality variables can be integrated into taxonomic models that represent different levels or hierarchies of dimensions, which encompass more specific components or facets [33]. These more specific dimensions, which are usually one-dimensional, can be grouped according to similar attributes, without the need to follow a rigid organization like network systems [34]. To achieve more coherent or concise associations between different personality dimensions, it is recommended to assess those that represent common characteristics, including behavioral aspects. An example of an association between dark triad traits and perfectionism is the shared tendency of people who possess these traits to have a strong need to control others and to impose high standards on both them and others. Both groups tend to be demanding and seek to get what they want through manipulation and the setting of strict standards. Therefore, due to similarities in attributed behaviors, these traits can coexist in the same system of personality variables [35].

Under this perspective, it is foreseeable that these traits reinforce each other in a more complex way, suggesting that their study would be more appropriate using network analysis. In this methodological approach, psychological variables, including personality variables, are represented by circular nodes, and connected by edges representing associations, considering the influence of other variables [36, 37]. This methodology allows us to explore the structure and relationships between personality traits from a different perspective than traditional models based on latent variables [38]. Network analysis has the advantage of being able to examine mixed variables, which can be of different types (continuous, categorical, nominal, among others). This allows the integration of connections between personality traits with relevant sociodemographic variables, such as age, gender, socioeconomic status, and others. The objective is to verify whether these influences condition any part of the systematic relationships present in the personality trait network. Furthermore, an additional advantage of this methodology is the ability to identify the most relevant dimensions that promote interconnection between all components of a network. These dimensions act as central points or key nodes that sustain associations and contribute to the configuration of a more stable network structure [39, 40].

Purpose of the study

Previous research has explored the link between the dark triad of personality traits and perfectionism, yet these studies have not applied network analysis to dissect these associations. Notably, prior investigations have primarily focused on the dark triad’s relationship with other aversive or stress-related personality traits [41, 42] and examined how perfectionism interacts with broader personality models like the Big Five or obsessive traits [13]. However, these studies were limited to English-speaking populations. This research gap underscores the critical need for a study focused on diverse cultural contexts, particularly in South America. In the Peruvian context, specific sociocultural characteristics—such as a predominantly collectivist orientation in family and community life, strong hierarchical structures in academic and social environments, and socioeconomic disparities that heighten competition for educational and professional opportunities—may influence how Dark Triad traits and perfectionism manifest. For example, cultural norms that value social harmony could moderate the overt display of antagonistic behaviors, while high academic pressure and the pursuit of upward mobility may intensify perfectionistic tendencies and strategic behaviors linked to Machiavellianism or narcissism.

In addition, the present study is grounded in a conceptual framework that integrates the motivational and self-regulatory mechanisms shared by Dark Triad traits and perfectionism. This model proposes that individuals high in antagonistic traits (Machiavellianism, psychopathy, narcissism) may channel their need for control, admiration, or immediate reward through perfectionistic strivings or concerns, with sociodemographic factors such as age and gender acting as potential moderators. By applying network analysis, we aim not only to map these associations but also to test whether the observed links align with this theoretically driven model. Methods

Participants

The study was conducted using a cross-sectional, correlational design with a quantitative approach.

During the initial data collection phase, 522 individuals participated. Participants were excluded if they had missing responses on more than 10% of the total items across the administered instruments or if they failed to complete any of the key variables (i.e., total scores on the Dark Triad Short Scale or the Multidimensional Brief Perfectionism Scale). This threshold was established in accordance with methodological recommendations in psychological research to minimize bias and maintain adequate statistical power. After applying these exclusion criteria, the final sample comprised 515 Peruvian university students, primarily from Lima (52.62%) and Chiclayo (47.38%). Within the sample, 56.70% were male and 43.30% were female, with ages ranging from 20 to 30 years (M = 24.2, SD = 2.30). The sampling approach was non-probability convenience sampling, selecting participants who were most accessible and who met the selection criteria: being over 18 years of age, enrolled as university students, holding Peruvian nationality, and having no sensory difficulties that could interfere with completing the online form. For the retained cases, any remaining missing values (< 2% per variable) were addressed using mean imputation at the item level within each scale, as the data were assumed to be missing completely at random (MCAR) and the low proportion of missingness allowed for a parsimonious imputation approach.

Instruments

Short Dark Triad Scale (SD3), Peruvian version of the Short Dark Triad Scale [43, 44] was used to measure dark personality traits, which has 20 items and 3 dimensions: Machiavellianism, with 7 dimensions (“I avoid direct conflict with others because they can be useful to me in the future”), narcissism, with 5 dimensions (“Many group activities tend to be boring without me”), and psychopathy, with 8 dimensions (“It is true that I can be cruel to others”). The items are measured on a 5-point Likert-type scale ranging from “strongly disagree (1)” to “strongly agree (5)”. The sum of the total score ranging from 20 points to 100 indicates the level of the presence of the Dark Triad trait, where a higher score indicates a higher level of this trait. In the present research, internal structure validity analyses were carried out by means of confirmatory factor analysis. Adequate values were obtained in different indexes, such as Chi-Square to Degrees of Freedom Ratio” (χ²/df) = 2.09, Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) =.95, Comparative Fit Index (CFI) =.96, Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) =.06, and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) =.05. In addition, reliability analyses were performed for the three dimensions, using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient. The results indicated that the coefficients were ordinal α =.76 for Machiavellianism, while for Psychopathy and Narcissism they were ordinal α =.67.

Frost Multidimensional Perfectionism Scale – Brief (F-MPS; Carrasco et al., 2010). This is the Spanish version which was adapted from the abbreviated version proposed by Burgess et al. (2016; F-MPS-Brief). The instrument used in this study consists of 8 items measuring two dimensions of perfectionism: perfectionistic concerns and perfectionistic strivings. An example of an item of perfectionistic concerns is “If I fail at work, at university or at home I am a failure as a person” (ordinal α = 0.63), while an example of an item of perfectionistic strivings is “I set excessively high goals for myself” (ordinal α = 0.60). The response to each item is measured on a five-option Likert-type scale, from “strongly disagree (1)” to “strongly agree (5)”, in which the level of perfectionism is calculated by adding the total score. The higher the score, the greater the presence of perfectionism, in a range of scores for the total scale from 8 points to 40 points. In this study, the internal structure analysis had adequate fit indices: χ²/df = 1.20, Normed Fit Index (NFI) = 0.92, TLI = 0.93, CFI = 0.93, Incremental Fit Index (IFI) = 0.93, SRMR = 0.060, and RMSEA = 0.071. It also presents a Cronbach’s α = 0.75.

Procedure

This study was conducted with the approval of the Research Ethics Committee of the Idema Institute. For data collection, an online questionnaire implemented through the Google Forms® application was used. The questionnaire was shared with university students using virtual platforms such as Facebook, WhatsApp, and through email. This recruitment strategy allowed reaching a diverse sample of participants, thus broadening the representativeness of the sample in terms of socio-educational and demographic characteristics. This data collection approach is widely used in psychological research and follows the Internet-Based Methodology, which gives it validity and reliability. In addition, this procedure allows for the effective application of relevant ethical recommendations, guaranteeing the protection of the participants and the integrity of the data collected [45]. It was decided to use this method of data collection due to the persistence of the State of Emergency in Peru because of the COVID-19 pandemic. Data collection was conducted over a three-month period from May to July 2022. This situation made it difficult to carry out the data collection in person, since there were restrictions on travel and the safety and well-being of the participants, and the research team had to be prioritized.

Before participants began answering the questionnaire, they were given clear and detailed information about the voluntary nature of their collaboration. They were assured that their participation was completely voluntary and that they could withdraw at any time without consequence. In addition, they were guaranteed confidentiality and anonymity of their answers and results. All procedures carried out in this study were in accordance with the ethical standards of the American Psychological Association and its equivalents [46].

Data analysis

The statistical programs JASP version 0.16.4 and Jamovi version 2.3.21 were used to perform the respective analyses. These tools provide statistical functionalities and allow various analyses to be carried out, such as descriptive data of mean, standard deviation, skewness, kurtosis, and internal consistency. The use of subscales was taken into account in the network analysis integrated by nodes that refer to a psychological nature related to personality traits. The present network approach is based on Gaussian graphical models, specifically the Mixed Graphical Model (mgm) [47], which is recommended for associations between continuous and nominal variables [37]. This analysis considered the best-fit parameter of 0.25 as the default parameter according to the Ebic Lasso estimator that provides insight into a stable network whose connections represent regularized partial correlations [36, 48]. In addition, the expected influence centrality index was used to identify the variables that influence direct and indirect network connections. Finally, the accuracy and robustness of the edge weights [49] were evaluated through running 3000 bootstrap samples.

Network analysis reveals the structure of connections by identifying central variables, clusters, and the strength and direction of links. This approach is particularly useful for exploring complex systems with many interacting variables, highlighting emergent patterns and key drivers that traditional methods might overlook. Furthermore, network analysis can uncover indirect connections and cascading effects, illustrating how changes in one variable can influence others through a series of relationships. While not inherently causal, it offers a more data-driven and exploratory approach compared to SEM, which often relies on predefined models. Finally, the visual representation of network graphs serves as a powerful tool for communicating complex findings and generating new hypotheses. In essence, if the research emphasizes exploring the structure and interconnectedness of relationships rather than simply quantifying individual correlations or testing predefined causal models, network analysis would be the more appropriate choice.

Discussion

In this study, the relationship between dark triad personality traits and perfectionism was investigated in a sample of university students, with a particular focus on how sex and age can influence these dynamics. The discussion of results is framed within the proposed conceptual model, which predicts that antagonistic traits may reinforce perfectionistic tendencies through motivational mechanisms (need for control, search for admiration, impulsive goal pursuit), and that these patterns may vary according to sociodemographic moderators.

It has been observed that university students often show tendencies towards unethical academic behaviors and interpersonal relationships based on opportunism. These behaviors include demands for personal gain, a low capacity for empathy towards others, and the use of deceptive tactics to stand out [2, 16]. On the other hand, there are students who are characterized by behaviors based on high demands and personal standards. These individuals strive to achieve high levels of academic and personal performance, which provides them with a sense of control and self-worth [16]. However, when they fail to meet their own expectations, these high standards can also become a source of stress and trigger emotional symptoms. In these cases, it is common for these students to severely criticize themselves and experience feelings of frustration, anxiety, or low self-esteem [12]. Based on these complex personality indicators, this psychological network analysis was carried out in a sample of Peruvian university students, which represents a novel perspective in the field of psychology.

Among the first results obtained in the network analysis, with respect to Dark Triad traits and perfectionism, a positive relationship was observed between Machiavellianism trait and mastery of perfectionistic strivings. This aligns with the model’s assumption that strategic, control-oriented traits will be associated with goal-directed perfectionism when achieving high standards serves instrumental purposes. This association may be explained by the motivation of some Machiavellian students to excel in their goals. To achieve this, many individuals with machiavellian traits tend to be strategic and spend more time and effort devising and implementing opportunistic tactics, rather than focusing on the learning process itself. In this way, they seek to avoid being detected in academic fraud practices and obtain favorable results without being questioned. This is especially reinforced in environments where these activities are widely implicitly accepted by their peers [14]. Some of the Machiavellian tactics may involve taking advantage of others’ work or using sensitive information to them to manipulate and get them to meet your high personal, academic, or even romantic demands [50]. In this way, these students can impose high standards on themselves, even at the cost of deceiving and manipulating others.

In addition, a relationship was found between Machiavellianism and perfectionistic concerns, indicating that students with a higher presence of this dark trait may be more sensitive to the consequences of making mistakes. This association can be explained by the need of these individuals to preserve their social reputation and achieve their goals at any cost. For them, image and success are priorities, and they are willing to use manipulative and deceptive strategies to achieve them. This implies constant pressure to demonstrate that they are competent in different areas, whether interpersonal or academic [16, 51]. This finding is supported by a meta-analytic study indicating that Machiavellian people experience concern about a lack of control over their environment [7]. This concern may be a compensatory mechanism for those individuals who lack certain skills and competencies, and whose identity may feel threatened when faced with perceived failure [15]. Finally, it is relevant to note that the association between Machiavellianism and perfectionistic concerns was lower compared to the association between Machiavellianism and perfectionistic strivings. This raises the importance of future research addressing the degree of self-criticism in these individuals, and whether these emotional characteristics have pathological consequences, especially in the context of Machiavellian university students [12].

As for the psychopathy trait, a positive relationship was observed between this dimension and perfectionistic concerns. This supports the model’s prediction that impulsivity and low empathy can co-occur with self-critical pressure when performance outcomes threaten the individual’s desired rewards. People with high levels of perfectionistic preoccupation may experience constant pressure to achieve perfection in different aspects of their lives. This pressure can generate an increase in self-criticism, where they judge themselves in a rigorous and demanding way. However, these traits can also manifest in individuals with psychopathic traits, who tend to have a high tendency to get what they want quickly and act impulsively when they don’t get it, according to previous studies based on rapid life history theory [52]. Therefore, university students with these psychopathic traits may feel greater concern when situations do not meet their performance expectations, especially if they cannot take advantage of others for short-term benefits (good grades, quick money, sexual partners, among others) [8].

On the other hand, a significant relationship was observed between narcissism and perfectionistic striving. This is consistent with the theoretical link proposed between admiration-seeking motives and self-imposed high standards, suggesting that perfectionism may serve as a self-regulatory strategy to maintain a grandiose self-image. Individuals with narcissistic traits may be obsessed with others’ admiration of them to feel special, and thus may strive for perfection in their work and personal achievements and feel justified in imposing their desires on others [9]. This finding supports the results of a meta-analysis that examined 33 previous studies and found a positive association between perfectionistic striving orientation and grandiose narcissism [20]. In that sense, narcissistic students may feel the need to set higher academic and social standards than others to gain recognition and admiration, which allows them to maintain an image of success. Their main motivation is to strengthen an identity based on outstanding achievements that allow them to boast and increase their self-esteem. This obsessive quest to appear successful may lead them to place higher academic and social demands on themselves than their peers to gain validation and excel in different areas of their lives [53].

In examining the influence of age on the relationship between perfectionistic striving and other variables, several potential explanations were identified. It is possible that older students have more opportunities and experiences to develop competencies in their areas of study and work, which may increase motivation and effort to achieve high standards for more recognition and even financial rewards. Likewise, as mentioned in a meta-analysis focused on the university population, as age increases, there may also be greater responsibility and competitiveness in the work and social environment, which may increase the pressure to achieve high standards and seek perfection in certain areas [10]. However, it is important to keep in mind that this effect may vary according to the individual context of each person, therefore more research is needed to explore this relationship in depth.

Regarding relationships with gender, Machiavellianism, and psychopathy to a lesser extent, they were more associated with the male group. This finding is consistent with previous studies suggesting that these traits are more prevalent in men than in women, including university samples [54]. These findings can be explained both by biological factors, such as elevated testosterone and low cortisol levels, as well as by social norms and roles that emphasize leadership and dominance. These combined factors may promote competitive and aggressive behaviors, characteristics of Machiavellian and psychopathic individuals, respectively [55, 56]. Thus, it is possible that these findings are more prevalent in male university students, who may show a greater willingness to excel academically, even if this implies harming their relationships with other peers. In addition, their motivation may be driven by the search for potential sexual partners, regardless of the possible emotional impact this may have on the individuals involved [4, 14].

The perfectionistic traits were nodes that connected the three traits of the dark triad to each other. From a theoretical standpoint, perfectionistic strivings emerge as a central mediator in the network, operationalizing the bridge between control-oriented and admiration-oriented antagonistic traits. Specifically, narcissistic, and Machiavellian traits were indirectly connected through the dimension of perfectionistic strivings. This finding may be congruent with the motivations of individuals who exhibit high levels of Machiavellianism and narcissism. Machiavellianism is characterized by a need to strategically control situations for personal gain at the expense of others, while narcissism is related to a desire to project a perfect image to gain admiration and social success [35]. Thus, perfectionistic strivings may strengthen dark triad traits in university students who are willing to engage in unethical academic behaviors to gain the approval of others and compensate for their internal beliefs of not possessing sufficient academic or interpersonal skills [19, 57].

The subclinical psychopathy measure exhibited the most significant and expected influence compared to all other network variables. This centrality aligns with the model’s notion that traits with broad interpersonal and self-regulatory implications (e.g., low empathy, impulsivity) can activate and reinforce multiple perfectionism pathways. This observation may be explained by the fact that this trait presented stronger correlations with both narcissism and perfectionistic concerns. The psychopathic trait may exhibit core features, such as low empathy and a reduced ability to manage emotions, which may facilitate the activation of other traits, such as narcissism and perfectionistic preoccupations, respectively. Therefore, in line with other research, many students with high psychopathy may be highly competitive and act impulsively out of a personal need to succeed academically or romantically, often at the expense of the needs of others [19, 58]. Similarly, this impulsivity can cause many of these students to constantly strive to maintain their achievements and experience frustration and negative emotions when their expectations are not met quickly. This situation may even reinforce vindictive or more vicious behaviors toward those who stand in the way of achieving their goals [59].

The results of this study provided overall support for the proposed specific hypotheses. Specifically, the findings confirmed that narcissism was positively and moderately associated with perfectionistic strivings, Machiavellianism was positively and moderately associated with both perfectionistic strivings and concerns, and psychopathy was positively associated with perfectionistic concerns. Furthermore, the network structure revealed that perfectionistic dimensions acted as bridging nodes connecting the three Dark Triad traits, as hypothesized. While the direction and strength of these associations were consistent with expectations, it is important to note that the network analysis, by its correlational nature, does not allow for causal inferences.

Psychological network analysis offers a nuanced and visually intuitive representation of relationships among variables, highlighting direct and indirect connections. However, it is primarily a descriptive tool: although it can reveal patterns of association and identify potentially central nodes, it cannot establish temporal precedence or causality. Consequently, interpretations should be made with caution, and future studies could benefit from longitudinal or experimental designs to examine the directionality and underlying mechanisms of the observed relationships.

Limitations and future studies

This study has some limitations due to the sampling approach used, which was non-probability based on convenience. This methodology of participant selection based on the availability and criteria established by the investigators may affect the representativeness of the sample and limit the generalizability of the results to the general population. Therefore, it is important to exercise caution when extrapolating the findings of this study to other populations or different contexts. It highlights the need for future studies using more representative sampling methods and covering a variety of contexts to obtain a more accurate picture of the relationship between the dark triad and perfectionism in different populations. In addition, it is important to note that the results and conclusions are based on a specific sample of Peruvian university students who participated in the study. Therefore, the results may be influenced by the characteristics of this sample and may not be generalizable to other populations or demographic groups. In addition, a limitation of this study is the lack of previous studies in Spanish that allow direct comparisons with the findings obtained. The absence of findings in the same language may make it difficult to assess the consistency of the results and their relationship with the existing literature in other languages. Given that culture and language can influence people’s attitudes, behaviors, and perceptions, it is important to consider the particularities and diversity of the samples and contexts studied. This highlights the need for further research in the Spanish-speaking context to generate a solid knowledge base and facilitate the comparison and validation of findings in different cultural and linguistic contexts. Another important aspect to consider is that data collection was carried out during a period of restrictions due to the COVID-19 health emergency. These constraints imposed due to the crisis and the security and social distancing measures may have influenced the availability and accessibility of the participants, as well as the way in which they responded to the online questionnaire. The exceptional situation of the health emergency could have generated changes in the routines and emotional states of the participants, which could influence their responses and behaviors. Finally, it is important to emphasize that network analysis is inherently correlational and primarily descriptive. While it enables the identification of patterns of association and centrality among variables, it does not allow for causal inferences or temporal ordering of relationships. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as depicting associations rather than cause–effect pathways, and future research using longitudinal or experimental methods is needed to examine the directionality and potential causal mechanisms underlying these connections.

Practical implications

The findings of this study have immediate practical applications for the well-being and development of university students. By identifying how Dark Triad traits correlate with perfectionism, college mental health professionals can better target their screening and preventive intervention efforts. For example, workshops can be developed with a specific focus on mitigating the negative consequences of psychopathy by improving self-compassion and emotional regulation among students. The findings indicate a need for mentoring programs in the educational setting that not only promote academic excellence but also foster resilience in the face of self-criticism and unrealistic perfectionistic expectations. This information could be used by guidance services to assist students in setting more balanced goals that prioritize learning and personal growth over superficial achievement. Furthermore, colleges and universities can enhance their offerings by integrating strategies that address the interplay between personality traits and academic pressures into their curricula and student support services. This could include integrating mindfulness practices and coping skills into programs targeting students with high levels of perfectionism, thus providing tools to manage pressure in a healthy way. Finally, these findings should prompt universities to review their policies and organizational culture to ensure that they are not inadvertently reinforcing a climate that values achievement over well-being.

Conclusion

In conclusion, it was observed that Machiavellianism showed associations with both perfectionistic strivings and narcissism, as well as with perfectionistic concerns in relation to psychopathy. In addition, a significant association was found between Machiavellianism and male gender. On the other hand, it was observed that perfectionistic strivings were directly related to the age of university students. However, psychopathy was found to be the dimension that showed the strongest connections within the network, indicating its significant influence on the interconnectedness with the different traits measured.
