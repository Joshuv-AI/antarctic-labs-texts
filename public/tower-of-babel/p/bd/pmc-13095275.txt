Comparative effectiveness of vancomycin and fidaxomicin in the treatment of Clostridioides difficile–associated diarrhea: A single-center experience in Taiwan
Authors: Wei-Chuang Tseng, Kuan-Chih Chen, Chen-Shuan Chung, Po-Chun Tseng, Cheng-Kuan Lin, Chun-Hsing Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Clostridioides difficile–associated diarrhea (CDAD) has become an increasingly common healthcare-associated infection worldwide. Although both vancomycin and fidaxomicin are approved for treatment, real-world comparative data from Taiwan remain limited. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of vancomycin and fidaxomicin in treating CDAD and to identify patients at risk of treatment failure who may benefit from fecal microbiota transplantation. This single-center retrospective observational study was conducted at Far Eastern Memorial Hospital from January 2018 to August 2020. Adult patients (≥20 years) diagnosed with CDAD and treated with oral vancomycin (125 mg 4 times daily) and/or fidaxomicin (200 mg twice daily) for 10 days were included. Treatment success and failure rates were compared between groups, and associations with age, sex, and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) were analyzed using a Student t test and chi-square test, with significance defined as P ≤ .05. A total of 166 patients were analyzed (mean age = 72.36 ± 14.72 years; mean CCI = 6.80 ± 2.61). Of these, 161 received vancomycin and 48 received fidaxomicin. The treatment success rate was 53.4% for vancomycin and 79.2% for fidaxomicin. Among vancomycin-treated patients, treatment failure was significantly associated with older age (P = .009) and higher CCI (P = .004). No statistically significant associations were observed for fidaxomicin outcomes with age, sex, and CCI. Fidaxomicin demonstrated superior clinical effectiveness to vancomycin, particularly among older adults and patients with multiple comorbidities. These findings support prioritizing fidaxomicin for high-risk patients and considering fecal microbiota transplantation in refractory cases.

1. Introduction

Clostridioides difficile (C difficile) is a gram-positive, spore-forming, toxin-producing, obligate anaerobic bacillus transmitted through the fecal–oral route via spores.[1] Two major toxins, toxin A and toxin B, are believed to play a crucial role in the virulence of the bacterium.[2] These toxins exhibit glycosyltransferase activities, leading to the transfer of glycosyl residues to small Ras homologous GTPases and resulting in the loss of intestinal membrane integrity and cell death.[3,4] Susceptibility to C difficile infection (CDI) is closely linked to prior antibiotic exposure, which disrupts the normal gut microbiota, reduces colonization resistance, and facilitates overgrowth of toxigenic C difficile strains.[5] Risk factors for CDI include antibiotic exposure, advanced age, and extended stay in hospitals or nursing homes.[6] The frequency and severity of CDI have increased worldwide in the past decade,[6] and C difficile–associated diarrhea (CDAD) has become one of the most common hospital-acquired infections.[7]

In Taiwan, the incidence of CDI ranges from 1.1 to 7.3 per 10,000 patient-days, with regional variation across northern, central, and southern areas. The overall in-hospital mortality rate is 20% to 26.2%, with a 4.7% disease-related in-hospital mortality rate for community-onset CDI.[8] The recurrence rate of CDI in Taiwan has been reported at approximately 8% to 10%, which is comparable to recurrence rates observed in other Asian populations.[8,9] These rates are broadly comparable to global estimates, where the pooled CDI incidence rate approximates 3.90 cases per 10,000 patient-days and 30-day all-cause mortality averages 8.32%, although cross-regional differences in surveillance methods, antimicrobial policies, and healthcare resources may influence these figures.[10]

Primary CDAD treatments include antibiotics, such as oral metronidazole, vancomycin, and fidaxomicin.[11] In this context, “targeted therapy” refers to the use of anti–C difficile antibiotics, including metronidazole, vancomycin, fidaxomicin, and teicoplanin, directed specifically against CDI rather than broad-spectrum empirical antibiotics. Previous studies have indicated the superiority of fidaxomicin in providing a sustained clinical response, particularly beneficial for high-risk patients with recurrent CDI.[12,13] Regional treatment guidelines vary considerably. The United States (Infectious Diseases Society of America and Society for Healthcare Epidemiology of America) and Europe (European Society of Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases) recommend fidaxomicin or vancomycin as first-line agents, reserving metronidazole for mild cases or when other agents are unavailable.[14] By contrast, Japan and Taiwan have adopted fidaxomicin primarily for recurrent or refractory CDI, reflecting the influence of cost, drug accessibility, and reimbursement policy on clinical decision-making.[8,15] In Taiwan, the Infectious Diseases Society of Taiwan (IDST) guidelines recommend metronidazole, vancomycin, or fidaxomicin for an initial CDAD episode, with vancomycin or fidaxomicin preferred in moderate-to-severe cases or patients at high risk of recurrence. For first recurrences, vancomycin, fidaxomicin, or teicoplanin are recommended depending on the prior regimen, while fidaxomicin and fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) are reserved for multiple recurrences or refractory cases. These recommendations reflect the practical influence of drug availability and reimbursement policies, where fidaxomicin use is often limited to cases of vancomycin failure or recurrent infection.[8]

FMT is recommended for patients with second or subsequent CDI recurrence.[16] A randomized controlled study showed that FMT combined with vancomycin outperformed fidaxomicin or vancomycin alone in terms of clinical and microbiological resolution, or clinical resolution, for recurrent CDI treatment.[17] Although FMT regulation and accessibility differ globally,[18] Taiwan officially approved its use in September 2018 for patients with recurrent or refractory CDI cases, defined as CDI reoccurrence within 8 weeks after symptom resolution following medical treatment or persistent clinical symptoms despite targeted antibiotic therapy.[19] This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of vancomycin and fidaxomicin in CDAD treatment and to identify potential candidates for FMT in cases of treatment failure.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Data source and ethical statement

This study was conducted at Far Eastern Memorial Hospital (FEMH), a 1200-bed tertiary referral and teaching hospital located in New Taipei City, Taiwan. FEMH provides comprehensive medical and surgical care for a large urban and suburban population and maintains an established infection control and microbiology laboratory capable of performing stool toxigenic C difficile cultures and related diagnostic assays. These characteristics make FEMH a suitable site for surveillance and comparative effectiveness studies of CDAD. Data were collected retrospectively through the electronic medical record system of FEMH. Trained research personnel reviewed inpatient charts and extracted relevant clinical information using a standardized data abstraction form to ensure consistency and accuracy.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of FEMH (IRB No. FEMH 111189-E). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of deidentified data.

2.2. Study design, study period, and study population

This was a single-center, retrospective observational study conducted at FEMH. The study period spanned from January 2018 to August 2020, a timeframe selected to coincide with the routine clinical availability of both vancomycin and fidaxomicin at FEMH, as well as the early implementation period following national approval of FMT in Taiwan. This period allowed evaluation of real-world treatment effectiveness under contemporary clinical practice and identification of patients potentially eligible for FMT. The study included adult patients aged 20 years or older who were diagnosed with CDAD and received oral vancomycin (125 mg 4 times daily) and/or fidaxomicin (200 mg twice daily) for a 10-day treatment course.

CDAD diagnosis was established only when both of the following criteria were met: occurrence of 3 or more loose stools within 24 hours, and a positive stool toxigenic bacterial culture for C difficile. Recurrence was defined as the reappearance of diarrhea (≥3 loose stools within 24 hours) with a positive toxigenic C difficile stool culture occurring within 8 weeks after completion of successful treatment of a previous CDAD episode. Patients who received antibiotic treatment for <10 days were excluded from the analysis. All eligible adult patients meeting the inclusion criteria during the study period were included.

Although FMT was nationally approved in 2018, its clinical implementation at our institution during the early approval period was limited; therefore, identifying patients at high risk of treatment failure during this interval was intended to inform future patient selection and the implementation of FMT in routine practice.

This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines, and the completed STROBE checklist is provided in the Supplementary Material (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R681).

2.3. Sample size and sampling considerations

As this was a retrospective observational study, no a priori sample size calculation was performed. Instead, all eligible adult patients who met the predefined inclusion criteria during the study period (January 2018 to August 2020) were consecutively included to maximize representativeness of the study population and reflect real-world clinical practice.

2.4. Institutional CDAD treatment protocols

During the study period, the management of CDAD at FEMH followed the national IDST recommendations, which are largely consistent with international treatment standards. For initial CDAD episodes, patients typically received a 10-day course of oral vancomycin (125 mg 4 times daily) or fidaxomicin (200 mg twice daily) as first-line therapy. For first recurrences without major risk factors, either vancomycin, fidaxomicin, or teicoplanin was administered, with selection based on the initial regimen used. For recurrent or refractory cases, extended regimens of vancomycin or fidaxomicin, teicoplanin (if not previously used), or FMT were considered in accordance with the Taiwan Microbiota Consortium recommendations.

2.5. Exposure, outcomes, and covariates

The main exposure was the type of antibiotic treatment (vancomycin or fidaxomicin) administered for CDAD. The primary outcome was treatment success rate, reflecting the treatment effectiveness and defined as clinical resolution of diarrhea following the 10-day antibiotic course. The secondary outcome examined risk factors associated with treatment failure to identify patients who might be potential candidates for FMT. Treatment failure was defined as persistence or recurrence of CDAD-related symptoms, including ongoing diarrhea (≥3 loose stools per 24 hours), abdominal pain, fever, or leukocytosis, after completion of the 10-day antibiotic course. Patient demographic and clinical variables were extracted, including age, sex, and the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), which was used to assess the overall severity of comorbid conditions.[20]

2.6. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables, including age and CCI, were presented as means ± standard deviation and compared using the Student t test. Categorical variables, such as sex, treatment type, and treatment outcomes, were summarized as frequencies and percentages and analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test as appropriate. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P-value ≤ 0.05. All analyses were performed using SPSS software, version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk).

In analyses of fidaxomicin treatment failure, age, sex, and CCI were selected a priori based on prior evidence linking these factors to treatment response and recurrence risk in CDI. Treatment failure was used to identify patients at higher risk of poor outcomes who may be considered potential candidates for FMT, and subgroup analyses were conducted accordingly.

3. Results

3.1. Patients selection

A total of 166 patients diagnosed with CDAD were included in this study. Among these, 161 patients who suffered their first episode of CDAD were treated with vancomycin. The remaining 5 patients with recurrent episodes and 43 patients who failed vancomycin treatment were subsequently treated with fidaxomicin (Fig. 1). Among patients who failed vancomycin treatment, fidaxomicin was initiated immediately after, or near the completion of, the standard 10-day vancomycin course because of persistent clinical symptoms, resulting in a minimal interval between the 2 treatments. These patients who failed initial vancomycin therapy and required second-line fidaxomicin represented a subgroup with refractory disease and were considered potential candidates for FMT in the event of subsequent treatment failure.

3.2. Clinical characteristics and outcomes of patients with recurrent CDAD

Five patients developed recurrent CDAD during the study period. All had received standard antibiotic therapy, primarily oral vancomycin, for the initial episode, with recurrence occurring approximately 6 to 14 weeks later. Recurrence was commonly associated with recent hospitalization and/or systemic antibiotic exposure, although 2 patients had no documented antecedent antibiotic use.

Among these 5 patients, 1 did not respond to fidaxomicin and subsequently underwent FMT, resulting in complete resolution of diarrhea and clinical symptom improvement. Of the remaining 4 patients treated with fidaxomicin, 3 demonstrated clinical improvement, characterized by reduced diarrhea frequency and/or resolution of symptoms. One patient initially treated with fidaxomicin died before achieving clinical cure due to severe underlying comorbid conditions.

3.4. Comparative treatment outcomes and associated factors with vancomycin and fidaxomicin effectiveness

Overall, the success rates for vancomycin and fidaxomicin treatment were 53.4% and 79.2%, respectively. In the vancomycin group, patients who failed treatment had significantly higher CCI (P = .004) and advanced age (P = .009; Table 2). No statistically significant differences were found between the successful and failed subgroups of fidaxomicin in terms of age (P = .29), sex (P = .39), and CCI (P = .22; Table 3). Patients who experienced fidaxomicin treatment failure were therefore identified as a subgroup at higher risk of poor outcomes and may represent potential candidates for FMT.

4. Discussion

This study evaluated the comparative effectiveness of vancomycin and fidaxomicin in the treatment of CDAD and identified patients at risk of treatment failure who may benefit from alternative strategies such as FMT. Using real-world data from a tertiary medical center, fidaxomicin demonstrated a higher treatment success rate (79.2%) than vancomycin (53.4%). Patients who failed vancomycin treatment were significantly older and had higher CCI scores, whereas no demographic or comorbidity differences were observed among fidaxomicin-treated patients. These findings indicate that fidaxomicin is more effective than vancomycin in achieving clinical resolution, particularly among older or multimorbid patients who are at greater risk of poor outcomes and recurrence.

The present study demonstrated a higher treatment success rate with fidaxomicin compared with vancomycin, in agreement with previous randomized trials and meta-analyses showing that fidaxomicin achieves comparable initial cure but markedly lower recurrence rates than vancomycin.[21,22] In the multicenter randomized controlled trial by Cornely et al, the clinical cure rates were 91.7% for fidaxomicin and 90.6% for vancomycin, yet fidaxomicin achieved markedly fewer recurrences, confirming its advantage in sustained cure and overall treatment effectiveness.[23] Among vancomycin-treated patients in our cohort, treatment success was associated with younger age and lower CCI, reinforcing the role of age and comorbidity burden as predictors of suboptimal therapeutic response.[24] These findings highlight fidaxomicin as a more effective therapeutic option, particularly for older adults or those with multiple comorbidities.

An emergent concern is the gradual increase in C difficile strains exhibiting reduced susceptibility to vancomycin, the mechanism of which remains under investigation.[25,26] In this context, early identification of patients likely to respond poorly to vancomycin is critical to guide timely therapeutic modification, including consideration of fidaxomicin or FMT. A previous study reported that advanced age, concurrent antibiotic use, gastric acid suppression, and infection with hypervirulent strains were significant risk factors for recurrent CDI.[27] The definition of recurrence in that study – symptom reappearance within 8 weeks of resolution – differs from our study’s definition of treatment failure, which refers to persistent diarrhea despite completion of a 10-day antibiotic course. Despite this difference, both studies consistently highlight advanced age as a key determinant of suboptimal treatment response. The higher CCI observed among patients with vancomycin failure in our cohort may further indicate that systemic frailty and medical complexity compound the risk of inadequate microbial clearance. Variations in study populations, antibiotic exposure patterns, and the prevalence of hypervirulent ribotypes may explain the differences in risk factors reported.[24] These findings suggest that older adults and patients with multiple comorbidities represent a high-risk subgroup who may benefit from early fidaxomicin use or alternative strategies such as FMT to improve treatment success.

Comparative evaluations of vancomycin and fidaxomicin in the management of CDI have yielded heterogeneous findings across different geographic regions. A prospective multicenter study encompassing 12 Asia-Pacific countries, including Taiwan, demonstrated that all clinical isolates remained fully susceptible to both vancomycin and fidaxomicin.[28] Consistent results from other investigations have shown that, although C difficile displays resistance to several antimicrobial classes, reduced susceptibility to vancomycin or fidaxomicin remains exceedingly uncommon.[29,30] Nevertheless, variations in clinical effectiveness have been observed among studies. While some real-world analyses reported comparable outcomes between fidaxomicin and vancomycin, including clinical cure, sustained response, and all-cause mortality, others demonstrated superior sustained cure rates and lower recurrence with fidaxomicin.[31–33] Such discrepancies may reflect regional differences in prevalent C difficile ribotypes (e.g., RT017, RT018, RT027), variations in patient characteristics such as age, comorbidity burden, and prior antibiotic exposure, as well as differences in disease severity and treatment practices across settings.[27,34] Although both agents retain excellent in vitro activity, emerging evidence of reduced fidaxomicin susceptibility among certain hypervirulent strains underscores the need for ongoing molecular surveillance and region-specific therapeutic evaluation.[35]

The American College of Gastroenterology,[36,37] the Infectious Disease Society of America, and the Society of Healthcare Epidemiologists of America[38] have issued guidelines recommending vancomycin or fidaxomicin as first-line agents and reserving FMT for recurrent or refractory cases.[8,36–38] In Taiwan, the IDST[8] provides similar guidance, aligning closely with international standards. For initial episodes, metronidazole, vancomycin, or fidaxomicin may be used, while vancomycin or fidaxomicin is preferred for recurrent cases. Teicoplanin and FMT served for refractory or multiply recurrent cases.[15] In this study, fidaxomicin was primarily administered to patients with recurrent CDAD or those who failed to respond to vancomycin, aligning with these national and international guidelines. Among the cohort, 9.7% of patients did not respond to either antibiotic, necessitating consideration of FMT as an adjunctive therapy. Notably, 46.6% of patients who failed vancomycin met the criteria for FMT, underscoring the importance of early identification and timely escalation to FMT in refractory or high-risk cases.[13] These results highlight the clinical relevance of individualized treatment approaches and reinforce the need for continued evaluation of FMT’s role in managing refractory or multidrug-resistant CDI.

The strengths of this study include the use of real-world hospital data that reflect actual clinical practice and patient diversity. Importantly, all eligible adult inpatients with CDAD treated during the study period were consecutively included, minimizing selection bias and ensuring that the study population is representative of hospitalized patients with moderate-to-severe disease. However, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, its retrospective, single-center design may limit generalizability, and reliance on electronic medical records may have resulted in misclassification of treatment outcomes due to incomplete symptom documentation, introducing potential recall bias. Second, data on key potential confounders, including laboratory parameters, prior antibiotic exposure, and infection severity, were incompletely recorded and could not be incorporated into the analysis, precluding multivariate regression to identify independent predictors of treatment failure. Third, the lack of microbiological characterization of C difficile strains prevented assessment of strain-specific treatment responses. These limitations were partially mitigated by standardized diagnostic criteria, consistent treatment protocols, and adherence to national and international guidelines. Future multicenter, prospective studies incorporating strain typing, molecular surveillance, and longer follow-up are warranted to validate these findings and strengthen causal inferences.

5. Conclusion

Fidaxomicin demonstrated superior clinical effectiveness compared with vancomycin in treating CDAD, particularly among older adults and patients with multiple comorbidities who exhibited a poorer response to vancomycin. Nearly half of the patients who failed vancomycin treatment met criteria for FMT, highlighting the importance of early identification and timely escalation to adjunctive therapy. These findings underscore the need for individualized therapeutic strategies tailored to patient risk profiles. From a clinical perspective, fidaxomicin should be prioritized for patients at high risk of treatment failure, while FMT should be considered in refractory cases in accordance with current national and international guidelines. Future research should integrate molecular data with clinical outcomes to refine patient selection criteria and optimize treatment algorithms for CDI.
