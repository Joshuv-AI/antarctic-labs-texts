Beyond cancer: CAR-T cells redefining liver disease therapy
Authors: Chittampalli N. Yashaswini, Ruchi Bansal
Published: 2026

Hepatic fibrosis remains a major unmet clinical challenge worldwide. Liver diseases can arise from a variety of etiologies with overlapping yet distinct pathogenic pathways. These etiologies include metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), chronic viral hepatitis, particularly hepatitis B and C, alcohol-associated liver disease, autoimmune diseases, biliary diseases, and drug-induced liver injury.1 Repeated or sustained hepatic injury from these insults drives fibrosis progression, which can culminate in cirrhosis – a late-stage condition characterized by extensive scarring, regenerative nodules, and loss of normal liver function – and may ultimately predispose to hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). HSCs comprise approximately 10–15% of total liver cells and are mesenchymal in origin.2 Under physiological conditions, these cells remain in a quiescent state, playing essential roles in maintaining hepatic homeostasis. In response to liver injury, however, HSCs undergo activation and transition into a myofibroblast-like phenotype. This activated state is marked by enhanced proliferation, contractility, immune signaling capacity, and robust production of extracellular matrix (ECM). In HCC, HSCs are the primary source of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), which are key players in the reorganization of the tumor microenvironment (TME), and help to modulate cancer cell proliferation, migration, invasion and chemoresistance.3 Although they represent a minority of liver cells numerically, HSCs display striking heterogeneity, with subsets playing distinct – and sometimes opposing – roles during fibrogenesis.4 As a result, while HSCs are widely recognized as the dominant drivers of fibrosis, strategies aimed at indiscriminate elimination of all HSCs pose significant risks. Consequently, effective antifibrotic therapies must selectively target pathogenic HSC populations, like activated HSCs, while preserving beneficial subsets, like quiescent HSCs.

Targeted elimination of defined cell populations can be achieved through chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy, a strategy in which T lymphocytes are genetically engineered to recognize and destroy cells expressing a specific surface antigen.5 In their native state, T cells recognize antigens through T cell receptors (TCRs), a process constrained by the need for antigen processing, presentation by MHC molecules, and engagement of costimulatory signals. CARs overcome these limitations by using antibody-derived domains that bind directly to antigens in an MHC-independent manner, and often with higher affinity. CARs incorporate intracellular signaling motifs and costimulatory domains, enabling robust T cell activation, expansion and target cell killing via perforin- and granzyme-mediated apoptosis.6

CAR-T cell therapy has transformed the treatment landscape for certain cancers, particularly hematologic malignancies, by offering a personalized immunotherapy wherein a patient’s own T cells can be used to destroy cancer cells.7 CAR-T cells targeting CD19 are approved for B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia and B-cell lymphomas, while CAR-T therapies directed against B cell maturation antigen are approved for multiple myeloma.8 Clinical data demonstrate that these therapies can induce deep and durable remissions, with long-term follow-up suggesting curative potential in a subset of patients.

The success of CAR-T cell therapy in cancer has catalyzed interest in extending this approach to non-oncologic diseases. Preclinical studies have demonstrated efficacy of CAR-T strategies in diverse contexts, including cardiac fibrosis through targeting of fibroblast activation protein-α,9 age-associated metabolic dysfunction via targeting of uPAR (urokinase plasminogen activator receptor),10 and autoimmune diseases such as pemphigus vulgaris through elimination of autoreactive immune cells.11 These advances highlight the versatility of CAR-T technology and suggest its potential as a powerful platform for treating fibrotic, degenerative, and immune-mediated diseases beyond oncology.

In this study,12 Zhang, Ren, Zhang, Pan et al., present a CAR-T cell therapy targeting activated HSCs for the treatment of hepatic fibrosis. The authors identified that fibroblast activation protein (FAP) is highly specific to activated HSCs and minimally expressed in quiescent HSCs, other liver cell populations, and other tissues (fetal and adult), making it a particularly attractive antigen for targeted cellular therapy. Building on this observation, the authors generated FAP-targeted CAR-T cells and demonstrated that a single administration markedly reduced liver fibrosis across multiple etiologies – carbon tetrachloride (CCl4)-induced fibrosis; 3,5-diethoxycarbonyl-1,4-dihydrocollidine-induced cholestatic fibrosis; high-fat, high-cholesterol diet-induced MASH-associated fibrosis; and alcohol plus CCl4-induced alcohol-associated fibrosis. Across all models, serum ALT levels, total hepatic hydroxyproline, Sirius red and α-smooth muscle actin expression, and canonical fibrosis-related genes were significantly reduced, suggesting attenuation of liver fibrosis. Notably, the antifibrotic effects of FAP CAR-T therapy exceeded those achieved with a pharmacological FAP inhibitor, highlighting the superiority of targeted cellular cytotoxicity (Fig. 1).

To dissect the mechanisms underlying the antifibrotic effects, the authors performed in-depth bulk RNA-seq, single-cell RNA sequencing, and single-nucleus RNA sequencing analyses in the CCl4-induced fibrosis model. FAP CAR-T treatment resulted in a marked reduction in the proportion of HSCs, with residual HSCs exhibiting downregulation of gene programs enriched for ECM production, ECM organization, and focal adhesion pathways. Immune profiling revealed that treatment increased hepatic T and NK cell infiltration, with these cell populations exhibiting enhanced activation, cytokine signaling, leukocyte function and cytotoxicity. Further analysis identified sub-populations of macrophages with upregulated phagocytosis-associated genes that cleared out the activated HSCs eliminated by FAP CAR-T cells. Altogether, these findings indicate not only immune-mediated depletion and clearance of activated HSCs, but also functional suppression of immuno-fibrogenic signaling via FAP CAR-T treatment.

The authors next questioned whether this approach could be effective in advanced liver disease, a setting in which therapeutic options are particularly limited. In a chronic CCl4 model with features of cirrhosis, FAP CAR-T treatment significantly reduced fibrosis, suggesting that FAP CAR-T cells can penetrate cirrhotic liver tissue and retain therapeutic activity despite a dense fibrotic microenvironment.

The study further explored the efficacy of FAP CAR-T in primary liver cancer (HCC) representing a late-stage consequence of chronic fibrosis. Using a diethylnitrosamine + CCl4-induced HCC model, FAP CAR-T cells were administered prior to overt tumor development. This intervention significantly reduced tumor incidence, tumor burden, serum AFP levels and was accompanied by decreased ECM accumulation. Single-cell analyses revealed reduced proportions of tumor cells and CAFs following CAR-T treatment. Excitingly, tumor cells exhibited transcriptional programs indicative of increased apoptosis and inflammation, alongside reduced cell cycle activity, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, invasion, proliferation, and stemness. Consistent with liver fibrosis models, T and NK cells displayed enhanced activation and cytotoxic signatures, underscoring the immune-mediated antitumorigenic effects of this approach.

These findings build on a growing body of literature demonstrating the potential of antifibrotic CAR-T therapy. Prior studies have shown that antifibrotic CAR-T cells can alleviate cardiac, liver and renal fibrosis.9,[13], [14], [15] Recently, a complementary study14 demonstrated the efficacy of in vivo-generated CAR-T cells for liver fibrosis from early MASH, using T cell-targeting lipid nanoparticles to deliver mRNA encoding an anti-FAP CAR. This in vivo anti-FAP CAR approach not only reduced liver fibrosis by targeting fibrogenic HSCs, but also promoted broader tissue remodeling, including endothelial cell restoration, hepatocyte reprogramming toward a healthier phenotype, and immune cell reprogramming toward fibrosis-resolving states.

Building upon these foundational breakthroughs, Zhang, Ren, Zhang, Pan et al. provide a critical expansion of the field. Beyond replicating key findings of antifibrotic efficacy in liver fibrosis, the authors demonstrate that FAP CAR-T therapy is effective across diverse etiologies of liver fibrosis, and remains effective in advanced disease states, including cirrhosis and HCC. Given that progressive fibrosis underlies both cirrhosis and HCC development, the ability to intervene at these late stages represents a particularly meaningful advance.

HCC remains notoriously difficult to treat, in part due to its dense, fibrotic, and immunosuppressive microenvironment, which can limit immune cell infiltration.16 The observation that FAP CAR-T treatment can not only penetrate and ameliorate advanced fibrosis, but also impede tumor progression, is particularly exciting. While important questions remain regarding efficacy against established HCC, durability of response, long-term safety, and the potential for combination strategies, this work provides a strong preclinical foundation for clinical translation of FAP CAR-T therapy to impact patients with advanced liver disease.

Looking ahead, this work suggests that FAP CAR-T-based approaches could have broader utility beyond fibrosis. Many solid tumors are characterized by a dense, desmoplastic stroma in which FAP-expressing fibroblasts actively drive tumor progression, invasion and immune exclusion, particularly of T cell infiltration. Indeed, previous studies have demonstrated that bispecific CAR-T cells targeting both FAP and tumor antigens such as glypican-3 can overcome antigen heterogeneity in HCC, improving tumor control compared with single-target strategies.17 Similarly, engineered FAP/IL-15 CAR-T cells have been shown to overcome tumor immunosuppression by targeting FAP-expressing CAFs while autonomously secreting IL-15, thereby enhancing CAR-T cell persistence, memory phenotype, activation, and overall antitumor efficacy.18

Collectively, these studies suggest that targeting stromal fibrosis through FAP CAR-T therapy could prevent cancer development and even complement existing (cancer) treatments by remodeling the tumor microenvironment. Such an approach may be particularly relevant for malignancies with prominent fibrotic components, including pancreatic, breast, esophagus, colorectal, lung, ovarian, and certain mesenchymal tumors. Collectively, these possibilities highlight the potential of FAP-directed or stromal-targeted immunotherapies as a platform strategy for treating fibrosis-driven diseases across organ systems and cancer types.
