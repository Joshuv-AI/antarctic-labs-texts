Simulation Analysis of Secondary Air Control on Combustion Flame and SNCR Technology in a 900 t/d Waste Incineration Furnace
Authors: Tao Lin, Tong-hua Dai, Chengdai chen, Haoqi Xian, Fengqin Han, Yan-fen Liao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The stable and efficient combustion of large-capacity waste incinerators is a critical factor in improving the operational efficiency and safety of the equipment. The present study employs numerical simulation methods to comprehensively analyze the combustion flame characteristics, temperature field distribution, temperature uniformity, and the distance between the high-temperature region and the water-cooled wall in a 900 t/d large-capacity waste incinerator. This is achieved by adjusting the secondary air distribution ratios at the front and rear walls. The findings suggest that under constant secondary air inlet angle conditions, optimizing the secondary air velocity can significantly enhance combustion stability within the furnace. It has been demonstrated that modifying the secondary air distribution ratio significantly enhances the flame separation within the furnace, leading to a 25.85% increase in the combustion efficiency of the combustible gases in the primary air zone. Furthermore, an increase in the average temperature of the first flue gas duct by 4.73% is observed, thereby ensuring a uniform temperature distribution in the subsequent burnout zone and reducing the risk of high-temperature corrosion. Furthermore, the optimization of the arrangement of the selective noncatalytic reduction (SNCR) injectors results in an enhancement of denitrification efficiency to 61.76%. The findings of this study provide theoretical support and practical guidance for improving the combustion efficiency and environmental friendliness of large-capacity waste incinerators, offering significant engineering application value.

Introduction

In recent years, the rapid growth of the global population, advancements in technology and the economy, and the acceleration of urbanization and industrialization have led to a significant increase in resource consumption, and this growth trend is expected to continue. As resources are overexploited, the volume of waste generated has also increased, particularly urban solid waste. , This not only leads to severe environmental issues but also exacerbates the pressure on waste management. In 2023, the global generation of urban solid waste reached 2.3 billion tons, and it is projected to exceed 2.6 billion tons by 2027. In comparison, China has seen the fastest growth in the production of urban solid waste. The increasing scarcity of land for landfills, along with the large land area required for constructing new landfills, has led many cities to close existing ones and seek more efficient waste management solutions. , Therefore, proper treatment of urban solid waste and the realization of resource recycling have become critical issues that need urgent resolution.

The commonly used management technologies for urban solid waste at present include landfilling, − composting, pyrolysis, anaerobic digestion by bacteria, and incineration. , Each method has its advantages and disadvantages. , Among these, incineration has been the fastest-developing treatment method in recent years. It effectively reduces the volume of waste, and the heat and electricity generated can be reused. Its coverage area is second only to landfilling. With the continuous advancement of technology, the efficiency and environmental performance of waste incineration furnaces have been improving. However, despite significant progress in incineration technology, there remain several issues that require further improvement, including further reduction of the total amount of urban solid waste, enhancing the recovery efficiency of heat and recyclable materials, and ensuring that the emissions meet current environmental standards.

The existing research on waste incineration furnaces is primarily supported by two methodologies: numerical simulation and experimental studies. Numerical simulation, due to its low cost, high repeatability, and ease of parameter optimization, has become the preferred approach for most researchers. Liu et al. designed a waste incineration furnace with an arched structure for a mixture of kitchen waste and branches, achieving an α-shaped flame combustion, and determined the optimal operating parameters through cold-state experiments. Xu et al., based on high-temperature low-oxygen diffusion combustion technology, used numerical models to investigate the impact of different flue gas duct structures and air supply modes on the temperature field, flow distribution, and NOx concentration field within the furnace flue. They proposed a new fuel structure and combustion method that effectively reduced the level of NOx emissions. Furnace shape and flow characteristics are important factors influencing combustion efficiency and pollutant emissions in waste incineration furnaces. Many studies have focused on the impact of in-furnace airflow characteristics on the combustion process. Zhou et al., through comprehensive CFD simulations, investigated the combustion process of a medium-arch double-channel incinerator, addressing NOx emissions and in-furnace overheating issues. The study showed that optimizing the ratio of primary air to secondary air, adjusting the air speed and distribution, significantly reduced NOx emissions, improved the in-furnace temperature distribution, and had a minimal impact on furnace load. Yang et al. established a numerical model of a 350 t/d waste incineration furnace using CFD to analyze the impact of different air supply methods on NOx formation, demonstrating that reducing the proportion of primary air helps to reduce NOx emissions, leading to a cleaner combustion process.Yan et al. , studied the impact of primary air temperature on combustion characteristics using a coupled FLIC-FLUENT program and found that the preheated primary air temperature has an optimal range. Exceeding this range not only fails to improve efficiency but also may lead to increased pollutant emissions and damage to the grate. Although significant progress has been made in studying the impact of primary air, research on secondary air remains relatively limited. In recent years, researchers have gradually focused on the effect of secondary air on combustion behavior within the furnace. Choi et al. used CFD simulations to study the impact of modifying secondary air nozzles on CO emissions during the combustion of wood waste in a grate-type incinerator, showing that modification of the secondary air nozzles resulted in more uniform combustion inside the furnace, and the simulation results were significant for predicting in-furnace flow and combustion phenomena. Zeng et al. established a numerical model of a 375 t/d mechanical grate incinerator to analyze the effects of air supply methods and grate speed on the combustion process. They proposed strategies for adjusting the secondary air layout under different primary air temperature conditions, providing a reference for the optimized operation of low-capacity waste incinerators. Liu et al. conducted numerical simulations using CFD on a 750 t/d waste incineration furnace, focusing on the impact of secondary air nozzle layout and wind speed on in-furnace combustion. However, this study neglected the impact of the secondary air injection angle and simulated a relatively small capacity furnace.

This study focuses on the issue of low single-unit processing capacity in small to medium-sized waste incinerators. In comparison, large waste incinerators have a significant advantage in terms of daily processing capacity. Numerical simulation and analysis of the combustion process within large-capacity waste incinerators (e.g., 900 t/d) remain relatively scarce. The present study employs ANSYS FLUENT computational fluid dynamics (CFD) software to conduct meticulous numerical simulations of the thermal flow field within the furnace of a 900 t/d large-capacity waste incinerator. The temperature and flow fields under different operating conditions are compared, and the impact of varying the secondary air velocity at the front and rear walls on the combustion flame characteristics, temperature distribution, and temperature uniformity is explored under optimal primary-to-secondary air ratios. Furthermore, an investigation was conducted into the layout of the Secondary Combustion Nozzle Ring (SCNR). The present study analyzes the causes of high-temperature wall attachment phenomena and proposes effective preventive and optimization measures. The present study provides a theoretical foundation for the optimization of the design of large-capacity waste incinerators. Furthermore, it offers novel insights and methodologies for the synergistic control of associated pollutants.

Method

The Guangdong garbage incinerator, which has a processing capacity of 900 tonnes per day, is the focus of the research project. The incinerator body measures around 32.5 m in height and 17 m in depth. It has three flue gas channels measuring 6, 4.1, and 3.9 m in width from left to right. The chamber width is approximately 15 m, and the flue width is 14 m. The grate’s entire length is around 14 m. It is made up of four components, each 3.5 m long. The incinerator grate comprises three sections: the drying zone, the combustion zone, and the burnout zone. Each zone is equipped with a wind chamber below, with a primary air wind temperature of 220 °C. There are 12 blowing gun ports on the front and rear arches of the furnace, with vertical angles of 53° and 45°, respectively, and a wind temperature of 25 °C. Two layers of secondary air are blasted into the front and rear walls. There is a certain height difference: the front and rear wall elevations are 6.5 m. There are 12 blowing gun ports on each side, and the air intake is shown in the Figure . The secondary air temperature is 25 °C.

The research procedure is structured as follows. Initially, the distributions of the temperature and flow fields under air supply conditions are examined to establish the optimal primary-to-secondary air ratio. Subsequently, at this predetermined optimal ratio, the effect of varying the secondary air velocities (SA1, SA2) at the front and rear walls is investigated with a focus on combustion flame characteristics, temperature distribution, and uniformity. Furthermore, the layout configuration of the SCNR is studied. Finally, the underlying causes of high-temperature wall adhesion phenomena are thoroughly analyzed, leading to the proposal of effective preventive and optimization strategies.

In this study, modeling of NO x formation and emissions is of critical importance. Specifically, during municipal solid waste (MSW) incineration, fuel-NO x and thermal-NO x constitute the dominant contributors to total NO x emissions. This study employed distinct modeling approaches for different NO x pathways. The thermal-NO x model was established by using an equilibrium approach to calculate the O and OH radicals. For fuel-NO x formation, the model employs an additional transport equation to simulate the intermediate species (HCN and NH3).

In the formula, k f,1 = 1.8 × 108e–38370/T , k f,2 = 1.8 × 104 Te–4680/T , and k f,3 = 7.1 × 107e–450/T represent the forward reaction rate constants, respectively; k r,1 = 3.8 × 107e–425/T , k r,2 = 3.81 × 103e–20820/T , and k r,3 = 1.7 × 108e–24560/T represent the reverse reaction rate constants, respectively.

The fuel-NO x model is based on the global reaction mechanism proposed by Soete. The reaction rate of fuel-NO x is strongly influenced by the local combustion temperature and the nitrogen content in the fuel. During the reaction process, nitrogen is released into the gas phase as intermediate species such as HCN, NH3, N, CN, and NH radicals, which are subsequently converted into NO x . In the present model, nitrogen allocation is specified as follows: 55% converts to intermediate HCN, and 5% transforms into NH3. The remainder is assumed to form NO directly.

In the simulation software, key model parameters are configured as follows. The feed rate is set at 36,030 kg/h. The theoretical air requirement was 2.67 N m3/kg. The excess air coefficient, defined as the reciprocal of the equivalence ratio, yields an actual air supply of 4.26 N m3/kg. The total air flow rate reaches 153,646 N m3/h. For fuel nitrogen, the heterogeneous conversion ratio is 0.34%, while the homogeneous conversion rate is 0.66%. Additionally, urea served as the reducing agent. It is mixed with water at a 35%:65% mass ratio to form an aqueous solution, which is introduced as atomized droplets.

Results and Discussion

Effect of Secondary Air Velocity Ratio on Combustion Efficiency

In the combustion process of a waste incineration furnace, the ratio of primary air to secondary air is one of the key factors affecting the combustion efficiency and stability. Numerous studies have shown that the optimal air velocity ratio is 0.65:0.35 for primary-to-secondary air. Based on this, this study focuses on a 900 t/day waste incineration furnace, analyzing the impact of primary and secondary air on the combustion performance while adopting the optimal air velocity ratio. Specifically, the study conducts numerical simulations for five different secondary air velocity ratios, where SA1 represents the secondary air from the front wall and SA2 represents the secondary air from the rear wall. Effect of Secondary Air Velocity Ratio on Flow Field and Temperature Field

As shown in Figure (a), the five operating conditions each undergo two combustion stages at the primary and secondary air inlets with the first flue. Due to incomplete combustion at the primary air inlet and the introduction of secondary air, the temperature in the first flue significantly decreases in the height range of 3500–6500 mm. The introduction of secondary air at y = 6500 mm promotes the reignition of combustible gases, causing the temperature to rise rapidly and reach a peak at y = 9000 mm before decreasing. The temperatures under conditions A4 and A5 are higher than those under conditions A1, A2, and A3, with an average temperature increase of 4.73%. The zoomed-in view in Figure (a) shows that the heights of the highest temperature points in the primary air combustion zone vary: for A1 and A2 conditions, they are located at y = 5500 mm and y = 4500 mm, respectively, while for A3, A4, and A5 conditions, they are consistently located at y = 4000 mm. Figure (b) further demonstrates that the combustion reaction rates at the primary air combustion stage and y = 6000 mm are higher for A4 and A5 than for the other conditions, indicating that adjusting the secondary air flow rate can enhance the completeness of primary air combustion and reduce combustion instability in the postsecondary air stage. Therefore, conditions A4 and A5 exhibit better thermal uniformity during the burnout phase.

(a) Temperature field distribution in the first flue of the combustion furnace. (b) Temperature uniformity at different elevations.

As shown in Figure (b), the region below 6500 mm is characterized by continuous single-flame combustion, while the region above 6500 mm is dominated by separated dual-flame combustion. In general, the temperature uniformity eq­() in the dual-flame region is superior to that in the single-flame region. Within the single-flame combustion zone, the temperature uniformity for all five operating conditions is similar, with a fluctuating downward trend. However, in the dual-flame combustion zone, temperature uniformity improves with increasing height. Compared to conditions A1, A2, and A3, conditions A4 and A5 exhibit a more uniform temperature distribution across the region. Notably, above y = 10000 mm, there is a significant difference between the temperature distributions of conditions A1, A2, A3, A4, and A5. Conditions A1, A2, and A3 show large fluctuations around y = 8000 mm and y = 14000 mm, indicating instability in their combustion process. By adjusting the secondary air flow rate, conditions A4 and A5 demonstrate a more stable combustion state and exhibit better temperature uniformity across the cross-section of the first flue, with smaller fluctuations in the average temperature.

As shown in Figure (a), the temperature isosurface of 1400 K within the furnace and the first flue reveals the flame morphology under various operating conditions. Under the influence of secondary air separation, the flames exhibit a helical upward shape. The main differences between the operating conditions lie in the fact that for the A1, A2, and A3 cases, the flame on the left side is lifted, while the flame on the right side is suppressed, resulting in an asymmetric phenomenon with differing flame sizes on both sides. This asymmetry is closely related to the instability of the combustion process. In contrast, the A4 and A5 cases do not exhibit such asymmetry, with both sides of the flame having a similar size and shape, indicating a more stable combustion process. Figure (b) shows the flow field distribution on the cross-section of the furnace’s first flue. The combustible gases, after combustion, rise along the trend of the flame with a velocity higher than that of the surrounding gases. Since the viscosity of high-temperature gases is lower than that of low-temperature gases, this creates a velocity gradient across the boundary layer between the high- and low-temperature gases, which eventually develops into vortices. Specifically, in the A1 case, two vortices are formed on both sides, while in A2 and A3, a single vortex is formed on the right side. In contrast, the A4 and A5 cases do not show any vortices. The vortices in the A1, A2, and A3 cases are located above the flame, affecting the flow direction of the high-temperature flue gases and leading to a “U” shaped high-temperature region displaced to one side, a phenomenon that has been theoretically validated by Yang T and other scholars. , The strength of the vortices is significantly influenced by the gas viscosity, with high-viscosity gases effectively suppressing vortex formation, as shown in Figure (c). Figure illustrates the turbulent viscosity of the gas between the two flames, with the results indicating that after the combustible gases are consumed, the flue gas viscosity in the A4 and A5 cases is generally higher than in the other cases. The A1 case has the lowest gas viscosity and forms two vortices, while the viscosities of A2 and A3 are similar and form a single vortex.

To further investigate the differences between the two operating conditions, this study selected Condition A2, which exhibited the poorest temperature uniformity, for comparison with Condition A4, which demonstrated the best temperature uniformity. As shown in Figure (a), during the temperature diffusion process, Condition A2 eventually resulted in the formation of a high-temperature flue gas plume accumulating on the left side of the incinerator flue duct, whereas no such accumulation phenomenon was observed under Condition A4. Notably, in Condition A4 (see Figure (b)), the volume expansion of the accumulated high-temperature flue gas caused it to approach closer to the water-cooled wall surface. Examination of the 1200 K isothermal surface revealed that the contact area between the high-temperature flue gas and the wall was significantly larger for Condition A4 compared to Condition A2.

Structural morphology of different temperature fields in the flue of the incinerator (a) A2 and (b) A4 working conditions.

A comprehensive analysis indicates that the wall-fouling phenomenon of high-temperature flue gas in the primary flue duct of the incinerator is influenced by three key factors. First, the accumulation of high-temperature flue gas leads to expansion in volume, bringing it closer to the furnace wall. Second, the distribution pattern of secondary air on the front and rear walls affects the trajectory of the flame flow; variations in air velocity cause the flame to deflect toward the wall, resulting in high-temperature wall fouling. Third, since combustion of the combustible gases primarily occurs in the front arch region of the furnace, the propulsive force generated during this process pushes the flame forward. This exacerbates the tendency of the high-temperature flue gas to adhere to the wall, as illustrated in Figure (a).

(a) 1400 K temperature isosurfaces, (b) distribution of temperature values at the back wall of the first flue for each operating condition, and (c) temperature field plot.

Figure (b) and (c) shows how temperature regions and the temperature field are distributed in the rear wall under different operating conditions. As the secondary air velocity increases, the area of the high-temperature regions on the rear wall decreases significantly. For operating conditions A1, A2, and A3, the temperature distribution fluctuates between 950 and 1,300 K, with a relatively high proportion of high-temperature regions. By contrast, for conditions A4 and A5, temperatures predominantly range between 950 and 1100 K, with the area of temperatures exceeding 1200 K decreasing by an average of 44.18%. Adjusting the secondary air velocity on the front and rear walls helps to reduce the risk of high-temperature corrosion on the water-cooled walls.

The particle movement trajectory of the urea solution can be observed in Figure . Under the A1 condition, the particles exhibit upward movement in the center while moving downward on both sides. Under the A2 and A3 conditions, the particles move upward on the left side, whereas on the right side, they move downward. Under the A4 and A5 conditions, the particles move uniformly upward. Notably, under the A5 condition, due to the more concentrated flue gas after separation compared to A4, the upward velocity is higher, and the upward movement of the urea solution particles on both sides of the duct is slightly greater than under the A4 condition. The movement of the urea solution particles is significantly influenced by the flue gas flow. The gas flow field distribution shown in Figure (b) indicates that large vortex regions exist on both sides under the A1 condition, causing the urea particles to flow downward in these areas. Under A2 and A3 conditions, the particles are primarily affected by the vortex on the right side. For the A4 and A5 conditions, due to the absence of vortices, the urea particles’ movement is not influenced by such flow fields.

Trajectory of urea solution particles and distribution of insufficiently mixed ammonia gas.

The urea solution decomposes into ammonia gas in the flame and subsequently diffuses. By observation of the isosurface at a mass fraction of ammonia gas (1.5e-4) in Figure , it is evident that, at this ammonia concentration, the distribution range of ammonia under conditions A1, A2, and A3 is significantly larger than that under A4 and A5. Moreover, within the larger distribution range, the ammonia concentration is higher but this ammonia does not effectively mix and react with the NOx in the flue gas, as confirmed in Figure . Further analysis of the ammonia concentration distribution in the first flue, which does not react with NOx, reveals that under conditions A1, A2, and A3, a large amount of ammonia is concentrated in the region where the urea solution particles move downward. In contrast, under conditions A4 and A5, ammonia is mainly distributed along the wall opposite the urea injection and the injection path. The trajectory of the urea solution particles is closely related to the flow state of the combustion flue gas. As shown in Figure (a), under conditions A1, A2, and A3, the downward motion of the urea solution particles is primarily influenced by vortices. Due to the lower NOx concentration in the vortex zone, the urea fails to fully mix with the flue gas in this region. In comparison, the ammonia concentration under conditions A4 and A5 is lower, with better mixing between ammonia and flue gas. Furthermore, Figure (b) presents the ammonia concentration distribution in the second and third flues. The results show that under conditions A1, A2, and A3, the ammonia escape concentration at the third flue exit is greater than 8 ppm, whereas under conditions A4 and A5, it is below 8 ppm. In conclusion, due to the uneven distribution of flue gas in the flue under conditions A1, A2, and A3, high-temperature flue gas creates vortices that affect the adequate mixing of the injected urea solution with the flue gas. This factor leads to a significantly lower SNCR denitrification efficiency and ammonia slip control capability under conditions A1, A2, and A3 compared to conditions A4 and A5.

Conclusion

This study conducts a detailed analysis of the secondary air distribution at the front and rear walls of a 900 t/day large-capacity waste incinerator under different air distribution ratios. It investigates the combustion flame characteristics, temperature field distribution, uniformity changes, and variation in the distance between high-temperature zones and the water-cooled wall surfaces. Under fixed incident angles, by optimizing the secondary air velocity, the following conclusions are drawn:(1)Improved combustion efficiency and temperature uniformity: Optimizing the secondary air distribution and velocity increased the combustion efficiency of combustible gases in the primary air region by 25.85%. This improvement not only enhanced the stability of the secondary air flow field but also significantly improved the temperature uniformity in the burnout region, ensuring a more efficient and stable combustion process.(2)Flame separation and temperature rise: Adjusting the secondary air distribution ratio effectively increased the degree of flame separation, resulting in a 4.73% increase in the overall furnace temperature. Furthermore, optimizing the secondary air flow field helped prevent the aggregation of high-temperature flue gases, thereby enhancing flame stability and reducing localized overheating.(3)Flame shift reduction and wall temperature decrease: By adjusting the secondary air velocity, the flame shift was effectively controlled, reducing the contact between the flame and the furnace wall. As a result, the area of high-temperature zones (above 1200 K) on the wall decreased by 44.18%, effectively reducing the thermal load on the walls and extending the service life of the water-cooled walls.
