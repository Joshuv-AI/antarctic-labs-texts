Pathways to agricultural carbon sequestration and emission reduction and food security synergy in Hunan China
Authors: Chenxi Dou, Xianzhao Liu, Hai Xiao, Jiaxi Liu, Yue Xing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryUnder intensifying climate warming and rising food demand, balancing low-carbon agricultural transformation with food security is urgent for China’s carbon peaking and carbon neutrality goals. Focusing on Hunan Province, a major grain-producing region, this study measures coordination between carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural cultivation and food security across prefecture-level cities from 2000 to 2023 using an improved coupling coordination degree model, and identifies driving pathways through a partial least squares structural equation model incorporating temporal and spatial lag effects. Coordination showed early heterogeneity but rose overall after 2011, with high-value areas shifting from scattered points to contiguous clusters. Agricultural policy was the strongest driver, acting directly and through economic benefits, technological improvement, and production conditions. Spatial spillover effects remained significant but weakened over time. These findings support differentiated local policies that jointly advance agricultural emission reduction, carbon sequestration, and food security.

Introduction

Global warming and food security crisis have emerged as pressing challenges facing humanity in the 21st century. These interacting pressures impose dual demands on agricultural cultivation, requiring both enhanced carbon sequestration and emission reduction as well as increased food production. As the world’s largest carbon emitter and food producer, China’s long-term grain output growth has been supported by traditional extensive production systems. With accelerating resource depletion and rising greenhouse gas emission, conventional input-intensive farming models can no longer meet the growing demand for sustained yield increases under the “dual carbon” targets. Advancing carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural cultivation has become an urgent task. In 2023, China’s agricultural carbon emission reached 8.28 × 108 tons, while agricultural carbon sequestration reached 7.86 × 108 tons, indicating dual potential for carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural system.1,2 Meanwhile, ensuring an adequate and stable food supply remains a top priority for safeguarding national food security and maintaining socio-economic stability. As the core component of food production, crop cultivation system transformation directly affects the stability and sustainability of food supply. Elucidating the spatiotemporal variations and driving mechanisms underlying the synergistic development of carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural cultivation and food security is critical for addressing tensions between agricultural carbon sequestration, emission reduction, and food security.

Carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural cultivation are not entirely incompatible with food security; instead, they involve complex dynamic interactions and synergistic potential.3,4 Some studies indicate that emission reduction measures, such as reduced agricultural inputs and fallow-crop rotation may lead to short-term declines in grain production, weakening the synergy between emission reduction and yield improvement.5,6 Other studies suggest that practices such as straw return, conservation tillage, soil testing, formula fertilization, and policy incentives, agricultural systems can achieve simultaneous gains in carbon sequestration, emission reduction, and food security under certain conditions.7,8,9,10 These studies provide policy guidance and technical support for the coordinated advancement of the two goals, practical challenges remain. Some regions rely excessively on agricultural inputs to pursue short-term grain yield increases, resulting in soil carbon stock degradation and a sharp rise in greenhouse gas emission. Some emission reduction measures remain hard for farmers to adopt widely because of technological immaturity or low economic viability, which hinders the achievement of carbon sequestration, emission reduction, and food security goals.

Currently, few studies have directly examined the drivers of synergistic development between carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural cultivation and food security, as existing research mainly focuses on individual dimensions of these issues. In studies on agricultural carbon sequestration and emission reduction, Gangopadhyay et al.11 found that fertilization is a key factor contributing to increased carbon emission from rice cultivation. Zhang et al.12 showed that agricultural subsidy policies can significantly curb carbon emission. Bennetzen et al.13 noted that agricultural input use, technological innovation, and agricultural product prices affect carbon emission by shaping farmers’ production decisions. In food security research, Mohamed et al.14 found that economic development, cultivated land area, and agricultural exports positively affect food security, whereas increases in the rural population and agricultural labor force may have inhibitory effects. Xue et al.15 identified socioeconomic factors and agricultural technological progress as important drivers of grain production growth. Some scholars have incorporated agricultural carbon emission efficiency and food security into a unified analytical framework to indirectly examine the synergy between agricultural carbon sequestration and emission reduction and food security.16,17,18,19,20,21 Their findings indicate that the coupling coordination level between agricultural carbon emission efficiency and food security in China’s provinces or major grain-producing areas has generally increased, although pronounced regional disparities, spatial spillover effects, and polarization characteristics remain.19,20,21,22 The effects of agricultural economic development, farmland transfer, rural human capital, and agricultural disasters on the synergy level exhibit heterogeneity.20

Studies on synergistic pathways have mainly addressed three dimensions: agricultural policy (AP), technological improvement (TI), and cropland management. From the AP perspective, Lal23 described the role of soil carbon sequestration in mitigating climate change and enhancing food security, and recommended payments for ecosystem services to incentivize carbon emission reduction. Chen et al.24 proposed that reducing agricultural input use, developing high-standard farmland, and optimizing the agricultural industrial structure are key pathways for achieving synergistic development. Liu et al.25 indicated that digital inclusive finance can promote agricultural carbon reduction and secure food supply. Kabato et al.26 and Wang et al.27 showed that resource-use efficiency and soil quality enhancement can reduce carbon emission while improving food security. Jiang et al.28 and Kamyab et al.29 noted that green-synthesized nanoparticles can substitute for conventional agricultural inputs and reduce their use. Sun et al.30 simulated smart-agriculture priority scenarios under different climate conditions and proposed a technology-environment-incentive pathway for coordinating agricultural carbon emission reduction with grain yield improvement. Cropland management studies show that the effects of conservation tillage on soil carbon sinks and crop yields vary across climate zones, with Zhang et al.31 emphasizing that locally adapted technology-matching strategies can promote synergy between these goals. Measures such as straw return, soil organic carbon enhancement, and diversified crop rotation have been shown to promote carbon sequestration, emission reduction, and grain production growth.32,33,34

As a major grain-producing region in China, Hunan Province has a representative rice-dominated agricultural cultivation structure. In recent years, tensions between carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural cultivation and food security, along with the pressure for transformation, are increasingly pronounced.35 Existing studies have mainly examined agricultural carbon budget accounting in Hunan Province,35,36,37 carbon compensation potential,35,38 and the effects of cultivated land-use transition on carbon emission,38,39 but few have systematically integrated carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural cultivation with food security within a unified framework. Thus, whether carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural cultivation and food security develop synergistically across Hunan’s prefecture-level cities, how this synergy evolves, and how policy, economic, technological, and production conditions (PCs) affect synergistic development remain urgent scientific questions.

Although existing studies have laid an important foundation for examining the relationship between carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural cultivation and food security, several limitations remain.3 First, the framework for synergy analysis requires refinement. Most studies focus on single dimensions, such as agricultural carbon emission, carbon sequestration enhancement, or food security, and direct measurement and empirical analysis of their synergy are limited. Second, the driving mechanisms are insufficiently elucidated. Common methods, including logarithmic mean divisia index (LMDI) decomposition, the Geodetector model, and STIRPAT, are effective in identifying the direct effects of influencing factors but have limited capacity to reveal indirect pathways and chain transmission among policy, economic, technological, and PCs.30 Third, spatiotemporal heterogeneity and dynamic mechanisms remain inadequately characterized, limiting the ability to reveal spatial differences, spatial spillovers, and time-lagged driving effects in synergistic development at the municipal scale.34,40

This study uses Hunan Province as a case to examine the synergistic development between carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural cultivation and food security from the perspectives of spatiotemporal change and pathway mechanisms. An improved coupling coordination degree (CCD) model measures the synergy level between carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural cultivation and food security across prefecture-level cities in Hunan Province from 2000 to 2023. A partial least squares structural equation model (PLS-SEM) incorporating spatiotemporal lag effects analyzes the driving effects and transmission pathways through which AP, economic benefits (EBs), PCs, TI, natural conditions (NCs), and spatial lag (SL) affect CCD. Policy recommendations are proposed based on the identified spatiotemporal patterns and driving pathways.

Compared with existing studies, the marginal contributions of this study makes three main contributions: (1) it characterizes the spatiotemporal evolution and driving pathways of the coordinated development of agricultural carbon sequestration, emission reduction, and food security across Hunan’s prefecture-level cities from 2000 to 2023, addressing the limitation of studies that have focused mainly on carbon emission or food production alone; (2) by integrating an improved CCD model with PLS-SEM incorporating spatiotemporal lag effects, this study improves the stability and discriminative power of coordination measurement and reveals the chain transmission mechanisms among CCD drivers and their dynamic changes; and (3) this study reveals the core characteristics of the coordinated advancement of agricultural low-carbon transformation and food security by examining regional heterogeneity, policy lags, and spatial spillover effects, providing an empirical basis for policy optimization in Hunan Province and similar major grain-producing regions.

Hunan Province is located between 24°38′–30°08′N and 108°47′–114°15′E (Figure 1). As one of China’s major grain-producing regions, it is characterized by a subtropical humid monsoon climate, with abundant sunlight, ample precipitation, and coincident rainfall and heat providing highly favorable NCs for crop growth. In 2020, Hunan’s agricultural carbon emission reached 7.94 × 107t, whereas carbon sequestration amounted to only 3.64 × 107t, highlighting the need to strengthen carbon sequestration and emission reduction.35 In 2024, Hunan’s grain-sown area remained stable at 4.67 × 106 ha, with a total grain output exceeding 2.68 × 1010 kg. With only 2.8% of China’s arable land, Hunan produces 4.4% of the nation’s grain, playing a pivotal role in safeguarding national food security. Therefore, examining the spatiotemporal evolution and driving pathways of the synergistic development between agricultural carbon sequestration, emission reduction, and food security in Hunan Province is of considerable practical significance. The province is divided into five regions based on NCs and administrative divisions: northern Hunan (Changde, Yiyang, and Yueyang), eastern Hunan (Changsha, Zhuzhou, and Xiangtan), southern Hunan (Hengyang, Yongzhou, and Chenzhou), central Hunan (Shaoyang and Loudi), and western Hunan (Zhangjiajie, Xiangxi Prefecture, and Huaihua).Figure 1Schematic diagram of the study area

Results

Model assumptions and construction

The concept of CCD and comparative analysis of models

This study uses the CCD model to measure the coordinated development level between carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural cultivation and food security. Carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural cultivation refers to the process by which carbon fixed by crops and soils offsets carbon emission from agricultural input use, crop growth, and soil respiration. It is represented by the carbon sequestration and emission reduction index (CSERI), which reflects the balance between carbon sequestration and carbon emission in agricultural cultivation systems. Food security refers to a condition in which all people can access sufficient, safe, and nutritious food at all times at physical, economic, and social levels, meeting their dietary needs and preferences for a healthy life. It is represented by the food security index (FSI), which reflects food production, supply-demand conditions, and sustainability. Specific indicator calculations and parameter settings are provided in STAR Methods.

Hunan’s prefecture-level cities differ markedly in resource endowments, grain production, agricultural input use, and low-carbon transition levels.35 Some areas may have a high FSI but a low CSERI, whereas others may show improved CSERI but low FSI. The traditional CCD model is widely used to evaluate coordinated development among multiple systems, but its coupling-degree calculation may yield high coupling values even when system values differ substantially. The model is sensitive to low and extreme values, which may produce zero-valued measurement results. This study addresses these limitations by adopting the improved CCD model proposed by Fan et al.41 and revising the traditional coupling-degree calculation formula.

To further assess the validity and robustness of the improved CCD model, by recalculating the synergy levels of 336 samples with the traditional CCD model. The two models produce broadly consistent trends in sample synergy levels (Figure 2), indicating that the improved model reliably identifies the direction of synergistic evolution. The traditional CCD model yields two extreme zero values, whereas the improved model produces no extreme values and has a minimum value substantially higher than that of the traditional model. This result suggests that the improved model is more stable in identifying low-value and threshold ranges, helping capture changes in the synergy level between carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural cultivation and food security in Hunan Province (Table 1). The improved CCD model retains the traditional model’s ability to identify overall trends while enhancing robustness in boundary-value handling, low-value differentiation, and synergy-level discrimination. Theoretical hypotheses

Based on the characteristics of agricultural cultivation systems and existing study findings, this study selects six latent variables: APs,23,24 EBs,30 PCs,32,33 TIs,21,24 NCs,42 and SL. Furthermore, to account for potential time-lag effects in the CCD driving mechanisms of synergistic development, the following research hypotheses are proposed.

H1: AP has a positive effect on the current/one-period lagged/two-period lagged CCD.

H1a: AP boosting EB promotes current/one-period lagged/two-period lagged CCD.

H1b: AP driving TI can enhance current/one-period lagged/two-period lagged CCD.

H2: EB enhancement exerts a positive influence on the current/one-period lagged/two-period lagged CCD.

H2a: EB enhancement driving PC improvement can promote current/one-period lagged/two-period lagged CCD.

H2b: EB enhancement promotes TI drive for current/one-period lagged/two-period lagged CCD.

H3: PC enhancement exerts a positive influence on the current/one-period lagged/two-period lagged CCD.

H4: TI has a positive impact on the current/one-period lagged/two-period lagged CCD.

H4a: PC enhancement under TI can promote CCD in the current/one-period lagged/two-periods lagged.

H5: NC has a positive effect on the current/one-period lagged/two-period lagged CCD.

H5a: NC drives the current/one-period lagged/two-period lagged CCD by influencing PC.

H6: the current/one-period lagged/two-period lagged CCD of a city has a positive driving effect on the current CCD of surrounding cities.

Indicator selection

Based on the principles of data availability and representativeness, this study selects three indicators for the AP dimension: fiscal budget expenditure (X1), the proportion of the rural labor force (X2), and agricultural research and development (R&D) investment (X3). The rationale for selecting these indicators is as follows: fiscal budget expenditure and agricultural R&D investment play crucial roles in promoting agricultural carbon sequestration, emission reduction, and TIs for enhancing grain yield, thereby directly reflecting AP efforts. Labor, as the executor of policies, converts policy dividends into synergistic driving forces in actual agricultural production. The EB dimension primarily considers income and output, represented by farmers’ grain production income (X4) and the per capita value of agricultural output (X5). The PC dimension is measured by the proportion of high-standard farmland construction (X6), the stability of cultivated areas (X7), the effective irrigation rate of arable land (X8), the grain disaster rate (X9), and the intensity of fertilizer and pesticide use (X10). For the TI dimension, following previous studies,43,44 the indicators include the level of agricultural mechanization (X11), proportion of soil testing and formulated fertilization (X12), energy use efficiency (X13), and grain yield per unit area (X14). The NC is characterized by air temperature (X15) and precipitation (X16). The SL is constructed from a geographic inverse-distance matrix and a vector matrix of endogenous latent variable scores, and its coefficient (Log-C) is used to represent the strength of spatial interactions in coordinated development among municipalities. Finally, SPSS 27 was used to preprocess all indicator data, including outlier removal, data standardization, and stationarity testing, as well as to perform multicollinearity diagnostics. The results showed that all indicators passed the ADF stationarity test (p < 0.05). In addition, the VIF values of all indicator variables were below 3, indicating no significant multicollinearity; therefore, the sample data generally satisfied the requirements for subsequent modeling. Time-lag order selection and model validation

Time-lag order selection

To avoid subjectivity in lag-order selection, this study examines the model’s lag order. Given the short time dimension of the sample and the large number of explanatory variables, using a panel vector autoregression (PVAR) model to determine the lag order may lead to parameter proliferation, insufficient degrees of freedom, and unstable estimation results. Therefore, this study first applies the within transformation to remove individual fixed effects from the data, then performs ordinary least squares (OLS) estimation, and subsequently comparison of information criteria across lag orders. As reported in Table 3, the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) and the Hannan-Quinn information criterion (HQIC) favor the one-lag model, whereas the Akaike information criterion (AIC) prefers the four-lag model. Considering that BIC and HQIC impose stronger penalties on parameter complexity in high-dimensional samples and are more consistent with model parsimony, the current-period model and the one-lag model are selected as the baseline specifications. To test the sensitivity of the main findings to lag-order selection and identify potential short- and medium-term cumulative effects, this study also reports the estimation results of the two-lag model. Internal consistency reliability was evaluated using Cronbach’s α, rho_α, and composite reliability (CR). Spatiotemporal variations of CCD

Based on the improved CCD model (for detailed calculation methods, please refer to Tables 6, 7, 8, and the STAR Methods section), this study measured coordinated development between carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural cultivation and food security across city-level regions of Hunan Province from 2000 to 2023. ArcGIS 10.8 visualized the CCD values, which were classified into six levels according to Fan et al.41: severe imbalance (0,0.389), moderate imbalance (0.389,0.483), mild imbalance (0.483,0.533), mild coordination (0.533,0.587), moderate coordination (0.587,0.714), and high coordination (0.714,1). Figure 4 shows two temporal CCD patterns: fluctuating change (Figure 4A) and U-shaped change (Figure 4B). The fluctuating-change type includes Changde, Yueyang, Yiyang, Changsha, Chenzhou, Xiangxi Prefecture, and Zhangjiajie, these regions showed slight early-stage fluctuations followed by gradual increases. The U-shaped type includes Zhuzhou, Xiangtan, Shaoyang, Loudi, Hengyang, Yongzhou, and Huaihua, characterized by an initial decline followed by an increase, with turning points in 2010 or 2011. After 2011, CCD values increased in most city-level regions except Xiangxi Prefecture, and increases were generally greater in U-shaped regions than in fluctuating-change regions. In U-shaped regions, CCD values in Zhuzhou, Xiangtan, and Yongzhou increased from 0.249, 0.341, and 0.395 in 2011 to 0.851, 0.726, and 0.761 in 2023, whereas Shaoyang, Loudi, Hengyang, and Huaihua showed more moderate rebounds. In fluctuating-change regions, CCD values in Changsha and Yiyang increased from 0.511 and 0.516 in 2011 to 0.736 and 0.730 in 2023; Zhangjiajie, Changde, Chenzhou, and Yueyang also followed upward trends, but their increases were limited. Economic benefits

Figure 7 shows that, in the current-period model, the total effect of EB on CCD was 0.319 (p < 0.01), with a direct effect of 0.171 and an indirect effect of 0.148. EB promoted CCD directly and indirectly through mediating variables. The path coefficient from EB to TI was 0.521 (p < 0.001), suggesting that increased EB promoted TI and thereby affected CCD. The EB→CCD effect increased from 0.319 in the current-period model to 0.388 (p < 0.01) in the one-period lag model and 0.467 (p < 0.01) in the two-period lag model, indicating strong temporal accumulation mainly driven by the strengthened direct effect. Table 9 shows that the EB→TI→PC effects were 0.101, 0.106, and 0.107 across the three models, all significant. The EB→PC→CCD and EB→TI→CCD coefficients decreased in the one-period lag model and rebounded in the two-period lag model. The EB→TI→PC→CCD coefficient remained 0.049 across the three models, indicating strong path stability. Hypotheses H2, H2a, and H2b are therefore supported.

Production conditions

Figure 6 shows that, in the current-period model, the total effect of PC on CCD was 0.486 (p < 0.01) mainly through the direct path, indicating that PC was an important explanatory variable of CCD. PC mediated of the effects of several latent variables on CCD: the indirect effect through PC accounted for 72% of NC’s total effect on CCD and 43% of TI’s total effect. Figures 6 and 7 show that the PC→CCD path coefficient decreased from 0.486 in the current-period model to 0.475 (p < 0.01) in the one-period lag model and 0.453 (p < 0.01) in the two-period lag model, but remained significant across the three models. Hypothesis H3 is supported.

Technical improvements

Figure 7 shows that, in the current-period model, TI had a direct effect of 0.123 on CCD (p < 0.01), indicating that TI directly promoted CCD. The indirect effect along TI→PC→CCD was 0.094 (p < 0.01), suggesting that TI also enhanced coordinated development between carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural cultivation and food security by improving PC. In the lagged models, the direct effect of TI on CCD decreased and then increased, decreased from 0.123 in the current-period model to 0.084 (p < 0.05) in the one-period lag model and then increased to 0.110 (p < 0.05) in the two-period lag model. This pattern indicates that TI affected CCD directly and indirectly through PC. The TI→PC→CCD path coefficients were 0.094, 0.097, and 0.096 in the current-period model, one-period lag model, and two-period lag model, all significant, confirming stable mediation. Hypotheses H4 and H4a are supported.

Natural conditions

Figure 7 shows that, in the current-period model, the total effect of NC on CCD was 0.340 (p < 0.01), comprising a direct effect of 0.095 and an indirect effect of 0.245. Compared with anthropogenic factors such as AP and PC, NC contributed less directly to CCD, and its effect was mainly mediated by PC. The direct effect of NC on CCD decreased from 0.095 in the current-period model to 0.045 in the two-period lag model, indicating that its direct influence weakened over time. The indirect effects along NC→PC→CCD were 0.235, 0.238, and 0.229 in the current-period, one-period lag, and two-period lag models, all significant, indicating that PC played a stable mediating role in the effect of NC on CCD. Hypotheses H5 and H5a are supported.

Spatial spillover

SL was used to identify spatial dependence and spillover effects in CCD values across city-level regions of Hunan Province. Figure 6 shows that the path coefficient from SL to CCD was 0.605 (p < 0.001) in the current-period model, 0.534 (p < 0.001) in the one-period lag model, and 0.477 (p < 0.001) in the two-period lag model. These results indicate a significant positive spatial association in CCD across city-level regions of Hunan Province. The spatial effect remained significant in the lagged models, but its path coefficient decreased as the lag length increased. Hypothesis H6 is supported.

Discussion

Spatiotemporal variations in CCD

The coordinated development between carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural cultivation and food security in Hunan Province showed stage-specific evolution from 2000 to 2023, with 2011 as a key turning point. From 2000 to 2011, Hunan Province promoted clean and low-carbon technologies and implemented rural clean-up projects, but relevant policies and technical measures still focused mainly on increasing grain output and improving unit yield. Agricultural production had not yet undergone a systematic green transition. During this stage, the Dongting Lake Plain, including Changde, Yueyang, Yiyang, and Changsha, served as a traditional commercial grain production base. Increased agricultural inputs improved production capacity but intensified pressure from agricultural carbon emission, leading to unstable fluctuations in the coordination between carbon sequestration and emission reduction and food security. In parts of central and eastern Hunan with high industrialization levels, construction land expansion and agricultural production contraction reduced cultivated land, weakening grain output and agricultural carbon sequestration capacity. In Hengyang and Shaoyang, long-term reliance on high-input and high-consumption cultivation patterns imposed high resource and environmental costs on grain production growth, leading to a decline in CCD. In the mountainous and hilly areas of western Hunan, fragmented cultivated land, topographic constraints, and limited large-scale grain production slowed improvements in grain production capacity and carbon sink capacity, causing CCD to remain low or fluctuate.

Agricultural development policy in Hunan Province shifted during 2011–2023 from an emphasis on output growth to a balanced focus on stable grain production, resource conservation, environmental governance, and green efficiency improvement. Policy documents such as the Implementation Opinions on Accelerating the Transformation of Agricultural Development Modes, the Implementation Opinions on Innovating Institutions and Mechanisms to Promote Green Agricultural Development, and the 14th Five-Year Plan for Agricultural and Rural Modernization in Hunan Province, incorporated agricultural green transformation and stable grain supply into a unified policy framework. These policies improved agricultural PCs by reducing fertilizer and pesticide use while improving efficiency, soil testing and formula fertilization, constructing high-standard farmland, and extending agricultural technologies.51 Policy support and technology diffusion significantly increased CCD in most city-level regions, indicating that coordinated development between carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural cultivation and food security can be achieved through institutional arrangements and optimized production modes.

The improvement in CCD showed marked spatial heterogeneity. High-value clusters in northern, eastern, and southern Hunan gradually formed after 2011. In northern Hunan, the Dongting Lake grain-producing area and high-standard farmland construction helped slow the growth of agricultural carbon emission while stabilizing grain production. Cities in eastern and central Hunan benefited from high levels of industrialization, urbanization, and fiscal investment, which supported agricultural technological innovation, infrastructure improvement, and green production promotion. Southern Hunan relied on favorable agricultural resource endowments and grain production functional zones to promote cropping-structure optimization and low-carbon technology diffusion. CCD remained low in Loudi and Xiangxi Prefecture, possibly because of fragmented cultivated land, small-scale agricultural production, weak infrastructure, and insufficient momentum for low-carbon transformation. In Xiangxi Prefecture, specialty crops such as tea, fruits, and vegetables account for a high share of the agricultural structure, whereas grain production accounts for a limited share, which may weaken coordinated development between carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural cultivation and food security.

The stage-specific characteristics of coordinated development between carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural cultivation and food security in Hunan Province align with previous findings on major grain-producing regions in China. This consistency suggests that the two dimensions are not necessarily in opposition; coordinated development can be achieved through policy guidance, technological progress, and improved PCs.20,21,30 Unlike national- or interprovincial-scale studies emphasizing overall coordination trends and macro-level spatial clustering,21,26 this study shows that city-level regions may follow different evolutionary pathways under the same provincial policy framework because of differences in resource endowments, cultivated land conditions, technology diffusion capacity, and policy implementation intensity. These differences indicate that coordinated development between carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural cultivation and food security is not a linear process but shows stage-specific characteristics and regional path dependence.

The multi-factor driving effect of CCD

This study analyzes the multifactorial mechanisms driving coordinated development between carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural cultivation and food security. Previous studies have mainly focused on the individual effects of policy support, economic benefits, technological progress, or natural endowments, whereas this study shows that CCD across city-level regions in Hunan Province is not driven by a single factor. AP, EB, TI, PC, NC, and SL jointly shape a dynamic coordination mechanism through multistep mediation, time-lagged transmission, and spatial spillovers.

AP was the core driver of CCD, with total effects increasing by lag order and direct and indirect effects strengthening in parallel. AP significantly promoted coordinated development between carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural cultivation and food security and generated spatial spillover effects, consistent with previous findings that agricultural subsidies and green incentive policies support carbon reduction, emission mitigation, and food security.12,21,23 AP promoted CCD in the short term and generated long-term multistep transmission effects through EB, TI, and PC, suggesting a pronounced time-lag pattern. AP did not improve PC directly; its effect was released mainly through multistep mediating pathways, including AP→TI→PC and AP→EB→TI→PC. TI played a key mediating role in the AP–PC relationship, indicating that policy support translated into TI and diffusion can improve agricultural PCs and promote coordinated development. The nonsignificant AP→PC path suggests that policy inputs do not directly improve agricultural PCs; their effects emerge after capital allocation, technological R&D, farmer adoption, and production-planning adjustments.44 The AP→TI→CCD, AP→EB→TI→CCD, and AP→EB→TI→PC→CCD pathways declined at lag 1 but rebounded at lag 2, suggesting asynchronous transmission among AP, EB, TI, and PC.52 The AP→EB→PC→CCD pathway was significant only in the current-period model, and its path coefficient decreased with longer lags, indicating that relying solely on EB to improve PC may be insufficient to generate sustained coordination effects. Long-term impacts require continuous optimization through TI and PC.

EB and TI are key drivers of coordinated development between carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural cultivation and food security, consistent with the findings of Sun et al. and Kaplan et al. that improving agricultural economic returns and upgrading agricultural technologies contribute to coordinated emission reduction and stable production.30,53 Their driving mechanisms differ. EB exerts a sustained direct effect on CCD, whereas its effects through TI and PC may take longer to materialize. Low-cost green cultivation practices with rapid effects are more readily adopted by farmers when the benefits of green transformation have not fully emerged, improving coordinated development more quickly. High-technology inputs and improvements in PCs require more time and financial investment.54 When direct economic returns are stable, some farmers may become less willing to pursue technological innovation and improve PCs, weakening the indirect effect of EB on CCD.55 TI does not promote CCD independently; its effect may be realized only through optimized PCs, such as irrigation technologies, cultivated land management, and high-standard farmland construction. As TIs are implemented and contribute to PC optimization, the effect of TI on CCD shifts from short-term direct influence to long-term indirect transmission. For regions with high agricultural returns, the comparative returns from grain production and compensation for green transformation should be increased. For regions with low technology conversion efficiency, greater attention should be paid to adaptive optimization after technology extension and iterative upgrading.

PC is a key mediator in the pathways driving coordinated development between carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural cultivation and food security in Hunan Province. It transmits multidimensional effects from AP, TI, and NC and has a significant, stable effect on CCD. These results confirm that basic cultivation conditions, including cultivated land quality and irrigation security, support coordinated development.32,33 They also help explain why Loudi and Xiangxi Prefecture have long remained at low coordination levels under PC constraints.56 PC’s mediating role indicates that PCs should be optimized to ensure that policy support, technological innovation, and other drivers are translated into coordinated agricultural production performance.

The direct effect of NC on CCD is more pronounced in the short term, whereas its long-term effect is driven through PC. This suggests that agricultural cultivation systems can alleviate constraints imposed by the natural environment through TIs and infrastructure development, providing important insights for low-carbon agricultural transformation and adaptation to extreme climate change in climate-vulnerable regions.42 The changing NC→CCD path coefficient in the lagged model indicates that suitable light and temperature conditions can directly affect crop growth and influence grain yield, carbon sequestration, and emission reduction capacity, but this effect tends to weaken over time and is unlikely to generate stable long-term cumulative effects.

The effect of SL on CCD persists in the two-period lagged model but weakens as the lag increases, indicating that agricultural cultivation patterns, low-carbon technologies, and green production experience across city-level regions of Hunan Province can generate spatial spillovers through interregional technology diffusion, talent mobility, and policy learning, improving the overall level of coordinated development across the province.21 This weakening suggests that spatial spillovers do not persist indefinitely and may be constrained by geographical proximity, regional cooperation, and technological adaptability.

Overall, this study interprets the drivers of coordinated development between carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural cultivation and food security as a dynamic process jointly shaped by policy, economic, technological, production-condition, natural-condition, and spatial-linkage factors, rather than a static matching relationship between two subsystems. It reveals the time-lagged nature of policy effects, the mediating role through which TIs are translated into PCs, and the spatial spillover characteristics of coordinated development across city-level regions. The coordinated advancement of carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural cultivation and food security in Hunan Province may follow a chain-like evolutionary logic: policy guidance, economic incentives, TI, optimization of PCs, and enhancement of coordination levels. These findings enrich the theoretical explanation of coordinated development between agricultural carbon sequestration and emission reduction and food security and provide empirical evidence for understanding multidimensional coordination mechanisms in agricultural green transformation at the city-level scale.

Policy recommendations

Based on the above discussion and the practical needs of the coordinated development of agricultural carbon sequestration, emission reduction, and food security in Hunan Province, this study proposes policy recommendations at three levels: (1) given the multi-pathway effects of AP, evaluations of AP effectiveness should not be limited to short-term outputs or current-period emission reduction, but should focus on how policies generate long-term synergistic effects through improved planting returns, technology diffusion, and optimized PCs. Considering the spatial heterogeneity in coordinated development across Hunan Province, highly coordinated regions in northern, eastern, and southern Hunan should strengthen the demonstration role of policies and technologies to release agglomeration effects. In relatively lagging regions such as Loudi and Xiangxi Prefecture, differentiated policy support should be implemented according to local resource endowments and development constraints to improve the targeting and effectiveness of policy measures. (2) A long-term and systematic policy framework for green and low-carbon agriculture should also be established by integrating green subsidies, fertilizer and pesticide reduction, grain-production income support, agricultural R&D investment, and high-standard farmland construction into a unified policy system. In this way, a chain transmission mechanism of policy guidance, benefit enhancement, TI, and production-condition optimization can be formed, steadily releasing the long-term policy effect of policies on the coordinated development of carbon sequestration and emission reduction and food security. (3) A cross-regional mechanism for coordinated agricultural development should be established to promote the transfer and sharing of green cultivation technologies and production management experience across cities. Policy coordination and production-factor linkages among neighboring regions should be strengthened to expand demonstration effects and accelerate the formation of a province-wide spatial pattern in which agricultural carbon sequestration, emission reduction, and food security advance in a coordinated manner.

Limitations of the study and future prospects

Although this study identified the spatiotemporal variation and driving pathways of the coordinated development of agricultural carbon sequestration and emission reduction and food security in Hunan Province, several limitations remain: (1) the factors influencing this coordinated development are complex. Future research could refine the model by incorporating additional observed variables to improve explanatory power. (2) Although the PLS-SEM model incorporated spatiotemporal lag effects, it did not capture threshold effects or nonlinear mechanisms associated with policy intensity and technological progress. Future studies could integrate threshold models or dynamic spatial econometric models. (3) The spatial weight matrix was based mainly on inverse geographic distance and did not fully reflect intercity agricultural economic linkages, factor flows, or industrial collaboration. An improved matrix incorporating these dimensions could enhance the explanatory power and robustness of the findings.

Method details

Quantification of carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural cultivation

Carbon sequestration and emission reduction in agricultural cultivation refer to the overall capacity of an agricultural cultivation system to balance carbon absorption and carbon emission. In this study, the carbon sequestration and emission reduction index (CSERI) is employed to quantify this capacity, thereby providing an intuitive measure of both the potential and the actual effectiveness of transforming agricultural cultivation from a carbon source into a carbon sink. Existing studies indicate that carbon emission from agricultural cultivation in Hunan Province mainly comprise three components35: (1) CH4 emission generated during rice cultivation; (2) N2O emission resulting from surface soil disturbance during the cultivation of rice, wheat, and maize; and (3) carbon emission associated with agricultural input use during farming activities. Carbon absorption consists of two components: carbon absorbed from the atmosphere through photosynthesis by major grain crops and carbon sequestered in farmland soils. Accordingly, the calculation formula for CSERI is presented in Equation 1:(Equation 1)CSERIi=FS×A+∑i=1n(ci×Yi×1−rHIi)∑i=1nTi×δi×21+∑j=1nTj×δj×310+∑k=1nTk×δk×4412

In the equation, CSERIi denotes the municipal carbon sequestration and emission reduction index; ci represents the carbon absorption rate of grain crops; Yi, HIi, and r denote the economic yield, economic coefficient, and moisture content of grain crops, respectively; FS denotes the carbon sequestration rate of farmland soil, which is adopted as 0.892 t/(hm2·a−1) based on the study by Han et al.57; A represents the cultivated land area; Ti indicates the planted area of different rice types; Tj denotes the cultivated area of rice, wheat, or maize; Tk represents the application rates of various agricultural inputs; δi, δj, and δk denote the emission factors of different carbon sources; and 21, 310, and 44/12 are the conversion factors for CH4, N2O, and from C to CO2 equivalents, respectively.58 The relevant CSERI parameters are provided in Tables 6 and 7.

Food security assessment

Guided by the principles of systematicity, scientific rigor, and practical applicability, this study developed a food security evaluation framework that reflects the concept and objectives of food security across three dimensions—production security, supply–demand security, and sustainable security—and comprises 11 indicators, as shown in the Table 8.3 The entropy-weighted TOPSIS method was then employed for comprehensive assessment, from which the FSI was derived. As a multi-criteria decision-making approach, the entropy-weighted TOPSIS method uses entropy weighting to objectively assign weights based on the information entropy and degree of variation of each indicator, thereby reducing subjective bias in weighting. It then applies the TOPSIS method to calculate the distance and relative closeness of each city to the positive and negative ideal solutions, thereby deriving a composite food security score. This method has the advantages of intuitive geometric interpretation, limited information loss, and computational flexibility, and can more comprehensively reflect differences in food security levels across the study area.59 It has therefore been widely used in food security assessment. Improved CCD model

The CCD model is a core tool for quantifying the intensity of interactions and the state of coordinated development among two or more subsystems, and it can effectively reflect the level of coordinated development between agricultural carbon sequestration, emission reduction, and food security. However, the traditional CCD model suffers from problems such as conceptual confusion between coupling degree and coordination degree, unreasonable boundary-value calculations, and insufficient discrimination of results,41 making it difficult to accurately identify the synergistic dynamics among subsystems. To improve the theoretical consistency and discriminative ability of the measurement results, this study adopted the improved CCD model proposed by Fan et al.42 and revised the calculation logic of the coupling–coordination index in the traditional model. This revision enables the coupling degree to focus more specifically on inter-system interactions, improves the model’s stability in identifying boundary values, and thereby more accurately reflects the actual process of coordinated development between agricultural carbon sequestration and emission reduction and food security. PLS-SEM model incorporating spatiotemporal lag effects

PLS-SEM has the advantages of requiring only a small sample size and not relying on the assumption of normal data distribution.46 It can effectively capture the complex relationships between latent variables and observed variables, as well as the structural pathways among latent variables. However, the effects of agricultural policies, technology diffusion, and resource flows on the coordinated development of agricultural carbon sequestration and emission reduction and food security may involve temporal lags and spatial dependence, which are not accounted for in conventional PLS-SEM models. Therefore, this study introduces spatial lag and temporal lag terms to modify the PLS-SEM model. The model design is as follows:

In the equation, Xit and Yit represent the exogenous and endogenous latent variables, respectively, for spatial unit i(or j) at time t; λX0, λY0, and β0 are the intercept terms; λXk and λYl are the loading factors of the kth and lth manifest variable on Xit and Yit, respectively; xitk and yitl denote the kth and lth observed variables for unit iat time t; Kand Ldenote the corresponding numbers of manifest variables, Nrepresents the number of spatial units; εXit, εYit, and ζit are error terms; βXY is the path coefficient from Xit to Yit; θY is the spatial spillover coefficient; and ∑j=1NωijYjt represents the spatial lag term for Y.

Model 3: Based on the current-period model in Equations 14 and 15, a two-period lag term is introduced. Since the basic frameworks of the measurement and structural models are similar to those in Model 2, only the lag term in Model 2 is modified to Yit-2, while all other coefficients remain unchanged.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Unless otherwise stated, all quantitative analyses were conducted at the prefecture level for Hunan Province from 2000 to 2023, yielding 336 city year observations. Data preprocessing, including outlier screening, standardization, stationarity testing, and multicollinearity diagnostics, was performed using SPSS 27. Stationarity was assessed using the augmented Dickey Fuller test, with p < 0.05 indicating stationarity. Multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors, and VIF values below 3 indicated no serious multicollinearity.

The carbon sequestration and emission reduction index, food security index, and coupling coordination degree were calculated using the equations described in method details. Descriptive statistics were used to compare the traditional and improved coupling coordination degree models. Driving mechanisms were estimated using partial least squares structural equation modeling under current period, one period lagged, and two period lagged specifications. Model reliability, validity, and performance were evaluated using Cronbach’s α, rho_A, composite reliability, average variance extracted, R2, Q2, goodness of fit, and standardized root-mean-square residual. Direct, indirect, and total effects were reported with t values and significance levels, denoted as ∗(p < 0.05), ∗∗(p < 0.01), and ∗∗∗(p < 0.001).

Spatial processing, mapping, and visualization were performed using ArcGIS 10.8 and Origin 2024, and PLS SEM estimation was conducted using SmartPLS 3.0. Robustness was assessed by substituting core explanatory variables and adjusting the time lag order. Randomization and blinding were not applicable because this study used prefecture level statistical, meteorological, and spatial datasets rather than experimental treatments.
