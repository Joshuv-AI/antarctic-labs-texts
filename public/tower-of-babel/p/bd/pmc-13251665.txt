Cystic fibrosis and sickle cell disease associated with nonadministration of VTE prophylaxis in hospitalized adults∗
Authors: Kiera Caparon, Matthew J Lankiewicz, Jessica Crow, Jennifer C Yui, John Lindsley, Brandyn D Lau, Michael B Streiff, Rakhi P Naik, Sophie Lanzkron, Lydia H Pecker, Peggy Kraus, Elliott R Haut, Jiangxia Wang, Kathryn E Dane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Patients with SCD or CF missed a larger proportion of pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis doses compared with otherwise medically ill patients.•Strategies are needed to improve VTE prophylaxis administration rates during hospitalizations in high-risk conditions such as CF and SCD.

Introduction

The risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE) in hospitalized medically ill patients is reduced by at least 50% with the use of VTE prophylaxis.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Prior research has shown that VTE prophylaxis nonadministration is a common patient care deficit, with previous studies demonstrating 18% of prescribed pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis doses are not administered during acute hospitalization for medically ill patients.6, 7, 8 Moreover, VTE prophylaxis nonadministration has been shown to be associated with an increased risk of hospital-associated VTE.9, 10, 11, 12 Pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis nonadministration is particularly important for patients with cystic fibrosis (CF) and sickle cell disease (SCD) because these are lifelong illnesses associated with frequent hospitalizations and an increased risk of VTE.

Approximately 100 000 individuals in the United States have SCD.13 SCD is associated with numerous complications including vaso-occlusive crises and infections. Hospitalization of patients with SCD occurs as frequently as 1.52 encounters per patient per year.14 VTE is a common cause of preventable morbidity and mortality in hospitalized patients, and patients with SCD are known to be at an increased risk for VTE.15,16 The cumulative incidence of VTE among patients with SCD has been reported to be as high as 11.3% by the age of 40 years.17 Similar to SCD, CF is an inherited disease associated with frequent hospitalizations. The National Inpatient Sample Database data reported CF-related diagnoses as the primary diagnosis in 3.53 per 10 000 hospital admissions.18 An increased risk of VTE has been documented in CF, with some overlapping risk factors with SCD, including long-term central venous catheter use, systemic inflammation, and increased infection risk.19,20

The rates of pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis nonadministration have not been evaluated in hospitalized adult patients with SCD and have only been reported in 1 single-center analysis of adult patients with CF.21 In addition, the rates of nonadministered pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis doses have not been directly compared between general medically ill patients and patients with either CF or SCD. We hypothesized that the nonadministered doses of pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis would be more common in patients with CF or SCD. In this study, we aimed to characterize pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis nonadministration among patients with CF or SCD to define prevalence and identify strategies for improvement.

Methods

Design

This was a single-center retrospective cohort study of patient admissions during which at least 2 doses of pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis were prescribed from 1 July 2016 to 31 December 2019. Three adult inpatient cohorts were evaluated: patients with CF, patients with SCD, and medically ill patients without CF or SCD. Patients were included if they were aged at least 18 years and were prescribed at least 2 doses of VTE prophylaxis with subcutaneous unfractionated heparin (UFH) or enoxaparin during their hospital admission. At the time of this study, our institutional guidance for prescribing VTE prophylaxis for nonsurgical patients was based on risk stratification (with prophylaxis being recommended for a Padua score of 3 or higher) and the decision to prescribe UFH or enoxaparin was based on prescriber preference. We excluded patients in pediatric and surgical units, and excluded doses prescribed in intensive care units, the emergency department, or with a one-time administration frequency. This study was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the proportion of nonadministered doses of pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis by cohort. Secondary outcomes included the proportion of patients who missed at least 1 dose by cohort, the proportion of nonadministered doses by medication and frequency, and the proportion of nonadministered doses in patients with >1 admission during the study period. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify patient characteristics associated with pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis nonadministration.

Definitions

Patients were placed into their respective cohorts using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes associated with the hospital admission. Pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis regimens included UFH doses below 10 000 units per dose administered subcutaneously every 8, 12, or 24 hours, and 30 or 40 mg of enoxaparin administered subcutaneously every 12 or 24 hours. For patients with multiple admissions, data for all VTE prophylaxis regimens and doses were aggregated at the patient level. Patient characteristics were evaluated at the patient level, whereas VTE prophylaxis regimens were evaluated at the dose level because patients could be prescribed different regimens during hospital admission. Statistical analyses

Data were analyzed using Stata (StataCorp LLC; College Station, TX). The χ2 test was used for categorical variables, and the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous, nonparametric variables. Multivariable logistic regression was used to analyze whether patient characteristics such as CF or SCD diagnosis, age, race, number of VTE prophylaxis doses prescribed, length of stay, and multiple hospital admissions are associated with a higher probability of missing at least 1 dose of ordered VTE prophylaxis. To develop dichotomous variables for the regression analysis, the median value was used as a cutoff for age, number of VTE prophylaxis doses prescribed, and length of stay. Characteristics were included in the initial multivariable model if the univariable analysis resulted in a P value of <.1. The variance inflation factor was used to assess collinearity of variables, and the optimal final model was selected by assessing the Akaike information criterion, Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness of fit test, and receiver operating characteristic area under the curve.

Results

A total of 13 316 patients were included for analysis. Of these patients, 89 were in the CF cohort, 246 in the SCD cohort, and 12 981 in the medically ill (without SCD or CF) cohort. Patients with CF and SCD were younger than the medically ill patients (median age: SCD cohort, 31 years vs CF cohort, 30 years vs medically ill cohort, 58 years; P < .01). As shown in Table 1, compared with medically ill patients, a higher proportion of patients with CF and SCD experienced multiple hospital admissions during the study period (CF cohort, 73.0% vs SCD cohort, 52.4% vs medically ill cohort, 21.8%; P < .01). The most prescribed VTE prophylaxis regimen was once-daily enoxaparin in the CF and SCD cohorts, whereas the most prescribed regimen in the medically ill cohort was UFH 3 times daily (Table 2).

Discussion

In this large study of hospitalized medically ill patients, we demonstrated that patients with CF or SCD miss significantly more doses of prescribed pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis than medically ill patients without these conditions. In addition, we found that a diagnosis of SCD or CF was an independent predictor of missing at least 1 dose of prescribed pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis compared with medically ill patients. Of these 2 diagnoses, patients with CF missed a significantly higher number of doses than patients with SCD. In this study of all injectable VTE prophylaxis agents, daily administration had the lowest percentage of missed doses within each cohort vs twice or 3 times daily frequencies. The decision to utilize enoxaparin more commonly in the SCD and CF cohorts could have potentially been based on a number of factors including but not limited to prior institutional data regarding nonadministration, renal function, a desire to increase adherence by minimizing injections, or prescriber preference.6

Multiple factors contributing to missed doses of VTE prophylaxis have been previously described. Prior studies have demonstrated patient preference for oral VTE prophylaxis over subcutaneous administration, with pain and dislike of needles being a cited concern.22,23 Correspondingly, patient refusal has been the most common reason for VTE prophylaxis nonadministration in prior studies.24 These issues may be more prevalent in populations with frequent interactions with the health care system from an early age. Qualitative investigation into needle distress in pediatric patients with CF has demonstrated that needle distress negatively affects medication adherence and that these issues can be compounded over time.25,26 Although there is a paucity of literature exploring needle distress in SCD, both SCD and CF are known to have a high rates of annual hospital presentations, and in our study, multiple hospital admissions was an independent risk factor for missing at least 1 prescribed VTE prophylaxis dose.14,18 Although it was not evaluated in this study, the preemptive use of oral agents during hospitalization in these populations is a potential strategy to mitigate VTE risk. The efficacy and safety of rivaroxaban for VTE prophylaxis have been demonstrated in randomized controlled trials in medically ill populations but requires careful patient selection to avoid use in patients with known bleeding risk factors.27, 28, 29

Although needle distress has been well studied, the connections between chronic illness, repeat hospitalizations, patient well-being, and a more general “medication fatigue” are less understood.30,31 Stressful interactions with the medical system in childhood have been posited to lead to future avoidance of the health care system as adults, and in adult patients, recurrent inpatient admissions have been shown to contribute to anxiety, depression, and stress.32, 33, 34, 35 Newman et al reported an increased frequency of missed VTE prophylaxis doses in hospitalized patients with HIV compared with those without HIV.36 It is conceivable that the negative impact on mental health from recurrent inpatient stays from a young age may contribute to exhaustion with the medical system. This is an area for further exploration.

Low health literacy and low understanding of the benefits of VTE prophylaxis is another potential contributor to pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis nonadministration. Developing educational resources targeted toward patients with chronic illness and describing the importance of VTE prophylaxis may be a helpful approach to increase VTE prophylaxis administration. Evidence shows that a small number of patients contribute to most missed VTE prophylaxis doses6 and that targeting educational interventions to these patients, rather than time- and resource-consuming hospital-wide initiatives, is an efficacious way to improve administration.37, 38, 39 With the added data provided from this study, the identification of admitted patients with chronic illnesses such as SCD or CF represent a target population for educational interventions to decrease missed pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis doses.

The implications of the significant difference in the proportion of missed doses between these 2 chronic illnesses themselves are also worth noting. Patients with CF, who are typically from racial and ethnic majorities in the United States, frequently have more social privilege and the skills and experiences to optimally navigate the health care system.40 Patients of racial and ethnic minorities, including most patients with SCD, often have less advantage in navigating health care encounters.41 Black patients may feel compelled to employ language, attire, and actions that will be perceived positively by their health care providers, to mitigate discrimination and negative stereotypes.42 As part of this positive self-presentation and a desire to avoid being perceived as difficult, Black patients might be more reluctant to refuse treatments.43 Therefore, when considering all of the aforementioned interventions, it will be beneficial to providers to consider these underlying socioeconomic factors.

Missed VTE prophylaxis doses are a modifiable risk factor for the development of VTE. We have published multiple interventions showing the benefit of interventions to educate nurses and patients on the administration of missed doses.37,38,44 We have previously shown that there are no disparities in the effectiveness of these interventions based on sex and race.45, 46, 47 However, we have yet to study potential differential effects on patients with specific medical diagnoses. This article offers 2 specific populations in which interventions might be additionally helpful.

There are several limitations to this study. Due to issues with outpatient coding, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services recommend case-finding algorithms for SCD disease that require at least 3 discrete claims of ICD-9-CM or ICD-10-CM codes for SCD.48,49 This study identified cases using ICD-10-CM codes associated with a single admission, which could potentially result in patients falsely identified as having SCD. Given that this is a retrospective study, it does not capture all possible confounding variables, including prior bleeding events, prior VTE, or other comorbidities that could influence VTE prophylaxis prescribing differences between cohorts, because we only evaluated the administration of prescribed medications. However, our results are similar to prior studies.24 In all cohorts, most nonadministered doses were due to patient refusal; however, the percentage of doses missed due to refusal was the lowest in the medically ill cohort. The medically ill cohort likely represents a heterogeneous patient population in terms of age and reason for admission and we did not review reasons for nonadministration other than refusal; therefore, we cannot comment on contributory clinical factors to this difference. Finally, no clinical outcomes such as VTE events were assessed. Therefore, the impact of nonadministered pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis doses cannot be commented on in these specific populations, although it has previously been reported elsewhere.9, 10, 11, 12

Conclusion

Patients with SCD and patients with CF missed a larger proportion of prescribed pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis doses than medically ill patients without SCD or CF. Factors independently associated with increased odds of pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis nonadministration include CF or SCD diagnosis, multiple admissions, and >5 prescribed doses of VTE prophylaxis. These findings shed light on an important clinical problem and provide justification for further research to assess reasons for the high incidence of missed doses in patients with SCD or CF. Ultimately, opportunities for targeted interventions should be identified, including oral options or the preferential use of parenteral agents with once-daily dosing schedules, to improve VTE prevention in high-risk chronic conditions such as SCD and CF. Additional research is needed to understand patient rationale for dose refusal among patients with SCD and CF to improve adherence to prescribed pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis regimens.
