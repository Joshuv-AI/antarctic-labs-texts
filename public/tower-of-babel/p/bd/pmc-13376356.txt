Genomic characterization of phosphate-solubilizing Klebsiella variicola strain PSEG-1 from earthworm gut
Authors: Sristy Salaj Swarnkar, Amrita Kumari Panda, Aseem Kerketta, Joystu Dutta, Satpal Singh Bisht, Enketeswara Subudhi, Surajit De Mandal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Phosphorus (P) is an essential macronutrient for plant growth that plays a critical role in photosynthesis, DNA synthesis, and energy transfer. Phosphorus deficiency is a major limitation to plant growth and agricultural productivity globally. This deficiency arises from a combination of soil composition, agricultural practices, plant physiological responses, and environmental factors. Understanding these factors is crucial for developing strategies to mitigate phosphorus deficiency and improve crop yields. The isolation, characterization, and application of potent indigenous bacteria capable of phosphorus solubilization are of significant importance in acidic soils to overcome the challenges posed by phosphorus fixation. In this study, we report the genome sequencing of Klebsiella variicola strain PSEG-1, isolated from the gut of the earthworm Aporrectodea sp. that exhibits notable phosphorus solubilization ability. Genomic analysis reveals that the PSEG-1 genome comprises a single chromosome of 5.69 Mb, with a GC content of 57.07%, and encodes 5,596 protein-coding sequences, 4 rRNA genes, and 80 tRNA genes. Functional annotation of the genome identified 19 distinct categories of protein-coding genes, with a notable representation of genes involved in amino acid transport and metabolism, carbohydrate transport and metabolism, and transcriptional regulation. Notably, the genetic repertoire of PSEG-1 comprises 37 genes implicated in organic acid metabolism, 5 genes associated with phosphatase activity, and 2 genes directly involved in phosphate metabolism. These findings highlight the potential of K. variicola PSEG-1 to enhance phosphorus availability in nutrient-deficient environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56541-2.

Introduction

The global population is expected to peak at approximately 10.3 billion in the 2080 s, according to recent forecasts from the United Nations. To meet the growing demand for food and shelter, the agricultural industry often overuses synthetic fertilizers. The application of phosphorus fertilizers is characterized by inefficiency, as a significant portion of the applied phosphorus quickly precipitates or becomes adsorbed. Research indicates that 75–90% of the soluble phosphorus applied can be fixed and subsequently rendered unavailable to plants shortly after application1,2. This inefficacy presents a strategic supply risk for global agricultural systems and necessitates the development of strategies that enhance phosphorus use efficiency. Phosphate-solubilizing bacteria (PSB) convert insoluble phosphates into bioavailable forms by the secretion of organic acids and phosphatase enzymes2,3. Among these, Klebsiella variicola has been recognized as a proficient PSB, exhibiting remarkable capabilities for both phosphate solubilization and plant growth promotion4,5. The earthworm gut represents a unique ecological niche that harbors diverse microbial communities with potential biotechnological applications6,7. The gut microbiome of earthworms constitutes an abundant reservoir of PSB, characterized by high phosphatase activity and increased populations of phosphorus-mobilizing bacteria compared to the bulk soil7,8. The digestive systems of earthworm’s function as active microbial incubators, facilitating the concentration of soil-sourced bacteria and promoting the proliferation of metabolically adaptable strains that possess enhanced capacities for promoting plant growth. K. variicola strains often possess intact nif operons and exhibit quantifiable nitrogen fixation activity6. Variability at the strain level concerning nitrogen fixation capability is associated with the organization of nif genes and their regulatory components. Nitrogen fixation is highly sensitive to phosphorus availability, with P-limitation significantly impairing nitrogenase activity6. This creates a synergistic relationship between phosphate-solubilizing and nitrogen-fixing capabilities in PGPR. Phylogenetic assessment of nifH sequences yields valuable perspectives on the evolutionary connections among diazotrophic Klebsiella isolates. K. variicola isolates derived from the gastrointestinal tracts of animals generally possess a wide array of carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes), encompassing cellulases, hemicellulases, and pectinases9. Comparative genomic investigations elucidate strain-specific CAZyme profiles that are indicative of ecological niche adaptation.

In our previous study, we have isolated K. variicola strain PSEG-1 from the gut of Aporrectodea sp. and demonstrated its phosphate-solubilizing capacity through phenotypic assays10. PSEG-1 demonstrated a characteristic morphology consistent with that of a Gram-negative, non-spore-forming, rod-shaped bacterium with positive catalase activity and displayed distinctive colonial morphology on conventional microbiological media. The strain produced clear phosphate solubilization zones on Pikovskaya agar, exhibiting a solubilization index (SI) of 1.6, which signifies a moderate yet reliable phosphate-mobilizing capacity. PSEG-1 also maintained phosphate solubilization activity over prolonged incubation periods, indicating metabolic stability under laboratory conditions10. Genome mining for nitrogen fixation (nif) operons, pyrroloquinoline quinone (pqq) gene clusters, and CAZyme families offers a molecular foundation for the observed phenotypic characteristics. Comparative genomics elucidates the evolutionary relationships and the conservation of functional genes among various K. variicola strains. Nevertheless, the genomic factors that underpin these phenotypic characteristics remain to be investigated. Although contemporary genome-centric research has pinpointed critical genetic markers such as the gcd gene (which encodes quinoprotein glucose dehydrogenase), pqq genes (involved in pyrroloquinoline quinone biosynthesis), and various phosphatase genes (phoD and phoC) as essential contributors to phosphate solubilization in soil bacteria11,12, a comprehensive genome analysis of K. variicola strains derived from earthworm gut microbiomes has not yet been studied. We postulated that K. variicola PSEG-1 possesses specific genomic determinants that elucidate its remarkable phosphate solubilization efficacy observed in our previous phenotypic investigations, including genes implicated in organic acid biosynthesis, phosphatase activity, and supplementary traits that promote plant growth. This genomic characterization aims to bridge the disparity between phenotypic observations and the mechanistic understanding of phosphate solubilization in PSB sourced from earthworm microbiomes. To elucidate the genetic basis of its plant growth-promoting traits, we performed whole-genome sequencing and genomic analysis of PSEG-1. This study provides insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying phosphate solubilization, nitrogen fixation, and other beneficial traits encoded in the PSEG-1 genome, thereby contributing to the development of sustainable biofertilizer strategies for agriculture.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strain, culture conditions, and genomic DNA isolation

Klebsiella variicola strain PSEG-1 was isolated from the gut of the earthworm Aporrectodea sp. collected from Bhatko village of Batauli block, Surguja, Chhattisgarh, India (Supplementary Table 1)10. The strain was isolated and cultivated in triplicate on Pikovskaya’s medium, a specialized growth medium designed to promote phosphate solubilization. This medium contains balanced mixture of nutrients including glucose (10.0 g/L), as a carbon source, various salts for essential minerals: ammonium sulfate (0.5 g/L), potassium chloride (0.2 g/L), magnesium sulfate (1.0 g/L), manganese sulfate (0.0001 g/L), ferrous sulfate (0.0001 g/L), yeast extract (0.5 g/L), and tricalcium phosphate (5.0 g/L) as a source of insoluble phosphate. The composition of this medium allows for the selective growth of bacteria capable of solubilizing phosphate. Growth of PSEG-1 occurred in this medium at a temperature of 37 °C and a pH of 7.0. Incubation was carried out in a rotary shaker subjected to periodic shaking at 150 rpm for a duration of 72 h. The inoculum density was standardized to an OD₆₀₀ of 0.10, corresponding to approximately 1.0 × 10⁷ CFU mL⁻¹ before inoculation. Following cultivation, DNA extraction was performed using the CTAB (cetyltrimethylammonium bromide) protocol13, a manual method known for its effectiveness in isolating high-quality genomic DNA from bacterial cells. DNA extractions with three replicates were performed before sequencing, which were used to confirm DNA quality and yield consistency. DNA quantity was determined using a Qubit fluorometer v4.0 (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, USA) using the dsDNA high sensitivity assay kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A sample volume of 1 µl was added to 199 µl of a Qubit working solution. A260/280 and A260/230 absorbance ratios acquired with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. High-quality genomic DNA was then prepared for downstream sequencing.

Genome sequencing, annotation, and analysis

The genome was sequenced at Gujarat Biotechnology Research Centre, Gandhinagar, Gujarat, India utilizing the Illumina NovaSeq platform, and a library was constructed using the QIAseq FX DNA Library Kit. The reads were assembled with the aid of Unicycler using BV-BRC14. The quality of the assembly was assessed using QUAST (Quality Assessment Tool for Genome Assemblies)15. Genome annotation was completed using the RAST tool kit (RASTtk)14. A circular map of the genome was generated via the Bacterial Bioinformatics Resource Center (BV-BRC)14. To accurately assign the taxonomy of the sequenced isolates, we used whole-genome-based phylogeny in the Type (Strain) Genome Server (https://tygs.dsmz.de) based on the Genome Blast Distance Phylogeny (GBDP) method. The phylogenetic tree was exported in Newick format and uploaded to the Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL v7)16. The tree was rooted using Nocardioides hankookensis JCM 15,302 (BAAAOY010000000) as the outgroup. Bootstrap support values were calculated to assess the reliability of the branching patterns. Accession numbers for all reference strains used in the analysis are provided in the figure legend (Fig. 2).

Finally, average nucleotide identity (ANI) was analysed based on BLAST + ANIb using the JSpecies web server17. The DNA-DNA hybridization (DDH) values were calculated using Genome-to-Genome Distance Calculator (GGDC) (http://ggdc.dsmz.de/ggdc.php#)18. Gene functions were annotated and analyzed using eggNOG-mapper v219 and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) server. The genome was further annotated with reference to the CAZymes and their substrate specificity via the dbCAN3 pipeline20. The dbCAN3 integrates three state-of-the-art tools/databases for automated CAZyme annotation: HMMER search for CAZyme family annotation vs. dbCAN CAZyme domain HMM database; DIAMOND search for BLAST hits in the CAZy database; and HMMER search for CAZyme subfamily annotation vs. dbCAN-substrate HMM database of CAZyme subfamilies. The annotation results of the CAZymes are reported if two of the specified tools identified them20. Biosynthetic gene clusters (BCGs) produced by the strains were predicted using the AntiSMASH server21(https://antismash.secondarymetabolites.org). The raw data used in this study have been submitted to the NCBI under the accession no PRJNA1243097.

Comparative genome analysis among Klebsiella variicola strains ZH07, DX120E, and PSEG-1

Comparative genomics among Klebsiella variicola PSEG-1 and other phosphate-solubilizing or plant growth-promoting bacterial strains (ZH07 and DX120E) was performed using OrthoVenn3 (https://orthovenn3.bioinf.me/). The complete genome sequences of ZH07 and DX120E were retrieved from the NCBI GenBank database, while the genome of PSEG-1 was annotated using the RAST server. The analysis was conducted using the following parameters: E-value cutoff, 1e-5; inflation value for MCL clustering, 1.5 (default); and Orthologous clustering algorithm, OrthoMCL. OrthoVenn3 identifies the unique and shared orthologous gene clusters among these strains, helping to distinguish core, accessory, and strain-specific genes.

Results and discussion

DNA concentration & general genome characteristics of K. variicola PSEG-1

DNA extraction was performed in triplicate; however, one high-quality DNA preparation exhibiting a concentration of 53.16 ng/µL, A 260/280 ratio of 1.92, and A 260/230 ratio of 1.81, was selected for sequencing library construction. Illumina sequencing generated a total of 1,605,764 reads encompassing 372.9 Mbp, with consistently high base quality scores across all positions (mean Phred > 30), indicating excellent read accuracy for downstream analysis. The whole-genome sequencing analysis of K. variicola PSEG-1 provides comprehensive insights into its genetic makeup and organization. The genome spanning 5.69 Mb, is distributed across 35 contigs, each exceeding 500 base pairs in length which reflects a moderately fragmented draft. However, this distribution is supported by robust sequencing quality metrics: N50 read length 475,786 bp; L50 (the count of the smallest number of contigs whose length sum makes up 50% of the total genome size) of 5 contigs and the genome was sequenced to an average depth of 40×. The genome exhibits a GC percentage of 57.08%, which is consistent with previously reported K. variicola DSM 15,968 (56.5%) and K. variicola X39 (57.3%)22,23.

The genomic structure of K. variicola PSEG-1 is illustrated in Fig. 1. This genomic structure encompasses 5,596 predicted coding sequences (CDSs), highlighting the strain’s potential for diverse protein production and functional capabilities. The presence of 4 ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes (one complete 16–23 S-5 S operon) and 80 transfer RNA (tRNA) genes further emphasizes the strain’s capacity for protein synthesis and cellular processes. The genome’s GC content is high when compared to several bacterial taxa, but is in line with values previously reported for Klebsiella variicola strains indicate the organism’s stability and adaptability to various environmental conditions (Table 1). To elucidate the evolutionary relationships of the isolated organism PSEG-1, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using Type (strain) Genome server, which showed that K. variicola strain PSEG-1 is closely related and forms a monophyletic group with K. variicola DSM 15,968, representing the type strain of K. variicola22, DX120E, ZH07 and LMG 23,571. The evolutionary position of PSEG-1 within the bacterial kingdom is clearly defined by the phylogenetic analysis shown in Fig. 2. The average nucleotide identity (ANI) and Genome-to-Genome Distance values showed that strain PSEG-1 is closely related to Klebsiella variicola DX120E24 with ANI of 98.86% and K. variicola DSM 15,96825 with an ANI of 98.70% and a digital DNA–DNA hybridization (dDDH) value of 91.20%, supporting their close taxonomic relationship (Supplementary Table 2).

Gene function annotation of K. variicola strain PSEG-1

Among the 5,596 protein-encoding genes, 19 distinct categories were identified (Fig. 3). Of these, 518 genes are responsible for encoding proteins associated with amino acid transport and metabolism (E), 401 genes are involved in carbohydrate transport and metabolism (G), and 528 genes encode transcription-related proteins (K). This suggests that the functional proteins encoded by the cellular genome could be crucial for maintaining cellular viability and genetic metabolism. Additionally, 70 genes related to the biosynthesis, transport, and catabolism of secondary metabolites (Q) were identified, including various organic acids involved in the dissolution of inorganic phosphorus. Furthermore, several genes with unknown functions (S) were identified, highlighting the need for further investigation.

KEGG annotations facilitated the prediction of 23 functional classes within the genome of the K. variicola PSEG-1 strain (Fig. 4). These classes can be categorized as follows: (1) Carbohydrate metabolism (447); (2) Amino acid metabolism (332); (3) Environmental Information Processing, encompassing membrane transport (288) and signal transduction (141); (4) Metabolism of cofactors and vitamins (241); (5) Xenobiotics degradation and metabolism (125); and (6) Organismal Systems. Among these, the metabolism-related systems represented the most substantial portion of the metabolic pathways in the Klebsiella variicola strain PSEG-1. The genomic potential identified in the KEGG pathway revealed a high abundance of genes associated with carbohydrate metabolism (Fig. 4) in the bacterial isolate, which correlates with the observed phenotypic traits of PSEG-1. Carbohydrate metabolism plays a crucial role in microbial energy production and the generation of metabolic intermediates, including various organic acids. The observed phosphate-solubilizing phenotype on Pikovskaya medium strongly supports the functional prediction derived from KEGG pathway analysis. Thus, there may be a functional connection between the bacteria’s metabolic capabilities and their ability to promote plant growth, as the abundance of carbohydrate metabolism pathways may contribute to the formation of organic acids that promote phosphate solubilization.

The strain’s unique plant-growth-promoting capabilities depend on the high abundance of genes (447 genes) devoted to glucose metabolism, which is more than merely a general survival feature. The processing of carbon sources within this metabolic framework supplies the energy and precursors required for the production of secondary metabolites, particularly organic acids. A high-flux metabolic system focused on extracellular secretion is suggested by the genetic capacity for the production of succinic, pyruvic, and acetic acids as well as the known transport routes for these acids. The observed phosphate-solubilizing phenotype on Pikovskaya medium, which enables the bacterium to efficiently lower environmental pH and dissolve mineral phosphates, is therefore the functional result of this substantial genomic investment in carbohydrate processing rather than an isolated trait.

Further investigation identified functional pathways in PSEG-1 for phosphate solubilization and the biosynthesis of succinic and pyruvic acids, as well as acetic acid transport. While these pathways are annotated in the genome (Supplementary Table 3), the current analysis does not directly measure their activity levels. Organic acids are pivotal in the solubilization of phosphate, as they lower the environmental pH, thereby aiding in the dissolution of insoluble phosphates. The annotation of pathways linked to phosphate solubilization and inorganic phosphatase suggests that PSEG-1 has the genetic potential to participate in the enzymatic degradation of phosphate compounds. The identification of transport pathways, including those for acetic acid (Supplementary Table 3), indicates that PSEG-1 may be capable of exporting organic acids, potentially enhancing its capacity to solubilize phosphate in extracellular settings. However, functional activity would require further experimental validation.

Carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZymes)

Plant-derived lignocellulosic polymers are effectively broken down by several bacteria that have lignocellulolytic activity. Carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) are among the several enzymes that give bacteria this ability. Glycoside hydrolases (GHs), carbohydrate esterases (CEs), polysaccharide lyases (PLs), glycosyltransferases (GTs), enzymes with auxiliary activities (AA), and carbohydrate-binding modules (CBMs) with carbohydrate-binding activity are examples of CAZymes. Based on their structural similarity and functional amino acid sequences, these CAZymes are divided into multiple families (CAZy database; http://www.cazy.org/). Carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZymes) plays an important role in the degradation of complex carbohydrates, thereby enhancing soil nutrient availability. Exploration of the carbohydrate-active enzyme database revealed 41 ORFs annotated as glycosyl transferases (GT) genes, one as polysaccharide lyase (PL) genes, 10 as carbohydrate esterase (CE) genes, 5 each as auxiliary activities genes, carbohydrate-binding modules and 70 as glycoside hydrolase (GH) genes in PSEG-1 whereas 33 ORFs as GT, 55 as GH, 11 ORFs as CE, 5 as AA, 7 ORFs as CBM and one as PL for DX120E; 32 as GT, 59 ORFs as GH, 11 as CE, 6 ORFs as AA, 9 as CBM and 1 ORF as PL in ZH07 (Fig. 5). The enzymes of class GH catalyze the hydrolysis and/or rearrangement of glycosidic bonds. The four abundant families of the GH class were recorded as GH23 (7 genes), GH3 (12 genes), GH43 (2 genes) and GH8 (2 genes). The predicted enzymes of GH23, GH3, GH43 and GH8 catalyze the degradation of carbohydrates by chitinase, glucosidase/glucanase/xylosidase, arabinofuranosidase and mannosidase, respectively. The enrichment of GH23 and GH3 families in PSEG-1, relative to other K. variicola isolates, suggests a specialized metabolic niche for degrading complex soil and gut-associated organic matter, further enhancing its utility as a biofertilizer.

The enzymes of the GT class catalyze the formation of the glycosidic linkage to produce a glycoside. The GT2 (9 genes), GT4 (8 genes) and GT51 (4 genes) were the three most dominant families of the GT class present in the genome of K. variicola strain PSEG-1. These three families of GT contain glycosyl synthase and glycosyl transferase. The enzymes of the CE class catalyze the hydrolysis of the carbohydrate esters of polysaccharides. The three abundant CE families, CE4 (2 genes), CE9 (1 gene) and CE14 (1 gene) were reported as catalyzing the hydrolysis of acetylated xylan and chitin, acetylated glucosamine and diacetyl chitobiose, respectively. The enzymes of the AA class contain the redox enzymes that act in conjunction with the CAZymes in the degradation of carbohydrates. The dominant family of the AA class recorded in the Klebsiella variicola strain PSEG-1 genome was AA3 (3 genes). AA3 are flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD)-dependent oxidoreductases from the glucose-methanol-choline (GMC) family. These enzymes play a crucial role in lignocellulose degradation by assisting other CAZymes through redox reactions. The PL class, which catalyzes the non-hydrolytic cleavage of glycosidic bonds, contains only one gene of the family PL22. Carbohydrate-binding module (CBM) are non-catalytic modules of CAZymes and catalyze the reaction by adhesion to carbohydrates. The dominant CBM was recorded as CBM48 (2 genes), which binds to the enzymes responsible for the degradation of xylan. In summary, the repertoire of carbohydrate-active enzymes discovered in the genome of K. variicola strain PSEG-1 reveals its potential for carbohydrate metabolism and highlights its role in nutrient cycling and overall soil health.

Gene mining for nitrogen fixation

In the genome of K. variicola PSEG-1, twenty nif genes were identified, including the core structural genes (nifH, nifD, and nifK), regulatory genes (nifA, nifL), several FeMo-cofactor biosynthesis genes (nifB, nifE, nifN, nifV, nifQ), and multiple genes involved in nitrogenase maturation and assembly (nifS, nifU, nifM etc.). The nitrogenase enzyme complex is synthesized by the expression of multiple genes in the nif operon. The nifD and nifK genes are responsible for encoding dinitrogenase, whereas nifH encodes the enzyme dinitrogenase reductase. The mechanism of N2 fixation is stringently regulated at the genetic level, encompassing both transcriptional and translational modifications of the nifHDK gene products28. The nifEN and nifBQ genes are tasked with encoding cofactor assembly proteins that function as molecular scaffolds for the assembly of Fe-Mo clusters29. The nifJ gene encodes the pyruvate-flavodoxin-oxidoreductase protein, which facilitates the conversion of pyruvate, flavodoxin, or ferredoxin to acetyl-CoA while concurrently transferring electrons to the nitrogenase enzymes30. The nifU and nifS genes encode the components of nitrogenase that are crucial for assembling [2Fe-2 S] and [4Fe-4 S] clusters, which are indispensable for growth under nitrogen-fixing conditions. nifUS is pivotal in supplying [Fe-S] clusters, which act as metabolic precursors for the biosynthesis of FeMo-Co29. The nifF gene facilitates electron transfer from the nifJ protein to the Fe protein of nitrogenase. The presence of a complete nif cluster in K. variicola PSEG-1 genome indicates that the organism is capable of biological nitrogen fixation (diazotrophy). These genes encode the proteins that comprise the nitrogenase enzyme complex and other proteins involved in the conversion of atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia, a form of nitrogen that plants can utilize. In a previously reported nitrogen-fixing strain, Klebsiella variicola GN0231, 57 nitrogen-fixation–related genes were identified, comprising FeMo cofactor assembly genes (nifS, nifU, nifM), structural nif genes (nifHDKEN), regulatory elements (nifA, nifL), nitrogen metabolism regulators (ntrB, ntrC, glnD), and the ammonium transporter amtB. Although the total number of nitrogen-fixation related genes is lower than that reported in strain GN02, the presence of the essential nif gene cluster indicates the conservation of the core nitrogen-fixation machinery in PSEG-1.

The strain harbors a robust diazotrophic cluster including the structural nifHDK operon and the essential cofactor assembly (nifENB) and regulatory (nifA) genes. In addition to diazotrophy, PSEG-1 also harbors genes associated with respiratory and assimilatory nitrogen metabolism, including nitrate reductase (nar), nitrite reductase (nir), nitric oxide reductase (nor), and nitrous oxide reductase (nos). The presence of these pathways indicates metabolic flexibility, enabling the strain to utilize nitrate and nitrite under different environmental conditions when atmospheric nitrogen fixation may be limited. This dual capability completes the nifHDK structural genes with accessory/regulatory elements, together with the nar/nir/nor/nos pathways—underscoring the ecological versatility of PSEG 1 and its potential role as a plant growth-promoting bacterium.

Gene mining for phosphate solubilization

PSB promotes plant development by converting mineral phosphate into a form that plants can easily absorb. This is accomplished through a variety of solubilization processes, such as the generation of extracellular enzymes and the release of hydroxyl ions, protons, and organic acids. Gluconic acid production is a well-known characteristic of gram-negative bacteria for phosphorus solubilization32. Pyrroloquinoline quinone (PQQ) is a cofactor for the glucose dehydrogenase enzyme, which is encoded by the glucose dehydrogenase (gcd) gene. The pqq operon, which is made up of the core genes pqqABCDEF, encodes PQQ, a redox-active molecule with dehydrogenase activity and mineral phosphate solubilization in microorganisms33. According to a previous work, the gluconic acid concentration in the culture media of gram-negative Rahnella aquatilis HX2 was drastically reduced from 9.68 to 0.65 g L− 1 when the pqqA gene was disrupted. Additionally, the pqqA gene mutation led to a lower soluble phosphorus content (99.7 mg L− 1) than the wild-type strain’s phosphorus content (465.9 mg L− 1)34. Numerous genes linked to the dissolution of inorganic phosphorus and iron carriers in plants have also been discovered by the genome-wide sequencing of the Klebsiella variicola GN0231 strain and Klebsiella variicola subsp. variicola ML.9ba235. These include indole-3-pyruvate decarboxylase gene (ipdC), iron cell enteromycin synthesis gene (entABCDEF), and coenzyme PQQ synthesis protein gene (pqqBCDEF).

Bioinformatic analysis using the antiSMASH platform identified gcd and pqq genes in the K. variicola PSEG-1 genome. The findings indicate that contig_6 is associated with a biosynthesis gene cluster (BGC), which encompasses the genes pqqBCDE (Table 2). The identification of a pqqBCDEF gene cluster (locus tags peg.3441–3437) in PSEG-1, exhibiting perfect synteny (Supplementary Table 3), substantiates the biosynthetic pathway of the cofactor PQQ, which is essential for the functionality of PQQ-dependent glucose dehydrogenase (PQQ-GDH). This enzyme catalyzes the conversion of glucose into gluconic acid, representing a critical phase in the direct oxidative pathway of glucose metabolism that contributes to increased phosphate solubilization. The small precursor gene pqqA was not detected by automated annotation, which is consistent with its short length (~ 20–30 amino acids) and frequent omission in draft genome annotations. However, the presence of the downstream biosynthetic machinery (pqqBCDE) together with the peptidase (pqqF) strongly supports a functional pathway for PQQ biosynthesis, as reported previously36. Multiple copies of gdhA (peg.898, peg.2370, peg.3783) were identified, providing redundancy for PQQ-dependent glucose dehydrogenase activity. This redundancy enhances the potential for gluconic acid secretion, a key mechanism for solubilising inorganic phosphate. The presence of multiple gdhA copies (peg.898, peg.2370, peg.3783) and the pqqBCDEF gene cluster provides a redundant and robust mechanism for gluconic acid production that is less vulnerable to single-locus mutations. This genomic redundancy likely explains the strain’s ability to maintain ‘reliable phosphate-mobilizing capacity’ over prolonged incubation periods as observed in an earlier study10. PSEG-1 produced a visible clear halo zone around colonies on Pikovskaya’s agar supplemented with tricalcium phosphate as the sole phosphorus source. The solubilization index (SI), was determined to be 1.6 after 3 days of incubation at 37 °C. This moderate yet reproducible SI value, confirmed across three independent biological replicates, indicates consistent phosphate-mobilizing capacity under laboratory conditions10. PSEG-1 has a highly developed strategy for phosphorus acquisition under nutrient-limited conditions by combining high-capacity metabolic “fueling” genes with redundant solubilization enzymes.

Both acid and alkaline phosphatases were detected (phoC, peg.2841; phoA, peg.3719). In contrast, phoD and phoX were not identified in the genome, suggesting that phosphate hydrolysis in PSEG-1 relies primarily on phoA/phoC. This finding is consistent with reports that phoA represents one of the major alkaline phosphatase families in gram-negative bacteria, proteins of either the phoX or phoA family (but rarely both) are found in many bacteria suggesting these families are functionally equivalent37. The phosphate-specific transport operon pstSCAB (peg.2575–2578) was complete, which codes for a high-affinity free-phosphate transport mechanism38 and the two-component regulatory system phoBR (peg.3706, peg.3705) was also intact. Together, these systems ensure efficient sensing, uptake, and regulation of phosphate availability. Overall, the presence of a syntenous pqq operon, multiple gdhA copies, complete pstSCAB and phoBR systems, and functional phosphatases (phoA/phoC) provides strong genomic evidence that K. variicola PSEG-1 possesses a robust and redundant phosphate-solubilisation machinery. K. variicola PSEG-1 also harbors phn genes (phnABCDEFGHIJKLMNP gene cluster) integral to phosphate solubilization (Table 2). The cluster of phn-genes implicated in C-P lyase functionality and the assimilation and translocation of phosphonates indicates the strain’s capability to metabolize alternative phosphorus sources, such as phosphonates in phosphate scarcity. In PSEG-1, the pqq operon (pqqBCDEF), gcd gene, two phn-related operons (phnCDE and phnABFGHIJKLMNP) are responsible for the uptake and degradation of phosphonate identified in the chromosome39.

Presence/absence matrix, detailing the distribution of key loci among all comparable strains, is provided in Supplementary Table 4. It was reported that Klebsiella variicola subsp. variicola ZH07 can produce indole acetic acid (IAA) due to the identified genes in the annotated genome, i.e. ipdC (gene encoding indolepyruvate decarboxylase, a key enzyme in the indole-3-pyruvic acid pathway (IPyA pathway), aldH (encoding aldehyde dehydrogenase, converting indole-3-acetaldehyde to IAA), nthAB (encoding nitrile hydratase, converting indole-3-acetonitrile to indole-3-acetamide), and amiE (encoding amidase, converting indole-3-acetamide to IAA)40. Genomic analysis of K. variicola PSEG-1 identified the ipdC gene (indole-3-pyruvate decarboxylase), which facilitates the conversion of IPyA to indole-3-acetaldehyde, and the aldH gene, which further oxidizes indole-3-acetaldehyde to the phytohormone indole-3-acetic acid (IAA). Functional synthesis of P-acquisition mechanisms

The implications of all findings suggest that K. variicola PSEG-1 possesses a diverse and robust metabolic capability, with a particular emphasis on carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism. This metabolic versatility may contribute to the organism’s adaptability and survival in various environmental niches. The presence of significant pathways related to membrane transport and signal transduction indicates that the strain is well-equipped to respond to environmental cues and efficiently uptake nutrients. Additionally, the notable presence of xenobiotics degradation pathways implies that this strain may have potential applications in bioremediation or resistance to antimicrobial compounds (Supplementary Table 5). These insights into the functional genomics of K. variicola PSEG-1 could inform future research on its ecological role, pathogenicity, and potential biotechnological applications. The comprehensive genomic analysis of K. variicola strain PSEG-1 has provided valuable insights into its genetic makeup, metabolic capabilities, and potential ecological roles. The strain’s ability to solubilize phosphorus, coupled with its diverse array of genes related to organic acid metabolism and phosphate-related activities, underscores its potential as a plant growth-promoting bacterium (Fig. 6). This strain uses a dual-track strategy of acidolysis and enzymolysis to increase P-bioavailability rather than relying on a single metabolic pathway, as shown in Fig. 6. The vital “chemical fuel” for organic acid production is provided by the significant genetic investment in carbohydrate metabolism (447 genes). This metabolic flow ensures that gluconic acid is consistently secreted by feeding the redundant pqqBCDEF gene cluster and three copies of the gdhA gene. This pathway facilitates the chelation of metal cations (e.g., Ca2+, Al3+, Fe3+), effectively dissolving mineral phosphates in the soil matrix. PSEG-1 uses phn gene cluster in addition to an enzymatic repertoire that includes acid and alkaline phosphatases (phoC and phoA) to support acid-based dissolution. These mechanisms enable the strain to scavenge phosphorus from complex phosphonates and organic materials, especially in situations where P is extremely scarce. The strain is able to sense, absorb, and internalize even minute amounts of released phosphate due to the integration of these pathways with a pstSCAB high-affinity transport system and the phoBR regulatory two-component system. Additionally, the nifHDK cluster indicates a metabolic synergy in which the high-energy requirements of biological nitrogen fixation are directly supported by the phosphorus released by PSEG-1. The genome sequencing revealed a single chromosome of 5.69 Mb, with 5,596 predicted coding sequences, highlighting the strain’s genetic complexity. Phylogenetic analysis positioned PSEG-1 in close relation to K. variicola DSM 15,968, while functional annotation uncovered a rich diversity of metabolic pathways, particularly in carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism. These findings not only enhance our understanding of K. variicola PSEG-1’s biology but also suggest its potential applications in agriculture as a biofertilizer and in bioremediation.

Comparative genomic analysis

Strains of K. variicola DX120E and ZH07 were chosen for comparison because they are well-characterized plant-associated members of the species with documented physiological traits such as phosphate solubilization, nitrogen fixation, siderophore production, and biocontrol activity. ZH07 was isolated from the rhizosphere of peanut plants40, while DX120E originates from the roots of sugarcane24. These ecological origins closely align with the niche of the focal strain PSEG-1 and phylogenetic proximity allowing meaningful comparison. These characteristics make them suitable candidates for comparison with K. variicola PSEG-1, which also exhibits plant growth-promoting potential. Their inclusion enabled a comparative genomic analysis to identify conserved and strain-specific genetic features relative to K. variicola PSEG-1.

To examine genomic similarities and strain-specific features among these isolates, a pangenome analysis was conducted using the genomes of K. variicola PSEG-1, K. variicola strain DX120E, and K. variicola strain ZH07. The pangenome consisted of 5,062 protein clusters, of which 4,579 clusters were commonly shared among them (Fig. 7) indicating a substantial conserved genomic core. Additionally, 14 protein clusters distinct to strain PSEG-1 were found through comparative analysis, suggesting possible strain-specific functional characteristics. Functional annotation of these unique proteins based on COG classification revealed their association with multiple biological processes including carbohydrate transport and metabolism (G), translation and ribosomal structure (J), and transcriptional regulation (K), replication, recombination, and repair (L), cell wall and membrane biogenesis (M), posttranslational modification, protein turnover, and chaperone activity (O), and inorganic ion transport and metabolism (P). In addition, several proteins were assigned to the category of unknown function (S), suggesting the presence of potentially novel or strain-specific functions (Supplementary Table 6). These results indicate that, although PSEG-1 shares a large proportion of conserved genes with the reference strains, it also possesses a small set of strain-specific proteins that may help explain its unique physiological traits.

The plant growth-promoting characteristics seen in strain PSEG-1, such as its ability to solubilize phosphate, may be attributed to some of these strain-specific genes, especially those linked to glucose metabolism and inorganic ion transport. Future research needs to include mechanistic studies of phosphorus solubilization and plant growth promotion by strain PSEG-1, incorporating complementation experiments to verify functional characteristics in soil, genetic modification of pqqBCDEF genes through site-directed mutagenesis, and analyses of differential gene expression and enzyme activity under P-limiting circumstances.

Conclusion

This study presents the genome sequencing of K. variicola PSEG-1 and provides a comprehensive analysis of genes associated with plant growth promotion, specifically those involved in nitrogen fixation and phosphorus solubilization. The genetic screening and analysis offer valuable insights into the functionality and potential applications of K. variicola PSEG-1. PSEG-1 exhibits a high level of genomic resilience by supporting redundant and ecologically adaptable systems. By offering a thorough molecular explanation for the “moderate yet reliable” phosphate-mobilizing capacity observed in phenotypic assays, this functional synthesis advances the research beyond simple genome reporting. This coordinated genetic repertoire establishes K. variicola PSEG-1 as a robust candidate for sustainable biofertilizer applications in nutrient-deficient agricultural soils. Further research into the strain’s unique genetic features and their practical implications could lead to innovative, sustainable agricultural practices and environmental solutions.
