Future anxiety and academic burnout among university students: a short-term three-wave longitudinal conditional process study
Authors: Yanli Tan, Shiqi Liu, Liuhong Zang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFuture anxiety may be relevant to academic burnout among university students, but its short-term temporally ordered association with burnout and the cognitive processes involved remain unclear. This study tested whether brooding rumination mediated this association and whether physical activity habit automaticity moderated the first stage.MethodsA short-term three-wave longitudinal questionnaire survey was conducted among 1271 university students recruited from five provinces in China. Future anxiety and physical activity habit automaticity were measured at Time 1, brooding rumination at Time 2, and academic burnout at Time 3. Pearson correlations, time-lagged mediation, first-stage moderated mediation, simple slope tests, and sensitivity analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 and PROCESS 4.2.ResultsFuture anxiety was positively associated with later brooding rumination (B = 0.275, p < 0.001), and brooding rumination was positively associated with later academic burnout (B = 0.432, p < 0.001). The indirect association from future anxiety to academic burnout through brooding rumination was significant (effect = 0.119, 95% CI [0.092, 0.147]). Physical activity habit automaticity moderated the association between future anxiety and brooding rumination (B = -0.072, p = 0.019), but the increase in explained variance was very small (ΔR² = 0.0037). The index of moderated mediation was significant (index = -0.031, 95% CI [-0.055, -0.007]).ConclusionsBrooding rumination statistically accounted for part of the short-term time-lagged association between future anxiety and academic burnout. Physical activity habit automaticity showed a statistically significant but very small moderating pattern. Because the study was observational, the follow-up period was short, and autoregressive controls for brooding rumination and academic burnout were unavailable, the findings should be interpreted as time-lagged statistical associations rather than causal or intervention effects.Keywords: Future anxiety, Brooding rumination, Academic burnout, Physical activity habit automaticity, Short-term longitudinal study, Moderated mediation, University students

Background

Future anxiety and academic burnout

University students are in a developmental period characterized by academic transition, career planning, interpersonal adjustment, and increasing responsibility for future life choices. Mental health problems are common in this population, and stress-related symptoms often cluster with academic and functional impairment [1]. Studies of college students have also shown that anxiety, depression, and perceived stress are closely tied to academic and social stressors [2]. Large international college-student initiatives further suggest that academic, financial, and future-oriented stressors are central correlates of student mental health [3]. Against this background, future anxiety is a relevant construct for understanding why some students show sustained cognitive distress and academic exhaustion over time.

Future anxiety refers to apprehensive and negatively toned anticipation of personal or broader future events. The construct was originally introduced to capture uncertainty and anxiety directed toward possible future outcomes rather than immediate threats [4]. The Dark Future Scale later provided a concise instrument for assessing this tendency in survey-based studies. Anticipatory stress can continue to activate psychological and physiological systems before a concrete stressor occurs [5]. Conceptually, future anxiety should be distinguished from general anxiety because it is defined by its anticipatory orientation, uncertainty, and cognitive elaboration of possible negative future scenarios. Whereas general anxiety may involve diffuse threat sensitivity and physiological arousal, future anxiety specifically concerns imagined future outcomes that are personally consequential but difficult to resolve in the present. This anticipatory and ambiguous nature may make future anxiety particularly likely to elicit repetitive negative thinking, because students may repeatedly attempt to mentally simulate, predict, or control uncertain academic and career outcomes. Recent longitudinal evidence suggests that future-oriented self-efficacy develops in connection with family and school support [6]. Related evidence links future time perspective and emotional problems with adolescent academic burnout [7]. Symptom-network and digital-behavior studies further indicate that internalizing and future-oriented cognitions are relevant in Chinese adolescent samples [8, 9].

The mediating role of brooding rumination

Brooding rumination may explain why future anxiety becomes academically consequential. Response styles theory argues that repetitive attention to the causes and consequences of distress can prolong negative mood and interfere with adaptive problem solving [10]. The brooding component of rumination captures passive comparison between one’s current situation and an unattained standard, making it especially relevant to future-oriented concerns. Broader reviews of repetitive thought also suggest that perseverative negative cognition can impair problem solving and emotional recovery [11]. Students who repeatedly dwell on uncertain futures may therefore find it more difficult to disengage from threat-related thoughts. Recent network evidence among adolescents with traumatic experiences also suggests that brooding is a maladaptive cognitive pattern that can be linked to poorer resilience-related processes [12].

Empirical findings have extended rumination beyond depression-specific contexts. Rumination has been identified as a transdiagnostic process associated with both depressive and anxiety symptoms [13]. Repetitive negative thinking has also been proposed as a shared cognitive process across emotional disorders [14]. Factor-analytic evidence supports the distinction between brooding and reflection, showing that brooding is especially linked to maladaptive psychological states [15]. These findings suggest that brooding rumination may operate as a cognitive bridge between future-oriented threat anticipation and subsequent burnout-related academic difficulties.

Academic burnout and short-term temporally ordered associations

Academic burnout reflects exhaustion, cynicism toward schoolwork, and feelings of inadequacy in academic contexts. Burnout theory emphasizes that prolonged demands can deplete psychological resources and lead to sustained disengagement [16]. The School Burnout Inventory conceptualizes school-related burnout as a multidimensional academic syndrome rather than a momentary mood state. Research among university students has shown that burnout-related processes are closely related to academic engagement and cross-cultural student adjustment [17]. Life stress has also been linked to academic burnout, supporting the view that sustained stress exposure can become embedded in students’ academic functioning [18]. Within a demands-resources framework, school burnout and engagement may develop as opposite responses to academic demands and available resources [19].

From a time-lagged perspective, brooding rumination is a plausible mediator because it may translate future-oriented stress into later academic depletion. Research on medical and university students has shown that academic stress and psychological distress can accumulate into burnout-relevant outcomes [20]. Conservation of resources theory provides the main theoretical basis for this pathway: students who repeatedly invest cognitive resources in anticipated threats may have fewer resources available for recovery, learning engagement, and adaptive coping [21, 22]. Contemporary resource-loss perspectives further suggest that resource loss can spiral when individuals have difficulty interrupting stress-related processes. This interpretation is also consistent with the longitudinal pattern discussed above, in which future-oriented concerns and emotional problems were related to adolescent academic burnout.

The moderating role of physical activity habit automaticity

Within this focused resource-based framework, physical activity habit automaticity may function as a boundary condition rather than a separate explanatory theory. The Self-Report Behavioural Automaticity Index measures the automaticity component of habit and has been validated as a concise habit measure. Earlier habit research showed that repeated behavior can become automatically cued by context and less dependent on effortful intention [23]. Habit formation studies further indicate that automaticity develops gradually through repeated performance in stable contexts [24]. In the physical activity domain, automatic habits may help students maintain activity even when academic pressure or future uncertainty reduces motivation.

Habit-based accounts are particularly relevant because students often know that physical activity is beneficial but do not always act consistently. Reviews of health behavior habit suggest that automaticity may be an active ingredient linking past behavior with future behavior [25]. Broader analyses of habit in health behavior also caution that habit should not be reduced to frequency alone, because automatic cue-response tendencies are conceptually distinct from how often a behavior occurs [26]. Work on non-conscious processes in health behavior further supports the idea that automatic processes can shape activity patterns independently of deliberate intentions [27]. This distinction is important in the present study because physical activity frequency was controlled as a covariate, allowing the model to focus on the automaticity of physical activity habits. Strong physical activity habit automaticity may attenuate the transition from future anxiety to brooding rumination by reducing the need for effortful decision-making, providing a predictable daily rhythm, and shifting attention from abstract threat simulation to embodied routine-based action. Therefore, automaticity may be more relevant to the cognitive escalation from future anxiety to brooding than to the direct reduction of burnout itself.

Physical activity is also linked to mental health and student adjustment. Meta-analytic evidence shows that intention does not fully translate into physical activity behavior, underscoring the need to examine automatic processes in addition to conscious motivation [28]. Current global guidelines emphasize regular physical activity as a core health behavior across populations [29]. Reviews among young people indicate that physical activity is associated with better mental health outcomes [30]. Meta-meta-analytic work also suggests that physical activity interventions can have beneficial effects on depression and anxiety outcomes [31]. Prospective meta-analytic evidence has further linked higher physical activity with lower risk of later depression [32]. Physical activity has also been discussed as a potentially relevant behavior for anxiety-related outcomes [33]. Recent multinational longitudinal evidence further indicates that physical activity shows longitudinal associations with adolescent well-being, supporting the developmental relevance of activity-related routines [34].

The current study

The present study was guided by two primary theories: conservation of resources theory and response styles theory. Conservation of resources theory was used as the main framework for explaining why anticipatory future-related anxiety may deplete students’ cognitive and emotional resources and relate to later academic burnout. Response styles theory was used to explain why brooding rumination may carry this association over time, because passive and repetitive attention to distress can prolong negative affect and impair adaptive problem solving. To avoid an overly diffuse theoretical framework, physical activity habit automaticity was not introduced as a separate broad theory; instead, it was treated as a habit-based boundary condition within the resource framework. Specifically, stronger automaticity may help students maintain routinized activity under stress, thereby weakening the association between future anxiety and brooding. Accordingly, we proposed that future anxiety would show a short-term temporally ordered association with subsequent academic burnout through brooding rumination. We further proposed that physical activity habit automaticity would moderate the first stage of this indirect association, such that the association between future anxiety and brooding rumination would be weaker among students with stronger automatic physical activity habits.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study employed a short-term three-wave longitudinal questionnaire design. Participants were recruited from five provinces in China, although the sampling frame was not designed to be nationally representative. To preserve institutional anonymity during the review process, the specific province-level and institutional identifiers are not disclosed in the manuscript. Future anxiety and physical activity habit automaticity were assessed at Time 1, brooding rumination was assessed at Time 2, and academic burnout was assessed at Time 3. A self-report questionnaire survey was conducted in 2026. The three waves were administered sequentially, with Time 2 approximately three weeks after Time 1 and Time 3 approximately six to seven weeks after Time 2. The overall observation interval was approximately 10 weeks. Therefore, the design allowed temporal ordering among the study variables, but it may not fully capture the longer developmental process of academic burnout. This temporal ordering was used because longitudinal mediation models are more appropriate than cross-sectional mediation models when the research question concerns indirect associations over time [35]. The final analytic sample included 1271 students aged 18 to 26 years (M = 20.23, SD = 1.53); 583 were male (45.9%) and 688 were female (54.1%). Grade distribution was as follows: first year, 438 (34.5%); second year, 384 (30.2%); third year, 301 (23.7%); fourth year, 100 (7.9%); and master’s students or above, 48 (3.8%). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xinjiang Normal University (approval number: XJNU2026LLSC53), and all participants provided informed consent before completing the survey.

Quality control and matching

Data screening was conducted before wave matching. Responses were excluded if participants did not provide consent, failed the attention-check item, completed the questionnaire faster than the minimum duration threshold, or submitted duplicate responses. The valid attention-check responses were 4 at Time 1, 3 at Time 2, and 5 at Time 3. The minimum duration thresholds were 82 s at Time 1, 53 s at Time 2, and 51 s at Time 3. For duplicate submissions, the last valid submission was retained. After quality control and anonymous matching across the three waves, 1271 students were included in the final analytic sample.

Measures

All core psychometric instruments used in this study were based on previously published scales rather than newly developed for the present study. Specifically, the Dark Future Scale, the Self-Report Behavioural Automaticity Index, the brooding subscale of the Ruminative Responses Scale, and the School Burnout Inventory were previously published instruments and are cited below. The physical-activity-specific wording of the Self-Report Behavioural Automaticity Index was adapted for the present study, but no new multi-item psychometric scale was developed. Physical activity frequency and employment/graduate-study stress were assessed as single-item covariates rather than newly developed scales.

Future anxiety was measured at Time 1 using the five-item Dark Future Scale, which was previously published and validated for assessing anxiety directed toward possible negative future outcomes [36]. The scale was not developed for the present study. The Chinese survey wording was prepared from the published items and checked by the research team before administration. Items were scored on a 7-point response scale, and mean scores were computed, with higher scores indicating greater future anxiety. No item reversal was applied in the present scoring. In the present sample, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.803.

Physical activity habit automaticity was measured at Time 1 using a four-item physical activity adaptation of the Self-Report Behavioural Automaticity Index, a previously published brief measure of habit automaticity [37]. The scale was not newly developed for the present study; the item wording was adapted to refer specifically to physical activity, but no new multi-item psychometric scale was developed. The Chinese survey wording was prepared from the published items and checked by the research team for clarity and content relevance before administration; no formal back-translation procedure was conducted. Items were scored on a 7-point response scale, and mean scores were computed, with higher scores indicating stronger automatic physical activity habits. No item reversal was applied in the present scoring. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.819. No additional pre-testing or independent validation of this adapted version was conducted before the main survey, which is acknowledged as a limitation.

Brooding rumination was measured at Time 2 using the five-item brooding subscale derived from the previously published Ruminative Responses Scale [38]. The subscale was not developed for the present study. The Chinese survey wording was prepared from the published items and checked by the research team before administration. Items were scored on a 4-point response scale, and mean scores were computed, with higher scores indicating stronger brooding rumination. No item reversal was applied in the present scoring. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.860.

Academic burnout was measured at Time 3 using the nine-item School Burnout Inventory, which was previously published and validated for assessing exhaustion, cynicism, and inadequacy in school contexts [39]. The scale was not developed for the present study. The Chinese survey wording was prepared from the published items and checked by the research team before administration. Items were scored on a 6-point response scale, and mean scores were computed, with higher scores indicating greater academic burnout. No item reversal was applied in the present scoring. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.904.

Gender, grade, and physical activity frequency were included as covariates in the main models. Age was examined in sensitivity analyses because age and grade were highly correlated (r = 0.855, p < 0.001), and grade was considered the more direct indicator of academic stage. Employment/graduate-study stress was also used in a sensitivity analysis. Physical activity frequency and employment/graduate-study stress were single-item covariates rather than multi-item psychometric scales. No new multi-item scale was developed for this study.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics, reliability coefficients, attrition comparisons, Pearson correlations, short-term time-lagged mediation analyses, moderated mediation analyses, and sensitivity analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 and PROCESS macro version 4.2. Mediation was tested using PROCESS Model 4, and first-stage moderated mediation was tested using PROCESS Model 7. Bias in cross-sectional mediation analysis was considered when interpreting the temporally ordered design [40]. Additional guidance on longitudinal mediation informed the cautious interpretation of short-term indirect associations over time [41]. First-stage moderated mediation was tested according to moderated-mediation recommendations [42]. The index of moderated mediation was interpreted following Hayes [43]. PROCESS-based regression models were implemented according to Hayes’ conditional process framework [44]. Simple slopes were probed at one standard deviation below and above the moderator mean following standard interaction procedures [45]. Regression coefficients were interpreted using conventional applied regression guidance [46]. Predictors involved in the interaction term were mean-centered before the product term was computed. Bootstrap confidence intervals were based on 5000 resamples. Because the interaction between future anxiety and physical activity habit automaticity was specified a priori as the only hypothesized interaction test, no formal multiple-comparison correction was applied. Because brooding rumination and academic burnout were not repeatedly measured at all waves, autoregressive effects of the mediator and outcome could not be controlled. Therefore, the models were designed to test short-term time-lagged statistical associations rather than strict causal longitudinal mediation.

Common method bias was assessed using Harman’s single-factor test and a single-factor confirmatory model. The interpretation of common method bias followed behavioral research recommendations [47]. Additional guidance on method bias in social science research was also considered [48]. Missing-data patterns were interpreted with reference to missing-data methodology [49]. Complete-item sensitivity analysis was considered in light of applied missing-data guidance [50]. Broader missing-data decisions were guided by applied missing-data analysis texts [51]. Confirmatory factor analysis was estimated using maximum-likelihood estimation in Python 3.11 and interpreted with reference to structural equation modeling guidance [52]. Additional CFA interpretation followed applied CFA guidance [53]. Reliability was interpreted in relation to general test theory [54]. Multivariate assumptions were evaluated using standard applied multivariate guidance [55].

Moderated mediation analysis

The first-stage moderated mediation results are shown in Table 5. Physical activity habit automaticity was significantly and negatively associated with brooding rumination (B = -0.243, p < 0.001). The interaction between future anxiety and physical activity habit automaticity was also significant (B = -0.072, p = 0.019), indicating that physical activity habit automaticity moderated the association between future anxiety and brooding rumination. However, the increase in explained variance was small (ΔR² = 0.0037), equivalent to approximately 0.37% additional variance explained in brooding rumination, so the moderation effect should be interpreted cautiously.

Discussion

This study examined the short-term time-lagged association between future anxiety and academic burnout among university students recruited from five provinces in China, as well as the mediating role of brooding rumination and the moderating role of physical activity habit automaticity. The findings supported a partial mediation model. Students with higher future anxiety at Time 1 reported higher brooding rumination at Time 2, and higher brooding rumination showed a short-term time-lagged association with higher academic burnout at Time 3. The direct association between future anxiety and academic burnout remained significant after brooding rumination was included, indicating that brooding rumination statistically accounted for part, but not all, of this short-term temporally ordered association.

The mediating role of brooding rumination is consistent with the view that future-oriented uncertainty may become more detrimental when students repeatedly focus on its causes, consequences, and possible negative outcomes. Future anxiety may keep students’ attention oriented toward uncertain academic and career scenarios. When this apprehension is accompanied by brooding rumination, students may become trapped in passive comparison and repetitive self-focused thinking. Such a cognitive pattern may consume psychological resources that would otherwise be available for academic recovery, planning, and problem solving. Therefore, brooding rumination can be understood as a plausible short-term temporally ordered cognitive association, rather than a confirmed causal mechanism, linking future-oriented uncertainty with subsequent academic burnout. This interpretation is also consistent with the aforementioned evidence identifying brooding as a maladaptive cognitive pattern in adolescent adjustment. The persistence of the direct association also suggests that future anxiety may be related to academic burnout through factors other than brooding rumination, such as sleep disturbance, reduced academic motivation, avoidance, social withdrawal, or digital-context coping processes.

The moderating result further showed that physical activity habit automaticity statistically moderated the association between future anxiety and brooding rumination. Students with stronger automatic physical activity habits showed a weaker positive association between future anxiety and subsequent brooding. This pattern suggests that automatic physical activity routines may provide a relatively stable self-regulatory context when students face future-related uncertainty. However, the effect size of the interaction was very small (ΔR² = 0.0037), indicating that the interaction explained only approximately 0.37% additional variance in brooding rumination. Thus, the result should be interpreted as a statistically detectable but very small boundary condition rather than evidence that physical activity habits substantively alter the cognitive consequences of future anxiety. Given the tiny incremental variance explained, this moderation effect is unlikely to be practically meaningful for intervention development when considered in isolation. Recent moderated-mediation research on resilience factors has similarly shown that buffering effects in adolescent affective-distress pathways should be interpreted cautiously when their magnitude is small [56].

The conditional indirect effects provide a more detailed interpretation of this pattern. The indirect association from future anxiety to academic burnout through brooding rumination was significant at low, mean, and high levels of physical activity habit automaticity, but the magnitude of this indirect association decreased as automaticity increased. This result resembles previous longitudinal moderated-mediation work among Chinese college students in which physical activity was considered as a factor in a psychological-risk association [57]. Comparable three-wave longitudinal research further suggests that mediation and moderation findings should be interpreted with attention to stable between-person dispositions as well as time-lagged associations. In addition, the aforementioned multinational longitudinal evidence suggests that physical activity may be relevant to adolescent well-being over time, although the present study focuses specifically on habit automaticity rather than activity volume.

This study contributes to the student mental health literature by focusing on future anxiety rather than general distress, thereby addressing a form of anticipatory stress that is particularly relevant to students facing academic, employment, and life-transition decisions. Identifying brooding rumination as a short-term temporally ordered mediator also extends work on repetitive negative thinking by showing how future-oriented anxiety may be statistically linked with subsequent academic burnout through a specific ruminative style. The moderation analysis further shifts attention from physical activity frequency alone to the automatic and routinized nature of physical activity behavior. Because physical activity frequency was controlled, the moderation result points to the habitual quality of physical activity rather than simply to how often students were active. Developmental-context research indicates that adolescent and student adjustment can be shaped by family, school, peer, and broader sociocultural contexts. Cross-cultural evidence on parent-adolescent warmth and internalizing symptoms further supports the need to interpret the present association within its institutional and regional setting [58].

Several practical implications can be drawn cautiously. Universities may help students identify repetitive future-oriented thinking and develop strategies for disengaging from unproductive brooding. Programs promoting physical activity may also benefit from habit-oriented components, such as stable cues, routine scheduling, and gradual integration of activity into daily life. However, these implications should be regarded as preliminary and hypothesis-generating. The present findings do not show that strengthening physical activity habit automaticity will necessarily reduce brooding rumination or academic burnout. Given the very small interaction effect, physical activity habit automaticity is unlikely to be a meaningful stand-alone intervention target for burnout prevention. Future intervention studies should test multi-component strategies that combine habit-based physical activity promotion with cognitive reappraisal, mindfulness, academic counseling, or stress-management components.

This study has several limitations. First and most critically, although the three-wave design provides stronger temporal ordering than a cross-sectional design, the data remain observational and cannot establish causal effects. The study did not include baseline measures of brooding rumination or academic burnout at each wave and therefore could not adjust for autoregressive effects. As a result, the findings should be interpreted as short-term time-lagged statistical associations rather than evidence of strict causal longitudinal mediation. Future longitudinal studies should measure the predictor, mediator, and outcome repeatedly across at least four waves or use latent growth, cross-lagged, or intensive longitudinal models to distinguish within-person change from stable between-person differences [59]. Network-based longitudinal designs may also help identify symptom-level dynamics that are not captured by conventional regression models. Second, the total follow-up period was approximately 10 weeks, which may be insufficient to capture the longer developmental process of academic burnout. Academic burnout is typically conceptualized as a syndrome that develops over extended exposure to academic demands, and the present short-term associations may reflect fluctuations in future anxiety, brooding rumination, and exhaustion-related experiences rather than the chronic formation of burnout. Longer follow-up periods are needed to examine how academic burnout develops and stabilizes over time. Third, although participants were recruited from five provinces in China, the sample was not nationally representative, and the specific province-level and institutional identifiers were not disclosed to preserve institutional anonymity during the review process. Only 3.8% of participants were master’s students or above. Therefore, the findings should not be generalized to all Chinese university students without caution, particularly to students from other geographic regions, institutional types, or postgraduate populations. Future research should also test whether the conditional process differs across academic stages, such as first-year versus fourth-year undergraduate students or undergraduate versus postgraduate students. The aforementioned multinational work on physical activity and well-being suggests that developmental adjustment processes may vary across geographic settings. Cross-cultural evidence on family-context differences and internalizing symptoms also supports the need for replication across broader relational and cultural contexts. Fourth, all variables were measured using self-report questionnaires, which may introduce reporting bias. Fifth, although the adapted SRBAI-PA showed acceptable internal consistency and supportive measurement evidence in the present sample, internal consistency alone does not establish content or structural validity. The physical-activity-specific adaptation was not independently validated before hypothesis testing, and future studies should further examine its psychometric properties in Chinese university samples. Finally, unmeasured variables such as sleep, socioeconomic background, clinical symptoms, and other academic stressors may have influenced the observed associations. Intervention or intensive longitudinal designs are needed to test whether strengthening physical activity habit automaticity can reduce brooding rumination or academic burnout over time.

Conclusions

This short-term three-wave longitudinal conditional process study found that brooding rumination statistically accounted for part of the short-term time-lagged association between future anxiety and academic burnout among university students. Physical activity habit automaticity showed a statistically significant but very small moderation of the association between future anxiety and brooding rumination, and the index of moderated mediation was significant. These findings suggest a short-term temporally ordered statistical association in which future anxiety was linked with subsequent academic burnout through brooding rumination, with the first stage of the association varying by physical activity habit automaticity. Because the study was observational, the follow-up period was short, and autoregressive effects for all core constructs were not controlled, the findings should not be interpreted as evidence of causal or intervention effects.
