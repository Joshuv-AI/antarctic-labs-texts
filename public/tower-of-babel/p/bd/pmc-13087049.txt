Skin metabolomic response to medicinal shrub Myrothamnus flabellifolia and effect on skin phenotypes
Authors: Misset Gabrielle, Gueniche Audrey, Bénizé Amélie-Marie, Flament Frederic, Cavusoglu Nükhet, Sébastien Marque, Aguilar Luc, Barilleau Laure, Jacquet Angélique, Misra Namita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Skin aging in humans is complex and is induced by internal and external factors. Studies have pointed towards biological processes including alterations in DNA repair and stability, mitochondrial function, cell cycle and apoptosis, ubiquitin-induced proteolysis, and cellular metabolism dysfunction. Untargeted metabolomics is key to better characterize and understand biological processes involved in (accelerated) aging at the level of multiple organ and tissues. Characterize metabolic changes in relation to human skin aging and explore if/how an extract from dessication tolerant medicinal herb, Myrothamnus flabellifolia, could affect skin aging and related metabolites. We evaluated clinical characteristics and untargeted metabolomic profiles of the skin of 32 individuals both before (D0) and after (D56) the application of a specific formulation containing extract from M. flabellifolia. Using conditional independence networks we explored how the formulation affected the correlation structure amongst skin metabolic features. Using an MWAS approach, we explored which molecular features were (i) dysregulated at the end of the experiment, and (ii) associated with changes in clinical characteristics during that period. Our analyses indicated clear metabolic changes and clinical improvement of skin radiance and texture after 56 days of treatment. Of the 419 assayed metabolites, 109 were either dysregulated at D56 or associated with at least one clinical sign. Of these, 12 compounds were detected in the green gel or red powder, the two active components of the extract, in particular trehalose, which was detected in both the green gel and the red powder. Collectively, this study demonstrates biochemical changes after application of the active formula, suggestive of its embodiment. Changes in the skin metabolome associated with the clinical signs we assayed helps hypothesizing molecular pathways involved in improvement of skin quality and aging signs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-39282-0.

Introduction

The process of skin aging in humans is complex and is induced by internal and external (e.g. environmental) factors. Although the understanding of underlying mechanisms remains partial, several studies have identified alterations in DNA repair and stability, mitochondrial function, cell cycle and apoptosis and cellular metabolism alterations as being influential1. Untargeted metabolomics facilitates the characterization and better understanding of the aging process at a molecular level along with the evaluation of the efficacy of topical applications. Some active ingredients are metabolites including coenzyme Q10 or vitamins that can directly act as effectors (antioxidants) or free amino-acids that replenish/integrate into the host metabolism and bring in benefits that are clinically measured2,3.

Botanical ingredients including extracts that are rich in biologically active substances of the plant may exhibit wide range of properties that are both medicinal and/or provide skin benefits such as wrinkles, pigmentation, and inflammation. These include primary metabolites, directly involved in the normal plant growth, development, and reproduction and secondary metabolites that are key for the plant survival in adverse/extreme conditions4,5. Myrothamnus flabellifolia, also known as the desiccation-tolerant resurrection plant, has colonized a unique and harsh niche in Southern Africa, that provides little protection from elements. It has been harvested for use in traditional preparations and several studies have confirmed its medicinal properties6. Metabolomic profiling of M. Flabellifolia has primarily been used for phylogenic studies and to identify potential medicinal components7. However, there are no studies exploring effects on the skin or on the identification of these effective compounds.

In the present study we propose to explore if and to what extent, extracts from M. Flabellifolia could potentially affect the skin metabolome and clinical signs. Specifically, we evaluated biochemical changes on skin of 32 individuals after application of a formulation containing an extract from desiccation tolerant medicinal herb, M. flabellifolia, using untargeted metabolomics analysis of skin epidermis at baseline and after treatment on day 56. Skin surface parameters were measured by clinical scoring and instrumental evaluation. To get a deeper insight on the influence of the individual components of M. Flabellifola, we analyzed the metabolites of the plant extract. Finally, we performed an integrative analysis of the clinical and instrumental measures, skin metabolites and the components of M. Flabellifola and the formulation gel.

Materials & methods

Extract and formulation

The M. flabellifola is a resurrection plant from south regions of Africa. The extract used was obtained from BASF Beauty Care Solutions and none of the authors was involved in the collection of these extracts. BASF Beauty Care Solutions harvested manually between May & September at the driest period in the country: this is the period when the air humidity and precipitation are at its lowest in the year with 79 days of dryness on average. BASF was granted permission to collect the M. flabellifolia from Chivi district, Zimbabwe. The plant was identified from its aerial parts by an expert phytochemist from BASF. Because biosamples collected for the study were neither a living specimen nor seeds, no voucher specimen was deposited in a public herbarium.

From desiccated state, the aerial parts of M. flabellifola were subjected to controlled grinding. Water was chosen as the extraction solvent. The extraction takes place in a double envelope reactor at a specific and hot temperature, 85°C and the extraction time were optimized to collect the rehydrated plant cells and specifically the secondary metabolites of the flavonoid family like gallic acid. The extract was subjected to a separation step to keep essential molecules. It is at the end filtrated to obtain a purified and homogeneous filtrate.

This was then formulated, by adding a selection of compounds (glycols, xanthan gum) which allowed the stabilization of the plant active fraction. The active fraction was concentrated at 0.56% in the raw material. The raw material was added at 2% in a specific aqueous gel carbomer formulation.

Study design and descriptive analyses

55 Asian women aged between 45–60 years representing all skin types and living in Singapore were recruited. Following the 15-day wash out period, during which participants used a neutral moisturizing cream, women applied on the face the formulation containing M. flabellifolia twice a day for 56 days. Skin characteristics were evaluated at inclusion (D0) and after 56 days (D56) using first on-site dermatologist assessment of 3 established skin characteristics: radiance, texture (capturing skin grain fineness) and smoothness. We used a 10-point scale based on modified Griffiths’ scale (0: best condition and 9: worst condition). We also collected objective measurement of 20 characteristics of the facial skin using the SkincamR tool from Newtone Technologies, France8 measuring 4 parameters linked to smoothness on overall face (wrinkle area, length, mean depth and volume) and 8 colorimetric parameters on cheeks and on temples (iii) a panel of 15 trained experts quantitatively assessing, via skin aging Atlas vol.2, ten skin characteristics relating to wrinkles, spots, sebaceous pores, ptosis from pictures of the volunteers taken by ColorFace© in diffuse light (standard60) and crossed polarized.

At D56, a self-assessment questionnaire was submitted to all participants.

For all these measurements, we compared the values measured at D0 to those obtained at D56 using a student T-test.

Data from our expert panel was analysed using a linear mixed model where changes in skin characteristics were the outcome variable, time (D0/D56) was a fixed effect and expert ID (15 modalities) was included as a random intercept to account for variability across judges.

For self-assessment data, proportions were calculated to gauge participants’ perceptions of their skin changes post-treatment at D56.

Sample collection and metabolomic profiling

D-Squame samples were collected from a subset (n = 32) of the study population from the cheek area as per manufacturer’s instructions. D-SQUAME is the registered name of a circular strip tape used to sample stratum corneum, Produced by CuDerm Corporation (Dallas, TX, U.S.A). D-Squame sampling on the cheek was done both at D0 and D56.

The untargeted metabolomic analysis was performed at Metabolon, Inc (Morrisville, NC). All samples were stored at − 80 °C until processed. Samples were prepared using the automated MicroLab STAR system from Hamilton Company. Several recovery standards were added prior to the first step in the extraction process for QC purposes. To remove protein, dissociate small molecules bound to protein or trapped in the precipitated protein matrix, and to recover chemically diverse metabolites, proteins were precipitated with methanol under vigorous shaking for 2 min (Glen Mills GenoGrinder 2000) followed by centrifugation. The resulting extract was divided into five fractions: two for analysis by two separate reverse phases (RP)/UPLC-MS/MS methods with positive ion mode electrospray ionization (ESI), one for analysis by RP/UPLC-MS/MS with negative ion mode ESI, one for analysis by HILIC/UPLC-MS/MS with negative ion mode ESI, and one sample was reserved for backup. All methods utilized a Waters ACQUITY (UPLC), and a Thermo Scientific Q-Exactive high resolution/accurate mass spectrometer interfaced with a heated electrospray ionization (HESI-II) source and Orbitrap mass analyser operated at 35,000 mass resolution. The MS analysis alternated between MS and data-dependent MS scans using dynamic exclusion. The scan range varied slightly between methods but covered 70–1000 m/z. Compounds were identified by comparison to library entries of purified standards or recurrent unknown entities based on retention time, molecular weight, preferred adducts and in-source fragments, as well as associated MS spectra and curated by visual inspection for quality control using software developed by Metabolon9.

We excluded metabolic features with > 50% missing values and imputed missing data in the remaining features using a uniform distribution with a support ranging between 0 and the minimal detected value for that specific compound. The data were log-2 transformed, standardised to ensure comparability of the effect size estimated across features.

Using the same approach, we measured the levels of same metabolites in red powder and green gel, the two active ingredients of the facial cream.

Graphical models

We used graphical LASSO models with stability selection, as implemented in the R package ‘Sharp’10,11, to infer undirected conditional independence networks across the metabolites at D0 and D56, separately. One hundred 80% subsamples of the data were drawn, and graphical LASSO models fit to each subsample. A stability score was calculated for each edge based on the proportion of subsamples in which each edge was selected. The sparsity (e.g. penalty parameter) and selection proportion threshold hyper-parameters were jointly calibrated by maximisation of an internal stability score10.

Metabolome-wide association

We first used logistic regression models to evaluate possible changes in each compound (as predictor) between D0 and D56 (binary outcome). We also investigated the association between each of the individual clinical measurements and the metabolic feature using linear models setting the measured levels of each compound as predictor and adjusting for the D0-D56 indicator to control for possible changes happening in the metabolites and outcomes during the 56 days of the experiment. We adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction method to control the family-wise error rate (FWER) at 5%.

Ethical statement

The study was conducted in Singapore, where it obtained ethical approval PIEC/2020/036 from Parkway Independent ethics committee (PIEC), Parkway Pantai Connection One (Tower One), Blk 168 Jalan Bukit Merah #03–01 Singapore 150,168. All participants from the study have provided written informed consent to use their data for research purposes. The design and implementation of study were carried out according to relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki.

Discussion

Our analyses suggest an improvement of radiance and skin texture after 56 days of treatment with M. flabellifolia formulation, based on dermatologist assessment, and improvements in sebaceous pores as graded by experts. More modest changes in (cheeks and temples) colour was detected at the end of the treatment using objective (instrumental) measurements. The 56 days treatment also corresponded to an unambiguous perceived improvement in 27 skin characteristics via self-assessment questionnaire. Furthermore, we analysed all the major metabolites superpathways in clinical samples with an aim to detect any modulation in skin metabolic processes as a result of application of the plant formulation. We also analysed the metabolite components of the plant extract and the formulation to enable a deeper interpretation of metabolic changes post treatment.

Previous studies of M. flabellifolia have resulted in characterisation of its major compounds and one these is the phenolic glycoside, hydroquinone beta-D-glucopyranoside or Arbutin5. Arbutin has long been recognized as an inhibitor of melanogenesis12,13 a function which is likely to confer protective benefits to plants in a high UV environment. We detected high levels of hydroquinone beta-D-glucopyranoside in M. flabellifolia extract but not on skin surface suggesting its rapid metabolism. Although this compound is known for its depigmenting and antioxidant properties, we could not show its contribution towards skin radiance improvement, a measured clinical endpoint in our study.

We detected other metabolites such as saccharides that were present in both extract and post-treatment human skin. Of these Trehalose, a hydration supporting saccharide, highlighted in desiccation response of M. flabellifolia,14 was previously detected in M. flabelliofolia15,16 and (at high levels) in the extract and in skin samples we presently analysed. However, we could not establish an association linking the levels of trehalose in the skin and the clinical measurements in our study. Alongside these saccharides, TCA cycle intermediates such as succinate emerged as post-treatment, potentially serving as energy substrates with hypothetical roles in mitochondrial function based on broader cellular physiology. Elevated weakly and without statistical ties to radiance or texture in this study, our observation warrants further investigations.

Two other classes of compounds that were observed to be elevated after treatment and were also found in the active ingredients were Vitamin E and purine related metabolites. Our observations suggest an increased utilization of alpha-tocopherol compared to other forms of vitamin E (gamma-tocopherol/beta-tocopherol) that were found in the green gel. Different forms of vitamin E have differing activity and alpha-tocopherol is selectively utilized in humans17,18. This interpretation is based on the established biological mechanism and is not directly supported by clinical associations from the present study.

Inosine is another metabolite that was detected post treatment but not in the active ingredients. Inosine is an intermediate in the degradation of purines and purine nucleosides to uric acid/allatoin that may provide several benefits19,20.

An analysis of metabolites on skin surface after treatments revealed a reduced protein degradation after treatment as inferred from reduced free amino acid levels. Previously we have reported higher levels of amino acids on skin surface of individuals exposed to chronic pollution, an indication of a compromised barrier and an enhanced repair/compensation in skin21. Enhanced amino acid have also been associated with aging skin profile3,22. Our observations on amino acid trend in current study are speculative and further validation would be required to establish any improvement in skin hydration and prevention of water loss.

An additional analysis of gamma-glutamyl amino acids showed the similar decreased trend that could indicate an altered gamma-glutamyl transferase (GCT) activity in treated subjects. Interestingly, enhanced detection of gamma-glutamyl l-leucine with Psoriasis vulgaris23. Overall, the reduced levels of peptides, amino acids and gamma-glutamyl amino acids indicate decreased protein turnover and hydration of skin following treatment.

Finally, an analysis of the metabolomics signature with clinical skin attributes revealed a correlation of certain by-products of histidine metabolism (imidazole propionate, imidazole lactate) with radiance. Previously we have observed an enhanced histidine metabolism in individuals living in polluted environment and the metabolites correlated with skin microbiome 24. Twenty-eight metabolites, mainly lipids, correlated with skin texture. The relevance of these observations needs to be further explored. Some of these could be indicative of lipolysis. For instance, elevated levels of 1-myristoleoylgycerol, a monoacylglycerol (MAG) are produced from breakdown of Triacylglycerols (TAGs) that make up over 50% of the human epidermal lipids and sebum. These TAGS are hydrolyzed to release MAGs through lipolysis25. These hydrolyzed species have additional hydration properties that probably manifested as improved skin texture in our study. Similar observations of an increase in other lipid species open new avenues to explore biological mechanisms that could be connected to clinical signs of skin.

Conclusions

In this study we show biochemical changes after application of the M. flabellifolius extract in a formulation gel. Molecular analysis of the plant extract indicates an impact on skin metabolism pathways in volunteers that could lead to an improvement of certain skin parameters such as radiance, texture and pores . Some of the changes in host metabolic processes could be linked to the components of the herb, such as sugars, purine, and vitamin metabolism. The metabolites that correlated with skin phenotype changes were unrelated to plant and formulation compounds. An important point regarding our study is that we preferred a non-invasive sampling of the skin for metabolomics analysis. Therefore, we cannot extrapolate these results to deeper layers of the skin or have a better understanding of bioavailability and stability of the compounds. There may be an indirect impact of the treatment on molecular pathways, and this needs further exploration. Lastly, larger cohorts with longer period of treatment and placebo-controlled study design would also be a step-forward in this direction.
