Association between cathepsins and skin cancers: A bidirectional two‐sample Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Xinyi Ma, Haocheng Zhuang, Mingze Xu, Fangzhen Hou, Chunyu Xue
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSeveral cathepsins have been identified as being involved in the development of cancer. Nevertheless, the connection between cathepsins and skin cancers remained highly elusive.MethodsA bidirectional Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis was performed to investigate the causal association between cathepsins and skin malignancies. The genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) data for cathepsins, malignant melanoma (MM), and basal cell carcinoma (BCC) were obtained from European research. The primary method employed was inverse variance weighted. In addition, MR‐Egger, weighted median, weighted mode, and simple mode were also executed. Sensitivity analysis was performed using Cochran's Q test, MR‐Egger, and MR‐PRESSO.ResultsFrom univariable MR (UVMR), cathepsin H, and S were determined to have a causal relationship with BCC. Additionally, cathepsin H was identified as associated with MM. Multivariable MR (MVMR) showed that after correcting for risk factors of skin carcinoma, cathepsin H was detected to be protective against BCC, whereas cathepsin S has been observed as a risk factor for BCC. No substantial pleiotropy and heterogeneity were identified in the sensitivity analysis.ConclusionThis study was the first to establish a direct link between cathepsins and skin malignancies. Cathepsin H and S have the potential to serve as new biomarkers for BCC, offering valuable assistance in the prompt identification, treatment, and prevention of the disease. Nevertheless, additional clinical trials are required to validate our findings.Keywords: basal cell carcinoma, cathepsins, genome‐wide association study, malignant melanoma, Mendelian randomization, skin cancer

INTRODUCTION

Skin carcinoma, the growth of abnormal skin cells, are the fifth most common cancer, particularly prevalent in the White population. 1 Approximately 20% of Americans are projected to develop skin cancer after reaching the age of 70, 2 and the American Cancer Society predicts an increase in skin cancers in 2024 as compared to last year. 3 The incidence of melanoma is increasing in European countries such as the UK and Denmark. 4 Considering the increasing incidence, early prevention is of the utmost importance, as these diseases impose a heavy burden on society and the health care system. Skin cancer is categorized into two main types: malignant melanoma (MM) and nonmelanoma skin carcinoma. The latter encompasses basal cell carcinoma (BCC), squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), and other rarer forms of skin cancer. 5 Ultraviolet radiation, sunburns, and a light skin tone are all known risk factors for developing skin carcinoma. It develops as a result of complex pathophysiological processes, whereby cancer cells interact with their surroundings, releasing a variety of cell signaling molecules to regulate protein metabolism, promote tumor angiogenesis, and evade recognition and attack by immune cells, 6 thus making them capable of rapidly expanding, invading, and metastasizing to other areas of the body. Proteolytic systems play a crucial role in carcinoma cell invasion and metastasis by breaking down the extracellular matrix. Cathepsins are the primary constituents of the cysteine protease group and the most prevalent proteases found in lysosomes. There are eleven major cathepsins in human bodies, which are involved in different physiological to maintain the stability of the internal environment, including a range of functions such as protein and lipid metabolism, coagulation, immune response, hormone release, peptide synthesis, and extracellular matrix degradation. 7 In order to prevent and treat skin cancer, it is helpful to fully comprehend the connection between cathepsins and skin malignancies.

Prior research has shown that cathepsin B, D, H, and K have the capacity to facilitate the invasion and metastasis of melanoma and BCC. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 Intracellular cathepsins degrade metastasis suppressor, NM23‐H1, which can inhibit cancer progression, 13 whereas extracellular cathepsins degrade a variety of proteins associated with cancer cell metastasis such as E‐cadherin and laminin 7 and so on. Nevertheless, different types of cathepsins play different roles in promoting or inhibiting the occurrence and progression of cancer, and the role of various types of skin carcinoma is not the same. Giammarioli proposed that cathepsin B stimulates Cytochrome C release from mitochondria in melanoma cells, thereby activating caspase‐dependent apoptosis and inhibiting MM proliferation. 14 The absence of cathepsin L has been demonstrated to result in elevated recycling of epidermal growth factor (EGF) and disruption of autocrine mitogenic signaling, 15 , 16 leading to abnormal proliferation of keratinocytes, which indicates that cathepsin L has a protective function in skin cancer. 17

A systematic, comprehensive, and definitive analysis of the relationship between cathepsins and skin cancers is currently unavailable. Mendelian randomization (MR) is a highly effective strategy for overcoming the limitations of prior research caused by confounding factors and reverse causality. It offers an innovative approach to determining whether cathepsins are the cause or the result of skin cancers. MR uses genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to investigate causal links involving modifiable exposures and outcomes in observational research. It follows the principles of Mendel's law of inheritance, which randomly assigns genetic variations during meiosis. More recently, MR analyses have paid increasing attention to skin tumors. Yu et al. did not discover any causal connection among psoriasis, psoriatic arthritis, and skin cancers, 18 and Li raised that a preventive approach to childhood sunburns could result in a reduction in MM and nonmelanoma skin carcinoma incidence. 19 Given the absence of a comprehensive and systematic comprehension regarding the causal link between cathepsins and skin cancer, further research was conducted utilizing MR.

METHODS

Data source

In this research, we employed a bidirectional two‐sample MR approach to evaluate the causal relationship between cathepsins and skin cancers. The data source is specified in Table 1. The data used in this work are publicly accessible summary statistics from a Genome‐wide association study (GWAS). As a result, no further ethical approval was necessary. In order to ensure the validity of the causal inference obtained from MR analysis, the IVs must fulfill three fundamental assumptions: (A) the relevance assumption states that IVs should be strongly linked to the exposure characteristics. (B) The independence assumption suggests that IVs should not be connected to any confounding factors. (C) The exclusion‐restriction assumption claims that the causal pathway should only involve the exposure of interest. 20

The INTERVAL project, 21 which involved 3301 European participants, provided genetic tools for measuring the concentrations that varied cathepsins (µg/L). Every donor was required to fill out the trial consent form, and The National Research Ethics Service (11/EE/0538) approved the INTERVAL project. Access to summary data is available at the following URL: https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk.

The genetic instruments for MM 22 and BCC 23 were acquired by browsing the GWAS Catalog (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/home) and applied from fast genome‐wide association (GWA) generalized linear mixed model (GLMM)‐based methods. The trait label of MM is described in the GWAS Catalog as “a primary melanoma arising from atypical melanocytes in the skin”. BCC is “a carcinoma involving the basal cells”. Summarized MM and BCC data from the summary‐level MR analysis comprised 456 348 subjects (2824 MM and 453 524 control cases) and 307 684 subjects (20 791 BCC and 286 893 control cases) from European ancestry, respectively. The diagnosis of skin malignancies was made through histological assessment, and all the patients included in the study met the clinical criteria.

Furthermore, the summary data of GWAS for the known risk factors of skin carcinoma, including sunburns, skin color, and ease of skin tanning, were acquired via the Integrative Epidemiology Unit (IEU) Open GWAS Project, accessible at https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/.

Genetic variants selection

As genetic IVs, prominent single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) connected to cathepsins, MM, and BCC were extracted from GWAS summary statistics. The IVs associated with cathepsins were chosen for MR analyses based on the following criteria: 24 (A) p‐values (5 × 10−6) that were lower than the genome‐wide significance level established in the related study. Additionally, SNPs that exhibited significant associations with MM and BCC (p < 5 × 10−8); 25 this value was determined considering the sample size limitation) were chosen. (B) An r 2 value of linkage disequilibrium (LD) among instruments less than 0.001 within a 10 000 kb interval. Weak IV bias tends to influence the reliability and precision of the estimates when the IVs present a limited cause for the exposure. To prevent this, the statistical reliability of the IVs after screening was determined via F statistic (SNPs with F‐statistics < 10 were subsequently excluded). 26 A causal relationship is not possible between genetic variation and outcome; alternatively, the same relationship may be transmitted via exposure factors. Thus, SNPs were then obtained and isolated from the outcome data, and those which substantially linked with the outcomes were discarded. Finally, the data were harmonized to ensure that the alleles of the exposure and outcome SNPs were aligned correctly. Incompatible alleles, such as A/C paired with A/G, or palindromic SNPs with medium frequency, were observed and removed.

Statistical analysis

We used Wald ratio‐based SNP‐specific estimations using inverse‐variance weighted (IVW) as the main analysis in univariable MR (UVMR) analysis, assuming no directional pleiotropic effect of each SNP. 27 Furthermore, we conducted multiple sensitivity analyses to evaluate the presence of pleiotropy and identify any probable genetic outliers. If greater than 50% of the IVs are valid, a weighted‐median estimator can yield a trustworthy estimate. 28 MR‐Egger regression's slope estimates an exposure's causal effect on an outcome if the instrument strength is independent of a direct effect assumption, and an intercept that deviates from zero shows pleiotropy. 29 MR‐Egger, while its greater confidence ranges resulting in slower statistically efficiency, employs regression slope to account for directional horizontal pleiotropy. Furthermore, the MR Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier (MR PRESSO) test was employed to detect and eliminate noteworthy outliers in order to account for horizontal pleiotropic effects. 30 , 31 The MR‐PRESSO outlier test computed a p‐value to represent the significance of pleiotropy for each SNP. 32 The SNPs were arranged in ascending order based on their MR‐PRESSO outlier test p‐values and subsequently eliminated individually. The MR‐PRESSO global test was conducted iteratively, with each iteration involving the removal of one SNP from the list. The process of recursion was iterated until the p‐value for the global test became statistically insignificant (p > 0.05). 33 , 34 We conducted a more detailed analysis of the diversity among all SNPs inside each database using Cochran's Q test statistic. Additionally, we created scatter plots that illustrate the connections between SNPs and exposure, as well as the associations between SNPs and outcomes, in order to depict the results of MR. A leave‐one‐out (LOO) analysis was conducted by systematically removing each SNP one at a time. The left‐over SNPs were then subjected to an IVW method to evaluate the possible impact of a specific variant on the estimations.

Furthermore, Multivariable MR (MVMR), 35 , 36 an expanded version of MR that utilizes variation in genes linked to several, potentially correlated exposures, has the capability to identify the causative impacts of numerous risk variables simultaneously. Due to their identification as established risk factors for skin malignancies, we adjusted sunburns, tanning easiness, and skin color in this study's MVMR analyses. The IVW procedure, akin to the UVMR, was utilized as the determining factor in conjunction with MR‐Egger and weighted median. The R (version 4.0.0), “TwoSampleMR” (version 0.5.4), and “Mendelian Randomization” (version 0.5.1) packages were utilized for all evaluations. 37

RESULTS

Characteristics of selected genetic variants

Overall, 11 cathepsin sites were selected for MR analysis. After thorough instrument identification steps, 5 to 20 SNPs were found to be associated with each cathepsin (Table S1). Besides, 8 and 90 SNPs were associated with MM and BCC (Table S2). All F‐statistics were > 10, indicating no weak IVs were employed. The IVW approach utilized a p‐value < 0.05 as the initial screening criterion to assess the potential causal association between exposure and outcome. Tables S3–S6 show the characteristics of SNPs used as genetic instruments for cathepsins and skin cancers in this bidirectional MR study.

analysis

In order to evaluate the impact of distinct cathepsins on the susceptibility to specific types of skin cancer, a Two‐Sample MR analysis was conducted. This analysis included 11 specific locations within the genome associated with 9 different cathepsins (cathepsin B, E, F, G, H, L2, O, S, and Z). Additionally, various histological subtypes of skin cancer (MM and BCC) were analyzed. Table 2 displays the forward association of distinct cathepsins with skin cancers and the Odd Ratio (OR) with a 95% confidence interval (CI), calculated from the methods described above. The results of the UVMR assessment (Figure 1 and Table 2) indicated a causal relationship between cathepsin H levels and the risk of BCC (IVW: p = 2.972 × 10−4, OR = 0.948, 95% CI = 0.921−0.976). Cathepsin S and BCC consistently exhibited the factual casual connection (IVW: p = 9.544 × 10−4, OR = 0.920, 95% CI = 0.876−0.967). The weighted median and MR‐Egger tools provided additional support for these consistently strong relationships, as seen in Table 2. Furthermore, the IVW technique showed a significant but less pronounced association between cathepsin H levels and the incidence of MM (p = 0.017, OR = 0.923, 95% CI = 0.864−0.986) (Figure 2). In addition, neither the MR‐Egger intercept nor the MR‐PRESSO global tests found any indication of directional pleiotropy in any of the above causal relationships. Scatter plots of three significant associations of cathepsins with BCC and MM are presented in Figure 3. Nevertheless, the IVW technique failed to uncover any causal links between the other varieties of cathepsins and MM, BCC (Table 2). analysis

Moreover, we conducted MVMR to correct for potential effects of skin cancer risk factors. After correcting for sunburns, ease of tanning, and skin color, the study found that elevated levels of cathepsin H (IVW: p < 0.001, OR = 0.96, 95% CI = 0.94−0.98) were consistently linked to a reduced incidence of BCC (Figure 4). Increased levels of cathepsin S (IVW: p > 0.001, OR = 1.06, 95% CI = 1.03−1.09) were observed to be causally associated with a higher risk of BCC (Figure 4). Nevertheless, after adjusting for these variables of risk factors, cathepsin H did not cause MM, just like other cathepsins (Figure 5).

DISCUSSION

This study is the earliest and most thorough analysis of the causal connection between cathepsins and skin malignancies, conducted in a comprehensive and systematic manner using bidirectional MR techniques. The results indicated that there is a protective effect of cathepsin H on MM, but the effect may be driven by the risk factors of MM such as sunburns, ease of skin tanning, and skin color. UVMR has verified the causal association between two cathepases, cathepsin H and S, respectively, and BCC. After using MVMR correction, it was observed that cathepsin H decreases the risk of BCC, whereas cathepsin S has the reverse effect. Furthermore, no reverse causality was found.

Numerous basic studies have been conducted on cathepsins and skin cancers, especially on MM. A variety of cathepsins are associated with melanoma metastasis and prognosis. 17 Cauthepsin B produces neutrophil extracellular traps that aid melanoma spread, according to Zhang et al. 38 Patients with metastatic melanoma who have elevated levels of cathepsin H and B experience a reduced overall survival rate. 39 Nevertheless, the current research investigating the function of it lacks complete agreement. Frohlich et al. discovered that in samples of superficial diffuse melanoma, there is a decrease in cathepsin H activity and mRNA, but the amount of cathepsin H immunoreactive cells increases. This suggests the cathepsin H may hinder the invasive capability of the melanoma. 40 According to the follow‐up study, only sporadic cathepsin H immunoreactive cells are found in melanoma samples, demonstrating that cathepsin H may not be associated with MM. 41 Our study confirmed the protective effect of cathepsin H on MM, albeit weakly, by the UVMR approach. After the risk factors of skin carcinoma were corrected, the statistical difference was no longer significant. Accordingly, it can be assumed that the effect is caused by risk factor driving rather than cathepsin H itself. Previous researches demonstrate that other types of cathepsins, such as cathepsin B, D, L, and K are also capable of promoting the occurrence, proliferation, invasion, and metastasis of MM. 10 , 36 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 MR did not find a correlation. There are relatively few previous studies on cathepsins and nonmelanoma skin carcinoma. As Frohlich found, in BCC, the occurrence of cathepsin B and L was linked to local invasion. It is noteworthy that the cathepsin H reaction byproduct was specifically discovered in the cells around the tumor nodes or in the spaces between them. The observed distribution suggests that cathepsin H is responsible for the degradation of the extracellular matrix, which plays a crucial role in tumor cell invasion. Additionally, it indicates that the production of cathepsin H is promoted via the activation of immune‐system cells such macrophages and lymphocytes. 44 There are currently no additional studies that elucidate the function of cathepsin H within the development or metastasis of BCC. In this MR, we used genetic instruments to demonstrate the protective effect of cathepsin H on BCC through UVMR and MVMR, while cathepsin S significantly increases the risk of BCC. In addition, it has been shown that in MM, dysregulation of the non‐receptor tyrosine kinases Abl and Arg (Abl/Arg) promotes cathepsin B and L through activation of Ets1, Sp1, and NF‐κB/p65 transcription factors. 45 The abnormal expression of STAT3 and STAT6 by tumor cells and tumor‐associated macrophages can stimulate the release of cathepsin B, C, S, and Z. 46 The acidic tumor microenvironment both enhances the development of procathepsins and stimulates their function. 47 However, this MR analysis does not suggest the reverse causality.

MR study found that cathepsin H exerts a protective effect on BCC, while cathepsin S increases the risk of BCC. Prior research has confirmed that cathepsin H is crucial for maintaining the integrity of the skin barrier. Additionally, mice lacking it are more prone to experiencing minor inflammatory responses on their skin. 48 In addition to the above functions, cathepsin H also plays a part in the peptide loading of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) II molecules, which is accountable for loading antigenic peptide fragments onto MHC II molecules, thereby promoting the activation of T‐cells upon correct recognition of antigens and then stimulating the specific immune system. 49 Nevertheless, cathepsin S may function as a risky factor in tumor tissues. Cathepsin S‐mediated autophagy drives the polarization of macrophages related to tumors into the M2 phenotype, ultimately promoting tumor growth. 50 Increased expression of cathepsin S negatively regulated the levels of anti‐angiogenic arresten and canstatin. These two peptides can inhibit endothelial cell proliferation and migration, induce to apoptosis, and suppress melanoma proliferation in mice. 51 Furthermore, Paraoan discovered that patients with highly metastatic uveal melanoma had higher levels of the collagenolytic and elastolytic cathepsin S in its active state. 52 Inflammation, immunity, and tumor pathophysiological processes involved in cathepsin H and S provide supporting evidence and direction to explore the intrinsic mechanism of their causal relationship with BCC, but further confirmation must be provided by observational studies or basic experiments.

As it stands, there are several obvious advantages to using this method. Primarily, it operates on the concept that alleles get assigned at random to gametes throughout the process of gamete production. 53 As a result, confounding factors and reverse causation can be eliminated from the design itself. Additionally, we also combine MVMR analysis 35 which gains the additional benefit of removing any potential risk factors from the analysis and ensures that the conclusions are robust and persuasive as well. Second, we chose different databases for cathepsins and skin cancers and the almost nonoverlapping databases further enlarged the sample size. Last, the samples in the databases were all European populations, which avoids confounding by ethnicity, resulting in generalizability and comprehensiveness of conclusions among European populations.

Inevitably, this study is subject to various limitations. First of all, the GWAS database samples were all taken from European populations who are light‐skinned Whites, and the incidence of skin cancers in Whites is much higher than in people of color. Therefore, there is still some uncertainty as to whether the conclusions outlined above can apply to other populations as well. Second, the GWAS statistics make use of the occurrence of the outcomes as an endpoint. A progressive disease, such as skin cancer, tends to exhibit some biological differences at different stages of the disease, yet the GWAS database does not allow for subgroup analyses to be conducted based on the staging of the tumor. Third, it is not guaranteed that the instruments used to measure cathepsins are the same and the batches of samples sent to the laboratory are the same, so the superposition of the two results in a large error in the results of cathepsin measurements. 54 To minimize batch effects, it is advisable to send samples for testing uniformly and to make sure that the same instrument is used for all the tests. Fourth, due to the limitations of the database, this research is not able to examine the relationship between SCC, other rarer forms of skin cancer, and cathepsins, so more basic studies or observational studies will be necessary in the future to examine whether there is a connection between these two.

In conclusion, elevated levels of cathepsin S heighten the susceptibility to BCC, whereas cathepsin H functions as a safeguarding element. This offers a novel viewpoint on the potential use of cathepsin S as an indicator in the efforts to prevent, screen, and diagnose skin cancer at an early stage. It also provides a comprehensive idea for future targeted intervention studies on specific cathepsin agonists or inhibitors, which may point out a potential path for the treatment of BCC.
