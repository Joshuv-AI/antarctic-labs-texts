HSF4 promotes renal cell carcinoma progression and is associated with PI3K/Akt pathway
Authors: Yue Li, Hao Xie, Changming Dong, Tianxi Yu, Shuang Wu, Xin Cui, Chongjun Xiang, Huiying Tao, Ning Liang, Jiahao Guo, Yufan Yang, Jinshan Yang, Xinxin Li, Jiazi Cha, Wenting Wang, Chunhua Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRenal cell carcinoma (RCC) is a common urological malignancy, accounting for approximately 2% of global cancer-related mortality. Heat shock factors (HSFs) are transcription factors that regulate cell stress reaction. However, the exact mode of action of HSF4 in cancer, particularly RCC, is still unknown.ObjectiveTo investigate the expression pattern of HSF4 in RCC and its potential molecular mechanism (especially the association with the PI3K/Akt pathway), so as to identify a new therapeutic target for RCC.MethodsBioinformatics analyses were performed to assess HSF4 expression and its prognosis relevance in RCC. The effects of HSF4 on RCC were studied with both in vitro and in vivo experiments. The transcriptome sequencing results were analyzed for both the gene knockdown and control RCC cells to predict relevant RCC signaling pathways.ResultsOur experimental results indicate that HSF4 knockdown can prevent the occurrence and spread of RCC in the body, as well as the growth and invasion of RCC cells. Mechanistically, HSF4 knockdown led to a significant decrease in the protein levels of PI3K, p-AKT, β-catenin, and c-Myc. These findings demonstrate that HSF4 promotes RCC progression by concurrently activating the PI3K/AKT and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways.ConclusionOur findings identify that HSF4 is highly expressed in RCC and can promote the occurrence and development of RCC. Therefore, HSF4 may be a potential therapeutic target for RCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12894-026-02196-0.

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is one of the most common malignant tumors of the genitourinary system with a high incidence rate and poor patient prognosis [1, 2]. Tubular epithelial cell derived RCC is the most common type of RCC, accounting for about 80% [3]. Approximately 2%–3% of all RCCs are hereditary and several autosomal dominant syndromes are described, each with a distinct genetic basis and phenotype, the most common one being von Hippel–Lindau (VHL) disease [4–6]. The early stages of RCC are often asymptomatic, and as the disease progresses, symptoms such as blood in urine, lower back pain, and abdominal mass may occur [7]. Identifying new therapeutic target holds significant benefits for effective RCC treatment.

Heat shock factors, HSFs, alone or in conjunction with the regulation of transcription programs, allow cells to adapt to protein-toxic stress conditions and physiological changes [8, 9]. HSF4 is a member of this HSFs family, HSF4 has been shown to play a role in multiple physiological processes and is not limited to regulating classical heat shock or stress response transcriptional programs [9, 10]. In fact, HSF4 has been shown to be involved in several physiological processes, including regulating cell differentiation and proliferation during development, and regulating DNA damage repair [11, 12]. Nevertheless, the mode of HSF4 activation and its function in controlling transcription during carcinogenesis remain remains poorly understood.

In the present study, we first identified high HSF4 expression in RCC tissue samples through online database analysis and clinical validation, which correlated with poor overall survival. Based on these findings, we further examined the specific role of HSF4 in RCC development and predicted the potential signaling pathways involved.

Methods

Data collection

This study was conducted between January 2022 and December 2023. UALCAN platform (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/index.html) was used to investigate the HSF4 mRNA levels in RCC vs. normal tissue samples [13]. GEPIA website (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/) was used for the survival analysis of HSF4 (with high and low expression levels) [14].

Cell culture and transfection

The RCC cell lines (786-O, 769-P, ACHN) and the human renal tubular epithelial cell line (HK-2) were acquired from Stem Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Four kidney cell lines were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute-1640 medium with 10% FBS, and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. The cells were all maintained at 37 °C, 5% CO2 in the cell culture incubator and the culture media were replaced every 2–3 days.

The HSF4-targeting shRNA (Sh-HSF4, target sequence 5’-GGCAGCAGAACGAGATCTT-3’), control shRNA (shCtrl), and the HSF4 overexpression (OE-HSF4) lentiviral particles were purchased from a commercial company (KeyGEN BioTECH, China). The OE-HSF4 construct was designed to carry synonymous mutations within the shRNA target site to ensure the overexpressed protein would escape silencing by Sh-HSF4.

For lentiviral infection, ACHN and 786-O cells were serum-starved for 24 h. Subsequently, cells were separately infected with lentiviruses carrying Sh-HSF4, ShCtrl, or OE-HSF4, with an empty vector virus serving as an additional control for overexpression experiments. 24 h after infection, the medium was replaced with complete culture medium containing puromycin for selection. The medium was changed every 2–3 days thereafter to establish stable cell lines. The successful knockdown or overexpression of HSF4 was confirmed at both the mRNA level by qPCR and the protein level by Western blotting.

Western blot

The cells were washed with PBS and then lysed with radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (NCM, China). Protein concentration was measured using the BCA Protein Assay kit (Sparkjade, China). The protein samples were separated using 8% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide (SDS-PAGE) gel and then transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, MA, USA). The membrane was blocked for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated with the primary antibody for 24 h at 4 °C. The blocking solution and antibody diluent were both Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20 (Malinckrodt Baker, NJ, USA) (TBST) containing 5% BSA (Solarbio, China). The following antibodies were used: HSF4 Mouse monoclonal antibody (ab128146, abcam); GAPDH antibody (MA9166, Abmart); AKT Monoclonal antibody (60,203–2-Ig, proteintech); Phospho-AKT Monoclonal antibody (66,444–1-Ig, proteintech); PI3K (67,071–1-Ig, proteintech); N-cadherin Monoclonal antibody (66,219–1-Ig, proteintech); Vimentin Monoclonal antibody (60,330–1-Ig, proteintech); Rabbit Anti-cyclinD1 (proteintech 26,939–1-AP,proteintech); Rabbit Anti-c-Myc (AF6054, Affinity); Rabbit Anti-β-catenin (AF6266, Affinity); Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) HRP (S0001, USA); Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) HRP (S0002, USA). After primary antibody incubation, the membrane was washed four times with TBST (8 min per wash) and then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000) for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, the membrane was treated with ECL kit(Affinity, USA) and a chemiluminescence detection system (ChemiScope 6200 Touch, CLINX, China) was used to visualize the protein bands.

CCK-8 assay

The stable transfected cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 103 cells per well with 200 μl culture medium, and incubated overnight. At 24, 48, 72, and 96 h, the cell culture plates were taken out from the incubator. To each well, a mixture solution of 10 μl of CCK-8 solution (KeyGEN BioTECH, China) and 90 μl of culture medium was then added, and incubated for an additional 2 h. The absorbance values at 450 nm of each well were measured, and the growth curves of the different groups were plotted.

Cell invasion assay

60 μl mixed solution (1:8 Matrigel™ matrix: serum-free culture medium) was added to each Transwell chamber placed on a 24-well plate. The cells were serum starved for 24 h. Transwell chambers were incubated at 37℃, 5% CO2 for 3 h and 0.5 ml cell suspension (8 × 104 cells/ml) was added to each Transwell chamber. 0.75 ml culture medium containing 10% FBS was pipetted into the lower part of the 24-well plate and the entire plate was then incubated for 24 h. After the culture period was completed, the culture medium was washed off and 1 ml 4% formaldehyde was used to fix the cells for 10 min. Then, 1 ml 0.1% crystal violet solution was added to stain the cells for 10 min at room temperature and the samples were observed using a microscope. At least three representative fields of view were chosen for each condition, in order to count the numbers of invading cells.

Cell scratch wound assay

The transfected cells were seeded onto a 6-well plate (approximately 5 × 105 cells per well) and cultured for overnight. A scratch wound was created using a pipette tip and then the cells were maintained in serum-free medium. Pictures were taken at 0, 12, and 24 h. The wound area was measured using ImageJ software, and the cell migration rate was calculated as: (Initial area—Final area)/Initial area × 100%.

Colony formation assay

ACHN and 786-O cells were seeded onto 6-well plates with 2,000 cells per well and cultured for 12 to 14 days. At the end of the culture period, the cells were fixed with 10% neutrally buffered formalin and then stained with 0.1% crystal violet to count the colony numbers in each condition.

Subcutaneous xenograft

Male nude mice (4–6 weeks old) were purchased from Yantai Keyou Experimental Animal Breeding Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China). Mice were randomly assigned to two groups: NC group and a knockdown (Sh-HSF4) group, with 9 mice in each group. Mice were housed in a temperature-controlled environment with a 12-h light/dark cycle and provided with ad libitum access to food and water. Lentivirus-infected ACHN cells carrying either shCtrl (NC group) or sh-HSF4 (KD group) were subcutaneously injected into the right arm pit of each mouse for a duration of 7 days. The tumors’ lengths (L) and widths (W) were measured by an investigator blinded to the group allocation starting from day 3 post-injection, and the measurements were taken every 5 days until day 67. mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation—a method performed by trained personnel: the mouse was gently restrained, and the cervical spine was quickly and firmly dislocated by applying slight traction to the tail while stabilizing the head, ensuring immediate loss of consciousness and minimal distress. Death was confirmed by the absence of respiratory movement, corneal reflex, and pedal reflex for 2 min. Tumor volumes were calculated using the formula: tumor volume = 0.5 × L × W × W. The mice were sacrificed at the end of day 67 and the tumors were excised to measure their volumes and weights. H&E staining was performed for the excised tumor tissues.

Immunohistochemistry

Sections (4-µm-thick) were cut from paraffin-embedded blocks of tumor tissue. Immunohistochemistry staining was performed as previously reported [15]. Repaired tissue sections were incubated with the Ki67 monoclonal antibody (1: 200, Beyotime, AF1738). Ki67 expression was defined as follows: +, > 0 and ≤ 25%; + +, > 25 and ≤ 50%; + + +, > 50 and ≤ 75%; and + + + +, > 75%; among which, + and + + were defined as low expression, while + + + and + + + + were defined as high expression.

Transcriptome sequencing analysis

TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA) was used for RNA extraction following the manufacturer's recommended protocol. RNA purity and quantity was evaluated using the NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA), while the RNA integrity was assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The construction of libraries was carried out using the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit, following the manufacturer's instructions. OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) conducted the transcriptome sequencing and analysis. The libraries were sequenced using an Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted using R (version 4.3.1), to assess the biological duplications of the samples. To identify the differentially expressed genes, transcriptome data was analyzed using DESeq2 with the screening criteria of |logFC|> 1 and p Value < 0.05.

Statistical analysis

ImageJ was used to analyze the data collected from the scratch wound assay, the cell invasion assay and the plate cloning experiment. R (version 4.3.1) was employed to analyze the RNA-seq data. Each experiment was repeated for at least three times. GraphPad Prism 8.0 (version 8, California, United States) was utilized for statistical analysis. Statistical significance was determined using unpaired Student’s t-test for cell experiments and paired t-test for clinical tissue samples, with p < 0.05 indicating significance. The significance levels were denoted as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

It was found that HSF4 mRNA level was significantly higher in RCC samples vs. normal kidney samples (p < 0.05), according to analysis using the UALCAN database (Fig. 1A). By analyzing the relationship between HSF4 expression and clinical prognosis via the GEPIA database, it was found that HSF4 was not correlated with DFS (Disease-Free Survival), but had a correlation with OS (Overall Survival) (Fig. 1B). Patients with high HSF4 expression showed significantly lower OS (p < 0.05). To validate this at the protein level, we collected paired tumor and adjacent normal tissues from 10 RCC patients who underwent radical nephrectomy. HSF4 expression affected the growth and survival of RCC Cells

We then attempted to construct HSF4-overexpressing cells. However, qPCR analysis showed that while lentiviral-mediated shRNA effectively knocked down HSF4 mRNA, the overexpression (OE) construct led to a slight but statistically significant increase in HSF4 mRNA levels in both ACHN and 786-O cells (Fig. 2B). This modest increase is likely attributed to the high endogenous HSF4 levels already present in these aggressive RCC cell lines. Therefore, the subsequent functional experiments focused on the loss-of-function approach using HSF4 knockdown. HSF4 knockdown was achieved using HSF4-targeting shRNA (Sh-HSF4) lentivirus and the cells transfected with control virus (NC) served as the control group. As can be seen in Fig. 2C, Western blots confirmed the successful HSF4 knockdown in RCC cell lines ACHN and 786-O) at the protein level. We first evaluated the effect of HSF4 on RCC cell proliferation. Results from the CCK-8 assay demonstrated a significant reduction in cell viability in HSF4-knockdown ACHN and 786-O cells (Sh-HSF4 group) compared to the control cells (NC group) over a 96-h period (Fig. 3A). Consistent with this, the colony formation assay revealed that HSF4 knockdown severely impaired the long-term clonogenic survival of both RCC cell lines (Fig. 3B). Mice xenograft models were established to investigate the kidney tumorigenic effects of HSF4 in vivo. Principal component analysis confirmed clear separation between groups (data not shown). We identified 657 significantly downregulated and 131 upregulated genes upon HSF4 knockdown (Fig. 6A, B). HSF4 activates both the PI3K/AKT and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways

Guided by the transcriptomic findings and considering the established pivotal role of the PI3K/AKT pathway in RCC [16], we first validated its status. Western blot analysis showed that HSF4 knockdown markedly reduced the levels of phosphorylated AKT (p-AKT, active form) and total PI3K, while total AKT protein remained unchanged (Fig. 7A). Furthermore, to explore the functional relevance of another top-hit pathway from the sequencing data, we assessed the activity of the Wnt/β-catenin cascade. The results demonstrated that HSF4 knockdown effectively reduced the protein levels of active β-catenin (non-phosphorylated) and its downstream oncogenic targets, including c-Myc and Cyclin D1 (Fig. 7B). Collectively, these data indicate that HSF4 concurrently activates both the PI3K/AKT and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways, which may cooperatively drive the aggressive phenotypes of RCC cells.

Discussion

In this study, we identify HSF4 as a novel oncogenic driver in RCC. We demonstrate that HSF4 is significantly upregulated in RCC tissues and correlates with poor patient prognosis. Functional assays consistently show that HSF4 is essential for the proliferation, migration, and invasion of RCC cells in vitro and for tumor growth in vivo. More importantly, moving beyond a single-pathway model, our integrative analysis combining transcriptomics and molecular validation reveals that HSF4 exerts its oncogenic effects by concurrently activating two pivotal signaling axes: the PI3K/AKT pathway and the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. This coordinated activation provides a mechanistic framework for the aggressive phenotypes induced by HSF4. Heat shock factor 4, which contains conserved DNA binding and trimerization domains, is a vital member of HSFs. Recent studies revealed that dysregulation of HSF4 expression could result in carcinogenesis. Notably, Saito et al. recently reported that targeting HSF4 could enhance the efficacy of cabozantinib and immune checkpoint inhibitors in RCC models [17]. Their findings in A498 and Caki2 cell lines are consistent with our observations in ACHN and 786-O cells, further reinforcing the pivotal role of HSF4 as a therapeutic target in RCC. HSF4 may promote aggressive tumor behaviour by enhancing EMT through activating AKT pathway in a HIF1α-dependent manner [18]. Moreover, HSF4 was also reported to regulate several important biological function of cancer cells. For example downregulation of HSF4 was negatively correlated with the expression of p21 and p27, which could directly induce cell cycle arrest and senescence and inhibit tumor cell proliferation [19, 20]. Furthermore, we showed that HSF4 expression levels were essential for the growth, migration, and invasion of RCC cells and that HSF4 may also have an impact on PI3K and AKT expression.

The activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway is a well-established hallmark in RCC progression. Our findings that HSF4 knockdown reduces the levels of both p-AKT and total PI3K suggest that HSF4 may regulate this pathway at a relatively upstream level. The concomitant downregulation of the EMT markers N-cadherin and Vimentin directly links HSF4-PI3K/AKT signaling to enhanced cell motility and invasion, a critical step in metastasis. This aligns with a previous report implicating HSF4 in EMT regulation in other contexts, yet positions it within the specific signaling landscape of RCC.

Equally significant is our novel discovery that HSF4 activates the Wnt/β-catenin cascade in RCC. The reduction of active β-catenin and its transcriptional target c-Myc upon HSF4 knockdown provides direct experimental evidence for this regulation. The Wnt/β-catenin pathway is a master regulator of cell proliferation and stemness. Its co-activation by HSF4 alongside PI3K/AKT suggests a synergistic mechanism: PI3K/AKT may primarily fuel survival and invasive capabilities, while Wnt/β-catenin drives proliferative and stem-like properties. This dual-targeting strategy could allow HSF4 to comprehensively orchestrate multiple oncogenic programs, offering a plausible explanation for its potent tumor-promoting effect.

Our study expands the functional repertoire of HSF4 beyond heat shock response and development. While HSF4 has been linked to carcinogenesis in other malignancies, its role in RCC and its association with the Wnt pathway are previously unrecognized. The finding that HSF4 regulates both PI3K/AKT and Wnt/β-catenin pathways places it at a critical signaling nexus. This is distinct from some known oncogenes that often dominate a single pathway, suggesting that targeting HSF4 could disrupt a broader oncogenic network. The significant enrichment of the cAMP pathway in our sequencing data further hints at a potential layer of complex regulation or crosstalk, warranting future investigation.

The transcriptomic data served as an unbiased starting point, correctly guiding us to the Wnt pathway validation. The prominent enrichment of the cAMP signaling pathway among the top hits presents an intriguing future direction. Although not functionally validated here, cAMP signaling is known to engage in complex crosstalk with both PI3K/AKT and Wnt pathways in a context-dependent manner [21]. Its presence in the analysis suggests that the HSF4-regulated network may be even more extensive, potentially involving metabolic or microenvironmental feedback loops that could modulate the primary oncogenic signals we identified. While this study provides compelling evidence for the oncogenic role of HSF4 in RCC, several limitations must be addressed to ensure a balanced interpretation of our findings. Most notably, although our data strongly suggest that HSF4 promotes RCC progression through the PI3K/AKT and Wnt/β-catenin pathways, the current evidence is predominantly correlative. Future rescue experiments, such as re-expressing HSF4 in knockdown cells to restore these signaling activities, are necessary to definitively establish a direct causal link between HSF4 and the activation of these specific pathways.First, the precise molecular mechanisms by which HSF4 transcriptionally regulates key components of the PI3K/AKT and Wnt/β-catenin signaling axes remain to be fully elucidated. As a transcription factor, HSF4 likely binds to specific promoter regions of genes within these pathways, but identifying these direct transcriptional targets through methods such as Chromatin Immunoprecipitation Sequencing (ChIP-seq) is a crucial next step that was not covered in the present study. Identifying the direct transcriptional targets of HSF4 in RCC is a crucial next step. Second, although we demonstrated the concurrent activation of the PI3K/AKT and Wnt/β-catenin pathways, the exact nature and hierarchy of the crosstalk between these two signaling cascades downstream of HSF4 require further biochemical dissection. Third, our transcriptomic analysis prominently highlighted the enrichment of the cAMP signaling pathway, yet its specific functional contribution to HSF4-mediated RCC progression remains a prediction that awaits rigorous experimental validation. Finally, while our subcutaneous xenograft model provided initial evidence of tumor suppression upon HSF4 knockdown, this model does not fully replicate the complex human tumor microenvironment. The therapeutic potential and safety of targeting HSF4 must be further evaluated in more advanced preclinical models, such as orthotopic xenografts or patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, before clinical translation can be considered.

Conclusion

The expression of HSF4 was markedly increased in RCC. Both in vivo and in vitro experiments demonstrated that HSF4 exerted its functions in RCC by enhancing the proliferation, migration, and invasion of RCC cells. Additionally, potential downstream pathways were identified. These findings collectively highlight the promise of HSF4 as a potential therapeutic target for RCC treatment.
