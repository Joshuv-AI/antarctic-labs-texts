Investigation of adherence to immunosuppressive therapy and perceived social support in organ recipients
Authors: Satı Doğan, Özkan Uğuz, Müjgan Solak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdherence to immunosuppressive therapy in organ transplant patients is an important factor for the patient’s health and the success of the transplant. In this study, we investigated the relationship between the perceived level of social support and adherence to immunosuppressive therapy in post-transplant patients.MethodsThis descriptive and cross-sectional study was conducted with 116 post-transplant liver and kidney transplant recipients. Data were collected using the Personal Information Form, the Multidimensional Perceived Social Support Scale, and the İmmunosuppressive Therapy Adherence Scale (ITAS). Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 30.0, and the significance level was set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe mean age of the participants was 41.52 ± 12.23 years. Of the patients, 62.7% had undergone kidney transplantation and 37.3% had undergone liver transplantation, with the majority (83.9%) receiving organs from living donors. The overall adherence to immunosuppressive therapy was relatively high, with 51.7% of the patients achieving full adherence (ITAS = 12), with a mean ITAS score of 10.22 ± 1.30 (out of 12). A positive and significant relationship was found between adherence to immunosuppressive therapy and perceived level of social support (B = 0.052, p < 0.001).ConclusionsThe importance of social support in the post-transplant period should not be overlooked; in particular, the involvement of family, friends, and other significant individuals should be systematically integrated into patient care. In this context, perceived social support is a practical and feasible strategy for improving adherence to immunosuppressive therapy. Healthcare professionals should routinely assess patients’ levels of social support and implement targeted psychosocial interventions for individuals with inadequate support.Keywords: Immunosuppressive therapy, Transplantation, Recipients, Social support

Background

For individuals with irreversible organ failure, transplantation is the most effective and cost-efficient treatment option, significantly improving survival and quality of life [1]. In both Turkey and worldwide, the number of kidney and liver transplantations has steadily increased, accompanied by improved life expectancy [2, 3]. According to recent data, 5265 organ transplantations were performed in Turkey in 2023, with kidney and liver procedures accounting for the largest proportion [3].

The long-term success of transplantation depends largely on adherence to immunosuppressive therapy. Despite its crucial role in preventing rejection and ensuring graft survival [4–7], non-adherence remains a frequent problem, with rates ranging from 20 to 50% [8–10]. Factors such as complex medication regimens, side effects, lifestyle changes, and psychosocial challenges often hinder adherence [11]. Previous studies report that forgetfulness, interference with daily routines, and medication-related side effects are among the common reasons for non-adherence [12–14].

Among psychosocial factors, social support has been shown to play a central role. Adequate support from family, friends, and significant others helps patients manage treatment regimens and cope with stress, while insufficient support is linked to poorer adherence [15, 16]. Literature suggests that social support strongly influences transplantation outcomes, including medication adherence [17–19]. In Turkey, where family ties and social solidarity are traditionally strong, perceived social support may play a distinctive role in shaping health behaviors.

Following hospital discharge, transplant recipients are expected to independently manage complex medication schedules, making social support particularly important during this period. Supportive networks may not only enhance adherence but also improve recovery, quality of life, and long-term outcomes [19, 20]. However, there is still limited evidence on this relationship in the Turkish context. Therefore, the present study aimed to examine the association between perceived social support and adherence to immunosuppressive therapy among kidney and liver transplant recipients. By clarifying this relationship, the study seeks to provide evidence for targeted interventions that can improve adherence and patient outcomes.

Methods

This descriptive and cross-sectional study was carried out to investigate the relationship between the perceived level of social support and adherence to immunosuppressive therapy after organ transplantation.

Design and participants

Following ethical approval, this study was conducted with transplant recipients at the organ transplant center of a training and research hospital in western Turkey. The study population consisted of individuals who had undergone transplantation and were being followed up at this center. To determine the appropriate number of participants, the sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.2 software, based on a theoretical power of 0.80, resulting in a total of 116 transplant recipients. In this center, the follow-up of patients after transplantation is maintained through organ transplant outpatient clinics. Accordingly, patients who visited the outpatient clinic for routine control appointments were approached. Among these individuals, those who agreed to participate voluntarily were included in the study. To ensure accurate and consistent data collection, the researchers conducted face-to-face interviews with the participants. During the interviews, the items and questions on the data collection form were presented to each patient individually, and their responses were recorded directly by the researcher, who marked the corresponding options on the form. Data collection instruments

Data were collected using the Personal Information Form, İmmunosuppressive Therapy Adherence Scale and Multidimensional Perceived Social Support Scale. Information about the data collection tools is given below.

Personal information form

The form was developed by the researchers based on expert opinions. It included questions designed to collect information about patients’ sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., age, gender, marital status, and education level) and transplant characteristics (e.g., type of transplant, donor type, and number of drugs used).

Immunosuppressive therapy adherence scale (ITAS)

The ITAS of Chisholm et al. [21] was used to assess the adherence of organ transplant recipients to immunosuppressive therapy. The scale was adapted for use in Turkish culture by Madran et al. [22]. The scale comprises four items assessing adherence over the preceding 3 months. Sample items include: “How many times did you miss taking your medications for the prevention of organ rejection in the last 3 months due to forgetting?” and “How many times did you not take your medications for organ rejection prevention due to reasons beyond your control (e.g., medical reports and pharmacy issues)?” Responses were categorized as 0%, 1–20%, 21–50%, and over 50%, and scored on a four-point Likert scale. The total score ranges from 0 (indicating poor adherence) to 12 (indicating perfect adherence). This scale has been frequently employed in studies conducted in the Turkish context. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the Turkish version was previously reported to be 0.65 [22]. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.71, suggesting that the scale exhibits acceptable internal consistency and is both valid and reliable for this sample.

Multidimensional perceived social support scale (MPSSS)

The MPSSS, originally developed by Zimet et al. [23], was culturally adapted for the Turkish population by Eker et al. [24]. This scale was used to evaluate perceived social support. It comprises 12 Likert-type items divided into three subscales corresponding to sources of support: family, friends, and significant others, with each subscale containing four items. Sample items include: “There is a significant other who is there for me when I need them,” “My family really tries to help me,” and “I can rely on my friends when things go wrong.” Each item was rated on a seven-point scale ranging from 1 to 7. Subscale scores were obtained by summing the responses to the four items within each sub-dimension, and the total score was calculated by summing all subscale scores. The scale ranges from 12 to 84, with higher scores indicating greater perceived social support. In the Turkish adaptation, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was reported to be 0.85 [24], whereas in the present study, it was 0.92, indicating excellent internal consistency.

Statistical analyses

After the data were coded by the researchers, IBM SPSS Statistics version 30.0 was used for statistical analysis. Initially, the normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. To evaluate internal consistency, the reliability of the ITAS and MPSSS scales was calculated using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient. In the descriptive phase of the analysis, continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation and examined using correlation analyses, whereas categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. To investigate the associations between patients’ scale scores and their descriptive characteristics, inferential statistical tests, including independent t tests and the Kruskal–Wallis test, were applied. All analyses were conducted at a 95% confidence interval, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05.

Ethical aspect of the study

Ethics approval Ethical approval was obtained from the Ege University Faculty of Medical Research Ethics Committee of a university hospital (Date: 03.11.2022, Number: 22-11 T/30). In accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, patients were informed about the purpose and procedures of the study, and the Informed Consent Form was presented to them. Only after obtaining written informed consent, data collection tools were administered and completed by the participants.

As shown in Table 1, the mean age of the patients was 41.52 ± 12.23 years. The majority were male (60.3%) and married (62.6%). Regarding educational status, 51.7% were high school graduates, and 83.6% of the patients lived in nuclear family settings. In terms of transplantation characteristics, 62.7% were kidney transplant recipients, and 83.9% had received organs from living donors. The mean time since transplantation was 21.19 ± 11.00 months, and patients were using an average of 6.54 ± 6.00 drugs.

ITAS and MPSSS scores did not significantly differ across the patients’ descriptive characteristics (Table 1).

As presented in Table 2, the patients’ mean total scores were 9.94 ± 0.77 (min: 8, max: 12) for the ITAS and 46.50 ± 13.60 for the MPSSS. Regarding the subdimensions of the MPSSS, the mean score for the family support subscale was 15.83 ± 4.56, for the friend’s subscale 14.71 ± 5.36, and for the significant other subscale 15.95 ± 4.38.

In summary, perceived social support emerged as the strongest predictor of adherence, explaining a significant proportion of the variance in ITAS scores. Patients with higher MPSSS scores consistently achieved better adherence, whereas neither polypharmacy nor the duration, since transplantation influenced adherence levels. This finding underscores the pivotal role of psychosocial factors—particularly perceived social support—over clinical characteristics in shaping patients’ adherence behaviors.

Discussion

This study examined the relationship between social support and adherence to immunosuppressive therapy in transplant patients and confirmed that social support plays a significant role in adherence.

In this study, the mean ITAS score was 10.22 ± 1.30 out of a possible 12, indicating a relatively high level of adherence among participants. This finding aligns with previous research, which has demonstrated strong adherence rates among liver transplant recipients. For instance, Karayurt et al. reported an ITAS score of 11.18 among Turkish liver transplant patients, suggesting a similarly high adherence level [25]. In addition, Promraj et al. found that 82.6% of liver transplant recipients achieved a perfect ITAS score of 12, with 16.3% scoring between 10 and 11, and only 1.2% scoring between 0 and 9 [26]. In this study, 51.7% (n = 60) of the patients showed full adherence with immunosuppressive treatment with a full score. In contrast, 48.3% (n = 56) scored below 12 points and were considered non-adherent. Russell et al. determined in their 2010 study that medication non-adherence was 86%. Medication non-adherence is estimated to increase treatment costs from $100 billion to $300 billion in patients, while in organ transplants, it is estimated to increase annual treatment costs from $15 million to $100 million [27].

In our current study, it has been determined that perceived social support positively affects adherence with immunosuppressive treatment. Although confirmatory findings, particularly in the systematic review conducted by Ladin and colleagues, do not show an objective and consistent relationship between social support and adherence [17], the presence of perceived social support directly contributes to medication use. Given Turkey’s cultural structure, the strengthened sense of family solidarity and responsibility stands out as an important support factor in helping patients take their medications on time and regularly. Indeed, a study conducted with data analysis from April to June 2021 found that perceived social support in general, family, and friend dimensions had a weak but positive effect on immunosuppressive medication adherence (r = 0.209–0.248; p < 0.01) [28]. However, some studies, particularly those based on high-quality research, indicate that this relationship is weak or statistically insignificant [29, 30]. For example, a study conducted among chronic kidney transplant recipients, which stated that social support does not predict medication adherence, highlighted the concept of the “adherence landscape,” emphasizing that treatment management is shaped by the interaction of multiple psychosocial and systemic factors [31].

Within this framework, the effect of social support on adherence is acceptable to a certain extent; however, the strength and consistency of this effect vary across different study designs and patient groups. Therefore, in future research, it should be clarified with objective criteria which components of social support (e.g., family support, peer sharing, and practical/emotional help) most affect adherence, as well as how this interaction changes with demographic and clinical variables, such as age, gender, and socioeconomic status. In addition, the question of whether insufficient social support constitutes a contraindication for pre- or post-transplant procedures can be discussed based on robust studies.

Strengths and limitations

This study had several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design prevents causal inferences between perceived social support and adherence to medications. Second, as the study was conducted at a single transplant center, the generalizability of the findings to other settings may be limited. Third, the interview-based data collection method, while allowing for clarification of misunderstandings, may have created a social desirability bias in the responses. Furthermore, the exclusion of individuals with language or communication barriers may have led to the under-representation of certain patient groups. Finally, the ITAS used in this study relied on patient recall over a 3-month period, which may have introduced recall bias and led to either overestimation or underestimation of adherence. To address this limitation, future studies in Turkey are encouraged to use instruments with shorter recall periods, such as the Brief Antiretroviral Adherence Index and Medication Adherence Self-Report Inventory, as recommended in the systematic review by Dobbels et al. [32], to minimize recall bias and enhance methodological rigor.

Despite these limitations, studying also has important strengths. Validated and reliable instruments (ITAS and MPSSS) were employed to measure adherence and social support. Data collection was carried out face-to-face by trained researchers, which enhanced the completeness and accuracy of responses. Furthermore, the sample size was relatively large compared to previous studies in Turkey, and the study contributes to the limited body of national data on psychosocial factors influencing adherence among transplant recipients. These strengths increase the value of the study and provide a strong basis for future multicenter and longitudinal research.

Conclusion

Adherence to immunosuppressive therapy is critical for graft survival and overall patient prognosis following organ transplantation. To enhance long-term treatment success, it is essential to support patients in coping with the challenges associated with the post-transplantation period. In this regard, strengthening patients’ perceived social support is a feasible and impactful strategy. In the present study, a positive and statistically significant relationship was identified between perceived social support and adherence to immunosuppressive therapy. Based on this finding, it is crucial that physicians and nurses actively assess and reinforce patients’ social support networks. Support from family members, friends, and significant others not only facilitates medication adherence but also contributes positively to patients’ psychosocial well-being. Looking ahead, future research should explore in greater depth the associations between the type, quality, and continuity of social support systems and their effects on treatment adherence and long-term survival. In addition, institutional support from hospital administrators for initiatives aimed at fostering social support mechanisms may further enhance the quality of care delivered to transplant recipients.
