City boundaries for global urban water scarcity assessment
Authors: Kiyoharu Kajiyama, Naota Hanasaki, Shinjiro Kanae
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRapid and continuous urbanization has increased water demand in cities worldwide. Global assessments of urban water scarcity should be conducted using gridded hydrological information, but are hampered by the lack of water-resource-based city boundary information. This study introduces HydroUrbanMap (HUM), a global gridded dataset of city boundaries for 1,604 cities at 5 arcmin resolution, incorporating hydrological attributes. HUM consists of two key components: delineation of city boundaries that include the population served by urban water services (supply and drainage), and estimation of accessible surface water sources within and outside these boundaries. The estimated city populations closely match census-based populations, with a correlation coefficient of 0.997. HUM incorporates hydrological inlets and outlets of main rivers in each city by overlaying the city boundaries with the river network. Combining our HUM dataset with outputs from global hydrological models supports city-specific water resource assessments worldwide, filling gaps in cities where data on urban water services are unavailable or incomplete.Subject terms: Hydrology, Sustainability

Background & Summary

The global urban population is projected to grow substantially. In 2018, 4.2 billion people (55% of the global population) lived in urban areas; this is projected to increase to 6.7 billion (69% of the world population) by 20501. As the urban population increases, urban water consumption is expected to rise, concentrating global water demand in cities2. Threats of Day Zero, when cities will exhaust the accessible freshwater supplies feeding their taps, have already been reported in locations such as Cape Town3, Chennai4, Harare4, São Paulo4, and Montevideo5. Consequently, the assessment of water resources at the urban scale has emerged as a critical new challenge for global water resource risk assessments6,7.

Reflecting the impact of urbanization on water resources, researchers have increasingly focused on water security at the city scale8–10. Previous global assessments of urban water scarcity can be broadly categorized into three approaches: (i) grid-based, (ii) basin-based, and (iii) documented-facility-based. In the first category, grid-based hydrological models compute water availability and demand independently for each grid cell11–14. While this approach resolves cities as grid cells with a large water demand, it tends to overestimate urban water scarcity because it does not consider urban water availability or urban water supply connectivity15,16. The second category, basin-based assessment, sought to mitigate this problem by aggregating grid cells, including cities, into basin-scale water scarcity maps17–19. These studies reduced the overestimation bias of the first approach by aggregating water availability and demand at the basin level. However, the estimated water scarcity depends strongly on the choice of the sub-basin20. Moreover, this approach cannot capture the reality that many cities withdraw water from multiple basins21,22. The third category, documented-facility-based approach, offers the most realistic depiction of urban water sources and transfers4,23–26. It identifies the catchment area of existing urban water facilities, thereby overcoming the sub-basin selection uncertainty inherent in the second approach. Yet, coverage is fundamentally limited to cities with available documentation, leaving the vast majority of cities without documentation unassessed.

To bridge these gaps, a new dataset is needed that provides gridded city boundaries suitable for coupling with hydrological models and identifies city-specific river access points and water-served areas, including for cities without documented infrastructure. Such a dataset would complement the three existing assessment approaches described above. Establishing appropriate urban boundaries for water-served populations is particularly critical because the spatial extent of these boundaries determines which water sources (i.e., river tributaries) are geographically accessible to cities. However, existing global urban delineation methods are not designed for this purpose. Methods such as the Degree of Urbanization (DoU)27, built-up density approaches28,29, and night-time light-based classification30,31 use fixed population or density thresholds to identify urban cores. For example, DoU defines urban areas as contiguous grid cells with at least 1,500 inhabitants per km2 and a total population of 50,000. While these thresholds enable global spatial and temporal comparability, they systematically exclude suburban and peri-urban zones where water utilities commonly extend service coverage32. Importantly, none of the previous approaches ensured a water-served population, as they focused on high-density urban areas or urban cores. For water resource assessment, city boundaries must represent the entire population served by urban water services (i.e., urban water supply and drainage). We presume that the population reported in the World Urbanization Prospects (WUP)1 represents the most complete proxy for water-served populations. WUP adopts each country’s national definition of urban population, designed for administrative purposes, such as urban planning and resource allocation33. To the best of our knowledge, no existing gridded dataset translates WUP populations onto spatially explicit grid cells. The absence of appropriate city boundaries and associated urban water source information has constrained earlier global water resources assessments to basin-wise analyses or to compilations of case studies of a limited number of cities.

We develop HydroUrbanMap (HUM), a global dataset of city boundaries for 1,604 cities at 5 arcmin resolution that meets the requirements discussed above. HUM delineates city boundaries and incorporates information relevant to urban water supply and drainage to support global water resource assessment. In this study, we refer to city boundaries for water resource studies as city masks, defined as contiguous grid cells with total population and boundary information. The total population of a city is calculated by summing the population of all grid cells within the city mask. At the same time, the boundary is defined by the outer lines of the contiguous grid cells. Each city mask is constructed to contain the full population reported in WUP. HUM integrates geographical data so that the uppermost and lowermost grid cells along each city’s main river can be identified; these grid cells are designated as the city water inlet (i.e., the primary point where river water enters the city) and the city water outlet (i.e., the primary point where river water exits the city), respectively. Additionally, urban water may be transported over long distances from outside city masks via aqueducts. HUM therefore identifies aqueduct origins based on topographic feasibility. Aqueduct origins represent geographically accessible external tributaries near city boundaries, identified through elevation gradients and proximity rather than documented infrastructure.

Methods

The methodology of this study consisted of three parts: (i) delineation of city masks, (ii) identification of city water inlets, city water outlets, and (iii) estimation of aqueduct origins for each city mask. Figure 1 illustrates examples of city masks, shown as solid black lines around various regions, and Fig. 2 provides a conceptual overview of the methodology. The city masks were generated using data from the Gridded Population of the World (GPW) dataset34 and WUP. Once the city masks were delineated, they were overlaid with the river map from MERIT Hydro35 to identify the upstream and downstream ends of rivers flowing through the cities, corresponding to the city water inlet and city water outlet, respectively. The grid cells with accessible surface water in the vicinity of each city mask are assumed to serve as aqueduct origins, conveying water resources from outside the city mask. Data pre-processing

This study used various globally available datasets, including gridded population, city inventory, and hydrological data. All datasets were resampled to a spatial resolution of 5 arcmin. A grid resolution of 30 arcmin has commonly been used in global hydrological models in the Inter-Sectoral Impact Model Intercomparisons Project phase 2b36. However, these models are being developed to address water cycle questions that cannot be solved at 30 arcmin resolution, and 5 arcmin resolution is expected to become the new standard for global hydrological studies37–39. Accordingly, HUM adopts a spatial resolution of 5 arcmin.

We used WUP as the city inventory. The WUP consists of city-wise population data presented as tabular counts of population by urban settlement. WUP includes data on 1,860 cities projected to have populations exceeding 300,000 in 2018. In addition to population counts, WUP includes the latitude and longitude coordinates of each city center. For global gridded population data, we used the GPW dataset, as both WUP and GPW are adjusted to align with the total population reported in World Population Prospects40. GPW encompasses nearly 12,500,000 administrative units, and the population within each administrative unit is distributed to grid cells using an areal-weighting41. Since GPW is based on censuses conducted between 2005 and 2014, we adopted 2010 as the base year for all data products.

The mean annual streamflow was calculated using the H08 global hydrological model42 to estimate aqueduct origins. We followed the standard procedure described by Hanasaki et al.42 to calculate global streamflow, with modifications to three settings. First, we used the W5E5v2 global meteorological dataset43 as the input meteorological forcing, which was downscaled to 5 arcmin resolution. Second, after interpolating all boundary conditions, we ran H08 at 5 arcmin resolution rather than the standard 30 arcmin resolution. Third, flow directions, watershed boundaries, and the digital elevation map were obtained from MERIT Hydro and MERIT DEM44. Finally, all human activities (e.g., water abstraction and reservoir operation) were turned off for simplicity. The average value for 2010–2019 was used as the mean flow rate.

Development of city masks

We developed a new algorithm to delineate city boundaries, referred to as the city mask, which contains the city population counts obtained from WUP. A key advantage of using WUP is its reliance on various national definitions of urban population counts, rather than on a fixed population density threshold. This approach is expected to minimize the underestimation of city population by including sparsely populated areas, such as suburbs, in GPW. The enlarged box in Fig. 2 shows an example of city mask formation. The city mask requires gridded population, city population, and city coordinates as input data. WUP provides the city population count (WUPpop) along with latitude and longitude coordinates (WUPlat,WUPlon). The primary algorithm uses gridded population data to estimate the urban population that matches the reported population (WUPpop). As illustrated in Fig. 2 (step 1), the process begins by selecting the grid cell with the highest population density near (WUPlat,WUPlon), which is then added to an initial tentative city mask (M). Following this method, we constructed city masks for 1,860 cities. However, some city masks overlapped or were entirely covered by other city masks. This issue primarily arose from double-counting populations across cities defined differently in the WUP and frequently affected highly built-up areas. In this study, we separated and extracted cities listed in WUP that were embedded within larger neighboring city masks. To achieve this separation, we propose a clustering method based on a population density gradient. The clustering algorithm aims to identify the minimal cluster unit within the extensive city mask. In addition to the clustering method, we established two criteria to address exploration failures. First, the initial grid cell must have a minimum population density of 300 persons per km2. Second, exploration is terminated if the newly added grid cell exhibits a population density of less than 100 persons per km2. This second condition is essential to prevent the city mask from including areas of low population density, which may arise from the areal-weighting method employed in GPW41.

Among 1,860 cities reported in WUP, valid city masks were delineated for 1,604 cities as of 2010. The remaining 256 cities were excluded for the following reasons. 134 cities had city center grid cells with population densities below 100 persons per km² and therefore did not meet the threshold for city-mask delineation, and the remaining 122 WUP-listed cities were nested within larger city masks even after the clustering procedure. For these 122 cities, the algorithm determined that no clear population density gradient existed between them and the embedded WUP-listed cities, indicating that they share the same water-supplied area developing around the nesting city’s center. Consequently, these nested WUP-listed cities fall entirely within the larger nesting city masks and are absorbed into their city masks. Only the nesting cities’ masks are retained in HUM.

As an example of a city mask formed without the clustering method, Riyadh in Fig. 1c shows that the city mask captures the area with high population density, corresponding to the darker orange regions in GPW. This result indicates that the city masks are shaped by the GPW population distribution, which spatially allocates the WUP-reported population to a gridded map. Of the 1,604 valid cities, 428 were characterized by the adjacency or overlap of multiple urban areas, and the exploration process for city masks in these areas was terminated before reaching the population reported in WUP, using the clustering method. An example of the clustering method is shown in Fig. 1e, which separates Guangzhou, Zhongshan, Dongguan, and Shenzhen in the Pearl River Delta, China. In urban water resource assessment, cities should be distinguished by their water sources (e.g., Zhongshan relies on surface water from the Xijiang River to the west, whereas Shenzhen relies on the Dongjiang River to the east). Another example, shown in Fig. 1d, is São Paulo and Santos in Brazil, which suggests that the clustering method is often applied in highly urbanized regions.

Identification of the city water inlet and city water outlet

HUM contains hydrological information linked to each city mask. It associates each city mask with the locations of water supply inlets and drainage outlets to address the absence of urban hydrological information in the global hydrological model16. This information was developed on the assumption that each city mask has water supply and drainage facilities that draw water from rivers for urban use and return wastewater to rivers within the urban area. In this study, these facilities were simplified to inflow and outflow for the city mask and denoted as the city water inlet and city water outlet, respectively. Withdrawn water from the city water inlet was assumed to be distributed throughout the entire city mask, and after use, this water was collected at the city water outlet. We hypothesized that each river basin within the city mask contained at least one city water inlet, optimally positioned at the most upstream edge of the river within the city mask, as wastewater is transported along natural gradients.

Identification of the city water inlet and outlet grid cells was achieved through the process illustrated in Fig. 4. First, the river basin map from MERIT Hydro was overlaid with a city mask, and all river basins intersecting the city mask were extracted as source basins, as shown in Fig. 4d. For each source basin, we identified the grid cell with the largest catchment area within the city mask. These grid cells, representing the most downstream points of the target basins within the city mask, were designated as city water outlets. Beginning at the outlet grid cells, we traced water flow upstream. During this process, whenever a grid cell where tributaries merged was encountered, the trajectory followed the tributary with the larger catchment area. This tracing process was repeated until the most upstream grid cell within the city mask was reached; such grid cells were designated city water inlets, as illustrated in Fig. 4e. River trajectories that contain the city water inlet and outlet grid cells are referred to as the main river.

The source basins must meet two conditions. First, a source basin must have a minimum catchment area of five grid cells to exclude smaller basins in coastal regions. Second, the main river path should contain at least two grid cells within the city mask to allow identification of the city water inlet and outlet. If only a single source was present within the city mask and the main river path corresponding to that source basin consisted of a single grid cell, the grid cell with the largest annual streamflow within the city mask was designated the city water inlet. At the same time, the grid cell with the lowest elevation within the city mask was assigned as the city water outlet.

Estimation of aqueduct origin

In cases where a city’s primary water source is located outside its city mask, the city may rely on aqueducts to access distant surface water (e.g., Greater Los Angeles in the United States depends on the water resources of the Sacramento and San Joaquin Rivers delivered through the California Aqueduct, and many others45). Large-scale inter-basin transfer projects spanning several hundred kilometres have been documented in previous studies46. However, this documentation covers primarily large-scale infrastructure serving megacities, whereas many mid-sized cities that rely on external water supplies lack detailed records. To address this gap, our study assumes that the origins of documented/undocumented aqueducts follow the general trend reported by McDonald et al.25,47, in which most cities obtain water from within 100 km of their city center. Based on this assumption, we searched all river segments within 100 km of the city mask whose abstraction would increase the city’s total water availability by more than 5%. To avoid double-counting along the same river channel, when multiple grid cells overlap in their upstream-downstream connectivity, only the grid cell with the largest mean annual streamflow is retained. Rather than representing the geolocation of existing infrastructure, these grid cells indicate potentially developable external water sources (see Usage Notes for details).

We explored candidate grid cells within 100 km of the city water inlets. Here, we imposed a constraint that candidate grid cells for potential urban water sources must be located at a higher elevation than the nearest city water inlet grid cell based on MERIT DEM (Fig. 4b). This constraint was used to avoid the unrealistic estimation of pumping from low-elevation sites. The gray area in Fig. 4f indicates the explored grid cells, while the light green area highlights the candidate grid cells that meet the elevation requirement. Ultimately, the green grid cells were identified as potential urban water source grid cells and designated as aqueduct origins.

In total, 168 cities (10%) among 1,604 cities in HUM have multiple main rivers located in different basins within their city masks. When accounting for inter-basin water transfers via estimated aqueducts originating outside city masks, the number of cities with access to multiple river sources increases to 1,089 (68%). Data Records

HUM dataset provides global gridded data representing population-based city boundaries, hydrological inlets and outlets, and potential external water sources for 1,604 cities, and is publicly available at Zenodo (10.5281/zenodo.18158552)48. The dataset is formatted as GeoTIFF rasters at a spatial resolution of 5 arcmin, using the WGS 84 coordinate reference system (EPSG:4326), and spans the full global extent from 90°N to 90°S and 180°W to 180°E. Each grid cell contains a city identifier (city ID), assigned in descending order of city population according to the WUP. All raster files use a uniform structure with grid dimensions of 2160 rows by 4320 columns, and a NoData value of -9999 is assigned to empty cells.

When multiple cities share the same aqueduct origin, individual instances are recorded in separate layers to avoid data conflicts. Cities are sorted by city ID, with layer numbers assigned sequentially. For example, if city ID 1 and city ID 10 share the same aqueduct origin, the location for city ID 1 is stored in aqueduct_layer0001, while that for city ID 10 is stored in aqueduct_layer0002. If a specific city’s aqueduct origin is not found in layer0001, users should check subsequent layers (i.e., layer0002 through layer0008). A metadata table accompanies the dataset and provides the mapping between city IDs, names, and countries.

Technical Validation

To assess the technical quality of the dataset, we performed a series of validation analyses focusing on the spatial representation of cities and their water-related features. First, we examined the alignment between population in city masks and reported population by comparing HUM with the WUP population to quantify estimation errors arising from spatial resolution constraints. Second, we evaluated differences in urban boundary delineation between our city masks and satellite-derived urban extent data, demonstrating that HUM’s city boundaries differed from those produced by existing methods that rely on built-up area. Third, we compared our city masks with urban areas extracted using the Degree of Urbanization method applied to gridded population data, and we classified the results across the 22 United Nations subregions. Finally, we validated the spatial accuracy of the estimated city water inlets and aqueduct origins in HUM by examining their alignment with documented facilities for 245 of the 1,604 cities.

Comparison of HUM’s city masks with the population reported in WUP

Of the 1,860 cities listed in WUP, 1,604 cities were successfully delineated as city masks in HUM and were evaluated in this validation. These comprise 1,176 non-clustered cities (Fig. 6a) and 428 clustered cities (Fig. 6b). The remaining 256 cities did not meet the threshold for city mask delineation (as described in Methods) and were therefore excluded from HUM.

Comparisons between the population within the HUM’s city masks and the population reported in WUP. (a) Results for 1,176 cities explored without the clustering algorithm. (b) Results for 428 cities delineated using the clustering algorithm.

To evaluate the quality of our city mask delineation, we compared population estimates within the city masks against the city population reported in WUP. This comparison serves two distinct validation objectives. First, for non-clustered city masks, we quantified our city-wise population estimation error arising from the 5 arcmin spatial resolution constraint (Fig. 6a). Our algorithm aggregated population grid cells to approximate population reported in WUP; however, the discrete nature of the 5 arcmin grid cells prevented perfect alignment with target values (i.e., the population of the cities in question), introducing systematic discrepancies that we characterized here. Second, for clustered city masks (Fig. 6b), we assessed whether the clustering method effectively resolved the population double-counting problem inherent in WUP data. When city masks were initially constructed using WUP populations, some masks overlapped due to nested city definitions (e.g., a metropolitan area containing an independently listed city). The clustering method was designed to estimate the population for clustered cities. It separates the nested cities from the nesting city (Fig. 3d). Consequently, clustered city masks are expected to contain fewer inhabitants than reported in WUP, as they may include nested cities. This population reduction was not an error but rather evidence that the method was working as intended. Note that there are 122 inseparable cities after clustering (see Method section for detailed interpretation). These cities were not excluded from HUM but absorbed into nesting cities (e.g., the city of Foshan absorbed into the city of Guangzhou). In HUM, such merged areas are represented under the larger (nesting) city’s name. When comparing with WUP, we use only the larger city mask, which is constructed to match the larger city’s WUP-reported population (i.e., not the sum of both cities’ populations).

Figure 6a shows the estimated populations of 1,176 cities, delineated without the use of the clustering method, plotted against the corresponding values reported in the WUP. The coefficient of determination (R2) was 0.997, indicating good alignment between the estimated and reported values. The mean absolute error (MAE) was 0.0418, corresponding to an average error of approximately 40,000 inhabitants per city. The percent bias (PBIAS) indicated a slight overestimation of 4.17%. Figure 6b presents the same comparison as Fig. 6a, but for the 428 clustered cities. In this case, R² decreased to 0.937, and MAE increased to 0.324. These metrics reflect the intentional population reduction resulting from the clustering method. On average, approximately 324,000 fewer inhabitants were counted in the adjusted city masks than in WUP. PBIAS was −17.4%, indicating that 83.6% of the initially reported population remains within the adjusted city masks after clustering. The high retention rate demonstrates that the clustering method preserves the core urban population while selectively removing only the overlapping peripheral areas where city masks conflicted, confirming that the method functions as intended.

Comparison of HUM’s city masks with satellite-derived urban extent data

HUM is designed to provide city masks that can be applied to gridded population data representing each city’s water-served population. Meanwhile, other global-scale approaches exist for detecting urban areas, such as techniques based on satellite-derived land cover data49. This urban land cover dataset classifies each grid cell as urban or non-urban using satellite imagery. To demonstrate why HUM adopts its own city delineation method, we compared HUM’s city masks with the earlier work primarily based on satellite imagery (referred to as H1949). The red points in Fig. 7 correspond to H19 upscaled to 5 arcmin resolution. H19 is land cover data, where only densely built-up cells are classified as urban areas, whereas HUM is data on the contiguous distribution of populated areas, where even non-built-up cells can be classified as urban areas. Because these two datasets are based on fundamentally different concepts, direct agreement is not expected. This comparison quantifies differences in city extent between H19 and HUM. For each city mask, we computed the proportion of grid cells within the HUM’s city mask classified as urban by H19, which we defined as the urban land cover rate.

The second column of Table 3 reports the median of the urban land cover rate across the United Nations 22 subregions. The global mean urban land cover rate was 0.7, indicating that, on average, approximately 30% of grid cells within HUM’s city masks extend beyond H19’s city extent. These grid cells correspond to non-urban land cover classes in H19, such as low-density built-up areas. This result demonstrates the necessity of HUM’s city delineation approach, since methods based on densely built-up areas used in H19 would systematically exclude suburban and peri-urban zones where water utilities are likely to extend service coverage (e.g., Riyadh in Fig. 7c). While some cities, such as Paris, showed an urban land cover rate of 1.0 (Fig. 7b), the majority of cities exhibited lower rates than 1.0 (Table 3), indicating that HUM captures a broader urban extent than H19. This broader coverage is partly attributed to the areal-weighting method used in GPW. GPW distributes census population across administrative units, allocating inhabitants to suburban and non-built-up areas rather than concentrating them in densely built-up zones. Because these administrative units are often used for water allocation management33, HUM’s approach is better suited for identifying water-served areas than employing H19.

Comparison of HUM’s city masks with the Degree of Urbanization method

To demonstrate how HUM’s delineation approach differs from existing threshold-based urban classification methods, we compared HUM’s city masks with urban population defined by the Degree of Urbanization (DoU) method26. The DoU method, widely adopted for harmonized global urban statistics, defines urban areas as contiguous grid cells with population densities of at least 1,500 persons per km². While this threshold-based approach enables global comparability, it systematically excludes suburban and peri-urban zones where population densities fall below the threshold, but water service is likely to exist.

For each city mask in HUM, we calculated the population for which densities were below the DoU threshold of 1,500 persons per km². The third column of Table 3 presents the results of the comparison across the 22 United Nations subregions. The global median indicates that applying the DoU density threshold to GPW underestimates urban populations by approximately 326,490 inhabitants per city compared to HUM. This discrepancy demonstrates that threshold-based methods, such as DoU, systematically underestimate the water-served population reported in WUP, as captured by our methodology.

The discrepancies vary considerably by region. In Western Europe, even though the median urban land cover rate was 1.0 (i.e., all grid cells within city masks are classified as urban in satellite-based datasets), the DoU threshold underestimated the number of residents by an average of 235,250. Similar patterns are observed in other European regions, Northern Africa, Southern Africa, and Central Asia. These regions demonstrate that even in highly urbanized areas with compact urban forms, the DoU threshold fails to capture suburban zones that remain integrated into urban water supply systems. The discrepancy is even more pronounced in regions with more spread-out urban development patterns. In Western Africa (median urban land cover rate of 0.25) and Southern Asia (0.40), the population below the DoU threshold averages 310,721 and 303,747 per city, respectively. In Western Asia, this number reaches 562,144 people per city. These substantial populations would be systematically excluded from water resource assessments if DoU-based boundaries were used, even though these residents receive urban water services and contribute to urban water demand.

This comparison highlights a fundamental conceptual distinction: DoU and similar density-based methods delineate urban cores suitable for demographic and economic statistics, whereas HUM delineates water-served populations essential for resource assessment. The difference arises because water service coverage extends beyond high-density cores into lower-density suburban and peri-urban zones. The observed differences also reflect the underlying population distribution models. We acknowledge that substantial uncertainty exists in the choice of population distribution models, which can significantly influence the resulting city mask extents50–53. The gridded population data (GPW) used as input to HUM employs areal-weighting methods to distribute census-reported populations across administrative units. Because this can result in population allocation to non-urban land cover (as evidenced by the urban land cover rate of 0.7 in Table 3), HUM’s approach is more suitable for covering the entire water-served population than a fixed-threshold approach.

Validation of city water inlets and aqueduct origins against documented facilities

To validate the spatial accuracy of estimated city water inlets and aqueduct origins, we compared HUM with water facilities documented in the City Water Map25 (hereafter, documented facilities), which provides georeferenced information on water sources globally. We focused on facilities classified as rivers, lakes, and reservoirs, excluding groundwater and desalination facilities, as these fall outside HUM’s surface-water focus. After screening, 245 cities were selected for validation. City water inlets and aqueduct origins in HUM do not necessarily represent the precise locations of dams or reservoirs. Instead, they indicate hydrologically plausible points along the river network where urban water withdrawals are most likely to occur. This design reflects the reality that reservoirs often deliver water to cities through river barriers (e.g., weirs, locks, barrages)54–56 located far downstream from the reservoir itself.

To evaluate whether our estimated locations are intended to be hydrologically plausible, rather than geographically precise, we employed two validation approaches. First, we conducted proximity-based validation by examining whether at least one documented facility exists within specified distances (i.e., 10–50 km) of each estimated location. Second, and more importantly, we conducted connectivity-based validation to evaluate hydrological coherence within the river network. For this approach, we verified whether each documented facility and its corresponding estimated location (i.e., city water inlet or aqueduct origin) are connected along the same river network. We traced the downstream flow paths from both a documented facility and an estimated location, and considered the estimated location correct if they merged. The connectivity-based validation ensures that our estimates capture hydrologically meaningful positions rather than the closeness of geographic coordinates.

The large spatial offsets between the HUM’s hydrological information and documented facilities (often exceeding 30 km) indicate that evaluating our estimates using Euclidean distance alone is insufficient to assess their validity. Instead, the reliability of the estimated locations should be evaluated by their hydrological coherence within the river network. In particular, whether they preserve the upstream–downstream connectivity with documented facilities. This functional validity is evaluated using the connectivity-based validation approach described in the following section.

Connectivity-based validation

To conduct connectivity-based validation, we defined a set of metrics based on spatial relationships among city water inlets (CWI), estimated aqueduct origins (EAO), and documented facilities in the City Water Map (CWM). The validation framework is built upon six fundamental counts. Each metric is explained in Fig. 10 with examples as follows. For city water inlets, we define true positives (CWI_TP) as estimated inlet grid cells that are hydrologically connected to at least one documented facility (Blue circles in Fig. 10a). This connection is confirmed when the documented facility is located upstream or downstream of the estimated city water inlet. Conversely, false positives (CWI_FP) are estimated in the city water inlet grid cells that lack a hydrological connection to any documented facility (Red circles in Fig. 10g). Similarly, for estimated aqueduct origins, we define true positives (EAO_TP) as the number of estimated origin grid cells connected to at least one documented facility (Blue star in Fig. 10a), and false positives (EAO_FP) as origins with no such connection (Red stars in Fig. 10c). This is especially true for mid-sized cities, which constitute the majority of HUM. For these cities, identifying potential external sources remains valuable for forward-looking water resource planning.

Interpretation of the results of connectivity-based validation

City water inlets and aqueduct origins collectively define the range of urban water availability (see the Usage Notes section for a detailed framework). For this framework to be meaningful, it is essential that city water inlets reasonably capture documented facilities with minimal false negatives. Usage Notes

The dataset is provided in GeoTIFF format, which can be directly imported into standard GIS tools. When overlaid on global basemaps such as Google Earth or OpenStreetMap, the spatial relationships between cities and their associated water sources become visually intuitive. To examine hydrological positioning, users may overlay HUM data on the 5 arcmin version of the global river network provided by the MERIT Hydro dataset. This enables users to locate city water inlets, city water outlets, and aqueduct origins within the global river network. To estimate the population within each city boundary, users can overlay city masks with gridded population data from GPW. By summing population values within the masked area for a given city ID, the total population of that city can be derived. When streamflow outputs from global hydrological models are available, users can overlay city water inlets to extract water availability at each city’s inlet points. By combining this information with gridded water demand data, urban water stress can be calculated for individual cities. Furthermore, overlaying aqueduct origins on streamflow data enables estimation of potential water availability.

Limitations of city masks

City masks in HUM are designed to represent the areas where water-served populations are likely to be inhabited rather than built-up urban areas. As a result, city masks are generally larger than satellite-derived urban extent datasets. Users should be aware that the extent of the city masks is influenced by the resolution of administrative units used during the development of GPW. The city masks in regions with coarser administrative divisions (e.g., level2-2 or level-3 administrative units in Western Asia, Southern Asia, and parts of Africa in Fig. 8) may include considerable non-urban territory because the population distribution is sparse in GPW.

Recommended usage of city water Inlets and aqueduct origins

City water inlets and aqueduct origins were developed under distinct concepts and should be interpreted accordingly. City water inlets indicate existing water sources flowing through city masks (Fig. 10a–f). In contrast, aqueduct origins represent external water sources flowing outside the city masks; these include both potentially developable sites (Fig. 10b,c,e,f,i) and existing infrastructure that city water inlets cannot capture because they are limited to rivers flowing through city masks (Fig. 10a,b,d,e,g–i). Users can interpret these two datasets as providing a range of urban water availability: from existing intra-city water sources (i.e., city water inlets) to potentially developable external water sources (i.e., city water inlets plus aqueduct origins).

Caveats regarding the geospatial accuracy of city water inlets and aqueduct origins

It is essential to recognize that city water inlets and aqueduct origins are not intended to represent precise geographic coordinates of water intake facilities or reservoirs. Instead, they indicate hydrologically plausible points along the river network where urban water withdrawals are likely to occur. Validation demonstrates that estimated locations remain hydrologically connected to documented facilities (i.e., they are on the same river).
