Profile of cases and factors associated with poor outcomes among patients admitted to the intensive care unit at a regional referral hospital in Bhutan: an observational study
Authors: Thinley Dorji, Sangay Wangmo, Sangay Tenzin, Tenzin Choden, Lok Bahadur Ghalley, Sangay Wangchuk, Choeda Gyaltshen, Kesang Namgyal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTimely admission and treatment of critically ill patients to Intensive Care Units (ICU) is shown to reduce premature mortality. However, low- and middle-income countries face many challenges in providing ICU services. This study describes the profile of patients admitted to ICU and their outcomes at the Central Regional Referral Hospital in Bhutan.MethodThis was an observational study with a review of records of patients from 2021 to 2023. The data were extracted into a pro forma, entered into EpiData Entry 3.1, and analysed in STATA 18.ResultsThere were 287 patients admitted to the ICU. The mean age was 56.53 (SD ± 19.18) years). The highest number of cases were admitted from the Emergency Department (117, 40.77%) and Medicine Department (50, 17.42%). The common indications for admissions were pulmonary disease (91, 31.70%), sepsis/infection (61, 21.25%), cardiovascular diseases (48, 16.72%), and cerebrovascular diseases (38, 13.24%). There were 140 (48.78%) patients who received mechanical ventilation, 27 (9.41%) required positive pressure airway support, 36 patients (12.54%) had central venous access insertion, and 19 patients (6.62%) had haemodialysis line insertion. The mortality rate was 51.22% (147/287 patients); the most common causes of mortality were septic shock (22, 14.97%), stroke (16, 10.88%), and pneumonia (14, 9.52%). Patients with chronic liver disease (adjusted OR 4.32, 95% CI 1.84–10.09, p = 0.001), and those receiving Ceftriaxone (adjusted OR 2.57, 95% CI 1.31–5.03, p = 0.006), Piperacillin-Tazobactam (adjusted OR 2.63, 95% CI 1.02–6.77, p = 0.045), and Cefazolin (adjusted OR 7.57, 95% CI 1.26–44.16, p = 0.026) had higher odds of mortality. Patients receiving Doxycycline (adjusted OR 0.49, 05% CI 0.25–0.96, p = 0.036) had lower odds of mortality.ConclusionAbout half of patients admitted to the ICU ended in mortality and the most common cause was septic shock. Introduction

Intensive care units (ICU) are settings with advanced capabilities for the care of critically ill patients [1]. Admission of patients to ICUs has a marked benefit on their survival across many disease types and, across age groups [2]. Early recognition of clinical parameters and administrative guidelines in ICU admission criteria are designed to improve patient survival [3]. However, ICUs in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) have fewer resources for the diagnosis and management of critically ill patients with wide variations in the level of services across urban/rural areas and, across public/private sectors [4]. ICU mortality rates vary between 25.6 and 56.18% in the periods 2014–2020 in countries in Africa [5, 6], and between 23.4 and 57.2% between 2008 and 2018 in countries in South Asia [7–10].

Globally, there has been an increase in both the number of critical care beds in hospitals and the number of critical care admissions. In hospitals in the United States between 2000 and 2010, there was an increase in ICU bed/hospital bed ratio by 20.4% [11]. Similarly, countries in South Asia also witnessed an increase in the availability of critical care beds and doctors trained in critical care medicine [4]. However, the rising cost of ICU care often leading to catastrophic health expenditure has received public attention and debates. In India, while the cost of an ICU bed in government public hospitals was estimated at Indian Rupees 10,364 per day in 2009 and Rupees 11,241 in 2021 [12, 13], the cost in private hospitals is higher often serving as a barrier to life-saving therapies. As much as cost is involved in access to ICU admissions, it is important to understand the clinico-epidemiological background of cases requiring critical care in the LMICs [14]. For optimal utilization of ICU beds and to optimize patient outcomes based on the local capabilities of ICUs, it is recommended to evaluate and measure the processes and outcomes of care [4, 14].

Bhutan is a lower-middle-income country with a three-tiered healthcare system where healthcare is provided free of cost by the government. The country in 2025 had ICUs inclusive of adult, paediatric, and neonatal ICUs in three of its twenty districts: Jigme Dorji Wangchuck (JDW) National Referral Hospital in Thimphu, Central Regional Referral Hospital in Gelephu and Eastern Regional Referral Hospital in Mongar (Fig. 1). These are staffed by three adult intensivists and one paediatric intensivist at the JDW National Referral Hospital, and by general physicians or paediatricians at the two regional referral hospitals. A dedicated surgical ICU and critical coronary care unit have been established at the JDW National Referral Hospital. The ICU nurses are trained at the Faculty of Nursing and Public Health, Khesar Gyalpo University of Medical Sciences of Bhutan. During the COVID-19 pandemic period in 2021 and 2022, the Ministry of Health provided ICU management training to medical officers and nurses.

Location of three tertiary care hospitals with facilities for intensive care units in Bhutan, 2025. JDWNRH = Jigme Dorji Wangchuck National Referral Hospital in Thimphu, CRRH = Central Regional Referral Hospital in Gelephu and, ERRH = Eastern Regional Referral Hospital in Mongar

In 2022, the adult ICU had 547 admissions in the JDW National Referral Hospital, 120 admissions in the Central Regional Referral Hospital and, 102 admissions in the Eastern Regional Referral Hospital. In this study, we describe the sociodemographic and clinical profile of patients, the processes of care involved, and the factors associated with poor outcome of admission at the Adult Intensive Care Unit, Central Regional Referral Hospital, Gelephu.

Method

Study design

This was an observational study with retrospective review of data of patients admitted at the Adult Intensive Care Unit, Central Regional Referral Hospital, Gelephu between 2021 and 2023.

Setting and study site

The health system in Bhutan is structured into three tiers that include 187 primary healthcare centres including three municipality centres at the primary level, 52 general and district hospitals at the secondary level and three referral centres at the tertiary level in 2023 [15]. The referral centres are located in three strategic locations: Jigme Dorji Wangchuck National Referral Hospital in Thimphu, Eastern Regional Referral Hospital in Mongar district and Central Regional Referral Hospital in Sarpang district (Fig. 1). As of 2024, the JDW National Referral Hospital had 10 adult ICU beds, the Eastern Regional Referral Hospital and the Central Regional Referral Hospital have five beds.

This study was conducted at the Central Regional Referral Hospital, Gelephu, Bhutan. It is the largest hospital in the south-central region with 150 beds catering to 174,840 out-patient visits and 3814 admissions in 2021 [16]. The Adult ICU in the Central Regional Referral Hospital is staffed by internal medicine physicians and a nursing team (not all are trained in critical care nursing). Care for patients is guided by the same set of standard operating procedures that are implemented across all ICUs in the country. However, there may be differences in the level of care at the regional referral hospitals based on the availability of resources and skills of the treating physicians.

Study population and sample size

In our study, the data of all patients admitted to the adult ICU between 2021 and 2023 were retrieved. This was a census sampling. Data were extracted between February and April 2024.

Data variables and data collection method

The data were recorded on a structured pro forma (see Appendix). The list of admissions to the ICU was extracted from the Indoor Admission Register and details about the admission and data variables were extracted from the patient files maintained at the Medical Record Section. The data variables included age, sex, occupation, pre-admission level of care, primary diagnosis at admission, co-morbid conditions, critical care-related processes (positive pressure airway, invasive mechanical ventilation, advance haemodynamic accesses, use of vasopressor agents, therapeutic procedures), bacterial cultures, and the use of antibiotics.

Data analysis and statistics

Data were entered between April and May 2024 into EpiData Entry (version 3.1, EpiData Association, Odense, Denmark) and, analysed using STATA (version 18, StataCorp LP USA). Continuous variables are summarized using means and standard deviations, or median and interquartile range. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies, proportions and, percentages. Factors related to ICU mortality were assessed using chi-square test, and t-test or Mann-Whitney U test. Those factors with p < 0.100 on univariable analysis were tested using multivariable logistic regression analysis. Those factors with p < 0.05 were considered significant.

Ethics considerations

This study was conducted as per the requirements and standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethics approval was obtained Institutional Review Board, Khesar Gyalpo University of Medical Sciences of Bhutan, Thimphu, Bhutan (IRB/Approval/PN/2023-024 /1126 dated 23 January 2024). Permission for this research was obtained from the Ministry of Health and hospital administrators before initiating the study. Informed consent was waived off by the ethics board as this was a review of records.

Results

Patient profile and ICU-related procedures

Data for 287 patients were extracted. The mean age was 56.53 (SD ± 19.18) years and 175 patients (60.98%) were male. There were 179 (62.37%) direct presentations to the hospital while the rest were cases (108, 37.63%) referred from the neighbouring districts. The highest number of cases were admitted from the Emergency Department (117, 40.77%), followed by the Medicine Department (50, 17.42%). The common indications for ICU admissions were pulmonary disease (91, 31.70%), sepsis/infection (61, 21.25%), cardiovascular diseases (48, 16.72%) and, cerebrovascular diseases (38, 13.24%). Factors associated with patient outcome

The mortality rate was 51.22% (147/287 patients). There were 128 patients (44.60%) who were transferred to the Wards and subsequently discharged, and 12 patients (4.18%) who were referred to the JDW National Referral Hospital or India. The most common causes of death were septic shock (22, 14.97%), stroke (16, 10.88%), pneumonia (14, 9.52%), and multi-organ dysfunction (9, 6.12%) and the others were grouped under others (82, 55.78%).

In the analysis of risk factors associated with poor outcome, patients with chronic liver disease (adjusted OR 3.80, 95% CI 1.59–10.97, p = 0.003) had higher odds of mortality. In terms of antibiotic therapy, higher odds of mortality were reported among those receiving Ceftriaxone (adjusted OR 2.57, 95% CI 1.31–5.03, p = 0.006), Piperacillin-Tazobactam (adjusted OR 2.63, 95% CI 1.02–6.77, p = 0.045), and Cefazolin (adjusted OR 7.57, 95% CI 1.26–44.16, p = 0.026). A lower odds of mortality was reported among those receiving Doxycycline (adjusted OR 0.49, 05% CI 0.25–0.96, p = 0.036). Discussion

This study describes the clinical profile of patients requiring ICU admission, the process and procedures involved in critical care and, the outcomes of admission in Bhutan. Mortality was reported in half of the patients admitted to our ICU, a rate which is higher than neighbouring countries. The mortality rate in reported studies ranges from 23.4% in India to 32% in Sri Lanka and 32.8% in Nepal [7, 9, 10]. Direct comparisons on ICU mortality need to be interpreted in the context of the healthcare system in each country, the levels of ICU services, the availability of ICU-related equipment and, the availability of trained medical doctors and nurses [1, 4]. ICUs in neighbouring countries are staffed by intensivists or anaesthesiologists with critical care training [9, 17]. In Bhutan, except for the JDW National Referral Hospital, the ICUs in the Regional Referral Hospitals are staffed by general physicians alone (no medical officers are posted in the ICUs); and only a few of the nurses have received additional critical care nursing certification. This situation of ICUs being staffed by general physicians [18] and having only a few nurses trained in critical care [4, 9] remains a major concern across LMICs. Even in countries with regulatory requirements for nurses to have postbasic qualifications in critical care nursing, it is found that the level of training provided to nurses is inconsistent across countries [19].

In the three-tiered healthcare system in Bhutan, the referral hospitals receive patients from the district hospitals and primary healthcare centres located in the neighbouring districts. Referral of patients in critical care situations such as sepsis, respiratory distress or trauma are done through ambulances and are accompanied by nurses from the referring hospitals. The other group of patients were those who presented directly to the hospital and were admitted through the Emergency Department or the Wards. As in other general ICUs, medical cases constituted the majority of the case loads admitted to the ICU [5, 9, 10].

The survival of patients in the ICU is not only linked to the level of care available but also the overall level of specialist/advanced services available at the hospital and patient pathways [1, 4]. Data from this study show that patients with cirrhosis of liver are at higher risk of mortality. Cirrhosis secondary to alcohol use is a major morbidity in Bhutan and the mortality reflects the sheer number of patients with frank complications resulting from decompensations.

In our study, sepsis and septic shock with multi-organ dysfunction were the major cause of mortality, followed by stroke and pneumonia. The early diagnosis and management of sepsis remains a practical challenge in many LMICs and sepsis contributes up to 50% of mortality in ICUs [10]. In efforts to improving timely diagnosis and management of sepsis across various health facilities in the country, a quick guide to sepsis management has been adopted in 2025 [20].

Ceftriaxone was the most common antibiotic used in the ICU, consistent with the findings of the national antibiotic use survey [21]. The overall duration of antibiotic use was between 3 – 11 days. The use of the Reserve group of antibiotics (Piperacillin-Tazobactam, Meropenem, Vancomycin and, Polymyxin B) remained low, most likely due to the antibiotic-restriction policy in place. While antibiotic-restriction policy is in place, antibiotics if required are available from the hospital pharmacy at any time of the day. Mortality was higher among those receiving Ceftriaxone, Piperacillin-Tazobactam and Cefazolin, and lower among those receiving Doxycycline. This reflects the need for diagnostic stewardship and administration of antibiotics appropriate to the clinical context. Beyond routine bacterial culture and serological tests for dengue, malaria and scrub typhus, the hospital lacks the capacity for diagnosis of other infectious aetiologies.

In this study, more than half of the patients admitted with stroke ended up in mortality. This might have been due to the critical nature of patients conditions requiring ICU admission and delayed hospital presentation due to lack of awareness on detection of early signs of stroke [22]. A dedicated stroke unit is available only at the JDW National Referral Hospital.

In 2024, a dialysis bed was established inside our ICU saving the need to transfer patient out to the haemodialysis unit. Other procedures such as therapeutic endoscopy, bronchoscopy, etc. are currently not available. Therefore, a comprehensive evaluation and development of a strategic plan is recommended to equip hospitals with the capability to deliver proper Level 3 ICU care in Bhutan [1, 23]. In addition to the patient care provided inside the ICU, there is a need to create awareness among health workers on the identification and management of post-intensive care syndromes [24, 25].

In this study, patients with cardiovascular diseases had higher proportions of mortality. The spectrum of cardiovascular diseases includes decompensations of heart failure, rheumatic heart diseases and among those with prosthetic heart valves. The number of patients diagnosed with coronary artery disease were based on electrocardiogram, echocardiogram and Troponin T levels. Bhutan’s first cardiac catheterization laboratory was established at the National Referral Hospital in September 2023. Following this, a more reliable reporting of the burden of coronary disease being documented.

This baseline description also provides an overview of the gaps that need bridging to improve the quality of care and patient outcomes. Some recommendations include the training of more intensivists to staff the ICUs in regional referral hospitals, training of nurses to provide critical care nursing and, the introduction of ICU-related therapeutic services. While these may have high up-front costs at the time of introduction, such interventions will be cost-effective in downstream prevention of ICU-related complications and mortality. During the COVID-19 pandemic, there were positive impacts on the expansion of critical care facilities and services in many LMICs [24]. However, after the pandemic, it has become increasingly difficult to sustain those critical care set up and many such facilities and equipment have been reportedly fallen out of use.

There is one major caveat in the interpretation of mortality rates reported in this study – the perception of family members towards end-of-life care. While there are ethical dilemmas surrounding practices related to Do Not Resuscitate or Do Not Intubate decisions [26], the choices of family members play an important role in the outcome noted in the ICUs. Data from Nepal suggests that withdrawal of life support accounted for 7.74% of deaths recorded in ICU [7]. Therefore, a prospective study taking into consideration the end-of-life care-related decisions made by family members is recommended to study the true rates of mortality in ICUs. In addition, it is recommended to streamline the admission processes to ICU so that resources prioritization of resources can be directed to those likely to have better outcomes.

Implications and next steps

This article draws attention to the standards of facilities and care that are available at and ICU at a referral hospital in Bhutan. A strategic plan may be developed on a timeline for training of appropriate human resources for providing multidisciplinary care; introduction of therapeutic procedures/interventions, appropriate diagnostic tests, therapies; and standard procedures or protocols for various care processes. In addition, policy guides are required to guide both clinicians and family members on the navigating care contacts related to ethically difficult clinical and care-giving decisions.

Limitations

The findings presented in this study are from a retrospective review of records. There were difficulties in finding records on the actual cause of death recorded by the clinicians. The study period also covered the COVID-19 pandemic years that posed serious challenges in health staffing that could have skewed the mortality data. A prospective study with data from all ICUs in Bhutan is recommended to study the baseline severity of illness at presentation, factors that contribute to mortality with an overall effort in improving the quality and standards of critical care.

Conclusions

Half of the patients admitted to the intensive care unit ended in mortality, with sepsis/septic shock as the most common cause of death. Patients with cirrhosis of liver had higher odds of mortality. However, the interpretation of mortality rate needs to be interpreted in the context of decision-making processes surrounding end-of-life care in Bhutan. This study provides baseline information on the identification of strengths, weaknesses, and opportunities in improving critical care outcomes in the country.
