Feasibility and early safety of minimally invasive lower median sternotomy in aortic valve replacement
Authors: Shaoye Wang, Pengfei Chen, Haochao Li, Chenyu Liu, Zujun Chen, Liqing Wang, Xinjin Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to compare perioperative outcomes between minimally invasive lower median sternotomy (LMS) and conventional full median sternotomy (FMS) in patients undergoing isolated aortic valve replacement (AVR).MethodsThis retrospective cohort study enrolled 41 patients who underwent isolated AVR (25 via LMS, 16 via FMS). Patient demographics, intraoperative data, and early postoperative outcomes were analyzed and compared between the two groups.ResultsThe two groups were comparable in terms of baseline characteristics. There were no significant differences in intraoperative metrics, including operation time (LMS 238.6 ± 45.3 vs. FMS 264.9 ± 77.7 min, P = 0.177), cardiopulmonary bypass time (145.5 ± 41.6 vs. 140.9 ± 27.7 min, P = 0.700), and aortic cross-clamp time (116.4 ± 32.9 vs. 111.6 ± 23.1 min, P = 0.609). No red blood cell or plasma transfusion was required in either group. Postoperatively, there were no 30-day deaths or severe adverse events. The lengths of hospital stay (7.5 ± 3.6 vs. 7.6 ± 4.0 days, P = 0.972), ICU stay (1.9 ± 1.1 vs. 2.2 ± 1.0 days, P = 0.366), and mechanical ventilation time (12.5 ± 5.9 vs. 12.6 ± 5.3 h, P = 0.954) were similar between the two groups. Patient satisfaction with scar appearance was significantly higher in the LMS group than in the FMS group (92.0% vs. 37.5%, P = 0.001).ConclusionLMS for isolated AVR represents a safe and feasible minimally invasive alternative to conventional FMS, with equivalent intraoperative efficiency and early postoperative safety profiles, and offers superior cosmetic outcomes. LMS offers a viable minimally invasive option without compromising procedural outcomes.Keywords: Minimally invasive sternotomy, Aortic valve replacement, Minimally invasive lower median sternotomy, Perioperative outcomes

Introduction

Aortic valve replacement (AVR) is one of the most widely used treatments for aortic valve disease. For decades, conventional full median sternotomy (FMS) has been the standard surgical approach for AVR, with well-documented efficacy in restoring valvular hemodynamics [1]. However, this technique is associated with substantial surgical trauma due to full median sternotomy (FMS), which may contribute to prolonged postoperative recovery and potential incision-related complications—limitations that have driven the evolution of minimally invasive surgical strategies [2].

Minimally invasive median sternotomy has gained recognition as a promising technique for aortic valve replacement (AVR). The reduced incision length not only yields superior cosmetic results but also contributes to the preservation of sternal integrity [3]. Compared with the upper sternal incision, the minimally invasive lower median sternotomy (LMS) is currently less commonly utilized in clinical practice [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]. LMS has been successfully applied in coronary artery bypass grafting, demonstrating favorable safety, graft patency, and cosmetic outcomes [10]. Accordingly, we adopted LMS for its superior cosmetic outcomes (due to scar concealment) and favorable perioperative safety profile. In this retrospective study, we compared perioperative outcomes between LMS and FMS and reviewed our institutional experience with LMS for isolated AVR.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This single-center, retrospective cohort study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Fuwai Hospital (Approval No.2023–2005), and the requirement for individual patient consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the analysis. We screened the medical records of all consecutive patients who underwent isolated surgical AVR for aortic valve disease between January 2019 and December 2024. These patients were treated in Treatment Group B, Ward 2, Department of Structural Heart Disease, Fuwai Hospital. Patients requiring concomitant procedures (e.g., coronary artery bypass grafting, aortic surgery) or those with a history of prior cardiac surgery were excluded. All procedures were performed by senior cardiac surgeons with more than 10 years of experience in aortic valve surgery. A total of 41 patients were ultimately enrolled and divided into two groups based on the surgical approach: LMS group (n = 25) and FMS group (n = 16).

The choice of surgical technique was at the discretion of the operating surgeon and based on patient anatomy. All enrolled patients underwent preoperative CT. Unlike other studies [11], [12], [13], we did not use the manubriosternal level corresponding to the Lewis angle as a reference. Instead, we used the intercostal space corresponding to the level of the left coronary artery ostium on preoperative CT to assess whether the aortic root was positioned sufficiently low relative to the planned incision to allow adequate exposure of the aorta and aortic root.

When the left coronary artery ostium was at the level of the fourth rib or fourth intercostal space (anterior axillary line), aortic exposure through a lower minimally invasive incision was poor in patients undergoing AVR. When it was at the level of the fifth rib, exposure was better, permitting aortic cannulation through the incision; however, exposure for the valve surgery itself remained slightly suboptimal, requiring an experienced surgeon. When the ostium was at or below the level of the fifth intercostal space, the surgical field was comparable to that of conventional median sternotomy. This preoperative assessment allowed for accurate determination of the incision site based on aortic position. In the LMS group, all patients showed the left coronary artery ostium at or below the level of the fifth rib or fifth intercostal space.

Surgical technique

The patient was placed in a conventional supine position, and the incision position was marked before surgery: the median incision, up to the level of the third intercostal space, down to 2 cm above the xiphoid process (Fig. 1). Through this incision, subcutaneous fat and muscle are dissected from the left second intercostal space down to the xiphoid process. The sternum saw splits the sternum upwards from the xiphoid process to the level of the third rib and splits the sternum between the second intercostal space on the left side of the sternum, in order to avoid damage to the left internal mammary artery.

The minimally invasive lower median sternotomy (LMS) in aortic valve replacement.

For incisions of approximately 10 cm, our commonly used standard retractors only minimally retracted the sternum. To improve exposure of the ascending aorta, two pericardial stay sutures are placed on either side to elevate the aorta from a deep to a more superficial plane, facilitating arterial cannulation and aortotomy. Arterial cannula should be selected according to the patient's weight, the position of arterial cannula as close as possible, sometimes behind the sternum, it is necessary to use forceps to lift the aortic adventitia to retract the aorta gently caudally to reveal the arterial cannulation site.

Venous drainage is achieved using standard superior vena cava, inferior vena cava, or bicaval atrial cannulas. We all perform superior vena cava drainage first, for superior vena cava exposure is good, we use right-angle superior vena cava cannula, and for poor exposure, we use straight superior vena cava cannula. Left heart drainage is still inserted through the right upper pulmonary vein incision. For aortic cross-clamping, we need to choose a long length and large angle of cross-clamp forceps to ensure that the blockade is sufficient and the impact on the surgical field is as small as possible. All cannulation and procedural steps are performed within the same incision. By fully evaluating the position of the aorta and heart before surgery, the process of establishing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) through the entire thoracotomy and arteriovenous cannula is basically similar to that of traditional median thoracotomy.

Data collection and outcome measures

Patient demographics, preoperative comorbidities, and baseline echocardiographic data were collected from electronic medical records. Primary outcomes included intraoperative metrics, including operation time, CPB time, aortic cross-clamp time, intraoperative bleeding volume, and transfusion requirements. Secondary outcomes were early postoperative outcomes, including length of hospital and intensive care unit (ICU) stay, duration of mechanical ventilation, 30-day all-cause mortality and the incidence of postoperative complications, and patient satisfaction with the overall surgical scar assessed via telephone follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using the independent Student's t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables are expressed as numbers (percentages) and were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher's exact test. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two-sided P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 41 patients were enrolled, including 16 in the FMS group and 25 in the LMS group. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were well balanced between groups. The mean age was 53.7 ± 12.0 years (FMS: 50.3 ± 14.4 years vs. LMS: 56.0 ± 9.9 years, P = 0.139). Female patients accounted for 19.5% overall (FMS: 18.8% vs. LMS: 20.0%, P = 0.922). The mean BMI was 25.9 ± 3.0 kg/m2 (FMS: 26.9 ± 2.9 kg/m2 vs. LMS: 25.3 ± 2.9 kg/m2, P = 0.108).

Comorbidity rates showed no significant differences: hypertension (41.5% total, 37.5% FMS vs. 44.0% LMS, P = 0.680), diabetes (4.9% total, 6.3% FMS vs. 4.0% LMS, P = 1.000), cerebrovascular disease history (2.4% total, 0.0% FMS vs. 4.0% LMS, P = 1.000), and CAD history (14.6% total, 18.8% FMS vs. 12.0% LMS, P = 0.886). No patients had COPD in either group. Cardiac function indices were comparable: mean LVEDD (54.7 ± 9.5 mm total, 56.3 ± 10.4 mm FMS vs. 53.8 ± 9.0 mm LMS, P = 0.420) and mean LVEF (64.0 ± 5.7% total, 62.8 ± 6.9% FMS vs. 64.8 ± 4.8% LMS, P = 0.266) (Table 1).

Intraoperative outcomes

There were no significant between-group differences in any intraoperative variable (all P > 0.05). The mean operation time was 248.9 ± 60.5 min (FMS: 264.9 ± 77.7 min vs. LMS: 238.6 ± 45.3 min, P = 0.177). Mean CPB time was 143.7 ± 36.5 min (FMS: 140.9 ± 27.7 min vs. LMS: 145.5 ± 41.6 min, P = 0.700), and mean cross-clamp time was 114.5 ± 29.2 min (FMS: 111.6 ± 23.1 min vs. LMS: 116.4 ± 32.9 min, P = 0.609).

No RBC or plasma transfusion was required in either group. The mean prosthetic valve size was 23.4 ± 2.4 mm (FMS: 23.4 ± 2.9 mm vs. LMS: 23.5 ± 2.0 mm, P = 0.893). Femoral artery cannulation rate was 7.3% total (0.0% FMS vs. 12.0% LMS, P = 0.410). Mean intraoperative bleeding volume was 589.6 ± 44.4 ml (FMS: 594.4 ± 25.0 ml vs. LMS: 586.6 ± 53.5 ml, P = 0.588), and postoperative 24-h drainage was 348.8 ± 198.1 ml (FMS: 345.0 ± 226.2 ml vs. LMS: 351.2 ± 182.8 ml, P = 0.924) (Table 2).

Early outcomes

No severe adverse events occurred in either group, and other early outcomes showed no significant differences. Mean postoperative stay was 7.5 ± 3.7 days (FMS: 7.6 ± 4.0 days vs. LMS: 7.5 ± 3.6 days, P = 0.972). Mean ICU stay was 2.0 ± 1.0 days (FMS: 2.2 ± 1.0 days vs. LMS: 1.9 ± 1.1 days, P = 0.366), and mean mechanical ventilation time was 12.6 ± 5.6 h (FMS: 12.6 ± 5.3 h vs. LMS: 12.5 ± 5.9 h, P = 0.954). No significant differences in incidence of complications: respiratory complications (9.8% total, 12.5% FMS vs. 8.0% LMS, P = 1.000), liver injury (9.8% total, 18.8% FMS vs. 4.0% LMS, P = 1.000), acute kidney injury (4.9% total, 6.3% FMS vs. 4.0% LMS, P = 1.000), and pericardial effusion (2.4% total, 6.3% FMS vs. 0.0% LMS, P = 0.820) (Table 3). Patient satisfaction with scar appearance was significantly higher in the LMS group than in the FMS group (92.0% vs. 37.5%, P = 0.001) (Table 3). During the 1-year postoperative follow-up, all patients in both groups survived, with no late mortality.

Discussion

Compared with FMS, LMS offers distinct clinical and patient-centred advantages in terms of cosmetic outcomes, surgical ease, and procedural safety. First, superior cosmetic outcome: The LMS incision (extending from the third intercostal space to 2 cm above the xiphoid process) avoids the visible upper chest region, enabling patients to wear V-neck garments or jewelry without visible scarring and substantially enhancing health-related quality of life, particularly in appearance-conscious individuals. In the present study, patient satisfaction with scar appearance was significantly higher in the LMS group than in the FMS group, supporting the cosmetic superiority of LMS. In addition, LMS preserves the relative integrity of the sternum, thereby facilitating accelerated postoperative recovery and contributing to improvements in patients' health-related quality of life. Abd Al Jawad and Mourad [14] reported that minimally invasive AVR approaches improve health-related quality of life. Notably, minithoracotomy yields significant improvements in physical functioning, social functioning, and general health perception. The smaller, concealed incisions and preserved sternal integrity further improve cosmetic outcomes, particularly valued by female patients. Second, LMS is easy to learn and perform: it aligns with the procedural logic and anatomical access of FMS, a technique most cardiac surgeons master. It can be performed using conventional surgical instruments without requiring minimally invasive dedicated devices or robotic platforms, making it widely accessible in most clinical centers. Key steps (sternal splitting, pericardial sutures, vascular cannulation) mirror FMS, shortening the learning curve; surgeons experienced in traditional cardiac surgery can master LMS with minimal extra training, aiding its widespread adoption [15]. Third, more effective cardiac deairing: In minimally invasive AVR, while the overall stroke risk remains extremely low, inadequate cardiac deairing has been associated with a higher incidence of asymptomatic brain injury [16]. The lower incision optimizes access to the ascending aorta and left heart chambers, letting surgeons fully remove residual air from the left ventricle, aortic root, and coronary arteries before stopping CPB—reducing air embolism risk. Fourth, facilitated emergency cardiac compression: the lower sternal incision provides direct access to the anterior surface of the ventricles – unlike upper median sternotomy, which is limited by the intact lower sternum – thereby enabling rapid compression in cases of intraoperative cardiac arrest or severe haemodynamic instability. Finally, easier temporary cardiac pacing lead placement: The lower incision enables easy access to the ventricular surface (especially the right ventricle), simplifying lead placement.

This study further validates the clinical applicability of LMS by comparing its perioperative outcomes with those of conventional FMS. Core findings confirmed well-balanced baseline characteristics (age, comorbidities, cardiac function indices) between groups. Intraoperative and early postoperative outcomes were comparable across groups with no statistically significant differences in operation time, CPB duration, bleeding volume, hospital stay, complication rates, or mortality. Both techniques achieved 30-day mortality of zero and no severe adverse events such as sternal wound infection or rethoracotomy. These results suggest that LMS and traditional thoracotomy are comparable in terms of perioperative efficiency and early safety for AVR. Consistent with our findings, Xie et al. reported that partial upper sternotomy is a safe and effective approach for isolated AVR in obese patients [17]. Compared with conventional full sternotomy, it reduced postoperative mediastinal drainage volume, blood transfusion requirements, and ICU stay, despite similar CPB and total operative times; no in-hospital deaths occurred in either group.

The 1-year all-cause survival rate of 100% in both groups further validates the long-term safety of LMS for AVR, consistent with previous studies on minimally invasive sternotomy approaches [6], [9]. Hlavicka et al. reported that partial upper sternotomy for AVR yields similar mid-term outcomes (including 3-year mortality, stroke incidence, and rehospitalization rate) to full sternotomy, despite longer cross-clamp and bypass times, while offering the advantage of reduced postoperative bleeding [7]. Notably, the absence of late mortality in the LMS group, despite its minimally invasive nature, confirms that reduced surgical trauma does not compromise long-term survival.

The consistency of baseline variables between the two groups (all P > 0.05) is a critical prerequisite for ensuring the reliability of comparative conclusions. Age, gender ratio, BMI, and comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease) showed no significant differences, indicating that patient selection bias was minimized. Cardiac function indices (LVEDD, LVEF) were also comparable, excluding the interference of preoperative cardiac function on surgical outcomes. Intraoperative efficiency metrics are key to evaluating surgical feasibility. Although LMS group had a numerically shorter operation time (238.6 ± 45.3 min vs. 264.9 ± 77.7 min), the difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.177), which may be attributed to the learning curve of minimally invasive techniques or the small sample size. Notably, CPB duration and cross-clamp time—two core indicators reflecting myocardial protection and surgical difficulty—were almost identical between the two groups.

In terms of perioperative blood management, neither group required red blood cell or plasma transfusion, and intraoperative bleeding volume and postoperative 24-h drainage were comparable (P = 0.588 and P = 0.924). This contradicts the traditional assumption that “minimally invasive surgery reduces bleeding” [2], but may reflect advancements in hemostatic techniques (e.g., improved suture materials, intraoperative hemostasis protocols) that have eliminated bleeding differences between approaches.

Additionally, the consistency of prosthetic valve size (P = 0.893) indicates that LMS does not restrict valve selection, supporting its clinical applicability across diverse patient anatomies. The higher femoral artery cannulation rate in LMS group (12.0% vs. 0.0%) was non-significant (P = 0.410), which is a routine adjunct to minimally invasive access and does not increase vascular complication risk [2], [3], [18], [19], [20].

Early outcomes are the primary concern for clinical adoption of new surgical techniques. This study showed zero 30-day mortality and no severe adverse events (e.g., sternal wound infection, cerebrovascular accident, low cardiac output syndrome) in either group, confirming the high safety profile of both approaches. Secondary outcomes, including postoperative hospital stay (7.5 ± 3.6 vs. 7.6 ± 4.0 days, P = 0.972), ICU stay (1.9 ± 1.1 vs. 2.2 ± 1.0 days, P = 0.366), and mechanical ventilation time (12.5 ± 5.9 vs. 12.6 ± 5.3 h, P = 0.954), were similar between groups. While theoretical advantages of LMS (e.g., accelerated recovery) were not observed here, this may be due to the short follow-up window or small sample size masking subtle differences.

The incidence of minor complications (respiratory complications: 8.0% vs. 12.5%, P = 1.000; AKI: 4.0% vs. 6.3%, P = 1.000; liver injury: 4.0% vs. 18.8%, P = 1.000) also showed no significant differences. Notably, the numerically lower liver injury rate in LMS group may reflect reduced surgical trauma and systemic inflammatory response, but this trend requires validation in larger cohorts. Overall, these results support the conclusion that LMS is non-inferior to traditional thoracotomy in early postoperative safety.

A systematic review of 14 randomized controlled trials (1395 participants) comparing limited sternotomy versus FMS for AVR showed no significant differences in key outcomes including mortality, deep sternal wound infection, re-exploration rate, and postoperative atrial fibrillation [2]. Furthermore, limited sternotomy may slightly prolong CPB time and aortic cross-clamp time, and significantly reduces postoperative blood loss. No differences were noted in pain scores or quality of life; however, limited sternotomy is associated with higher index admission costs and poor cost-effectiveness.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, surgical approach selection was based on anatomical suitability, and the retrospective design introduces potential selection bias. The small sample size limits statistical power. Large-scale prospective studies are therefore warranted to validate these findings. Second, follow-up was restricted to early outcomes; long-term data on valve durability, late mortality, and chronic complications are lacking. Finally, unlike Abd Al-Jawad and Mourad [14], who reported that both minimally invasive incisions improved quality of life (with minithoracotomy superior to ministernotomy in physical, role-related, and social functioning among 189 patients followed for ≥1 year), our study did not assess patient-reported outcomes such as postoperative pain or quality of life.

In conclusion, LMS is a safe and feasible alternative to conventional FMS for isolated AVR. It achieves comparable intraoperative efficiency and early postoperative safety, with no significant differences in key outcomes such as operation time, hospital stay, or complication rates. Beyond equivalent clinical performance, LMS offers distinct advantages, including superior cosmetic outcomes, preserved sternal integrity, a short learning curve, more effective cardiac deairing, and simplified emergency interventions and pacing lead placement. Therefore, LMS represents a valuable minimally invasive option for anatomically suitable patients with isolated aortic valve disease.
