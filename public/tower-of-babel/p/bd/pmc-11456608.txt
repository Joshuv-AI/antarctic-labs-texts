Motion capture and AR based programming by demonstration for industrial robots using handheld teaching device
Authors: Guoliang Liu, Wenlei Sun, Pinwen Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the industrial robots field, efficient and convenient programming methods have been a hot research topic. In recent years, immersive simulation technology has been developing rapidly in many fields, which provides new horizons for the development of industrial robots. This paper presents a HTC VIVE laser scan motion capture and Holohens augmented reality (AR) based interactive Programming by Demonstration (PbD) system for industrial robot. A portable Handheld Teaching Device (HTD) and its calibration algorithm are designed in the system. The portable HTD which is tracked by a laser motion capture system can be viewed as an AR robot end-effector to teach paths. Meanwhile, the AR robot can be simulated in real time during programing. In addition, the robot reproducing the operator’s actions at the same position in space is the focus of programming. So, Multi-system registration methods are proposed to determine the relationship between robot systems, motion capture systems and virtual robot systems. Meanwhile, a path planning algorithm is proposed to convert the captured raw path points into robot-executable code. For unskilled operators, they can easily perform complex programming using the HTD. For skilled senior workers, their skills can be quickly learned by robots using the system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73747-4.Keywords: Robots, Motion capture, AR, Programming by demonstrationSubject terms: Engineering, Mechanical engineering

Introduction

Robot technology continuously integrates new technologies and concepts to improve the flexibility and adaptability of robots. Industrial robots are widely used in hazardous and repetitive production tasks such as welding, handling, assembly, and polishing. Although robots have been widely used in the industrial field, they are difficult to flexibly apply in uncertain and unstructured environments1. At present, teaching programming and offline programming are commonly used methods for programming robots in factories. In the teaching programming mode, workers use a teach pendant to define the interpolation points of the end effector on the workpiece. Additionally, teaching programming becomes time-consuming when programming complex workpieces. In offline programming mode, commercial programming software plays a key role. Offline programming requires a higher level of skill and considerable experience. With the current manufacturing trend shifting towards small batches and customization, there is a growing need for more flexible and convenient programming methods.

Programming by Demonstration (PbD), also known as Learning from Demonstration (LfD), is a method where human skills are transferred to robots through operator demonstrations rather than complex pre-programming. Pbd offers a user-friendly interface for workers who lack professional programming and robotics skills2. Additionally, PbD is a promising robot application in both dynamic and unstructured environments3. PbD is generally categorized into three types: passive observation, teleoperation, and kinesthetic teaching4. Based on the logical hierarchy of learning information, PbD can further be divided into motion based demonstration learning and task based demonstration learning. In industrial robot programming, motion based demonstration learning is one of the primary approaches. Motion capture technologies and tracking methods have been key areas of research in PbD. The mainstream tracking technologies include laser scanning tracking, vision-based tracking, magnetic tracking, GPS tracking, inertial tracking, and hybrid tracking. In addition, to enhance the user experience, the combination of motion capture and augmented reality/virtual reality (VR/AR) has become a growing trend in designing PbD robotic systems.

In recent years, with the rapid advancements in motion capture technology and VR/AR technology, the animation and game industry have undergone revolutionary transformations. In animation, human actions and expressions are captured in the real world through the motion capture system, enabling virtual characters to replicate these movements and expressions with high accuracy. This approach enhances the realism and real-time performance of simulations. In VR games, for instance, players feel fully immersed in a virtual world, interacting with virtual characters in real-time through motion-capture-based interfaces. AR technology goes a step further by integrating virtual environments into the real world, offering a blended experience of both real and virtual settings. These advantages of VR/AR technology align well with the demands of advanced industrial robotics5,6. VR/AR devices are continuously being updated and iterated, and affordable high quality Head Mounted Display (HMD) is rapidly entering the market, such as the HTC VIVE, the Oculus Rift, the PlayStation VR, the Zeiss VR One, and Holohens. Many large companies are heavily investing in the development of competitive VR/AR equipment, including Samsung GearVR Innovator Edition, Google Cardboard and Gameface Mark IV. This indicates that immersive VR/AR equipped with motion capture system has become an important development trend and begun to change the way we work. In the industrial field, VR/AR has delivered outstanding results, such as assembly methods7, aircraft industry8, product design9–11, building design12, automobile industry13,14, and maintenance services15,16.

Motivated by advancements in VR/AR technology and motion capture technologies, many researchers have proposed various advanced VR/AR-based robot operating systems17–19. These VR/AR-based robot systems are developed using game engines rather than traditional commercial robot programming software. Compared to traditional commercial programming software like ABB’s Robot Studio, KUKA’s KUKA SIM, Fanuc’s Roboguide, and Motoman’s MotoSim, game development engines offer several advantages. First, most game development engines are open source and receive regular updates. In addition, a wide range of application plugins is readily available online. Second, the game engine can seamlessly interface with hardware devices from VR/AR devices to tracking systems, controllers, haptic systems and stereo cameras. As a result, game engines represent a growing trend in the development of advanced robotic systems.

One of the key principles of PbD is that the robot replicates the operator’s movements with high precision. In this paper, a laser motion capture and AR based interactive PbD system for industrial robot is proposed. The main contributions of this paper are as follows:

(1) A laser based motion-tracked HTD design method along with its calibration algorithm is proposed, HTDs with different profiles can be designed using this method and the position of the end reference point of the HTD can be calibrated. Operators can perform contact path planning on complex workpiece surfaces using the designed HTDs. The HTD can be used to program robots in a wide range of tasks such as welding, handling, assembly, and polishing.

Related work

Display and simulation technologies significantly influence the operational modes of robotic systems. Offline programming20–22 technique based on computer-aided simulation replaces the physical robot with a virtual robot to accomplish programming tasks23. However, since the virtual robot operates within a computer, there is a spatial separation between the virtual and physical robots. As a result, the workpieces need to be reconstructed in 3D before being loaded into the virtual offline programming system, and the generated offline programs often require complex adjustments and calibration before execution.

Immersive VR offers a more effective display method than traditional computer-based displays. For example, Bolano et al.24 propose a VR-based offline programming system where users can experience immersive simulations using VR headsets. VR technology also has significant advantages in robotic surgery training25. However, the virtual environment provided by VR remains separate from the physical world, making it difficult to seamlessly integrate virtual robots into real-world settings. Augmented reality (AR) has gained popularity among developers for its ability to overlay virtual robots onto physical environments, providing users with intuitive visual references. Additionally, to enhance human–robot interaction, motion-capture-based interfaces have been integrated into PbD systems.

Bolano et al.24 propose a VR-based offline robotic programming of robotic system in which users can experience immersive simulation using VR headset. VR technology also has significant advantages in robotic surgery training25. Additionally, to enhance human–robot interaction, motion capture-based interfaces have been introduced into the PbD system.

Inertial tracking is an important human–robot interface widely used in the PbD method. Pan et al.26. propose an AR-based robot teleoperation system combining depth cameras and inertial sensors. The operators can specify a path from RGB_D images via a mouse and teach the robot posture using an attitude teaching device designed with inertial sensors. A robot PbD method based on human upper limb tracking is proposed in27, where three wearable IMU sensors track the operator’s upper limb movements. The robot then reproduces the human’s demonstrated movements using the specific inertial-two-segment procedure. This method has great advantages in picking and placing application where a high level of accuracy is not necessary.

Haptic feedback serves as an intuitive motion capture interface that is often used in PbD robotic systems. Du28 introduces a novel approach of sensory manipulation for induced human adaptation in delayed teleoperation. Ueda29 design a surgical manipulation robot with haptic feedback to improve patient safety in robotic surgery. Ni et al.30. propose a haptic and visual AR interface for remotely programming welding robots, where the operator uses a haptic input device to define the welding path based on the 3D model of the workpiece reconstructed by a depth camera. However, when haptic-based control method is applied to precise path planning in complex robotic tasks, a major challenge lies in the high level of operator experience required.

Gesture control is a human–robot interaction control method in which sensors are used to recognize human gesture for control a robot. Cruz31 proposes a gesture recognition system using electromyography signals (EMGs) and inertial measurement unit signals (IMUs) to control a robotic platform, achieving fast and accurate responses. Chen32 develop a mobile robot control system based on gesture recognition using a LeapMotion sensor. The robustness and stability of mobile robot motion is effectively improved using the proposed scheme. A remote robot control method using hand tracking is proposed in33, where 3D anatomical positions and orientations obtained from Kinect-based hand tracking algorithm are used to control a remote robot, successfully applied in pick-and-place tasks. Gesture-based PbD robot systems allow efficient robot control through human gestures using depth cameras. However, it is difficult to generate accurate robot path when processing a complex workpiece surface.

Optical motion capture system, based on computer vision principles, uses multiple high-speed cameras to monitor and track target feature points from different angles. Optical capture based HTD is the dominant design scheme for accurate path planning in PbD systems. Optical capture systems are generally categorized into active36 and passive34,37,38 motion capture systems based on marker illumination technology. For instance, Zaeh et al.34. present an intuitive and efficient industrial robot programming system using projection-based AR. The system is equipped with a 3D-laser projector, triangulation sensor, tracking sensor and handheld pointing device (or HTD). The motion capture system is composed of tracking sensors (two infrared cameras) and a handheld pointer (a handheld pointer with retro-reflective marks). The user can virtually draw the desired motion paths for processing a workpiece surface. However, projection-based AR is limited to displaying trajectories on a workpiece and does not virtual the robot or its trajectories in space. Fang et al.35. present a method for planning the end-effector posture of an industrial robot. The inter-action device attached with a marker cube is used to guide the virtual robot to facilitate the demonstration of a path based on the application of AR. Since the camera is fixed, the AR robot can only be shown on the display from the fixed camera viewpoint, which makes operation and observation inconvenient. Ferreira et al.36. design a novel system for efficient and intuitive industrial robot reprogramming. The HTD is a luminous marker built with high-intensity LEDs, which are tracked by a set of industrial cameras. But the absence of an AR robot as a reference prevents the user from visualizing programming effects during operation. Chen et al.37 propose a virtual-physical collision detection interface for AR-based interactive teaching robot system. The tracking system consists of five infrared cameras and an HTD attached with markers, enabling users to control the virtual robot using the HTD. However, the effectiveness of the system is verified by the fact that the virtual robot can accomplish the specified tasks and is not verified on a real robot. To apply the PbD method to real production lines, industrial robots need to reproduce the HTD-based demonstration movements in space with the same position and attitude. So, multi-system registration methods and path planning algorithms need to be addressed to enable the robot to replicates the operator’s movements with high precision.

Unlike the optical tracking principle, laser motion capture technology obtains the 3D coordinate information of an object by calculating the angular difference between the two beams of light from the base station reaching the localized object. Recent advancements in laser scanning tracking sensors, such as the HTC VIVE, have significantly reduced the cost of VR devices, making them accessible for home use. Chu et al.38 propose a practical human–robot collaboration (HRC) system that enables humans to collaborate with machines complementary to each other. In this system, an HTC VIVE tracker is mounted on each glove to capture the user’s hand gesture and position in the real environment. Wang et al.39 propose a AR human–robot collaborative welding system, allowing users and robots to collaborate with each other to complete welding tasks. Users can also remotely control the robot’s movements along the weld seam using a motion-tracked handle. The system performs excellent in real-time teleoperation. However, since the reference coordinate system ( in Fig. 4) of the HTC VIVE motion-tracked handle is internal to the device, the motion capture devices designed by38 and39 use an internal reference coordinate system, which is suitable for teleoperation but are less suited for path planning on the surface of the workpiece.

Calibration principle of the HTD.

In this paper, we aim to develop a flexible and efficient PbD robot system. A laser based motion-tracked HTD design method along with its calibration algorithm is proposed. This method allows for the design of handheld HTDs with various shapes tailored to different robotic task scenarios, shown in Fig. 3. The proposed calibration algorithm can calibrate the end of the laser-tracked HTD so that the HTD can be used for contact continuous 3D measurement on the object surface. The designed HTD is used to build a human–computer interactive programming AR interface in which the AR robot simulates HTD-based path planning on complex workpiece surfaces in real time. To enable practical application of the system, it is essential for the physical robot needs to repeat the HTD-based demonstrates movements at the same position in space. Thus, a multi-system registration approach is proposed to solve the above problem. The raw data extracted by the system is difficult to be directly executed by the robot due to the different habits of the operators and the noise of the motion capture system, so a path planning algorithm is proposed to convert the extracted raw data into the executable code of the robot. This system allows unskilled operators to complete complex path planning tasks with ease using the HTD, while enabling skilled technicians’ skills to be transferred to the robot effectively.

In this paper, we present the system as a case study in welding programming and writing experiments. The following section details the principles of the motion capture and AR based system.

Principles

To accurately and efficiently translate demonstrators’ movements into executable programs for robots, the HTC VIVE devices are adopted to develop the PbD robot system. The system is fundamentally composed of two environmental modules and three functional units. As shown in Fig. 2, the two environment modules consist of a real physical environment and a unity virtual environment. The three functional units include a path planning unit, a registration unit, and a virtual robot drive unit. The overall framework and the interrelationships between each module are depicted in Fig. 2.

Physical environment

In the physical environment, the base station (lighthouse) emits infrared lasers to scan the robot’s workspace. The HTD, equipped with multiple laser receiving sensors, can be tracked in real time by the base station. Consequently, the position and orientation of the HTD in three-dimensional space can be tracked by the base station. The base station and the HTD communicate wirelessly, allowing their position and orientation data to be transmitted to a computer. The communication flow between these subsystems is shown in Fig. 9.

Unity virtual environment

The virtual environment is developed using the Unity 3D game development engine. The HTC VIVE tracking system integrates with Unity via the Steam VR interface plugin. In the virtual environment. The position and orientation of the virtual HTD are continuously updated in real time using the tracking data from the motion capture system in the physical environment. The position and orientation of the virtual HTD can be converted into signals to drive the virtual robot via the robot drive unit. The virtual environment is mapped to the real environment through Holohens so that it can assist the operator to program robots.

Registration unit

The registration unit is responsible for establishing the interrelationships between the subsystems to ensure seamless operation of the entire system. A detailed registration is provided in Sect. “Multi system registration”. Essentially, the registration unit maps the virtual environment to the physical environment in the spatial dimension. The proposed multi-system approach establishes the relationship between the motion capture system and the robot system. Vuforia registers the virtual environment to the physical environment, creating a mixed reality display in the HoloLens glasses.

Virtual robot drive unit

As analyzed above, the operator can manipulate the virtual HTD by manipulating the physical HTD. The endpoint of HTD is mapped to the tool centre point (TCP) of the robot’s end effector. In this way, the position and orientation of the robot end effector are obtained in real time. The position and orientation information of the robot end effector is converted into the robot’s joint angle using inverse kinematics. Through forward kinematics, these joint angles are used to drive the robot, ensuring that the robot’s end effector aligns with the HTD endpoint. The robot’s movement can be displayed in real time on the Holohens glasses.

Path planning unit

To facilitate efficient path planning, the HTD is designed to function as the end effector of the industrial robot. The detail design method of the HTD is show in following section. Operators can conveniently use the HTD to control virtual robots and plan paths with the assistance of augmented reality visual feedback. The proposed path planning algorithm converts the raw data into robot executable code.

HTD design

The proposed HTD design method allows for the design of HTDs with different shapes, as shown in Fig. 3. This section analyzes the method using Fig. 3a. Robots are widely used in advanced factories, but complex programming operations pose significant challenges in the field of industrial robotics. To address this, a tool mapping method is employed to ensure that the designed HTD can function as a robot end-effector, enabling rapid path teaching on the workpiece. As shown in Fig. 3, the handheld teaching device centre point (HTDCP) map the tool centre point (TCP) of the robot end effector. To create the HTD, 3D printing technology is utilized to convert an HTC VIVE wireless game controller into a teaching device. The HTD primarily consists of three components: a wireless communication game handle controller (Fig. 3(1)), a 3D printing connection part (Fig. 3(2)), and a teaching pen (Fig. 3(3)). The objective of using the HTD for path planning is to obtain the position and orientation of the HTDCP. To achieve precise measurement of the HTDCP’s position and orientation, two two key issues need to be addressed. First, selecting a stable and accurate motion capture system for tracking the HTD is essential. Second, designing a calibration method specifically suited for the HTD is necessary.

HTD calibration

The HTC VIVE motion capture system has a high degree of positioning accuracy, reaching the millimeter level, and under good conditions, achieving sub-millimeter accuracy43. However, the reference coordinate system ( in Fig. 4) of the HTD is located inside the device itself, which makes it impossible for the HTD to directly locate a point on the surface of the workpiece or to plan a path. To address this issue, we propose a four-point calibration method to calibrate the end position of the designed HTD. The HTC VIVE motion capture system which uses two base stations to track the HTD in real time is selected. From the perspective of tracking principle, this system falls under the active laser scan motion capture technology. The laser base station scans the robot workspace by emitting horizontal and vertical infrared lasers through a galvanometer. The HTD is equipped with 24 infrared receiving sensors placed at different positions on its head. This configuration ensures that the HTD can be scanned and accurately positioned, even when the operator holds it in various positions and postures. Regular path planning

Regular paths (e.g., straight lines, circular arcs) are more common in robotic scenario. The robot has corresponding motion commands, such as MoveL for straight lines and MoveC for circular arcs. Linear programming is taken an example to illustrate the efficiency of the system. With the assistance of AR robot simulation in Holohens glasses, the operator uses the HTD to teach the virtual robot key points in the robot workspace. The planned path connects these key points via straight lines. Each key point () contains information about the position and orientation of the robot’s end-effector.

To execute the interpolated point sets () by the robot, two tasks are required: first, the interpolated point sets need to be converted into a program that robot can execute; second, the program is transferred to the robot for execution via the communication interface. The communication between the robot and Unity is established through a Socket41,42, as shown in Fig. 9. The UR robot’s control cabinet and the Unity areconnected via a middleware industrial router. The Socket server is pre-configured in the control cabinet by the UR robot manufacturer. The Unity client communicates with the robot’s server through port 30003, which is specifically dedicated to transferring URScripts. URScripts is a scripting language commonly used for programming UR robots. The MoveL instruction is employed to sequentially processes the interpolated point sets () and generate URScripts. The UR robot starts executing the planned path as soon as it receives the URScripts program.

Experimental methods

The registration of the system is completed by a professional using the proposed multi-system registration method before the experiment. During the path planning phase, the physical robot remains in a shutdown state to ensure safety. The operator uses the HTD to control the virtual AR robot, as shown in Fig. 10. The operator can visualize the AR virtual robot and the planned path via the Holohens glasses. Once the path is planned, the operator steps away from the robot workspace. Error verification

Efficiency and accuracy are two important properties in robot programming. In this study, the proposed accuracy of the motion capture system is millimeter level, and under good conditions, it can achieve sub-millimeter accuracy43. To evaluate the system’s performance, the trajectory (as shown in Fig. 19c) executed by the robot is compared with the trajectory written by the operator. A total of 282 points are taken from the entire trajectory in Fig. 19c to calculate the mean error of distance between the actual trajectory executed by the robot and the trajectory written by the operator, as shown in Fig. 20. The mean error is 1.15 mm, with a maximum error is 3.61 mm. In terms of efficiency, the system can generate teaching interpolation points at 50 Hz. The experimental results show that the system has high accuracy and motion resolution. It is important to note that the system has two significant features. One is that the words written by the robot closely resemble those written by the operator, which lays the foundation for robots to learn other complex skills of people, such as welding skills, grinding skills, and painting skills. The other is that the words written by the robot and the words written by the human have a good spatial overlap, indicating that the robot end-effector can accurately replicate the demonstrated trajectory of the HTD in space. This feature makes the system advantageous in unstructured environments, as workpieces can be placed freely in the robot’s workspace without the need to position the workpieces. The operator uses the system to quickly complete path planning, generate programs, and download programs for execution.

Discussion

To demonstrate the advantages of our method, we conducted experiments and user studies to discuss the system from the experiences of professional operators and inexperienced volunteers, respectively.

For comparison, five operators who are familiar with the operation of the robot are invited to program the workpieces in Fig. 12 using the traditional teaching programming method and the proposed HTD method, respectively. The time statistics of the experiment from the beginning of the programming to the end of the execution of the prepared program by the robot. It takes an average time of 468 and 131 s to complete the programming operation using the traditional teaching programming and the proposed HTD method, respectively. In the traditional teaching programming method, the operator controls the TCP of the physical robot to move along the weld seam using a teach pendant. The traditional approach is time-consuming because the weld curve needs to be fitted with multiple interpolated points and each interpolated point needs to be adjusted using a teach pendant. Unlike traditional methods, the proposed method enables the use of HTDs as robot tools to directly teach paths, and the virtual robot can provide a simulation reference which makes it easy to adjust the robot’s pose and path.

A total of 30 volunteers, none of whom has experience in operating robots, are invited to participate in the user studies. The System Usability Scale (SUS) questionnaire is used to assess the usability of the proposed system. All volunteers have all received basic training before the experiment. Volunteers are requested to undertake two distinct tasks: the first task involves the programming the workpiece shown in Fig. 12; the second task entails the robot’s learning of writing skills through a demonstration of writing the word ‘Robot’ by the volunteers as shown in Fig. 19. The results of the experiment are shown in Fig. 21. The time statistics are recorded from the beginning of the operation of the HTD to the end of the robot’s execution of the prepared program. From the results, it can be observed that the mean time for volunteers to complete task 1 is 218 s, and the mean time to complete task 2 is 177.8 s. Results of user survey.

Overall, the proposed method has better flexibility than the traditional teaching programming mode. The system gives full leverages human initiative, allowing workers to quickly teach the robot welding paths using the HTD. It shows great potential in unstructured work environments, such as the programming of non-standardized and small batches of workpieces. Additionally, senior workers’ skills can be learned using the system, which lays the foundation for robots to tackle more complex tasks.

Conclusions

To make programming the robot convenient, a PbD system for industrial robot based on motion capture and AR is proposed. A portable HTD is designed in the system, allowing operators use it as a robot end-effector to quickly teach the welding path on workpieces. The proposed calibration algorithm enables the motion capture system to accurately output the position and orientation of the HTDCP. The proposed path planning methods are adapted to the designed HTD, allowing the system to efficiently program various types of weld seams, such as lap weld seams and complex curve weld seams. Meanwhile, the operator’s skills can be reproduced by robots using the system.

The system also has its limitations. The registration process requires using the robot’s TCP and the end of the HTD to separately measure three fixed marker points, which need be accomplished by a professional robot operator. In future research, we plan to simplify this process. Furthermore, we plan to incorporate artificial intelligence into our systems to enable robots to learn more complex skills.
