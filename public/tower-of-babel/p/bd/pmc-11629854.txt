The impact of age‐related differences in emotion dysregulation on refugee mental health and social outcomes
Authors: Yulisha Byrow, Angela Nickerson, Philippa Specker, Richard Bryant, Meaghan O'Donnell, Tadgh McMahon, Vicki Mau, Belinda Liddell
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe refugee experience is typically characterized by exposure to numerous premigration traumatic events and postmigration stress in the resettlement environment. Refugees’ experiences can lead to elevated rates of psychopathology, including posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and depression. Emotion regulation is a key mechanism contributing to mental health outcomes among refugees. This study examined the impact of age on the association between emotion regulation and critical social outcomes relevant to refugee resettlement, such as social engagement and functional impairment. Participants were 1,081 Arabic‐, Farsi‐, Tamil‐ and English‐speaking adult refugees. Premigration trauma exposure, postmigration stressors, PTSD symptoms, depressive symptoms, emotion regulation, social engagement, and functional impairment were measured. A series of hierarchical regression and Poisson regression analyses revealed emotion dysregulation as a significant predictor of functional impairment, β = .36, p < .001, and social engagement, Exp B = 0.99, p = .002. A significant interaction between age and emotion dysregulation was associated with both PTSD, β = .05, p = .048 and depressive symptoms, β = .06, p = .010, suggesting a stronger positive association between emotion dysregulation and both PTSD and depressive symptom severity for older individuals. Postmigration stressor exposure, emotion dysregulation, and older age are important factors that may negatively impact social engagement and functional impairment in the resettlement environment. Additionally, higher levels of trauma exposure may negatively impact social engagement. These findings have implications for public health and social services in the context of resettled refugee communities.

In 2022, there were 103,000,000 people forcibly displaced as a result of persecution, violence, or conflict; the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) estimates that 29% of these individuals are either refugees or asylum seekers (UNHCR, 2022). Refugees are exposed to numerous potentially traumatic events (PTEs) due to exposure to conflict and persecution in their home countries or while fleeing their home countries, such as a lack of food or water, forced separation from family members, and torture. Once these individuals have reached relative safety in a host country, they face numerous postmigration stressors, including unemployment and poverty, financial obligations to family back home, accessing an interpreter or language support, and having to frequently change their place of residence (Li et al., 2016). Research suggests that levels of psychopathology are elevated among refugees resettled in host countries (Blackmore et al., 2020; Hoell et al., 2021; Nickerson et al., 2019). Recent meta‐analytic findings suggest the prevalence rates of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and depression in refugees are approximately 30% and 30%–40%, respectively (Blackmore et al., 2020; Hoell et al., 2021). Premigration PTEs and postmigration stressors are important factors that contribute to the development and maintenance of psychopathology, including PTSD and depression (Byrow et al., 2022; Hynie, 2018; Liddell et al., 2019; Miller & Rasmussen, 2017; Nickerson et al., 2010, 2019; Rasmussen et al., 2010; Steel et al., 2009). Thus, each step of the refugee journey is associated with unique risks and consequences for mental health. The confluence of previous trauma exposure, current postmigration stress, and mental ill health can have a deleterious impact on an individual's ability to successfully adapt to the resettlement environment.

Researchers are becoming increasingly cognizant of key outcomes distinct from psychopathology, such as functional impairment and social engagement, that are relevant to adaptation in the resettlement environment (Schick et al., 2016). Social engagement is a key protective factor in recovery following trauma exposure (Chen et al., 2017; Kaniasty, 2012; Nickerson et al., 2022; Schick et al., 2016) and the prevention of the development and/or maintenance of severe depression in refugees (Byrow et al., 2022; Nickerson et al., 2019). A robust association exists between functional impairment, including impaired functioning at work (Kessler, 2000), and impaired psychosocial functioning (Pagotto et al., 2015) in nonrefugee samples of individuals with PTSD. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines functioning and disability as multidimensional constructs related to body functions (e.g., psychological function) and structures (e.g., organ or limb functioning), activities (e.g., execution of a task), participation (e.g., involvement in life), and environmental factors (e.g., social environment; WHO, 2013). Functional impairment has emerged as an important outcome that is associated with trauma and postmigration stress in refugees (Byrow et al., 2022; Minihan et al., 2018; Nickerson et al., 2019). Importantly, functional impairment has been shown to persist beyond psychological recovery in nonrefugee populations, including military samples (Murphy et al., 2016) and the general population (Bryant et al., 2016; Westphal et al., 2011). Thus, when considering refugee adaptation to the resettlement environment, it is essential to adopt a holistic view of relevant outcomes, such as daily functioning and socialization, in addition to mental health outcomes.

Emotion regulation has emerged as an important psychological mechanism that contributes to psychopathology in a diverse range of populations (Sheppes, 2020), including refugees (Doolan et al., 2017; Koch et al., 2020; Nickerson et al., 2015; Specker & Nickerson, 2019). Emotion regulation is defined as the process through which an individual monitors, evaluates, and modifies their emotional states and behavior in response to a particular situation (Gross, 1998). Koch et al. (2020) found that emotion regulation was associated with mental health symptoms (i.e., PTSD, depression, and anxiety/insomnia) and social impairment in a group of adolescent and adult refugees. Longitudinal research has shown that emotion dysregulation precedes increased PTSD symptom severity related to negative alterations in cognitions and mood (i.e., one of the four PTSD symptom clusters outlined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders [5th ed.; DSM‐5; American Psychiatric Association, 2013]) and a bidirectional association with increased hyperarousal symptoms (Specker et al., 2024). As such, several interventions that aim to treat PTSD in refugees target emotion dysregulation (e.g., Tissue et al., 2023; Koch et al., 2020).

There has been a relative neglect of the association between emotion dysregulation and social and functional outcomes. Further, there has been no focus on how these associations may alter with age. Several theories posit normative developmental changes in emotion regulation across the lifespan. The “selection, optimization, and compensation with emotion regulation” (SOC‐ER) model proposed by Urry and Gross (2010) suggests that changes in emotion regulation across the lifespan reflect changes in emotion regulation strategy use, which is dependent upon available resources (e.g., internal capacity, environmental influences). The “socioemotional selectivity theory” (SST), however, calls attention to age‐related changes in motivation, which drive changes in emotion regulation (Carstensen et al., 2003). Research examining age‐related differences in emotion regulation has produced mixed findings regarding the key emotion regulation strategies relevant to different life stages. A recent systematic review examining age‐related differences in emotion regulation strategy use highlighted the complexity of emotion regulation, its adaptability over time, and its context‐dependent nature as key factors that contribute to disparate findings across studies (Allen & Windsor, 2019). Within broader trauma‐affected populations, emotion regulation has been found to mediate the association between childhood trauma and psychopathology in adulthood (e.g., Choi & Oh, 2014; Cloitre et al., 2019). Pfluger and colleagues (2022), showed that adults older than 50 years of age who experienced complex trauma during childhood reported more use of maladaptive emotion regulation strategies (i.e., emotional suppression) and engaged less often with adaptive emotion regulation strategies (i.e., emotion reappraisal) compared with those who had not experienced complex trauma. These strategies were shown to mediate the association between childhood trauma and subsequent psychological disorders (e.g., depression, anxiety). Thus, it seems that the experience of cumulative or complex trauma—regardless of when it occurs— can have a long‐lasting negative impact on mental health, continuing into older adulthood. Importantly, the literature identifies emotion regulation as a key mechanism underpinning the association between trauma and psychopathology later in life (e.g., Cloitre et al., 2019).

Given the unique nature of the refugee experience, which is characterized by exposure to multiple traumatic events and ongoing postmigration stressors, it is imperative to consider the influence of age‐related differences in emotion regulation among refugees to better understand the potential mechanisms that may influence adaptation, including mental health, social outcomes, and functional impairment, in the resettlement environment. Understanding individual differences relevant to the impact of key psychological mechanisms on mental health and social outcomes is necessary to ensure interventions are ideally tailored to an individual's distinct needs. The aim of this study was to understand the association between emotion regulation and key psychological and social outcomes relevant to refugees in the resettlement environment. As such, our first study goal was to replicate previous findings showing that emotion dysregulation is a key contributor to PTSD and depressive symptom severity, controlling for trauma exposure and postmigration stressors (Koch et al., 2020; Nickerson et al., 2015; Specker et al., 2024). Second, investigated the association between emotion dysregulation and other outcomes relevant to the refugee context, such as functional impairment and social engagement. We predicted that emotion dysregulation would negatively impact social engagement and reinforce functional impairment in a sample of recently resettled refugees. Finally, we examined whether any age‐related differences impacted the association between emotion dysregulation and the outcomes of interest (i.e., PTSD symptom severity, depressive symptoms, functional impairment, and social engagement) in a refugee sample. We predicted that age would moderate the associations between emotion dysregulation and these outcomes.

METHOD

Participants

The sample included refugees and asylum seekers living in Australia (N = 1,085, n = 463 women, n = 615 men). Participants ranged in age from 18 to 77.14 years (M = 38.19 years, SD = 11.89). Most participants (68.2%) had an Arabic‐speaking background, followed by Farsi (17.2%), Tamil (5%), and English (9.6%). Participants included both permanent and temporary visa holders. Snowball sampling research procedures for engaging difficult‐to‐access populations were implemented (Sadler et al., 2010), and participants were recruited via in‐language advertising at relevant support service locations and organizations, community noticeboards and newspapers, and social media platforms. At the time of data collection, permanent visa holder status referred to refugees living in Australia who held a valid visa that allowed them to remain in Australia permanently. Temporary visa holder status allows an individual to remain in Australia temporarily.

Procedure

This study drew on data collected from the first wave of a longitudinal study, the Refugee Adjustment Study (RAS; Nickerson et al., 2019). The recruitment of 1,085 refugees and asylum seekers residing in Australia occurred between May 2015 and January 2018. From this sample, four participants were excluded due to study withdrawal or technical issues that compromised the validity of their survey responses. The final sample included 1,081 refugees or asylum seekers who were older than 18 years of age, had arrived in Australia after January 201, and had an Arabic‐, Farsi‐, Tamil‐, or English‐speaking background. Eligible participants provided informed consent before completing a survey, administered online or via pen and paper, in their preferred language. Participants were reimbursed with a $25 (AUD) voucher upon survey completion. All procedures were approved by the University of New South Wales Human Research Ethics Committee.

Measures

All measures were translated from English to either Arabic, Farsi, or Tamil using “gold standard” procedures for translating survey‐based measures (Bracken & Barona, 1991). These procedures include forward translation, blind back‐translation, and a process of reconciliation with the research team. The adaptation and translation of the measures included were conducted in consultation with community leaders from each language group and/or experts working with these cultural groups.

Premigration traumatic events

The Harvard Trauma Questionnaire (HTQ; Mollica et al., 1992) includes 16 items indexing traumatic events commonly reported by refugees (e.g., “murder of family or friend”). Participants indicated whether they had experienced, witnessed, or learned about the event happening. Scores were dichotomized to create a variable whereby each item endorsed as either experienced or witnessed was considered present. A total score was created for each participant by summing the traumatic events they had either witnessed or experienced.

Postmigration stressors

The Post‐migration Living Difficulties Questionnaire includes 36 items indexing commonly reported postmigration stressors experienced by refugees in the resettlement environment; the measure was adapted to suit the Australian context (e.g., “Difficulties accessing interpreter or language support,” “Difficulties accessing Medicare”). Participants rated whether they had experienced each stressor on a 5‐point scale ranging from 1 (was not a problem/did not happen) to 5 (a very serious problem), with higher scores indicating higher levels of postmigration stressors. In the present sample, this scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency, Cronbach's α = .95.

PTSD symptom severity

The Posttraumatic Diagnostic Scale for DSM‐IV (PDS‐IV; Foa et al., 1997) is a 16‐item measure assessing PTSD symptom severity. The DSM‐5 version of the PDS was not available when study recruitment began, thus four additional items were added to assess additional DSM‐5 symptoms. This adapted version of the PDS‐IV has been used in previous studies with refugees (e.g., Nickerson et al., 2019; Schick et al., 2016) Participants were asked to rate each item (e.g., “Having bad dreams or nightmares about the traumatic event”) on a 4‐point scale ranging from 1 (not at all or only one time) to 4 (5 or more times a week/almost always), with higher scores indicating more severe PTSD symptoms. In the present sample, the adapted PDS‐IV demonstrated excellent internal consistency, Cronbach's α = .96.

Depressive symptoms

The Patient Health Questionnaire (Kroenke et al., 2001) (DSM‐5) is a nine‐item measure indexing the severity of depressive symptoms. Participants rated each item (e.g., “Feeling down, depressed, or hopeless”) on a 4‐point scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). A mean score was created, with higher scores indicating higher levels of symptom severity. In the present sample, the PHQ‐9 demonstrated excellent internal consistency, Cronbach's α = .93.

Functional impairment

The WHO Disability Assessment Schedule 2.0 (WHODAS 2.0; Usten et al., 2010; WHO, 2012) is a 12‐item measure developed by the WHO to assess health and disability across six dimensions (i.e., cognition, mobility, self‐care, getting along, life activities, and participation). Participants were asked to rate the degree of difficulty associated with each item (e.g., “How much difficulty have you had with taking care of household responsibilities”) on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (none) to 5 (extreme/cannot do), with higher scores indicating more impairment. In the present sample, the WHODAS 2.0 demonstrated excellent internal consistency, Cronbach's α = .91.

Social engagement

A single item from the Short Social Capital Assessment Tool (SASCAT; De Silva et al., 2007) was used to index social engagement. The item used indexed the number of groups, from a total of nine group types, of which a participant was an active member (i.e., work‐related organizations, cultural community groups, women's groups, men's groups, political groups, religious groups, sports groups, student groups, volunteer/charity groups, refugees from your own community, refugees from other communities, nonrefugees from the Australian community). This single‐item index of social engagement was drawn from a validated, standardized measure that has previously been used in refugee samples (e.g., Nickerson et al., 2019). A total score was created by counting the number of groups for which participants reported membership, with higher scores indicating higher degrees of social engagement.

Emotion dysregulation

The Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS; Gratz & Roemer, 2004) was used to index overall levels of emotion dysregulation. The abridged 18‐item version of the original 36‐item self‐report questionnaire, which has been validated for use in refugee samples (e.g., Specker & Nickerson 2019), was used. Each item (e.g., “I am confused about how I feel,” “When I'm upset, I become out of control”) was rated on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (almost never) to 5 (almost always). Responses were summed to create a total score, with higher scores indicating higher levels of emotion dysregulation. In the present sample, the scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency, Cronbach's α = .94.

Data analysis

The data for the current study were drawn from a longitudinal study, the RAS, that has produced several published papers (e.g., Byrow et al., 2022; Nickerson et al., 2022, 2019; Specker et al., 2024). These previously published papers are distinct from the current publication as they examined different research questions, included different analyses, and used different data time points.

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (Version 28). Descriptive statistics were computed using means and standard deviations for continuous variables and counts and percentages for categorical variables. Linear regression analyses were conducted to examine predictors of PTSD, depressive symptoms, and functional impairment. Poisson log‐linear regression analysis was used to analyze count data associated with social engagement (i.e., the number of different categories of groups participants reported membership in). Predictors for all analyses were sex (male, female), visa status (permanent visa holder, temporary visa holder), PTEs, postmigration stressors, emotion dysregulation, age, and the Age x Emotion Dysregulation interaction. For linear regression analyses, predictors were entered using the “enter” stepwise method. Gender, visa status, PTEs, and postmigration stressors were entered in Step 1. Emotion dysregulation and age were entered in Step 2, and the interaction between age and emotion dysregulation was entered in Step 3. Age and emotion dysregulation variables were mean‐centered.

DISCUSSION

Emotion dysregulation was associated with PTSD and depressive symptom severity over and above the impact of postmigration stressors and PTEs. These findings are consistent with previous research demonstrating a significant and positive association between emotion dysregulation and both PTSD and depressive symptoms in refugee populations (Doolan et al., 2017; Koch et al., 2020; Nickerson et al., 2015; Specker & Nickerson, 2019; Specker et al., 2024) as well as other trauma‐affected populations (e.g., Ehring & Quack, 2010; O'Bryan et al., 2015).

For older individuals, relative to younger participants, there was a stronger positive association between emotion dysregulation and the severity of PTSD and depressive symptoms. A study examining the impact of age on emotion regulation ability and symptoms of depression found that older adults engaged in a smaller repertoire of emotion regulation strategies compared to middle‐aged and young adults, particularly strategies involving social support (Nolen‐Hoeksema & Aldao, 2011). The authors suggest that as individuals age, they tend to become more selective about from whom they seek support. This aligns with the SST proposed by Cartensen and colleagues (2003), which suggests that as individuals progress into the later stages of adulthood, they tend to reduce the number of people within their social network and focus more on optimizing emotionally meaningful social experiences. Previous research conducted with refugee samples supports the notion that social engagement is a critical protective factor associated with both mental health and positive adaptation in the resettlement environment (Nickerson et al., 2019). It is possible that older refugees who find themselves having to resettle in a new country following conflict and the disruption of existing key social and familial networks are less likely to engage in social support options than their younger counterparts. This deficit in social support may contribute to elevated symptoms of PTSD and depression. Taken together, these findings highlight that emotion regulation may be a key factor to address when treating refugees’ mental health, particularly with regard to PTSD and depressive symptoms. Further, the age of adult clients may be an important consideration when formulating treatment plans. Regarding social services, engaging refugees, particularly older adults, in programs that are culturally sensitive and encouraging appropriate social engagement relevant to an individual's life stage may be an important factor in bolstering mental health in the resettlement environment.

Higher degrees of PTE exposure, more postmigration stressors, higher levels of emotion dysregulation, and older age are all important factors that may negatively impact social engagement in the resettlement environment. According to the ADAPT model, which provides a framework for mental health and psychosocial intervention for conflict‐affected populations, repairing interpersonal and familial bonds and providing social support is critical to promoting healthy adjustment in the resettlement environment (Silove, 2013). Previous research conducted with refugee samples suggests that social isolation negatively impacts psychological adjustment (Byrow et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2017; Jayawickreme et al., 2017; Nickerson et al., 2019). It may be that older individuals who have experienced more PTEs, were exposed to higher levels of postmigration stress, and are more emotionally dysregulated are more vulnerable to becoming isolated members of society. The association could potentially be in the opposite direction whereby the destruction of key interpersonal networks and changing roles within a family network that is synonymous with a conflict‐affected population causes or exacerbates maladaptive emotion regulation. When considering cultural context, older participants in this study, who typically represented collectivist cultures, may have found the adjustment to a Western, individualistic society more challenging than younger individuals. Further longitudinal research is required to understand the complex association between emotion regulation capacities and social engagement tendencies of refugees in the resettlement environment. Building an evidence base to better understand the direction of the association between emotion regulation and social engagement is a logical next step. Addressing these research questions will be critical to informing evidence‐based interventions. Regarding social services, refugees who characteristically are socially disengaged may benefit from emotion regulation skills training in addition to engaging with culturally appropriate support networks to bolster social connections and adaptive resettlement.

Exposure to higher levels of postmigration stress, higher levels of emotion dysregulation, and older age were associated with higher levels of functional impairment. Unexpectedly, a secure visa status was associated with greater functional impairment. This finding suggests that compared to their counterparts with secure visa status, insecure visa holders are less impaired in terms of their participation in daily activities. Similar findings have highlighted the capability of insecure visa holders to adapt to and participate in the resettlement environment (Nickerson et al., 2019). Emotion dysregulation demonstrated the strongest unique contribution to the variance in functional impairment, suggesting that emotion regulation is an important psychological mechanism that may underpin levels of daily functioning in the resettlement environment. Previous research suggests emotion regulation capacities may contribute to functioning in other trauma‐exposed samples, including survivors of childhood sexual abuse (Cloitre et al., 2005, 2008), individuals exposed to terrorism (Malta et al., 2009), and inpatients with PTSD (Boyd et al., 2020). Due to experiences of conflict and persecution, refugees are at an increased risk of having a disability or mental health–related disability compared with other populations (Nickerson et al., 2019; Steel et al., 2005). The current findings identify emotion dysregulation as a key factor that may contribute to the high observed rates of disability in refugees. The current study did not specifically aim to recruit an elderly sample of refugees. Thus, these findings may not be observed in elderly people who have fewer cognitive resources, more postmigration stressors, and more social isolation. Further research is required to explore the association between emotion dysregulation and functional impairment in refugees, which is imperative to consider when developing interventions for refugees living with disability.

We note several limitations. First, these findings were drawn from a convenience sample of refugees residing in Australia rather than a representative sample, thus limiting the generalizability of the current findings to all resettled refugees in Australia. Second, due to the cross‐sectional research design, these findings highlight associations rather than causality. It is also possible that the significant associations reported here occur in the opposite direction or bidirectionally. For example, a lack of social engagement may exacerbate the dysregulation of emotions and postmigration stress. Longitudinal research examining changes in emotion regulation over time will allow for a better understanding of the associations among emotion dysregulation, age, and mental health consequences. Longitudinal research, such as a cross‐lagged panel analysis or random intercept cross‐lagged panel analysis, that utilizes data from the longitudinal RAS will assist in determining causality and temporal associations, thereby allowing researchers to disentangle whether the association between emotion dysregulation and social engagement is unidirectional or bidirectional. Third, social engagement was measured using a single item indexing the diversity of social group memberships. Future research may benefit from using a more comprehensive measure that also captures the quality of social relationships. Finally, data were collected using standardized self‐report measures of psychopathology rather than diagnostic interviews. This approach is common in difficult‐to‐access populations, such as refugee groups (e.g., Chen et al., 2017; Jayawickreme et al., 2017), and this methodology is reinforced by following the gold‐standard forward‐ and back‐translation procedures outlined by the WHO.

The findings from the current study highlight the complexity of the association between emotion dysregulation and mental health. Specifically, the findings suggest that the negative impact of emotion dysregulation on symptoms of depression and PTSD strengthens with age. Furthermore, emotion dysregulation is an important mechanism when considering social engagement and functional impairment, outcomes that are key to adaptive resettlement for forcibly displaced refugees.
