Preventive effects and mechanisms of yam exosome-like nanoparticles on acute liver injury
Authors: Yaqi Yao, Zhenna Xu, Haoran Ding, Yehan Zhu, Shen shen Yang, Shuo Wang, Simiao Fan, Jingwen Hu, Mengjiao Zhou, Hongfei Wu, Zihang Xu, Jingyao Xia, Yubo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute liver injury (ALI) represented a significant clinical challenge due to its high mortality rate and the absence of specific therapeutic interventions. Plant-derived exosome-like nanoparticles (ELNs) had recently garnered increasing attention in the field of disease prevention and treatment, owing to their favorable biocompatibility and potential for targeted delivery. In this study, we isolated and systematically characterized ELNs from the medicinal plant yam (Yam ELNs). Structural analysis revealed that Yam ELNs possess a homogeneous bilayer membrane structure with an average particle size of 214.7 nm and demonstrated considerable stability in gastrointestinal fluids. These nanoparticles were found to be enriched with lipids (primarily phosphatidylcholine, PC), functional proteins (including GPX1 and GSTP1), and microRNAs (such as the miR159 family). In vivo imaging and cellular uptake assays indicated that Yam ELNs were efficiently internalized by the liver and HepG2 cells. Using a carbon tetrachloride (CCl₄)-induced ALI model, we demonstrated that oral administration of Yam ELNs significantly reduced serum markers of liver injury (ALT and AST), ameliorated oxidative stress imbalance (as evidenced by decreased MDA and increased GSH levels), and attenuated histopathological damage in the liver. Furthermore, in vitro experiments confirmed that Yam ELNs modulate cellular oxidative stress through their cargo of endogenous functional RNA molecules, thereby exerting a prophylactic protective effect. Desorption electrospray ionization mass spectrometry imaging (DESI-MSI) visually demonstrated that Yam ELNs remodel the spatial distribution of glycerophospholipid metabolism and suppress lipid peroxidation and the propagation of inflammation. Targeted lipidomics analysis further revealed that disturbances in several core metabolic pathways, including glycerophospholipid metabolism and linoleic acid metabolism, were corrected by Yam ELNs. As a result, the homeostasis of key phospholipid species, such as PE, PI, and PS, was maintained, and the propagation of inflammatory responses was limited. Notably, Yam ELNs pretreatment significantly reduced serum levels of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and downregulated hepatic Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) expression, indicating preservation of intestinal barrier integrity and attenuation of endotoxin-triggered hepatic inflammation. Furthermore, an integrated correlation analysis based on gut microbiota 16S rRNA sequencing, fecal metabolomics, and untargeted serum metabolomics clarified the relationships between differential serum metabolites, fecal metabolites, and microbial community structure. Following treatment with Yam ELNs, modulation of gut microbiota composition was observed, marked by the enrichment of Lactobacillus spp. These microbial shifts correlated with concurrent improvements in both serum lipid profiles and fecal metabolite profiles. Collectively, these effects were associated with enhanced hepatoprotective efficacy, suggesting a gut-initiated and metabolism-mediated mechanism underlying the prophylactic protection against ALI. In summary, this study systematically establishes the substantial potential of Yam ELNs in the prevention and treatment of liver injury, offering a novel strategy for the development of natural vesicle-based liver-targeted therapies.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04321-5.

Introduction

As the primary site of metabolism and detoxification, the liver was particularly vulnerable to oxidative damage during xenobiotic processing, resulting in self-inflicted injury [1]. Acute liver injury (ALI), characterized by massive hepatocyte death and robust inflammatory infiltration, represents a critical pathological event that often progresses to severe chronic conditions, including fibrosis and cirrhosis [2–5]. Although drug development had advanced, conventional therapies for ALI frequently remained suboptimal, largely due to their single-target mechanisms and lack of tissue specificity. Therefore, exploring novel natural products with multi-targeting capabilities, which could concurrently address the complex pathogenesis of ALI, was increasingly recognized as imperative [6, 7].

Against this backdrop, plant-derived exosome-like nanoparticles (ELNs) had emerged as promising therapeutic candidates due to their bioactive cargo and favorable properties, including biocompatibility, gastrointestinal stability, and organ-targeting capability [8–10]. For example, ELNs derived from Houttuynia cordata were found to ameliorate colitis through NLRP3 inhibition, whereas ginger ELNs achieved a similar effect by modulating TGF-β1 [11–13]. In contrast, garlic and grape ELNs acted through the reshaping of the gut microbiota to improve metabolic disorders [14, 15]. A crucial advantage was their phospholipid bilayer structure, which encapsulated bioactive components. This facilitated targeted delivery to damaged tissues, thereby overcoming a key limitation of traditional herbal extracts [16].

Based on these premises, this study focused on Dioscorea opposita Thunb., also called Yam, a medicinal and edible plant whose rhizome was rich in mucopolysaccharides, polysaccharides, saponins, and minerals [17, 18], and had been reported to regulate gastrointestinal function [19], enhance immunity [20, 21], exert antioxidant [22, 23] and antitumor effects [24], and delay aging [25]. In traditional Chinese medicine, classical texts such as Essential Prescriptions from the Golden Cabinet reflected the principle that "the liver can transmit pathogens to the spleen, so treating the spleen preemptively is crucial." This highlighted the recognized physiological and pathological interconnection between the liver and spleen [26]. As established by modern studies, spleen deficiency was closely associated with gut microbiota dysbiosis, and splenic function was understood to be intrinsically linked to intestinal flora [27–30]. Additionally, the integrated microbiome-metabolomics analysis in this study revealed that alterations in gut microbiota structure drove changes in host serum metabolite profiles. We thus hypothesized that Chinese yam may improve spleen function by modulating the gut microbiota, and subsequently exerted hepatoprotective effects by regulating host metabolic homeostasis via the gut-liver axis.

As a natural carrier of bioactive components derived from yam, yam extract–derived exosome-like nanoparticles (Yam ELNs) exhibit considerable therapeutic potential. However, their hepatoprotective mechanisms, particularly their regulatory effects on the gut–liver axis, have not yet been systematically elucidated. To address this issue, the present study integrated multi-omics analyses, desorption electrospray ionization mass spectrometry imaging (DESI-MSI), and gut microbiota profiling to investigate the in vivo distribution of Yam ELNs, their metabolic regulatory networks, and their impact on gut microbial structure in an ALI model. The results demonstrated that ALI could be alleviated by Yam ELNs through a coordinated multi-target mode of action, involving antioxidant delivery, regulation of lipid metabolism, and modulation of the gut–liver axis. Together, these findings provide a mechanistic basis for the application of Yam ELNs in the prevention and treatment of liver diseases and support the development of naturally derived therapeutic strategies with multi-target regulatory capacity.

Extraction and characterization of Yam ELNs

Separation and purification

Fresh Chinese yam was washed, peeled, and ground to obtain a juice. This juice was first centrifuged at 8,500 r/min for 30 min to remove fibrous material, then at 15,000 r/min for 1 h to pellet large debris. The resulting supernatant was further centrifuged at 50,000 r/min for 2 h. The pellet from this step was collected and resuspended in 1 × PBS (phosphate-buffered saline). The suspension was then layered onto a discontinuous sucrose gradient (8%, 30%, 45%, and 60%) and centrifuged at 100,000 r/min for 2 h. The band collected from the 30–45% sucrose interface was diluted with an appropriate volume of 1 × PBS and centrifuged again under the same conditions to remove residual sucrose. Finally, the pellet was resuspended in 1 × PBS to obtain the purified product [8].

1 × PBS was prepared by the dilution of 10 × PBS stock solution. An 8% sucrose solution was prepared by accurately weighing 8 g of sucrose into a 100 mL volumetric flask, dissolving, and bringing to volume. Sucrose solutions at 30%, 45%, and 60% were prepared using the same procedure.

Characterization

The morphology of Yam ELNs was examined by transmission electron microscopy (JEM-1200EX). Samples were deposited onto copper grids, negatively stained with 3% uranyl acetate for 3 min, and air-dried prior to imaging. Meanwhile, the hydrodynamic diameter, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential of diluted Yam ELNs were determined in triplicate at 25 °C using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS3600 instrument, with results presented as mean values [31].

In vitro stability testing

The stability of Yam ELNs in the gastrointestinal tract was assessed by incubating equal volumes of the concentrate with simulated gastric (SGF, pH 1.2), intestinal (SIF, pH 7.4), and colonic fluids (SCF, pH 6.8) at room temperature. Aliquots were collected at 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 6 h. The structural integrity of Yam ELNs under these conditions was evaluated by measuring changes in particle size and zeta potential using dynamic light scattering (DLS) [32].

SGF was prepared by mixing 8.2 mL of diluted hydrochloric acid (9.5–10.5%) with 400 mL of water, followed by the addition of 5 g of pepsin. The mixture was then diluted with water to a final volume of 500 mL. SIF was prepared using a phosphate buffer system consisting of 20% (v/v) 0.1 mol/L potassium dihydrogen phosphate solution and 80% (v/v) 0.1 mol/L disodium hydrogen phosphate solution, to which 2% trypsin was added. SCF was prepared by combining equal volumes of 0.1 mol/L potassium dihydrogen phosphate solution and 0.1 mol/L disodium hydrogen phosphate solution.

Component analysis of Yam ELNs

Lipidomics analysis

A 200 μL aliquot of Yam ELNs was mixed with 800 μL of extraction solvent (dichloromethane/methanol, 3:1, v/v). The mixture was vortexed for 5 min and centrifuged at 11,000 r/min for 10 min. Subsequently, 560 μL of the dichloromethane layer was collected and evaporated to dryness under a nitrogen stream at room temperature. The dried residue was reconstituted in 160 μL of a solvent mixture (acetonitrile/isopropanol, 1:1, v/v), vortexed for 3 min, and centrifuged at 11,000 r/min for 5 min. A 120 μL aliquot of the resulting supernatant was subjected to lipidomics analysis using a Waters ACQUITY UPLC system coupled with a Xevo G2-XS Q-Tof mass spectrometer. Chromatographic separation was carried out on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) maintained at 45 °C. The injection volume was set at 1 μL, and the flow rate was 0.25 mL/min. Mobile phase A consisted of acetonitrile/water (6:4, v/v) containing 0.1% formic acid, while mobile phase B consisted of isopropanol/acetonitrile (9:1, v/v) containing 0.1% formic acid. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–1.5 min, A:B = 60:40; 1.5–14.5 min, linear gradient to A:B = 5:95; 14.5–16.5 min, maintained at A:B = 5:95; and 16.51–20 min, returned to the initial ratio. Mass spectrometric analysis was performed in positive electrospray ionization (ESI⁺) mode. The capillary voltage was set at 0.5 kV, with a desolvation temperature of 500 °C and a desolvation gas flow of 1000 L/h. The cone gas flow was 50 L/h, and the collision energy was set at 20–40 eV in segmented mode. The source temperature was 100 °C, the sample temperature was maintained at 4 °C, and the collision-induced dissociation voltage was 6 kV. Data were acquired over a mass range of 50–1200 m/z in MSE mode. Raw UPLC-Q-TOF/MS data were imported into Progenesis QI software for automated batch processing, including fragment ion extraction and structural annotation. Lipid species were identified by matching accurate mass (m/z), characteristic fragment ions, retention time (RT), and collision cross-section (CCS) values against the LipidMaps database, followed by their relative quantification.

Proteomics analysis

Yam ELNs samples were mixed with SDT lysis buffer at a volume ratio of 3:1 and thoroughly pipetted. The mixture was incubated in a boiling water bath for 3 min and then centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was collected as the protein extract. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit. For SDS-PAGE analysis, 20 μg of protein was mixed with 5 × loading buffer and heated at 100 °C for 5 min. Proteins were separated on a 12.5% SDS–PAGE gel at a constant current of 14 mA for 90 min. After electrophoresis, protein bands were visualized by Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 staining. Protein digestion was performed using the filter-aided sample preparation (FASP) method. Briefly, 200 μg of protein was dissolved in SDT buffer, denatured by heating, and loaded onto a 10 kDa ultrafiltration unit. Low-molecular-weight impurities were removed by repeated ultrafiltration with 8 M urea buffer, and cysteine residues were alkylated with iodoacetamide (IAA). The samples were then washed sequentially with UA buffer and 25 mM NH₄HCO₃ buffer. Trypsin (1.5 μg) was added, and enzymatic digestion was carried out overnight at 37 °C. The resulting peptides in the filtrate were desalted using a C18 solid-phase extraction column, vacuum-dried, and reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid prior to analysis. Proteomics analysis was performed using a Waters ACQUITY UPLC system coupled to a Xevo G2-XS Q-Tof mass spectrometer. Peptide separation was achieved on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) maintained at 45 °C. The injection volume was 5 μL, and the flow rate was set at 0.3 mL/min. Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water, whereas mobile phase B consisted of 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. The gradient elution was programmed as follows: 0–3 min, A:B = 92:8; 3–43 min, linear gradient to A:B = 74:26; 43–48 min, adjusted to A:B = 60:40; 48–50 min, rapidly adjusted to A:B = 10:90; and 50–60 min, maintained at A:B = 10:90. Mass spectrometric detection was conducted in ESI⁺ mode. The capillary voltage was set to 3.0 kV, with a desolvation temperature of 450 °C and a desolvation gas flow of 800 L/h. The cone gas flow was 50 L/h, and the collision energy was set at 20–40 eV. Data were acquired over a mass range of 300–1800 m/z, with an AGC target of 3 × 10⁶ and a maximum injection time of 10 ms. Dynamic exclusion was set to 40.0 s. Data were acquired in a full scan plus data-dependent MS/MS mode at a scan rate of 12 spectra per second, with 20 MS/MS spectra collected per cycle. Raw MS data were processed and analyzed using MaxQuant software (version 1.5.3.17) for protein identification and quantification. Identified proteins were subsequently subjected to bioinformatic analyses. Subcellular localization was predicted using CELLO software, and protein domains were annotated with InterProScan. Gene ontology (GO) functional annotation, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, and protein–protein interaction (PPI) network construction were performed based on the Blast2GO, KEGG, and STRING databases to characterize the associated biological processes, metabolic pathways, and signaling networks.

microRNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from Yam ELNs using TRIzol® reagent. RNA concentration and purity were determined using the Nanodrop ND-2000. After sample testing was approved, RNA libraries were constructed according to the kit instructions, followed by in-depth analysis using the Illumina sequencing platform.

Cell experiments

Cell culture and passaging

HepG2 cells were maintained in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator. The culture medium was refreshed regularly according to cell density. For subculturing, cells were dissociated with trypsin upon reaching approximately 80% confluence. The digestion was terminated by adding complete medium, and the cell suspension was centrifuged at 1000 r/min for 5 min. The pellet was then resuspended and seeded into new culture flasks at an appropriate density.

Establishment of cell injury models

To establish an oxidative stress model, HepG2 cells in the log-phase were seeded into 96-well plates and cultured for 24 h. After adhesion, the spent medium was replaced with fresh medium containing various concentrations of H₂O₂ (100–800 μM), with six replicate wells per concentration. A set of six wells served as a negative control. Following a 4-h incubation, cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay to determine the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) of H₂O₂. Concurrently, the levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), malondialdehyde (MDA), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and reduced glutathione (GSH) were measured to evaluate oxidative stress.

Cytotoxicity assay

The effect of Yam ELNs on HepG2 cell viability was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL and incubated overnight. Subsequently, they were treated with various concentrations of Yam ELNs (0–1000 μg/mL) for 24 h, with six replicates per group. After treatment, the medium was aspirated, and the cells were washed with PBS. Then, 100 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by incubation for 4 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader, and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) was calculated.

Cellular pharmacodynamic assay

HepG2 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 1 × 105 cells/mL and cultured for 24 h. The cells were then divided into four groups: blank control (complete medium), model (complete medium + H₂O₂), Yam ELNs pre-treatment (Yam ELNs + H₂O₂), and Yam ELNs-RNA (RNA + H₂O₂). After adhesion, the medium was replaced as follows: the blank control and model groups received fresh complete medium, while the Yam ELNs and Yam ELNs-RNA groups were supplemented with medium containing Yam ELNs (100–300 μg/mL) or equivalent RNA concentrations, respectively, for 24 h. Subsequently, all groups except the blank control were exposed to 600 μM H₂O₂ for 4 h to induce oxidative injury. Cell viability was then assessed by adding CCK-8 solution, incubating for 4 h, and measuring the absorbance at 450 nm.

In vitro uptake experiment

To evaluate cellular uptake, HepG2 cells were seeded on coverslips in 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well and cultured overnight. The cells were then incubated with DiR-labeled Yam ELNs for 1, 3, and 6 h. Following incubation, the cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and nuclei were stained with DAPI. The cellular internalization of Yam ELNs was visualized using confocal microscopy, and uptake efficiency was quantified by flow cytometry (BD Accuri® C6 Plus).

Animal studies

Model establishment and administration

Twenty-eight SPF male C57BL/6 mice (approximately 20 g) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (License No. SCXK (jing) 2021–0006) and raised at the Animal Center of Tianjin University of Traditional Chinese Medicine under controlled conditions (24 ± 2 °C, relative humidity 35 ± 5%) with free access to food and water. All mice were allowed to acclimate for 5 days before the experiment. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Tianjin University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Approval No. TCM-LAEC2023003).

After acclimation, the mice were randomly divided into four groups: blank control group, model group, positive drug group, and Yam ELNs administration group (n = 7 per group). Mice in the Yam ELNs group were administered with Yam ELNs at a dose of 200 mg/kg by oral gavage once daily for 14 consecutive days. Mice in the blank control group and model group were administered with the same volume of PBS, while mice in the positive drug group were administered with bifendate at a dose of 2.92 mg/kg by oral gavage. One hour after the final administration, ALI was induced in all groups except the blank control group by intraperitoneal injection of 10 mL/kg of 0.2% CCl₄ dissolved in olive oil. Mice in the blank control group were intraperitoneally injected with an equal volume of olive oil alone. After anesthesia, blood samples were collected from the abdominal aorta, allowed to clot at room temperature, and centrifuged to obtain serum. The serum samples were stored at -80 °C until further analysis. The livers were rapidly excised, rinsed with pre-cooled physiological saline, and blotted dry with filter paper. Portions of liver tissue were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for paraffin embedding and histopathological examination, while the remaining tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analyses.

Biodistribution of oral Yam ELNs

According to the kit instructions, DiR fluorescent dye was diluted tenfold with 1 × PBS to obtain a working solution at a final concentration of 100 μM. The DiR working solution was added to 10 mL of Yam ELNs suspension and incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min to allow fluorescent labeling. After incubation, the mixture was supplemented with 1 × PBS and centrifuged at 4,000 r/min for 1 h using a 100 kDa ultrafiltration unit to remove free dye. The labeled Yam ELNs were then resuspended in 1 × PBS for in vivo distribution studies. As a control for free dye, 1 × PBS without Yam ELNs was incubated with DiR dye and processed in the same manner to obtain a free dye solution. To assess the effect of structural integrity on Yam ELNs function, another 10 mL of Yam ELNs suspension was heat-disrupted in an 80 °C water bath for 45 min and then processed identically to generate DiR-labeled heat-disrupted Yam ELNs. Mice were randomly assigned to three groups: Yam ELNs-DiR, free dye (Free-DiR), and heat-disrupted Yam ELNs-DiR (Disrupted Yam ELNs-DiR). Each mouse was administered with 200 μL relative solutions by oral gavage. At 1, 2, 6, 8, 12, and 24 h post-administration, mice were anesthetized and imaged using an IVIS Spectrum CT small-animal in vivo imaging system. After imaging at each time point, mice were deeply anesthetized with 5% isoflurane and euthanized by cervical dislocation. Death was confirmed by the absence of respiration, heartbeat, and pupil reflexes. Heart, liver, spleen, kidney, stomach, and intestinal.et al. tissues were collected, and ex vivo imaging was performed using the same imaging system. All images included a standardized photon intensity scale for subsequent quantitative analysis.

Histopathological analysis of the liver

Mouse liver tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned consecutively. The sections were stained with H&E and examined under a light microscope to assess histopathological changes in liver tissues across different groups.

Detection of protein expression in liver tissues

Liver tissues were homogenized, and the homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 r/min for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected as the total protein solution, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit. Proteins were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to membranes via a wet transfer method. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 30 min, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted as follows: β-actin (1:3,000), HO-1 (1:1,000), GCLM (1:1,000), Nrf2 (1:1,000), and GCLC (1:1,000). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with secondary antibody (1:3,000) at room temperature for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using ECL substrate, and band intensities were quantified with β-actin as the internal reference.

Detection of inflammation-related gene expression

Total RNA was extracted from liver tissues using a TRIzol® kit and reverse-transcribed according to the instructions of the reverse transcription kit. The qPCR reaction mixture was prepared as follows: 7.5 μL of 2 × Universal Blue SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix, 1.5 μL of 2.5 μM gene primers (forward + reverse), 2.0 μL of cDNA, and 4.0 μL of nuclease-free water. qPCR amplification was performed on a real-time PCR instrument with GAPDH used as the internal reference. Primer sequences were as follows: GAPDH, forward 5’-CCTCGTCCCGTAGACAAAATG-3’, reverse 5’-TGAGGTCAATGAAGGGGTCGT-3’; TNF-α, forward 5’-CCCTCACACTCACAAACCACC-3’, reverse 5’-CTTTGAGATCCATGCCGTTG-3’; IL-1β, forward 5’-GTAATGAAAGACGGCACACCC-3’, reverse 5’-CAGGCTTGTGCTCTGCTTGTG-3’. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^ − ΔΔCt method, where ΔCt = Ct of target gene − Ct of reference gene, and ΔΔCt = ΔCt of experimental group − mean ΔCt of control group.

Biochemical marker assay

Serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), bilirubin, and prealbumin levels were measured in each group of mice using a fully automated biochemical analyzer. Liver tissue expression levels of superoxide dismutase (SOD), malondialdehyde (MDA), and glutathione (GSH) were determined. Specific experimental procedures were performed according to the kit instructions.

Serum metabolomics

80 μL of mouse serum obtained in Sect. "Model establishment and administration" was mixed with 240 μL of acetonitrile, vortexed for 1 min, and sonicated in an ice-water bath for 10 min. The mixture was then centrifuged at 13,000 r/min for 15 min at 4 °C, and 200 μL of the supernatant was transferred to injection vials for UPLC/Q-TOF–MS analysis. For quality control (QC) samples, 80 μL of serum from each sample in each group was pooled, vortexed, and processed in the same manner.

Metabolomics analysis was performed using a Waters ACQUITY UPLC system coupled to a Xevo G2-XS Q-Tof MS. Chromatographic separation was carried out on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) at 45 °C, with an injection volume of 5 μL and a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. Mobile phases were A, 0.1% formic acid in water, and B, 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. The gradient program was as follows: 0–0.5 min, A:B = 90:10; 0.5–2 min, linear to A:B = 45:55; 2–9 min, linear to A:B = 1:99; 9–10 min, held at A:B = 1:99; 10–10.5 min, rapidly adjusted to A:B = 99:1; 10.5–12 min, held at A:B = 99:1. Mass spectrometry was performed in dual ESI⁺/ESI⁻ mode, with a capillary voltage of ± 3.0 kV, cone gas flow 50 L/h, nebulizer pressure 310 kPa, cone voltage 40 V, collision energy 20–50 eV, collision-induced dissociation voltage 6 kV, desolvation gas temperature 325 °C, source temperature 120 °C, desolvation gas flow 600 L/h, and mass range 50–1500 m/z. Data were acquired in MSE mode.

Raw data were processed using Compound Discover 3.1 for deconvolution, peak extraction, and feature ion selection. The data were then denoised, missing values were filled, and normalization was applied to generate a two-dimensional matrix containing retention time, molecular weight, and peak intensity. The processed data were imported into SIMCA 14.1 for multivariate statistical analysis, including principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA). Model quality was assessed using R2X, R2Y, and Q2 values, and 200 permutation tests were performed to evaluate overfitting. Differential metabolites were identified based on variable importance in projection (VIP) values; metabolites with VIP > 1 and P < 0.05 were considered significantly altered and selected as potential biomarkers.

Spatial metabolomics

Spatial metabolomics analysis of liver tissues was performed using DESI-MSI. Mouse liver tissues were equilibrated for 30 min in a − 20 °C cryostat, embedded in Cryo-Gel, mounted on sample holders, and sectioned into continuous 20 μm slices. Sections were then placed on pre-cooled glass slides and air-dried at room temperature for 15 min. Fixed tissue sections were immediately used for mass spectrometry imaging.

Mass spectrometry was performed on a Waters Synapt XS HDMS Q-TOF instrument equipped with a DESI ion source. Positive ion mode scanned m/z 80–1500, while negative ion mode scanned m/z 50–1200. The capillary voltage was set at 0.5 kV, source temperature at 100 °C, and spatial resolution at 50 μm. The spray solvent consisted of methanol:water:formic acid (98:2:0.01) and was applied at a flow rate of 2 μL/min, covering the entire tissue section. Data acquisition and spectral preview were performed using Omni Spray 2-D 2.0.1 and MassLynx 4.2 software. Raw DESI-MSI data were imported into HDI software for image processing, converting the data into a matrix of mass, m/z, and signal intensity. The 1,000 strongest peaks were collected and normalized to the total ion current (TIC). m/z values were rounded to two decimal places and aligned across pixels to match metabolite features between samples. Peak intensities were normalized to TIC, generating a three-dimensional data matrix containing spatial coordinates, m/z, and normalized intensity. Normalized data were exported to Excel, and metabolite feature libraries were constructed by integrating matched m/z features and their intensities across groups. Preliminary metabolite identification was performed using HMDB. Multivariate statistical analyses, including PCA and OPLS-DA, were conducted using SIMCA 14.1. Metabolites with VIP > 1.0 and P < 0.05 were considered potential differential metabolites.

Targeted lipidomics

Liver samples were homogenized in 200 μL methanol and vortexed thoroughly. Then, 10 μL of internal standard mixture and 800 μL MTBE were added, followed by vortexing and ultrasonic extraction in a low-temperature water bath for 20 min. After standing at room temperature for 30 min, 200 μL water was added and mixed, and the samples were centrifuged at 14,000 r/min and 4 °C for 15 min. The upper organic phase was collected and evaporated under nitrogen, then reconstituted in 200 μL of 90% isopropanol/acetonitrile (1:1), vortexed, and centrifuged again at 14,000 r/min and 4 °C for 15 min. The supernatant was used for targeted lipidomics analysis.

Targeted lipidomics analysis was performed using an LC-30AD UPLC system coupled with an AB Sciex 7500 + QTRAP mass spectrometer. Chromatographic separation was conducted on C18 and Amino columns with an injection volume of 2 μL and a sample plate temperature of 10 °C. Samples were injected in random order, and QC samples were periodically included within the analytical sequence. For the C18 column, the temperature was set at 45 °C and flow rate at 0.35 mL/min. Mobile phase A consisted of acetonitrile/water (7:3) with 5 mM ammonium acetate, and mobile phase B was isopropanol. The gradient program was as follows: 0–5.0 min, A:B decreased linearly from 80:20 to 40:60; 5.0–13.0 min, A:B decreased linearly from 40:60 to 0:100; 13.1–17.0 min, A:B was held at 0:100; 17.1 min, the system returned to the initial ratio of A:B = 80:20 for equilibration. For the Amino column, the temperature was set at 40 °C and flow rate at 0.4 mL/min. Mobile phase A consisted of methanol/acetonitrile (1:1) with 2 mM ammonium acetate, and mobile phase B was acetonitrile/water (1:1) with 2 mM ammonium acetate. The gradient program was as follows: 0–3.0 min, A:B held at 97:3; 3.0–13.0 min, A:B decreased linearly from 97:3 to 0:100; 13.0–17.0 min, A:B held at 0:100; 17.1–22.0 min, A:B returned to 97:3 for equilibration. Mass spectrometry was performed in dual ESI⁺/ESI⁻ ionization mode. Source temperature was set at 400 °C, curtain gas at 35 psi, nebulizer gas at 50 psi, auxiliary heating gas at 55 psi, and spray voltage at + 3000 V/ − 2500 V. Data were acquired in MRM mode. Raw MRM data were processed using Sciex OS software for peak extraction, and targeted quantification was performed using internal standard correction. Peak area ratios of analytes to internal standards were calculated to determine compound concentrations.

Multivariate statistical analyses, including PCA and OPLS-DA, were conducted using the R package ropls. Model robustness was evaluated by seven-fold cross-validation and permutation testing. VIP values from the OPLS-DA model were used to assess variable contributions to group discrimination. Significant differences between two groups were analyzed using independent-sample t-tests, with P < 0.05 and VIP > 1 used to select differential metabolites. Pearson’s correlation analysis was applied to evaluate relationships between variables, and pathway enrichment analysis was performed using commercial databases.

Gut microbiota testing

Fresh mouse fecal samples were collected using the tail-lifting method, and total DNA from the fecal microbiota was extracted using a CTAB/SDS–magnetic bead method. DNA purity and concentration were assessed by electrophoresis and subsequently diluted to 1 ng/μL. The V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 341F/806R with KOD FX Neo polymerase. The PCR program consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 25 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 50 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 40 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min. Amplified products were quantified by electrophoresis, pooled in equal amounts, purified using column purification and gel extraction, and finally subjected to paired-end sequencing on the Illumina platform.

Fecal metabolomics

Fresh mouse fecal samples were freeze-dried and an appropriate amount was weighed and homogenized with five volumes of water. A 100 μL aliquot of the homogenate was mixed with 400 μL ice-cold acetonitrile, vortexed for 5 min, sonicated in an ice-water bath for 10 min, and centrifuged at 14,000 g at 4 °C for 20 min. The supernatant was collected and evaporated to dryness under nitrogen. The residue was reconstituted in 100 μL of 50% methanol, vortexed, centrifuged, and 40 μL of the supernatant was used for UPLC-Q/Orbitrap MS analysis. QC samples were prepared by pooling equal volumes from each fecal sample and processed alongside the experimental samples to monitor method stability and data quality.

Fecal metabolomics analysis was performed using a Thermo Fisher U3000 UHPLC system coupled to a Thermo Fisher Orbitrap MS. Chromatographic separation was carried out on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) at 35 °C. The flow rate was set to 0.3 mL/min, and the injection volume was 2 μL. Mobile phases were A: acetonitrile and B: 0.1% formic acid in water. The gradient program was as follows: 0–7 min, A:B = 5:95; 7–10 min, linear to A:B = 100:0; 10–10.1 min, returned to A:B = 5:95; 10.1–13 min, held at A:B = 5:95. MS analysis was conducted using a HESI source in both positive and negative ionization modes. The capillary voltage was set to + 3.0 kV/ − 2.8 kV, capillary temperature to 320 °C, and source temperature to 350 °C. Sheath gas, auxiliary gas, and sweep gas were set to 35, 10, and 50 L/h, respectively. Collision energies of 20, 40, and 60 eV were applied. The mass range was 100–1500 m/z, and data were acquired in Full-ms/dd-ms2 mode. QC samples were used to evaluate instrument precision, reproducibility, and sample stability.

Raw LC–MS data were processed using Compound Discoverer 3.1 for peak extraction, alignment, denoising, and normalization. A two-dimensional matrix containing retention time, mass, and peak area was generated. The data were imported into SIMCA 14.1 for PCA, PLS-DA, and OPLS-DA. Model quality was assessed using R2X, R2Y, and Q2, and reliability was confirmed by 200 permutation tests. Differential metabolites were selected using VIP > 1 and P < 0.05. Potential biomarkers were annotated with HMDB and confirmed using MS/MS fragments and literature. Metabolite trends were visualized with hierarchical clustering and heatmaps in MetaboAnalyst, followed by KEGG-based pathway enrichment and topology analysis to identify key metabolic pathways.

Statistical analysis

Statistical data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD). Data were analyzed using SPSS 22.0 or GraphPad Prism 9.0. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed by one-way ANOVA. For comparisons with homogeneous variances, the LSD post hoc test was applied. In cases of heterogeneous variances, Dunnett’s T3 test was employed. Comparisons between two groups were performed using independent-samples t-test. Correlations were assessed by Spearman rank correlation. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Results

Preparation and characterization of Yam ELNs

Yam ELNs were isolated from Chinese yam using ultracentrifugation combined with sucrose density gradient centrifugation, yielding 658 mg/kg. The target components were primarily enriched within the 30–45% sucrose concentration gradient range (excluding the bottom micrometer-sized precipitate). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that Yam ELNs were nanoscale particles ranging from 100 to 300 nm in size. The majority displayed the defining double-layered membrane of extracellular vesicles. Their morphology was predominantly the classic saucer‑shaped or concave hemispherical form, as shown in Fig. 1A. The Malvern particle size/potential analyzer detected the particle size of Yam ELNs to be approximately 214.7 ± 9.19 nm, with a well-symmetrical particle size distribution curve, as shown in Fig. 1B. Monitoring and analysis using a laser particle size analyzer revealed that Yam ELNs exhibited negative surface potentials ranging from −15 mV to −26 mV, as shown in Fig. 1C. To assess the storage stability of Yam ELNs, samples were stored at 4 °C and −80 °C for 15 days. Subsequent monitoring with a particle size/potential analyzer revealed no significant changes in particle size, as shown in Fig. 1D. Additionally, to determine the stability of these Yam ELNs in bodily fluids, we analyzed their particle size and potential changes when suspended in PBS and gastrointestinal simulant (Fig. 1E). Results showed that the particle size of Yam ELNs slightly decreased after treatment with gastric or intestinal fluids, but did not differ significantly and remained within the normal physiological range. The zeta potential of Yam ELNs was pH-dependent. In near-neutral environments (PBS and simulated small/colonic fluids), they maintained a stable negative charge (−14.2 mV and −13.3 mV, respectively). Conversely, under acidic conditions (simulated gastric fluid), a weak positive charge of 0.26 mV was recorded. This indicated that Yam ELNs were pH-responsive biological nanoparticles whose stability did not rely on electrostatic repulsion. In summary, Yam ELNs exhibited the standard morphological characteristics of exosomes and demonstrated high gastrointestinal stability, thereby providing a solid basis for subsequent studies of their in vivo biological functions.Fig. 1Identification and characterization of Yam ELNs. Analysis of the active ingredient profile of Yam ELNs

Lipidomics results revealed that Yam ELNs contained 1,275 lipid components, primarily glycerophospholipids (GP, 33.54%), fatty acids (FA, 16.53%), and glycerides (GL, 13.32%). Among glycerophospholipids, phosphatidylcholine (PC, 8.15%) constituted the highest proportion (Fig. 2A). These lipids not only form the structural foundation of ELNs but, more importantly, exhibit multiple biological functions including regulating lipid metabolism [33, 34], maintaining cell membrane stability [35] and exerting anti-inflammatory effects [36, 37]. Additionally, protein gel staining results indicated that the molecular weights of major proteins in Yam ELNs fall within the range of 25–37 kDa (Fig. 2B). A total of 91 proteins were identified through mass spectrometry analysis (Fig. 2C). GO functional annotation indicated that 29 protein components were associated with metabolic processes, while 2 exhibited antioxidant activity (Fig. 2D). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further revealed 4 proteins associated with lipid metabolism, including 2 involved in glutathione metabolism (Fig. 2E). These findings suggested that Yam ELNs regulated intracellular lipid metabolism via their specific protein components, particularly those involved in glutathione metabolism and antioxidant activity, thereby reducing oxidative stress.. RNA sequencing results revealed that Yam ELNs harbor multiple miRNAs, with 106 miRNAs exhibiting moderate to high expression levels (64 of which had expression values > 1000). The most highly expressed were atr-miR159 and ptc-miR159a, with the miR159 family accounting for 53% of total miRNA expression. In plants, miR159 was identified as a key mediator, which exerted anti-inflammatory and metabolic effects in mice by modulating gut microbiota composition [38]. These components were identified as the foundation for the potential biological activity of Yam ELNs. In vitro validation: efficient uptake of Yam ELNs by HepG2 cells

The bioactive components of natural nanoparticles primarily perform their biological functions intracellularly. To assess their uptake by liver cells, the internalization of Yam ELNs by hepatocytes was studied in vitro. Confocal microscopy was used to observe DiR-labeled Yam ELNs, with nuclei stained blue and the fluorescent probe shown in green. Most nuclei were surrounded by green fluorescence (Fig. 3D). The proportion of hepatocytes internalizing DiR-labeled Yam ELNs increased with longer incubation times, consistent with the observed mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) (Fig. 3D,H). These results indicated that Yam ELNs could be efficiently taken up by hepatocytes and could enter liver cells to exert their biological effects.

Activation of endogenous hepatic protective pathways by Yam ELNs

To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms of Yam ELNs’ hepatoprotective effects, relevant signaling pathways were examined. Attenuation of gut–liver axis inflammatory signaling by Yam ELNs

To further investigate whether the anti-inflammatory effects of Yam ELNs are associated with modulation of the gut-liver axis, we measured serum levels of lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a commonly used indicator of intestinal barrier integrity, together with hepatic expression of its cognate receptor, Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4). As shown in Fig. 5I, serum LPS levels were significantly elevated in the CCl4 model group compared with controls (p < 0.01), suggesting increased intestinal permeability and enhanced translocation of microbial-derived endotoxins. Notably, pretreatment with Yam ELNs markedly reduced serum LPS levels (p < 0.01 vs. model group). In parallel, hepatic TLR4 protein expression, which was significantly upregulated in the model group, was substantially attenuated in the Yam ELNs pretreatment group (Fig. 5H). These results indicate that Yam ELNs may help maintain intestinal barrier integrity and reduce portal endotoxin load, thereby limiting TLR4-mediated activation of hepatic inflammatory signaling. This gut-originated regulatory effect may contribute, at least in part, to the observed suppression of hepatic pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β) as described in Sect. Targeted lipidomics quantitatively validates key lipid alterations

To further validate the remodeling effects of Yam ELNs on hepatic lipid metabolism, high-throughput targeted lipidomics was performed. PCA analysis of the quantitative data showed clear separation among NG, MG, and DG groups, with replicate samples tightly clustered within each group. Seven-fold cross-validation indicated good model R2X and Q2 values. PLS-DA enhanced the discrimination of intergroup differences, and 200 permutation tests confirmed no overfitting, demonstrating robust model performance (Supplementary Fig. S2). After noise removal, OPLS-DA models showed distinct group separation and concentrated intragroup variation, with permutation tests confirming good model fit and predictive ability. Volcano plots highlighted upregulated (red), downregulated (blue), and non-significant (gray) lipids, with VIP values represented by point size (Fig. 8G–H). Differential lipids were selected based on VIP > 0, P < 0.05, and fold change > 1 or < 1. A total of 198 differential lipids were detected in NG vs MG, including 129 downregulated and 69 upregulated, while 70 differential lipids were detected in MG vs DG, including 27 downregulated and 43 upregulated; across all three groups, 263 differential lipids were identified. Hierarchical clustering of differential lipids showed good intragroup consistency and clear intergroup differences (Fig. 8I). Pathway enrichment analysis indicated significant involvement of glycerophospholipid metabolism and related pathways (Fig. 8J).

In MG, glycerophospholipid metabolism was markedly downregulated. Specifically, PS(18:0/20:4), identified as a key differential lipid in DESI-MSI results, was markedly decreased in MG (36,226.82 ± 12,360.77 ng/g vs NG 128276.08 ± 101,035.44 ng/g, P < 0.05), the regulatory trend was consistent with the DESI-MSI spatial distribution results, supporting cross-platform consistency for this representative lipid species. As a regulator of apoptosis signaling, membrane phospholipid asymmetry, and immune recognition, its reduction may be associated with enhanced hepatocyte apoptosis and inflammation, reflecting systemic disruption of membrane lipid homeostasis. Moreover, PI(16:0/22:6) was reduced to 4827.99 ± 588.97 ng/g, approximately 0.52-fold of NG (9308.74 ± 834.02 ng/g, P < 0.05). As a precursor for PI3K/Akt and other cell survival and metabolic signaling pathways, its reduction may limit signaling lipid availability in hepatocytes, impairing anti-apoptotic capacity and stress tolerance and aggravating ALI. PE(16:0/22:2) also decreased significantly in MG (181.77 ± 33.52 ng/g vs NG 616.47 ± 93.32 ng/g, ~ 0.30-fold, P < 0.05). As a key component for cellular and mitochondrial membrane stability and fluidity, its depletion indicated disrupted membrane lipid composition and energy homeostasis, potentially impairing mitochondrial function, promoting oxidative stress, and accelerating liver injury. The consistent downregulation of PI(16:0/22:6), PE(16:0/22:2), and PS(18:0/20:4) in MG highlights impaired membrane lipid remodeling, shortage of signaling lipids, and dysregulated apoptosis-related membrane events during ALI.

In DG, these glycerophospholipids showed varying degrees of recovery following Yam ELNs intervention. PI(16:0/22:6) increased to 4429.92 ± 683.42 ng/g, significantly higher than MG (P < 0.05) and approaching NG levels, indicating preventive effects on liver injury. PE(16:0/22:2) increased to 259.62 ± 93.39 ng/g (~ 1.43-fold vs MG, P < 0.05), suggesting improved hepatocyte membrane stability and energy metabolism. PS(18:0/20:4) increased to 38,783.45 ± 11,067.56 ng/g, significantly higher than that in MG, indicating effective restoration of key glycerophospholipid alterations. These results suggested that Yam ELNs help to maintain membrane lipid homeostasis through modulation of PI-, PE-, and PS-related metabolic networks, thereby preventing inflammation and hepatocyte apoptosis and ultimately exerting hepatoprotective effects. Although some lipid species identified by DESI-MSI were not fully covered by the targeted quantitative panel, focused analysis demonstrated that, within the high-throughput dataset, PI(16:0/22:6) and PE(16:0/22:2), together with various other lipid species, which are functionally related to the lipid species identified by spatial metabolomics, exhibited concordant directional changes. The coordinated alterations of these closely related lipid species provide indirect supportive evidence for the findings derived from spatial metabolomics.

Reshaping of composition and metabolic function of the gut microbiota

Evaluation of Yam ELNs’ effects on gut microbiota based on 16S rRNA sequencing

16S rRNA sequencing results indicated that Yam ELNs exert hepatoprotective effects by modulating the composition and function of the gut microbiota. The Venn diagram showed that a total of 3,563 operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were detected across the three sample groups, with 923 shared OTUs. The model group exhibited significantly higher OTU abundance (2,019) compared to both the control group (1,921) and the Yam ELNs group (1,896) (Fig. 9A). Alpha diversity analysis revealed significantly elevated Ace and Chao1 indices in the model group compared to the control group (Fig. 9B–C), indicating increased gut microbial richness induced by CCl4. Shannon, Simpson, and Pielou_e indices showed no significant differences in microbial diversity or evenness among groups (Fig. 9D–G), while the Coverage index demonstrated sufficient sequencing depth. Both Ace and Chao1 indices increased in the model group, indicating that CCl4 administration in mice caused temporary gut microbiota disruption, leading to increased microbial abundance in the intestine. Additionally, the composition of gut microbiota at the phylum and genus levels was further evaluated to investigate the regulatory effects of yam exosomes on the gut microbiota of model mice. At the phylum level, compared with the blank group, the model group exhibited significantly increased relative abundance of Proteobacteria (p < 0.05), significantly decreased relative abundance of Actinobacteria (p < 0.05); the abundance of Firmicutes decreased, while that of Bacteroidetes and Verrucomicrobota increased. Among these, the Proteobacteria phylum contains various Gram-negative bacteria, whose increased levels elevate lipopolysaccharide (LPS) release and induce systemic inflammation [39, 40]; whereas the Actinobacteria phylum includes beneficial bacteria such as Bifidobacteria, whose reduction weakens the intestinal mucosal barrier function [41]. As shown in Fig. 9, Yam ELNs significantly reversed the decline in Proteobacteria abundance (p < 0.05) and slowed the decrease in Actinobacteria (p < 0.05). Other disrupted bacterial genera also generally exhibited a trend toward recovery toward the control group. At the genus level, the model group showed a significant increase (p < 0.05) in the relative abundances of several potentially harmful genera, including Prevotellaceae_UCG-001, Escherichia-Shigella, and Alloprevotella. In contrast, beneficial bacteria or those associated with beneficial metabolites, such as Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, were significantly reduced (p < 0.05). Following Yam ELNs treatment, the abundances of Lactococcus and Negativibacillus significantly decreased (p < 0.05), with the remaining disrupted bacterial genera also showing a trend toward recovery. Yam ELNs disrupt the gut microbiota homeostasis, suppressing pathogenic Gram-negative bacteria while promoting the proliferation of beneficial bacteria. This approach intervenes at the primary source in the gut to remove a key aggravating factor, thereby interrupting a major pathogenic cascade driving liver damage.

Revealment of the regulatory effects of Yam ELNs on ALI-associated metabolic disturbances based on fecal metabolomics

To investigate the modulatory effects of Yam ELNs on ALI-associated metabolic disturbances, fecal metabolomics analysis was performed using UPLC-Q/Orbitrap MS. Multivariate statistical analysis was conducted in SIMCA 14.1. PCA(Supplementary Fig. S3) showed partial overlap among the three groups within the 95% confidence interval, reflecting the complexity of ALI-related metabolic changes. Further PLS-DA modeling demonstrated clear separation among NG, MG, and DG in the score plot, indicating that Yam ELNs can regulate ALI-associated abnormalities at the global metabolic level. To identify differential features, OPLS-DA models were first constructed for the NG–MG and MG–DG comparisons. The reliability of these models was confirmed by permutation testing (200 iterations) with no indication of overfitting. Subsequently, based on the OPLS-DA results (VIP > 1 and p < 0.05), 21 endogenous metabolites associated with acute ALI were identified, encompassing fatty acids, glycerophospholipids, bile acids, and amino acids. Verification using HMDB and MS/MS confirmed significant differences among the three groups, indicating that Yam ELNs can intervene in ALI-related disturbances across multiple metabolic layers. Pathway analysis (Fig. 9J) indicated that these differential metabolites were primarily enriched in several key pathways, including linoleic acid metabolism, unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis, glycerophospholipid metabolism, ubiquinone biosynthesis, and primary bile acid biosynthesis. Among these, alterations in linoleic acid metabolism and ubiquinone biosynthesis were notably prominent. Linoleic acid, an essential polyunsaturated fatty acid involved in lipid metabolism and inflammation regulation, was significantly elevated in MG, indicating marked disruption of fatty acid metabolism. In the MG group, levels of key fatty acid metabolites and the glycerophospholipid mediator lysophosphatidylcholine (Lyso-PC) were significantly elevated. Preventive administration of Yam ELNs in the DG group partially attenuated these increases, suggesting a role in maintaining fatty acid metabolic homeostasis and modulating glycerophospholipid pathways during ALI-associated inflammation.Fecal phenylalanine levels were markedly increased in MG, suggesting potential mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired energy metabolism under CCl4 exposure. In DG, this abnormal elevation was reduced, indicating partial maintenance of hepatocyte energy metabolism homeostasis. Bile pigment metabolism was also substantially disrupted in MG, whereas Yam ELNs intervention restored metabolic features closer to physiological levels, potentially reflecting protection of liver function and antioxidant capacity. Collectively, these fecal metabolomics results indicated that Yam ELNs exert a preventive protective effect against CCl4-induced ALI by modulating gut–liver axis–related metabolic networks across multiple pathways.

Revealment of the gut–liver axis interaction network mediated by Yam ELNs and its systemic hepatoprotective mechanism based on multi-omics integration

To systematically explore the consistency and potential biological associations across multi-level metabolic regulations following preventive Yam ELNs intervention, a multi-omics integration analysis was performed. At the genus–fecal metabolite level (Fig. 10A), Yam ELNs intervention promoted the restoration of protective taxa, including 1 and Actinobacteriota. These taxa were positively correlated with multiple metabolites involved in lipid homeostasis and anti-inflammatory responses, such as (10E,12Z)-Octadecadienoic acid, PC (22:1/14:0), Ricinoleic acid, and PGD2 ethanolamide. In contrast, potentially pathogenic genera, including Proteobacteria, Escherichia–Shigella, and Negativibacillus, were more closely associated with Phthalic acid and certain sphingolipids. Consistent with previous reports, protective taxa are closely linked to lipid homeostasis and the production of anti-inflammatory lipid mediators, whereas metabolites such as Phthalic acid are associated with oxidative stress and cellular injury [42]. At the genus–serum metabolite level (Fig. 10B), microbial changes were reflected in systemic circulation. Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group exhibited coordinated changes with lipid molecules (e.g., PC (16:0/16:3) and PE (16:0/18:2)) and inverse trends with potentially injurious metabolites, including LysoPC. These findings align with established mechanisms: elevated protective phospholipids correlate with membrane stabilization, increased LysoPC is known to contribute to ALI, and accumulated arachidonic acid participates in inflammatory amplification. This indicates that Yam ELNs may thus function through the microbiota–serum metabolite axis to coordinately regulate lipid metabolism and inflammatory risk [43]. Concurrently, metabolic profiles of bile acids and unsaturated fatty acids exhibited overall restoration trends, suggesting that alterations in the gut microbiota exert regulatory effects that extend to systemic metabolism.

Further fecal–serum metabolite correlation analysis (Fig. 10C) demonstrated that most key differential metabolites exhibited consistent directional changes between the two compartments, indicating that improvements in gut lumen metabolism contribute to systemic metabolic homeostasis via fecal-to-serum metabolite transfer. Metabolites associated with lipid homeostasis and anti-inflammatory responses, including (10E,12Z)-Octadecadienoic acid, PC (22:1/14:0), Ricinoleic acid, and PGD2 ethanolamide, were upregulated, whereas metabolites related to toxicity, membrane damage, and inflammation (e.g., LysoPC, LacCer, and Phthalic acid) were downregulated [44]. Combined with pharmacodynamic outcomes—including improvements in ALT, AST, MDA, GSH, SOD, and Nrf2/HO-1 signaling—these fecal–serum metabolic consistencies reflect strong coordination with the overall hepatoprotective effect of Yam ELNs. Collectively, the multi-omics integration results indicated that Yam ELNs may restore systemic lipid homeostasis and mitigate ALI risk through a continuous regulatory axis spanning the gut microbiota, fecal metabolites, and serum metabolites.

Discussion

From the perspective of ALI prevention and hepatic homeostasis maintenance, miR159, which is abundant in Yam ELNs, may represent a key active component mediating hepatoprotective effects. Previous studies have shown that miR159, a plant-derived exogenous microRNA, can be taken up by the host via cross-kingdom mechanisms and subsequently regulate critical signaling networks, primarily by targeting glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK-3β). As a key mediator, GSK-3β amplifies inflammatory responses, activates hepatic stellate cells, and promotes fibrotic progression; its suppression by miR159 may therefore attenuate liver injury, in part by dampening TGF-β/Smad pathway activation. The reduction of p-Smad2 is accompanied by decreased expression of downstream fibrotic markers, including Col I and α-SMA. Subsequently, hepatic stellate cell activation and excessive extracellular matrix deposition are suppressed, thereby preventing the transition from acute liver injury to fibrosis. In addition, the downregulation of GSK-3β is associated with its interaction with the NF-κB pathway, leading to reduced phosphorylation of NF-κB p65. This decrease subsequently results in diminished release of inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-6, thereby mitigating acute inflammatory responses, secondary ALI, and contributing to the stabilization of the hepatic microenvironment. Furthermore, the mTOR/p-p70S6K-related metabolic pathway may be influenced by miR159, leading to modulation of the metabolic reprogramming of hepatic stellate cells under injury conditions [45,46]. This results in the suppression of stellate cell proliferation and ECM synthesis, potentially limiting post‑injury fibrotic development. Taken together, these mechanistic insights suggest that miR159, delivered by Yam ELNs, acts as a multi‑target synergistic regulator, concurrently affecting inflammation, fibrosis initiation, and cellular metabolism to establish a protective hepatic state—a conclusion that is consistent with the present findings. Nevertheless, the precise contribution and critical nodes of miR159 in hepatoprotection require causal experiments such as functional inhibition or overexpression within the Yam ELNs system.

Based on multi‑omics analysis, the gut–liver metabolic interactions mediated by Yam ELNs can be interpreted as a directional and coordinated metabolic remodeling, rather than being merely a shift in microbiota composition. The restoration of protective taxa such as Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group was correlated with the enrichment of molecules related to lipid homeostasis. It is suggested that these microbes influence gut barrier function and local immune status through the regulation of short-chain fatty acids, unsaturated lipid metabolism, and phospholipid interconversion, thereby reducing the delivery of endotoxins and inflammatory signals to the liver and consequently lowering basal hepatic injury stress. Enhanced membrane stability and repair capacity in hepatocytes can be attributed to restored levels of key phospholipids such as PC and PE, through which cellular integrity and functional stability are thus maintained. Taken together, the reduction of pro‑inflammatory serum lipids (e.g., arachidonic acid metabolites, LysoPC) suggests that Yam ELNs may attenuate systemic inflammatory signaling and oxidative stress, thus disrupting the pathogenic cycle linking inflammation, lipid dysregulation, and ALI. This is complemented by the restoration of bile acid and unsaturated fatty acid metabolism, further corroborating Yam ELNs’ role in remodeling systemic metabolic networks.This provided additional protection at the level of hepatic homeostasis. The observed fecal–serum metabolic consistency suggested that the effects of Yam ELNs extended beyond the intestinal lumen and were linked to a continuous, cross‑tissue, multi‑level metabolic regulatory cascade. Collectively, the lipidomic and pharmacodynamic data indicated that this cascade underpinned key improvements, including restored hepatic lipid homeostasis and elevated antioxidant capacity. A preventive liver protection mechanism originating in the gut and propagated via metabolic signaling was suggested. This model could be validated by microbiota interventions, key metabolite manipulation, and pathway analysis to clarify the critical nodes and causal flow in the Yam ELNs–driven gut–liver axis. The inclusion of fecal metabolomics further strengthened the functional linkage between intestinal metabolic remodeling and hepatic protection. Although the current data consistently support involvement of the gut–liver axis, the gut microbiota in mediating Yam ELNs–induced hepatic protection has not been directly validated using antibiotic-ablation model, which represents a limitation of the present study. In this regard, our experimental results on intestinal barrier function and hepatic TLR4 signaling provide a functional, albeit indirect, line of evidence. Circulating LPS reflects intestinal barrier integrity and microbiota-derived inflammatory load, and TLR4, as a key pattern recognition receptor that specifically binds to LPS, plays a crucial role in initiating the inflammatory response cascade in the liver. Upon LPS binding, TLR4 activates downstream signaling pathways (such as NF-κB pathway), which in turn promotes the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and exacerbates hepatic inflammation and injury. The observed reduction in serum LPS levels, coupled with downregulated hepatic TLR4 expression, provides a mechanistic link between the gut microbiota remodeling and the attenuated hepatic inflammatory response, indicating that preventive administration of Yam ELNs may at least partially alleviate liver injury through modulation of gut-derived inflammatory signals. But to further clarify the causal contributions within this regulatory cascade, microbiota-depletion or functional interference strategies are still needed.

Conclusion

For the first time, natural nanoparticles were isolated from yam and named Yam ELNs. Their systematic characterization was also accomplished in this study, covering the entire process from material discovery to pharmacological evaluation and mechanistic exploration. Following confirmation of their preventive effects against ALI, mechanistic studies on Yam ELNs were conducted. They were found to significantly enhance hepatocyte antioxidant defenses and precisely regulate hepatic lipid metabolism. This dual action prevented ALI-induced glycerophospholipid dysregulation, suppressed lipid peroxidation, and attenuated inflammatory responses, thus elucidating key protective pathways. Desorption electrospray ionization mass spectrometry imaging (DESI-MSI) was used in the study, which showed that the spatial distribution of key antioxidant lipids (e.g., PS (18:0/20:4)) was maintained at physiological levels. Targeted lipidomics was employed for further verification, confirming that these lipids were significantly enriched in multiple pathways, including glycerophospholipid metabolism. Mechanistic exploration along the gut–liver axis revealed that Yam ELNs pretreatment markedly decreased circulating endotoxin levels and suppressed hepatic TLR4 expression, indicating reinforcement of intestinal barrier function and subsequent alleviation of endotoxin-mediated hepatic inflammation. Integrative multi-omics correlation analysis was performed, indicating that systemic lipid homeostasis could be restored through a continuous regulatory axis involving gut microbiota, fecal metabolites, and serum metabolites, thereby reducing the risk of ALI. Collectively, these results demonstrated that Yam ELNs exerted protective effects against ALI via multi-level regulation of antioxidant status, lipid metabolic homeostasis, and gut-liver axis-related signaling pathways. This study conducted a systematic investigation into the structural characteristics and biological functions of Yam ELNs, thereby laying an experimental foundation for their future application in the prevention and treatment of ALI.
