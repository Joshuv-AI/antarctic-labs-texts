Acceptability of a reduced dose of therapeutic food in the management of severely acutely malnourished children aged 6–59 months in Mbuji-Mayi, Democratic Republic of Congo
Authors: Julien Ntaongo Alendi, Marie-Claire Muyer, Cécile Salpeteur, Steve Botomba, Jean Baptiste Mayavanga, Aimée Mupuala, Florence Mbiya Muadi, Samuel Mampunza, Léon Quénée, Marlène Camrrubi, Carine Magen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite a higher prevalence of Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM) in children living in Sub Saharan Africa, access to outpatient treatment remains limited. In relation to this, the 2023 WHO guidelines proposed reducing the dose of Ready to Use Therapeutic Food (RUTF) distributed on the basis of recent evidence. One of the positive consequence is that savings made by reducing RUTF prescription per child can then be reinvested to admit more children and hence increase treatment coverage. But this RUTF reduction may not be acceptable in all contexts and more evidence is needed to ascertain this strategy is acceptable by main actors of SAM management.MethodsThis qualitative study assessed the acceptability of the reduced dose as part of a nutritional intervention programme in Mbuji-Mayi, Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC). The present qualitative study, a sub-study of the EfRAMAS clinical trial, assessed the acceptability of the reduced dose in the context of a nutritional intervention programme run by Action Against Hunger (AAH) in Mbuji-Mayi, DRC. Information was gathered through 54 semi-structured interviews, 10 focus groups and 6 in situ observations. Deductive analysis (from pre-established hypotheses) and inductive analysis (using themes that emerged in the field) were based on a triangulation of different sources of information. The hypothetico-deductive analysis was based on a triangulation of the different sources of information.ResultsThe acceptability of the reduced dose of RUTF during the treatment of children 06 to 59 months with SAM was confirmed provided that two conditions were met: firstly, that the savings made are used to treat other children suffering from SAM, but who do not have access to treatment, and secondly, that accompanying measures are put in place to address food insecurity, lack of access to drinking water, and gender-based violence. The reduced dose was deemed acceptable by the majority of health workers. Others, on the contrary, feared that it would increase their workload by lengthening the duration of children’s treatment or increasing the number of children treated. Some caregivers showed a high degree of acceptance of the idea of reducing the dose to enable more children with SAM to have access to the treatment. Other caregivers felt that this would affect the children’s health and reduce household income or food sources.ConclusionsOverall, the reduction in RUTF doses seems relatively acceptable to the majority of those interviewed, considering the benefit of more children accessing treatment, in that aligned with recent WHO recommendations. However, the caregivers, health care personnel and local authorities recommend paying attention to their vulnerable situation prone to food insecurity, low access to drinking water, lack of income-generating activities, and gender-based violence in Kasaï Oriental, eastern DRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26767-w.

Introduction

Ready-to-use therapeutic foods (RUTFs), composed of peanut paste mixed with milk powder, vegetable oils and micronutrient powder and supplied in 92 g sachets providing 500 kcal each, have enabled outpatient treatment for severe acute malnutrition (SAM) [1] since the 2000s, facilitating access to treatment for a large number of children suffering from SAM. RUTF is directly accepted and adopted by families of children with SAM, especially when the ingredients used are familiar to the beneficiaries [2–7]. A multicentre study conducted in Ghana, Ethiopia, India and Pakistan showed good acceptability of both standard and home-produced RUTF [8]. With outpatient treatment, family caregivers manage the stock of RUTF sachets for children with SAM [1, 9]. For the purposes of this study, a family caregiver is defined as anyone who cares for and follows the treatment of a child with SAM. Some family caregivers or household members see RUTF as a source of income [10, 11]. Despite the importance of RUTF in the management of SAM [12], caregivers consider other household or personal constraints which explains certain practices leading to misuse such as sharing [13], bartering or reselling in markets.

Although the management of severe acute malnutrition has facilitated access to RUTF, the number of children managed worldwide remains low compared with the number of children suffering from SAM at any given time [14]. In preparation for the update of the new WHO guidelines, the experts reviewed the scientific evidence on reducing the dose of RUTF in the management of SAM. They found that it was possible to reduce the dose of RUTF from 150 to 185 kcal/kg/day to 100–130 kcal/kg/day until recovery. To increase outpatient treatment coverage, the WHO proposes, among other things, to produce RUTF locally using local ingredients or to reduce the dose currently prescribed in the DRC’s 2016 national management protocol [1, 2]. As in most communities most affected by SAM, all actors involved in the fight against malnutrition (caregivers, health care workers, community leaders) are familiar with RUTF and its use. Furthermore, in the Bonzola and Nzaba health zones, as in the rest of the DRC, the perceptions of local communities have a significant influence on the management of SAM [15]. Since reducing the dose is a major change in the management of SAM, acceptance by key stakeholders is crucial. The degree of acceptance of the reduced dose by communities, community leaders, traditional practitioners, community health workers, and health care workers ((i.e. nurses providing SAM care in each of the studied health centres) is a predictive, and probably decisive, indicator for scaling up. Good acceptability hence adherence has the potential to improve program coverage, with more children treated with the same available stock of RUTF, as well as treatment effectiveness in terms of recovery and relapse rates, and eventually the generalizability of such strategy to other contexts.

As these are new protocols, their acceptance by key stakeholders is crucial. The degree of acceptance of the reduced dose by communities, community leaders, traditional practitioners, community relays and health care workers is a predictive—and probably decisive—indicator for going to scale. The provider is any nurse providing SAM care at one of the selected health centres. Good acceptability can potentially improve program coverage, with more children treated with the same available stock of RUTF, treatment efficacy in terms of recovery and relapse rates, and transferability to other settings [1].

The present study was carried out in parallel with a randomized controlled trial on the efficacy of RUTF dose reduction during SAM management in the context of severe food insecurity and with routine medical staff without additional personnel. The aim of this study was to assess the acceptability of a reduced dose of RUTF by key players in the management of SAM.

Study methodology

Research team and reflexivity

The research team for this study was comprised 10 people, including three researchers, six investigators, and a technical expert in nutrition and health programs from Action Against Hunger. They were divided into three groups and involved in all phases of the study. The researchers were of different nationalities with a gender balance (sex ratio 1:1). These researchers were specialists in anthropology, nutrition, and public health. Before the field study began, all members received training in qualitative methodology and then participated in a pre-test of the tools to ensure their relevance and effectiveness.

The fact that the researchers were of different nationalities and backgrounds and that the investigators were all part of the local community helped to minimize potential bias and promote the researcher’s reflexivity about the study by enabling him or her to position him or herself from different points of view. The participants and researchers did not know each other personally, but some interviewers were recruited for their knowledge of the region or the languages used by the participants. A presentation of the research objectives was systematically shared before all interviews. The interviewers worked in pairs and mixed (male/female) most of the time.

Theoretical framework

Grounded theory and content analysis, sometimes discourse analysis according to the sociolinguistic elements that emerged in the analysis of the interviews (semantics of representations, analysis of “learned” and “lay” discourse on the children’s therapeutic paths) were used.

The study used qualitative survey methodology in a nutritional intervention program [16, 17]. Focus groups, in-depth interviews and direct observations were used to assess the acceptability of this approach to care [18].

This study is part of a non-inferiority randomised controlled trial (EfRAMAS) which targeted children with SAM and not MAM. The main results of the EfRAMAS study showed that the reduced dose was as effective as the standard dose in terms of weight-gain velocity and performance indicators (recovery, defaulted, non-response and relapse rates) [19].

Participants

Purposive sampling was used to select participants. Family caregivers, health care workers and community health workers had to be residents of the Bonzola or Nzaba health zone. Age, gender, level of education and profession guided the selection of family caregivers to obtain a diverse panel. Community leaders (any official or unofficial opinion leader who could influence the population’s decisions) and traditional healers were selected for convenience and according to their availability. Traditional practitioners are people who use local plants and recipes to treat SAM. There were a total of 64 recordings (54 interviews, 10 FGDs) + 6 in situ observations in the homes of children with SAM.

The participants were family caregivers, healthcare health care workers, community health workers, traditional practitioners, community leaders and political and administrative authorities living in Mbuji-Mayi and involved in the fight against malnutrition. All health care workers employed as interviewers for the present study were excluded from the list of participants.

The interviewees were included by category until data saturation, when no new information emerged from the interviews.

Setting

Many ethnic groups coexist in Kasai Oriental, with the Lubas in the majority [20]. The province is also marked by a strong religious tradition, with the social influence of the churches and the maintenance of ancestral rites [21]. These beliefs influence local communities’ perceptions of SAM and the therapeutic path of affected children [15] as we reported it in in this area. In addition to the heavy workload and gender-based violence they suffer, women are disadvantaged in terms of education, employment and income, with a significant impact on children’s nutritional status [15, 22, 23]. With the bankruptcy of MIBA, which had been the mainstay of the province’s economy, artisanal diamond mining, the main activity for the majority of men, has seen a drastic drop in production, resulting in deepening poverty. Some have converted to agriculture, but soil exhaustion, pollution and a lack of modern farming techniques mean that yields are low [24]. Access to drinking water relies on rain water or purchase of private well water, due to broken municipal network [3]. The people of Kasai depend almost exclusively on imports of foodstuffs and other necessities [24]. All these factors are among the determinants of chronic food insecurity in Kasaï [25]. According to the latest Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) survey, over 31% of the population of Kasaï Oriental is severely food insecure [26]. The situation is even worse for the households of children affected by SAM and admitted to EfRAMAS, where over 93% live in severe insecurity [19].

Data collection

Between October and November 2021, in the Bonzola and Nzaba health zones, family caregivers, community leaders and traditional practitioners were interviewed about the use of RUTF and the acceptability of the reduced dose in the management of SAM. At the same time, a literature review was carried out, including published scientific articles, gray literature, media coverage, and reports from the various institutions involved in the fight against SAM in Kasai. A detailed bibliographical synthesis enabled us to put these data into perspective with those collected in the field, with the aim of triangulating the information.

The interviews were conducted in Tshiluba, Lingala or French, depending on the interviewee. Semi-structured individual interviews lasted an average of 60 min, and focus groups lasted between 60 and 90 min. Each interviewee was isolated from the others in a quiet area. The study was not longitudinal, so we did not proceed to repeat interviews, but some of respondents has been involved in several data collection (interviews, focus group and observations) like community health workers, mothers of SAM child and traditional healer. Direct observations lasted for a full day each. All interviews were recorded using dictaphones. The research team comprised 3 researchers, 6 interviewers trained in qualitative data collection and 2 staff members from Action Against Hunger’s Mbuji-Mayi office. They also shared their knowledge of the context and worked upstream on translation issues in preparation for data collection. Each evening, the entire team met for a debriefing session, and corrective action was taken in the event of any errors. All the researchers and interviewers translated and transcribed the interviews.

The pre-test was organized in the field at the end of the training course by all the interviewers and researchers. The survey consisted of a few interviews with respondents other than those selected for the study. Debriefing and analysis sessions at the end of the pretest to improve the data collection tools and translations produced by the researchers and interviewers.

Data processing and analysis

All the data were encoded using NVivo 12 software (QSR International, 2018). Deductive analysis was performed for the themes pre-established in our hypotheses before data collection began. The themes that emerged in the field were analyzed using the inductive method [18]. All of these analyses were based on triangulation of the various sources of information [27]. The analysis was carried out in two main stages: familiarization and examination of themes. Each transcript was read and reread separately by the researchers to familiarize themselves with the respondents’ narratives and identify the main themes that emerged on the basis of the research question, but any other new themes were noted and included. Themes were then examined for links, similarities and differences.

The reliability of the study was ensured by training the teams in interview techniques, pretesting the guides and discussions between the research team and the communities. The participants did not have the opportunity to validate the transcriptions but community restitutions of preliminary results were organized to gather their feedback on the study.

Reporting

Direct quotations from participants were used to illustrate the different aspects of this study. For each quotation, apart from the quality, the number of the corresponding respondent was added to mark the difference between the different quotations. The data presented are consistent with the various results of this study. The specific problems of female victims of violence emerged as particular themes during investigations into children’s care pathways.

Results

The acceptability of reducing the dose of RUTF in the management of SAM was closely linked to its use by beneficiaries and remained a major determinant for the success of a scale-up and for inducing a change of protocol. Representations and uses of this product were diverse and evolving. RUTF could be considered medicine, functional food, foodstuff or commercial product. In all cases, it was highly prized and sought after, particularly in the context of food insecurity. For health care workers, the reduced dose was morally well accepted, as it theoretically increases outpatient treatment coverage. However, others feared that the reduced dose would increase their workload by prolonging the children’s length of stay or that it would affect their recovery. Some family caregivers felt that reducing the dose of RUTF was acceptable, as this strategy shall enable other children with SAM to access it. Others felt that reducing the dose would have a negative impact on children’s health and reduce household income or food sources in the context of severe food insecurity.

Observed therapeutic use of RUTF

All those interviewed knew that RUTF sachets were the treatment for children suffering from SAM and were aware of the prescriptions for their use.

“I receive 14 sachets for a week and I find that this is enough for me because he consumes 2 sachets a day, one in the morning and one in the evening. If we reduce the number of sachets, there is not always a problem because it is always the nurses who decide how to give him the sachets and if we give him only a few RUTF sachets, they saw that my child is already in good health”(Mother of SAM child, File\\211102-0007).

Some caregivers engaged in inappropriate practices to have more RUTF. According to a rapid sociocultural study of the Kalomba health zone (ACF, 2014), the appropriate use of RUTF depended on the indications given at the health centre. Some sensitized caregivers knew which dose to administer, and the stock was well kept away from other children. Sometimes, however, health-care staff did not always have time to clearly explain the use of RUTFs, and the community had little understanding of the criteria for admission to treatment or even the use of RUTFs. In such cases, RUTF was shared between siblings or with visitors.

Family caregivers believed that RUTF was a good medicine for recovering children suffering from SAM. They reported that, before the support of humanitarian actors such as Action Against Hunger, families used traditional practitioners’ recipes to treat SAM but that many affected children died due to a lack of appropriate treatment. The arrival of a program to treat the disease with nutritional products reduced the use of these traditional recipes, as families felt that the RUTF was more effective. They considered that the quantity distributed according to the national protocol was sufficient.

“Of all these foods, Plumpi is the most important for the recovery of a malnourished child. I have found that my child recovers quickly with Plumpi. I give him Plumpi every morning before any other meal”. (Mother of a SAM child. Files\211108-2350).

Health care workers also felt that family carers understood the benefits of RUTF and how to use it. RUTF had a prize place in the range of care available, such as traditional medicine, self-medication and the therapeutic practices of revival churches. This helped changing the perception of malnutrition as a disease and reducing the stigma attached to it. In addition, the population saw in the product’s ingredients a similarity with the traditional local porridge recipes used by both traditional practitioners and Action Against Hunger teams during cooking demonstrations. However, at the same time, health care workers felt that parents needed to be aware that RUTF is only an emergency solution that will not last forever.

“Family carers think that the solution lies in the plumpi, but we have to explain to them plumpi that it is for a time: there is a duration, there is a project, there is a barrier: if we do not fund it, then we stop providing it.” (AAH employee. Files\\2002110130).

The treatment of children with RUTF was well understood by family caregivers. They understood how to administer RUTF to children: the schedule, the hygiene measures to take and the quantities to administer per dose.

“I easily give plumpi to my child because we were taught the technique at the centre: I wash the sachet, I knead it, I cut the indicated corner and the contents come out and the child consumes it. He takes a sachet in the morning before any other meal and another sachet in the evening”. (Mother, SAM child. Files\211102_2259).

Misuse, sharing and commercialization of the RUTF

Very thin adults and elderly people often asked for nutritional management. Cases of RUTF consumption by non malnourished people were reported in our study. Given the scale of trade in RUTF products outside of SAM management in this area of the country, official measures were taken to limit this trade, including a provincial decree. The issue of trade in RUTF was not sporadic but rather widespread and indicated that in the context of major food insecurity, it was very difficult to maintain the medicinal status of RUTF, which became a source of food and income for the population [28].

Others, on the other hand, saw RUTF as food that everyone can eat. It was in these families that sharing practices were most widespread. RUTF was shared not only with siblings but also with visitors. In some cases, it was the mothers themselves who consumed or gave to the non sick children in the household part of the RUTF distributed for children with SAM. The study team made pictures of the RUTF sharing practices within observed families [29].

As far as I am concerned, since God has not tolerated lies, I tell you the truth. When I arrive with the porridge, I take two bags and give them to my brothers, and the rest I say: ‘Do not touch, what is left is for the child’”. (Mother, SAM child. Files\\211101-1242).

The observations documented sharing practices in one family: a rather rational practice based on a “risk economy” between caring for the sick child and preserving the health of other family members. The mother of the family gave part of the sachet to the SAM child, mixing it with tea (to limit thirst), gave another part to the older sister (with a spoon), added part as a condiment to the other children’s dish and finished the rest herself.

Community health workers believed that the food insecurity and poverty in which the inhabitants of Mbuji-Mayi live explained the practice of sharing RUTF. Some households did not have enough money to buy enough food for all members in terms of quality and diversity. Knowing all the virtues of the RUTF, they shared it with the whole family.

“There is only fufu here. Because, what makes the RUTF shared, is the lack of means if the family has, for example, fufu for the other children; yes, I do not think they are going to give the RUTF to those who are not sick.” (Health care worker. Files\211103 1138).

For other caregivers, RUTF was seen as a source of household income. They sold part of the RUTF received to buy water or other things the family needs. Sometimes, health care workers themselves sold the RUTF. The commercialization of RUTF was growing to the point where people knew where to buy it such as schoolchildren. Some pharmacy owners bought large quantities of RUTF, which they then resold to potential consumers.

Acceptability of RUTF reduced dose

The acceptability of the reduced dose of RUTF was subject to certain conditions. First, parents’ fears about their sick child’s recovery had to be allayed; particularly through messages delivered by nurses and community health workers. The context of widespread poverty and food insecurity in the region lead some caregivers to consider RUTF as a vital additional resource for families. For them, reducing access to this precious product amounted to worsening their situation. Secondly, in this context, while reducing RUTF dose was perceived beneficial for the health system, allowing more children to be treated, it required concrete support for vulnerable families, and women in particular, in terms of income-generating activities, food security support, improved access to drinking water, and actions combating gender-based violence, among other recommendations (Table 1).

For communities and families, the acceptability of the reduced dose depended on the right key messages given to family caregivers on the therapeutic effects of RUTF, insisting that RUTF was effective and that the quantity distributed, even if reduced, would recover the child better than other treatments (including traditional recipes). In addition, a relationship based on honesty and competence needed to be established between health centre nurses and the family caregivers of children with SAM. This relationship would provide reassurance about the number of RUTF sachets distributed. It remained preferable to clearly informing the mother of the doses envisaged to treat the child.

“The quantity I am given is sufficient for the treatment because it is given according to weight. However, I had already been told that from the third week onwards, they were going to reduce the quantity, and I cannot worry because I had already been told that long before.” (Mother, SAM child. Concerns about the consequences of reducing rations for certain members of the community focused primarily on children’s health. Very quickly during the discussions, a much greater overall concern emerged, namely that reducing rations would lead to a loss of income or food resources for families. This was also the case for some community leaders, who believed that the standard quantity distributed in standard doses was insufficient as by sharing and other misuses reported, children were already treated using a reduced dose. They even suggested increasing the quantity rather than reducing it, considering that it would prevent the advent of new malnourished children within the same vulnerable households.

The majority of health workers, particularly those who already tested this method, accepted the reduced dose, which they considered effective in treating children suffering from SAM. Discussion

Our study highlights the practices, risks, and recommendations of communities in Mbuji-Mayi regarding this strategy to reduce RUTF for the care of children with SAM.

The acceptability of the reduced dose of RUTF depended on how recipients perceived and used it. On one side, most participants in this study knew that RUTF was a medicine for children with SAM and should only be used for these children. On the other side some confused RUTF intended for the management of SAM with other foods in general, and the status of RUTF changed depending on the context of food insecurity. In this case, they found it natural to share it with all other family members. Due to poverty exacerbated by the economic downturn in the region, some families found RUTF to be also a source of income and sold part of the quantity distributed for their children with SAM. Those who considered RUTF to be a medicine accepted a reduction in the dose in order to increase coverage, i.e., to treat a larger number of children. Some health workers felt that reducing the dose would increase their workload if the children receiving it had to stay longer in treatment. Studies on the reduced dose conducted in the Kamwesha health zone [30] or EfRAMAS [31] showed that children with SAM receiving a reduced dose had the same length of stay than those receiving the standard dose. This was also the case in the MANGO study conducted in Burkina Faso [4]. This alleviated the legitimate concerns of health care workers and caregivers. For those who shared or sold RUTF or were concerned about nutritional insecurity in their community, the amount distributed should not be reduced but increased to cover all community needs.

Perception of RUTF evolved in the past years, and nowadays majority of people was familiar with RUTF as a medicine for children with SAM, which was a common process observed in other contexts [2, 5]. Awareness-raising efforts by community health workers during home visits and nutrition education sessions in health centres were the basis for this perception among the community. Support from humanitarian actors contributed significantly to this positive perception.

As a natural social practice around the world, sharing food was cultural in Kasai, as throughout the DRC. There were even popular sayings to this effect, that “if there is food for two, there is food for three or four”. In this community, sharing food helped strengthen social bonds. Other studies carried out in several countries where the management of SAM programs were implemented also noted cases of RUTF sharing [10, 32, 33]. Without breaking this spirit of fraternity, and in view of adopting a reduced dose strategy, we need to raise awareness so that RUTF is reserved for the treatment of SAM to avoid criminalization of these survival strategies. Indeed, to discourage the exchange and sale of RUTF, the governor launched an extensive awareness campaign. At the same time, local authorities issued a decree punishing offenders with imprisonment [34].

For the majority of respondents, the reduced dose was acceptable under two conditions: that the savings be used to treat a larger number of children. Second, that the implementation of a SAM management program be accompanied by multi-sectoral support measures, particularly in the areas of food security, access to drinking water, combatting gender-based violence, income-generating activities, and awareness-raising to reduce women’s workload. Acceptability was conditional for the majority of respondents, nuancing the 2023 WHO guidelines recommendations, of which one was to reduce the dose of RUTF once children improved out of SAM and depending on context [35]. The involvement of community health workers and traditional practitioners should also be considered to optimize prevention and ensure adequate follow-up of children treated for SAM .

Respondents reported severe food insecurity for households in Mbuji Mayi. In the same population, the EFRAMAS study showed that more than 94% of children caregivers faced the context of generalized food insecurity [19]. Hence it is very unlikely to prevent sustainably malnutrition or obtain good and sustainable recovery rates if no parallel intervention is carried out to improve household food security. The Global Nutrition Cluster recommended that interventions to improve food security be given the same importance as those to treat malnutrition [36]. Other researchers suggested combining household food security interventions such as income-generating interventions or cash transfers with individual SAM management for greater effectiveness [37]. Interventions that address the direct causes of malnutrition, including food security, disease control and childcare practices, demonstrated a very high cost-effective impact in the short term [38]. Another indirect cause, poor access to drinking water, is responsible for approximately 50% of malnutrition cases [39, 40]. Good access to drinking water would significantly help prevent malnutrition as in Kasaï Oriental, households had to pay for drinking water, a rare resource due to the breakdown of city water supply network. Several studies conducted worldwide showed that another indirect cause of malnutrition, gender-based violence against women, had a negative impact on child nutrition. The impact was even more negative if, in addition, the mother had no control over household finances [41–43]. Eventually, as highlighted by 2023 WHO guidelines, children with SAM and their caregivers need psychosocial support to ensure their recovery and prevent relapses [44, 45], which could mitigate gender and gender based violence impact in Kasaï Oriental.

As demonstrated in the article by same authors [6], community health workers in DRC, sometimes being also traditional practitioners, were the first point of contact for families seeking care and expect special support [46]. To implement an intervention using such a dose reduction strategy, traditional practitioners would need to be supervised, and their integration into the health system be facilitated at all levels in the country. At the Ministry of Public Health, traditional practitioners were recognized [47–49]. There was even a directory of local traditional medicine recipes in the DRC [25]. However, in the field, traditional medicine practices were not regulated, and the recipes used were not yet standardized or codified [46]. Usually they received general training in the treatment of diarrhoea, pneumonia and malaria; the promotion and acceptance of family planning; the promotion of twelve [12] key behaviours including the community management of SAM; and the identification and referral of obstetrical complications to the health centre or directly to the first referral hospital. In addition to these generalist community health workers, some were trained for specialized programs [46, 50]. For their role in SAM management to be more effective, community health workers will need to be regularly trained in nutrition and outpatient treatment and supervised.

Study limits

The study faced strong linguistic constraints, sometimes requiring multilevel translations between Tshiluba, Lingala and French. To remedy this, for all those who did not know French, the interviews were conducted in the local language, then translated into French by teams of two translators on each occasion.

The time devoted to the survey did not allow us to explore all the elements useful to the analysis, particularly those relating to existing self-help networks and local community initiatives to support mothers and children. Furthermore, the investigations carried out focused on context-specific aspects, which limits the possibility of extrapolating results to other contexts. They did however, shed light on the points of vigilance and the socio-cultural aspects that needed to be documented in order to design a scale-up of RUTF dose reduction that is acceptable and appropriate for the populations concerned, particularly in areas of high food insecurity.

Conclusion

In our study, the reduced dose was relatively well accepted by the majority of those interviewed in this study, especially if the quantity saved enabled more children with SAM to be managed. Some, on the other hand, expressed concern about the potential negative impact of the reduced dose on children’s recovery and did not accept the reduction due to the prevalent high food insecurity at their household level. All of these findings suggested that, to be effective in Kasaï Oriental, SAM treatment interventions with a reduced dose should be accompanied by a multisectoral component covering food security, access to drinking water, capacity building for community health workers, support for traditional healers, the fight against gender-based violence and psychosocial support.

The time devoted to the survey did not allow us to explore existing self-help networks and local community initiatives to support mothers and children, but this could be the subject of a complementary study. Furthermore, the investigations carried out focused on context-specific aspects, which limits the possibility of extrapolating results to other contexts.

This study highlighted the importance of studying contextual elements in parallel with a clinical trial that could affect revisions in national protocol and/or scaling-up of the reduced-dose strategy.

Ethical issues

This study following the principles set by the Declaration of Helsinki. It was approved as a sub study within the mainstream EfRAMAS clinical trial by the Ethics Committee of the School of Public Health of the University of Kinshasa, ESP/CE/127/2021. Informed oral consent was sought from each participant of legal age, and was reconfirmed at the start of each interview. They were free to leave the study at any time and without consequence for him or her or his or her child under care for an episode of SAM. All interviews and focus group discussions were held in the language chosen by the participant, whether Lingala or Tshiluba or French, then transcribed into French before analyses.
