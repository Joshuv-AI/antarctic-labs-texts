Role of health workers and representative health worker organisations in LMIC pharmaceutical policy: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Daniel Eisenkraft Klein, Muhammad Naveed Noor, Lindsey Eiwanger, Janice Linton, Ursula Ellis, Veena Sriram
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionHealth workers (HWs) and their representative health worker organisations (RHWOs) contribute to the design of pharmaceutical policy in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), but their roles remain underappreciated. HWs and RHWOs can influence drug development, distribution, financing and access; however, which specific aspects HWs and RHWOs contribute to, and how they create change, remains insufficiently mapped within the global health literature. This protocol describes our process for conducting a scoping review to derive, describe, and classify existing literature on how HWs and RHWOs engage in pharmaceutical policy processes in LMICs.Methods and analysisThis review will follow the updated Arksey and O’Malley five-stage scoping review framework supported by iterations of methodological guidance and will be reported according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews. We will search Ovid Medline, Ovid Embase and CAB Global Health for English-language peer-reviewed literature published between 2005 and 2025. Studies must discuss HW and RHWO involvement or influence in pharmaceutical policy or describe the roles, governance contexts or strategies of HWs or RHWOs in the context of pharmaceutical policy. Two reviewers will independently screen titles, abstracts and full texts using Covidence software to determine eligibility. We will chart data using Excel and summarise the findings thematically. We will consult stakeholders in the final stage of this review to provide feedback on the results of our review and guide our findings further in terms of actionable policy implications.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval is not required for this scoping review of published literature. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, academic presentations and policy engagement with global health actors. This review will inform future research and support evidence-informed pharmaceutical policymaking in LMICs.Keywords: Health policy, Medicine, Prescriptions

Introduction

Health workers (HWs) and their representative health worker organisations (RHWOs) have become increasingly recognised for their pivotal role in shaping health policy. Acting as intermediaries between governments, industry and patient populations,1 2 these actors are central to the design and implementation of regulatory frameworks, particularly in the pharmaceutical sector.3 4 They contribute to critical decisions around drug development, distribution, financing and accessibility, ensuring that policy reflects both clinical realities and public health priorities.5

Within this overall policy landscape, HWs and RHWOs play an especially prominent role in shaping pharmaceutical policy by engaging in advocacy, regulatory discussions,3 policy implementation6, drug accessibility,4 affordability7, and safety.5 8 HWs and RHWOs now play a pivotal role in shaping the access, promotion and consumption of drugs.8

In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), these same efforts are further complicated by resource limitations and regulatory process uncertainty.9,13 Yet despite their recognised influence, the scholarly understanding of how these actors navigate pharmaceutical policymaking in LMICs remains fragmented. While recent studies have examined pharmaceutical policy in LMICs, much of the literature tends to focus on broader governance mechanisms,14,17 regulatory frameworks18,20 or industry influences,21 rather than the specific contributions of HWs and RHWOs. Despite this limited evidence base, the extent to which they mediate and participate in policy processes, the strategies they employ, and the governance contexts in which they operate remain unclear, as this body of work has not yet been systematically synthesised.

To address this gap, we will conduct a scoping review of the existing literature to develop a comprehensive understanding of how HWs and RHWOs associations engage in pharmaceutical policy processes in LMICs. The review specifically aims to (1) identify and categorise the roles these actors play in pharmaceutical policy processes, (2) situate these actor dynamics within the governance contexts in which these actors operate, (3) examine the strategies they employ to influence policy, and (4) assess how their involvement shapes policy outcomes. In doing so, this review seeks to provide a structured framework for understanding how these actors shape pharmaceutical policy landscapes and how scholarly discourse on this topic has evolved in terms of output, disciplinary focus, and research design. By collating and synthesising insights from diverse disciplines, this study aims to provide clarity on the various pathways through which these actors engage in policy processes and the implications of their involvement. Furthermore, understanding these dynamics is essential for policymakers, practitioners and researchers interested in designing more effective and equitable pharmaceutical policies that balance professional expertise, regulatory oversight and public health imperatives. This scoping review will begin in June 2025 and is expected to be completed by December 2025.

Objective

Our objective is to conduct a scoping review of existing literature on the participation of HWs and their RHWOs in pharmaceutical policy in LMICs, to explore the extent to which they contribute to shaping such policy. Building on our research questions and following Munn et al’s recommendations22 for scoping review aims, our review seeks to systematically map and synthesise existing literature by:

Identifying and categorising evidence on how HWs and their RHWOs engage in pharmaceutical policymaking processes in LMICs, including their roles, governance contexts, strategies, and policy influence.

We define pharmaceutical policy as ‘the development, distribution, financing and accessibility of pharmaceutical products’.8 Building from Sriram et al’s1 paper, we define RHWOs as:

Organisations of health workers that identify and act on the collective interests of members (both actual and claimed) regarding labour conditions, articulate collective positions on policy issues (pertaining to health systems and beyond) and engage in other activities as determined by their organisational mandates.

In turn, we define HWs as health workers who are current or potential members of such organisations and who engage—individually or collectively—in actions that reflect or advance their professional, labour or policy-related interests, particularly as they relate to pharmaceutical policy. We specifically aim to identify and categorise the roles these workers and organisations play, the governance contexts in which they operate and the strategies they employ to influence policy.

Methods

The order of our review will follow Arksey and O’Malley’s five-stage framework.23 A flowchart depicting the order of this study is attached as figure 1. We began by developing research questions focused on health worker and representative organisations involvement in pharmaceutical policy. We will next systematically search OVID Medline, Embase and Global Health databases for English-language empirical studies published between 2005 and 2025. After removing duplicates, we will screen titles, abstracts and full texts using Covidence to identify eligible studies. We will then chart the data in Excel and synthesise the findings into primary themes to guide our analysis and reporting. We will also conduct a consultation exercise (an optional sixth stage in the framework) to gather feedback from key stakeholders, validate our findings, identify any missing evidence, and enhance the relevance and applicability of our results to current pharmaceutical policy debates.

This scoping review follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews24 and the latest methodological guidance for scoping reviews. The protocol was developed in consultation with a research librarian to ensure a rigorous and comprehensive search strategy.

Stage 1: developing the search strategy

We have begun by drafting initial research questions and we will now begin exploring key themes. Our preliminary scan of the literature using Google Scholar suggested that few studies directly addressed the roles of HWs and their RHWOs in pharmaceutical policymaking in LMICs. However, emerging themes related to governance and strategic influence will guide further refinement of our questions. To ensure broad disciplinary coverage, we have identified a range of relevant databases and developed a preliminary search string using a combination of keywords and controlled vocabulary. We have added the initial search strategy as an appendix, developed in collaboration with a librarian that is a member of the research team (online supplemental appendix 1).

Stage 2: database search and supplementary strategies

A comprehensive search will be conducted across the following databases : Medline (Ovid), Embase (Ovid) and CAB Global Health (via CABI Digital Library). Grey literature will also be identified from references in order to provide additional information and context, rather than as part of the formal systematic search strategy. This approach ensures the inclusion of institutional perspectives on the role of HWs and their RHWOs in pharmaceutical policy. Our search strategy will use a combination of keywords and controlled vocabulary (eg, Medical Subject Headings terms where applicable) to identify relevant studies. The full search strategy, including additional keywords, Boolean operators, and search parameters for each database, will be documented to ensure replicability.

Stage 3: study selection and screening

Search results from all databases will be imported into Covidence by a research assistant (RA), which will be used for deduplication and screening. Duplicate records will first be removed automatically and then verified manually. The RA will be primarily responsible for managing the screening workflow within Covidence, including organising records, assigning articles to reviewers and monitoring screening progress. Title and abstract screening will be conducted by two independent reviewers for each article. The RA will also participate in screening as one of the reviewers, particularly for records requiring additional clarification or context. Articles meeting the inclusion criteria (described below) at the title and abstract stage will be retrieved in full. The RA will collect and upload full-text PDFs into Covidence and conduct the first round of full-text screening. A second reviewer (either a senior team member or the lead reviewer) will independently review these full texts. Any discrepancies will be discussed between the two reviewers, and if consensus is not reached, the lead reviewer (MNN) will serve as the final adjudicator. Throughout the process, the RA will maintain a screening log to document reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage and to ensure transparency and replicability.

Screening criteria

We will include articles which discuss the roles of the HWs and/or the RHWOs in influencing pharmaceutical policy processes in LMICs. Studies must provide insights into the governance context (including the institutions these actors work within), strategies, and/or influence on policymaking. Additionally, relevant articles should contribute to understanding how scholarship on this topic has evolved over time in terms of publication output, disciplinary focus and research design. Only academic literature will be considered (including empirical studies, systematic or scoping reviews and opinion pieces, such as commentaries and editorials). Given resource constraints, only articles published in English will be included, and the review will focus on literature published from 2005 onward.

Articles will be excluded if they do not focus on LMICs or do not specifically address the role of HWs or their RHWOs in pharmaceutical policy processes. We will not consider grey literature, including reports, policy briefs, dissertations and other non-peer-reviewed sources, because they call for separate evaluation criteria and a customised extraction process. Studies published before 2005 will be excluded, as well as studies that are not published in English.

Selection process

The selection process will be undertaken in two stages. In the first stage, two independent reviewers will evaluate the title and abstract of all peer-reviewed article titles to make a decision on its relevance using the inclusion and exclusion criteria. For reviewed article titles without abstracts, we will refer to the key sections of the article. In the second stage, two independent reviewers will read in full (and for those articles selected for full-text review) to ensure they meet the inclusion criteria. Any disagreement or discrepancies during the process will be settled by discussion, with a third reviewer making the final decision where necessary.

Stage 4: data extraction and charting

A structured data extraction template will be developed to systematically record relevant information, including bibliographic details (author(s), publication year, journal or source), study characteristics (study design, country focus, discipline), key themes related to the role of HWs and their RHWOs in pharmaceutical policy processes, governance context (including institutions involved, strategies employed and policy influence) and the evolution of research on this topic in terms of publication output, disciplinary focus, and research design. The template will be piloted on an initial set of included articles and refined accordingly.

Stage 5: data analysis

We will use Covidence to support data extraction and coding, applying both deductive and inductive approaches. Deductive coding will rely on predefined categories drawn from existing conceptual frameworks, while inductive coding will allow for the identification of emerging themes within the data. The data extraction forms will be piloted, refined, and completed independently by two reviewers, with discrepancies resolved through consensus. Extracted data will be thematically synthesised to develop a structured framework for understanding the role of HWs and their RHWOs in pharmaceutical policymaking in LMICs.

Limitations

This study is limited by our inclusion of only English-language articles, which may result in missing relevant studies published in other languages. Additionally, we excluded grey literature because it would require its own distinctive appraisal criteria and unique extraction approach, falling outside the scope of our current study. However, we acknowledge that the exclusion of grey literature may limit our insights into policy discussions outside of peer-reviewed academic sources. Finally, we recognise the potential for publication bias, whereby studies reporting successful HW or RHWO engagement in pharmaceutical policy processes may be disproportionately represented in the literature compared with studies documenting limited influence, failed policy interventions or null findings. Notwithstanding, the inclusion of social science literature could help address this bias, as such research typically focuses on examining policy processes and power dynamics rather than solely evaluating success or failure outcomes.

HWs and their RHWOs are critical, yet often overlooked, actors in health policy processes.1 As we have discussed, RHWOs exist across a spectrum of forms, scales and mandates, and they operate within complex institutional, political and social contexts. Their roles are multifaceted, extending from advocacy to norm-setting to participation in regulatory structures—functions that often blur the line between interest representation and institutional governance.1 Despite this centrality, global health scholarship has only begun to explore the role of RHWOs and the power dynamics that shape their influence on pharmaceutical policy.

This work points to the need for more systematic research on how RHWOs engage with and influence pharmaceutical policy, particularly in LMICs where much remains unknown. Key areas requiring further attention include the internal dynamics of RHWOs, financial relationships between RHWOs and industry stakeholders and their influence on policy process, the legitimacy of their representational claims, the impacts of intersectional identities on their composition and advocacy, and the varying degrees of access and authority they hold in health governance structures.
