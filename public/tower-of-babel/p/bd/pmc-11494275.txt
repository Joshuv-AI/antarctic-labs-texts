The genome sequence of the common awl robberfly, Neoitamus cyanurus (Loew, 1849)
Authors: Liam M. Crowley, Ruth Y. Akinmusola, University of Oxford and Wytham Woods Genome Acquisition Lab, Darwin Tree of Life Barcoding collective, Wellcome Sanger Institute Tree of Life Management, Samples and Laboratory team, Wellcome Sanger Institute Scientific Operations: Sequencing Operations, Wellcome Sanger Institute Tree of Life Core Informatics team, Tree of Life Core Informatics collective, Darwin Tree of Life Consortium, Jason Charamis, Manee M Manee, Frances Swift, Frances Swift, Manee M Manee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
We present a genome assembly from an individual female Neoitamus cyanurus (the common awl robberfly; Arthropoda; Insecta; Diptera; Asilidae). The genome sequence has a total length of 365.5 megabases. Most of the assembly is scaffolded into 10 chromosomal pseudomolecules. The mitochondrial genome has also been assembled and is 16.63 kilobases in length. Gene annotation of this assembly on Ensembl identified 12,046 protein-coding genes.

Background

The common awl robberfly, Neoitamus cyanurus (Loew, 1849) is a member of the Asilidae family. N. cyanurus individuals can be easily spotted by their greyish thorax, characteristic darkish elongated abdomen narrower than the thorax, darkish long legs, yellowish extreme base of the tarsi, and hindlegs with many black plus a few yellowish bristles ( Larsen & Meier, 2004; Verrall, 1909). N. cyanurus predates various insect species, such as moths, parasitic wasps, and other flies, with their extended bristly legs ( Tończyk et al., 2007; Verrall, 1909). It can be distinguished from its closest relative, N. cothurnatus, where most of the bristles on the hindlegs are yellow ( Chandler, 1975; Speight, 1988; Verrall, 1909).

N. cyanurus is distributed worldwide, with increasing records in Europe and other parts such as Iran and Turkey ( Samin et al., 2010). This robberfly has also been recorded in the UK, with peak adult fly activities in June and July ( NBN Atlas Partnership, 2024). The fly exhibits seasonal preferences in habitat composition with high abundance in forest habitats during the summer but can also be detected in soil samples during winter ( Kirse et al., 2021). In the woodlands, they are usually found on the foliage of lower tree branches and tree trunks ( Speight, 1988).

This article describes the first whole genome sequence for Neoitamus cyanurus. The sample is from a female specimen obtained from Wytham Woods, Oxfordshire, UK. The high-quality genome data could help understand the genetic diversity and evolutionary history of predation and other traits in robberflies. The genome has been generated as part of the Darwin Tree of Life Project, a collaborative effort to sequence all named eukaryotic species in the Atlantic Archipelago of Britain and Ireland.

Genome sequence report

The genome was sequenced from one female Neoitamus cyanurus ( Figure 1) collected from Wytham Woods, Oxfordshire, UK (51.77, –1.34). A total of 56-fold coverage in Pacific Biosciences single-molecule HiFi long reads was generated. Primary assembly contigs were scaffolded with chromosome conformation Hi-C data. Manual assembly curation corrected 27 missing joins or mis-joins and removed two haplotypic duplications, reducing the scaffold number by 45.16%, and decreasing the scaffold N50 by 9.90%.

Photograph of the Neoitamus cyanurus (idNeoCyan1) specimen used for genome sequencing.

The final assembly has a total length of 365.5 Mb in 16 sequence scaffolds with a scaffold N50 of 42.7 Mb ( Table 1). The snail plot in Figure 2 provides a summary of the assembly statistics, while the distribution of assembly scaffolds on GC proportion and coverage is shown in Figure 3. The cumulative assembly plot in Figure 4 shows curves for subsets of scaffolds assigned to different phyla. Most (99.96%) of the assembly sequence was assigned to 10 chromosomal-level scaffolds. Chromosome-scale scaffolds confirmed by the Hi-C data are named in order of size ( Figure 5; Table 2). The X chromosome was not determined due to insufficient evidence. While not fully phased, the assembly deposited is of one haplotype. Contigs corresponding to the second haplotype have also been deposited. The mitochondrial genome was also assembled and can be found as a contig within the multifasta file of the genome submission.

Genome annotation report

The Neoitamus cyanurus genome assembly (GCA_947538895.1) was annotated at the European Bioinformatics Institute (EBI) on Ensembl Rapid Release. The resulting annotation includes 20,929 transcribed mRNAs from 12,046 protein-coding and 1,403 non-coding genes ( Table 1; https://rapid.ensembl.org/Neoitamus_cyanurus_GCA_947538895.1/Info/Index). The average transcript length is 12,082.69. There are 1.56 coding transcripts per gene and 5.91 exons per transcript.

The estimated Quality Value (QV) of the final assembly is 67.1 with k-mer completeness of 100.0%, and the assembly has a BUSCO v5.3.2 completeness of 96.2% (single = 95.3%, duplicated = 0.9%), using the diptera_odb10 reference set ( n = 3,285).

Metadata for specimens, barcode results, spectra estimates, sequencing runs, contaminants and pre-curation assembly statistics are given at https://links.tol.sanger.ac.uk/species/468767.

Methods

Sample acquisition and nucleic acid extraction

A female Neoitamus cyanurus (specimen ID Ox001442, ToLID idNeoCyan1) was collected from Wytham Woods, Oxfordshire (biological vice-county Berkshire), UK (latitude 51.77, longitude –1.34) on 2021-05-25 by beating. The specimen was collected and identified by Liam Crowley (University of Oxford) and preserved on dry ice.

Protocols developed by the Wellcome Sanger Institute (WSI) Tree of Life Core Laboratory have been deposited on protocols.io ( Denton et al., 2023b). The workflow for high molecular weight (HMW) DNA extraction at the WSI includes a sequence of procedures: sample preparation; sample homogenisation, DNA extraction, fragmentation, and clean-up. In sample preparation, the idNeoCyan1 sample was weighed and dissected on dry ice ( Jay et al., 2023). Tissue from the head was homogenised using a PowerMasher II tissue disruptor ( Denton et al., 2023a), setting aside tissue for Hi-C sequencing. HMW DNA was extracted in the WSI Scientific Operations core using the Automated MagAttract v2 protocol ( Oatley et al., 2023). HMW DNA was sheared into an average fragment size of 12–20 kb in a Megaruptor 3 system ( Bates et al., 2023). Sheared DNA was purified by solid-phase reversible immobilisation ( Strickland et al., 2023), using AMPure PB beads to eliminate shorter fragments and concentrate the DNA. The concentration of the sheared and purified DNA was assessed using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer and Qubit Fluorometer and Qubit dsDNA High Sensitivity Assay kit. Fragment size distribution was evaluated by running the sample on the FemtoPulse system.

RNA was extracted from abdomen tissue of idNeoCyan1 in the Tree of Life Laboratory at the WSI using the RNA Extraction: Automated MagMax™ mirVana protocol ( do Amaral et al., 2023). The RNA concentration was assessed using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer and a Qubit Fluorometer using the Qubit RNA Broad-Range Assay kit. Analysis of the integrity of the RNA was done using the Agilent RNA 6000 Pico Kit and Eukaryotic Total RNA assay.

Sequencing

Pacific Biosciences HiFi circular consensus DNA sequencing libraries were constructed according to the manufacturers’ instructions. DNA sequencing was performed by the Scientific Operations core at the WSI on a Pacific Biosciences SEQUEL II (HiFi) instrument. Hi-C data were also generated from remaining head tissue of idNeoCyan1 using the Arima2 kit and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 instrument. Poly(A) RNA-Seq libraries were constructed using the NEB Ultra II RNA Library Prep kit, following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq X instrument.

Genome assembly, curation and evaluation

Assembly was carried out with Hifiasm ( Cheng et al., 2021) and haplotypic duplication was identified and removed with purge_dups ( Guan et al., 2020). The assembly was then scaffolded with Hi-C data ( Rao et al., 2014) using YaHS ( Zhou et al., 2023). The assembly was checked for contamination and corrected using the gEVAL system ( Chow et al., 2016) as described previously ( Howe et al., 2021). Manual curation was performed using gEVAL, HiGlass ( Kerpedjiev et al., 2018) and Pretext ( Harry, 2022). Scaffolds were visually inspected and corrected as described by Howe et al. (2021). Any identified contamination, missed joins, and mis-joins were corrected, and duplicate sequences were tagged and removed. The curation process is documented at https://gitlab.com/wtsi-grit/rapid-curation (article in preparation).

The mitochondrial genome was assembled using MitoHiFi ( Uliano-Silva et al., 2023), which runs MitoFinder ( Allio et al., 2020) and uses these annotations to select the final mitochondrial contig and to ensure the general quality of the sequence.

A Hi-C map for the final assembly was produced using bwa-mem2 ( Vasimuddin et al., 2019) in the Cooler file format ( Abdennur & Mirny, 2020). To assess the assembly metrics, the k-mer completeness and QV consensus quality values were calculated in Merqury ( Rhie et al., 2020). This work was done using Nextflow ( Di Tommaso et al., 2017) DSL2 pipelines “sanger-tol/readmapping” ( Surana et al., 2023a) and “sanger-tol/genomenote” ( Surana et al., 2023b). The genome was analysed within the BlobToolKit environment ( Challis et al., 2020) and BUSCO scores ( Manni et al., 2021; Simão et al., 2015) were calculated.

Genome annotation

The Ensembl Genebuild annotation system ( Aken et al., 2016) was used to generate annotation for the Neoitamus cyanurus assembly (GCA_947538895.1) in Ensembl Rapid Release at the EBI. Annotation was created primarily through alignment of transcriptomic data to the genome, with gap filling via protein-to-genome alignments of a select set of proteins from UniProt ( UniProt Consortium, 2019).

I confirm that I have read this submission and believe that I have an appropriate level of expertise to confirm that it is of an acceptable scientific standard.

The authors describe the genome of an individual female Neoitamus cyanurus caught in Wytham woods, Oxfordshire. They report a genome with a total sequence length of 365.5 megabases. 95 contings were scaffolded into 16 scaffolds using HiC of which 10 represent chromosomes. No X chromosome was assembled due to insufficient evidence. Whilst not fully phased a single haplotype assembly was created, and scaffolds associated with a secondary haplotype were also deposited. Figure 3 shows little to no contamination in the genome assembly. The scaffold N50 length was reported as 42.7 mb. A mitochondrial genome of length 16.63 kb was also assembled. The BUSCO score of 96.2% and the other assembly statistics presented in Table 1 assure that the deposited genome is high quality.

Whilst there are no quality concerns about the genome, this report could benefit from an author statement regarding how reliably the HiC data was used in the mapping as from Figure 5, there appear to be regions scaffolded into chromosomes that don’t show long-distance connectivity. Hence my partly answer for the question ‘Are the protocols appropriate and is the work technically sound’.
