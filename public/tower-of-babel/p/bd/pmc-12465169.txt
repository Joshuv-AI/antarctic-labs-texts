Magnetic resonance imaging follow-up after anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction
Authors: Hui Luo, Minzhi Zhong, Kun Feng, Zhuoran Li, Min Wang, Yuemin Weng, Zhiping Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study endeavors to systematically investigate factors influencing knee joint stability following anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), with the goal of providing quantitative imaging evidence for determining clinical rehabilitation timelines and objectively evaluating treatment efficacy.MethodA total of 31 patients undergoing ACLR were retrospectively evaluated with MRI scans at 1, 4, and 12 months postoperatively. Parameters included graft length, tibial anteroposterior displacement, tunnel position, and angulation metrics JGS, JGC, angle α, angle β, angle θ)).Clinical outcomes were assessed using the Lysholm score. Longitudinal differences in stability parameters were analyzed by repeated-measures ANOVA (p < 0.05), with ROC curves evaluating diagnostic sensitivity.ResultAmong 31 patients, 28 achieved satisfactory graft healing, while three required revision reconstruction. At 4 months postoperatively, significant reductions in joint effusion volume and soft tissue swelling were observed. Knee joint stability at 12 months was significantly superior to values at both 1- and 4-month intervals (p < 0.05). Tibial range of motion and β-angle were significantly associated with joint stability, with AUC values of 0.64 and 0.62, respectively.ConclusionMultiparametric MRI objectively evaluates post-ACLR intra-articular healing and joint stability, delivering critical imaging evidence to guide evidence-based rehabilitation planning.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, Magnetic resonance imaging, Quantitative imaging, Joint stability, Rehabilitation

Introduction

The anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) plays a pivotal role in preserving the stability and biomechanical integrity of the knee joint [1]. ACL injuries demonstrate high incidence, with post-traumatic instability substantially compromising function [2]. In the general United States population, the annual incidence of ACL tears is approximately 0.33‰, whereas in athletic populations, this rate can increase up to tenfold [3]. The primary etiological mechanisms of anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries involve torsional loading or direct physical impact during biomechanically defined pivoting maneuvers, predominantly in sports-related scenarios [4]. These injuries manifest as pain, joint instability, and functional impairment. Without intervention, progression to cartilage degeneration and subsequent osteoarthritis may occur [5, 6].

Ligament reconstruction remains the primary intervention for ACL injuries [7]. Critical determinants of postoperative efficacy include graft morphology, tunnel positioning, tension, fixation methods, and bone tunnel placement [8]. Graft stability directly impacts long-term functional outcomes [9]. Although clinical assessments (IKDC score [10], Lysholm score [11], drawer test, and Lachman test [12] are widely adopted, their reliability is compromised by operator-dependent subjectivity. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) serves as the optimal noninvasive modality for postoperative evaluation [13]. Current research predominantly examines outcomes at > 12 months post-surgery [14], with limited data on early-phase changes. This study aims to conduct early MRI examinations during postoperative rehabilitation, assess the consistency between imaging and clinical evaluation results, and provide a basis for formulating clinical rehabilitation plans [15].

Data and methods

General information

From January 2018 to December 2023, 98 patients undergoing anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) for ACL injuries were enrolled at Guangzhou Red Cross Hospital. Serial MRI scans were obtained at 1, 4, and 12 months postoperatively to measure graft length, tibial anteroposterior displacement, tunnel position, and angulation parameters. Longitudinal comparisons of the three time-point datasets were performed.

Inclusion criteria: (1) Unilateral ACL injury treated with arthroscopic reconstruction using autogenous hamstring tendon grafts (harvested from semitendinosus and gracilis tendons); (2) Full compliance with postoperative rehabilitation and complete imaging/clinical follow-up at 1, 4, and 12 months. Exclusion criteria: (1) History of ipsilateral knee surgery; (2) Contraindications to MRI (e.g., metallic implants, claustrophobia); (3) Inadequate image quality for diagnostic/analytical assessment.A total of 31 patients were ultimately enrolled.

Instruments and methods

MRI scans were performed using a Siemens 1.5T Avanto system with a dedicated knee coil. Patients were positioned supine in the feet-first orientation with the knee fixed at 135°of flexion. The scanning volume extended from the superior patellar pole to 3 cm distal to the tibial tuberosity, covering the entire femorotibial joint. Scanning sequences and parameters:

T1 (axis): Repeat time/Echo time(TR/TE) = 550/15(ms), flag angle(FA) = 90°, slice thickness = 3 mm, scan time = 1.50 min;

PD fat-set (sagittal and coronal): TR/TE = 3000/30ms, FA = 90°; Fatset; slice thickness = 3 mm, scan time = 3.20 min.

T2 (coronal): TR/TE = 3500/100ms, FA = 90°; slice thickness = 3 mm, scan time = 1.45 min.

Image observation and measurement

Images were analyzed at a workstation using MRI to evaluate bone and soft tissue edema within the joint.Multi-Planar Reconstruction (MPR) images displayed ligament fixation screws, femoral and tibial bone tunnels, and grafts. The images were independently evaluated by two radiologists: (1) A musculoskeletal imaging specialist with 5 years of experience (including 5-year medical degree, 3-year residency, 2-year clinical practice, and 5-year ACL-focused research). (2) An associate chief physician with 20 years of dedicated MSK radiology practice. To control intra-observer variability, both performed blinded measurements independently in all three planes. Inter-reader reliability was validated by intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) calculated for key parameters (tunnel lengths, angles α/ β/ θ),), with ICC > 0.90 defining excellent consistency per ESSR standards.Both performed blinded measurements in all three anatomical planes (transverse, sagittal, and coronal). Measured contents included: tunnel length, ACL length, transverse and anteroposterior tibial diameters, sagittal and coronal tibial tunnel entrance positions relative to anteroposterior and lateral tibial diameters, tunnel entrance diameter, tibial tunnel length, JGS angle (angle between the tibial tunnel and tibial plateau on the sagittal plane), JGC angle (angle between the tibial tunnel and tibial plateau on the coronal plane), α angle (angle between the femoral shaft and femoral tunnel on the coronal plane), β angle (angle between the total length of the ACL on the sagittal plane and the line connecting the femur and tibial front), θ angle (angle between the ACL and tibial plateau on the sagittal plane), and anterior-posterior tibial movement distance (d). d = 0 indicates no overlap, negative values indicate posterior tibial margin behind the femoral margin, and positive values indicate otherwise. Measurements were repeated twice, and the average was taken as the final resultIn (Fig. 1).

Clinical function score

Clinical assessments (Lachman test and Lysholm score) were performed by a blinded sports medicine specialist at 1, 4, and 12 months postoperatively. The Lysholm scale comprises eight domains (limp, support use, locking, instability, pain, swelling, stair climbing, squatting) scored 0-100. Per the original validation, scores < 70 define poor knee function. Evaluations were conducted in a temperature-controlled clinical room (22 ± 1 °C).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 24.0. Normality was assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance by Levene’s test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation ( ± s). Differences in knee stability parameters across postoperative time points were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed to evaluate the sensitivity of postoperative stability parameters [16], calculate the area under the curve (AUC), and determine diagnostic efficacy.

Results

General information

From a cohort of 98 ACLR patients, 31 met inclusion criteria with complete clinical records, diagnostic-quality MRI scans, and longitudinal follow-up per protocol (20 males, 11 females; median age: 32 years; range: 13–59 years). Injury-to-surgery intervals spanned 3 days to 4 years. Affected knees included 21 left and 10 right; 25 cases had concomitant meniscal injuries(Table 1); 15 had fractures; 3 developed graft laxity with joint instability within one year. Joint effusion and edema predominantly resolved by month 12, whereas peak exudation severity occurred at month 4. Synovitis showed progressive subsidence in later phases (Table 2; Fig. 2).

Discussion

Advances in medical technology have propelled anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction to become a mainstay procedure for restoring knee ligament integrity [18]. The refinement of bone tunnel positioning techniques and the adoption of advanced surgical methods have notably decreased postoperative complications [19].Nevertheless, the evaluation of intra-articular conditions following ACL reconstruction remains a crucial area of clinical interest, warranting continued investigation.

The incidence of knee instability following anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) ranges from 1 to 8%, necessitating rigorous postoperative rehabilitation surveillance [20]. Three patients (9.7%, 3/31) in our cohort required revision surgery at 12 months postoperatively due to critically small β angles (5°-11°) and excessive anterior tibial translation, with maximal displacement of -8 mm (indicating abnormal posterior tibial displacement). Notably, this revision rate approaches the upper limit of reported instability incidence, and all revised cases clustered exclusively in patients with β angles < 12°.β angles < 12° cause graft horizontalization, leading to repetitive impingement against the intercondylar roof during knee flexion [21]. This results in collagen micro-tears through abrasive wear; while anterior tibial translation excessively elongates the graft, causing irreversible collagen damage and ultimately resulting in permanent laxity [22].

MRI examination following anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction allows for evaluation of graft continuity and course, signal intensity, as well as periarticular structures [23]. Multi-parameter analysis indicated comparable knee stability in ACLR patients at 1-month and 4-month postoperative intervals. Joint effusion, soft tissue swelling, and synovial thickening were all alleviated 4 months post-surgery compared to 1 month post-surgery; at the 12-month postoperative follow-up, joint effusion was minimal or resolved, swelling substantially subsided, and synovial thickness had largely returned to normal levels, consistent with established literature [24, 25]. However, current rehabilitation guidelines [26, 27] primarily focus on strength/neuromuscular training, overlooking the importance of monitoring these imaging-based inflammatory markers. Incorporating MRI assessments into rehabilitation protocols could help identify patients at risk of delayed healing or persistent inflammation, enabling more personalized treatment strategies.Concurrently, the ACL graft demonstrated mild elongation, potentially attributable to increased physical activity and subtle ligamentous relaxation. Paradoxically, this elongation did not correlate with decreased clinical scores or instability, diverging from the traditional belief that graft lengthening inevitably leads to functional decline [28]. A possible explanation could be the compensatory remodeling of the surrounding soft tissues.

During the 4- to 12-month postoperative period, prosthesis rupture occurred in a subset of patients. MRI confirmation required fulfillment of all three criteria: prosthesis discontinuity, abnormal orientation of ligament stumps, and significant alterations in prosthesis thickness (thinning or thickening) [29]. Three cases in this cohort required revision surgery at 12 months after reconstruction, all being young patients with high-energy traumatic injuries. No significant correlation with preoperative surgical parameters was observed, indicating possible links to patient-related factors such as young age and injury severity.

MRI provides excellent soft tissue contrast, offering image parameters for functional recovery after ACL reconstruction. It can detect cruciate ligament injuries before and after surgery and assess cartilage and meniscus injuries [30]. Given the well-documented limitations of early postoperative assessments (e.g., 1-month Lachman test and Lysholm score) due to confounding effects of pain and inflammation, these time-point data were excluded from our final analysis. Quantitative MRI measurements demonstrated strong correlations with validated clinical outcomes at critical recovery phases (Lysholm score and Lachman test grade, p < 0.05), providing objective radiological evidence for rehabilitation protocol formulation and therapeutic decision-making. MRI is a non-invasive method to display morphological and signal changes in ACL grafts and femoral and tibial bone tunnels, crucial for ACL reconstruction efficacy and follow-up [29].

This study has several limitations: Early (1-month postoperative) assessment tools—notably the Lysholm score (where pain/swelling constitute 50% weighting) and the inherently subjective Lachman test may obscure true graft function. Furthermore, the absence of computed tomography (CT) correlation restricts comprehensive evaluation. Integrating MRI with CT would more accurately elucidate bone tunnel remodeling and graft integration. Additionally, stringent inclusion criteria yielded a limited sample size and short follow-up duration. Future work will implement integrated methodologies focusing on longitudinal (≥ 12-month) clinical-radiological assessments, incorporating refined clinical evaluations with reduced subjectivity correlated with multimodal imaging (CT/MRI) to establish objective recovery assessment benchmarks.

Conclusions

This study utilized MRI to longitudinally track ACLR patients at 1, 4, and 12 months post-surgery, comparing measurement data of various factors affecting joint stability. Imaging is an ideal choice for evaluating short-term ACL reconstruction outcomes. MRI aids early graft recovery observation and provides valuable non-invasive quantitative evaluation of graft stability post-ACLR.
