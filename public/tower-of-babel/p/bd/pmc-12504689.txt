Smart and secured investment casting process for better sustainability
Authors: Amit Sata, Divya Mobarsa, Minal Shukla, Slaheddine Jarboui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Initially, the investment casting process relied on precious alloys to create metallic components for artistic endeavours. Nowadays, it finds applications in various industrial fields such as aerospace, automotive, biomedical, chemical, and defense. The quality of investment castings is largely influenced by several process-related parameters requiring strict control. Most investment casting foundries typically monitor and record these parameters manually, without the use of specialized tools. Nevertheless, the potential for advanced technologies to enhance product quality and process control is still largely untapped. Adopting Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) technologies can provide better process monitoring and control, leading to improved overall performance. However, it is crucial to address security and privacy concerns, particularly when supplying a diverse range of products to multiple clients. This study examines the integration of IIoT and Blockchain technologies within the investment casting sector. A detailed framework for implementing these technologies in real-world investment casting foundries has been proposed and tested. The results show that these technologies enhance monitoring capabilities while ensuring adequate security and privacy. This integration is anticipated to significantly change the traditional investment casting process, often regarded as an art, into a SMART and secure system.

Introduction

The investment casting process has undergone remarkable changes since it began around 5,000 years ago. Originally seen as an art form, it was appreciated more for its craftsmanship than for any scientific or engineering aspects. A significant milestone was reached in 1875 when an industrial patent was filed for investment casting methods, which introduced the use of vulcanized rubber dies to create wax patterns. These patterns were subsequently used in the production of implants for dentistry and orthopaedics1. Literature review

An extensive review of existing literature has been attempted to analyze the Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) and its applications within the manufacturing sector. In5, Sathyan Munirathinam analyzed factors that could hinder the adoption of IoT in the manufacturing industry, addressing the architecture of IIoT and the spheres in which it is used. In particular, he pointed out two issues: data privacy and data security. In6, Brijesh Sivathanu et al. covered the adoption of IIoT by small and medium enterprises (SMEs) in the automotive component manufacturing industry in India. This was done using a structured questionnaire directed at Information Communication Technology (ICT) officers, managers, and owners of different SMEs. The results showed that organizational size does not influence the adoption of IIoT to a great extent. V. Chandra Shekhar Rao et al.7 designed an IIoT architecture that captures the advantages of 5G communication in manufacturing industries. The restructured architecture seeks to change the smart service offering of industries by designing for enhanced mobile broadband (eMBB), massive machine type communications (mMTC), ultra-reliable low latency communications (URLLC) and narrowband-IoT (NB-IoT).

Christian Arnold and others8 examined the relevance of the Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) to business models (BMs) adopted by manufacturing industries. With the help of semi-structured interviews with experts from 69 manufacturing firms, it has been concluded that managing the IIoT is the key to managing the changing requirements of the workforce in the industry. Rakshith Badarinath et al.9 argued that enabled technologies such as cloud, knowledge management systems, big data, WSNs, smart sensors, and others annexed to the IoT are defining the manufacturing sector. They noted the fundamental issues regarding the application of IoT, such as concerns about data security and privacy, incorporating with existing systems, the need for developing open platforms, real-time control, sensor equipment expenses, and regulation on ownership of data. To promote IoT-enabled manufacturing, Chen Yang et al.10 found out that determining enabling technologies and issues to be solved is of paramount importance. They placed a lot of emphasis on the need for reference architectures in cyber-physical manufacturing systems, service management and usage of manufacturing big data and catering for pervasive environments. Ray Y. Zhong et al.11 proposed a Cloud-based Internet of Things (IoT) framework as a solution for the real-time monitoring and tracking of machine status in cloud manufacturing. This was illustrated through a practical example where IoT devices were installed in a manufacturing shop to collect data by using a machine to which RFID tags had been attached and where skilled workers, machines and robots were carrying out the operation with RFID readers. The IIoT in manufacturing was analyzed by Senthil Kumar et al.12, who noted the associated difficulties, which included threats to security, interfacing, connectivity, and visibility. In their paper, they offered remedies that included disruption of activities, active industrial IoT gateways, some edge computing market prospects, perception technologies, and clustering of IoT gateways, plus improvement of terminal and network components.

Christian Arnold et al.13 undertook a qualitative study further to explain why and how the Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) is embraced, surveying 197 manufacturers in Germany. The findings showed that relative advantage was the most important predictor, whereas top management support and competitive pressure were also enablers. More so, the findings showed that the presence of environmental uncertainty would hinder the adoption of IIoT technology. D. Mourtzis et al.14 presented a technological solution for the phenomenon of big data generation in the context of the IIoT operated in the manufacturing domain using a sensory system combined with mobile devices. The changes were made based on a case study where an industry employed 150 and had 100 machine tools, which also had sensors attached to them. An IoT Application developed by the researchers was used to obtain sensor and camera data and, due to 50 companies being part of the interconnected network, provided sheer volume and variety for the use of big data in the industry. N. Santhosh et al.15 studied the impacts of IoT on production processes and found that it resulted in lower energy use, enhanced supply chains, less production waste, reduced costs, and more satisfied customers with increased revenues and reduced costs of idle time.

Furthermore, they advocated for the implementation of a distributed system and stressed the need for the protection of information. Patricia Deflorin et al.16 investigated the interaction between enablers of IIoT and its application in a manufacturing network through a case study drawn from a manufacturing facility. For the study, Data was gathered by employing 15 group-oriented interviews with employees whereby the authors were able to conclude that favorable outcomes like a centralized data hosting and analysis service for predictive maintenance and shared available capacity for IIoT and manufacturing networks, should be at the forefront of developing it and manufacturing networks. A systematic literature review of the current status of the Internet of Things (IoT) in manufacturing carried out by Tahera Kalsoom et al.17 unveiled issues including a lack of compatible workforce, intelligent systems for decision making, modelling systems, managing big data, protecting cyberspace, employment effects, and need for compatibility. The framework provided by Andrea Caputo et al.18 for additive solution manufacturing includes a comprehensive model with four levels: radical, modular, architectural and incremental innovation. The stages where a 3D printing exercise was carried out were examined, showing how an RFID tag was fixed, how the tag was connected to a source, and how the information collected was utilized. Mohd Javaid et al.19 highlighted the main advantages, elements and functions of IIoT in manufacturing, including machine monitoring, enhanced safety for workers, maintenance optimization and cost savings, real-time data gathering and control over manufacturing processes and warehousing.

The highest portion of such related research has been spread among the application of IIoT within different sub-domains of manufacturing processes, while its application in the investment casting domain is scarce. Although, it consists of a large number of parameters and is equally important for producing quality products. Data security and privacy, specifically in the collection and management of data, have always been a major challenge for the successful implementation of IIoT within manufacturing. Most confidential information comprises parameters, customer details, and specifications of the product, which are transmitted to the cloud and, consequently, exposed to some level of security breaches. Several studies focused on the potential use of blockchain technology to address issues of data security and privacy within the implementation of IIoT.

Blockchain is promising to add a new dimension to smart factories and improve information safety within the Industry 4.0 framework. It minimizes risk levels, thereby improving process safety, but critical barriers stand in its way. Decentralised transactions and knowledge co-sharing with blockchain promise the reduction of energy consumption in manufacturing processes. As part of efforts to propose a peer-to-peer network architecture based on blockchain, BCmfg has aimed to come up with solutions addressing issues around centralized industrial networks and third-party trust in manufacturing. Since 2018, five layers have comprised this architecture using IoT that enhances visibility and traceability in smart factories20. It incorporates various layers of blockchain clients using smart contracts for data exchange and on-demand services. In the work of Hu et al. (2018), digital twins have been studied for integration into the CPCM system with a method developed with CBDT to optimize computing resources and permit efficient human-operator interactions with physical machines. This method exploits the protocol MT Connect to reduce communication overhead and delay and is demonstrated to have better performance compared to previous approaches21. Additionally, Angrish et al. (2018) emphasized that capacity optimize Industry 4.0 production processes, for which a mathematical model of capacity management was offered based on Activity-Based Costing (ABC) and Time-Driven Activity-Based Costing (TDABC)22.

This model introduces decentralized networks of both manufacturing machines and computing nodes by enabling the transparency, verification, and automation of contracts based on smart contracting. The framework, known as ‘FabRec,’ supports the decentralization of ecosystems in manufacturing while identifying various uses of blockchain in manufacturing services. Lohmer et al. (2020) further explored the potential of blockchain for management and manufacturing with existing barriers through qualitative research involving semi-structured interviews of industry and academic experts. The literature review indicates a developed state in the use of IIoT and blockchain technologies to improve data security, whereas, in contrast, the application to optimize investment casting foundries remains underexplored23.

There is great potential for the exploitation of these technologies to better improve performance in such foundries. However, before implementation, a well-established framework that outlines the roadmap for integrating IIoT and blockchain technologies is necessary. A paper, therefore presents a proposed framework especially designed for modern investment casting foundries as opposed to all the other traditional ones, and it emphasizes a holistic approach towards implementation in transforming all these foundries from the traditional to smart and secure systems, carefully taking note of financial implications.

Methodology

As indicated above, the investment casting process consists of several sub-processes: pattern making and assembly of wax, formation of shells, dewaxing, preheating, melting and pouring, fettling, cleaning and finishing, and quality checking and testing. Each one of these sub-processes, as well as the overall process, requires specific equipment. These basic units are composed of a wax preparation unit; semi-automatic/automatic wax injection presses for the casting of wax pattern; ceramic slurry preparation unit which has slurry mixers, slurry drums, and rainfall sanders; autoclave unit for dewaxing and wax recycling; preheating furnaces; melting units with melting furnaces, cooling towers, crucibles, and ladles; fettling units like knock-out machine, shot blasters, welders, and cutters; and CNC machines for in-house die making and finishing. In addition, quality inspection units include equipment for spectrography, universal testing, impact testing, non-destructive testing, microscopy and measurement.

These devices generate voluminous data about the process parameters; however, most of them do not have any form of internet connectivity to receive or stream data necessary for monitoring and analytics purposes. There is, therefore, a need for a series of dedicated IIoT gateways that could collect information directly from the majority of these machines. Figure 2. IIoT implementation

The investment casting process includes multiple sub-methods and additional equipment to facilitate their execution, as previously discussed. This equipment also generates a significant amount of information, particularly about the process parameters associated with each sub-process. Acquiring this information from each piece of equipment presents a challenge because most of it lacks the fundamentals of IIoT, which involves streaming data to a server via internet technology. It is crucial to have equipment that can both gather information and stream it to a local or cloud-based server via the internet. One of the simplest solutions is to replace all existing equipment with smart equipment that can connect to the Internet for data acquisition. However, this approach is not feasible, particularly in developing nations like India. Therefore, specific IIoT gateways have been developed that connect to existing equipment and acquire data from the equipment. These IIoT gateways in turn transform the existing equipment into IIoT enabled equipment, and that will lot of financial implications on existing foundrymen. The newly developed IIoT gateways (Fig. 4) have also been deployed in the one of industrial investment casting foundries (Figs. 5 and 6) located in Rajkot (India) in order to evaluate its feasibility. It was observed that deployed IIoT devices successfully acquired the data, and streamed the data to either centralized server or decentralized server (i.e., blockchain). Other technical literature24,25 discusses the development of IIoT in detail.

Specific care has been taken for financial implication in the deployment, and these gateways are developed very cost effective. It was observed from the real industrial implementation of these IIoT gateways saved close to 60% of financial implication in comparison for procuring the existing equipment. Also, special care has been taken about handling the real time data acquisition and streaming to the server. In order to facilitate the streaming of data smoothly and with any interruption to blockchain server, acquired data through various equipment, and in turn gateways are initially streamed to local server as data is collected at various frequency (i.e., one second, 15 s, 30 min).

Executing real-time monitoring in investment casting results in significant data generation, especially for regulated parameters such as temperature and humidity, and the data can be collected as frequently as every second. Due to limitations in transaction throughput and confirmation times, it is not efficient for an investment casting system to write every data point directly on the blockchain. The use of a hybrid event-driven approach will keep the investment casting system scalable and responsive while also allowing processed data collection, leveraging the advantage of filtering and analyzing data near real-time. This entails a configuration with a fog layer or edge architecture where only relevant events and important anomalies or threshold enforcement will be written to the blockchain; thus, it is not a challenge for an investment casting system to write many peer-to-peer transactions or interactions to the blockchain while providing the same behaviors elsewhere in the complete manufacturing process. To ensure the integrity and traceability of the important aspects of the manufacturing process, the complete set of sensor data would be retained off-chain in a secure database, but their periodic signatures remotely verified on-chain would provide tamper-proof detection between the sig management and various sourced signals about the manufacturing process. This hybrid approach will allow the investment casting system to counter the processing latency on the edge or fog layer while remaining scalable for long-term continuous monitoring efforts. The next steps for this work will be to benchmark the latencies and throughput of the system based on differing parameter loads or envelopes of data to ascertain the overall industrial-scale maturity as a process control methodology or one of is process-control style monitoring data.

There were also challenges to establish connection with blockchain server directly through gateways and equipment as it was in need of uninterrupted internet as well as specific protocols for streaming the data. Those were itself another domain of research, and can be explored in futuristic industrial implementation. One of most important aspect in implementation of IIoT with existing investment casting foundry is not to limit the domain expertise available with foundry however this technology support their capabilities, and helped in enhancing monitoring capabilities.

IIoT implementation leads to acquiring various information from different equipment associated with sub-processes, streaming the acquired information to a centralized server, and further used in providing a real-time monitoring system. A typical industrial investment casting foundry, which produces an average of 40 tons of investment castings annually, houses 10–12 wax injection presses for wax pattern-making sub-processes and separate units for shell dewaxing, preheating, and melting-pouring sub-processes. These equipment usually generates over 115 process parameters.

However, the implementation of IIoT may have issues with data security and privacy. Researchers have also explored the potential use of Blockchain technology to address these issues. However, obtaining these process parameters second by second, transmitting the data to a decentralized Blockchain server, validating the streamed data, and ensuring real-time monitoring through Web 3.0 are all challenging tasks. We have adopted a novel hybrid approach to overcome these challenges, first streaming the data to a centralized server and then to a decentralized Blockchain server. Next, we will explore the integration of Blockchain into IIoT-enabled investment casting.

Integrating blockchain in IIoT-Enabled investment casting

As previously mentioned, Blockchain is a decentralized, immutable digital ledger solution that has garnered significant interest due to its ability to maintain a high level of security and privacy, all while operating without the need for specific central authorities or repositories. A hash function, a mathematical operation, secures any transaction utilizing the fundamentals of Blockchain by transforming an input numerical value into a compressed output. The hash function accepts inputs of any length, but its output is always of a fixed length. Both public and private key pairs. The community involved in recording, editing, and verifying the transaction cannot alter or change this transactional information, which is considered a block (group of transactions). We attach each such block (transactional information) to the previous chain of transactions, forming a Blockchain. Various consensus mechanisms validate a Blockchain network. Blockchain has also evolved since its inception and is capable of storing value as well as information in recent times. A digital wallet, an electronic tool that allows individual transactions, has also been associated with specific values.

Some of the most important Blockchain features in the IIoT-enabled investment casting process are interoperability (the ability of IIoT systems to work with Blockchain systems), transparency, traceability, and accountability. The initial sensor data, at different stages of the investment casting process, captures the temperature, pressure, humidity, and other parameters relevant to the study.

The data thus collected are prepared for integration in the blockchain. In this form, it follows appropriate protocols suited to the IIoT environment for secure transfer over the network. Later, data integrity and authenticity are ensured to establish the legitimacy of it while keeping at bay any possible alteration while transmission. After validation, certain prerequisites are met which call for smart contracts execution. This means that the investment cast process automatically undergoes some sections including quality control and scheduling.

Further, the valid data is collected in blocks, with every block consisting of a timestamp and a cryptographic hash and then encrypted to ensure its integrity and immutability. Finally, it involves a Proof-of-Stake (PoS) mechanism for the validation and agreement on the transactions added to the Blockchain.

After reaching a consensus, we add the block to the Blockchain. The term “Blockchain” refers to the chain of blocks formed by linking each block to its predecessor. A decentralized network distributes the Blockchain across multiple nodes. Each node maintains a copy of the Blockchain, ensuring redundancy and fault tolerance. Cryptographic keys control access to the Blockchain. Permissions determine who can read, write, or execute smart contracts on the Blockchain. Finally, for compliance purposes, one can audit Blockchain data. Analytics can provide insights into process optimization and quality improvement. Once added, the data on the Blockchain becomes immutable, providing a reliable and auditable record of all transactions and process parameters.

In estimation, each transaction (data acquired from each piece of equipment at the interval of a second and streamed to the Blockchain server) directly executed without a cloud-based server costs very high and is in the range of 0.03 ETH (Fig. 8). If an investment casting foundry does not use a cloud-based server at the intermediate level, this transaction will be significantly more expensive. This is because the foundry must acquire up to 115 process parameters at a frequency of one second, with an average production of 40 tons of castings per month. This will have huge financial implications, particularly when integrating Blockchain with IIoT-enabled investment casting, and there is a pressing need to overcome this challenge. Future research will be conducted that will primarily focused on on producing implementation models specifically for SMEs that require minimal technical effort and on investigating support modalities to alleviate the financial burden of digital transformation for traditional foundries. It will also be proposed to develop specific blockchain for IIoT applications with minimal and affordable financial implications in future.

Discussion

The investment casting process is of immense importance for producing parts utilized in high-value segments of the economy. Even though it is very crucial, much investment casting is still considered an art with little exploration of advanced technologies to improve its performance. The potential integration of IIoT technology presents an opportunity for improvement in the casting process since it is capable of carrying multiple parameters that enhance the quality of end products. Most equipment in the casting process cannot stream data to servers through internet technology, which forms the heart of data analytics and IIoT implementation.

With this consideration, retrofitting with IIoT gateways has evolved that can transform existing equipment into smart devices with the potential to acquire data through internet connectivity. This has been applied very successfully to real industrial scenarios and transformed the traditional technique of investment casting into smart investment casting. However, functionality security for the IIoT is still a prime concern. Recently, proposed studies include applying Blockchain technology into IIoT-enabled investment casting to improve security and privacy.

A hybrid framework has been developed, utilizing Blockchain fundamentals based on Web 3.0 principles, to achieve safe and private monitoring. However, the integration of process monitoring with added safety of data using such a combination has proven effective but still poses challenges in instances concerning latency due to limiting factors in current internet infrastructure, the selection of proper platforms, management of digital wallets, and financial repercussions. Further research is necessary to overcome these obstacles and fully realize the benefits of these disruptive technologies in industrial applications.
