Effects of door‐to‐tolvaptan time on short‐term clinical outcome in patients with acute heart failure
Authors: Ryohei Nomura, Tetsuji Morishita, Yusuke Sato, Daisetu Aoyama, Tomohiro Shimizu, Hiroyasu Uzui, Akira Nakano, Hiroshi Tada
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsWe investigated the effects of door‐to‐tolvaptan (D2T) time on short‐term urine volume and in‐hospital clinical outcomes in patients with acute heart failure (AHF).Methods and resultsPatients with AHF, treated with tolvaptan at two hospitals, were enrolled in this retrospective observational study. The D2T time was defined as the time elapsed from the arrival of a patient at a participating hospital to the first administration of tolvaptan. The group with the D2T time within 6 h was defined as the ‘early group’. The primary outcome was 48‐h urine volume. The secondary outcomes were in‐hospital death, length of hospital stay, and worsening renal function (WRF) incidence. A restricted cubic spline model was used to evaluate the presence of a nonlinear association between the D2T time and 48‐h urine volume and the odds ratio of WRF incidence. Our study included a total of 138 patients with AHF who were started on tolvaptan after hospitalization. The median D2T time was 5.3 h (interquartile range: 3.0–31.9 h). Seventy‐four patients (53.6%) were classified to be in the early group. Baseline characteristics were similar in the two groups: mean age (85.4 ± 9.6 years vs. 84.5 ± 9.5 years; P = 0.59) and male sex (n = 22 [33.3%] vs. n = 29 [46%]; P = 0.16), except that patients in the early group had higher systolic blood pressure than those in the delayed group (138.2 ± 22.9 vs. 125.7 ± 21.7; P = 0.001). The initial tolvaptan dose in the delayed group was much lower than that in the early group (7.5 [7.5, 7.5] vs. 7.5 [5.6, 7.5] mg; P = 0.01). Total urine volume in 48 h did not differ in the early and delayed groups (4113 ± 1758 mL vs. 4201 ± 1893 mL; P = 0.80). The relationship between D2T time and total urine volume within 48 h increased slightly, with a peak at a D2T time of 15 h, and gradually decreased, thereafter. In‐hospital death and the length of hospital stay did not differ significantly between the two groups (n = 1, 1.3% vs. n = 4, 6.3%; P = 0.18, and 5.0 [12.0, 30.0] vs. 22.0 [14.5, 30.0] days; P = 0.17, respectively). Notably, the restricted cubic spline model for the odds ratio of WRF incidence increased as the D2T time was delayed (P for effect<0.01).ConclusionsThe shorter D2T time did not affect the short‐term urine volume and in‐hospital outcomes but reduced the risk of WRF incidence in patients with AHF.Keywords: Acute heart failure, Door‐to‐diuretic time, Tolvaptan, Worsening renal function

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) has become a major public health and economic issue in developed countries. Among other factors, the increase in HF incidence is also attributable to an aging society. 1 , 2 The management of acute HF (AHF) is particularly important in improving patient outcomes. 3 Loop diuretics have been the mainstay in managing AHF for decades. Time‐sensitive treatment with intravenous diuretics has been reported to improve clinical outcomes in patients with AHF. 4

The oral vasopressin‐2 receptor antagonist, tolvaptan, inhibits vasopressin‐mediated water reabsorption in the distal nephrons and produces free water diuresis. In Japan, this drug has been used, together with loop diuretics, in volume overload HF patients since 2010. 5 Over the past few years, several studies have been conducted to investigate the favourable effects of tolvaptan on clinical outcomes in HF patients under various situations. 6 , 7 Early initiation of tolvaptan, within a couple of days, provided immediate decongestion and better clinical outcomes, such as prevention of worsening renal function (WRF) and shortening of the length of hospital stay. 8 , 9 , 10

Regarding time‐sensitive treatment of AHF, door‐to‐furosemide (D2F) time has been reported to affect clinical outcomes. However, the relevance of door‐to‐tolvaptan (D2T) time in AHF patients remains unknown. 4 We expected that the earlier use of tolvaptan would be associated with a better urine volume and would lead to better clinical outcomes. This study was, therefore, aimed at examining the effects of the D2T time on short‐term urine volume and in‐hospital outcomes. The primary outcome was total urine volume within 48 h, and the secondary endpoints included the pre‐defined effectiveness and safety measures of in‐hospital management of AHF.

Methods

Study subjects and design

This retrospective observational study employed the registry database at the Department of Cardiovascular Medicine of the University of Fukui Hospital and Hikone Municipal Hospital. A total of 626 consecutive patients who were hospitalized for AHF and treated with tolvaptan between January 2014 and December 2020 were considered. Of these, 488 patients were excluded in view of the following exclusion criteria: current tolvaptan use on admission, missing D2T time, serious general condition, malignancy, tracheal intubation, or haemodialysis. Finally, 138 patients with AHF were started on tolvaptan after hospitalization (Figure 1 ). These participants were categorized into an ‘early group’ (n = 75) and a ‘delayed group’ (n = 63) based on the D2T time. The D2T time was defined as the time elapsed from the arrival of a patient at a participating hospital to the first administration of tolvaptan. The early and delayed groups were distinguished by the D2T time being within 6 h in the former and more than 6 h in the latter. 11 AHF was diagnosed based on symptoms and signs of HF, laboratory data, chest X‐ray, and echocardiography findings. 1 The treatment for AHF, including tolvaptan use, depended on the attending physician's discretion at each hospital. Tolvaptan was administered to all the participants adding to standard HF therapy, such as with beta‐blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, and angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors (ACE‐Is) or angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs). Angiotensin receptor neprilysin inhibitors and sodium‐glucose cotransporter two inhibitors were unavailable for HF treatment in Japan during the study period.

This investigation conformed to the principles outlined in the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki and later amendments. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of each participating hospital, and written informed consent was waived, considering the retrospective nature of the study. This trial was registered with the University Hospital Medical Information Network (UMIN) Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN 000023840).

Definitions

The severity of HF on admission was assessed using the New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional classes. HF was categorized as ‘HF preserved ejection fraction’ (HFpEF) (left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF] ≥ 50%), ‘HF mildly reduced EF’ (HFmrEF) (LVEF 40–49%), and ‘HF reduced EF’ (HFrEF) (LVEF <40%). 1 The doses of 60 mg azosemide and 10‐mg torasemide were equivalent to 40 mg furosemide. 7 The D2F time was defined as the time from arrival of a patient at a participating hospital to the first administration of furosemide. 4

Outcome measurements

Patients' characteristics and outcome measurements were assessed and compared between the two groups. The primary outcome was total urine volume within 48 h of admission. Secondary effectiveness outcomes were changes in body weight within 7 days of admission, in‐hospital death, length of hospital stay, duration of non‐invasive positive pressure ventilation (NPPV) use, and duration of O2 use. We also assessed the association between days from the admission and the 24‐h urine volume. Secondary safety outcomes were the incidence of WRF and hypernatraemia (Na ≥ 150 mEq/L) within 7 days of admission. We also conducted a sensitive analysis for WRF within 14 days. WRF was defined as an absolute increase in serum creatinine ≥0.3 mg/dL within 7 days of admission. 12

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, medians for non‐normally distributed parameters, and mean ± SD for continuous variables. The unpaired Student's t‐test or the Mann–Whitney U test was performed to compare continuous measurements between the two groups. Categorical variables were compared using the χ 2 test or Fisher's exact test. Restricted cube spline with knot 3 s was applied to evaluate the association of the D2T time with total urine volume within 48 h, the length of hospital stay, and the odds ratio of WRF incidence within 7 days. These analyses were adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, NYHA classification on admission, LVEF, history of AHF admission, coronary artery disease, atrial fibrillation, hypertension, diabetes, initial dose of tolvaptan, dose of intravenous furosemide, use of beta‐blockers, ACE‐Is or ARBs, and estimated glomerular filtration rate. To correct for heterogeneous variance and correlated responses from repeatedly measured values, multivariate nonlinear regression with the Huber–White robust sandwich estimator of variance–covariance matrix was used. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using the R version 4.1.3 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient characteristics

A comparison of the baseline characteristics of the early and delayed groups is presented in Table 1 . These characteristics were similar in the two groups, except that patients in the early group had higher systolic blood pressure than those in the delayed group. Likewise, no significant differences were observed in the severity of HF and in the history of AHF, coronary artery disease, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and atrial fibrillation between the two groups. The proportions of HFpEF, HFmrEF, and HFrEF were also similar in both the groups. Regarding the medication administered on admission, treatment with calcium channel blockers was more common in the early group. There were no significant differences in the use of loop diuretics and mineral receptor blockers and in the dose of furosemide on admission between the groups. Laboratory data, such as values of serum creatinine, haemoglobin, albumin, serum sodium, and brain natriuretic peptide, were similar in the two groups.

With regard to the primary outcome, the total urine volume within 48 h did not differ significantly between the patients in the early and delayed groups (4141.8 ± 1749.7 vs. 4201.3 ± 1914.1 mL; P = 0.87, Table 3 ). Secondary outcomes

Among the secondary effectiveness outcomes, the mean change in body weight within 7 days of hospitalization did not differ between the early and delayed groups (−3.7 ± 2.6 vs. −3.0 ± 2.8 kg, P = 0.19; Table 3 ). The data for in‐hospital outcomes are also shown in Table 3 . No significant differences were observed in the proportion of in‐hospital deaths (n = 1, 1.3% vs. n = 4, 6.3%; P = 0.18), length of hospital stay (15.0 [12.0, 30.0] vs. 22.0 [14.5, 30.0] days, P = 0.17), duration of NPPV use (2.0 [2.0, 3.5] vs. 2.0 [1.0, 2.0] days, P = 0.42), and duration of O2 use (6.0 [4.0, 11.3] vs. 7.0 [4.0, 12.8] days, P = 0.77) in the two groups. In addition, the length of hospital stay was not affected by a shorter D2T time (Figure 3 A ). With regard to the secondary safety outcomes, incidences of WRF and hypernatraemia did not differ between patients in the early and delayed groups (n = 20, 26.7% vs. n = 26, 41.3%; P = 0.1, and n = 5, 6.7% vs. n = 4, 6.3%; P = 1, respectively). Notably, the adjusted odds ratio of WRF incidence increased as the D2T time was delayed (P for effect <0.01) (Figure 3 B ).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study wherein the effects of the D2T time on short‐term urine volume and in‐hospital outcomes in patients with AHF have been assessed. The main findings in this study were as follows: first, an early D2T time, such as 6 h, was not related to increased total urine volume within 48 h of admission in patients with AHF. Indeed, the 48‐h total urine volume peaked at a D2T time of 15 h, and decreased, thereafter; second, in‐hospital outcomes were not significantly effected by the D2T time; third, delayed D2T time was associated with an increase in the risk of WRF incidence.

Effect of the door‐to‐tolvaptan time on the short‐term urine volume

The AHF treatment requires the early use of diuretics to obtain immediate decongestion, especially in patients with fluid overload and pulmonary congestion. 1 Moreover, a shorter door‐to‐diuretic time provides a better in‐hospital outcome in AHF patients. 4 The administration of tolvaptan in the very early phase offers possibly better diuretic response in patients with AHF. 11 Therefore, we postulated that a much shorter time to initiate tolvaptan would lead to a larger urine volume in AHF patients. Our findings indicate that the short‐term urine volume increased slightly, with a peak at a D2T time of 15 h, which apparently supports the importance of immediate decongestion with intravenous diuretics in the very‐acute phase of AHF treatment. In patients with congestive HF, oral drug absorption is restricted due to decreased gastric empty time and intestinal motility, reduced intestinal blood flow, and lower hepatic drug‐metabolizing activity. 13 An intravenous aquaretic drug (tolvaptan sodium phosphate) has been newly available in Japan since 2022. In a previous study, an intravenous injection of 16 mg tolvaptan sodium phosphate was demonstrated to be equally effective and safe as the oral administration of 15 mg tolvaptan in patients with congestive HF. 14 However, these results were not obtained in congestive HF patients in the very acute phase. Additionally, the maximum drug concentration‐time of tolvaptan in patients with intravenous injection was much earlier than that in the case of oral administration (median T max 1.52 vs. 4.07 h). 15 Thus, intravenous administration of tolvaptan in the acute phase may be one of the treatment options for congestive HF patients, regardless of whether these patients can take drugs orally or not.

In‐hospital outcomes

Previous studies have indicated that the early use of tolvaptan within 3 days of hospitalization was associated with a shortened length of hospital stay and improved in‐hospital outcomes. 9 , 10 On the contrary, our results indicate that early initiation of tolvaptan within 6 h of admission was not related to improved in‐hospital outcomes; moreover, the D2T time did not affect the length of hospital stay in our study (Figure 3 A ). This dissimilarity possibly arises from the difference in the D2T time between the present and previous studies. Almost all patients in the present study were initiated on tolvaptan within 3 days of hospitalization; hence, they were categorized as the early‐use group patients in the previous studies. In fact, the median length of hospital stay in our study was about 20 days, similar to that in the early groups in previous studies. 9 , 10 Therefore, we speculated that the D2T time within 6 h did not improve in‐hospital outcomes in the present study.

Association between the door‐to‐tolvaptan time and worsening renal function incidence

The results of this study highlight the fact that much shorter D2T time is associated with a lower risk of WRF incidence. In previous randomized controlled studies, early initiation of tolvaptan within 24 h reduced the WRF incidence in AHF patients. 8 , 16 For the first time, we analysed the effect of a much shorter D2T time, such as within 6 h, on the risk of WRF. WRF is a predictor of prolonged length of hospital stay and heightened mortality in patients with AHF. 12 , 17 However, the D2T time was not related to the length of hospital stay and in‐hospital mortality in our study. In a previous study, the differences in clinical outcomes between studies on the D2F time were suggested to be due to the heterogeneity in AHF unlike that in acute myocardial infarction. 18 Thus, in future studies, attempts should be made to establish the favourable effects of much earlier tolvaptan initiation on in‐hospital outcomes.

Clinical implications

The latest global guidelines indicate that the mainstay of the diuretic strategy is the administration of loop diuretics in the very acute phase (within 6 h) of AHF patients with volume overload. 1 , 2 Patients with poor response to loop diuretics require combination therapy, including tolvaptan, in the re‐assessment phase within 24 h of admission. 3 In the present study, tolvaptan initiation in the very acute phase reduced the risk of WRF incidence. Thus, our work suggests that tolvaptan initiation within 6 h of hospitalization may improve in‐hospital outcomes in AHF patients.

Study limitations

Our study has several limitations that need to be acknowledged. Firstly, due to its retrospective observational design, we cannot establish causal relationships between tolvaptan administration timing and clinical outcomes. The study is focused on exploring associations rather than determining causation. Secondly, the definition of ‘early’ and ‘delayed’ tolvaptan administration is based on fixed time points, which may not fully capture the variability in acute decompensated heart failure presentations. We have attempted to address this limitation by performing a regression analysis using a restricted cubic spline to interpret the relationship continuously. Additionally, our study is conducted on a specific patient population, mainly comprising patients admitted through the emergency room, which limits the generalizability of our findings to other settings. Fourth, while we strive to control for potential confounders, the lack of a control group further adds to the complexity of drawing definitive conclusions. Lastly, we only investigated the effect of the D2T time on in‐hospital outcomes in AHF patients. The association between the D2T time and in‐hospital outcomes was adjusted by several medical factors in this study. However, the heterogeneity of regional, social, and economic differences may affect the length of hospital stay. Additionally, tolvaptan was continued after discharge on the part of participants. Therefore, we could not assess the impact of a shorter D2T time on long‐term outcomes in AHF patients.

Despite these limitations, our study provides valuable insights into the association between tolvaptan administration timing and short‐term clinical indicators in real‐world clinical practice, serving as a basis for further investigations utilizing more robust causal inference methods.

Conclusions

In this study, a short D2T time, within 6 h of admission, did not affect the 48‐h urine volume and in‐hospital outcomes in patients with AHF. However, the shorter the D2T time was, the lower was the WRF incidence risk. Based on our results, we suggest that the administration of tolvaptan should be considered in the very acute phase to improve in‐hospital outcomes in AHF patients with fluid overload.
