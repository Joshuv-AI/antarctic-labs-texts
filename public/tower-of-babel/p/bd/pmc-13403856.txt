Neutrophil extracellular traps promote macrophage activation through Toll-like receptor engagement and PKA and NF-kB signaling pathways

ABSTRACT

Background

Neutrophils and macrophages are key cells in the innate immune response to inflammatory disorders and infectious agents, and they also contribute to maintaining homeostasis under physiological conditions. Neutrophils can release extracellular traps, known as neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), characterized by chromatin exteriorization associated with cytoplasmic and granule proteins, such as neutrophil elastase, HMGB1, myeloperoxidase, and others [1–3]. These structures are typically released upon neutrophil activation by a variety of factors, including mitogens, synthetic products, endogenous molecules, and pathogens. Initially, NETs were described as able to restrain, inactivate, and, concomitantly, prevent dissemination of microorganisms [1, 4–6]. For instance, our group and others described that NETs can restrict HIV-1 infection, both by direct inactivation of viral particles or reducing the cDNA integration into macrophages genome and viral production [4, 7]. Afterward, several studies reported that NETs can also interact with other cells and modulate their functioning, for example, the microRNAs carried by NETs can regulate TNF-α production by macrophages [8–14]. In physiological conditions, NETs are eliminated by DNases present in the milieu or by macrophages that engulf and digest them into lysosomes [15, 16]. However, although NETs are essential for host protection, when neutrophil swarming is exacerbated and NET release is uncontrolled, these structures can be harmful. For example, their excessive or dysregulated formation has been implicated in pathological processes, such as tissue damage, thrombosis, autoimmune and inflammatory disorders, and even facilitating tumor metastasis [3, 17–23]. In SARS-CoV-2/COVID-19 infection NETs can, for example, exacerbate the cytokine storm, systemic inflammation, and local damage [23–25].

Macrophages are a heterogeneous and plastic group of mononuclear phagocytes that play crucial roles in immunity and tissue homeostasis. These cells are widely distributed throughout the tissues and organs, where they are known as resident macrophages, originated during the embryonic period in the yolk sac and from hematopoietic stem cells [26–29]. Besides resident macrophages, tissues also harbor macrophages derived from inflammatory monocytes, which are recruited from the circulation in response to injury or infection, and differentiate according to local stimuli [28, 30, 31]. Monocyte-derived macrophages can phagocytose pathogens and infected or apoptotic cells, produce soluble mediators, such as cytokines and chemokines, and contribute to adaptive immunity by presenting antigens to lymphocytes. They can interact with neutrophils that infiltrate tissues during physiological, inflammatory, or infectious conditions, and their functioning may be modulated towards an inflammatory or pro-resolving profile [32]. Several studies have demonstrated the presence of NETs in tissues and their interaction with macrophages [1, 10, 14, 15, 21, 22, 33–35]. A silent interaction between these two innate immune components has been described, suggesting that macrophages can digest NETs in lysosomes without releasing inflammatory mediators [15]. In contrast, other authors have described macrophage activation in response to NETs in the context of sepsis, atherosclerosis, and autoimmune diseases, usually aggravating the inflammation and leading to a worse prognosis [33, 36, 37]. Macrophage activation is regularly triggered by a repertoire of pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), including Toll-like receptors (TLRs), which can recognize nucleic acids and a wide range of proteins, some of them also present in NETs [38–40]. TLR2 and TLR4, for example, have been described to interact with some of these proteins, such as, for example, elastase, HMGB1 and S100 family [40–46]. Despite accumulating evidence of NET-macrophage interactions, the molecular mechanisms by which NETs influence macrophage activation still require more detailed studies. Given that NETs carry various proteins recognized by TLR2 and TLR4, we hypothesize that these structures modulate macrophage function through these two receptors and other signaling pathways. To explore this hypothesis, we investigated how human primary monocyte-derived macrophages (hereinafter nominated simply as macrophages) respond to NET exposure in vitro, focusing on key receptors and intracellular mediators involved in this interaction.

Methods

Cell isolation and culture

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were obtained from buffy coats of healthy donors by density gradient centrifugation (Ficoll-Paque Premium, GE Healthcare Life Sciences). Human monocyte-derived macrophages were differentiated from PBMCs culture and monocyte adhesion onto plastic plates in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Nova Biotecnologia) supplemented with 5% (vol/vol) heat-inactivated human AB serum (Sigma) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin-amphotericin B (PSA, Sigma) for 7 to 10 days. PBMCs were plated at densities of 1 × 106 cells in 96-, 2 × 106 cells in 48-, 4 × 106 cells in 24- and 1.6 × 107 cells in 6-well plastic culture plates according to the experiment. After this period, non-adherent cells were removed by washing with Phosphate Saline Buffer (PBS, Nova Biotecnologia), and macrophages were maintained in culture, as we previously described [7, 47].

Neutrophil isolation and induction of NET formation

Neutrophils were obtained from buffy coats of healthy donors by density gradient centrifugation followed by erythrocyte sedimentation with 3% Dextran (Sigma). Remaining red blood cells were lysed by hypotonic solution (155 mM NH4Cl, 12 mM NaHCO3, 0.1 mM EDTA), and neutrophil purity was > 95% as determined by flow cytometry analysis using anti-CD16 (BD Biosciences). For NET formation, neutrophils were cultured (1 × 107/mL) in RPMI without serum and stimulated with IL-8 (50 ng/mL, PeproTech) or with beta-propiolactone (BPL, Selleckchem)-inactivated viruses SARS-CoV-2 (inSARS; MOI 0.1) or HIV-1 (inHIV, virus input of 5 ng/mL of p24 protein) for 3 h at 37 °C. The viral strains used were SARS-CoV-2 (human/RJ01/2020; GenBank accession number MT710714.1) and HIV-1 BaL strain subtype B (donated by the AIDS Research and Reference Reagent Program, NIH, Bethesda, MD). Of note, viral expansions and inactivation were performed as described elsewhere [7, 48, 49]. The supernatants containing NETs were removed through extensive pipetting, centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min to remove residual neutrophils, and centrifuged again at 1000 × g to remove cell debris. NET-DNA content was measured by Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay (Invitrogen), as described [7]. In some assays, NETs were further centrifuged at 18.000 × g for 1 h before DNA-NET quantification, as previously described [50], and this preparation is identified as isolated NETs. All the recovered NETs were stored at -80 °C until further use. For studies of macrophage interaction and activation, NETs were pooled from 4 to 5 individual NETs. Of note, as a control for the possible effect of residual IL-8 on the NET-containing supernatants (carry-over, IL-8CO), macrophages were exposed to the equivalent residual IL-8 from the input used to induce NET formation (IL-8NET), with final concentration based on the dilution factor (DF) to reach the final NET concentration for macrophage treatment. To this, we applied the following formula: [IL-8CO] = [IL-8NET]/DF. For DNA digestion or protein degradation, NETs were incubated with DNase I (10 U/mL; Promega) or proteinase K (2.5 µg/mL; Sigma) for 30 min at 37 °C before macrophage treatment.

Quantification of Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS)

Macrophages were treated with NETs at different concentrations and with the Dihydrorhodamine 123 probe (DHR, Invitrogen; final concentration of 1.2 µM). NETs and the probe were added at the same time. The cells were incubated for 3 h at 37 °C in the SpectraMax Paradigm Multi-Mode Detection Platform (Molecular Devices) with SoftMax Pro 6.2 software. Fluorescence was detected at an excitation/emission wavelength of 500/570 nm, with measurements taken at 15-minute intervals. ROS production is shown as the area under the curve (AUC) normalized by the untreated cells, used as a control.

Analysis NF-κB activation

Macrophages were exposed to NETs for 2–8 h, and then the culture medium was removed, and the cells were subsequently lysed. Cell lysates were used to measure NF-κB p65 phosphorylation by ELISA (Invitrogen), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The data are represented as the ratio between phosphorylated and total protein, normalized to the untreated cells, used as a control.

Quantification of inflammatory mediators by ELISA

The production of inflammatory mediators was quantified in cell-free supernatants collected from macrophages treated with different concentrations of NETs and kept in culture under standard conditions for 24 h. In some assays, macrophages were treated with the pharmacological inhibitors of NF-κB (10 µM; Bay 11-7082, Sigma), Toll-like receptor 2 (100 µM; C29, Invivogen), Toll-like receptor 4 (25 µM; TAK-242, Cell Signaling), Protein kinase A (50 µM; H89, Tocris), Protein kinase C (50 µM; Go6983, Tocris) or Cytochalasin D (10 µg/mL; Sigma) 30 min prior to NET addition. The ELISAs for TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, MIP-1α, MIP-1β, RANTES, and CXCL-10 were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (R&D Systems).

Analysis of macrophage viability

The viability of macrophages treated with NETs was assessed by lactate dehydrogenase (CyQUANT LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit, Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s protocol, and by XTT method using 2,3-Bis-(2-Methoxy-4-Nitro-5-Sulfophenyl)-2 H-Tetrazolium-5-Carboxanilide. The viability of macrophages was not compromised by NETs (Supplementary Figs. 2 A-C). Cell viability was also confirmed through Annexin-V binding and Propidium Iodide (PI) staining by flow cytometry (FACS Celesta), and cells were considered viable when they were negative for both Annexin-V and PI staining. Viability of cells exposed to the pharmacological inhibitors, analyzed by LDH method, remained above 90% (Supplementary Figs. 2D-H).

Imaging of NETs by fluorescence microscopy

Neutrophils were plated on Permanox chamber slides (Nunc™ Lab-Tek™) and stimulated with inHIV or inSARS for 3 h. Then, the cultures were fixed with 4% formaldehyde (Merck) for 30 min and rinsed 3 times with PBS. Next, the samples were blocked with human serum for 1 h and then incubated overnight with a polyclonal anti-elastase (Calbiochem) or monoclonal anti-LL-37 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) antibodies. Samples were washed and incubated with anti-rabbit or anti-mouse secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa 546 (Life Technologies) for 2 h, then rinsed twice with PBS and mounted with ProLong Diamond antifade containing DAPI (Life Technologies). Slides were examined using a confocal microscope (Leica DMi8) and LasX software. The brightness, contrast, and color of digital images were adjusted using the ImageJ Fiji program.

Transcriptomic profiling by RNA-seq of macrophages

Macrophages were treated for 24 h with IL-8-induced NETs (50 ng/mL) or with the estimated concentration of IL-8 carried over by IL-8-stimulated NETs, and the total RNA was extracted with the RNeasy® Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen) with RLT buffer supplemented with β-mercaptoethanol, including an on-column DNase treatment performed with the RNase-Free DNase Set (Qiagen), following the manufacturer’s protocols. The concentration of total RNA per sample was initially assessed using a NanoDrop® 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). RNA quality and integrity were evaluated using the High Sensitivity RNA ScreenTape Kit on the TapeStation 4200 system (Agilent Technologies), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Only samples with an RNA Integrity Number (RIN) greater than 8.0 were selected for downstream analysis. Library preparation was performed using poly-A selection with the Illumina® Stranded mRNA Prep Kit (Illumina), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Amplification was conducted using a LightCycler® qPCR System (Roche), and library quality was assessed by analyzing the distribution of cDNA fragment sizes using the TapeStation 4200 system (Agilent Technologies). Multiplexed libraries were then sequenced on the NextSeq 2000 system (Illumina) using a 2 × 100 bp paired-end configuration, with the NextSeq 2000 P3 Kit (Illumina). All steps related to RNA integrity assessment, library preparation, and quantification, and sequencing were performed at the Next-Generation Sequencing Platform (RPT01J) of the Oswaldo Cruz Institute (IOC)/FIOCRUZ, Rio de Janeiro, Brazil.

After sequencing, adapters and low-quality bases (< Q20) were trimmed using the BBduk tool (https://sourceforge.net/projects/bbmap), and read quality was monitored using FastQC v0.11.7 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc). Reads were aligned to the human reference genome GRCh38 using STAR v2.7.10b [51].

RNA sequencing data were processed and analyzed using a standardized bioinformatics pipeline to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and enrichments in biological pathways following stimulation of macrophages and neutrophils. Raw read counts were generated using feature Counts [52] (v2.0.1) against Ensembl GRCh38 annotations (Genome Reference Consortium Human Build 38, INSDC Assembly GCA_000001405.29, December 2013; accessed: August 2022). Low-expression genes were filtered prior to normalization: in the first experiment, genes with total counts across all samples less than 4 were removed, while in the second experiment, the threshold was set at 6 to account for increased sample size and variability. Normalization was performed using the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) method implemented in the edgeR package [51], which adjusts for differences in library size and RNA composition between samples.

To model the effects of experimental conditions while accounting for donor-specific variability, a linear mixed-effects model was fitted using the ‘dream’ function from the variance Partition package. This approach integrates the flexibility of linear mixed models with the empirical Bayes moderation framework commonly used in microarray analysis, enabling robust inference in small-sample settings. Donor identity was included as a random effect to account for repeated measures from the same individual, and the model was specified as ~ condition + (1|donor). The normalized count data were then transformed using ‘voomWithDreamWeights’, which applies precision weights to stabilize variance estimates and improve detection of true differential expression.

Differential expression analysis was carried out within the limma-voom framework. Genes were classified as differentially expressed if they satisfied two criteria: an adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg correction – False Discovery Rate, FDR) and fold change expression in log2 (log2FC) ≥ 1 [53]. These thresholds ensured DEG identification while controlling multiple hypothesis testing. All analyses were implemented using the edgeR, limma, and variance Partition packages in R (v4.2.1), tools that have been widely adopted for their accuracy and reproducibility in transcriptomic studies.

To interpret the functional implications of DEGs, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted using the WEB-based Gene SeT AnaLysis Toolkit 2024 (WebGestalt) R package [54]. GO terms were mapped to genes via ENSEMBL identifiers using the org.Hs.eg.db annotation database (version 2.1), which incorporates NCBI, GO, and Ensembl data updated through early 2025. Overrepresentation analysis was performed separately for biological process (BP), molecular function (MF), and cellular component (CC) ontologies with adjusted p-value (FDR) cutoff of 0.05 and conditional testing enabled to account for the hierarchical dependencies and to avoid redundancies among GO terms.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance was evaluated using paired t test or one-way ANOVA with Sidak’s multiple comparisons test using the software GraphPad Prism version 10.1.0 for Windows. Data are reported as mean ± SEM (Standard Error of the Mean) and considered statistically significant when p ≤ 0.05.

Ethics statement

Experimental procedures involving cells from healthy donors were performed with blood samples obtained after written informed consent and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Oswaldo Cruz Institute (IOC)/FIOCRUZ (Rio de Janeiro, RJ, Brazil) under the number 49971421.8.0000.5248. All experiments were performed following the relevant guidelines and regulations of Research Ethics Committee cited above.

Results

IL-8-induced NETs promote transcriptional changes in macrophages

We have previously reported that NETs induced by IL-8 interact with HIV-1-infected macrophages, restraining viral replication and inducing macrophage production of β-chemokines, an indication that NETs can activate these cells [7]. In this work, we evaluated the response of uninfected macrophages to NETs, exploring cell receptors and signaling pathways involved in the modulation of cellular functioning. To have a glimpse of the effects of NET exposure in our model of monocyte-derived macrophages, we applied an unbiased transcriptomic approach evaluating cells 24 h after exposure to IL-8-induced NETs. RNA-seq data identified 12,856 genes, of which 406 were differentially expressed (above |log2FC| and -log10padj (FDR) cutoffs) (Fig. 1A and Supplemental file 1). From those data, we performed an enrichment analysis (Figs. 1B and C), which pointed out the most modulated gene ontology (GO) of biological process (BP), cellular component (CC) and molecular function (MF) classes in IL-8-induced NET-exposed macrophages (Supplemental files 2, 3 and 4). From the original 406 DEGs identified, 378 (for BP and MF) and 391 (for CC) were found annotated to the selected functional categories and were used for the enrichment analysis. Of note, we performed the same analysis including macrophages treated with the estimated concentration of IL-8 carried over by IL-8-stimulated NETs identifying 137 genes differentially expressed, however no significant ontologies were identified (Supplementary Fig. 1A-B and Supplemental File 5). The profile of DEGs distribution between modulated GO classes indicates that NET exposure in an initial stage (24 h) could induce an activation profile in macrophages (Figs. 1B and C, and Supplemental files 2, 3, and 4), where the principal GO classes clustered involve processes of metabolism regulation, binding and transport of ions, and mitochondrion-related processes. Consistently with the fact that NETs are products of controlled neutrophil death [1, 55], representing and presenting DAMPs [56], thus acting as mediators of cellular communication [14, 57, 58], we found enrichment in GO pathways related to extracellular vesicles, extracellular space and RAGE receptor binding in NET-exposed macrophages. Moreover, macrophage exposure to NETs also promoted modulation of metabolic-related processes, as expected and described in macrophage activation during inflammation [10, 16, 59, 60]. The increased expression of CCL3 was commensurate with the detection of the protein (also known as MIP-1α) in the supernatants of IL-8-induced NET-treated macrophages (Fig. 1D). This result is also consistent with our previously published data, that showed increased production of other two β-chemokines by macrophages treated with IL-8-induced NETs [7], joining with the induction of other chemokines like MIF, CCL2, CCL7 and CCL24 observed in the RNAseq (Supplemental File 1). The modulation of these chemokines associated with the upregulation of MMP25 and downregulation of ITGA3 suggests a more active macrophage profile. Moreover, the upregulation of UCP2 and downregulation of VLDLR, SLC7A5, SLC1A5 and SLC16A9 point to a metabolic change of these cells [61–64]. Together, these results support that IL-8-induced NETs modulate cellular activation and metabolic regulation in macrophages.

Induction of NETs by inactivated viruses HIV-1 and SARS-CoV-2

NET release can be triggered by a variety of different stimuli, including endogenous cytokines and pathogens [1, 4–6]. Here, to explore other NET-triggers, we induced and quantified NETs released after neutrophil stimulation with beta-propiolactone-inactivated HIV-1 and SARS-CoV-2 viruses for 3 h. We observed that both inactivated viruses are potent inducers of NETs, leading to 3.2- and 2.6-fold increase over control, respectively, as measured by the DNA double-strand detection (Figs. 2B and C). This increase was even higher than that induced by the classical NET inducer IL-8, which promoted only a 1.57-fold rise (Fig. 2A). The release of NETs was further confirmed by fluorescence microscopy (Figs. 2D and E), in which we detected the NET typical web-like structures by staining the granule protein elastase or LL-37 (both in red) associated with DNA (blue).

NETs induce ROS production in macrophages

To extend our studies on NET-macrophage activation beyond our previously published results with IL-8-induced NETs [7, 14], we addressed the macrophage response to inHIV- and inSARS-induced NETs, which has not been described up to now. Thus, we initially quantified the release of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the culture supernatant during the 3 h of stimulation. We found a significant increase of ROS species with NETs induced by IL-8, inHIV or inSARS (Fig. 3). We observed a significant increase in ROS production by macrophages treated with IL-8-induced NETs (Fig. 3A), consistent with the enrichment of pathways related to the redox balance, as identified by RNAseq (Fig. 1). We detected a dose-dependent response to inHIV-NETs, with an increase of 1.23-, 1.32-, and 1.38-fold in the concentrations of 25, 50, and 100 ng/mL, when compared to the control group (Fig. 3B). Regarding inSARS-NETs there was also a dose-dependent increase in ROS production, 1.9-, 2.46, and 2.8-fold higher than in control for the concentrations of 50, 100, and 150 ng/mL (Fig. 3C). These results indicate a change in cellular functioning, possibly towards an inflammatory response, together with metabolic effects.

NETs promote the release of inflammatory mediators by macrophages

Activation of macrophages can be evaluated by the analysis of their different functions, such as the production of inflammatory mediators. Hence, we measured the production of these mediators 24 h after NET-treatment. The interaction between macrophages and NETs induced by both inHIV and inSARS promoted the release of the β-chemokines MIP-1α, MIP-1β, and RANTES, which are known to recruit cells to inflammatory sites, in a dose-dependent manner (Figs. 4A-F). The dose of 100 ng/mL of inHIV-NETs was the only one from those tested (from 10 to 100 ng/mL) to stimulate the production of all three chemokines, whereas all tested concentrations of inSARS-induced NETs (from 25 to 150 ng/mL) promoted a significant macrophage production of these three chemokines. Hence, concentrations of 100 ng/mL for inHIV-NET and 150 ng/mL for inSARS-NET were chosen for the next studies. These results corroborate the RNA sequencing performed on macrophages treated with IL-8-NETs, which detected increased expression of MIP-1α. Also, they support our published study, which showed increased β-chemokine production by macrophages treated with IL-8-induced NETs [7].

The levels of inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-6, IL-10 and IL-8 were evaluated, and all of them were increased in the supernatant of NET-treated macrophages (Figs. 5A-J). The potency of induction was variable between these cytokines and the NET origin. inHIV-NET and inSARS-NET promoted, respectively, 6- and 16.3-fold increase of TNF-α, 12.6- and 4-fold of IL-6, and 2.7 and 1.6-fold of IL-8 release. Additionally, both NETs induced a significant 3.6- and 1.3-fold increase in IL-10 production (Figs. 5D and H). Similar results were observed in macrophages exposed to NETs induced by IL-8, resulting in a 2.77-, 2.14-, and 1.96-fold increase in the production of IL-6, CXCL-10, and TNF-α, respectively (Figs. 5I-K). To rule out that any undetermined soluble factor present in the NET-enriched supernatant used to treat macrophages could have contributed to the observed cellular activation, we did a parallel control experiment using isolated NETs (as described in Methods) induced by inSARS. The isolated NETs increased by 11.6-, 6.6-, 3.1, and 11.9-fold the macrophage production of TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10, respectively, confirming that the cell response was due only to NET stimuli (Supplementary Fig. 3A-D). Of note, the ability to promote macrophage activation was dependent on NET-scaffold integrity, since protein degradation abrogated this effect (Supplementary Fig. 3E). DNA digestion did not change cytokine production, further supporting that, in our model, macrophage activation is driven by NET-associated proteins. Together, these results indicate that the different sources of NETs evaluated here can activate macrophages towards an inflammatory profile with the release of soluble molecules.

NETs induce NF-κB phosphorylation on macrophages

ROS act as a second messenger and as an inducer of redox balance sensitive transcription factors, such as NF-κB [65–67]. Moreover, the inflammatory molecules produced by NET-treated macrophages suggest the activation of this transcription factor, leading us to assess the phosphorylation of the NF-κB p65 subunit. After two hours of macrophage exposure to NETs, we detected a significant increase in the ratio between phosphorylated and total NF-κB p65 subunit (Figs. 6A and B). These results were detected with NETs induced by inHIV and inSARS, increasing phosphorylation of the NF-κB p65 subunit by 1.35- and 1.55-fold over control, respectively, revealing that either one can promote NF-κB activation. A similar result was observed after 8 h of macrophage interaction with inSARS-induced NETs, which increased p65 phosphorylation 1.17-fold over control (Supplementary Fig. 4). The total amount of NF-κB p65 subunit did not statistically increase upon macrophage treatment with NETs, although a slight increment can be seen for both NETs (Figs. 6C and D). The increment of NF-κB p65 phosphorylation relates to the cellular activation-related ontologies found in the RNAseq analysis (Fig. 1).

Macrophage activation by NETs depends on NF-κB

Since we detected higher levels of phosphorylated NF-κB p65 subunit in macrophages after exposure to NETs, and because activation of NF-κB is associated with enhanced production of inflammatory mediators, we analyzed whether inhibiting this transcription factor would reverse the NET-inducing cytokine production. Because all NETs we tested promoted similar effects, we selected only inHIV-NET to pursue this investigation. Thus, we treated the macrophages with Bay 11-7082, a pharmacological inhibitor of IκBα phosphorylation, a key regulator of NF-κB activity, prior to exposure to NETs. The inhibition of NF-κB resulted in an expressive decline in the production of TNF-α (72%), IL-6 (61%), IL-10 (93%), MIP-1α (41%), MIP-1β (62%), and RANTES (45%) (Fig. 7). The release of IL-8 was not significantly modified by NF-κB inhibition, reducing only 8.2%. Overall, these results clearly show that NF-κB participates in the NET-mediated macrophage activation.

TLR2 and TLR4 participate in NET recognition by macrophages

To better understand how NETs interact with macrophages and which receptors might be involved in this communication, we performed assays in the presence of inhibitors of TLR2 and TLR4 receptors. Both receptors have been described as targets for proteins that have been detected in NETs, such as, for example, neutrophil elastase, myeloperoxidase, histones, HMGB1, heat shock proteins and S100 family [40–46, 68], and their engagement can culminate in NF-κB activation. After 30 min of treatment with TLR2 or TLR4 inhibitors, NETs were added to macrophage culture and, 24 h later, the supernatants were collected and analyzed for production of inflammatory mediators. Of note, we performed a concentration curve of inhibitors and DMSO control condition to select the better doses, and verified the viability of cells exposed to the inhibitors by XTT and LDH methods which remained above 90% (Supplementary Fig. 2). The blockade of TLR2 significantly reduced the production of TNF-α (43%), IL-6 (57%) and IL-10 (55%) and RANTES (26%) (Figs. 8A, B, D and G), while the release of IL-8, MIP-1α and MIP-1β induced by NETs was not affected by TLR2 inhibition (Figs. 8C, E and F). The reduction in TNF-α, IL-6, IL-10 and RANTES production indicates that TLR2 participates in NET recognition by macrophages and its downstream signaling activation. Although substantial, this inhibition does not abrogate all the induction promoted by NETs and was not observed for all molecules analyzed, indicating that other receptors may be involved. To explore this hypothesis, we used a TLR4 inhibitor before adding NETs to the macrophage culture and observed a decrease of 49.9% in the TNF-α, 41.3% in the IL-6 and 64.1% in the IL-10 production induced by NETs (Figs. 8H-J). These findings show that both receptors participate in macrophage activation induced by NETs and that, together, they make a significant contribution to the neutrophil-macrophage communication and cellular response. Of note, blockade of TLR2 and TLR4 also reduced the macrophage activation by IL-8-induced NETs, as measured by MIP-1α release (Fig. 8K and L). Importantly, assorted pools of NETs were analyzed in different moments for endotoxin contamination using the Limulus Amebocyte Lysate (LAL) method, whose results were systematically negative.

Actin polymerization is required for macrophage activation by NETs

Some studies report that NET clearance by macrophages is an active process dependent on actin rearrangement [14, 15]. Since we observed that macrophage activation by NETs is partially dependent on TLR2 and TLR4 engagement, we aimed to investigate whether the process of NET engulfment is also important to this phenomenon, thus contributing to the production of inflammatory mediators simultaneously with TLR binding. To verify this assumption, we pretreated macrophages with cytochalasin D, an inhibitor of actin microfilament polymerization, and then exposed cells to NETs for 24 h. We found that cytochalasin D treatment strongly impaired the production of cytokines, reducing the release of TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-10 by 81.4%, 61% and 59.4%, respectively (Fig. 9). Interestingly, NET-induced IL-8 release was not affected by cytochalasin D treatment, suggesting an alternative pathway for the induction of this mediator. These results indicate that macrophage activation by NETs is also dependent on the engulfment of these traps, which in turn is mediated by actin polymerization and rearrangement.

PKA, but not PKC, mediates macrophage activation by NETs

Some proteins, as cathelicidin (LL-37) and elastase, present in the NET composition, are known to interact with G-protein coupled receptors (GPCRs) and activate downstream kinases, sometimes promoting ROS production in various cells [69–72]. To verify the participation of protein kinase A (PKA) and C (PKC) in NET-mediated macrophage activation, we treated the macrophages with PKA or PKC pharmacological inhibitors before cell exposure to NETs. Inhibition of PKA abrogated the NET-induced release of TNF-α and IL-10, whereas the blockade of PKC did not modify this release (Fig. 10).

Discussion

Many authors have described the effects of NETs on immune cells in a non-infectious scenario. For example, Guimarães-Costa et al. reported that NETs interact with primary monocytes and downregulate IL-4 receptor expression, thereby impairing their differentiation into dendritic cells [12]. Apel et al. found that macrophages and other myeloid cells phagocytose NETs, which leads to activation of the innate immune sensor cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS), thus stimulating the STING signaling pathway and inducing type I interferon (IFN) production [73].

Our group also described that NETs carry microRNAs able to modulate TNF-α production by macrophages [14]. Farrera and Fadeel demonstrated that macrophages make the clearance of NETs without the induction of proinflammatory cytokines [15]. Other authors have suggested that NETs can induce macrophage polarization [74, 75]. In addition to innate immune cells, T lymphocytes can interact with NETs and be primed by them, resulting in a reduced lymphocyte activation threshold and increased responses to specific antigens [8]. LL-37-DNA complexes in NETs are recognized by B lymphocytes, triggering TLR9 activation, which promotes polyclonal B cell activation and the expansion of self-reactive memory B cells, thereby generating anti-LL-37 antibodies [76]. Nonetheless, further investigations are needed to describe additional molecular mechanisms underlying macrophage activation by NETs.

In this work, using NETs formed upon stimulation of neutrophils by two novel inducers, the HIV-1 and SARS-CoV-2 inactivated virions, we searched for receptors and signaling pathways possibly involved in the modulation of macrophage functioning by these structures. Although some authors have suggested that the macrophage interaction with NETs is a silent process [15], we observed, in our model, that NETs formed upon three different stimuli induced an opposite effect, as all of them promoted a strong inflammatory response in macrophages. These cells have many functions in innate and adaptive immune systems, producing and releasing a variety of soluble mediators to orchestrate the immune response to sterile or pathogen-induced inflammatory damage. Neutrophils and monocytes circulate in the blood, and, by the release of chemokines, macrophages can recruit these cells to the inflammatory site, where they can be activated by cell-derived soluble mediators, by damage- or pathogen-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs or PAMPs, respectively), or by other molecules. Neutrophils are one of the first cells to be recruited, and once in the tissue milieu, they can release NETs that will interact with resident macrophages, but also with incoming monocytes [13, 28, 30, 77, 78].

In this study, we show that NETs interact with macrophages promoting their activation. This effect was investigated by different analysis and approaches that revealed cellular transcriptional changes, increased production of ROS and inflammatory molecules, and stimulation of transcription factor NF-κB, with the engagement of Toll-like receptors 2 and 4 and the participation of PKA and cytoskeleton polymerization. Moreover, the upregulation of EGR1, UCP2, and UBASH3B detected by RNA sequencing in IL-8-NET-treated macrophages can be correlated with TLR2 or TLR4 activation by NET-proteins, based on known immune signaling pathways and functional roles of these genes. These genes have been shown to be involved, respectively, in chemokine and cytokine induction, ROS production, and NF-kB activity regulation [79–81]. In fact, in our subsequent analyses we found that all of these processes were modulated in macrophages exposed to NETs. The production of IL-8 and β-chemokines by macrophages after interaction with NETs can boost neutrophil recruitment (neutrophil swarming) and create a positive feedback loop, in which a massive amount of NETs are released and the activation of macrophages may become uncontrolled, bypassing their resolutive capacity. In this scenario, exacerbated macrophage activation by NETs may cause tissue injury during inflammatory illnesses and infectious diseases. The elevated production of proinflammatory mediators by NET-stimulated macrophages, as we describe here, may contribute to tissue damage in diseases in which an enhanced NET formation occurs, such as, for example, Covid-19, adult-onset Still’s disease, atherosclerosis, sepsis and acute lung injury [20, 82, 83]. We could, at this point, speculate that a heightened macrophage activation by NETs may induce a cytokine storm resembling the macrophage activation syndrome (MAS), a systemic hyperinflammatory condition that causes disseminated tissue damage, and deleterious effects to patients [84, 85]. Thus, controlled activation is needed to preserve basal responses and surveillance in the tissues [86–88]. The balance between neutrophil recruitment, NET formation, and macrophage response will determine the outcome, whether it will become an inflammatory condition or a resolutive response. Understanding this interaction and its possible outcomes is essential to control the excessive innate immune response and the inflammatory damage in sterile and infectious conditions.

To delineate how NET-macrophage communication occurs, we sought to verify the participation of the innate immune receptors TLR2 and TLR4, which are known to recognize some endogenous molecules, including NET proteins [38, 40, 45, 89]. Thus, using pharmacological inhibitors widely employed to investigate TLR2 and TLR4 involvement in cell activation, we found a significant decline in the macrophage production of TNF-α and IL-6, which shows the engagement of both receptors in NET-mediated macrophage activation. Based on published NET proteomic [2, 19, 39, 90], we suppose that both TLRs might be activated by various NET proteins, such as HMGB1, S100A8/9, neutrophil elastase, myeloperoxidase, heat shock proteins, among others, that have been described to bind TLR2 and/or TLR4 [40, 42, 44–46].

Our findings indicate that TLR2 and TLR4 receptors at the macrophage surface can simultaneously recognize NET proteins and trigger downstream signaling, which promotes cell activation. It is reasonable that none of the inhibitors completely abrogated the activation induced by NETs, since these structures are composed of a wide variety of proteins and nucleic acids that can bind to and activate other innate receptors, such as endogenous TLRs or cytosolic DNA sensors [73]. Further studies are required to precisely determine the ligands and additional receptors involved in NET-mediated macrophage activation.

Since macrophages can also engulf NET by endocytosis, possibly triggering an intracellular response [11, 15], we used cytochalasin D to verify the contribution of NET endocytosis and actin polymerization to the process of macrophage activation. We found similar levels of reversion of cell stimulation upon blocking internalization, suggesting that both surface receptors and engulfment of NETs are required for a full macrophage response to NET exposure. Interestingly, the NET-induced IL-8 production was not impaired by TLR2 and TLR4 blockade, nor by inhibition of actin polymerization, suggesting that other receptors or pathways are involved in this specific cytokine synthesis and release, whose definition will require further analysis.

Connected to engagement of TLRs and, possibly, other innate receptors, we detected that NF-κB was activated as early as 2 h after NET treatment, in line with previous findings showing that this transcription factor is rapidly activated after TLRs engagement to induce the transcription of cytokines and other proteins [40, 91]. Here, we show that this activation persists at least 8 h after NETs exposure, suggesting a prolonged inflammatory response.

The activation of NF-κB, detected here by the increased ratio between phosphorylated p65 subunit and total protein, is not only enhanced by NET treatment, but is also required to induce inflammatory cytokines, since the use of Bay 11-7082, an inhibitor of IκBα phosphorylation, significantly reduced the release of TNF-α, IL6, IL-10, and RANTES. Because NF-κB is a downstream factor that connects different signaling pathways, it can be activated by DAMPs carried and exposed by NETs to intra- and extracellular receptors in macrophages. These pathway connections could explain the potent decline of macrophage cytokine production secondary to NF-kB inhibition observed in our results. Although significant, the NET-induced cytokine production was not completely abrogated, suggesting the participation of other transcription factors or a residual activation of NF-κB. As such, Giri et al. showed that amyloid peptides induce the production of TNF-α, IL-8, and MIP-1β through the activation of the transcription factors Egr-1 and AP-1, without activating NF-κB [80]. By RNA sequencing, we detected a differential and increased expression of Egr-1 in NET-treated macrophages, suggesting that this transcription factor may also be involved in macrophage activation by NETs.

The release of β-chemokines and IL-8 by macrophages exposed to NETs was previously described by Lazzaretto et al. likewise our findings [9]. In contrast, the production of IL-10 was not detected by them, which might be explained by methodological differences between our model and theirs. First, the NET-inducer used by them was PMA, which is a synthetic molecule that may have a different mechanism of neutrophil activation from inactivated viruses. Also, they differentiated macrophages from monocytes obtained by positive selection and maintained with recombinant M-CSF, while we maintained all PBMCs during monocyte-macrophage differentiation and did not add any growth factors to the culture.

A response of macrophages to IL-8-induced NETs, thought moderate, can be observed in the RNA sequencing (Fig. 1), by the increased ROS production (Fig. 3A) and augmented release of inflammatory mediators (Figs. 5I-K). The transcriptomic analysis shows a degree of macrophage activation by IL-8-induced NETs that is apparently modest when correlated to the protein levels of mediators found at the same time-point (24 h). This supposed imbalance suggests an early transcription and release of molecules, which accumulate in the culture, or a release of preformed cytokines and chemokines. Also, because IL-8 is an endogenous factor present in physiological conditions, it is expected that NETs induced by this molecule are more homeostatic than inflammatory, as can be observed by fold increase in the production of soluble mediators stimulated by IL-8-induced-NET compared to other NETs (Fig. 5). Nonetheless, our transcriptional findings show that macrophage-NETs interaction results in an active cellular modifying process, denoting a macrophage functional response to NETs.

To describe the mechanisms by which NETs activate uninfected macrophage is important to understand how macrophages and neutrophils interact to maintain homeostasis in the tissues and to induce an inflammatory response. Also, the increased ROS production suggests an improved antimicrobial capacity of macrophages, which can be useful in infectious conditions to destroy both extracellular pathogens and phagocytosed ones [92, 93]. Finally, using NETs induced by three stimuli and evaluating different and connected characteristics of cellular response, we provide novel findings to consolidate the knowledge that macrophages are activated upon interaction with NETs.

Limitations of the study

We recognize that our work has limitations that must be addressed in further investigations. For example, the small sample size and the few conditions used in transcriptomic analysis limit to draw a direct and conclusive correlation with the other results. It is important to note that 24 h time-point used for both RNAseq and release of inflammatory mediators may not be suitable to simultaneously detect up-modulation of genes and the related cytokines in the supernatant. Earlier time points can clarify activation kinetics and reveal modulation of the cytokines measured at 24 h. How long the activation promoted by NETs lasts is an important question we did not address in this work. Thus, new RNA sequencing analysis should be conducted using macrophages exposed to NETs induced by a greater number of stimuli and at different time points. We are aware that, in addition to the clear roles of TLR2 and TLR4, endosomal or cytoplasmic receptors may also be involved in the macrophage response to NETs, and that their roles should be further examined to build a more complete assembly of the molecular connections underlying the macrophage activation by NETs.

Conclusions

Our data indicate that macrophage transcriptional profile and functioning are modulated upon interaction with NETs through TLR2 and TLR4 receptors, NF-kB and PKA signaling pathways, and actin polymerization (Fig. 11). These findings add new evidence that NETs participate in cellular communication upon their release by neutrophils and interact with and modify the functioning of macrophages. For further studies, deeper analysis of other innate receptors and signaling pathways is required to refine the molecular mechanisms involved in communication between NETs and macrophages and, by extension, with monocytes.
