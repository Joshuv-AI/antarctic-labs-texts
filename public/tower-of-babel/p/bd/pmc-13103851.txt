Molecular Insights into the β‑Lactam α‑Methyl Modulation of Pseudomonas aeruginosa Penicillin-Binding Protein 3 (PBP3): Loop Dynamics and Hydration Network Modulation
Authors: Donnifer V. Reyes, Ricky B. Nellas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The stereochemical recognition of the α-methyl group at the d-Ala–d-Ala terminus of peptidoglycan by penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs) has been implicated in shaping the evolutionary divergence of dd-peptidases. Here, we investigate how the β-lactam α-substituent identity influences the conformational dynamics of wild-type Pseudomonas aeruginosa PBP3 using molecular dynamics simulations of covalent acyl-enzyme complexes with CEFacyl (α-hydro) and its α-methyl derivative, MECacyl. Our analysis reveals that the α-methyl group in MEC-PBP3acyl is accommodated within a defined methyl pocket predominantly composed of conserved residues K297, S349, N351, and V333. Hydration network into the buried active site is disrupted in the MEC-PBP3acyl complex, which consequently results to the loss of a deacylation-competent geometry of water at K297 toward the β-lactam acyl carbon required for hydrolytic deactivation. Time-resolved analyses of the CEF-PBP3acyl simulation reveals that the contraction of the active site α-loop is associated with the coupling of water bridge networks that connects the α2 helix active site motif 294 STVK 297 to a distal salt bridge cluster–the “water sink” (R284 α1b, D288 loop, R504 β4, and D525 β5) which corroborates crystal structure evidence (PDB ID: 6R3X) [ BelliniD., J. Mol. Biol. 2019, 431, 3501–3519 31301409 10.1016/j.jmb.2019.07.010]. Pocket-based analyses show that the expansion of the methyl pocket into the STVK motif coincides with active site solvation. These dynamic observations are observed to be associated with the shifting salt bridge interactions of R504 β4 on the α-loop, which may rationalize resistance in R504 mutants. The steric bulk of the α-methyl group in MECacyl toward the K297 α2 side chain disables active site plasticity and consequently impairs the loop mobility required for the influx of water into the active site. These findings provide mechanistic molecular insights into how α-substituent chemistry modulates active site hydration dynamics in PBP3 and support the importance of α-methyl recognition in dd-peptidases. This work also establishes a structural framework for future studies of PBPs and β-lactam drug design relevant to antimicrobial resistance.

Introduction

Cefiderocol (CEF), the first FDA-approved sideromycin, was introduced in 2020 for the treatment of hospital-acquired and ventilator-associated bacterial pneumonia. However, a recent systematic review reveals the emergence of CEF resistance in vivo, implicating mutations in the target penicillin-binding protein 3 (PBP3). In one study, minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) increased by up to 128-fold post-treatment with CEF, even in the absence of detectable changes in siderophore receptor expression. In vitro and clinical mutations in Acinetobacter baumannii and Pseudomonas aeruginosa PBP3 have been correlated with reduced antibiotic susceptibility and resistance, even in the absence of classical resistance determinants such as β-lactamases, efflux pumps, and porin loss. − Collectively, these findings suggest that alternative resistance mechanisms, particularly those affecting the target PBP3, may be at play.

β-Lactam antibiotics closely resemble the PBP native substrate, the d-Ala–d-Ala terminus. One key difference is the presence of a stereospecific α-methyl group on the d-alanine residue, which is absent in the β-lactam scaffold. Simulations of the acylation transition states hint at the preference for the α-methyl-substituted substrates by PBPs, while they are disfavored by β-lactamases. −

Pseudomonas aeruginosa PBP3 (PaPBP3) is a class B high molecular weight PBP that possesses all three conserved PBP motifs SXXK, SXN, and KXGT. It is classified as a transpeptidase and lacks the ability to hydrolyze its α-methyl-containing native substrate. Despite this, however, it has been shown to be capable of hydrolytic deacylation of several β-lactams. , These observations allude to the potential for specialized deacylation machinery capable of discriminating between α-hydro- and α-methyl-substituted substrates.

Class C PBPs also share the same conserved motifs but primarily function as dd-carboxypeptidases. Their hydrolytic activity is understood to be facilitated by bridging waters that are hydrogen-bonded to the catalytic lysine of the SXXK motif. − Structural insights from related class C PBPs may help us understand the unconventional hydrolytic properties of PaPBP3 that do not possess inherent d-Ala–d-Ala carboxypeptidase activity.

In this study, we explore the structural and dynamic effects of the α-methyl group on PaPBP3 using molecular dynamics simulations of acylated covalent complexes (PBP3acyl). We compare cefiderocol (CEF), a clinically relevant β-lactam antibiotic, to its α-methylated analog, α-methyl cefiderocol (MEC). Through this comparative analysis, we aim to investigate how the α-substituent identity modulates local hydration and conformational behavior in covalent PBP3 acyl-enzyme complexes.

Methodology

Simulation Overview

We performed all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of covalent acyl-enzyme Pseudomonas aeruginosa PBP3wt complexes with cefiderocol (CEF) and its α-methyl derivative (MEC). Each system was simulated for 100 ns to probe the hydration patterns and solvation at the catalytic site. Covalent models were parametrized with a modified acylated serine residue to capture β-lactam adduct chemistry. Analyses focused on active site fluctuations, hydration networks, and pocket formation.

System Preparation

The crystal structure of Pseudomonas aeruginosa PBP3 (PDB ID: 6R3X) was used as the starting model. Co-crystallized ligands, ions, and water molecules were removed to prepare for MD. Protonation states of titratable residues were assigned at pH 6.5 using the H++ server. Each protein–ligand complex was solvated in a TIP3P water box and neutralized with NaCl using AmberTools tleap. AMBER ff14SB and GAFF force fields were applied to the protein and ligands, respectively.

Covalent Complex Preparation

The acyl-enzyme complexes were modeled by parametrizing the nucleophilic serine residue O γ-acylated with the open-ring β-lactam. The O γ-acylated Ser-294 was capped with ACE and NME to mimic backbone connectivity, and its geometry optimized at the B3LYP/6–31G*level using Gaussian09. Electrostatic potentials were computed using the HF/6-31G* with the Merz–Kollman (MK) method, and RESP charges were derived via Antechamber. GAFF atom types and bonded parameters were assigned using AmberTools parmchk.

Molecular Dynamics Simulation

MD simulations were performed using NAMD v2.14. Each of the systems (Table ) underwent a three-stage minimization protocol at 0 K for 5000 steps (10 ps) per stage, employing a progressive reduction of restraints to enable gradual system relaxation: (1) water minimization (fixed protein complex); (2) side chain minimization (fixed protein backbone); and (3) minimization with fixed secondary structures. Following minimization, each system was gradually heated from 0 to 310 K in 50-K increments under an NVT ensemble for 10,000 steps (20 ps). This was followed by an equilibration period of 2000 ps (2 fs time step) in an NPT ensemble, after which a 100 ns production run, at a 2 fs time step, was conducted at 310 K and 1 atm. Simulation convergence was evaluated by monitoring the global protein backbone root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) relative to the crystal structure (PDB ID: 6R3X), alongside total potential energy profiles. Both metrics remained stable throughout the 100 ns production period, indicating system equilibration (Figure S1).

Summary of System Set-Up

Temperature control was maintained using a Langevin thermostat with a collision frequency of 2 ps–1, while pressure was regulated using the Nosé-Hoover piston barostat implemented in NAMD, with isotropic position scaling at a relaxation time of 1 ps. All covalent bonds involving hydrogen were constrained using the SHAKE algorithm to allow a 2 fs time step. Long-range electrostatics were treated using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method under periodic boundary conditions.

Results and Discussion

To investigate the effect of α-methyl substitution on the protein–ligand dynamics in the covalent acyl-enzyme complexes, the control compound cefiderocol (CEF) was modified in silico to incorporate an α-methyl group aligned with the stereochemistry of the d-alanine residue in a bacterial peptidoglycan. Dynamic insights on the 100 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulation trajectories were obtained through a combination of biophysical analyses. Root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) calculations were used to quantify local and global flexibility across residues, while water bridge analyses captured hydration patterns and bridging interactions. Time-resolved contact dynamics were examined to track the evolution of key residue motions over the course of the simulation.

Redefining the Methyl Pocket Accommodating the α-Methyl Group

The α-methyl recognition site previously proposed by Sauvage et al. corresponds to a cleft formed by the aromatic side chains of Y407 and Y409 and the G408 backbone at the rear end of the binding cavity (black, Figure ). The α-methyl group was hypothesized to serve as the evolutionary handle in the divergence of PBPs and β-lactamases from ancient dd-peptidases. However, time-averaged structure from the MD simulation of the acyl-enzyme MEC-PBP3acyl complex reveals that the α-methyl group remains distal to the YGY cavity, at a wide range of distances of ∼4–7Å (black, Figure ). Furthermore, the β-lactam canonical carboxylate anchor has maintained strong contacts at 1.9 ± 0.1 Å with K484 (green) of the 484 KSGT 487 conserved motif on the opposite end, indicating that the β-lactam motion to the 407 YGY 409 motif would be less likely as it would compromise canonical binding geometry.

α-Methyl environment showing the YGY motif (black) vs this study’s identified methyl pocket (orange), together with the time-averaged minimum distances. The carboxylate anchor K484 (green) of the 484 KSGT 487 motif is also shown.

Instead, the α-methyl group occupies a different site formed by residues K297, V333, S349, and N351, herein referred to as the methyl pocket (orange, Figure ). The methyl pocket residues have maintained close association with the methyl group at a distance range of ∼2.7–4 Å. The proximity of this pocket to the MEC α-substituent persists even in the noncovalent MEC-PBP3Michaelis complex simulation (Figure S2).

Examination of the pocket (orange, Figure ) shows that three of the four participating residues arise from PBP conserved motifs: K297 from the SXXK motif, and S349/N351 from the SXN motif on the α4–α5 loop. The nucleophilic S294 in the SXXK motif is the residue that is acylated, while K297 functions as the general base by activating the Ser-Oγ for nucleophilic attack and facilitating deacylation. , The SXN motif contributes to substrate anchoring via hydrogen bonding to the N α-amide of the peptide stem. The structural and evolutionary conservation of these residues strongly supports the newly proposed methyl pocket as a functionally significant site for α-methyl recognition in DD-peptidases.

To probe the involvement of V333, we analyzed the χ1 dihedral angle formed by valine N–C α –C β–C γ in the acyl-enzyme complexes (Figure ). In MEC-PBP3acyl (red), V333 predominantly adopts a stable trans rotamer (χ = 180°) with its C γ rotated 105.1° away from its position in the CEF-PBP3acyl complex, moving away from the MEC α-methyl group (Figure S3). In the absence of α-methyl in CEF-PBP3acyl, V333 displays a broader ensemble of gauche and anti rotamers (Figure ). This pattern persists more dominantly in the MEC-PBP3Michaelis complex, where the V333 side chain is almost exclusively locked in the trans conformation (Figure S4).

Val-333 χ1 angles of the CEF-PBP3acyl (red) and MEC-PBP3acyl (blue) complexes, corresponding to the valine N–C α –C β–C γ dihedral, shows gauche and anti rotamer relative distribution.

In Staphylococcus aureus PBP2a (SaPBP2a), the residue analogous to the V333 position is Y466–a known gatekeeper residue that occludes β-lactam access to the active site, contributing to methicillin resistance in methicillin-resistant strains (MRSA). The functional convergence of this position across species underscores the structural and evolutionary importance of the methyl pocket residues (K297, V333, S349, and N351) and their roles in substrate access and recognition.

Active Site α-Loop Contraction along a Solvent Pocket in CEF-PBP3acyl

Investigations of residue flexibility from the per residue RMSFs of the covalent acyl-enzyme complex simulations reveal that there is a global stabilization of the complex (up to 41.9%) in MEC-PBP3acyl compared to CEF-PBP3acyl (Figure S5).

Within the 294 STVK 297 active site region, G293 loop emerges as the most stabilized residue in the MEC-PBP3acyl complex (41.9% more stable), and is thereby comparatively the most mobile residue in CEF-PBP3acyl. This is particularly notable given the intrinsic flexibility of glycine. The G293 loop residue is located on the α-loop that connects the active site α2 helix294–307 and the α1b helix284–287, and it is the immediate residue entering the nucleophilic S294 α2 at the active site. Time-resolved contact analysis shows that, in CEF-PBP3acyl, G293 migrates toward the R284 α1b backbone of the 284 RAMI 287 α1b helix (Figure S6) as the G293 loop moves down the sets of antiparallel β-subdomain as a result of the α1b-α2 loop contraction (Figure ).

(A) Minimum distance calculations of G293 with β-sheet residues show contraction of the α1b-α2 loop in the CEF-PBP3acyl complex. (B) α-loop contraction results in the downstream descent of E291 up to 4.3 Å, which shifts its interactions from β3 to β4.

In the crystal structure of the hydrolysis-susceptible piperacillin (PIP) in its acyl-PBP complex (PIP–PBP3acyl, PDB ID: 6R3X), the G293–R284 distance at 17.7 Å coincides with the dominant contracted α-loop state in CEF-PBP3acyl (Figure S6). In contrast, the loop in MEC-PBP3acyl remains relaxed and stable locked at G293–R284 beyond 18 Å contact distances (Figures , S5, and S6). These observations suggest two distinct conformational states for the α1b-α2 loop in the CEF-PBP3acyl complex simulation (Figure ): (1) a relaxed α-loop state, spanning the initial ∼0–20.0 ns (first state); and (2) a contracted α-loop state dominating during ∼20.1–100.0 ns (second state).

Structural evidence for solvent accessibility in this region comes from the crystal structure of PaPBP3 in acyl-enzyme complex with carbenicillin (PDB ID: 3OCL), a hydrolysis-susceptible β-lactam. Particularly, the α1b-α2 loop288–293 region houses a trapped glycerol molecule flanked by two ordered water molecules, situated between the loop and the adjacent β sheets. The presence of cryoprotectant glycerol and solvent molecules suggests a solvent-accessible channel or pocket capable of accommodating small polar molecules, which is a characteristic feature of structurally flexible and hydrated regions.

At the entrance of this loop-lined path to the α2 helix active site lies a cluster of salt bridges we designate as the water sink, composed of D288 loop, R284 α1b, D525 β5, and R504 β4. This site marks the beginning of a polar-to-nonpolar transition from α1b helix284–287 to α2 helix294–307. The path continues through a hydrophobic segment surrounding the acidic helix E291 loop and terminates in the polar catalytic motif 294 SXXK 297 on the α2 helix. This repeating polar (water sink) → nonpolar → polar (E291 loop) → nonpolar → polar (active site) architecture hints at a specialized route for water transport into the buried active site cleft, potentially facilitating the relay of a hydrolytic water into the buried site, crucial for β-lactam deacylation.

α-Loop Contraction Drives Hydration Access to the Active Site

Analysis of water bridges along the α1b-α2 loop288–293 path in the CEF-PBP3 acyl complex simulation reveals a continuous hydrogen-bonded network of water bridges extending from the distal water sink residues to catalytic K297 α2 (Figure ). In contrast, this connectivity is disrupted in the MEC-PBP3 acyl complex, with the water path terminating prematurely at G293 loop.

Network of water bridges and their contact frequencies from the water pocket to the active site motif in the CEF-PBP3acyl and MEC-PBP3acyl complexes.

Structural waters in the crystal structure of the PIP–PBPacyl complex (PIP–PBP3acyl, PDB ID: 6R3X) support the existence of several key water bridges observed in the CEF-PBP3acyl complex simulation (Figures and S7). Most notably, the high-frequency CEF-PBP3acyl water bridges: (1) four water molecules occupying the water sink network; (2) three water molecules corresponding to the E291–V491–R504 water bridges, and (3) the K297–Y407 water bridge. On the other hand, in the MEC-PBP3acyl complex simulation, three water bridge networks were absent (Figure ): (1) the E291–V491–R504, (2) the G410–L411–Y498, and (3) the G293–V296–K297–Y407 water bridges. To probe the temporal dynamics of the water bridge networks, the complex trajectories were segmented and clustered based on covariance of the water bridge interactions using the Toeplitz Inverse Covariance Clustering (TICC) implementation of GetContacts (Figure ). ,

Network of correlated water bridge clusters in the CEF-PBP3 acyl-enzyme complex represented as dashed arrows: (A) first state, Cluster 2; (B) second state (contracted loop), Cluster 1. Persistent Cluster 3 water bridges are represented by dashed lines. Water molecules are only representative structures of the water bridges..

Clustering of the CEF-PBP3acyl trajectory revealed three distinct water bridge clusters (Figure ): Cluster 3 (red) comprises persistent water bridges throughout the simulation, while Cluster 1 (blue) and Cluster 2 (green) represent mutually exclusive dynamic states, with Cluster 1 being more prevalent. The relaxed α-loop conformation (first state, Figure A) facilitates the formation of Cluster 2 water bridges, wherein E291 loop aligns with the β3 sheet to interact with R489 β3, L411 loop, and Y498 loop. Meanwhile, the more dominant contracted α-loop conformation (second state, Figure B) promotes the formation of the more frequent Cluster 1, where the consequent descent of E291 loop enables its water bridges with R504 β4 and V491 loop. Time-resolved water bridge analysis of E291–R504 shows that it predominantly forms in the latter 80 ns (second state) (Figure ). Cluster 3 bridges remain stable across both states.

Water bridge grouping within each state uncovered functionally distinct hydration subnetworks, marked by continuous water-mediated hydrogen-bonded networks, related to α-loop dynamics in CEF-PBP3acyl. In the first state, a relaxed α1b-α2 loop enables three water bridge networks, depicted by three distinct color regions (Figure A): (1) the water sink network (cyan); (2) a loop network (magenta) centered on E291 loop and its bridges with L411, S412, R489 β3, and Y498; and (3) the Cluster 3 K297–Y407 water bridge (orange).

In the second state, contraction of the α1b-α2 loop promotes the formation of three CEF-PBP3acyl-specific water bridges that are absent in MEC-PBP3acyl. First, E291 and V491 are recruited into the water sink network (cyan), forming the E291–V491–R504 water bridges. Second, G410–L411–Y498 bridging appears, including a two-water bridge with G293 (Figure ). Time-resolved solvation analysis correlates the G293 hydration pattern to the contact formation of Y498 with G410, L411, and G293 (Figure S8), establishing the continuity of the G410–L411–Y498 water bridges with G293 as part of the second state’s second water bridge network (orange) (Figure ). Radial distribution of water (RDFwater) around G293 shows a shift from bimodal to trimodal distribution beyond 20 ns, indicating internal hydration dynamics at the active site in the latter states (Figure S9) – a feature absent in the MEC-PBP3acyl simulation and has remained bimodal throughout the entire 100 ns. The emergence of a third RDFwater peak (∼2.7 Å) at G293 in CEF-PBP3acyl suggests restructuring of the local environment permitting the formation of a transient hydration cavity resulting from the opening of space at the α2 active site that enables the formation of the new G293–V296–K297–Y407 water bridges of the active site hydration network (orange, Figure ).

Electrostatic interaction energies, E H2O elec (Table ), quantify the stability of each water bridge cluster. In the first state, the first (water sink, cyan) and second (magenta) networks exhibit strong interaction energies (−2938 ± 114 and −3635 ± 117 kcal/mol, respectively), while the third network (active site, orange) is energetically the weakest (−33 ± 10 kcal/mol) (Figure ). In the second state, expansion of the water sink network to include the E291 and V491 leads to a 2-fold increase in electrostatic stabilization (−6363 ± 164 kcal/mol), while the active site network sees at least a 3-fold increase (−108 ± 21 kcal/mol) as a result of the expansion. These results suggest that as a consequence of the α-loop contraction is the influx of favorably interacting water molecules into the buried hydration network at the catalytic K297 α2 from the water sink.

The absence of the key water bridges in the MEC-PBP3 acyl complex connecting to K297 α2 in the active site: E291–V491–R504, the G410–L411–Y498, and the G293–V296–K297–Y407, may be intricately tied to its restricted α-loop dynamics, where the contracted α-loop conformation found in CEF-PBP3acyl and in the PIP–PBP3acyl crystal structure facilitates their formation (Figures S6 and S7). Highlighted in the loop is the hydration of the buried G293 that opens into the active 294SXXK297 motif. RDFwater at G293 in MEC-PBP3acyl reveals a hydration distribution that is identical to the first state of CEF-PBP3acyl, where the same α-loop is also in a relaxed state (Figures S6 and S9). Clustering of the TICC segmented water bridge interactions in MEC-PBP3acyl shows no distinct cluster separation (Figure S10). Particularly, all E291, G293, and R504 water bridges can be found in the same cluster, suggesting the absence of temporally distinct water bridge networks. These dynamic characterizations build on the growing body of molecular evidence into the known implications of general active site loop mobility to PBP-mediated resistance and deacylation catalysis. ,

Expansion of the Methyl Pocket into the Active Site Accommodates Solvation in CEF-PBP3acyl

To further characterize the spatial features of the regions occupied by the water bridge networks, pocket detection and analysis were performed using MDPocket, which implements a Voronoi tesselation-based algorithm for identifying and tracking pockets. A default detection threshold of 0.5 with a 50% minimum frequency was used to extract α spheres, which represent empty space in contact with four protein atoms.

Distinct pockets, depicted as colored surface structures (Figure ), were identified along the path of the water bridge network extending from the water sink to the α2 active site. In the first 20 ns, three discrete pockets were observed, each aligning with a specific water bridge network (Figure ): pocket A (cyan), located at the water sink; pocket B (magenta), aligning with the E291 water bridges; and a pocket that spatially aligns with this study’s defined methyl pocket (orange), containing the K297–Y407 water bridge.

Detection of pockets in CEF-PBP3acyl in intervals of 20 ns using MD Pocket. Pockets are depicted as surface structures colored according to the label.

In the second state (20.1–100.0 ns), rearrangements in the water bridge networks were accompanied by corresponding changes in pocket topology (Figure ). During the 20.1–40.0 ns period, changes to the E291 and G293 water bridges have translated to three key transitions: (1) the splitting of pocket B (magenta), giving rise to a smaller adjacent pocket, pocket C (blue) that accommodates the E291–V491–R504 water bridges; (2) the expansion of the methyl pocket from its initial definition (K297, 349 SXN 351, V333) into the α2 helix catalytic motif which gives rise to the active site G293–V296–K297–Y407 water bridges; and (3) the assimilation of pocket B into the methyl pocket (orange), expanding the network to the G410–L411–Y498 water bridges. Dynamic changes to pocket B are the most prevalent in the simulations. Particularly, it is its alternating assimilation into the methyl pocket, coinciding with the Y498–α-loop contacts centered at G293 (Figures and S8). RDF water reveals that in time intervals where pocket B is assimilated into the methyl pocket, the distribution of water molecules at G293 is higher, further underscoring the importance of G293 accessibility to active site hydration (Figures and S8). Conversely, during the 40.1–60.0 ns period, where accessibility at G293 is completely absent, it can be observed to have the lowest distribution of water molecules (Figure S9).

Together with the electrostatic interaction energies calculated for water molecules within each network (Table ), this supports a putative directional pathway for water diffusion progressing from the water sink toward the catalytic K297 α2: pocket A → pocket C → (pocket B) → methyl pocket, where the formation of a distinct and transient pocket B is regulated at G293. Throughout the second state (20.1–100.0 ns), the expansion of the methyl pocket into the α2 active site remains to persist, which could be visualized as an innate consequence of the contraction of its α-loop that opens up the active site. The PBP active site expansion marked by increased volume and flexibility has already been previously associated with the influx of hydrolytic water leading to the formation of a transient deacylation-competent complex.

Loss of Deacylation-Competent Water at K297 in MEC-PBP3acyl

In order to assess whether the observed loss of active site hydration in the MEC-PBP3acyl complex would translate to a deacylation-incompetent complex, we examined the active site hydrogen-bonded waters. Free energy surface plot (Figure ) of the waters at K297–N ζ shows a dominant state in CEF-PBP3acyl where two water molecules are within nucleophilic attack distance toward the acyl carbon. In contrast, in MEC-PBP3acyl, K297–N ζ accommodates only one water molecule, and is 1 Å farther than in CEF-PBP3acyl (Figure ).

Free energy surface plots showing distribution of water molecules at the active site, plotted as a function of the distance (d) to the open-ring β-lactam acyl carbon and the number of water molecules within 3.4 Å of catalytic K297–N ζ .

Acyl-enzyme complex of Escherichia coli PBP6 with hydrolysis-susceptible ampicillin reveals two well-ordered water molecules at the active site that are hydrogen-bonded to lysine of the SXXK motif, one of which is 3.2 Å away from the acyl carbon. Consistent with this, QM/MM simulation of the hydrolysis of the E. coli PBP5 acyl-enzyme, captures a starting H2O–C acyl distance of 3.0 Å, corresponding to the prereactive configuration preceding bond formation. In another study, 20 ns MD simulations of acyl-enzyme complexes have been able to demonstrate that deacylation competence is encoded in the hydration geometry at the conserved lysine residue: E. coli PBP5, incapable of carbapenem turnover, adopts a more rigid ensemble of water distances.

Water at the MEC-PBP3 acyl K297 maintains a distance of >3.5 Å from the β-lactam acyl carbon, which is beyond nucleophilic attack distance prerequisite for hydrolysis. The structural basis of this selectivity can be traced to steric gating: the α-methyl substituent obstructs water delivery along the K297–Y407 water bridge, preventing its passage across acylated S294 to the position typically occupied by the second water molecule in CEF-PBP3acyl (Figure ). Identifying which of these waters acts as the nucleophile in hydrolytic deacylation remains an open question that can be interrogated by targeted QM/MM simulations.

Arg-504 Shifting Salt Bridge Interactions Correlate with the α-Loop Contraction in CEF-PBP3acyl

The contraction of the α1b-α2 loop288–293, dynamics not observed in the α-methyl-modified MEC-PBP3acyl, appears to be essential for the completion of the water bridge network that connects the water sink to the catalytic motif 294 STVK 297. However, the key interaction driving this loop motion still remains to be completely elucidated. The α1b-α2 loop288–293 is primarily composed of weakly interacting hydrophobic residues (V289, F290, P292, G293) interspersed with two acidic residues, D288 and E291. Situated between these acidic residues is the basic R504 β4 residue located on the adjacent and structurally rigid β4 sheet.

Time-resolved analysis of the interactions of R504 β4 with D288 loop and E291 loop in the CEF-PBP3acyl complex reveals mutually exclusive salt bridge dynamics, where the formation of the E291–R504 salt bridge coincides with the disruption of the D288–R504 salt bridge (Figure ). This anticorrelated contact trend is also observed in the MEC-PBP3acyl simulation, despite the absence of the E291–R504 salt bridge (>4 Å) but still within water bridge-mediated interaction, suggesting that the inverse coupling between the two interaction pairs is an intrinsic feature of the local dynamics.

Time evolution of the R504 salt bridge interactions with D288 and E291 show anticorrelated dynamics. The presence of water bridge interactions is indicated through the trace plot alongside the line plot of the minimum distances of the salt bridge-interacting side chains. Default water bridge interaction cutoff in GetContacts was used (hydrogen-bonded water distance <3.5 Å).

The shifting interactions involving R504 may constitute the critical machinery in modulating the observed α1b-α2 loop dynamics. This suggests that substituting R504 with a residue capable of forming more transient, yet still energetically favorable interactions with adjacent acidic residues E291 and D288 could accelerate this dynamics and enhance the delivery of water into the active site. Indeed, Bellini et al. reported that an R504C mutation significantly reduced the acylation/deacylation rates in vitro. The crystal structures of the PBP3R458C variant remain nearly identical and superimposable to that of the wild-type variant (PBP3wt), with global RMSDs of only 0.4 Å for the apo form and 0.3 Å for the acyl-enzyme complex with piperacillin (PIP–PBP3acyl). This is particularly intriguing given that the Arg-504 residue also does not participate in substrate binding or in the acylation catalysis. Moreover, R504 β4 resides on the conserved β-subdomain generally considered to be structurally rigid, suggesting that its mutation is less likely to induce large-scale conformational changes, corroborating the observed negligible differences in the RMSDs between PBP3wt and the PBP3R458C. Instead, the mutation effects likely manifest in altered rates of local dynamics in the interacting adjacent α1b-α2 loop. Although the R504C substitution is nonconservative, the introduced cysteine side chain retains capacity for hydrogen bonding, thereby preserving its interactions with D299 and E291 surrounded by a hydrophobic environment. This attenuated yet preserved interaction network – D288–C504 and E291–C504, may promote faster shifting dynamics observed in the α1b-α2 loop288–293 contraction, facilitating the penetration of water molecules into the expanded active site. Furthermore, cysteine is a substantially smaller residue which may open up the α-loop path to an influx of more water molecules. Such dynamic changes could underlie the experimentally observed reduction in acylation/deacylation kinetics in the PBP3R504C variant and warrants potential investigation on its dynamic effects.

Clinical studies of multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa isolates from cystic fibrosis patients have identified the R504C and R504H mutations as the most frequent PBP3 variants associated with enhanced β-lactam resistance. − , These mutations correlate with increased minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of several β-lactams, despite no detectable changes in the expression of various canonical resistant determinants such as the OprD porin, MexAB-OprM efflux pump, or AmpC β-lactamase. , This lack of concordance suggests that traditional mechanisms alone do not fully account for the observed resistance phenotypes.

Further highlighting this discrepancy, certain clinical isolates with elevated β-lactamase expression, including carbapenemases, remained susceptible to β-lactams, indicating that overproduction of these enzymes is not solely predictive of resistance. In contrast, reduced susceptibility in carbapenem-resistant strains that retain porin expression and do not overexpress efflux pumps or β-lactamases has been consistently linked to the R504C missense PBP3 mutation. Collectively, these findings support a growing body of evidence implicating R504 as a critical determinant of PBP3-mediated resistance to β-lactams in multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa.

The α-Methyl Group Disrupts α-Loop Mobility in MEC-PBP3acyl

To elucidate the molecular basis underlying the suppressed dynamics of the α1b-α2 loop in the α-methyl-substituted derivative, MEC acyl, we investigated the influence of the α-substituent: specifically, the α-hydro group in CEF acyl versus the α-methyl group in MEC acyl. In both acyl-enzyme complexes, the covalently bound, ring-opened β-lactam scaffold positions its α-substituent in close proximity to the conserved active site motif 294 STVK 297, interacting most closely with K297 α2 of this study’s methyl pocket (Figures and ).

Time evolution of distance between K297–N ζ and the β-lactam open-ring α-substituent (X) of the acyl-enzyme complexes shows the distance plateau in MEC-PBP3acyl as a consequence of the steric locking between the α-methyl group and lysine.

Contact analysis reveals that the α-hydro group of CEF acyl maintains a variable interaction distance with K297 averaging at 3.6 ± 0.6 Å, indicative of a dynamic and conformationally flexible active site (Figure ). This looseness permits motion of the α1b-α2 loop, consistent with the contractile behavior previously described. The resultant loop displacement translates to a downward shift of the catalytic S294 α2 residue and its covalently linked β-lactam, effectively drawing the substrate deeper into the α2 helix. This repositioning increases the separation between the α-hydro group of CEF acyl and K297, diminishing stabilizing interactions and further promoting loop flexibility. In contrast, the α-methyl group in MEC acyl forms a more stable and spatially restricted interaction with the K297-N ζ side chain, maintaining an average contact distance of 2.7 ± 0.3 Å (Figure ). The added steric bulk of the α-methyl group, as a consequence of the substitution, brings it to within van der Waals contact of the lysine side chain, such that further approach would incur steric repulsion. This close packing effectively locks the adjacent α1b-α2 loop in place, suppressing its inherent flexibility and disrupting the conformational dynamics required for active site adaptability, including the rearrangement of the water-mediated hydrogen bond network.

Further underscoring the importance of α-substituent identity is the experimental evidence that a bulkier α-methoxy substitution in temocillin induces a significantly higher rate of deacylation in PaPBP3 – more than 20-fold faster deacylation than its α-hydro analogue, ticarcillin. This can be rationalized by the restrictive geometry at K297 α2 that limits the α-substituent size to the scale of a methyl group (Figure ). These structural insights support the hypothesis, suggesting that the stereospecific recognition of the α-methyl group represents an evolutionary handle central to the functional divergence of dedicated dd-transpeptidases and β-lactamases from ancestral DD-peptidases.

Putative Molecular Machinery for the Hydration Pathway in CEF-PBP3acyl

Comparative analysis of the CEF- and MEC-PBP3acyl simulations reveals a clear mechanistic divergence in which α-substituent identity governs deacylation propensity by modulating the conformational plasticity of the active-site α-loop and, in turn, regulating access to the catalytic hydration network.

Collectively, the following molecular events form a coupled sequence: (1) reorganization of salt bridge interactions, (2) contraction of the α-loop, (3) expansion of the active site cavity, (4) penetration of structured water into the catalytic region, and (5) accommodation of deacylating water (Figure ).

Consistent with this model, analyses based on radial distribution functions, contact dynamics, water bridge networks, and pocket detection converge on a hydration relay mechanism, leading to a deacylation-competent acyl-enzyme state (Figure ). This relay initiates at the water sink, where the solvent is accommodated within pocket A, and progresses through loop contraction associated with a shift in R504 electrostatic interactions toward E291, forming water-mediated connectivity within pocket C. These changes coincide with expansion of the methyl pocket further into the active site, permitting solvent access to catalytic K297. The transient appearance of an intermediate cavity (pocket B) in the contracted state remains incompletely characterized but appears to be regulated at G293, indicating a role for local loop dynamics in maintaining the hydration continuity.

Within this mechanistic model, the β-lactam deacylation capability is linked to the ability of the acyl-enzyme complex to access these conformational transitions, with the α-substituent acting as a molecular switch sensed at the conserved methyl pocket. The degree of steric constraint at this site determines whether the dynamic rearrangements required for formation of a continuous hydration pathway can occur.

Conclusion and Recommendations

This work establishes the utility of water-mediated interactions in probing hydration pathways into the active site of PaPBP3, which leads to the investigation of deacylation-competent geometry. Central to the framework of the hydration network machinery is the active site loop flexibility that is sterically sensitive to the interaction between the β-lactam α-substituent and the active site Lys-297 – providing a dynamic rationale for the observed resistance phenotypes associated with Arg-504 variants and warrants further investigation. Complementary to the hydration pathways are the identified pockets that can be explored as potential druggable targets for repurposing β-lactam antibiotics that have been rendered ineffective through PBP3-mediated resistance.

Beyond illuminating the biophysical underpinnings of active site hydration modulation in PaPBP3, these molecular insights contribute to the importance of the α-methyl group in the functional divergence of modern dd-peptidases. More broadly, this study underscores the established principle that active site hydration and loop plasticity are critical determinants of the β-lactam efficacy and resistance. These findings lay the groundwork for a new generation of antibiotics designed to deactivate the conformational and solvation dynamics of PaPBP3 and related systems.
