CM512, a potentially novel IgG1-based bispecific antibody dual-targeting TSLP and IL-13, for the suppression of type 2 inflammation
Authors: Yunxiao Yang, Yuzhen Lan, Linxi Bian, Yufei Li, Qin Song, Xinmei Gao, Qiaoyun Hou, Yundan Bai, Bo Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCM512, a novel IgG1-based 1 + 1 bispecific antibody designed with one arm targeting thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP) and the other targeting interleukin-13 (IL-13), is currently under phase 2 clinical investigation for multiple autoimmune diseases. Here, we reported for the first time the translational pharmacology of CM512.MethodsWe measured the binding affinity of CM512 to each cytokine and tested its ability to block TSLP- or IL-13-mediated biological activity. CM512 was also evaluated in an OVA-induced mouse asthma model to assess its therapeutic effects on type 2 inflammation. Pharmacokinetics were characterized in cynomolgus monkeys to test its half-life.ResultsCM512 bound both TSLP and IL-13 with high affinity and effectively prevented their receptor engagement. In vitro bioassays demonstrated that CM512 potently inhibited the activation of downstream signaling pathways, cell proliferation, and the release of inflammatory factors induced by either TSLP or IL-13. Moreover, CM512 showed greater inhibition of type 2 inflammatory responses compared with single-target antibodies against TSLP or IL-13. An in vivo study further confirmed that CM512 robustly inhibited type 2 inflammatory responses and alleviated disease symptoms. Pharmacokinetic analyses revealed a circulating half-life of approximately 396 to 557 h in cynomolgus monkeys.ConclusionsOur study establishes that dual TSLP/IL-13 inhibition achieves broad suppression of type 2 inflammatory pathways, overcoming limitations of single-cytokine therapies and demonstrating greater inhibition than single blockade efficacy in type 2-driven diseases. These findings provide the first translational evidence supporting the potential of dual cytokine blockade for treating refractory type 2 inflammatory conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08012-5.Keywords: Bispecific antibody, CM512, IL-13, TSLP, Type 2 inflammatory diseases

Introduction

Type 2 inflammatory diseases are a category of chronic inflammatory conditions primarily characterized by the abnormal activation of type 2 immune responses. These diseases, including asthma, atopic dermatitis (AD), chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP), eosinophilic esophagitis and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), present a substantial and ever-increasing global health challenge, affecting more than 30% of the global population, significantly impairing the quality of patients’ lives and imposing a substantial economic burden on individuals and society [1–3]. Type 2 immune responses, mainly conferred by JAK-STAT signaling pathway, are orchestrated through a complex interplay of various cytokines and immune cells, such as alarmin cytokines, type 2 cytokines, IgE, eosinophils, and mast cells [4].

Emerging targeted therapies have revolutionized the treatment landscape for type 2 inflammatory diseases, but patients with autoimmune diseases may still experience incomplete disease control. In the phase III NAVIGATOR trial, tezepelumab significantly reduced the annualized asthma exacerbation rate (AAER) by 56% compared to placebo [5]. In addition, tezepelumab reported also a modest 17% reduction in the rate of moderate to severe COPD exacerbations overall [6]. IL-13 inhibitors have also showed improvements in EASI scores (mean difference of -20.37, 95% CI -32.28 to -8.47) and higher rates of pruritus reduction in atopic dermatitis [7]. Dupilumab, which targets the IL4/IL13-JAK-STAT6 signaling pathway, has also demonstrated certain therapeutic effects in various autoimmune diseases. Treatment of dupilumab has achieved a 50% EASI-75 response rate in atopic dermatitis at 16 weeks [8], a 47.7% reduction in asthma exacerbations [9], and the modest improvements in chronic spontaneous urticaria scores (Δ15.86 vs. placebo) [10], approximately 30% reduction for the rate of moderate to severe COPD exacerbations [11, 12]. To break through the efficacy ceilings observed with single-target therapies in the treatment of type 2 inflammatory diseases, concurrent targeting of multiple cytokines is expected to achieve better therapeutic outcomes through potential synergies between targets. Currently, several multi-target agents are undergoing early clinical investigations, such as thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP) /interleukin-13 (IL-13) bispecific nanobody (lunsekimig), TSLP/ interleukin-4 receptor alpha subunit (IL-4Rα) bispecific antibody (IBI-3002), interleukin-31(IL-31)/IL-4Rα bispecific antibody (NM26-2198), TSLP/IL-4/IL-13 tri-specific antibody (PF-07275315), and IL-33/IL-4/IL-13 tri-specific antibody (PF-07264660).

Among them, TSLP serves as an upstream initiation cytokine of type 2 inflammatory responses, inducing the differentiation of Th2 cells and promoting the secretion of type 2 cytokines (IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, etc.) [13, 14]. IL-13 is a pivotal cytokine implicated in the pathogenesis of type 2 inflammatory diseases, promoting B cell proliferation, IgE class switching, and eosinophil recruitment [15, 16]. IL-13 acts on peripheral tissue cells, driving airway hyperresponsiveness, mucus production, and tissue remodeling [17]. Concurrently targeting TSLP and IL-13 not only inhibits the initiation of the inflammatory cascade reaction from an early stage, effectively reducing Th2 cell differentiation and the release of type 2 cytokines, but also directly inhibits IL-13-driven pathological symptoms, thereby offering a more effective treatment strategy. Preliminary data from a phase I clinical trial of lunsekimig has shown a reduction in FeNO levels by 40.9 ppb. This represents a significant improvement compared to reductions observed in trials of anti-TSLP and anti-IL-13 monotherapies, which typically do not exceed 15 ppb [18]. The above highlights the potential of simultaneously targeting TSLP and IL-13 strategy in the treatment of type 2 inflammatory diseases. CM512 is an innovative asymmetric bispecific antibody, designed with one arm targeting TSLP and the other arm targeting IL-13. The heavy-chain heterodimerization was achieved through the knob-into-hole (KIH) design, and charge-pairing engineering was incorporated in the CH1/CL interface to minimize light-chain mispairing, ensuring correct assembly of the asymmetric bispecific format. Currently, it is undergoing phase 2 clinical trials for the treatment of AD, CRSwNP, asthma, COPD, and other type 2 inflammatory diseases.

In this study, we comprehensively evaluated the translational pharmacology of CM512, a bispecific antibody simultaneously targeting thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP) and interleukin-13 (IL-13). The study was designed to elucidate its molecular binding characteristics, blocking potency, and functional activity, as well as its in vivo pharmacodynamics and pharmacokinetic behavior. We first compared single versus dual cytokine blockade using RNA-seq analyses of human PBMCs to determine additive transcriptional effects. Binding kinetics and blocking efficiency were quantified by surface-plasmon-resonance (SPR) and ligand-competition assays, followed by in vitro evaluation of downstream STAT5/STAT6 activation, cell proliferation, and TARC release. In vivo efficacy was verified in OVA-induced hTSLP/hTSLPR and hIL-13 humanized mouse asthma models, while pharmacokinetics and immunogenicity were characterized in cynomolgus monkeys. Statistical analyses included four-parameter logistic regression to calculate IC₅₀ values, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc multiple-comparison test for group differences, and values were considered significant at p < 0.05. Collectively, these integrated preclinical studies establish a robust translational framework supporting the clinical development of CM512 and highlight its potential to overcome the efficacy limitations of single-cytokine biologics in type 2 inflammatory diseases.

Methods

RNA sequencing and data analysis

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from healthy donors were co-stimulated with TSLP and IL-13 and assigned to three antibody-treatment groups (5 biological replicates per group): anti-TSLP, anti-IL-13, or combined anti-TSLP plus anti-IL-13. Total RNA integrity was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). Sequencing libraries were prepared from poly(A)-selected mRNA using the NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions to generate non-strand-specific libraries. Indexed libraries were quantified and quality assessed (Qubit and qPCR), pooled at equimolar concentrations, and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate 150-bp paired-end reads. Raw reads were quality-filtered using fastp, (v0.23.2) [19] and clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.1.0) [20]. Resulting alignment files (BAM) were sorted and indexed using samtools (v1.14) to facilitate quantification and quality assessment [21].​ Gene-level read counts were obtained with featureCounts (v2.0.6) [22] in unstranded mode. Differential expression was performed in DESeq2 (v1.42.0) using the Wald test with Benjamini–Hochberg multiple-testing correction; genes with adjusted P value (FDR) ≤ 0.05 and |log2(fold change)| ≥ 1 were considered differentially expressed. Functional enrichment analyses for Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathways were conducted using the clusterProfiler R package (v4.10.0).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) validation of selected genes

To validate the transcriptomic results, quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed on representative genes identified from the RNA-seq analysis. Total RNA was extracted from PBMCs treated under identical stimulation and blockade conditions as described above using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). One microgram of RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (AllStyle Gold Reverse Transcription Kit (Cat#41006885, AllStyle Biotech, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. qPCR was conducted on a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA) using SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The amplification protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Melt-curve analysis was performed for each reaction to confirm single-product amplification.

Primers were designed using NCBI RefSeq sequences and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). All primer pairs were verified by BLAST to ensure specificity and absence of secondary structures. Each reaction was run in triplicate, including no-template controls. Gene expression levels were normalized to the housekeeping gene β-actin, and relative mRNA expression was calculated using the 2^–ΔΔCt method, with the single-antibody treatment group as the reference for assessing expression changes induced by dual blockade.

All qPCR data are presented as mean ± SD from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc multiple-comparison test (GraphPad Prism 9.0, GraphPad Software, USA). Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Cell lines

Each cell line was cultured in its specific medium and maintained at 37 °C in an incubator with 5% CO₂. All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling and confirmed to be mycoplasma-negative through PCR analysis. HEK293 cells (ATCC) were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) and 2 mM L-glutamine. HEK293-STAT6 reporter cells were generated by stably transfecting HEK293 cells with a STAT6-inducible luciferase reporter gene. The other genes involved in the IL-13 pathway are naturally expressed in HEK293 cells. BA/F3 cells (RIKEN) were cultured in RPMI-1640 with 10% FBS, 2 mM L-glutamine. Mouse IL-3 (10 ng/mL) was added during cell passaging. The BA/F3-TSLPR-IL7Rα cells were generated by stably transfecting BA/F3 cells with human TSLPR and human IL-7Rα. Additionally, BA/F3-TSLPR-IL7Rα cells were further transfected with a STAT5-inducible luciferase reporter gene to create a BA/F3-STAT5 reporter cell line. TF-1 cells (ATCC) were maintained in RPMI-1640 with additional supplements including 10% FBS, 2 mM L-glutamine, 1% sodium pyruvate, 0·4% glucose, 1% HEPES, 2·7% sodium bicarbonate, and 2 ng/mL GM-CSF. All lines were authenticated by short‑tandem‑repeat profiling and confirmed mycoplasma‑free by PCR.

Affinity determination(SPR)

SPR assays were conducted on Biacore T200 (GE Healthcare) to investigate the binding kinetics of CM512 to TSLP and IL-13 from human, cynomolgus monkey, respectively. To determine the binding kinetics, CM512 was captured by Protein A chip (Cytiva). Then, serially diluted TSLP or IL-13 protein of human and cynomolgus monkey was introduced as an analyte, flowing over the chip at a rate of 30 µL per minute. The association step was set to 120 s, followed by a dissociation step of up to 1000s followed by a regeneration step using glycine HCl (pH2.0). Directional capture of antibodies on the Protein A chip is reversible, and the surface was regenerated to its initial state using glycine–HCl (pH 2.0) between cycles. The fitting parameters to determine on -and off-rates were obtained from global fit of multi-cycle kinetics with Langmuir 1:1 kinetics model with mass transfer model using Biacore T200 Evaluation software (version 3.1) of the interaction between CM512 and TSLP or IL-13. The Langmuir 1:1 model is widely applied in surface plasmon resonance (SPR) studies because it provides a clear and reproducible description of simple bimolecular interactions and allows reliable derivation of kinetic parameters under well-controlled conditions.

Then we used SPR to investigate the effect of CM512 on IL13 blocking. The mechanism by which IL-13 exerts its biological effects is as follows: it first binds with low affinity to its specific receptor subunit IL-13Rα1, and the resulting complex then recruits the IL-4Rα subunit to assemble into a high-affinity complex (IL-13/IL-13Rα1/IL-4Rα). Stepwise binding indicates that subsequent to initial binding, IL-13 and its receptor subunit (IL-13Rα1) undergoes conformational rearrangement, which in turn exposes a novel interface for interaction with IL-4Rα [23, 24]. We observed that CM512 exerts no impact on the binding between IL-13 and IL-13Rα1 (Supplementary Figure S1).

Therefore, to evaluate the blocking efficiency of CM512, 30ug/ml of IL-4Rα was immobilized on a CM5 chip by amino coupling. Subsequently, the IL-13/IL-13Rα1 complex was incubated with gradient diluted CM512 and then passed over the chip surface as the analyte. The binding signal of the analyte was used to evaluate the activity of CM512 in blocking the binding of IL-13/IL-13Rα1 complex to IL-4Rα.

Blocking activity of CM512

96-well plates were coated with 100 µL per well of human TSLPR-hFc protein at a concentration of 1 µg/mL and incubated overnight at 4 C. After washing and blocking, 100 µL of the mixed solution of diluted antibodies and 200 ng/mL TSLP-mFc was added to each well and incubated at room temperature for 60 min. Then the plates were washed, and 100 µL of Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse antibody was added to each well and incubated for 60 min at room temperature. The plates were washed and developed color by adding 100 µL of 3,3,,5,5,-Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate solution to each well for 30 min at 37℃ in the dark. The reaction was stopped with 50 µL of 2 M Sulfuric acid solution. The absorbance of the wells was read at 450 nm using an ELISA reader (Spectra Max i3x). The data was analyzed using SoftMax which is included with the instrument and a four-parameter logistic regression analysis was employed to calculate the half maximum inhibition concentration (IC50).

TSLPR overexpressed cell line BA/F3-TSLPR-IL7Rα cells were collected and centrifuged at 4°C, followed by adjusting the cell density to 1 × 107 cells/mL with blocking buffer on ice for 30 min. Mix the antibody dilution with the TSLP solution in 1:1 ratio and incubate at 37℃ for 30 min. Add 100 µL of antibody-TSLP mixture to each well with 5 × 104 BA/F3-TSLPR-IL7Rα cells and incubate at 4℃ for 60 min. After incubation, cells were washed twice with flow cytometry buffer and labeled with the binding of test samples on the cell surface with 50 µL of secondary antibody dilution solution per well on ice for 20 min. At last, add 50 µL of PI solution to each well and incubate on ice for 5 min after washing. Cells were resuspended by 50 µL of PBS, and a flow cytometer was used to read the average fluorescence intensity of the cells and record the determination results.

Luciferase reporter assay for STAT5/STAT6 Activation

HEK293-STAT6 reporter cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 104 cells per well in a volume of 50 µL and cultured under 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 16–18 h. The cells were then incubated with IL-13 (2ng/ml) in the presence of either CM512 or anti-KLH-hIgG1 at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 6 h.

BA/F3-STAT5 reporter cells were collected and washed by PBS and resuspended with an appropriate amount of 10% FBS/RPMI1640 solution, free of antibiotics and mouse IL-3 cytokines.

The cells were starved in 10%FBS/RPMI1640 without mouse IL-3 cytokines and cultured overnight at 37℃ and 5% CO2. Then 5 × 104 of cells per well were incubated with TSLP (12ng/ml) in the presence of either gradient diluted CM512 or anti-KLH-hIgG1 at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 6 h.

Remove the plate to be tested and equilibrate it to room temperature. Add 100 µL of luciferase detection reagent, also equilibrated to room temperature, to each well, and allow it to stand for approximately 5 min until complete cell lysis occurs. Measure the cell luminescence intensity using a microplate reader and record the results. Analyze the data using GraphPad Prism software version 9.0, with the concentration of the test sample as the horizontal axis and the cell luminescence intensity (expressed in relative light units) as the vertical axis, and calculate the IC50 value using the four-parameter logistic regression method.

Cell proliferation assay

TF-1 or BA/F3-TSLPR-IL7Rα cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in a volume of 100 µL and cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 20 h, respectively. Subsequently, CM512 or anti-KLH hIgG1 were prepared as serial dilutions and mixed with an equal volume of IL-13 (200 ng/ml) or TSLP (20 ng/ml). Aliquots of 100 µL from each mixture was added to the wells and cultured for 96 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cell viability was measured using the Cell Titer-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay kit. The results were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software 9.0 and the IC50 values were determined through a four-parameter logistic regression analysis.

TARC release assay

PBMCs were resuspended in culture medium and seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well in a volume of 100 µL. CM512, anti-IL-13 monoclonal antibody, anti-TSLP monoclonal antibody and negative control anti-KLH hIgG1 were prepared as serial dilutions and mixed with an equal volume of IL-13 or TSLP (40ng/ml synergistically or 20ng/ml solely), respectively. Subsequently, 100 µL of each mixture was added to the wells and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 96 h. The levels of TARC in the cell culture supernatants were measured using a human TARC ELISA kit. Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software 9.0, applying a four-parameter logistic regression method to determine the IC50 values.

OVA-induced hTSLP/hTSLPR humanized mouse asthma models

The hTSLP/hTSLPR mice (female, 18–21 g) were randomly divided by body weight into six groups of 12 mice each based on body weight: a blank control group, a model group, CM512 treatment groups at doses of 3, 10, and 30 mg/kg, and a negative control KLH group at 30 mg/kg. Randomization was performed by body-weight stratification to minimize baseline variability. Blinding was not implemented in the original study, and this limitation has been explicitly acknowledged. No a priori power calculation was conducted; the group size (n = 12) followed established OVA-model practice and feasibility constraints. On the first day of the study (Day 0), mice were sensitized by multiple injections of OVA adjuvanted with Al(OH)3. On Day 14, they received a booster injection of OVA adjuvanted with Al(OH)3. From Day 21 to 27, mice were challenged with aerosolized OVA solution. The first administration of the study drug was given the day before sensitization (Day − 1). From Day − 1 to Day 19, the drug was administered every four days at specific time points (Day − 1, Day 4, Day 9, Day 14, and Day 19), totaling five administrations. From Day 21 to Day 27, the drug was administered every other day, totaling four administrations, two hours before OVA challenge, via subcutaneous injection. The blank control group and the model group were administered the vehicle solution for CM512 subcutaneously. This dosing schedule therefore included both prophylactic (pre-sensitization) and therapeutic (post-sensitization) phases, allowing evaluation of CM512 under conditions that better simulate real-world clinical treatment.

During the experiment period, general symptoms were observed daily, and mouse body weight was measured before each administration. On Day 20 and Day 28, blood was collected from the retro-orbital venous plexus of the mice, and serum total IgE and OVA-specific IgE levels were detected using ELISA. On Day 27, the number of nose scratches and sneezes within 30 min after the OVA challenge was counted. On Day 28, airway hyperresponsiveness induced by Mch was measured using the EMKA animal pulmonary function monitoring system. Bronchoalveolar lavage was performed, and the total number of white blood cells in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was counted under a microscope. Wright-Giemsa staining was used to count eosinophils, macrophages, and lymphocytes. Lung tissue RNA was extracted to detect the mRNA expression levels of IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, h-TSLP, and MUC5AC using q-PCR. Lung tissue was fixed with 10% neutral buffered formalin solution, and after complete fixation, pathological sections were made. H&E staining was used to observe eosinophil infiltration in lung tissue and to perform pathological scoring; PAS staining was used to observe and count the positive rate of goblet cell hyperplasia in the airway; toluidine blue staining was used to observe mast cell degranulation in lung tissue and to count the degranulation rate; Masson’s staining was used to observe airway remodeling and collagen deposition around the airway, and the positive area expression rate was calculated.

OVA-induced hIL-13 humanized mouse asthma models

The B-hIL13 mice (female, 18–21 g) were randomly divided by body weight into three groups of 6 mice each based on body weight: a blank control group, a model group, and a CM512 treatment group at a dose of 30 mg/kg. Randomization was performed by body-weight stratification to minimize baseline variability. Blinding was not implemented in the original study, and this limitation has been explicitly acknowledged. No a priori power calculation was conducted; the group size (n = 6) followed established OVA-model practice and feasibility constraints. On Day 0, mice were sensitized by intraperitoneal (i.p.) and subcutaneous (s.c.) injections of 1 mg OVA combined with 5 mg Al(OH)3. On Day 14, a booster sensitization was performed via intraperitoneal injection of 0·4 mg OVA combined with 2 mg Al(OH)3. From Day 21 to Day 27, mice were challenged daily with aerosolized 1% OVA solution for 30 min each day.

CM512 was administered via subcutaneous injection at a dose of 30 mg/kg. Administration began the day before sensitization (Day − 1) and continued every five days (Q5D) for a total of five times (Days − 1, 4, 9, 14, and 19). During the challenge phase (Day 21 to Day 27), CM512 was administered every other day (Q2D), totaling four administrations (Days 21, 23, 25, and 27), two hours before OVA challenge. The control and model groups received subcutaneous injections of the vehicle solution at corresponding time points. This dosing schedule therefore included both prophylactic (pre-sensitization) and therapeutic (post-sensitization) phases, allowing evaluation of CM512 under conditions that better simulate real-world clinical treatment.

Throughout the experimental period, general symptoms were observed daily, and mouse body weight was recorded regularly. On Day 20 and Day 28, serum was collected to measure total IgE and OVA-specific IgE levels using ELISA. On Days 21 and 27, the number of nose scratches and sneezes within 30 min post-OVA challenge was recorded. On Day 28, airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR) was evaluated using the EMKA animal pulmonary function monitoring system following methacholine (Mch) inhalation.

BALF was collected on Day 28, and the number of inflammatory cells, including eosinophils, macrophages, and lymphocytes, was counted after Wright-Giemsa staining. Lung tissue RNA was extracted for q-PCR analysis of IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, and MUC5AC mRNA levels. Lung tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for histological analysis. H&E staining was used to evaluate eosinophil infiltration in lung tissues, PAS staining assessed goblet cell hyperplasia, and toluidine blue staining determined mast cell degranulation rates. Histological results were quantified to assess the treatment effects of CM512 in the OVA-induced B-hIL13 humanized mouse asthma model.

PK and immunogenicity studies in cynomolgus monkeys

Study Design and Grouping: To investigate the pharmacokinetic characteristics and absolute bioavailability of CM512 following single subcutaneous (SC) or intravenous (IV) administration in cynomolgus monkeys, 24 monkeys with no prior drug exposure were randomly divided into four groups, each consisting of 6 animals with an equal number of males and females. The groups included low (3 mg/kg), medium (10 mg/kg), and high (30 mg/kg) dose groups for subcutaneous injection, as well as an intravenous injection group (10 mg/kg). All animals received a single dose of CM512 at a volume of 0·5 mL/kg via subcutaneous or intravenous routes. Blood samples for drug concentration analysis were collected from the subcutaneous injection groups before dosing and at 4, 24, 48, 72, 120, 168, 336, 504, 672, 840, 1008, 1176, 1344, and 1512 h post-dosing. For the intravenous injection group, blood samples for drug concentration analysis were collected before dosing and at 5 min, 4, 8, 24, 48, 72, 120, 168, 336, 504, 672, 840, 1008, 1176, 1344, and 1512 h post-dosing. Blood samples for immunogenicity analysis were collected from all groups before dosing and at 168, 336,672, and 1512 h post-dosing. A validated ELISA method (method number: A2023058-ME-BA104-01) was used to detect the concentration of CM512 in the serum samples of all groups. The standard curve for CM512 ranged from 19·5 to 1250 ng/mL. Pharmacokinetic parameters for CM512 were calculated using the non-compartmental analysis (NCA) in Phoenix WinNonlin 8·2 software. A validated Bridging-ELISA method (method number: A2023058-ME-BA204-01) was used to detect the immunogenicity in the serum samples of all groups.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Homogeneity of variance was examined before comparison. For multiple-group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison test was applied.

IC₅₀ values for TSLP- or IL-13–induced BA/F3 proliferation, STAT5 activation, and PBMC-TARC release were calculated using a four-parameter logistic regression model.

A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For in-vivo data, mean ± SEM is reported, and exact statistical tests for each endpoint (one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s post-hoc test or Mann–Whitney U test) are specified in the figure legends. For variables showing only a statistical tendency (0.05 ≤ P < 0.1), the term “trend” is explicitly used to avoid over-interpretation. Although effect sizes and confidence intervals were not calculated retrospectively, future confirmatory studies will include these metrics and multiplicity control to further improve statistical robustness.

Results

Anti-TSLP in combination with anti-IL-13 shows a potentially additive effect against type 2 inflammation

PBMCs were treated simultaneously with TSLP and IL-13. The cytokines were blocked either individually or in combination to assess how dual blockade modulates type 2 immune responses. When compared with anti-TSLP alone, the combination exhibited a significantly broader transcriptional impact. A greater number of DEGs were identified, showing extensive downregulation of key type 2 inflammatory genes, including FCER2, SUCNR1, CCL18, ALOX15, CD200R1 and CD209, which were less prominently affected by anti-TSLP alone (Fig. 1A). Comparing the combination blockade to anti-IL-13 alone similarly demonstrated substantial differences, with the combination showing enhanced downregulation of key type 2 inflammatory genes, notably CCL17, CCL22 and FLT3 (Fig. 1A). The downregulated genes were further validated using QPCR (Supplementary Figure S2). qPCR analysis confirmed the RNA-seq results, showing that CM512 produced a stronger overall suppression of type 2-associated genes compared with single-cytokine blockade. In the IL-13 versus TSLP + IL-13 comparison, CM512 significantly reduced CCL17 expression compared with the anti-IL-13 antibody (P < 0.01), while CCL22 and FLT3 showed no significant differences. In the TSLP versus TSLP + IL-13 comparison, CM512 markedly downregulated FCER2, SUCNR1, CCL18, ALOX15, and CD209 (all P < 0.05). These qPCR data are consistent with the transcriptomic findings, confirming that dual TSLP/IL-13 blockade by CM512 leads to broader inhibition of type 2-inflammatory gene expression than either monotherapy. The analyses of both comparisons highlighted overlapping and additive effects, indicating that dual blockade leads to more comprehensive transcriptional modulation than single cytokine blockade. Hierarchical clustering of expressed genes showed that each treatment group has a unique transcriptional profile (Fig. 1B).

(A) Volcano plots of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from human PBMCs stimulated with TSLP + IL-13, comparing dual anti-TSLP/IL-13 versus single anti-TSLP (left) or anti-IL-13 (right). Red dots: DEGs (|log₂FC| ≥ 1, adjusted P ≤ 0.05; DESeq2 Wald test, Benjamini–Hochberg). (B) Heatmap of top-ranked DEGs across treatment conditions and donors (row-wise Z-scores); highlighted genes include Th2 (FLG, CCL17, CCL13) and IFN-responsive (CXCL11). (D) KEGG pathway enrichment for the same comparisons; top 15 significant pathways presented as − log₁₀(FDR) with gene counts indicated

Gene Ontology enrichment analysis further identified significant overrepresentation of immune-related and cellular processing categories (Fig. 1C). Inflammatory responses, especially leukocyte migration, leukocyte mediated immunity, chemokine activity and cytokine activity, were significantly enriched, suggesting a profound reprogramming of the immune system.

KEGG pathway analysis provided additional pathway-specific insights into these mechanisms, further confirming the findings from GO enrichment (Fig. 1D). Notably, the Th2 cell differentiation pathway was significantly enriched, reinforcing the perturbation of the IL-4/IL-13-driven Th2 lineage specification program observed with the combination treatment. Additional enriched KEGG pathways included chemokine signaling pathway, hematopoietic cell lineage and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction. These data indicate a mechanistic rationale for combining anti-TSLP and anti-IL-13 therapies, which shows additive efficacy against type 2 inflammatory diseases. Based on these findings, we develop CM512, a bispecific antibody that enhances suppression of type 2 immune responses through dual blocking of both cytokines.

CM512 binds with high affinity to TSLP and IL-13, thereby blocking TSLP binding to TSLPR and IL-13/IL-13Rα1 complex to IL-4Rα

CM512 is an asymmetric bispecific antibody designed with one arm targeting TSLP and the other targeting IL-13. The constant region of CM512 is of the IgG1 subtype and incorporates knob-into-hole mutations to reduce mismatches, as well as additional mutations to extend its half-life (Fig. 2A). In addition, a Supplementary Data file including the complete mature amino acid sequences for all four chains (anti-IL-13 heavy chain and κ light chain; anti-TSLP heavy chain and λ light chain), along with the subsequent engineering information of the V domains for both anti-IL-13 and anti-TSLP has been provided. The binding affinity of CM512 to TSLP or IL-13 was determined by surface plasmon resonance (SPR). CM512 demonstrated potent binding affinity to human TSLP and IL-13, with equilibrium dissociation constants of 3·39 pM and 2·95 pM, respectively, and similarly exhibited high affinity for cynomolgus monkey TSLP and IL-13, with KD values of 1·02 pM and 4·97 pM, respectively (Fig. 2B). Representative raw sensograms for these binding interactions are provided in Supplementary Figures S3–S4. While on rat and mouse TSLP or IL-13, CM512 showed no detectable binding activity (Supplementary Figure S5). The above data proved that the cynomolgus monkey was the related toxicity species for non-clinical safety evaluation since CM512 showed comparable affinity on target binding between human and cynomolgus monkey.

A ligand competition inhibition ELISA assay was conducted to evaluate the impact of CM512 on the TSLP-TSLPR interaction. It was shown that CM512 effectively blocked the binding of TSLP to TSLPR, with a half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of 7·26 nM (Fig. 2C). Then flow cytometry was further used to confirm the blocking of CM512 on the interaction between TSLP and TSLPR at the cellular level. CM512 achieved an effective blockade of TSLP-TSLPR interaction with an IC50 of 1·46 nM. (Fig. 2D). Then we used SPR to investigate the effect of CM512 on the interaction between the IL-13/IL-13Rα1 complex and IL-4Rα. Results showed that CM512 effectively blocked the binding of the IL-13/IL-13Rα1 complex to IL-4Rα (Fig. 2E). Collectively, these findings prove that CM512 exerts its mechanism of action through high-affinity dual antagonism, binding both TSLP and IL-13 to prevent TSLP from engaging the TSLPR complex and blocking IL-13/IL-13Rα1 from interacting with IL-4Rα.

CM512 demonstrates in vitro potent inhibition of TSLP and IL-13-mediated signaling pathways and biological activity critical to type 2 inflammation

Next, we explored the downstream signaling pathways and in vitro biological activity critical to type 2 inflammation after TSLP or IL-13 was blocked with CM512. TSLP, upon binding to the TSLPR/IL-7Rα receptor complex on the cell surface, primarily activates signal transduction and transcription activation factor 5 (STAT5) to drive type 2 inflammatory responses, whereas IL-13 also played an important role in type 2 inflammatory responses by binding to the IL-13Rα1/IL-4Rα receptor complex to mainly induces JAK-STAT6 pathway to activate downstream effector cells [25, 26]. Activation of STAT5 or STAT6 subsequently initiates the release of thymus activation-regulated chemokine (TARC). Accordingly, we conducted comprehensive in vitro evaluations to explore the inhibition of CM512 against signaling pathways or biological activity induced by TSLP or IL-13 (Fig. 3A).

We first studied the potent inhibitory activity of CM512 against TSLP-mediated signaling pathways. In BA/F3-STAT5 reporter cells, CM512 inhibited TSLP-induced STAT5 activation with an IC₅₀ of 0·24 nM (Fig. 3B). In addition, CM512 suppressed TSLP-driven proliferation of BA/F3-TSLPR-IL7Rα cells, with an IC₅₀ of 1·67 nM (Fig. 3C), and significantly reduced TSLP-stimulated TARC release from PBMCs, with an IC₅₀ of 0·497 nM (Fig. 3D). In parallel, CM512 exhibited robust inhibition of IL-13-induced pathways, as evidenced by suppression of STAT6 activation in HEK293-STAT6 reporter cells (IC₅₀ = 0·027 nM, Fig. 3E), inhibition of IL-13-induced TF-1 cell proliferation (IC₅₀ = 4·31 nM, Fig. 3F), and blockade of IL-13-stimulated TARC release from PBMCs (IC₅₀ = 0·069 nM, Fig. 3G). Furthermore, in co-stimulation assays, CM512 achieved a maximum inhibition rate of 97·4% for TARC release from PBMCs induced by both TSLP and IL-13, which was substantially greater than that observed with either anti-TSLP (53·6%) or anti-IL-13 (74·0%) alone (Fig. 3H), thereby demonstrating the additive efficacy of dual cytokine blockade. Consistent with previous findings, blockade of TSLP or IL-13 signaling has been shown to cause consistent changes in STAT5 and STAT6 protein levels. These findings support our observations and will be further validated in future work.

In summary, in vitro pharmacodynamic experiments demonstrated that CM512 effectively inhibits the activation of downstream signaling pathways, cell proliferation, and the release of inflammatory factor induced by TSLP and IL-13. Additionally, CM512 effectively inhibits the additive effect of TSLP and IL-13, with stronger activity than anti-TSLP or anti-IL-13 agents.

CM512 alleviates disease severity in the OVA-induced mouse asthma model

To validate the in vivo efficacy of CM512, an OVA-induced hTSLP/hTSLPR humanized mouse asthma models was first used (Fig. 4A). Phenotypically, CM512 treatment dose-dependently reduced allergic nasal symptoms, with nose-scratching behavior inhibited by 7·08%, 26·15%, and 49·54%, and sneezing episodes by 9·89%, 26·37%, and 36·26% at 3, 10, and 30 mg/kg, respectively (Fig. 4B). CM512 also significantly attenuated airway hyperresponsiveness to methacholine, reducing airway resistance by 23·72%, 46·33%, and 57·04% at these doses (Fig. 4C). At the cellular level, BALF analysis showed that CM512 relieved pulmonary inflammation, with total leukocyte counts in BALF reduced by 17·16%, 28·82%, and 41·03%, and eosinophils by 32·39%, 58·74%, and 69·87%, at 3, 10, and 30 mg/kg, respectively (Fig. 4D–G). Molecularly, CM512 induced a dose-dependent reduction in serum IgE levels, decreasing by 7·81%, 49·91%, and 56·12% at doses of 3, 10, and 30 mg/kg, respectively (Fig. 4H). Concurrently, it exhibited marked attenuation of ovalbumin-specific IgE production. Pathologically, CM512 treatment resulted in significant improvements in lung histopathology compared to vehicle controls (Fig. 4I–J). Specifically, CM512 markedly reduced airway and perivascular eosinophilic infiltration, suppressed mucus hypersecretion and goblet cell hyperplasia, attenuated mast cell degranulation, and decreased peribronchial collagen deposition. Quantitative histological analyses were performed to support these observations, including inflammation scoring from H&E staining, PAS-positive epithelial cell percentage for goblet-cell hyperplasia, degranulated-to-total mast-cell ratio from toluidine-blue staining, and collagen-positive area percentage from Masson’s trichrome staining. Next, in a parallel OVA-induced hIL-13 humanized mouse asthma model, CM512 exhibited also therapeutic efficacy. Pharmacokinetics and immunogenicity of CM512 in cynomolgus monkeys

In cynomolgus monkeys, PK parameters were evaluated following a single subcutaneous (SC) administration of CM512 at doses of 3, 10, and 30 mg/kg, and a single intravenous (IV) administration at a dose of 10 mg/kg. The time to peak plasma concentration (Tmax) ranged from 24 to 120 h after SC administration. Drug exposure, including the maximum concentration (Cmax) and area under the curve (AUC0−t), showed approximate dose-exposure proportionality of 1:2·4:8·1 for Cmax and 1:2·2:7·6 for AUC0−t, respectively, indicating a nearly-exposure linear PK profile for CM512 within the tested dose range. As for IV administration, Tmax was 0·0833 h. The Cmax and AUC0−t values were approximately 250 ± 24 µg/mL and 100,000 ± 12,700 h*µg/mL, respectively. The bioavailability of a single SC dose was calculated to be 63·8%. The circulating mean half-life of CM512 was ranged from 396 to 557 h in cynomolgus monkeys (Fig. 5, the mean ± SD half-life of 3\10\30 mg/kg SC group and 10 mg/kg IV group was 548 ± 280 h、396 ± 265 h、530 ± 185 h、557 ± 135 h). Results above demonstrate that CM512 is slowly cleared from the system and support the extended dosing regimen for administration.

Discussion

In this study, we comprehensively evaluated the translational profile of CM512, a bispecific antibody with high affinity for both TSLP and IL-13. CM512 effectively blocks the binding of TSLP to its receptor TSLPR, as well as the binding of IL-13/IL-13Rα1 complex to IL-4Rα, thereby inhibiting the activation of the downstream signaling pathway, cell proliferation, and the release of inflammatory factors induced by either TSLP or IL-13. Moreover, CM512 significantly suppresses the additive effect of TSLP and IL-13, demonstrating greater activity than blocking either cytokine alone. Furthermore, subcutaneous administration of CM512 significantly alleviated inflammatory symptoms in the OVA-induced mouse asthma model, highlighting its potential therapeutic role in the treatment of these diseases. Consistent findings across multiple experimental models, including cell-based assays, cytokine release tests, and humanized mouse studies, support the robustness of CM512’s pharmacological effects. The complementary design of these in vitro and in vivo systems allowed cross-validation of target engagement and biological efficacy under different physiological contexts.

In FcRn-humanized mice, the LA (M428L/N434A) substitutions in the Fc region of CM512 significantly prolonged systemic exposure compared with the wild-type IgG1 (t₁/₂ = 17.1 days vs. 11.2 days; Supplementary Figure S7). The Fc effector function of CM512 is intentionally preserved. Because both TSLP and IL-13 are soluble cytokines rather than cell-associated targets, maintaining Fc activity does not confer cytotoxic risk and supports normal antibody clearance. Consistent with this design, Phase 1 clinical data demonstrated a favorable safety profile without Fc-related adverse effects.

To further dissect the molecular mechanisms underlying these transcriptomic signatures, we next verified the RNA-seq results by qPCR and analyzed the specific Th2-associated genes affected by dual cytokine blockade. We selected a group of genes previously reported to positively regulate type 2 inflammation and Th2-axis activation for qPCR validation. The results showed that in the comparison between IL-13 single blockade and TSLP + IL-13 dual blockade, CCL17 exhibited a much stronger downregulation under the dual blockade, whereas CCL22 and FLT3 remained largely unchanged. CCL17 and CCL22 are CCR4 ligands that selectively recruit Th2 cells and sustain allergic inflammation; thus, the enhanced reduction of CCL17 by CM512 indicates that adding TSLP inhibition to IL-13 blockade further disrupts Th2-cell chemotaxis, consistent with the additive transcriptional suppression observed in RNA-seq. In contrast, FLT3, a critical regulator of dendritic-cell (DC) differentiation and homeostasis, did not decline further with the addition of IL-13 blockade, suggesting that TSLP signaling—rather than IL-13—is the dominant upstream modulator of DC-mediated Th2 priming, in agreement with previous studies on DC-dependent allergic airway inflammation.

In the comparison etween TSLP single blockade and the combined blockade, FCER2 (CD23), SUCNR1, CCL18, ALOX15, and CD209 (DC-SIGN) were all significantly downregulated by CM512. FCER2 encodes the low-affinity IgE receptor and is a positive regulator of IgE-mediated responses; its reduction suggests that CM512 simultaneously suppresses both the sensitization phase and effector amplification of allergic inflammation. SUCNR1, a succinate receptor linking metabolic stress to allergic inflammation, promotes mast-cell activation and bronchoconstriction through IgE-dependent signaling; its further decline under dual blockade indicates attenuation of the metabolic–immune amplification loop. CCL18 is an inducible Th2 cytokine target abundantly expressed in the lung and contributes to allergic inflammation by enhancing Th2 responses; its marked reduction agrees with the overall RNA-seq pattern showing broad downregulation of Th2-related pathways. ALOX15 (15-lipoxygenase-1), an IL-4/IL-13-responsive enzyme, catalyzes lipid mediator production that drives goblet-cell differentiation, mucus hypersecretion, and airway remodeling; its inhibition by CM512 provides molecular evidence of downstream tissue-level improvement. CD209, a dendritic-cell marker facilitating allergen recognition and promoting Th2 polarization, was also strongly decreased, suggesting that dual blockade dampens DC–T-cell positive feedback loops sustaining type 2 immunity.

Collectively, these qPCR data validate that the genes tested—CCL17, CCL22, FLT3, FCER2, SUCNR1, CCL18, ALOX15, and CD209—act as positive regulators of Th2 differentiation and type 2 inflammatory amplification, and their consistent downregulation at both transcriptomic and mRNA levels demonstrates that dual TSLP and IL-13 blockade exerts stronger and broader inhibition than either monotherapy. Together with the RNA-seq enrichment of the Th2 differentiation pathway, these results support the mechanistic model that combined inhibition simultaneously intercepts upstream epithelial-DC–Th2 initiation and downstream effector responses, thereby achieving additive and additive deactivation of the Th2 inflammatory cascade.

Pharmacokinetic analyses revealed a circulating half-life of approximately 396 to 557 h in cynomolgus monkeys, and the dose-exposure proportionality and PK curve all exhibit a linear property, together with the low levels of TLSP and IL-13 in cynomolgus monkey and human body, which indicates a low target-mediated disposition in humans. CM512 can induce some immunogenicity in cynomolgus monkeys, but due to species differences in the immune system, the results of ADA studies in animals can not be extrapolated to humans. Currently, CM512 is undergoing phase 2 clinical investigations for the treatment of AD, CRSwNP, asthma and COPD.

TSLP is an epithelial-derived alarmin that initiates upstream immune responses by promoting dendritic cell-mediated priming of Th2 cells and activating group 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s) [27]. In contrast, interleukin-13 (IL-13) functions as a downstream effector cytokine, contributing to airway hyperresponsiveness, mucus hypersecretion, and tissue remodeling [28]. Anti-TSLP monotherapy, exemplified by tezepelumab, significantly reduces exacerbation rates in both type 2-high and type 2-low asthma phenotypes [29]. By contrast, IL-13-targeted biologics yield inconsistent reductions in exacerbations despite consistently improving lung function [30]. These clinical observations imply that TSLP and IL-13 mediate overlapping yet distinct pathological processes. By inhibiting TSLP and IL-13 simultaneously, CM512 is able to shut down both the initiation of type 2 inflammation and its end-organ effector functions. Our data support this additive effect. Dual blockade yielded additive suppression of type 2 output, including chemokine CCL24 and ALOX15 expression, that single-cytokine blockade could not achieve. These molecules are known markers of allergic inflammation and Th2 activity. Moreover, elevated CCL13 and CCL17 expressions are characteristic of Th2-driven responses, and increased VCAM1 expression on endothelium mediates the recruitment of eosinophils and T cells. This aligns with the notion that targeting an upstream cytokine alone may leave residual activity from downstream mediators, and vice versa. Indeed, research by others has suggested that combined inhibition of TSLP and IL-13 can produce additive benefits in immune-mediated disease models [31].

In allergic airway disease, previous preclinical and clinical data show that blockade of a single cytokine can partially attenuate inflammation but rarely abolishes disease activity. Selective inhibition of TSLP mainly suppresses upstream epithelial activation and Th2 priming, while IL-13 blockade primarily limits mucus secretion, airway remodeling, and IgE class switching. Because these cytokines act at distinct levels of the type 2 cascade, inhibition of one leaves the other’s signaling largely intact, resulting in residual inflammatory activity. In contrast, dual inhibition of TSLP and IL-13 has been shown to suppress both initiation and effector phases of type 2 inflammation. Experimental studies have demonstrated that combined targeting of these pathways leads to greater reductions in eosinophilic infiltration, cytokine release, and airway hyperresponsiveness than monotherapy. These findings align with our observations that CM512 produces additive suppression of Th2-associated chemokines and effector molecules compared with single-pathway blockade. Together, these data underscore the biological rationale for dual-target approaches in diseases where upstream epithelial cues and downstream cytokine effectors act in concert to sustain chronic inflammation.

In summary, the mechanistic advantage of dual TSLP/IL-13 targeting lies in its ability to concurrently intercept an upstream driver of type 2 immunity and a key downstream effector, thereby delivering a more comprehensive immunomodulation.

IL-4Rα blockade with dupilumab has transformed the management of moderate-to-severe AD. Nevertheless, complete lesion clearance is achieved in only a minority of patients. In the pivotal LIBERTY AD SOLO 1 and SOLO 2 phase 3 trials, 37% and 36% of participants, respectively, reached an Investigator’s Global Assessment (IGA) score of 0 or 1 (clear or almost clear) after 16 weeks of dupilumab monotherapy [8, 32]. This indicates that even in AD, which is prototypically Th2-driven, alternative inflammatory pathways can sustain disease activity when one cytokine is blocked. In asthma, despite the availability of multiple biologics targeting IgE, IL-5/IL-5R, IL-4/IL-13, or TSLP, many patients continue to have exacerbations, airflow limitation, and poor quality of life [34]. Heterogeneity in asthma endotypes means that a single cytokine blockade often cannot fully control the disease - for example, an eosinophilic asthmatic on anti-IL-5 may still have airway hyperreactivity due to epithelial cytokines like TSLP, whereas a patient on anti-IL-4Rα may still experience neutrophilic inflammation or other non–type 2 processes driving exacerbations. Our findings are corroborated by recent reports on lunsekimig, a bispecific antibody targeting both TSLP and IL-13. In a Phase 1b proof-of-mechanism trial in asthma, administration of a single 400 mg dose resulted in rapid improvements across multiple biomarkers of type 2 inflammation [35]. Notably, lunsekimig treatment resulted in a significant reduction in fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) which is a recognized biomarker of persistent type 2 airway inflammation. This was accompanied by broad suppression of type 2 immune activity and clinically meaningful improvements in lung function in patients with mild-to-moderate asthma [35]. Gene expression analyses in lunsekimig-treated patients likewise showed downregulation of epithelial-derived type 2 chemokines, like CCL26 [35]. Furthermore, researches strongly supports the concept that dual TSLP/IL-13 inhibition yields broad immunological benefits: by concurrently targeting an upstream inducer and a downstream effector of type 2 inflammation, this approach dampens allergic inflammatory circuits more effectively than single-pathway antagonism [36–39]. Our study’s results are in agreement with these findings and provide further molecular insight into how dual blockade normalizes immune cell profiles and gene expression in type 2-driven disease. Compared with Lunsekimig, with a reported serum half-life of about 10 days in humans [40], CM512 exhibited a substantially longer half-life in cynomolgus monkeys (396–537 h) and achieved more pronounced functional outcomes in preclinical models. CM512 not only inhibited TSLP- and IL-13-induced STAT6 activation and cytokine release in vitro but also alleviated airway inflammation and remodeling in vivo, while published data for lunsekimig have thus far been limited to biomarker modulation. These results indicate that CM512 provides a broader and more durable blockade of type 2 inflammatory pathways, although direct clinical comparison remains to be established.

In this context, a dual cytokine inhibitor like CM512 offers a more robust therapeutic effect by simultaneously addressing multiple inflammatory axes. Because TSLP is an upstream master switch released at epithelial barriers and IL-13 is a central effector in tissue inflammation, their dual blockade could translate to clinical benefits across a spectrum of atopic and immune-mediated disorders. Beyond asthma and AD, other conditions characterized by type 2 immunity, such as CRSwNP, eosinophilic esophagitis, and food allergies, may likewise benefit from a combined TSLP/IL-13 targeting strategy. The broad activity of CM512 also raises the possibility of treating patients with multiple atopic comorbidities. For example, an individual with co-existing eczema, asthma, and nasal polyps could have all three conditions addressed concurrently by dual TSLP/IL-13 inhibition. Although the animal sample size was relatively limited, the statistical differences observed across multiple endpoints remained consistent and robust, supporting the reliability of the findings.

Preclinical data show that CM512 has a significant effect on type 2 inflammation in mouse models, which is consistent with the well-established role of TSLP in driving Th2 responses. In our clinical trials, we will select subjects with elevated Th2 cytokines (IL-4, IL-5, IL-13) through pharmacodynamic markers (PD markers) to ensure relevance to the targeted mechanism of action of CM512 [35–37].

In conclusion, CM512 is a bispecific antibody targeting both TSLP and IL-13, demonstrating high affinity and potent blocking activity against their signaling pathways. Our preclinical studies of CM512 provide robust evidence supporting the potential of this dual-targeting strategy in the treatment of type 2 inflammatory diseases. Currently, CM512 is under phase 2 clinical investigation for multiple autoimmune diseases.
