Fat-soluble vitamin deficiency in children with neurodevelopmental disorders: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jingjing Rao, Xiaomei Wang, Ping Zhou, Yun Xiang, Lei Xi, Changzhen Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) affect 15–20% of children worldwide, and emerging evidence suggests nutritional status may influence neurodevelopmental outcomes. However, disorder-specific patterns of fat-soluble vitamin deficiency remain incompletely characterized in large clinical populations.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study of 14,911 children aged 1–18 years who underwent serum vitamin testing between 2022 and 2025 at a tertiary pediatric center in China. Participants included healthy controls (n = 6391) and children with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD; n = 4392), tic disorders (n = 2020), autism spectrum disorder (ASD; n = 1312), and comorbid neurodevelopmental disorders (n = 796). Serum concentrations of vitamin A, 25(OH)D, and vitamin E were measured using standardized assays. Group differences were assessed using age-, sex-, and season-adjusted analyses, including Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) and multivariable logistic regression.ResultsAfter adjustment, children with ADHD and tic disorders exhibited modestly lower serum 25(OH)D concentrations compared with controls (adjusted Cohen’s d = 0.273 for ADHD, small effect), whereas ASD profiles were largely comparable to controls. Vitamin A deficiency affected approximately one quarter of participants across all groups, with a modest increase in ADHD (odds ratio [OR] = 1.26, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.14–1.40; small effect size), indicating widespread background deficiency with a modest additive risk in ADHD. Although absolute vitamin E deficiency rates were low, children with ADHD showed higher relative odds of deficiency (OR = 2.91). Multiple concurrent deficiencies (≥ 2 vitamins) were more common in ADHD (OR = 2.81) and moderately increased in tic disorders (OR = 1.66), while triple deficiencies remained rare. Children with comorbid conditions showed intermediate patterns. Clustering analyses identified heterogeneous vitamin profiles, with low-vitamin patterns disproportionately represented in ADHD and tic-disorder groups.ConclusionsIn this large pediatric cohort, fat-soluble vitamin status differed across NDD subtypes. Children with ADHD exhibited higher burdens of vitamin D deficiency and multiple concurrent deficiencies, while ASD profiles were comparatively preserved. These findings highlight nutritional heterogeneity among pediatric NDD populations and underscore the need for prospective studies to clarify temporal relationships and clinical relevance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04853-5.Keywords: Neurodevelopmental disorders, ADHD, Autism spectrum disorder, Tic disorders, Vitamin D, Vitamin E, Nutritional deficiency, Children, Observational study, Real-world data

Background

Neurodevelopmental disorders, which include attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), autism spectrum disorder (ASD), and tic disorder, pose a major public health challenge and affect millions of children worldwide. Overall, approximately 15–20% of school-aged children are affected [1], with ADHD alone affecting 5–10% of children internationally [2], the prevalence of ASD reaching 1 in 36 children in the USA [3], and tic disorders affecting 0.3–0.9% of the pediatric population [4]. In addition to their immediate behavioral and cognitive manifestations, these disorders impose a significant long-term burden on affected individuals, families, and healthcare systems, with annual costs estimated at over $240 billion in the USA alone [5].

The pathophysiology of neurodevelopmental disorders involves complex interactions between genetic susceptibility, environmental factors, and developmental timing [6]. Given the emerging understanding of the role of nutrition in neurodevelopment, there is growing interest in the use of nutritional and micronutrient interventions as complementary treatments for neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs). This knowledge is critical for a paradigm shift from a one-size-fits-all approach to precision nutrition, where interventions are tailored to an individual’s nutritional and metabolic profile. Our study aims to close this gap. While genetic factors are known, with the heritability of ADHD and ASD estimated at 70–90% [7], there is increasing evidence that modifiable environmental factors, particularly nutritional status, can significantly influence both the risk and severity of these disorders [8]. The critical periods of brain development, which extend from the prenatal period to adolescence, are characterized by rapid neuronal proliferation, synaptogenesis, and myelination, which are highly dependent on adequate micronutrient availability [9].

Among the micronutrients, the fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, and K) play a particularly important role in neurodevelopment due to their involvement in fundamental neurobiological processes. Vitamin D acts as a neurosteroid hormone that regulates the expression of more than 1000 genes, including those that control the development of dopaminergic neurons, neurotransmitter synthesis, and synaptic plasticity [10]. New evidence shows that vitamin D receptors are widely distributed in the developing brain, with concentrations particularly high in regions critical for executive function and motor control [11]. Vitamin A, via its active metabolite retinoic acid, serves as a master transcriptional regulator that controls neuronal differentiation, axonal guidance, and synaptic plasticity [12]. As a primary lipid-soluble antioxidant, vitamin E protects the sensitive neuronal membranes from oxidative damage during periods of rapid brain growth [13].

Accumulating epidemiologic evidence suggests a link between vitamin deficiency and neurodevelopmental disorders, although results are inconsistent and limited by methodological constraints. Several population-based studies have reported higher rates of vitamin D deficiency in children with ADHD compared to normally developing peers, with prevalence estimates ranging from 60 to 90% in clinical populations [14, 15]. Similarly, maternal vitamin D deficiency during pregnancy has been associated with an increased risk of ADHD and ASD in offspring in large-scale cohort studies [16, 17]. Meta-analyses show an inverse dose–response relationship between vitamin D exposure in early childhood and the risk of neurodevelopmental disorder, suggesting possible causal mechanisms [18]. However, the existing literature suffers from several critical limitations that have hindered translation into clinical practice. First, most studies have focused on single vitamins in isolation, failing to capture the complex interplay of multiple micronutrients that characterize real-world nutritional status [19]. Second, sample sizes have generally been modest (typically < 500 participants), limiting statistical power to detect disorder-specific patterns or assess effect sizes with clinical significance [19]. Third, previous studies have rarely examined multiple neurodevelopmental disorders simultaneously using standardized methods, preventing direct comparison of diet-related susceptibilities across conditions [20]. Fourth, most studies have been conducted in Western populations, with limited representation of different geographic regions with different dietary patterns and fortification strategies [21]. Furthermore, current research has largely overlooked the potential for systematic patterns of multiple concurrent vitamin deficiencies in individuals, despite theoretical reasons to expect a clustering of fat-soluble vitamin deficiencies due to common absorption pathways and shared risk factors [22]. The clinical significance of such multivitamin deficiency patterns has not yet been explored, nor has their potential utility in identifying high-risk subgroups that may benefit from targeted nutritional interventions [23].

Recent advances in nutritional psychiatry have highlighted the promising effects of micronutrient interventions in neurodevelopmental disorders. For example, randomized controlled trials have shown positive effects of vitamin D supplementation on core symptoms of ASD [24] and combined vitamin-mineral therapies on emotional regulation in ADHD [25]. However, the lack of comprehensive baseline characterization of vitamin status across different neurodevelopmental disorders has limited our clear understanding of population-level nutritional patterns and their potential clinical relevance [26]. Without a detailed understanding of disorder-specific deficiency patterns, effect sizes, and clustering phenomena, clinicians lack sufficiently robust evidence to accurately characterize nutritional heterogeneity and to identify subgroups that may warrant closer nutritional assessment in future studies [27]. The identification of vitamin deficiency patterns in neurodevelopmental disorders also has broader implications for understanding disease mechanisms and developing new therapeutic targets. If certain vitamin deficiencies cluster within diagnostic categories, this could shed light on shared pathophysiological pathways and enable the development of biomarkers [28]. Given the safety profile and low cost of vitamin supplementation compared to conventional pharmacological treatments, the characterization of nutritional deficiencies could also help motivate future interventional studies to evaluate whether correcting specific deficiencies yields measurable clinical benefit in selected subgroups [29].

To fill these knowledge gaps, we conducted one of the largest comprehensive analyses to date of fat-soluble vitamin status across select neurodevelopmental disorders. Using data from over 14,000 children with standardized vitamin measurements, we attempted to (1) quantify disorder-specific patterns of vitamin A, D, and E deficiencies using robust statistical methods with sufficient power to detect clinically meaningful differences; (2) assess the prevalence and clinical significance of multiple concurrent vitamin deficiencies within individuals; (3) identify latent vitamin metabolic phenotypes through unsupervised cluster analyses that may reveal novel subgroups beyond traditional diagnostic categories; and (4) assess the potential for precision nutrition approaches by examining demographic modifiers and effect size distributions across different vitamin-disorder combinations. Our findings provide robust baseline epidemiological data that inform population-level risk stratification and motivate future mechanistic and interventional research on the role of micronutrient status in neurodevelopmental disorders.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies (Additional file 1: Table S1).

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted at Wuhan Children’s Hospital, China, from July 2022 to July 2025. Among all individuals who underwent routine vitamin-level testing as part of clinical care during this period (n = 112,396), we first restricted the cohort to children aged 1–18 years. Duplicate laboratory records and records with incomplete or non-classifiable diagnostic information were then removed, yielding 72,237 eligible records (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). From this pool, a diagnostic-based selection approach was applied to identify 14,911 participants who met criteria for one of five predefined study groups. Participants were categorized into five groups based on their clinical diagnoses: (1) healthy controls (n = 6391), who attended the outpatient clinic for routine health examinations and had no documented diagnosis of any neurodevelopmental disorder, chronic medical condition, or acute illness at the time of visit in their outpatient medical records; (2) children with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD, n = 4392); (3) children with tic disorders (n = 2020); (4) children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD, n = 1312); and (5) children with comorbid neurodevelopmental disorders (n = 796), defined as having two or more concurrent neurodevelopmental diagnoses among ADHD, tic disorders, and ASD. All neurodevelopmental disorder diagnoses were made by qualified developmental pediatricians or child psychiatrists according to standardized assessment protocols based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) diagnostic criteria [30]. The diagnostic process for each neurodevelopmental disorder group followed established clinical guidelines. ADHD diagnosis required persistent patterns of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity that interfered with functioning across multiple settings and were consistent with validated diagnostic criteria [31]. The diagnosis of tic disorders included both transient and chronic motor or vocal tics, including Tourette syndrome, according to established prevalence and diagnostic guidelines [32]. ASD diagnoses were based on persistent deficits in social communication and interaction, as well as restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior, interests, or activities, using standardized assessment protocols [33]. For healthy controls, classification was based on the absence of any diagnostic codes indicative of neurodevelopmental disorders, chronic medical conditions, or acute illness in the electronic medical records at the time of the outpatient visit for routine health examination; no active clinical re-screening was performed, consistent with the retrospective design of the study. Participant classification was based on diagnoses recorded in institutional electronic medical records, which document all diagnoses assigned to a child during their clinical encounter. Children whose medical records contained exactly one neurodevelopmental diagnosis (ADHD, tic disorder, or ASD) were classified into the corresponding single-diagnosis group. Children whose medical records documented two or more concurrent neurodevelopmental diagnoses were classified into the comorbid NDD group and were excluded from all single-diagnosis groups at the point of data extraction. The five groups were therefore mutually exclusive by design: group assignment was determined directly by the medical-record diagnostic profile, and no participant could be assigned to more than one group. Children not meeting diagnostic criteria for any of the five study groups (n = 57,326) were excluded from the analysis. These primarily included children with other neurodevelopmental or pediatric diagnoses, those with medical conditions identified through diagnostic coding that might affect vitamin metabolism, and cases with diagnostic uncertainty. The diagnostic-based selection process is detailed in Figure S1 (Additional file 1).

Specimen collection and detection

Venous blood samples were collected from children in the morning after an overnight fast. After centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 5 min, serum samples were isolated and transported under cold-chain conditions for analysis. Serum vitamin concentrations were measured in the certified clinical laboratory of Wuhan Children’s Hospital using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Serum vitamin D status was assessed based on total 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D], calculated as the sum of 25(OH)D2 and 25(OH)D3 (hereafter referred to as vitamin D). Total 25(OH)D concentrations were classified as deficient (≤ 20 ng/mL), insufficient (20–30 ng/mL), or sufficient (> 30 ng/mL) according to the Japanese Society for Bone and Mineral Research (JSBMR) guidelines [34]. For vitamins A and E, as well as the individual vitamin D metabolites 25(OH)D2 and 25(OH)D3, universally accepted pediatric definitions for an intermediate “insufficiency” category are not available. Therefore, these analytes were categorized as deficient, sufficient, or excess according to laboratory-specific reference ranges routinely applied in the accredited clinical laboratory of Wuhan Children’s Hospital. Specifically, vitamin A deficiency was defined as < 300 ng/mL and excess as > 800 ng/mL, and vitamin E deficiency as < 5168.6 ng/mL and excess as > 20,000 ng/mL. Vitamin K was not included in this analysis for three reasons: (1) serum vitamin K was not measured in any participant in this cohort, as it is not part of the routine fat-soluble vitamin panel at our institution and is ordered only for specific clinical indications such as coagulation assessment [35]; (2) universally accepted pediatric reference ranges and consensus deficiency thresholds for vitamin K have not been established in the clinical literature [36]; and (3) the existing evidence base linking fat-soluble vitamins to neurodevelopmental outcomes is concentrated on vitamins A, D, and E, providing the clinical framework necessary to contextualize our findings. Measurements of 25(OH)D2 and 25(OH)D3 were used only to calculate total 25(OH)D and for laboratory quality control, with reference thresholds defined by the clinical laboratory (25(OH)D2 deficiency < 1.2 ng/mL; 25(OH)D3 deficiency ≤ 23.3 ng/mL). The laboratory participates in regular internal and external quality control programs, and intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation were less than 5% for all vitamin measurements.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Descriptive statistics are reported as mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables and as frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Two complementary analytical approaches were applied to characterize between-group differences, each addressing a distinct research question and applied to a different outcome type. For continuous outcomes (serum vitamin concentrations), between-group differences were assessed using linear models within an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) framework, with age, sex, and season of blood collection included as covariates to control for potential confounding. Estimated marginal means (EMMs) and 95% confidence intervals were calculated for each diagnostic group. Post hoc pairwise comparisons used Tukey’s HSD procedure. Results are summarized as adjusted mean differences with 95% CIs and standardized effect sizes (adjusted Cohen’s d = (EMM difference) / σ-residual, where σ-residual is the residual standard deviation (SD) from the ANCOVA model [37]. Effect sizes were interpreted according to conventional thresholds: negligible (|d|< 0.2), small (0.2 ≤|d|< 0.5), medium (0.5 ≤|d|< 0.8), and large (|d|≥ 0.8) [38]. Cohen’s d is reported alongside p-values specifically to distinguish biological effect magnitude from statistical significance, a distinction that is particularly critical given the large sample size (n = 14,911) in which small biological differences can achieve statistical significance. For binary outcomes (clinical deficiency, yes/no), multivariable logistic regression models adjusted for age, sex, and season were used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs for single-vitamin deficiencies in each NDD group versus healthy controls. These two approaches are complementary, not interchangeable. Cohen’s d from ANCOVA captures the magnitude of separation in continuous concentrations, whereas OR from logistic regression captures the probability of clinically defined deficiency at established thresholds. Both are reported throughout to provide a complete characterization of between-group nutritional differences. Multiple vitamin deficiency was defined a priori as the concurrent presence of deficiencies in two or more of the following three fat-soluble vitamins: vitamin A, total 25(OH)D, and vitamin E. 25(OH)D2 and 25(OH)D3 are metabolic sub-forms of a single fat-soluble vitamin (vitamin D) and were therefore not counted as separate vitamins in the multiple deficiency analysis. Only total 25(OH)D was used to represent vitamin D status in all deficiency burden analyses, consistent with standard clinical and epidemiological practice. This approach avoids artificial inflation of concurrent deficiency counts that would result from treating mathematically related sub-forms as independent nutritional entities. Categories of exactly two deficiencies and deficiencies in all three vitamins were additionally examined. Using this definition, multiple-vitamin-deficiency burden was assessed by evaluating the co-occurrence of deficiencies across the three fat-soluble vitamins. The proportions of participants with 0, 1, 2, or 3 concurrent deficiencies were compared across diagnostic groups using Fisher’s exact tests with Haldane-Anscombe correction where appropriate. Unsupervised K-means clustering was performed on standardized vitamin concentration data to identify latent vitamin metabolic phenotypes independent of diagnostic classification. The optimal number of clusters was determined using convergent evidence from the elbow method and silhouette coefficient analysis. Differences in cluster membership across diagnostic groups were assessed with chi-square tests. Correlations among fat-soluble vitamin concentrations were evaluated using Pearson correlation coefficients. Stratified analyses by age, sex, and season were conducted to explore potential effect modification. Age-related trends were examined using locally weighted scatterplot smoothing (LOWESS), and seasonal effects were assessed by categorizing blood collection dates into four meteorological seasons (Spring: March–May; Summer: June–August; Autumn: September–November; Winter: December–February). All statistical tests were two-sided, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. For multiple comparison correction, Tukey’s HSD was used for primary pairwise group comparisons. No correction was applied to descriptive or stratified analyses. Given the large sample size (n = 14,911) and the multivariable modeling framework, the study was sufficiently powered to detect modest between-group differences in adjusted mean vitamin concentrations with adequate precision.

Results

Demographic and baseline characteristics

A total of 14,911 children were included: 6391 healthy controls, 4392 with ADHD, 2020 with tic disorders, 1312 with ASD, and 796 with comorbid neurodevelopmental disorders. The groups differed substantially in age and sex composition. Children with ASD had the youngest mean age (3.5 ± 1.6 years), while those with tic disorders and ADHD were older (7.7 ± 2.5 and 8.8 ± 2.0 years, respectively). The comorbid group had an age distribution similar to the ADHD group. In all neurodevelopmental disorder (NDD) groups, the proportion of males was higher than females (male-to-female ratio approximately 4:1 for ADHD, tic disorders, and ASD, and approximately 9:1 for the comorbid group) compared to controls (approximately 1.5:1), consistent with previously reported sex differences in NDD prevalence. In unadjusted analyses, children with ADHD and comorbid NDDs had lower mean levels of 25(OH)D3 and vitamin E, while the crude vitamin levels of the ASD group were closer to those of the healthy controls (Table 1, Fig. 1). Vitamin A concentrations were broadly similar across diagnostic groups, with mean values of 357.3 ± 84.7 ng/mL in controls, 354.8 ± 82.8 ng/mL in ADHD, 363.5 ± 79.4 ng/mL in tic disorders, 358.7 ± 83.3 ng/mL in ASD, and 362.4 ± 80.0 ng/mL in the comorbid group (Table 1). In contrast, vitamin A concentrations showed relatively small differences between groups and were broadly comparable across diagnostic categories. Crude vitamin A concentrations overlapped substantially across groups in Fig. 1, indicating that any disorder-specific separation for vitamin A, if present, would likely be small compared to the marked group separation observed for vitamin D-related measures. 25(OH)D2 levels were generally low in all groups (mean approximately 2.0 ng/mL), with no notable differences between the NDD and control groups, suggesting a minimal contribution of this form to overall vitamin D status in this cohort. Given the marked differences in age and sex composition across groups, these unadjusted descriptive findings should be interpreted with caution and were therefore further examined using adjusted analyses.

Composition of the comorbid group

Among children with comorbid neurodevelopmental disorders (n = 796), the majority had ADHD with tic disorders (586/796, 73.6%), followed by ADHD with autism spectrum disorder (198/796, 24.9%), and a small proportion had all three diagnoses (12/796, 1.5%). The combination of tic disorders and ASD without ADHD was not observed in this clinical cohort, likely reflecting typical diagnostic and referral pathways in pediatric neurodevelopmental practice, where ADHD-related symptoms often prompt initial clinical evaluation and facilitate the subsequent identification of co-occurring tic or autism spectrum features, rather than indicating a true absence of such comorbidity at the population level. Descriptive vitamin status profiles across these comorbid subtypes (Additional file 1: Table S2) show generally comparable mean concentrations with modest variations, consistent with the heterogeneous composition of this group. Formal statistical comparisons across comorbid subtypes were not performed because of small sample sizes in some diagnostic combinations.

Adjusted comparisons of serum vitamin concentrations across diagnostic groups

After adjusting for age, sex, and season, significant differences in several serum vitamin concentrations were observed between groups (Table 2; Fig. 2). Children with ADHD had lower adjusted mean concentrations of total 25(OH)D compared with healthy controls (adjusted mean difference, 2.52 ng/mL; 95% CI, 1.95 to 3.08; adjusted Cohen’s d = 0.273, indicating a small effect size; Tukey-adjusted p < 0.001), primarily due to lower 25(OH)D3 levels (2.36 ng/mL; 95% CI, 1.81 to 2.91; p < 0.001). Similar but slightly smaller reductions in total vitamin D were seen in children with tic disorders and in the comorbid neurodevelopmental disorder group, which was predominantly composed of children with both ADHD and tic disorders. Compared with healthy controls, adjusted mean vitamin E concentrations were lower by 917.24 ng/mL (95% CI, 759.21 to 1075.26; adjusted Cohen’s d = 0.354) in children with ADHD, lower by 758.26 ng/mL (95% CI, 569.85 to 946.66; adjusted Cohen’s d = 0.293) in those with tic disorders, and lower by 689.15 ng/mL (95% CI, 412.11 to 966.18) in those with comorbid neurodevelopmental disorders (all Tukey-adjusted p < 0.001). Although adjusted vitamin E concentrations in ASD were lower than in controls, the standardized effect size was small (Cohen’s d = 0.332) and broadly comparable to those in other neurodevelopmental groups, indicating limited biological separation despite statistical significance. Adjusted vitamin A differences across diagnostic groups were also small. Specifically, children with ADHD showed statistically significant but small reductions compared with controls (adjusted mean difference, 18.24 ng/mL, 95% CI, 13.27–23.21; Tukey-adjusted p < 0.001; adjusted Cohen’s d = 0.224), as did children with comorbid neurodevelopmental disorders (10.45 ng/mL, 95% CI, 1.74–19.16; p = 0.009; d = 0.128). In contrast, children with ASD showed significantly higher adjusted vitamin A levels than controls (− 10.69 ng/mL, 95% CI, − 17.57 to − 3.80; p < 0.001; d = − 0.131), while no statistically significant differences were observed for tic disorders (5.52 ng/mL, 95% CI, − 0.40 to 11.44; p = 0.082; d = 0.068). However, standardized effect sizes were negligible-to-small overall (largest |d| ≈ 0.22), indicating limited biological separation despite statistical significance in this large sample. These adjusted mean differences were small relative to those observed for vitamin D-related measures and vitamin E. No statistically significant between-group differences were observed for 25(OH)D2, which remained uniformly low across all diagnostic groups. All Tukey-adjusted pairwise comparisons among diagnostic groups are presented in Additional file 1: Table S3.

Deficiency prevalence and risk by clinical thresholds

Based on established clinical thresholds, descriptive analyses show differences in vitamin status distributions across diagnostic groups (Fig. 3; Additional file 1: Table S4). Crude prevalence of total vitamin D deficiency varied substantially across groups: 31.1% in ADHD, 25.4% in comorbid group, 21.0% in tic disorders, 14.1% in controls, and notably only 7.2% in ASD. In contrast, vitamin A deficiency showed more uniform distribution (~ 25% across all groups), and vitamin E deficiency remained low in all groups (0.6–2.6%). To distinguish background deficiency burden from disorder-specific risk, these prevalence patterns are further evaluated using multivariable logistic regression models adjusted for age, sex, and season (Table 3). For vitamin A, deficiency affected about one quarter of children in all diagnostic groups. Although crude prevalence was similar across groups, adjustment for age, sex, and season revealed a modest increase in the odds of vitamin A deficiency in children with ADHD compared to controls (OR = 1.26, 95% CI, 1.14–1.40; p < 0.001), while no statistically significant associations were found for tic disorders, ASD, or comorbid neurodevelopmental disorders. Deficiency of 25(OH)D2 was highly prevalent in all diagnostic groups, affecting nearly 60% of participants, with minimal variation between groups. Given the uniformly low concentrations of 25(OH)D2 and its limited contribution to total vitamin D status in this cohort, inferential interpretation focused on total 25(OH)D and its dominant component, 25(OH)D3. In contrast, clearer disorder-specific associations were observed for 25(OH)D3 and total vitamin D. Adjusted regression analyses showed significantly increased odds of 25(OH)D3 deficiency in children with ADHD (OR = 1.39, 95% CI, 1.25–1.53; p < 0.001) and tic disorders (OR = 1.16, 95% CI, 1.02–1.31; p = 0.019), while no statistically significant associations were found for ASD or the comorbid group. When total vitamin D deficiency was considered, adjusted analyses confirmed a significantly increased risk in children with ADHD (OR = 1.43, 95% CI, 1.28–1.60; p < 0.001), while associations for tic disorders, ASD, and the comorbid group were not statistically significant. For vitamin E, absolute deficiency rates were low across all diagnostic groups (≤ 2.6%). However, adjusted analyses revealed significantly increased odds of vitamin E deficiency in children with ADHD (OR = 2.91, 95% CI, 1.98–4.38; p < 0.001), tic disorders (OR = 2.17, 95% CI, 1.34–3.52; p = 0.002), and ASD (OR = 2.19, 95% CI, 1.06–4.23; p = 0.025), whereas the comorbid group showed a non-significant association (OR = 1.84, 95% CI 0.92–3.45; p = 0.067). Given the rarity of this outcome, these findings should be interpreted primarily as a relative increase in risk rather than evidence of a high absolute burden at the population level. Overall, these findings indicate that ADHD is associated with the most consistent increases in deficiency risk for vitamin D-related measures (primarily total vitamin D and its major component D3) and vitamin E, while tic disorders show more selective associations. In contrast, vitamin A deficiency and 25(OH)D2 deficiency largely reflect background population patterns rather than stable disorder-specific risks.

Multiple vitamin deficiency burden and cluster analysis

To assess the cumulative micronutrient deficiency burden, we examined the co-occurrence of deficiencies in three fat-soluble vitamins: vitamin A, total vitamin D, and vitamin E (Fig. 4; Additional file 1: Tables S5–S6). Based on descriptive distributions (Additional file 1: Table S5), children with ADHD had a markedly higher burden of multiple vitamin deficiencies. Specifically, 10.7% of children with ADHD had deficiencies in exactly two vitamins and 0.3% had deficiencies in all three vitamins (combined: 11.0% with ≥ 2 deficiencies), compared with 4.1% and 0.1% (combined: 4.2%), respectively, among healthy controls. Inferential comparisons confirmed these patterns (Additional file 1: Table S6), with ADHD being associated with significantly increased odds of having two deficiencies (OR = 2.80, 95% CI 2.39–3.27; p < 0.001), three deficiencies (OR = 2.62, 95% CI 1.07–6.41; p = 0.038), and ≥ 2 deficiencies overall (OR = 2.81, 95% CI 2.40–3.27; p < 0.001). Children with tic disorders also had elevated odds of having two deficiencies (OR = 1.65, 95% CI 1.33–2.04; p < 0.001) and ≥ 2 deficiencies (OR = 1.66, 95% CI 1.35–2.06; p < 0.001), whereas no statistically significant associations were observed for ASD in these multiple-deficiency categories. The comorbid group showed higher odds of multiple deficiencies, especially for two or more deficiencies (OR = 1.79, 95% CI 1.34–2.40; p < 0.001). Consistent with these findings, the proportion of children without any vitamin deficiency was lowest in the ADHD group (52.4%), followed by children with tic disorders (63.8%), while children with ASD (69.7%) and healthy controls (64.2%) had the highest rates of sufficiency across all three fat-soluble vitamins studied (Additional file 1: Table S5).

Intercorrelations and stratified analyses

Levels of fat-soluble vitamins showed predominantly positive correlations across the cohort, with 8 of 10 unique pairwise comparisons demonstrating positive correlations (all p < 0.001), indicating that children with low levels of one vitamin often had concomitantly low levels of others—a pattern consistent with the clustering results (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). Age-stratified analyses (Additional file 1: Fig. S3) reveal distinct vitamin-specific patterns. Vitamin A levels increased progressively with age. In contrast, 25(OH)D3 concentrations declined from early childhood into adolescence, while total vitamin D showed a more attenuated and non-linear age-related pattern. The apparent age-related patterns of vitamin D differed across diagnostic groups, with visual differences in the shape and magnitude of age trajectories observed in the stratified plots. Sex-disaggregated analyses (Additional file 1: Fig. S4) show that females were more likely to have vitamin D deficiency than males (OR = 1.75, p < 0.001, Table 3), particularly during adolescence. This sex difference was observed across all five diagnostic groups, with comparatively larger association estimates in ASD and comorbid presentations. No consistent sex differences were observed for vitamin A or vitamin E. Finally, seasonal variation in vitamin D concentrations was evident across all diagnostic groups (Additional file 1: Fig. S5). Both 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 and total vitamin D showed significant seasonal fluctuations (all p < 0.001), with lower levels in winter and spring and higher levels in summer and autumn. The magnitude of seasonal differences varied across diagnostic groups, with a larger seasonal amplitude observed in the ADHD group (approximately 7.8 ng/mL), followed by the tic disorder group (6.7 ng/mL) and the comorbid group (7.0 ng/mL).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is one of the largest real-world clinical investigations of fat-soluble vitamin status (vitamins A, D, and E) in children with neurodevelopmental disorders, including 14,911 participants with ADHD, ASD, tic disorders, comorbid neurodevelopmental conditions, and healthy controls. Our findings reveal disorder-specific patterns of vitamin deficiency that build on observations from previous smaller-scale studies. Children with ADHD showed the most pronounced relative differences, with larger adjusted mean reductions in vitamin D concentrations and increased odds of vitamin E deficiency compared with healthy controls, despite the low absolute prevalence of vitamin E deficiency. Notably, deficiencies in vitamins D and E were not randomly distributed but tended to cluster. Children with ADHD had approximately three times higher odds of having multiple concurrent deficiencies, with this association primarily driven by deficiencies in vitamin D and vitamin E. This pattern indicates that deficiencies in vitamin D and E tend to co-occur in children with ADHD, which has been less frequently described in pediatric neurodevelopmental populations. Additionally, cluster analysis identified four distinct serum vitamin status patterns, with the profile characterized by lower vitamin D and E concentrations being disproportionately represented in children with ADHD and tic disorders. In contrast, children with ASD showed relatively preserved fat-soluble vitamin profiles. Although adjusted vitamin D concentrations in ASD were slightly lower than those in healthy controls, the standardized effect sizes were uniformly small, and no statistically significant difference in vitamin D deficiency risk was observed after adjustment. These findings do not support the presence of a generalized fat-soluble vitamin deficiency phenotype in children with ASD within this cohort. This pattern differs from several European studies reporting higher deficiency rates in ASD [39, 40], highlighting the potential influence of geographic context and population-specific factors such as sun exposure, dietary habits, and healthcare practices. Finally, adjusted vitamin A concentrations showed relatively modest between-group differences with negligible-to-small effect sizes (largest |d| ≈ 0.22), suggesting that statistically significant differences likely reflect population-level background variation rather than disorder-specific metabolic alterations. Taken together, the vitamin A findings require nuanced interpretation.

Despite the statistical significance observed in logistic models (OR = 1.26 for ADHD), which is partly driven by the large sample size, the standardized effect sizes for serum concentrations remained small (Cohen’s d = 0.224). This contrast suggests that the biological magnitude of the difference is limited. Therefore, vitamin A exhibited minimal biological separation across diagnostic groups, indicating that it is unlikely to represent a core nutritional biomarker distinguishing neurodevelopmental disorder subtypes in this cohort. The apparent discrepancy between crude vitamin A deficiency prevalence (25.3% in ADHD vs. 25.5% in controls) and the adjusted odds ratio (OR = 1.26 favoring increased risk in ADHD) can be explained by age-composition differences between groups. The ADHD group was substantially older (mean 8.8 ± 2.0 years) than controls (mean 5.3 ± 3.9 years), and vitamin A concentrations naturally increase with age during childhood (Additional file 1: Fig. S3). After adjusting for this age differential, ADHD children showed a small residual elevation in deficiency risk. However, the negligible standardized effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.224) and the overlapping crude prevalence rates indicate that this represents a minimal biological difference rather than a disorder-specific metabolic alteration. Taken together, these findings are consistent with the interpretation that age-related developmental factors may contribute to vitamin A status differences observed in ADHD. These findings underscore the importance of interpreting effect sizes alongside p-values in large-scale nutritional epidemiology, particularly in studies with sample sizes exceeding 10,000 participants. The increased risk of vitamin D deficiency observed in children with ADHD, and to a lesser extent in those with tic disorders, particularly for 25(OH)D3, appears broadly consistent with prior literature reporting associations between vitamin D status and neurodevelopmental processes [41, 42]; however, these associations should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal relationship. Alternative explanations, including underlying metabolic, behavioral, or lifestyle characteristics in children with ADHD and tic disorders that may influence vitamin D status (such as reduced outdoor activity or dietary selectivity), cannot be excluded. Vitamin D has been implicated in several neurodevelopmentally relevant pathways, including dopaminergic signaling and regulation of tyrosine hydroxylase expression, as described in experimental and observational studies related to ADHD neurobiology [41, 42]. In addition, large-scale epidemiological studies have reported associations between lower prenatal or early-life vitamin D status and increased ADHD risk, including findings from a Finnish birth cohort and a Danish case-cohort study [16, 43], providing important developmental context for observed associations between vitamin D status and ADHD. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have further described inverse or dose–response relationships between vitamin D status and outcomes related to ADHD, and in some populations, traits related to autism [21, 44], highlighting substantial heterogeneity across study designs, developmental windows, and populations. For tic disorders, a recent meta-analysis reported lower serum vitamin D levels in affected children compared with healthy controls [45], which is consistent with the direction of associations observed in the present study, although the magnitude of these associations differed. Taken together, our findings are broadly consistent with international reports describing differences in vitamin D status between children with ADHD and controls [46, 47], while extending this evidence to a large real-world pediatric cohort without implying causal inference.

In contrast to several previous reports, children with ASD in our cohort showed a relatively low crude prevalence of vitamin D deficiency (7.2%) compared with other neurodevelopmental disorder groups. Wang et al. [40] conducted a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis of 34 studies (N = 20,580) and found that children with ASD had serum vitamin D concentrations 7.46 ng/mL lower than controls, along with a more than five-fold increased risk of deficiency. Similar associations have been reported in earlier meta-analyses [39, 40]. However, although adjusted vitamin D concentrations in Chinese children with ASD in the present study were modestly lower than those of healthy controls (27.59 vs 30.80 ng/mL), the standardized effect size was small, and no statistically significant difference in vitamin D deficiency risk was observed after adjustment for age, sex, and season. This apparent discrepancy between crude prevalence and adjusted risk underscores the importance of considering demographic and seasonal factors when interpreting vitamin D status in young pediatric populations. Several non-mutually exclusive, hypothesis-generating explanations may account for the differences between our findings and those reported in predominantly European or Middle Eastern cohorts [39]. First, geographic and ethnic factors may play a role. Dietary patterns in coastal regions of China, characterized by higher fish and seafood consumption, may contribute to higher baseline vitamin D intake, while ethnic variation in VDR and CYP gene polymorphisms could influence vitamin D metabolism and requirements. Age-related factors also warrant consideration. The relatively young age of the ASD cohort in our study (mean 3.5 ± 1.6 years) may partially explain the preserved vitamin D status, as vitamin D supplementation during infancy is nearly universal in China and deficiency rates tend to increase with age as supplementation becomes less consistent [48]. Differences in healthcare practices may also contribute, as families of children with ASD often receive intensive medical follow-up and nutritional counseling after diagnosis, potentially leading to improved vitamin D status. Selection bias cannot be excluded, as a hospital-based cohort may preferentially include children receiving more intensive medical and nutritional care compared with community-based samples [49, 50]. Heterogeneity within the ASD spectrum, including variation in behavioral phenotypes and feeding patterns, may result in distinct nutritional profiles across subgroups [51]. Taken together, these findings do not support the presence of a generalized fat-soluble vitamin deficiency or malabsorption phenotype in children with ASD within this large clinical cohort. After adjustment, vitamin A, D, and E status in children with ASD largely overlapped with that of healthy controls, underscoring the need for caution when extrapolating results from smaller or etiologically distinct ASD populations.

Recent Mendelian randomization studies suggest that lower circulating vitamin D levels may not causally contribute to the development of ASD, but instead may reflect downstream consequences of behavioral characteristics associated with ASD [52]. These findings provide important context when interpreting observational associations between vitamin D status and ASD. Behavioral features commonly observed in ASD, such as reduced outdoor activity, preference for staying indoors, restrictive eating patterns, and social avoidance, may lead to decreased sunlight exposure and lower endogenous vitamin D synthesis, raising the possibility of reverse causation in cross-sectional studies. This reverse causation framework is consistent with the patterns observed in the present study. The relatively preserved vitamin D status among young children with ASD in our cohort may reflect their early developmental stage and intensive medical supervision, during which vitamin D supplementation and nutritional monitoring are common, and ASD-related behavioral characteristics that could negatively affect vitamin D status may not yet be fully established. As children age and behavioral phenotypes evolve, vitamin D status may change accordingly. This possibility cannot be evaluated within the cross-sectional design of the current analysis but may help explain age-related increases in vitamin D deficiency reported in other populations. These considerations underscore the importance of accounting for age, healthcare access, behavioral phenotypes, geographic context, and cultural practices when interpreting nutritional findings in neurodevelopmental disorders. Future research should prioritize longitudinal cohort studies that track vitamin status from early diagnosis through adolescence, multicenter investigations across diverse geographic and ethnic settings, and studies examining associations between specific ASD behavioral subtypes and nutritional trajectories to better clarify temporal relationships and underlying mechanisms.

This study provides large-scale epidemiological evidence to the limited literature linking vitamin E deficiency with ADHD and contributes to understanding nutritional vulnerability in this population. Although the absolute prevalence of vitamin E deficiency in children with ADHD was low (2.6%), the adjusted odds of deficiency were substantially higher compared with healthy controls (OR = 2.91). This pattern—characterized by elevated relative risk in the context of low absolute prevalence—suggests the presence of a small, potentially vulnerable subgroup rather than a widespread population-level deficiency. Children with tic disorders and ASD also showed increased odds of vitamin E deficiency; however, these associations were less precise and should be interpreted cautiously due to wider confidence intervals and smaller subgroup sizes. Vitamin E is the primary fat-soluble antioxidant and plays an important role in maintaining neuronal membrane integrity and limiting oxidative damage. The biological plausibility of the observed associations is supported by extensive evidence of oxidative imbalance in ADHD, including elevated oxidative stress markers and altered lipid-soluble antioxidant profiles compared with controls [53, 54]. Similarly, a large meta-analysis of 87 studies reported reduced blood vitamin E concentrations in children with ASD, along with elevated malondialdehyde and decreased glutathione levels [23]. Together, these findings are compatible with a potential link between oxidative stress pathways and neurodevelopmental disorders, particularly ADHD, but do not establish such a relationship. Vitamin E is essential for neurodevelopment, and deficiency during critical developmental periods has been associated in specific clinical contexts with long-term neurologic sequelae [55]. Nevertheless, the cross-sectional design of the present study precludes causal inference. From a clinical perspective, the low absolute prevalence of vitamin E deficiency indicates that these findings do not support universal screening or supplementation for all children with ADHD. Rather, they highlight the importance of targeted nutritional assessment and increased clinical awareness in subgroups of children with ADHD who may be at higher risk, such as those with restrictive diets, limited dietary diversity, or other indicators of oxidative stress. Future longitudinal studies and cost-effectiveness analyses are needed to clarify the clinical relevance of vitamin E deficiency and to determine optimal strategies for nutritional assessment in neurodevelopmental disorders.

This study identified a sex difference in vitamin D-deficiency risk after adjustment for age and season, with females showing higher odds of deficiency than males, consistent with previous pediatric studies [48]. Several hypotheses may contribute to this pattern. Behavioral factors, including differences in outdoor activity, clothing-related variation in skin sun exposure, and lifestyle habits, have been reported to influence vitamin D status in children and adolescents [56]. Given that ADHD, ASD, and tic disorders are more frequently diagnosed in males, the higher baseline risk of vitamin D deficiency observed in females suggests that girls with neurodevelopmental disorders may warrant particular attention in nutritional assessment. Age also emerged as an important correlate of vitamin status. Each additional year of age was associated with increased odds of vitamin D deficiency, indicating greater nutritional vulnerability in older children. This age-related decline in vitamin D status has been consistently documented in pediatric populations and may reflect reduced outdoor activity, increased screen time, and dietary changes during school years [57]. In contrast, age-related patterns differed across other fat-soluble vitamins, with vitamin A concentrations tending to increase with age and vitamin E showing comparatively stable levels, underscoring the dynamic and vitamin-specific nature of nutritional status throughout childhood and adolescence. Seasonal variation in vitamin D status was evident in our cohort, consistent with the dependence of endogenous vitamin D synthesis on ultraviolet B exposure. Summer and autumn were associated with lower odds of vitamin D deficiency compared with spring, whereas winter was associated with higher risk. Previous studies have reported substantial seasonal and geographic variation in vitamin D status among children, including those with ADHD [50, 58]. Despite Wuhan’s relatively low latitude (31°N), the persistently elevated prevalence of vitamin D deficiency observed in children with ADHD suggests that behavioral factors—such as reduced outdoor activity and increased screen time—may exert a greater influence on vitamin D status than geographic latitude alone, consistent with prior reports of activity restriction in this population [51].

The identification of multivitamin deficiency clusters is an exploratory and descriptive finding that highlights heterogeneity in circulating vitamin concentration patterns across diagnostic groups. Conceptually, children with comorbid neurodevelopmental conditions may plausibly experience greater nutritional vulnerability due to overlapping behavioral, metabolic, and lifestyle-related factors. In the present analysis, clustering and multiple-deficiency distributions revealed a higher proportion of individuals with concurrent (≥ 2) fat-soluble vitamin deficiencies in the comorbid group, a pattern also noted in previous studies examining correlated micronutrient insufficiency in neurodevelopmental disorders [51, 59]. However, when individual vitamins were examined separately using adjusted models, the comorbid group did not show a consistently higher risk compared with the ADHD-only group. This apparent lack of additivity likely reflects the intrinsic heterogeneity of the comorbid category, which includes multiple diagnostic combinations rather than a uniform clinical phenotype. In particular, the inclusion of comorbidities involving ASD, given the relatively preserved vitamin profiles observed in the single-diagnosis ASD group, may attenuate risk signals for individual vitamins otherwise associated with ADHD or tic disorders. Differences in age distribution and age-related supplementation practices may further contribute to this attenuation. Importantly, these multivitamin clusters should be interpreted as descriptive patterns of circulating vitamin concentrations rather than as etiologically distinct biological subtypes. Accordingly, the comorbidity-related patterns observed in this study are best understood as reflecting heterogeneous nutritional profiles within a mixed diagnostic population, rather than as evidence of additive biological severity associated with comorbid neurodevelopmental disorders.

These findings may help in understanding nutritional patterns in children with neurodevelopmental disorders; however, they should be interpreted considering the observational design of the present study. The higher prevalence of vitamin D and vitamin E deficiency observed in children with ADHD and tic disorders highlights the potential value of routine nutritional awareness and targeted assessment in these populations, without implying direct treatment or supplementation recommendations. Evidence from trials evaluating vitamin D-related interventions in neurodevelopmental disorders remains limited and heterogeneous. For example, a meta-analysis reported modest changes in stereotypical behaviors following vitamin D supplementation in children with ASD [60], and a randomized trial observed reduced tic severity after high-dose vitamin D3 supplementation [61]. In children with ADHD, combined vitamin D and magnesium supplementation has been examined in relation to selected conduct-related outcomes [25]. Notably, a recent Danish randomized trial demonstrated that pre-intervention maternal 25(OH)D status, rather than the supplementation dose itself, was the primary predictor of offspring ADHD risk [62], emphasizing the importance of baseline nutritional status when interpreting supplementation trials. In contrast, evidence supporting vitamin E supplementation in neurodevelopmental disorders is currently sparse, and the present findings should not be interpreted as support for interventions targeting vitamin E. Rather, the observed associations highlight the need for careful nutritional evaluation and for well-designed longitudinal and interventional studies to clarify the clinical relevance of correcting specific deficiencies in selected subgroups.

The disorder-specific deficiency patterns identified in this study suggest that targeted nutritional assessment approaches may be warranted, rather than uniform strategies for all neurodevelopmental disorders. While routine vitamin D supplementation remains consistent with current pediatric guidelines, our findings indicate that children with ADHD and tic disorders experience more pronounced seasonal variation in vitamin D status. This suggests that seasonal patterns of vitamin D deficiency may require closer attention in these groups, particularly regarding nutritional monitoring and risk assessment, rather than implying different supplementation strategies. Evidence from randomized trials evaluating vitamin D supplementation as an adjunct to standard therapy in neurodevelopmental disorders remains limited and context-dependent. For example, vitamin D supplementation combined with methylphenidate has been studied in relation to selected symptom outcomes compared with medication alone [63]. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution and do not establish clear clinical benefit across populations. In contrast, evidence regarding the efficacy of vitamin E supplementation remains limited, highlighting an important gap for future interventional research. From a broader public health perspective, children with neurodevelopmental disorders may face multiple barriers to achieving adequate nutritional status, including sensory food selectivity, limited outdoor activity, and comorbid obesity, which has been associated with reduced vitamin D bioavailability [64]. The elevated burden of multiple concurrent vitamin deficiencies observed in children with ADHD further suggests that nutritional vulnerability in this population may extend beyond single nutrients. Together, these findings underscore the importance of considering broader micronutrient patterns and integrated nutritional assessment, rather than focusing exclusively on isolated single-vitamin measures [27].

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. The observed vitamin deficiencies may precede the development of neurodevelopmental symptoms, result from disorder-related behaviors such as selective eating or reduced outdoor activity, or reflect a combination of both. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify temporal relationships between vitamin status and symptom onset or progression. Second, this was a single-center, hospital-based study. While this design ensured standardized laboratory measurements and diagnostic procedures, it may limit generalizability to populations with different dietary patterns, food fortification practices, healthcare access, and levels of sun exposure. Children presenting for clinical evaluation may also differ systematically from community-based populations in symptom severity, health-seeking behavior, or socioeconomic background, introducing potential selection bias. Additionally, the study period (2022–2025) coincided with the post-pandemic recovery phase, during which changes in outdoor activity and healthcare utilization may have influenced both vitamin D synthesis and sample composition. Third, our analysis was restricted to three fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, and E), providing only a partial assessment of overall nutritional status. Vitamin K, the fourth fat-soluble vitamin, was not included because it was not part of the routine fat-soluble vitamin panel ordered at our institution and therefore no vitamin K data were available for any participant in this cohort. In addition, vitamin K lacks established pediatric deficiency thresholds and has a less-developed evidence base in the neurodevelopmental literature; its relationship with neurodevelopmental outcomes warrants dedicated investigation in future prospective studies. Other micronutrients relevant to neurodevelopment, such as iron, zinc, B vitamins, and omega-3 fatty acids, were also not evaluated and may interact with vitamin status or independently influence neurodevelopmental outcomes [51]. Furthermore, important data limitations restrict causal and mechanistic interpretation. We lacked detailed information on dietary intake patterns, gastrointestinal function, medication use, socioeconomic status, and parental education, preventing us from determining whether observed vitamin deficiencies reflect primary metabolic differences, behavioral factors (e.g., reduced outdoor activity), or gastrointestinal dysfunction. Future longitudinal studies with comprehensive dietary assessments and behavioral phenotyping are needed to clarify these mechanisms. Fourth, data on disorder severity and ADHD subtypes (predominantly inattentive, predominantly hyperactive-impulsive, or combined presentation) were not systematically available in this retrospective cohort. Similarly, ASD severity levels and comorbid intellectual disability status were not captured, precluding stratified analyses by clinical phenotype or symptom severity. Given that nutritional patterns may differ by symptom severity or diagnostic subtype, such as more severe ASD with greater feeding selectivity or ADHD with more pronounced hyperactivity potentially limiting outdoor activity, the inability to examine these relationships represents an important limitation. Future prospective studies should incorporate standardized severity assessments and diagnostic subtyping to clarify whether nutritional vulnerabilities vary across the clinical spectrum within each neurodevelopmental disorder category. Fifth, although neurodevelopmental diagnoses were made by qualified clinicians using DSM-5 criteria, inter-rater diagnostic reliability was not formally assessed, which may introduce some heterogeneity in diagnostic classification. Comorbid neurodevelopmental disorders comprised only 5.3% of our sample, considerably lower than the 30–50% comorbidity rates reported in other studies [65], which may limit the generalizability of our disorder-specific findings. In addition, comorbid neurodevelopmental disorders were analyzed as a single group due to sample size constraints. As detailed in Additional file 1: Table S2, this group comprised heterogeneous diagnostic combinations, predominantly ADHD with tic disorders or ASD, which may attenuate disorder-specific associations and limit generalizability to other comorbidity profiles. Diagnostic coding at initial presentation may also underestimate comorbidity, particularly in younger children, despite efforts to identify comorbid cases. Despite these limitations, this study provides robust real-world evidence of heterogeneous fat-soluble vitamin status across neurodevelopmental disorders in a large pediatric cohort. The findings highlight the importance of systematic nutritional assessment as part of comprehensive clinical evaluation and underscore the need for future longitudinal cohort studies to establish temporal relationships, multicenter investigations to assess generalizability across diverse populations, and mechanistic studies examining genetic, metabolic, and behavioral determinants of vitamin regulation. Comprehensive micronutrient profiling may further clarify patterns of nutritional vulnerability in children with neurodevelopmental disorders.

Conclusions

This large cross-sectional study shows that fat-soluble vitamin status differs significantly across neurodevelopmental disorder subtypes in a pediatric population. Children with ADHD showed the most pronounced nutritional burden, characterized by elevated risks of vitamin D and vitamin E deficiency and a higher prevalence of multiple concurrent deficiencies, while children with ASD exhibited relatively preserved fat-soluble vitamin profiles comparable to healthy controls. These disorder-specific patterns suggest that a uniform nutritional screening approach may be insufficient, and that targeted assessment strategies may be warranted for children with ADHD and tic disorders in particular. However, given the cross-sectional design of this study, causal inferences cannot be drawn. Well-designed prospective and interventional studies are needed to clarify the temporal relationships between fat-soluble vitamin status and neurodevelopmental outcomes, and to determine the clinical relevance of targeted nutritional interventions in selected subgroups.
