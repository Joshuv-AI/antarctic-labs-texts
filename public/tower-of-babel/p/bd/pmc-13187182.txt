Risk attitudes and value of hope: survey results from Japanese hematologists and oncologists treating patients with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma
Authors: Yasuyuki Arai, Timothy Bolt, Hikaru Onishi, Michael LoPresti, Victoria Arguissain, Jörg Mahlich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe “value of hope” refers to treatments that offer a small probability of cure without necessarily improving average survival. While this concept has gained attention in the academic literature, little is known about how physicians perceive and integrate it into clinical decision-making. This study explored the treatment preferences of Japanese hematologists and oncologists in managing diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), with a focus on factors influencing the choice between riskier and more predictable options. An online survey was distributed to 231 physicians, using hypothetical patient vignettes that varied by age, ECOG performance status, and likelihood of long-term remission. For each scenario, respondents chose between a treatment with a certain but limited survival outcome and a riskier “hopeful” treatment offering the same expected survival but a chance of longer remission. Physician demographics, experience, and institutional factors were also collected. Across scenarios, 29–40% of physicians selected the hopeful treatment. The likelihood of choosing this option decreased when certain survival was longer (6 vs. 3 months). Younger physicians and those working in larger or university hospitals were significantly more likely to favor hopeful treatments, as were those in institutions meeting CAR-T use guideline criteria. These findings suggest that physicians, like patients, may place value on treatments offering even small chances of extended survival.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44232-x.Keywords: Value of hope, Physician preferences, Hematologists, CAR-T therapy, Clinical decision-makingSubject terms: Cancer, Health care, Oncology

Introduction

The concept of the value of hope has received increasing attention in the literature in recent years1–5. It is well established that patients differ in their preferences when faced with uncertain treatment outcomes. The value of hope is a reflection of how people may favor treatments offering a chance at long-term survival, even if the expected outcome is modest, hoping to be among the few who benefit substantially. Conversely, others may prefer treatments with more predictable outcomes and lower variability. As different treatment options can be characterized by differences in the expected mean outcome as well as by the variability of outcomes, to compare the value of treatment alternatives, it is crucial to consider risk preferences. According to this framework, individuals can be categorized as risk-averse, risk-neutral, or risk-seeking based on their preference for more certain outcomes or more variable (i.e., riskier) pay-offs with the same expected payoff (such as expected survival). Moreover, people often exhibit preferences for skewed distributions: they tend to seek right-skewed risks (low probability, high reward) and avoid left-skewed risks (low probability, high loss)6. This behavior explains why people both buy insurance (to avoid rare but severe losses) and participate in lotteries (to pursue rare but large gains) simultaneously, and why they may prefer medical treatments with "fat-tailed" outcome distributions. Only a few empirical studies of this have been performed with varying results7–10. By and large, patients with chronic diseases tend to exhibit risk-averse behavior11 whereas patients with incurable conditions are often more risk-seeking9. Moreover, surveying a representative U.S. population Mulligan et al.12 observed that individuals are risk-seeking at low levels of health, and become increasingly risk-averse as health improves. Similar findings were reported for the Netherlands13.

In this paper, we survey Japanese hematologists and oncologists to explore their perspectives on risky treatment outcomes. Japan presents a compelling case, as it is widely considered one of the most risk-averse societies globally, according to Hofstede’s cultural dimensions14,15. Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) also provides a unique context, as it is a field where advanced therapies such as chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapies are already established. In Japan, two CAR-Ts (lisocabtagene maraleucel [Breyanzi®] and axicabtagene ciloleucel [Yescarta®]) are approved for patients with DLBCL who failed first-line therapy. These therapies often exhibit skewed outcome distributions, making the assessment of risk preferences particularly relevant. Moreover, diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), the most common subtype of non-Hodgkin lymphoma, has the highest incidence among hematologic malignancies in Japan, with 11.07 cases per 100,000 population16 and is associated with a significant economic burden17.

Materials and methods

A self-administered online survey was conducted by INTAGE Healthcare Inc., Tokyo, Japan among physicians selected via a physician panel managed by PLAMED Inc., Tokyo, Japan. The panel includes about 900 hematologists, as well as some oncologists, corresponding to 32% of all hematologists registered in Japan18. A survey invitation was sent by email to hematologist and oncologist members of the physician panel maintained by PLAMED Inc. and physicians were asked to complete the survey within a one week of receipt of the request. The survey included an initial consent question and some initial questions to determine the eligibility of physicians for the study. To be included in the study, physicians were required to have treated at least one DLBCL patient in the last 12 months. Those meeting the eligibility criteria were asked to continue with the main survey. An attempt was made to include physicians (at least 30) who worked at institutions that have already adopted CAR-T therapies or are in the process of doing so. The study was conducted in accordance with the protocol, ICH guidelines, applicable regulations and guidelines governing clinical research in Japan, and the Declaration of Helsinki. The study received approval from the Toukeikai Kitamachi Clinic Ethics Review Board (approval no. KXJ09854) prior to the administration of the online survey, with informed consent obtained from all participants. Table 1 outlines the items collected.

For both of these two Patient Vignettes, each physician was asked to make a recommendation based on two extended survival time scenarios: a treatment with 3-month extended expected survival and a 6-month extended expected survival. For the 3-month extended expected survival, the physician was presented with two treatment options for the patient: Treatment A that provided a certain extended survival of 3 months and Treatment B that provided a 25% chance of extending survival by 12 months and a 75% chance of not prolonging survival. The exercise was then repeated but with Treatment A providing certain extended survival of 6 months and Treatment B with a 25% chance of prolonging survival by 24 months. While both treatment options are purely hypothetical, median survival among patients with refractory DLBCL prior to the advent of CAR-T therapies was approximately six months19. Today, CAR-T therapies are considered as standard of care in this indication with cure rates reaching up to 40%20.

Results

The survey respondents were predominantly male (201 out of 231), with a small number of female participants (17) and 13 preferring not to disclose their gender. Most respondents were between 30 and 49 years old, with 76 aged 30–39 and 71 aged 40–49.

In terms of workplace characteristics, the majority were affiliated with large hospitals, especially those with over 500 beds, and none worked in facilities with fewer than 100 beds. Facility types varied, with 85 working at university hospitals, 64 at national or public hospitals, 72 at general hospitals, and 10 at cancer centers. Geographically, the Kanto region had the highest representation (87 respondents), followed by Kansai (59 respondents), while the Tohoku region had the fewest (6 respondents). Most respondents (175) reported that their hospitals had intensive care units (ICUs) capable of managing systemic adverse events. On average, hospitals had 4.5 physicians meeting all criteria for providing CAR-T therapy, with a range from 0 to 25. Regarding clinical background, the vast majority specialized in hematology (182 respondents), with smaller numbers in hematology-oncology (30 respondents) and oncology (19 respondents). The average number of years since graduating from medical school was 17.1, with a range of 3 to 43 years. In terms of certification, 195 respondents were certified hematologists by the Japanese Society of Hematology, 76 respondents were certified by the Japanese Society of Hematopoietic and Immune Cell Therapy, and 35 respondents had neither of these qualifications.

Table 5 reports the share of responding physicians selecting the “hopeful” treatment depending on both the presented level of certainty in the alternative option and the patient vignette. Across both patient profiles, physicians were less likely to choose the hopeful option when the certain survival benefit increased from 3 to 6 months. This effect was particularly pronounced for the older patient with less advanced disease, for whom the share selecting the hopeful option declined by nearly nine percentage points (from 37.2% for 3 month expected survival scenario to 28.6% for the 6-month expected survival scenario). For the younger patient with more advanced disease, the share preferring the “hopeful” alternative was a little higher but also did not fall as much with the longer expected survival.

To examine the preferences for “hopeful” treatment further, we examined the correlation of each responding physicians choosing of the “hopeful” option across the four scenarios. Table 6 shows the results of that analysis. Correlation was stronger for the same patient vignette than it was across the same survival gain time scale. The correlation across the 3-month and 6-month survival gain within the vignettes (same described patient) was 0.564 for the Patient Q vignette and 0.510 for the Patient R vignette. In contrast, correlations across the two different patient vignettes at the same survival gain were lower (0.409 for the 3-month scenarios and 0.398 for the 6-month scenarios). This suggests that physicians’ willingness to select the hopeful option is more strongly driven by patient characteristics rather than by the length of the survival benefit.

The next most significant characteristic was the type of hospital. Both working at a large hospital with greater than 500 beds and working at a university hospital were significantly associated with choosing a “hopeful” (riskier) treatment. However, there is much overlap in these as almost all university hospitals are typically required to have at least 500 beds. The large hospitals which were not university hospitals allowed for some testing of these independently and both factors were found to increase the odds of recommending at least one “hopeful” treatment. The odds of a university hospital physician choosing the “hopeful” treatment in at least one vignette are 2.04 times higher than those of a non-university hospital physician (OR 2.04, 95% CI 1.15–3.62, p = 0.015).

The third characteristic of significance is whether the institution at which they worked had at least 2 physicians who met all the CAR-T optimal use guideline (OUG) criteria or if they didn’t (Table 8). OUG are guidelines established in collaboration with the relevant medical society in Japan that restrict the prescribing of CAR-T therapies to a specific patient population and set criteria that treatment centers must meet to administer them21. Based on those guidelines, physicians must have at least 6 years of clinical experience after obtaining a medical license, including at least 3 years of training in hematological malignancies; or, have at least 7 years of clinical experience after obtaining a medical license, including 5 or more years of training in pediatric hematological malignancies. Moreover, prescribing physicians must have one year or more of clinical experience with internal medicine or pediatric training related to hematopoietic cell transplantation and have acquired the necessary experience and academic skills. Lastly, they must have a clinical record of 5 or more cases of allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation.

Factors which were not found to be significant were the number of refractory DLBCL patients the physician treated in the last year, years of experience, and whether working in the major population regions of Kanto and/or Kansai. Due to the small number of female clinicians in our sample, we were also not able to find significant gender effects, although female sex is usually associated with a higher risk aversion22–24. A more detailed table with these factors separated by vignette and time cases is provided in the Supplemental Appendix.

Discussion

This paper contributes to the growing literature on incorporating “hope” as a component of in healthcare decision-making. Specifically, it investigates whether physicians in the diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) and CAR-T therapy space consider hope, defined as a preference for treatments with uncertain but potentially longer extended survival, as an element in treatment selection. By presenting physicians with hypothetical treatment choices framed as a “hopeful gamble” versus a "safe bet," we aimed to assess their risk preferences in a context where such assessments had not previously been tested.

Our findings show that physicians opted for the hopeful alternative in 29–40% of cases. While this range is numerically consistent with Shafrin et al.9, who reported that approximately 40% of surveyed US oncologists preferred the hopeful option for lung cancer and 30% for melanoma, direct comparisons across these indications should be made with caution. The clinical contexts differ substantially, as lung cancer generally represents a poorer prognosis, whereas melanoma may carry a comparatively better prognosis. Given these differences, cross-disease comparisons are of limited interpretive value.

Instead, our results suggest that the propensity to recommend the hopeful treatment in DLBCL varies not only by physician characteristics but also, and perhaps most importantly, by the specific clinical context in which decisions are made. Consequently, our data cannot support strong conclusions about cultural differences in health-related risk preferences between Japan and Western countries, although it had been argued that preferences of Japanese patients and those of their Western counterparts are converging over time25. However, our findings could be viewed as a hypothesis to be explored in future research.

This paper builds upon prior work by Lakdawalla and colleagues8, who explored the value of hope from the patient perspective, by extending this inquiry to physicians. Previous studies suggest that patients are more likely than physicians or healthy controls to favor treatments offering a small chance of extended survival9. For instance, around one-third of German melanoma patients prefer a chemotherapy with an expected 3 months of remaining life expectancy over palliative care with the same survival time. On the other hand, only 4% of health care professionals and 10% of healthy controls had this preference26. A recent Japanese study also revealed that physicians tended to be more risk-averse than the general population when making treatment decisions27.

We also explored whether specific physician characteristics influence the likelihood of selecting the “hopeful” option. As in many other studies on risk preferences28–30, age showed the expected directional effect: younger physicians were more likely to select the “hopeful” option, while those over 60 were significantly less likely to do so. The reason for the age effect is not entirely clear; however, neuroscientists have suggested that age-related declines in the neuromodulator dopamine may contribute to the observed reduction in risk-taking31. On the other hand, years of experience, or patient volume, were statistically not significant across all scenarios.

The institutional context also played a role. Physicians working in large hospitals and university hospitals were more inclined to choose the “hopeful” treatment, a finding consistent with the assumption that academic environments foster openness to innovation. Likewise, hospitals with two or more CAR-T–experienced physicians demonstrated a stronger propensity toward “hopeful” decisions. These patterns underscore that treatment choices are shaped not only by clinical evidence but also by organizational context and prior experience. Building on this, the case of CAR-T therapies illustrates how decision-making extends beyond conventional clinical endpoints such as survival probabilities or progression-free survival (PFS). Additional considerations including the substantial financial burden, heterogeneity in patient preferences, and the potential for severe treatment-related toxicities, play a pivotal role. These factors may constrain the broader adoption of CAR-T therapies, notwithstanding the considerable hope they generate among patients and clinicians with respect to survival benefits.

Our findings have implications for health policy. Although we did not directly quantify physicians’ willingness to pay (WTP), prior studies10,21,32 suggest that a preference for riskier options is associated with higher WTP among both patients and physicians. Lakdawalla and Phelps recently incorporated risk preferences into cost-effectiveness analysis through the Generalized Risk-Adjusted Cost-Effectiveness (GRACE) model33,34. In GRACE, risk preferences affect cost-effectiveness by (1) adjusting the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) through a multiplicative parameter that reflects patients’ risk attitudes, and (2) modifying WTP thresholds based on disease severity and the elasticity of utility with respect to health. Compared with conventional CEA, this framework often lowers ICERs and raises WTP thresholds, particularly for severe conditions12. For example, a recent GRACE-based analysis of two gene therapies for sickle cell disease increased conventional WTP thresholds by 50% while reducing ICERs by roughly USD 10,00035.

Despite growing academic interest, no HTA agency has formally adopted GRACE. The US Institute for Clinical and Economic Review (ICER) has acknowledged its relevance but continues to address such elements through deliberation within broad threshold ranges36. In Japan, HTA guidelines introduced in 201637 established a baseline WTP threshold of JPY 5,000,000 (≈USD 35,000), with adjustments for disease severity allowing increases up to JPY 15,000,000 (≈USD 104,000)38.

Limitations that need to be considered start with recognizing that these decisions were based on hypothetical patient vignettes rather than real-world clinical encounters, which may limit external validity and the extent to which responses reflect actual practice. This may also lead to responses reflecting what they deem the more appropriate treatment recommendation from professional standards rather than actual decisions reflecting a social desirability bias. This study takes a quantitative approach to see numbers and shares and understand the overall views of clinician specialists. Alternative and further qualitative research would help in understanding their motivation and thought processes as demonstrated by Bluhm et al.39. While subgroups of interest were pre-defined, some included few respondents. In particular, the small number of female physicians limits the statistical power and interpretability of subgroup analyses. The generalizability of this sample of Japanese hematologists and oncologists may be limited and may not reflect the same preferences of physicians in other nations or specialties. It is likely factors beyond the scope of this study would influence treatment choices and recommendation of hopeful options.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides new evidence from a previously untested cultural and therapeutic context, highlighting that physicians, like patients, may assign value to treatments that offer even a small chance of extended survival. It also underscores the need to reconsider how value is quantified in health technology assessments, particularly for novel, high-impact therapies such as CAR-T. Future research should further investigate patient perspectives within this therapeutic context and assess the extent to which physicians’ recommendations align with patients’ preferences. This line of inquiry would meaningfully extend the current literature, as prior studies have demonstrated discrepancies between patient preferences and physicians’ perceptions40 , which have in turn been associated with reduced patient satisfaction41–43.
