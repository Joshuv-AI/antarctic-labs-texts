A circadian transcriptional subnetwork and EARLY FLOWERING 3 control timing of senescence and grain nutrition in bread wheat
Authors: Christopher R. Buckley, Joshua M. Boyte, Yalin Liu, Robert L. Albiston, Jessica Hyles, Jesse T. Beasley, Alexander A.T. Johnson, Ben Trevaskis, Alexandre Fournier‐Level, Michael J. Haydon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Summary Circadian clocks control daily and seasonal timing of physiology and development. Because of their influence on photoperiodic flowering, variants in circadian clock genes have been selected for phenology during domestication of cereal crops. To explore the potential impact of this genetic variation on circadian‐regulated traits, we investigated the relationship between the circadian clock and leaf senescence in hexaploid bread wheat.We phenotyped a collection of elite wheat cultivars for circadian rhythms, senescence and grain nutrition and used genotypes of multiple circadian clock genes to assign cultivars to circadian multilocus genotypes (MLGs). We compared the circadian‐regulated transcriptomes in mature and senescent leaves by RNA sequencing and used near‐isogenic lines (NILs) of a deletion in EARLY FLOWERING 3‐D1 (ELF3‐D1), a known phenology locus, to test for a functional contribution to the timing of senescence and grain protein content (GPC).We detected variation in circadian rhythms between wheat cultivars, which was significantly associated with the timing of senescence. We revealed substantial reorganisation of the circadian‐regulated transcriptome during senescence and identified a transcriptional subnetwork representing a link between the circadian oscillator and regulators of leaf senescence. NILs of an ELF3‐D1 deletion confirmed a significant effect on the timing of senescence and GPC.Our results demonstrate an important role for the circadian clock in controlling the timing of senescence in wheat, which highlights potential consequences of circadian clock genes selected for phenology on other valuable crop traits.

Introduction

Leaf senescence is a controlled process involving downregulation of photosynthesis, the degradation of Chl and the transport of remobilised nutrients to nascent tissues (Gan & Amasino, 1997). Efficient mobilisation of nutrients from vegetative to reproductive tissues during leaf senescence is a key determinant of plant fitness. The relative timing of senescence affects a trade‐off between resource accumulation and usage (Distelfeld et al., 2014) and can be adjusted to balance these processes for adaptation to different environments (Estiarte & Peñuelas, 2015; Chen et al., 2020; Bucher & Römermann, 2021). In cereal crops, the timing of senescence has a major impact on yield traits. In bread wheat (Triticum aestivum), high grain protein content (GPC) is a valuable trait that can be achieved with earlier senescence, but typically comes at a cost of lower yield (Simmonds, 1995). Furthermore, these relationships can be environment‐dependent (Christopher et al., 2016, 2018; Alhabbar et al., 2018). Increasing nutrient content without yield loss across multiple environments is a priority target for crop breeding.

The circadian clock is a biological timing mechanism, comprised of a highly influential gene regulatory network (GRN) that functions to synchronise development and physiology with daily and seasonal environmental cycles. Plant circadian clocks strongly affect metabolism, transport and signalling underlying growth and development (Sanchez & Kay, 2016). In numerous cereal crops, the role of the circadian clock in photoperiodic flowering has led to widespread selection of circadian clock gene variants (Bendix et al., 2015), although the impact of these variants on other circadian‐controlled traits has not been explored. For example, in hexaploid bread wheat, several variants in the PSEUDO‐RESPONSE REGULATOR (PRR) gene Photoperiod‐1 (Ppd‐1) confer photoperiod insensitivity (Beales et al., 2007; Shaw et al., 2012) and variants in EARLY FLOWERING 3 (ELF3), a core component of the evening complex (EC), are attributed to the flowering time locus Earliness per se (Eps‐1; Zikhali et al., 2016; Wittern et al., 2023).

The circadian clock has been shown to directly influence leaf senescence in Arabidopsis thaliana. Core circadian clock genes have been reported to repress dark‐induced senescence, jasmonic acid (JA)‐induced senescence and age‐dependent senescence (Sakuraba et al., 2014; Kim et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2018). LUX ARRHYTHMO (LUX), the DNA‐binding component of the EC, binds to and represses the MYC2 promoter, a key activator of JA‐induced senescence (Zhang et al., 2018). PRR9 binds to the promoter of ORESARA1 (ORE1), a positive regulator of ethylene‐ and age‐dependent senescence (Kim et al., 2018). Furthermore, circadian period accelerates in ageing tissue in Arabidopsis (Kim et al., 2016) and ethylene can shorten circadian period (Haydon et al., 2017).

Based on the evidence for circadian control of leaf senescence in Arabidopsis, we sought to explore the role of the circadian clock in wheat in controlling leaf senescence and determining grain nutrient content. We compiled a panel of elite wheat cultivars and found these traits covaried with phenotypic and genotypic variation in the circadian clock. We compared the circadian transcriptomes of mature and senescent flag leaves and used genotypic information for circadian clock genes to define circadian multilocus genotypes (MLGs) within the wheat panel. These suggested a role for the clock gene ELF3‐D1, and using near‐isogenic lines (NILs) of a widespread deletion variant in ELF3‐D1, we found a functional contribution to the timing of senescence and GPC.

Materials and Methods

Plant materials

Multi‐environment phenotyping experiments of Triticum aestivum L. (wheat) used a panel of 15 elite Australian cultivars (Calingiri, Cobra, Corack, Gladius, Hydra, Mace, Magenta, Scout, Sunguard, Suntop, Supreme, Viking, Wyalkatchem, Yitpi and Zen), which were obtained from the Australian Grains Genebank (AGG). Subsequently, a 25‐cultivar circadian diversity panel was developed by including 10 additional cultivars from the OzWheat diversity panel developed by the Commonwealth Scientific and Industrial Research Organisation (CSIRO; Hyles et al., 2024). These cultivars were Axe, Bayonet, Buckley, Cascades, EGA Wedgetail, Insignia, Lillimur, Takari, Wilgoyne and Yarralinka.

Four pairs of NILs in T. aestivum with the presence or absence of a deletion in intron 2 of ELF3‐D1 were derived from a large NIL population with phenology gene variation developed by CSIRO (Boden et al., 2015; Steinfort et al., 2017). The NILs were generated by crossing donors of phenology genes with the cultivar Sunstate, which carries the ELF3‐D1del allele. This was followed by five rounds of recurrent backcrossing to obtain an average 97% genetic identity between NILs.

Growth conditions

For glasshouse experiments, seeds were sown in July–August (Parkville, Victoria). Full details of growth conditions are described in Supporting Information Methods S1. Internal glasshouse temperature data, as well as solar exposure and daylength data for glasshouse seasons, are provided in Dataset S1. Controlled environment room and growth cabinet experiments were set to 12 h : 12 h, light : dark (200–280 μmol m−2 s−1, 20°C) (15°C). In all phenotyping experiments, seeds were chilled at 4°C for 48 h and sown in a mix of steamed potting soil with 4.5 g l−1 Osmocote 6–9 month slow‐release fertiliser.

Delayed leaf fluorescence imaging

Delayed leaf fluorescence (DF) was imaged in the second leaves of plants at the five‐leaf stage, except in the development time‐course experiment. In all experiments, 2.5‐cm sections were dissected from excised leaves (cut 3 cm from the leaf tip) of individual plants for each biological replicate and placed on 12 cm2 0.5% agar (type M; Sigma‐Aldrich) Petri plates, arranged according to a randomised controlled block design. Leaf sections were imaged using a Retiga LUMO charge‐coupled device (CCD) camera (Teledyne Photometrics, Tucson, AZ, USA) fitted with a 25 mm f/0.95 lens housed within light‐tight dark boxes. Light (> 45 μmol m−2 s−1) was provided by two LB3 red/blue LED panels (Photek, St Leondards on Sea, UK). The lights and camera were controlled by beanshell (bsh) scripts in μManager v.2.0 (Edelstein et al., 2014). DF was measured in 120 h of continuous light (LL) by 1‐min camera exposure in the dark, following 59 min of light. Camera properties were binning = 4 × 4, gain = 1, readout rate = 0.650195 MHz 16 bit. DF was measured in leaf sections from image stacks as integrated density using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health). Time series data were uploaded to BioDare2 (Zielinski et al., 2014) for period analysis of background‐subtracted data using the fast Fourier transform nonlinear least squares method (Plautz et al., 1997). Data were detrended according to the amplitude and baseline detrending method and analysis was performed using a 24–96 h window. Estimates of period that fell outside the range of 24 ± 5 h were excluded. Time series intensity data presented are Z‐score normalised. Best linear unbiased predictors (BLUPs) for each cultivar were calculated from circadian period estimates from three environments. For this, a linear mixed model was produced using the R/lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015). Environment was modelled as a fixed effect, and cultivar was modelled as a random effect.

Quantifying time to senescence

Leaf Chl content was recorded using a handheld SPAD meter (Konica Minolta, Marunouchi, Japan). Measurements were taken three times a week from heading (full emergence of ear). Five to ten SPAD values per plant were measured across the whole flag leaf of the main tiller, and the mean value was recorded. Time to senescence was defined as the number of days after anthesis (DAA) to reach 50% of the maximum Chl content. Plants with time to senescence values three SD above or below the overall mean were excluded. BLUPs for each cultivar were calculated from time to senescence estimates from two environments. Environment was modelled as a fixed effect, and cultivar was modelled as a random effect.

Nutrient profiling of grain and leaf samples

Whole flag leaves were harvested at anthesis from six plants of each genotype and mature grains were harvested from another six plants. Flag leaf harvest was performed between 9:00 and 12:00 h (c. 2–5 h after dawn). For grain samples, all ripened grain heads from each plant were harvested. Thousand grain weight (TGW) was calculated by weighing 50 seeds from each sample. Leaf and grain samples were dried at 65°C for 7 d. Threshed grain samples were sieved with a 2‐mm slotted sieve and then milled with a Tube Mill ‘Control’ (Ika). Each sample was ground for 90 s at 25 000 rpm. Dried leaf samples were milled using a TissueLyser (Qiagen), with each sample milled for 2 min at 21.5 Hz with a single 18‐mm stainless steel ball bearing. Powdered leaf and grain samples were digested in HNO3 and ion concentration was determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy. Total remobilisation efficiency was calculated as the mean normalised ratio of seed‐to‐leaf concentration across eight elements (P, S, K, Ca, Mg, Fe, Zn and Mn). Total protein of grain samples was extracted following the protocol of Dupont et al. (2011) and quantified by bicinchoninic acid assay.

Defining circadian multilocus genotypes

Clock gene markers were identified by searching for single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the OzWheat diversity panel (Hyles et al., 2024). Genotyping of the entire OzWheat panel has been performed with allele‐specific markers for agronomically important genes for phenology (e.g. Ppd‐1 and ELF3) and c. 26 K SNPs were identified by transcriptome sequencing (crown tissue from seedlings) of the panel. The OzWheat polymorphism database was filtered against a set of putative wheat clock genes (Table S1), and novel nonsynonymous SNPs in clock genes were retained. Kompetitive allele specific PCR genotyping of T. aestivum clock gene markers (Ppd‐B1, Ppd‐D1 and ELF3‐D1) and novel SNP markers (PRR73‐A1, PRR59‐B1, TOC1‐B1 and LUX‐B1) was completed for the circadian diversity panel. Homoeologue‐specific primers for each clock gene marker were designed using PolyMarker (Ramirez‐Gonzalez et al., 2015; Table S2).

Genotype data for the seven clock gene markers from cultivars in the OzWheat panel plus additional cultivars in this study were used as input for k‐modes categorical clustering, using only cultivars with complete genotypic data for the seven genes. To choose the value for k, the total within‐cluster simple‐matching distance was calculated and plotted for a range of possible values of k (2–10). k‐modes clustering was performed using the R/klaR package (Weihs et al., 2005). Circadian MLGs were visualised in space by multiple correspondence analysis (MCA), performed using the R/FactoMineR package (Lê et al., 2008). To estimate the number of dimensions to retain for MCA, k‐fold cross‐validation was performed using the R/missMDA package (Josse & Husson, 2016), and the number of dimensions (3) that produced the smallest mean square error of prediction was used for MCA.

RNA‐sequencing

Wheat seeds of the Australian elite cultivar Mace were sown in a single controlled environment room in two staggered batches, 28 d apart. When ‘mature’ plants were at ear emergence (Zadok's stage 51–59) and ‘senescent’ plants had evident Chl degradation in flag leaves, the controlled environment room was set to continuous light and temperature (20°C) at dawn (Zeitgeber Time 0; ZT0). Whole flag leaves from the primary tiller of four plants per developmental stage were collected concurrently every 2 h from ZT45 to ZT91 (48 h). Samples were immediately flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C.

Frozen leaf tissue was ground to a fine powder in liquid nitrogen‐cooled mortar and pestles. Lysate buffer was added to the tissue (350 μl per 100 mg of tissue) and the mixture was vortexed immediately. RNA was extracted using ISOLATE II RNA Plant Kit (Meridian Bioscience, Cincinnati, OH, USA). For each timepoint and developmental stage, the four RNA samples were divided into two equally pooled RNA samples, each from two individual flag leaf samples. Library preparation and RNA‐sequencing for all 96 samples was performed by a commercial provider (Genewiz from Azenta, South Plainfield, NJ, USA). Library preparations were constructed using NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). Libraries with different indices were multiplexed and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Sequencing was carried out using a 2 × 150‐bp paired‐end (PE) configuration. An average of 24 million 150‐bp PE reads were generated for each library.

Reads were filtered for quality and mapped to the T. aestivum cv Mace (PGSBv2.1) genome sequence (Walkowiak et al., 2020) and annotation using Spliced Transcripts Alignment to a Reference (Star) with default parameters (Dobin et al., 2013). Mapped reads were quantified and normalised using the cuffquant and cuffnorm modules of Cufflinks (Trapnell et al., 2010). Normalised counts (fragments per kilobase transcript per million reads (FPKM)) outputted from cuffnorm were used for downstream analyses. For comparison, we also calculated transcripts per million (TPM) values the formula TPMi=FPKMi∑FPKM106. We observed a high correlation (R 2 < 0.997) between FPKM and TPM values across the dataset, which is expected when libraries have a consistent read depth (Fig. S1a,b). Only transcripts that were expressed (i.e. FPKM > 0) at least once in each 24‐h period (ZT45‐ZT67 and ZT69‐ZT91) were retained.

Parameterisation of transcript circadian rhythms

The packages Bio_cycle (Agostinelli et al., 2016) and r/MetaCycle (Wu et al., 2016) were used to estimate rhythmicity and parameterise circadian rhythms of transcripts. BIO_CYCLE was run using default parameters, with minimum and maximum expected period values of 20 and 28 h. MetaCycle incorporates different methods for rhythmicity detection to generate a combined prediction of rhythmicity. JTK_CYCLE and Lomb‐Scargle (LS) were selected for integration by MetaCycle. Default settings were maintained for all other parameters. Transcripts with q < 0.05 were retained from both BIO_CYCLE and MetaCycle, and the intersection of rhythmic genes from these datasets was used for downstream analyses. All circadian rhythm parameter estimates (period, phase, amplitude and periodicity) presented are derived from BIO_CYCLE. Phase values are adjusted to period (phase = phase (24/period)). The mature‐to‐senescent change in each of these parameters at the gene level (Δperiod, Δphase and Δamplitude) is used for several analyses. ΔPeriod and Δphase are calculated as the difference between the mature and senescent value of these parameters for a given gene (e.g. Mature period – senescent period). ΔAmplitude is the log ratio of mature and senescent amplitude for a given gene (i.e. log(Mature amplitude: senescent amplitude)).

Gene Ontology and transcription factor family enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) terms for the T. aestivum cv Mace (PGSBv2.1) annotation were sourced from Ensembl Plants (Cunningham et al., 2022). r/t was used to perform GO enrichment analysis of various gene sets using the ‘classic’ algorithm (Alexa et al., 2006). Fisher's exact test was selected to compare the enrichment of GO terms in each gene set to a gene universe of 87 493 unique wheat genes with GO term annotations. For enrichment analyses of transcription factor (TF) families, Chinese Spring wheat genes annotated as TFs were sourced from https://opendata.earlham.ac.uk/wheat/under_license/toronto/Ramirez‐Gonzalez_etal_2018‐06025‐Transcriptome‐Landscape/data/data_tables/ (Ramírez‐González et al., 2018). Mace orthologues of each gene were assigned through orthology data available from Ensembl Plants (Cunningham et al., 2022). For enrichment analyses, 2 × 2 contingency tables were built for each TF family and analysed with Fisher's exact test (from r/stats). Enrichment analysis of TF families that were only rhythmic in senescent tissue was performed against all TFs detected as rhythmic in either tissue. Enrichment analysis of differentially rhythmic TFs (detected by LimoRhyde, described below) was performed against all TFs that were rhythmic in both tissues.

Cis‐regulatory element enrichment

Promoter sequences (1000‐bp upstream of the start codon) were obtained from all wheat genes using the ‘retrieve sequences’ tool from Regulatory Sequence Analysis Tool (RSAT; Nguyen et al., 2018). Promoter sequences were subsetted into groups of interest (e.g. short period genes), and these groups were used as input for cis‐regulatory element enrichment analysis. For this, promoter sequences from the target set of genes were used as input for RSAT peak‐motifs oligo‐analysis (Thomas‐Chollier et al., 2012), with all genes that were rhythmic in either mature or senescent flag leaves used as the control set. Peak‐motifs settings were kept as default, and significantly enriched oligos with E‐value < 0.05 were retained. For each significantly enriched oligo, motif matching was performed with Similarity, Tree‐building, and Alignment of Motif Profiles (STAMP; Mahony & Benos, 2007) against the PLACE database of plant motifs (Higo et al., 1998), using default settings. The top match for each oligo (with smallest P‐value) was retained.

Differential expression and differential rhythmicity

LimoRhyde (linear models for rhythmicity, design) was used to test for statistically significant changes in rhythmicity between mature and senescent tissue (differentially rhythmic; DR; Singer & Hughey, 2019). LimoRhyde first decomposes Zeitgeber Time into sine and cosine with a period of 24 h. Then, DR transcripts are identified using a linear model that tests for significant interactions between developmental stage and the time components. LimoRhyde was also used to identify differentially expressed (DE) transcripts. For this, a linear model tests for interactions between developmental stage without interaction of the time components (Singer & Hughey, 2019). Code used to perform LimoRhyde's functions was developed from a vignette of the LimoRhyde GitHub repository (https://github.com/hugheylab/limorhyde). P‐values were adjusted by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure with a false discovery rate = 5% (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995). DR and DE genes were assigned using a q‐value threshold of 0.05. DE genes have log2 (fold change) > 1.2 or < −1.2. To allow comparison of circadian rhythm parameters (i.e. period, phase and amplitude), the DR gene set was filtered against the set of genes that are rhythmic in both tissues. Examples of DE and DR transcripts between tissues are shown in Fig. S2.

STRING protein–protein network analysis

Protein–protein interactions of DR wheat TFs were analysed using STRING (Szklarczyk et al., 2023). STRING uses an early genome assembly and annotation for wheat (cluster sum‐of‐squares (CSS); International Wheat Genome Sequencing Consortium (IWGSC), 2014). CSS gene IDs for the DR gene list were acquired via BLAST searches within STRING, although not all Mace gene annotations had an equivalent locus in the CSS assembly. DR TFs with STRING entries were used to build a protein–protein network in STRING. The network was built using both physical and functional protein interactions, and the minimum weighted interaction score was set to 0.4.

GENIE3 gene regulatory network analysis

Putative regulatory interactions of the subnetwork of DR TFs were identified using GENIE3 (Huynh‐Thu et al., 2010). GENIE3 uses tree‐based classification methods to predict the importance of a given gene (i.e. a regulator) for the expression of a target gene. Expression values from senescent tissue were used as input for GENIE3. The analysis was performed using default parameters and constrained to the 8593 transcripts that were assigned as rhythmic by BIO_CYCLE and MetaCycle in both mature and senescent tissue. The list of putative regulatory links was filtered to retain only the top 0.5% of links by predicted strength. Targets were identified by filtering the list to retain regulatory links associated with transcripts of interest.

Clustering of oscillator transcript responses

Mace orthologues of putative wheat circadian oscillator genes from Chinese Spring were identified using orthology data from Ensembl Plants (Cunningham et al., 2022; Table S3). The list of genes was filtered to include only transcripts that are rhythmic in both mature and senescent tissue. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed for the 59 clock genes in this list using three variables: mature‐to‐senescent changes in circadian period, phase and amplitude (Δperiod, Δphase and Δamplitude). The three PCs from this analysis were used as input for k‐means clustering. To determine an optimal value of k, the ratio of within‐cluster sum‐of‐squares to total sum‐of‐squares was plotted against a range of potential k values (1–8). PCA and k‐means were performed using r/stats.

Results

Circadian period and timing of senescence covary in elite wheat cultivars

Since the circadian clock influences leaf senescence in Arabidopsis (Sakuraba et al., 2014; Kim et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2018) and the pace of circadian rhythms shortens with age (Kim et al., 2016; Rees et al., 2019), we investigated the relationship between phenotypic variation of circadian rhythms in wheat and the timing of senescence. Using DF (Fig. 1a), we first measured circadian rhythms in the second leaf of three wheat cultivars 14 d after sowing (DAS; seedling), 33 DAS (5‐leaf stage) and 44 DAS (end of tillering). Relative amplitude error (a proxy for rhythm robustness) did not significantly differ by leaf age (Fig. 1b), but circadian period was significantly shorter in leaves of older plants (Fig. 1c). This demonstrates that a change in the pace of the circadian clock occurs in wheat leaves during ageing.

To assess the extent of phenotypic variation of circadian rhythms in wheat, we chose 15 elite cultivars grown across diverse regions of Australia (Fig. S3) and measured circadian rhythms of DF in three different environments: two glasshouses and a controlled environment room (Figs 1d, S4, Dataset S2b). Large differences in mean circadian period were identified across the panel, and the distribution of these differences was largely consistent across different environments (Fig. 1d). Significant differences were identified between cultivars at the tails of the distribution (Figs 1e,f, S4). Some cultivars exhibited strong genotype‐by‐environment variation, particularly in glasshouse # 1 (Figs 1d, S4). Nevertheless, there is significant variation in circadian rhythms within elite wheat germplasm that could influence circadian‐regulated agronomic traits.

To investigate the relationship between circadian rhythms and leaf senescence, we measured the timing of senescence in the 15 wheat cultivars. We monitored the decline of Chl content in flag leaves following anthesis in two environments (Dataset S2d). There were large differences in the timing of senescence between cultivars (Fig. 1g), with a maximum mean difference of 10.3 d in glasshouse #2 (Fig. S4). To account for the influence of different environmental conditions, we calculated BLUPs of circadian period and time to senescence across environments (Figs 1h,i, S5). The ranking of cultivars by their BLUP values was consistent between phenotypes; cultivars that were ranked highly for circadian period were typically ranked highly for time to senescence, and vice versa (Fig. 1h). Indeed, BLUPs of circadian period and time to senescence were significantly correlated (Fig. 1i), such that cultivars with shorter period tended to senesce earlier. Significant correlations showing the same trend were also identified within individual environments (Fig. S5). Overall, our data suggest potentially meaningful relationships between circadian period, leaf ageing and timing of senescence in elite wheat cultivars.

Since the timing of leaf senescence has a major influence on grain filling, we also measured the total ion content of mature flag leaves and harvested grain in the 15 wheat cultivars grown in a glasshouse experiment. This was used to calculate nutrient remobilisation efficiency (Dataset S2f). We detected substantial variation in remobilisation efficiency between cultivars (Figs S4, S6) and observed a strongly negative correlation with the timing of senescence (Fig. S7a). This trend was consistent with expectations (Uauy et al., 2006; Kipp et al., 2014) and suggested that variation in circadian rhythms that affects the timing of senescence could impact grain nutrient content in wheat.

Reshaping of the circadian transcriptome during leaf senescence

To explore transcriptional changes in the circadian network during leaf senescence, we compared the transcriptomes of mature and senescent flag leaves in cv Mace over 48 h in continuous light (Fig. 2a). Mace was chosen because it is an elite Australian wheat cultivar with available genome sequence (Walkowiak et al., 2020). PCA separated each individual transcriptome by developmental stage (Fig. S8a) and time of day (Fig. S8b–e). We used BIO_CYCLE (Agostinelli et al., 2016) and MetaCycle (Wu et al., 2016) to detect rhythmic transcripts and estimated their period, phase and amplitude (Dataset S3). A total of 11 705 expressed transcripts were called rhythmic in mature flag leaves (15.7% of 74 550 expressed) by both algorithms and 16 906 transcripts were rhythmic in senescent flag leaves (22.8% of 74 272 expressed; Fig. 2b). Since fewer rhythmic genes were detected in mature flag leaves compared with senescent flag leaves, the changes in the circadian transcriptome during senescence are unlikely to be due to a general decay in gene expression. We performed GO enrichment analysis of transcripts that were rhythmic only in senescent flag leaves (Dataset S4). Significantly enriched terms included ‘protein transport’, ‘peptide metabolic process’ and ‘nitrogen compound transport’ (Fig. 2c), suggesting that the circadian network is connected to the reprogramming of metabolism and transport during senescence.

To identify potential regulators of the circadian transcriptome during leaf senescence, we compared ‘periodicity’ (BIO_CYCLE's measure of rhythmicity, from 0 to 1; not to be confused with period) across families of TFs (Ramírez‐González et al., 2018). PRRs, which are core circadian oscillator genes (Nakamichi et al., 2005), were the most rhythmic family in both tissue types, followed by Sigma70‐like TFs, which regulate rhythmic chloroplast gene expression in Arabidopsis (Fig. 2d; Noordally et al., 2013). Mean periodicity of the WRKY and CCAAT‐HAP2 families was significantly higher in senescent tissue than in mature tissue (Fig. 2d). To summarise the overall changes in TF gene expression, we calculated metagene expression values of entire TF gene families (Fig. 2e). We observed a clear increase in WRKY metagene rhythmicity during senescence, suggesting a global increase in the rhythmic activity of members of this TF family (Fig. S9). By contrast, a similar change in rhythmicity was not observed for other TF families with established roles in senescence, for example NAC, AP2 and MYB (Cao et al., 2023; Fig. 2e). WRKY genes are known as regulators of leaf senescence (Guo & Gan, 2005), but our data suggest that the influence of the circadian clock on their regulation might have been underestimated.

Among the 8593 rhythmic transcripts in both tissues, the mean circadian period was c. 0.5 h shorter in senescent flag leaves compared with mature flag leaves (Figs 3a, S10b). This is consistent with the expectation for a shorter period in older leaves (Fig. 1c). In addition, a circadian phase advance of c. 0.5 h (corrected for period) was also detected in senescent leaves (Fig. S10a,b). Amplitude differences were small between the two developmental stages, although the mean amplitude was higher in mature tissue (Fig. S10c).

We noted that there was substantial variation in the period differences for individual transcripts outside the global trend (Fig. 3a). To identify patterns in differential period, we performed GO term enrichment of transcripts with shorter (> 0.5 h), longer (< −0.5 h) or unchanged (< ±0.5 h) circadian period in senescent leaves (Dataset S4). Rhythmic genes with shorter period were significantly enriched for GO categories related to primary metabolism (Figs 3b,c, S11). Among transcripts with longer period in senescent tissue, terms related to photosynthesis were overrepresented. The longer period of these transcripts could be related to decreased amplitude and loss of rhythmic precision, perhaps reflecting the breakdown of normal rhythmic regulation of photosynthesis (Figs 3b,c, S11). The most strongly enriched GO terms within transcripts with unchanged period included ‘rhythmic process’ and 'circadian rhythm' (Figs 3b,c, S11). This suggests that the global shortening of period in the senescent transcriptome could be driven by amplitude or phase changes in the circadian oscillator.

To identify other transcriptional changes between mature and senescent leaves, we used LimoRhyde (Singer & Hughey, 2019) to identify DE and differentially rhythmic (DR) genes (Dataset S4). DE genes have different levels of expression overall, independent of time, whereas DR genes include differences in phase, period or amplitude (Fig. S2). Among DE genes in senescent leaves, 6310 upregulated genes were enriched for GO terms for transport and catabolism, and 8785 downregulated genes were enriched for GO terms related to biosynthesis (Fig. S12). There were 1743 DR genes, enriched for GO terms related to photosynthesis, light signalling and circadian rhythms (Fig. 3d). When we compared DR genes to the remainder of the rhythmic transcriptome, we observed an advanced mean phase in senescent tissue, rather than a change in period or amplitude (Fig. 3e).

A circadian‐regulated subnetwork in senescent leaves

We hypothesised that the phase‐advanced DR genes, which are enriched for the ‘circadian rhythm’ GO term, represent a subnetwork driving shorter periods of the wider transcriptome. We identified 106 TFs among the DR genes (Dataset S4), which were characterised by large phase advances (Fig. S13a). Several families of TFs were significantly over‐represented, including C2C2‐Dof, TAZ, FHA, PRR and Sigma70 (Fig. S13b). To analyse the interactions between DR TFs, we modelled the putative interactions between members of this gene set using STRING (Szklarczyk et al., 2023). We identified 308 putative functional and physical interactions, which is sevenfold more than expected (Hypergeo. test, P < 1 × 10−16). The network includes genes related to hormone signalling, light signalling and photoperiodic flowering and is centred around core clock genes: PRRs, REVEILLEs (RVEs), LUX and LATE ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL (LHY; Fig. 3f). Evening elements, which are cis‐regulatory motifs recognised by clock TFs (Harmer et al., 2000), were highly significantly enriched among all DR gene promoters (Table S1). To identify potential targets of this subnetwork, we performed GRN analysis of the circadian transcriptome at senescence using GENIE3 (Huynh‐Thu et al., 2010). Among the top 0.5% of regulatory interactions, the 106 DR TFs were predicted to regulate 4063 genes, which is a significantly larger number of targets than would be expected by chance (One‐sided permutation test, P = 0.0021). The distribution of change in circadian period and phase among these reflected the global distribution (Fig. S14a,b), suggesting that this subnetwork of TFs strongly influences the global changes in rhythmic expression observed during senescence. The most significantly enriched GO terms across the putative target genes included hormone signalling, metabolism, transport and photosynthesis (Fig. S14c). These results suggest that a circadian‐controlled transcriptional subnetwork with advanced phase during senescence targets genes that are involved in the mechanisms of senescence‐related change.

We next used the transcriptome datasets to perform a systematic analysis of circadian oscillator gene expression. We identified 59 out of 79 putative circadian oscillator genes (Table S3) that were rhythmic in both tissues (Fig. S15). Mean period difference of this group was 0.52 h, which was similar to the global trend. This was unexpected, since genes that we observed as unchanged for circadian period were enriched for the GO term ‘circadian rhythm’ (Fig. 3b). This suggests that there are multiple discrete patterns of gene expression that occur within the circadian oscillator in response to senescence. Strikingly, the mean phase advance of oscillator genes was 1.37 h, which is significantly larger than the global average (Welch's t‐test, t (59.5) = 4.21, P < 0.001). To categorise the oscillator genes by rhythmic expression patterns, we performed PCA of changes in period, phase and amplitude (Fig. 4a). The three PCs were grouped into six transcript clusters of 1–20 genes by k‐means (Fig. 4c). Clusters 5 and 6 are mainly comprised of genes expressed around dawn, including RVEs, NIGHT LIGHT–INDUCIBLE AND CLOCK‐REGULATED (LNKs) and LHY genes (Fig. 4c) and have shorter period in senescing tissue, consistent with the global trend (Fig. 4b,d). Clusters 2, 3 and 4 have advanced phase in senescing tissue (Fig. 4b), similar to the identified subnetwork (Fig. 3f). These are mostly afternoon‐ or evening‐phased genes and include the majority of PRR and LUX genes (Fig. 4c). Amplitude was increased in Cluster 2 genes, and period was unchanged in Clusters 2 and 4 (Fig. 4b,d). To assess whether transcript clusters influence different aspects of senescence, we compared GO enrichment of the GRN associated with each cluster. Predicted regulatory targets of Cluster 5 and 6 genes within the GRN were relatively more enriched for terms related to transport and primary metabolism whereas terms related to hormonal responses and cellular signalling were relatively more enriched among targets of Cluster 2, 3 and 4 genes, with ‘response to cytokinin’ particularly prominent for Cluster 2 targets (Fig. S14d). Thus, there are distinct changes in expression of oscillator genes during senescence according to their phase of expression and these changes appear to affect different aspects of the senescence programme.

Identification of circadian multilocus genotypes within elite wheat cultivars

Since the circadian clock appears to strongly influence the wheat transcriptome during leaf senescence, we sought to maximise circadian gene variation within the wheat panel for further phenotyping. We explored SNP data for the OzWheat diversity panel comprised of 283 Australian cultivars (Hyles et al., 2024). We found four novel nonsynonymous SNPs in conserved regions of clock genes from distinct transcript clusters (Fig. 4c); PRR59‐B1 (Cluster 2), LUX‐B1 (Cluster 3), TOC1‐B1 (Cluster 4) and PRR73‐A1 (Cluster 6). We used these SNPs and three major polymorphic clock gene loci in wheat (Ppd‐B1, Ppd‐D1 and ELF3‐D1; Beales et al., 2007; Cane et al., 2013; Wittern et al., 2023), to categorise cultivars into six circadian MLGs (Fig. 5a,b; Table S4) and compiled a circadian diversity panel of 25 cultivars, representing all six circadian MLGs (Table S5). The panel was grown in a glasshouse environment to measure circadian period and timing of senescence (Figs 5e,f, S16; Dataset S2b,d). There were significant differences between some circadian MLGs for both circadian period and time to senescence (Fig. 5c,d). Specifically, circadian period in MLG 6 was significantly longer than in MLG 1 and 5. Time to senescence was significantly earlier in MLG 3 than in MLG 4. Grain filling was modestly affected, as TGW was significantly higher in MLG 1 than in MLGs 3–6 (Fig. S17a,b; Dataset S2I). However, there were no significant differences between MLGs for GPC (Fig. S17c,d; Dataset S2g). Although the circadian period values do not correlate with time to senescence and TGW for all MLGs, MLG 5 exhibited a short period, early senescence and low TGW, while MLG 6 showed both a longer period and later senescence (Figs 5c,d, S17).

ELF3‐D1 deletion impacts timing of senescence and GPC

Circadian MLGs 2 and 5 both have a deletion in ELF3‐D1 and relatively early senescence, and LUX, the DNA‐binding component of the EC, was detected among TFs in the transcriptional subnetwork (Fig. 3f). To test the potential for this circadian polymorphism affecting the EC on the timing of leaf senescence, we used four pairs of NILs that differ by variation at ELF3‐D1 (Tables S4, S5; Dataset S2c,e,h,j). Across the four pairs of NILs, circadian period was not significantly different (Fig. 6a,b) and neither was TGW (Fig. 6c,d). However, mean time to senescence was earlier for the ELF3‐D1 deletion in three out of four NIL pairs, and this difference was significant in two pairs (Fig. 6e,f). GPC was significantly lower in lines with the ELF3‐D1 deletion in the same three NIL pairs (Fig. 6g,h). The exception is NIL pair c, which carries a photoperiod insensitive allele for Ppd‐D1 (Table S6), a putative downstream target of ELF3. Thus, the absence of an effect in this NIL pair could be explained by an epistatic interaction.

Discussion

We have used a diverse collection of elite bread wheat cultivars and comparative transcriptomics to explore the relationship between circadian clock function and the process of leaf senescence in wheat and assess its impact on grain quality. Our data revealed significant phenotypic variation in circadian rhythms between wheat cultivars and identified a correlation with the timing of leaf senescence, which affects nutrient remobilisation efficiency. Comparison of the circadian transcriptome in mature and senescent leaves identified a circadian‐controlled subnetwork, centred around several core clock genes and connected to transcriptional regulators of senescence and their downstream targets. In order to reduce some of the complexity of comparing phenotypic traits in cultivars with diverse genetic composition, we used genotypic information for circadian clock genes in these wheat cultivars to define circadian MLGs, which we show have the potential to predict circadian phenotypes and yield traits. Phenotyping of NILs for an extant deletion variant in ELF3‐D1 of bread wheat suggested a functional contribution of this core circadian clock gene to the timing of senescence and GPC.

The overall trend in the rhythmic transcriptome was of shorter circadian period in senescent tissue (Fig. 3), which is consistent with expectations of accelerating rhythms with age (Kim et al., 2016; Rees et al., 2019; Fig. 1). However, there was substantial variation across the transcriptome. For example, photosynthesis genes were significantly enriched among those with a longer period at senescence, which could be explained by general decay in expression and an uncoupling from the core oscillator. Of particular note, genes that had relatively unchanged period during senescence were enriched for circadian genes but had an advanced phase (Fig. 3). In a natural diel cycle, the effect of shorter period and advanced phase on expression patterns will be similar but imply different underlying regulatory mechanisms. Three of the circadian transcript clusters we defined had advanced phase in senescent tissue and included numerous PRR genes, EC genes and GIGANTEA (Fig. 4). We propose that the phase adjustment of these oscillator genes could be driving the period change of the wider transcriptome, which is supported by GRN analysis (Fig. S14). Interestingly, ELF3 genes and Ppd‐D1 are among the few oscillator genes that have a substantially shorter period in senescent leaves and might represent a regulatory link between the adjusted oscillator and senescence‐regulated genes.

ELF3 is a core component of the circadian oscillator as part of the EC, a transcriptional repressor complex which includes ELF4 and LUX in Arabidopsis (Nusinow et al., 2011). Null mutants in ELF3 in Arabidopsis and double null mutants in tetraploid wheat have arrhythmic circadian phenotypes (Hicks et al., 1996; Wittern et al., 2023). Hypomorphic alleles of elf3 in Arabidopsis have short period phenotypes (Kolmos et al., 2011; Anwer et al., 2014). MLGs 2 and 5 have an ELF3‐D1 deletion, and MLG 5 had a relatively short period (Fig. 5). Since we did not detect significant differences for circadian period between the ELF3‐D1 NIL pairs, it seems unlikely that this variant alone causes a short period phenotype. Notwithstanding that the ELF3‐D1 deletion might be recessive to functional A and B homoeologues for circadian period, there are several genotypic differences in clock genes between these two MLGs, so the short period of MLG 5 could be due to genetic interactions between different clock alleles. For example, MLGs 2 and 5 have different alleles at Ppd‐D1 and Ppd‐B1, which are putative regulatory targets of the EC (Alvarez et al., 2023).

We found an overall significant effect of the ELF3‐D1 deletion on the timing of leaf senescence and GPC across four NIL pairs (Fig. 6). This suggests that the widespread ELF3‐D1 deletion, attributed to the Earliness per se (Eps‐D1) locus (Zikhali et al., 2016; Wittern et al., 2023), has a potential negative effect on grain quality, independent of any effect on the circadian period. Interestingly, the ELF3‐D1 deletion has also been reported to affect the heading date, without an effect on the circadian period (Wittern et al., 2023). The exception in our experiment was NIL pair c, which has a promoter deletion within Ppd‐D1 that confers a photoperiod‐insensitive phenotype, in contrast to the functional alleles present in the other three (Table S6). Thus, the effect of ELF3‐D1 on senescence might depend on the genotype at Ppd‐1. Notably, Ppd‐B1 is among the PRR genes identified within the transcriptional subnetwork (Fig. 3) that we predict to make a regulatory contribution to leaf senescence.

Increased GPC is commonly associated with reduced yield in cereal crops (Simmonds, 1995), and this relationship has been attributed to the timing of senescence (Distelfeld et al., 2014). TGW, which is an important yield component, was not significantly different between ELF3‐D1 NILs (Fig. 6), but accurate estimates will require multi‐year field trials. These will also be required to better assess the robustness of the relationship between circadian period and timing of senescence, since we detected substantial phenotypic variation across environments within some cultivars.

Our findings have potentially important implications for breeders and growers because they suggest that extant variation in circadian clock genes in crops, which were selected for their effects on phenology, can have a significant impact on other traits, such as grain quality. The extent of these effects is likely to depend on genetic interactions between other circadian loci, and so identifying circadian MLGs within wheat germplasm could be a useful tool to predict phenotypic effects, particularly if defined by a wider collection of circadian polymorphic loci than the seven used here. The TFs within the subnetwork are candidates that could be targeted to fine‐tune circadian‐regulated leaf senescence without affecting the core circadian clock.
