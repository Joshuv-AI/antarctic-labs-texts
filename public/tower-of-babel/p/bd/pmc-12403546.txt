The ‘selves’ in doping and its psychosocial mechanisms: harmonised multi-country evidence from high-performing athletes in the UK, US, and China
Authors: Shuge Zhang, Ian Boardley, Jingdong Liu, Jules Woolf
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDoping remains an ongoing threat to clean competition. To date, global preventive initiatives have not addressed critical psychosocial antecedents of doping thoroughly due to the scarcity of knowledge regarding its psychosocial mechanisms from a harmonised cross-country perspective. We, therefore, conducted a multi-country investigation testing the interplay of two important yet overlooked attributes, namely narcissism and self-compassion, and examined their psychosocial mechanisms underpinning doping.MethodsUsing a sample of 499 high-performing athletes (80% competing at national level or above) from the UK, US, and China, we performed a series of multi-variate multi-group moderation models to test the narcissism × compassion interaction on doping and examined potential psychosocial mechanisms underlying such effects. In all analyses, we applied cluster control to adjust for coach-/team-level confounds and compared fixed vs. random effects models for cross-country comparisons.ResultsWe found an identical interaction across study countries showing self-compassion alleviates narcissism-related doping willingness (especially that vulnerable narcissism drives). Grandiose narcissism’s protective effect on vulnerable narcissism-related doping moral disengagement was invariant across countries. Resilient coping appears to be a consistent mechanism across study countries that explains narcissism-related risk and compassion-related protection. Fear of failure manifested varied mechanistic effects in different study countries, inferring potential cross-cultural differences.ConclusionVulnerable narcissism is a critical person-level correlate of doping. Grandiose narcissism, in the presence of self-compassion, can alleviate such risk thanks to enhanced resilient coping. Future education and interventions should tackle these important person-level attributes for anti-doping.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12954-025-01304-x.Keywords: Narcissism, Self-compassion, Resilient coping, Fear of failure, Anti-doping

The global fight against doping in sport presents ongoing challenges to the integrity and fairness of athletic competitions [1]. While prevention methods, such as education provision, continue to evolve, evaluations of prevention initiatives to date have uncovered a lack of multi-faceted approaches in addressing psychosocial drivers of doping [2, 3]. Identifying and addressing psychosocial factors driving doping, therefore, is informative to doping prevention [4]. A critical but overlooked aspect is athletes’ ‘selves’ or self-concepts and the associated psychosocial mechanisms underpinning doping.

One specific self-concept that is prevalent in athletes and relevant to doping is narcissism. Narcissism is a personality trait that features self-importance, self-centredness, and a disposition of self-enhancement for maintaining a (overly) positive self-portrait [5], and has been considered a potential driver in doping [6–8]. One commonly agreed view is that doping provides a (potential) ‘shortcut’ to outperform competitors, thus narcissistic athletes (compared to their counterparts) are more tempted to dope to satisfy their inflated self-image [9]. With preliminary evidence supporting narcissism-related risk for doping [6], recent work in the UK found that the grandiosity-vulnerability distinction is central to how narcissism exerts influence on risk factors for doping [10]. Specifically, athletes’ vulnerable narcissism (i.e., featuring hypersensitivity and hypervigilant to ego threat and a defensive, resent behavioural tendency) appeared to be more problematic than grandiose narcissism (i.e., featuring self-assertiveness with a hubris, exhibitionistic, and dominant behavioural tendency) as the former was associated with greater risk for doping when the latter was low compared high. In other words, narcissism-related risk for doping appeared to be driven by vulnerable (not grandiose) narcissism.

Nevertheless, existing narcissism-doping research had two limitations for consideration. One was the use of intermediate-level athletes from a single country (i.e., UK), the other was the insufficient adjustment of team- and coach-level confounds (e.g., team climate, coaching behaviours) when assessing risks for doping at the athlete level. Indeed, athletes varying in competitive levels demonstrate different psychological characteristics, and psychosocial drivers of doping can play different roles in lower- and higher-level athletes [4]. Research has also demonstrated that motivational climate and coaching behaviours explained between-team differences in doping risks and influenced athletes’ (pro-)doping attitudes [6]. Therefore, it is critical to control or adjust for team-/coach-level confounds when examining self-concept (e.g., narcissism) and doping at the athlete level.

A ‘healthier’ self-concept alternative to narcissism and may protect against narcissism-related risk for doping is self-compassion [11]. In sport, self-compassion encapsulates the sensitivity to distressed feelings or negative emotions in training and performance, with a motivation to reduce such distress [12]. It involves recognising that one is too harsh on oneself due to unrealistic or overly ambitious goals, with a gentle mindset al.lowing one to slow down, step back, or recuperate before continuing to engage in training and performance [13]. Compassion researchers also argued that self-compassion allows individuals to satisfy psychological needs without having to bolster one’s (overly) inflated self [11, 14–16]. A self-compassionate mind (e.g., high self-compassion, low fear of self-compassion) offers a buffer to athletes’ desire to lionise the self in sport, as it fosters acceptance that competitive environments are not always rewarding and frequently involve failures and setbacks [17]. Given the benefits of self-compassion (i.e., addressing grandiosity-related issues) and the prior knowledge that grandiose narcissism protects against doping risk associated with vulnerable narcissism [9], we propose that the benefits of grandiose narcissism in the context of doping may be valid only in the presence of self-compassion. More specifically, we hypothesised that self-compassion would amplify the vulnerable × grandiose narcissism interaction in the way that grandiosity’s protection on risk for doping related to vulnerable narcissism becomes greater when self-compassion is high compared to low (the moderation hypothesis; see Fig. 1 panel A for the conceptual model).

Despite sound conceptualisations, the psychosocial mechanisms explaining how narcissism drives – and self-compassion resists – doping are unclear. However, research has demonstrated that athletes are more open to dope or rationalise the use of banned substances under risk-conducive situations, such as when returning from injury and struggling to keep up with training and performance [18, 19]. According to these findings, we argued that one’s perceived ability to cope with and performance expectations or appraisals may be central to their risk for doping under certain risk-conducive circumstances. Given the link between vulnerable-grandiose narcissism and avoidance-approach motivation [20] and between self-compassion and low fear of failure [21] and adaptive coping [22], we hypothesised that athletes’ fear of failure (i.e., one’s negative appraisal towards performance failure) [23] and resilient coping (i.e., one’s ability to maintain optimal functioning under highly disruptive situations or setbacks) [24] would explain narcissism-related risk (via increase fear of failure and reduced resilient coping) and compassion-related protection (via reduced fear of failure and increased resilient coping) (the mediation hypothesis; see Fig. 1 panel B for the conceptual model).

We examined these hypotheses using a high-performing athlete sample from the UK, US, and China, providing the first harmonised multi-country evidence of narcissism, compassion and related psychosocial mechanisms underlying doping. Besides a multi-country approach, we advanced the predominant statistical approach in existing doping research by including cluster control to adjust for the nested data structure (e.g., athletes nested within coaches), allowing for more accurate estimation of doping-related outcomes by accounting for potential coach or team-level confounding factors. The present research, therefore, would offer implications to future anti-doping programmes regarding its design (i.e., what person-level characteristics to address), implementation (i.e., what psychosocial mechanism to tackle), and monitoring/evaluation (i.e., what process and outcome measures to consider).

Methods

Participants

Participants were 499 high-level athletes (Meanage = 21.89, SD = 3.75, 54.5% men; 10% competing at international level, 70% competing at national level, 20% competing at regional/state/county levels representing a competitive team/club) from the UK (35%), China (33%), and the US (32%). These participants (58.3% from team sports) had an average of 8.83 years of competitive experience and were training with 202 coaches (i.e., 2–5 participants nested under one coach). Top sports involved were track & field (32.7%), cross-country (13.2%), baseball (7.5%), softball (6.9%), soccer (6.3%) for the US sample; track & field (22.6%), basketball (14.6%), tennis (12.2%), badminton (9.1%), volleyball (7.3%) for the China sample; with soccer (54%), rugby (14.2%), volleyball (4.5%), track & field (3.4%), tennis (1.7%) for the UK sample. A prior power analysis [25] suggested that a minimum of 159 athletes were required from each study country (477 in total) to achieve 0.80 power to detect a small-to-moderate interaction effect (i.e., Cohen’s f2 = 0.05, for testing the moderation hypothesis) and a small-to-moderate indirect effect (i.e., standardised coefficient of all paths = 0.25; for testing the mediation hypothesis). The study samples fulfilled these requirements.

Measures

Risk factors for intentional doping

Doping moral disengagement

We used the Doping Moral Disengagement Scale - Short (DMDS-S) [18] to assess the extent to which one may rationalise the use of banned substances in sport. The DMDS-S contains six statements describing thoughts that athletes might have about doping (e.g., “Doping is okay if it helps an athlete advice other on how to do it right”) rated on a 7-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree), 4 (neutral), to 7 (strongly agree). Standard translation-back translation strategies were followed to create the Chinese version DMDS-S (see Procedures). We generated mean scores for further analysis, with higher scores indicating greater (risk of) doping moral disengagement in sport.

Doping willingness

We employed the Doping Willingness in Sport Scale (DWISS) [19] to evaluate the level of one’s intention to use prohibited substances. The DWISS assesses athletes’ willingness to dope under eight hypothetical circumstances (e.g., “You thought everyone you were competing against was using a banned substance and getting away with it”) rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all willing) to 5 (extremely willing). Standard translation-back translation strategies identical to the DMDS-S were followed to create the Chinese version DWISS (see Procedures). We generated mean scores for further analysis, with higher scores indicating greater doping willingness.

Self-concepts

Narcissism

We adapted the 16-item Narcissistic Personality Inventory (NPI-16) [26] and the Hypersensitive Narcissism Scale (HSNS) [27] to assess grandiose and vulnerable aspects of narcissism, respectively. More specifically, we adopted a 6-point Likert scale version of the 40-item NPI [28] to replace the forced-choice response of the original NPI. This is because the Likert-scale items yielded better psychometric properties than the force-response items of NPI in its Chinese version [29], whilst recent research has supported the advantages of Likert-scale over forced-choice response in assessing grandiose narcissism using NPI-based measures [30]. Participants rated the extent they agreed on sixteen narcissistic self-statements (e.g., “I like to be the centre of attention”) from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree).

For the HSNS, we instructed participants to determine the extent to which the ten HSNS statements describe their feelings or behaviours (e.g., “My feelings are easily hurt by ridicule or by the slighting remarks of others”) from 1 (very uncharacteristic or untrue) to 5 (very characteristic or true). We implemented the original HSNS for the UK and US participants and used the Chinese version HSNS [29] which contains identical items of the original HSNS for the Chinese participants. Mean scores were generated for both the NPI-16 and the HSNS, with higher scores indicating greater levels of grandiose and vulnerable narcissism, respectively.

Self-compassion

We measured dispositional self-compassion and fears of compassion in sport using the 12-item Self-Compassion Scale Short (SCS-S) [31] and the 4-item Fear of Self-Compassion in Sport Scale (FSC) [12]. The SCS-based measure is the most commonly used inventory for assessing self-compassion in athletes [32]. Participants rated the frequency they behave in the stated manners described in the SCS-S (e.g., “When I fail at something important to me, I become consumed by feelings of inadequacy”) from 1 (almost never) to 5 (almost always). The Chinese version SCS-S validated in a recent study involving 21 countries [33] was used in this project. We adjusted the reversed items and generated mean scores with higher scores indicating greater self-compassion.

To supplement the use of SCS-S, the FSC offered an alternative, rather opposing or simply contrasting perspective on self-compassion, focusing on an individual’s fearful feeling towards rather than the disposition of being self-compassionate. It contains four items (e.g., “I fear that if I start to develop compassion for myself, I will become dependent on it”) rated from 1 (do not agree at all) to 5 (completely agree). We used the Chinese translation of the FSC items from Matos et al.’s Matos et al.’ (2021) multi-country study of fears of compassion [34] and instructed participants to rate the items considering themselves participating or competing in sport using the contextualised instructions provided by Zhang and McEwan [12]. Mean scores were generated for FSC with higher scores reflecting greater fears.

Psycho-behavioural factors

Resilient coping

We used the Brief Resilient Coping Scale (BRCS) [24] and its Chinese version [35] to assess resilient coping. The BRCS consists of four statements describing one’s behaviour and action under adversities (e.g., “I believe I can grow in positive ways by dealing with difficult situations”) rated from 1 (does not describe me at all) to 5 (describes me very well). Higher mean scores indicated greater resilient coping.

Fear of failure

We adopted the Performance Failure Appraisal Inventory – Short (PFAI-S) [23] and the Chinese translation-back translation version for assessing fear of failure. The PFAI-S consists of five statements describing one’s perceptions and beliefs when performance failure occurs (e.g., “When I am failing, I am afraid that I might not have enough talent”, “When I am failing, important others are disappointed”). Participants rated the PFAI-S items from 1 (do not believe at all) to 5 (believe 100% all the time). We generated mean scores based on all PFAI-S items, with higher scores representing greater fears.

Procedure

We obtained independent ethics approval from the World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) and the lead author’s institution prior to starting data collection. To allow implementation of the study measures in all study countries (i.e., UK, China, US), we translated questionnaire measures that did not have a validated Chinese version (i.e., DMDS-S, DWISS, PFAI-S) using a translation-back translation strategy. Specifically, two PhD researchers in the field of sport psychology from a Chinese background first independently translated the relevant measures into Chinese. The lead Co-I from China then generated a test Chinese version based on the independently translated versions from the two PhD candidates. A professional Chinese-English translator was then employed to translate the test Chinese version of the targeted measures into English, followed by independent assessments of the back translated versions by the PI and the lead Co-I in the UK. Any potential issues or gaps between the back translated version and the original English version were identified and discussed between the reviewers, with feedback and comments sent back to the Chinese translators (i.e., the two PhD and the lead Co-I in China) for revision until satisfaction was reached between back translated and the original English versions for measures that required a Chinese translation.

With satisfaction translations for required study measures (see Supplementary Material - Scales), we built an online survey using Qualtrics with support from a project research assistant under the supervision of the PI and support from the Co-Is. The online survey distribution in all study countries was centrally managed at the lead institution by a project research assistant with support from the PI. The other Co-I’s facilitated the recruitment of participants in their corresponding country, which included but not limited to contacting gatekeepers for assistance, organising local research student helpers to facilitate participants recruitment and survey distributions. We created country-specific flyers that contains essential study information and a QR code for accessing the online survey. Prospective participants could scan the QR code using their mobile devices or obtain the survey URL link to access the online survey, which started with presenting full details of the study information and request for completing informed consent. The survey system would not direct a participant to the study questionnaires unless a formal consent was given. The whole survey took approximately 15 min to complete. Participants were encouraged to contact the project research assistant should they have any questions about the survey, which would be either answered by the research assistant straightway or forwarded to the PI or the Co-I in the relevant study country for a follow up. Following the completion of each survey, the research assistant would check the survey data for participants’ eligibility in receiving a cash incentive (i.e., e-voucher or prize; £6 or $8 equivalence) and provide a standard debriefing via email.

Data analysis

We used IBM SPSS Version 28 and Mplus Version 8 for data analysis. We first checked missing data and descriptive statistics (i.e., mean, SD, skewness, kurtosis) and examined the composite reliability of each study measure to assess internal consistency. We also examined zero-order correlations of study variables at the country level.

For testing the moderation hypothesis, we examined the three-way interaction between vulnerable narcissism (VN), grandiose narcissism (GN), and self-compassionate mind (i.e., self-compassion/SC, fear of self-compassion/FSC) on risk factors for doping. To explore potential cross-country differences, we conducted two multi-variate (i.e., doping moral disengagement/DM, doping willingness/DW) multi-group (i.e., UK, US, China) moderation models testing the VN × GN × SC and the VN × GN × FSC interactions whilst implementing model constraints on regressive paths to compare whether fixed (i.e., invariant across countries) vs. random (i.e., varying across countries) coefficient(s) achieved better fits (see Table 2). We used the robust maximum likelihood estimator (i.e., MLR) for model testing and compared robust Chi-Square (Rχ2) and degree of freedom (df) with a significant Rχ2 reduction suggesting better model fit [36]. We report comparative fit index (CFI), standardised root mean square residual (SRMR), and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) to assess model fit [37] and interpret the model with optimal fit only. For the final retained moderation models, we interpret main effects only when no 2-/3-way interactions were significant; we report 2-way interaction (i.e., VN × GN) only when no 3-way interaction (i.e., the moderation of SC/FSC on VN × GN) was significant. Simple slopes were assessed at mean ± 1SD [38].

For testing the mediation hypothesis, we conducted a multi-variate (i.e., DM, DW) multi-group (i.e., UK, US, China) mediation analysis, testing GN/VN/SC/FSC as predictors when examining their direct and indirect effects on DM/DW via resilient coping/RC and fear of failure/FOF. Identical approaches for cross-country comparison were taken (i.e., comparing models with fixed vs. random coefficients). All these moderation and mediation analyses were conducted with group mean centring of predictor variables and the operation of TYPE = COMPLEX command in Mplus to adjust for the nested nature of data (i.e., athletes nested in coaches) thus accounting for coach-/team-level confounds when assessing outcomes at the athlete level (Level 1) [6, 17, 39, 40]. These analytical strategies were pre-determined when registering for WADA’s Social Science Research Grant (Ref.19271).

Results

Preliminary analysis

Participants were excluded if found to be missing in the cluster (i.e., coach; 1.2%) or an exogenous variable (i.e., VN/GN/SC/FSC; 2.2%). Missing data in endogenous variables (i.e., DM, DW, RC, FOF; < 3.6%) were retained via the Full Information Maximum Likelihood approach (i.e., FIML; Hirose et al., 2015). Skewness and kurtosis of all variables were within ± 0.73 and ± 1.21, respectively, supporting data normality [42]. Composite reliability coefficients were all good. No outliers or unexpected correlations were found. Intraclass correlation (ICC) at the between-level (Level 2) for DM was 0.05, 0.02 and 0.01, whilst ICC for DW was 0.21, 0.03 and 0.03, in the UK, China and the US samples respectively. Such a range of ICCs indicated supported the use of TYPE = COMPLEX command in Mplus, rather than a full multilevel approach, for bias correction and model parsimony. Specifically, RC demonstrated a significant relationship with reduced DM in all study countries. The indirect effect of SC on reduced DM via RC was significant in all study countries, whereas the indirect effect of GN on reduced DM via RC was consistent in the UK and China. However, RC manifested weaker indirect effects of GN and SC on DW than DM. For comparison, FOF appeared more prone to cross-country differences given its negative, positive, and non-effect on both DM and DW in the UK, China, and US, respectively. The indirect effects of VN on DM and DW via FOF were significant and negative in China, but an opposing effect for UK and a non-effect for the US. Discussion

Doping remains a critical threat to clean competition and can cause various health issues in athletes [1]. Despite the ongoing global doping prevention effort, important psychosocial drivers and protective factors have yet to receive sufficient attention [4]. Tackling this research priority, the current research offered the first multi-country insights into the interplay of athletes’ self-concepts, especially those between narcissism and self-compassion, on risk factors for doping. More importantly, the present study examined potential psychosocial mechanisms underlying narcissism-related risk and compassion-related protection in sport. Using samples of high-performing athletes from the UK, US, and China, we found considerable support for both the moderation and mediation hypotheses, with a mixture of consistent and divergent findings across the UK, China, and the US.

To expand, moderation analyses revealed that the magnitude of relationships between athletes’ vulnerable narcissism and doping risk factors appeared greater as grandiose narcissism was high compared to low. Such an interaction was amplified, or the benefits of grandiose narcissism increased, when self-compassion was high compared to low and when fear of self-compassion was low compared to high (especially for doping willingness). Importantly, the nature of these 3-way interactions of vulnerable and grandiose narcissism and self-compassion or fear of self-compassion, as illustrated in Figs. 3 and 4, suggests that grandiose narcissism is adaptive in the presence of self-compassion but can be maladaptive or problematic when self-compassion is low or fear of self-compassion is high. Mediation analysis revealed that resilient coping consistently predicted reduced doping moral disengagement and explained the protection of self-compassion on the risk associated with doping moral disengagement in all study countries (not doping willingness). Cross-country differences were observed in fear of failure as it contributed to increased, decreased, and null effects in doping risk factors for athletes from China, UK, and US, respectively.

Research and policy implications

The findings highlight several noteworthy points that should inform the design, implementation, and monitoring/evaluation of anti-doping programmes. First, anti-doping agencies, policy makers and practitioners should consider assessing athletes’ narcissism (especially the vulnerable dimension) and incorporating SC in doping prevention programmes. Doping prevention attempts should address the vulnerability-driven in doping, whilst regarding SC as adaptive psychosocial resources that help resist doping. One specific application is that one could assess athletes’ levels of narcissism and self-compassion as indicators of relative risk for doping, especially those high in vulnerable narcissism and/or low in self-compassion. Such an approach has an exclusive benefit that the assessment of narcissism and SC is ‘implicit’ in comparison to common doping risk factor measures and thus resistant to social desirability. However, it is noted that the level of narcissism and self-compassion in the normal population is a relative rather an absolute concept [17, 40]. Therefore, one should avoid using cut-off values and consider repeated measures to tackle changes over time when implementing these measures for identifying ‘high-risk’ profiles.

Second, resilient coping is a protective factor of doping. Doping prevention programmes would benefit from building activities or practices to enhance athletes’ resilient coping. This suggestion is supported by our finding that resilient coping appeared to be the consistent mechanism to explain narcissism-related risk and compassion-related protection in doping across all study countries (especially that in relation to doping moral disengagement). Indeed, narcissistic personality is relatively stable across lifespan [43], and athletes may resist embracing self-compassion due to the dominant norm and sporting culture that stimulate self-criticism [13]. Our data suggest resilient coping exerts more proximal influences that operate the protection or benefits of grandiose narcissism and self-compassion on doping risk factors (especially doping moral disengagement) associated with vulnerable narcissism. Cultivating resilient coping, therefore, offers a feasible approach to doping prevention because it is intervenable in nature and provides adaptive psychological resources for resisting doping.

Finally, fear of failure likely has different meanings across countries. Our data suggested that fear of failure exerted contradicting effects on doping risk factors across study countries. Although literature has agreed on the debilitative effects of fear of failure on several sport-related outcomes [44, 45] and considered it a vital-to-address agenda in training for healthcare professionals working in other performance settings [46], results from the current study indicated that it is not necessarily a bad thing as it had positive, negative, and null effects on doping risk factors in China, UK, and the US, respectively. The findings suggest that performance-related fear in the context of doping is nuanced rather than a simple binary concept. Such fear may contribute to varying motivation or behavioural regulation, which subsequently amplify or alleviate risks for doping. Anti-doping agencies, therefore, should consider accounting for the cultural meanings of doping drivers when designing and delivering education programmes. Practitioners would do well to incorporate appropriate psychological training and support to tackle performance-related fears, whilst researchers should examine effectiveness of such practices in reducing risks for doping.

Strengths and limitations

No study has previously provided harmonised multi-country evidence of person-level characteristics relevant to selves alongside psychosocial mechanisms underlying doping. Compared to previous studies, one asset of the present study is the utilisation of a high-performing (i.e., 80% competing at the national level or above) multi-country sample in examining the person-level drive (i.e., narcissism) and protective (i.e., self-compassion) factors for multivariate risk factors of doping. Advancing previous studies [7–9], we conducted our analysis with cluster control adjusting for coach-/team-level confounds when assessing doping at the athlete-level. We employed robust model testing methods to determine if an effect was (in)variant for cross-country comparison. However, we acknowledge that our sample size may be limited in testing the mediation hypothesis that contributes to the understanding of psychosocial mechanisms of doping. This is due to the possibly overly estimated indirect effects when conducting a priori sample estimation and the feasibility issue we faced when recruiting high-performing athletes. Nevertheless, this limitation did not affect the test of the moderation hypothesis nor wash out the observed effects when testing the mediation hypothesis.

Another potential limitation was the lack of evidence for the measurement invariance of the study measures across the study countries. Measurement invariance [42] is a critical psychometric property that assesses the extent to which scores of a certain psychometric measurement obtained from different groups (e.g., athletes from the UK, China and US) are comparable. We acknowledge that no prior evidence exists for the measurement invariance of the study measures (e.g., DMDS-S, DWISS) across the UK, China, and the US, and thus one should not compare score levels of a measure across study countries. Due to the insufficient sample size in each study country for measurement invariance analysis, we did not perform measurement invariance analysis in the present research. However, given the nature of the multigroup analysis, measurement invariance of study measures was less of an issue, because the multigroup analysis we conducted did not pool the data from the different study countries. Instead, the analysis examined our hypotheses in each of the study countries as separate groups simultaneously for cross-country comparison (treating data from different countries as heterogeneous rather than homogeneous). Nevertheless, future research would do well to examine measurement invariance for measures such as doping moral disengagement and doping willingness to allow direct comparison of levels of risk for doping across these countries.

Additionally, given the observational and quantitative nature of the current study, we were unable to explore or offer insights into why cross-country differences existed, especially regarding the role that fear of failure play in the context of doping. Future research should employ longitudinal and interventional designs to further test and extend the current findings, as well as consider adopting qualitative methods for better understanding cross-country differences in the underlying mechanisms at play (e.g., fear of failure).

Conclusion

The present research provided harmonised multi-country cross-sectional evidence supporting the role of narcissism × compassion interaction in predicting doping. Vulnerable narcissism manifested a consistent undermining effect on resistance to doping in all study countries, especially when athletes’ grandiose narcissism and self-compassion were low. The presence of a compassionate mind, either in the form of high self-compassion or low fear of self-compassion, appeared essential for grandiose narcissism’s protective effect on vulnerability related doping willingness. Resilient coping accounted for these varied driving and protective effects in all study countries and is thus an essential psychosocial mechanism underlying links between the ‘selves’ and doping. In contrast, fear of failure as an alternative psychosocial mechanism manifested varied effects across countries. Future education and interventions should consider tackling and addressing these important person-level attributes for anti-doping.
