Isatuximab in combination with chemotherapy for pediatric patients with relapsed/refractory acute lymphoblastic leukemia or acute myeloid leukemia: The ISAKIDS study

ABSTRACT
AbstractChildren with relapsed acute leukemia have a poor prognosis; current relapse treatments are toxic, and novel treatments are needed. The anti‐CD38 antibody isatuximab is approved for relapsed‐refractory multiple myeloma in adults. We present results of the ISAKIDS study (NCT03860844) investigating isatuximab in children with relapsed‐refractory acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) or acute myeloid leukemia (AML). This Phase 2, single‐arm, multicenter, open‐label study enrolled children aged 28 days to <18 years. Patients received isatuximab 20 mg/kg induction on Day 1, then weekly for 5 weeks (ALL) or 3 weeks (AML). Standard salvage chemotherapy was added on Day 8. Participants showing possible response could receive consolidation with every‐other‐week isatuximab (two doses) plus chemotherapy (T‐ALL, B‐ALL) or optional second induction (AML). The primary endpoint was the complete response (CR) rate (proportion with CR or CR with incomplete peripheral recovery [CRi]). CR/CRi was observed for 32/59 (54%) evaluable patients (B‐ALL, 13/25 [52%]; T‐ALL, 5/11 [45%]; and AML, 14/23 [61%]). Secondary endpoints included minimal residual disease (MRD) status and safety. Based on local and central analysis, 56% (18/32) of CR/CRi patients reached MRD negativity using 10−4 sensitivity threshold for ALL and 10−3 sensitivity threshold for AML. One event of fatal cytokine release syndrome was reported in a patient with a high baseline white blood cell count, leading to trial adaptation. The toxicity of isatuximab with chemotherapy was otherwise manageable. Despite initial evidence of efficacy of isatuximab combined with intensive chemotherapy, CR/CRi rates did not meet stringent prespecified criteria to proceed to ISAKIDS Stage 2 (≥60% [T‐ALL] and ≥70% [B‐ALL and AML]).

INTRODUCTION

Approximately 80% of childhood leukemias are classified as acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), with acute myeloid leukemia (AML) accounting for most of the remaining cases. 1 , 2 Over time, advances in intensive chemotherapy, risk‐adapted treatment, and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) have led to improved outcomes for newly diagnosed pediatric patients with acute leukemias, with current 5‐year survival rates of approximately 90% for ALL and 70%–80% for AML. 2 , 3 However, an estimated 15% of ALL cases and up to one‐third of AML cases experience primary refractory disease (failure to achieve first remission) or relapse, and the poor long‐term outcomes and associated short‐ and long‐term toxicity burdens underscore an unmet therapeutic need for these patients. 4 , 5 , 6 To improve response rates and quality of response, particularly among poorer prognosis patients, there is a substantial need to find novel and better tolerated therapies.

CD38 is expressed at high levels in pediatric acute leukemias, 7 , 8 making CD38 a potential target for immunotherapy in children with ALL or AML. Isatuximab (Isa), an immunoglobulin G1 monoclonal antibody that targets a unique epitope of CD38, has the capacity to kill CD38‐positive tumor cells via multiple mechanisms including antibody‐dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), antibody‐dependent cellular phagocytosis, complement‐dependent cytotoxicity, direct apoptosis, direct activation of natural killer cells, and CD38 enzymatic inhibition. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 In the United States, isatuximab is approved for use in adults with relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma (RRMM) who have received two or more prior therapies (in combination with pomalidomide and dexamethasone [Pd]), or one to three prior lines of therapy (in combination with carfilzomib and dexamethasone [Kd]); or in patients with transplant‐ineligible newly diagnosed MM (Ti NDMM; in combination with bortezomib, lenalidomide, and dexamethasone [VRd]). 13 In Europe, Isa‐Pd is approved for patients with RRMM who have received two or more prior therapies, Isa‐Kd is approved for patients with relapsing MM who have received one or more prior therapies, and Isa‐VRd is approved for the treatment of Ti NDMM. 14 In Japan, Isa‐Pd is approved for patients with RRMM who have received two or more prior therapies, Isa‐Kd is approved for patients with RRMM, and Isa‐VRd is approved for the treatment of Ti NDMM. 15

Isatuximab demonstrated in vitro CD38‐expression–dependent activity against ALL and AML cells isolated from the bone marrow and peripheral blood of children and adults, with particularly robust ADCC effects. 7 , 8 In the TALL‐1 subcutaneous ALL xenograft mouse model, isatuximab plus vincristine reduced tumor volume to a significantly greater extent than that of either agent alone. 8 Isatuximab monotherapy was not effective in a Phase 2 study of heavily pretreated adults (many having received eight or more prior lines) with refractory T‐ALL or T‐lymphoblastic lymphoma (T‐LBL). 16 However, it is unknown whether the combination of isatuximab with chemotherapy is effective in both ALL and AML.

ISAKIDS is a planned two‐stage Phase 2 trial investigating isatuximab in combination with standard salvage chemotherapies in children with relapsed or refractory T‐ALL, B‐ALL, and AML. After partial enrollment of Stage 1, a prespecified interim analysis of safety and pharmacokinetics confirmed that the appropriate isatuximab dose is 20 mg/kg for all ages. 17 Here, we present the efficacy and safety results of the trial.

METHODS

Study design and patients

ISAKIDS (NCT03860844) is a Phase 2, multicenter, open‐label, single‐arm study conducted at 39 sites in 16 countries as part of a Pediatric Investigation Plan. Patients aged 28 days to <18 years with a confirmed diagnosis of first or second relapse of ALL of T‐ or B‐cell origin, T‐LBL (included in T‐ALL cohort) or AML (including those with a history of myelodysplasia) were assigned to three separate cohorts: B‐ALL, T‐ALL, and AML. Eligible patients had received no more than one prior salvage therapy and had white blood cell (WBC) counts below 20 × 109/L on Day 1 before the first isatuximab administration (requirement added per the protocol amendment in November 2020 after adverse events [AEs] were evaluated in the first nine treated pediatric patients, including one fatal case). Patients who underwent HSCT within the previous 3 months and those exposed to anti‐CD38 therapies within the last 6 months were excluded. A complete list of inclusion and exclusion criteria is provided in Supplementary Methods in Supporting Information S1.

Treatments

Treatment regimens for all cohorts are shown in Figure 1. Patients received induction therapy with isatuximab 20 mg/kg on Day 1 and then weekly for a total of five infusions in 5 weeks (ALL cohorts) or three infusions in 3 weeks (AML cohort). Standard salvage chemotherapy was added on Day 8 but could be added earlier, at the discretion of the investigator, if the clinical condition of the patient worsened after the first isatuximab infusion and required earlier intensive therapy. If per‐protocol signs of possible response to induction therapy (i.e., blood neutrophil counts ≥ 500/mm3 and platelet counts ≥ 50,000/mm3) were attained, then patients with ALL could proceed to consolidation therapy with every‐other‐week isatuximab (two doses) plus chemotherapy, and patients with AML could receive a second similar induction cycle with isatuximab plus chemotherapy (provided that they had <20% blast cells). Intrathecal prophylaxis with chemotherapy as per site's practice was mandatory for all cohorts. Dexamethasone 10 mg/m2/day (or equivalent) for 3 days before the first isatuximab administration was mandatory for the B‐ALL and T‐ALL cohorts and optional for the AML cohort. Granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor was optional for the AML cohort and could be continued until neutrophil recovery. As dexamethasone was part of both the backbone treatment regimen and isatuximab premedication in ALL cohorts, the Day −3 timepoint was considered as the first day of investigational medicinal product administration in ALL cohorts. All participants received the following oral premedication to prevent or reduce the incidence or severity of infusion‐related reactions (IRRs): montelukast, diphenhydramine (or equivalent), dexamethasone, ranitidine (or equivalent), and acetaminophen (paracetamol).

Procedures and assessments

Bone marrow aspirations for disease assessment were performed at screening, once between Days 8 and 15 (optional), at the end of induction for the B‐ALL and T‐ALL cohorts, once between Days 22 and 29 for the AML cohort, and once at the time of complete hematological recovery in all cohorts. The investigator could perform a bone marrow aspiration at any time if hematological recovery was not reached (for any reason), as long as no additional hematological change was expected and/or there was an urgent need to decide on the therapeutic strategy.

For the ALL cohorts, both bone marrow aspirations and peripheral blood draws for minimal residual disease (MRD) assessment were performed at the end of induction, at the time of hematological recovery, and/or at the time of each bone marrow aspiration for disease assessment if a complete response (CR) or CR with incomplete peripheral recovery (CRi) was achieved. MRD assessment was performed at a central laboratory by next‐generation sequencing for B‐ALL and T‐ALL using clinically validated clonoSEQ® and T‐cell receptor assays, respectively. 18 , 19 For the AML cohort, bone marrow aspirations for MRD assessments were performed at the time of hematological recovery, and/or at the time of each bone marrow aspiration for disease assessment if CR or CRi was achieved. MRD assessment was performed at a central laboratory by flow cytometry.

Populations for analyses and endpoints

The all‐treated (and safety) population included all patients who received at least one dose (even incomplete) of isatuximab regardless of the availability of response assessment in Cycle 1. The efficacy evaluable population included patients who received at least one full dose of isatuximab in Cycle 1 and who had at least one valid response assessment.

The primary endpoint in all cohorts was CR rate (2018 National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines) 20 , 21 in the efficacy evaluable population, defined as the proportion of participants who achieved either CR or CRi. The following were required to meet CR criteria: bone marrow blasts < 5%; no circulating blasts or lymphoblasts in cerebrospinal fluid (B‐ALL, T‐ALL); no blasts with Auer rods (AML); no extramedullary disease (central nervous system included in extramedullary disease assessment); absolute neutrophil count > 1000/mm3; platelet count > 100,000/mm3; and independence from red blood cell transfusion. A CR for LBL also required complete resolution of lymphomatous enlargement on computerized tomography. 21 A patient was deemed to have achieved CRi if all criteria for CR, except neutrophil and/or platelet recovery, were met. In addition, CRi could be reported if red blood cell transfusion dependency was related to study treatment rather than to the patient's underlying disease. A partial response (PR) was defined as a decrease of at least 50% in the percentage blasts to between 5% and 25% in the bone marrow aspirate and the normalization of blood counts as defined for CR.

Secondary efficacy endpoints included MRD status in patients achieving CR or CRi; overall response rate (ORR, defined as the proportion of patients with CR, CRi, or PR for blood and bone marrow disease); overall survival (OS); event‐free survival (EFS); duration of response (DoR); and the relationship between clinical effects and CD38 receptor density and occupancy. Post hoc, an additional secondary efficacy endpoint of CR rate in the all‐treated population was included.

Safety (with events graded according to National Cancer Institute‐Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0) was a secondary endpoint, including assessment of IRRs and AEs of special interest (AESIs; defined as any of pregnancy, symptomatic overdose, IRRs of certain timing/duration, or seizure of Grade ≥3).

Statistical analysis

Sample size calculations for each cohort were based on a two‐stage Simon's minimum–maximum design. In the T‐ALL cohort, a maximum sample size of 24 evaluable participants provided 80% power to reject the null hypothesis that the CR (CR + CRi) rate is ≤60% if the CR rate is ≥80%, based on a one‐sided exact binomial test at a significance level of 0.1. In the B‐ALL and AML cohorts, a maximum sample size of 36 evaluable participants provided 80% power to reject the null hypothesis that the CR rate is ≤70% if the CR rate is ≥85%, based on the same testing and significance level. If the CR rate exceeded 60% (T‐ALL) or 70% (B‐ALL, AML) in the evaluable populations after full enrollment of Stage 1 (at least 11 evaluable patients in the T‐ALL cohort and at least 23 evaluable patients in the B‐ALL and AML cohorts), the study would proceed to Stage 2. These were customized thresholds, based on previous studies in acute pediatric leukemias. 22 , 23

RESULTS

Study cohort

From August 6, 2019, to June 8, 2022, a total of 67 patients (27 with B‐ALL, 13 with T‐ALL, and 27 with AML) were enrolled. Demographic and baseline characteristics for the all‐treated population are shown in Table 1. On average, patients in the AML cohort were slightly older than patients in the B‐ALL and T‐ALL cohorts (median age 10 years compared with 7 and 9 years, respectively). Most patients in the B‐ALL cohort were in second relapse (numbers in second relapse: B‐ALL, 19 of 27 [70%]; T‐ALL, four of 13 [31%]; and AML, six of 26 [23%]), whereas most in the T‐ALL and AML cohorts were in first relapse (numbers in first relapse: B‐ALL, eight of 27 [30%]; T‐ALL, nine of 13 [69%]; and AML, 20 of 26 [77%]). Seventeen patients (five B‐ALL, 12 AML) had received prior HSCT. Among 61 patients with available data for CD38 expression, all except two (one B‐ALL and one AML) had a CD38‐positive leukemia as per local results.

Patient disposition and extent of isatuximab exposure

Overall, 59 of 67 treated patients (B‐ALL, 25 of 27; T‐ALL, 11 of 13; and AML, 23 of 27) were evaluable for response per protocol and comprised the efficacy evaluable population; a breakdown of patients deemed not evaluable is provided (Table S1). At the time of data cutoff (September 12, 2022), 19 of 27 (70%), seven of 13 (54%), and 20 of 27 (74%) of patients in the B‐ALL, T‐ALL, and AML cohorts, respectively, had received the full intended doses of isatuximab (defined as completing the entire 5 weeks of induction in the ALL cohorts and the full 3 weeks of induction in the AML cohort). Of the 67 patients enrolled, 46 patients (69%) completed treatment, and 21 patients (31%) discontinued: 20 patients before completing induction (progressive disease, n = 12; AE, n = 8) and one patient before completing consolidation (to avoid jeopardizing planned allotransplant).

To provide additional clarification on patient disposition, participant diagrams are provided for each cohort in Figures S1–S3. Of note, treatment completion was reported differently by the study sites, reflecting treatment strategies tailored to individual patient scenarios. For example, bridge to allotransplant, rather than the protocol‐specified consolidation therapy, was chosen in 3 of the 40 treated ALL patients (B‐ALL, n = 2; T‐ALL, n = 1) as the treatment strategy following induction. Only 26 (39%) of 67 treated patients started their second cycle, that is, a consolidation cycle (ALL) or second induction (AML), of isatuximab. Nine of 67 patients (six in the B‐ALL cohort and three in the AML cohort) achieved CR and completed the full treatment period (i.e., the consolidation period in B‐ALL and second induction cycle in AML).

The breakdown for isatuximab exposure by cohorts, including median durations of exposure and median cumulative doses of isatuximab, is shown in Table 2. The overall median (range) cumulative dose of isatuximab received was 100 (60–140) mg/kg in the B‐ALL cohort, 100 (1–144) mg/kg in the T‐ALL cohort, and 60 (20–122) mg/kg in the AML cohort.

Efficacy

Among evaluable patients, CR or CRi was achieved in 13 of 25 B‐ALL patients (52%), five of 11 T‐ALL patients (45%), and 14 of 23 AML patients (61%) (Figure 2). PR was achieved in one T‐ALL patient and one AML patient, yielding ORRs of 52%, 55%, and 65% in the B‐ALL, T‐ALL, and AML cohorts, respectively (Table S2). CR/CRi rates among selected groups in the evaluable population, including age groups, hyperleukocytosis status, cytogenetic status, prior immunotherapy, and prior HSCT, are shown in Table S3. The CR/CRi rates in the all‐treated population (examined post hoc) were 48% (13 of 27 patients) in the B‐ALL cohort, 38% (five of 13 patients) in the T‐ALL cohort, and 52% (14 of 27 patients) in the AML cohort.

Complete response (CR)/complete response with incomplete peripheral blood count recovery (CRi) rate (evaluable population). Among evaluable patients, the primary endpoint of CR or CRi was achieved in 13 of 25 B‐acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) patients (52%), 5 of 11 T‐ALL patients (45%), and 14 of 23 acute myeloid leukemia (AML) patients (61%). CR/CRi rates did not meet stringent prespecified criteria to proceed to ISAKIDS Stage 2 (≥60% [T‐ALL] and ≥70% [B‐ALL and AML]).

Among the 32 patients who achieved CR/CRi, bone marrow samples for MRD assessment were sent to the central laboratory for 21 patients and evaluable for 18 patients (B‐ALL, n = 6; T‐ALL, n = 4; and AML, n = 8) (Table 3). Of the six patients from the B‐ALL cohort, three patients achieved MRD negativity at 10−4 sensitivity threshold. For the four patients from the T‐ALL cohort, three patients achieved MRD negativity at 10−4 sensitivity threshold. Among the eight patients from the AML cohort, no patient achieved MRD negativity at 10−3 sensitivity threshold. Based on MRD data from local laboratories provided for the 32 patients who achieved CR/CRi, an additional 12 patients (B‐ALL, n = 5; AML, n = 7) could be considered to have reached MRD negativity in their bone marrow at the time of CR, using 10−4 threshold for B‐ALL and T‐ALL and 10−3 threshold for AML. These values were obtained after excluding patients with discrepant central and local laboratory results at the same time point (i.e., MRD positive in central assessment and MRD negative in local assessment) owing to different technologies and levels of sensitivity used. These results led to 56% (18/32) of MRD‐negative CR/CRi patients (B‐ALL, n = 8; T‐ALL, n = 3; AML, n = 7), using 10−4 threshold for B‐ALL and T‐ALL and 10−3 threshold for AML.

Safety

A summary of safety events, with details of AESIs, is shown in Table 5. All patients in the B‐ALL and T‐ALL cohorts had at least one treatment‐emergent AE (TEAE), as did 26 (96%) of 27 patients in the AML cohort. The most common all‐grade TEAEs (by preferred term; 43% each in the overall population [Table 6]) were febrile neutropenia, pyrexia, and IRRs (majority of Grade 1 or 2). Grade ≥3 TEAEs occurred in 53 of 67 (79%) patients; those occurring in >10% of patients included febrile neutropenia (42%), septic shock (12%), and stomatitis (10%). IRRs occurred in 29 of 67 (43%) patients overall, of whom three patients had Grade ≥3 IRRs: two (Grade 4) in a patient with T‐ALL and a patient with AML, which led to definitive treatment discontinuation (the patient with AML received only part of the first isatuximab infusion and no subsequent treatment), and one in a patient with B‐ALL (Grade 3), which did not lead to definitive treatment discontinuation. DISCUSSION

In a poor‐prognostic population of pediatric patients with relapsed/refractory ALL or AML, 32 of 59 (54%) evaluable patients achieved CR or CRi following therapy with isatuximab plus standard salvage chemotherapy. Based on local and central analysis of MRD data, 56% (18/32) CR/CRi patients reached MRD negativity using 10−4 sensitivity threshold for B‐ALL and T‐ALL and 10−3 sensitivity threshold for AML. Despite some initial evidence of efficacy, CR/CRi rates among the evaluable patients in the individual B‐ALL, T‐ALL, and AML cohorts (52%, 45%, and 61%, respectively) did not meet the prespecified thresholds to proceed to Stage 2 of the ISAKIDS trial. When considering all enrolled patients, these rates of responses were even lower. Consequently, the trial was closed to inclusions after Stage 1 for all three cohorts.

Based on historical data and a desire for any new treatment for acute pediatric leukemias to show meaningful improvement in efficacy versus the standard of care, we set high and stringent CR/CRi thresholds for proceeding to Stage 2 of the trial: 60% or above for the T‐ALL cohort and 70% or above for the B‐ALL and AML cohorts. Two previous studies that investigated the addition of bortezomib to standard reinduction agents in multiple relapsed/refractory patients with ALL demonstrated 70%–80% CR rates. 24 , 25 A Phase 1/2 study of blinatumomab in pediatric patients with relapsed/refractory ALL had a set CR threshold of 27.5%, with a total of 32% achieving CR within the first two cycles. 26 Our decision to enroll heavily pretreated patients (29 of 66 [44%] in second relapse) and the inclusion of 15 patients with primary refractory disease likely contributed to CR/CRi rates not meeting these prespecified thresholds.

Another possible reason for not achieving predefined efficacy criteria is that the duration of isatuximab exposure may not have been sufficient to achieve an optimal response even if major CD38 downregulation was observed at Day 15 of Cycle 1 in CD38‐expressing cells from all patients with evaluable samples. The patients in our study only commenced a median of one cycle (median exposure: B‐ALL, 6.0 weeks; T‐ALL, 5.3 weeks; and AML, 3.0 weeks). Notwithstanding methodological differences between ISAKIDS and the Phase 2 DELPHINUS study (NCT03384654), 27 including variations in background chemotherapy, adjunctive therapy with the anti‐CD38 antibody daratumumab was ineffective in children with relapsed/refractory B‐ALL and was associated with a similar CR rate to ours at the end of Cycle 1 among children with relapsed/refractory T‐ALL (ISAKIDS: 45%; DELPHINUS: 42%). 27 A median of two cycles of daratumumab was received by pediatric patients with T‐ALL (median exposure: 2.2 months) in DELPHINUS, which was associated with a combined CR/CRi rate of 83.3% (50.0% for CR alone) at any time before the start of subsequent therapy or HSCT. Although this is higher than the 38% CR/CRi rate in the ISAKIDS all‐treated T‐ALL population, nine of our 13 patients did not receive a consolidation cycle (one due to bridging to allotransplant after a CR), and the DELPHINUS population was less refractory to treatment. 27 Regarding the B‐ALL cohort in ISAKIDS, 15 of 27 patients did not receive a consolidation cycle; in two patients, the reason was to bridge to allotransplant after achieving CR. In the AML cohort, the second induction cycle was optional and required that bone marrow blasts were <20%. Nevertheless, among 17 patients with AML who did not receive a second induction, only six patients received an allogenic transplant after the first induction. Other potential reasons for CR/CRi rates not meeting their prespecified thresholds include the very high‐risk characteristics of our population (multiple relapses, refractory cases, and heavy pretreatments including HSCT and even chimeric antigen receptor [CAR]‐T cells for B‐ALL).

Preclinical studies of isatuximab monotherapy showed significant activity in ALL and AML cell lines, including particularly robust ADCC, and a clear association between CD38 expression and isatuximab activity. 7 , 8 As a result of these promising preclinical data, isatuximab has now been studied (as monotherapy) in adult patients with relapsed/refractory T‐ALL or T‐LBL 16 and, in the current study, as combination therapy with standard salvage chemotherapy in pediatric patients with relapsed/refractory B‐ALL, T‐ALL, and AML. Despite comparable expression of CD38 among patients in both studies, CD38 expression does not appear sufficient to predict the effectiveness of isatuximab as monotherapy, 16 or in combination with standard salvage chemotherapy, in heavily pretreated patients. Indeed, we were unable to find any clear associations for this study as well, between the likelihood of CR/CRi and the presence of CD38 expression or the level of CD38 receptor density in our study. A similar lack of association between CD38 expression and response to daratumumab has been reported in a retrospective cohort study of adult and pediatric patients with relapsed or refractory T‐ or B‐lineage ALL or LBL. 28 In that study, a low ORR of 20% was observed among 20 heavily pretreated patients who received daratumumab as monotherapy or with concomitant chemotherapy. 28

We did not observe any new safety issues when isatuximab was added to standard salvage chemotherapy in patients with B‐ALL, T‐ALL, or AML in our study, except that high WBC count at baseline may be associated with a risk of severe CRS during isatuximab therapy. During our study, a 7‐year‐old male with AML and a WBC count of 188 × 109/L on the day of the first isatuximab infusion experienced a fatal episode of CRS on Day 20 of his first cycle of investigational therapy. Though progressive disease was listed as his cause of death, CRS was listed as a secondary cause of death. As a result of this CRS event, a protocol amendment was issued requiring a WBC count of 20 × 109/L or below before the first isatuximab infusion.

A strength of our study was setting intentionally high CR/CRi thresholds to identify whether the addition of isatuximab to standard salvage chemotherapy could represent an improvement in the standard of care. However, applying a uniform response threshold to a population of patients who were at different stages of disease engenders difficulty in assessing treatment success. 4 , 29 The main limitation of our study was the inclusion of heavily pretreated patients and patients with primary refractory disease, potentially making it more difficult to discern the extent of any added value provided by the study drug. Moreover, for some patients, discontinuation of treatment may have occurred before optimal exposure to isatuximab could be reached and/or sustained. Finally, the enrolled patients consisted of a heterogeneous group of different types of leukemia, with each type represented by small numbers, rendering the study unable to conclude selective efficacy in particular subtypes. Our attempt to maximize the sample size by analyzing efficacy post hoc in the all‐treated population was confounded because not all patients received therapy as planned.

In conclusion, despite showing a manageable safety profile, the response rates after isatuximab and intensive combination chemotherapy did not warrant continuation into Stage 2 in the individual cohorts of pediatric patients with relapsed/refractory B‐ALL, T‐ALL, or AML of the ISAKIDS study. Investigation of isatuximab in earlier lines of therapy and/or in combination with other targeted therapies or immunotherapies, or over longer periods of time might reveal a role for anti‐CD38–directed therapy in children with acute leukemias.
