Midwifery students’ perceptions of factors influencing their clinical feedback in educational clinical environment: A qualitative study
Authors: Soheila Mohamadirizi, Zohreh Abbasi, Elham Shirdel, Sakineh Nazari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Feedback in bedside education poses numerous challenges and complexities, particularly in stressful environments such as the labor and delivery ward, where it can significantly impact the quality of learning and maternal and neonatal health. This study aims to explore the experiences of Midwifery students regarding clinical feedback in the labor and delivery ward.MATERIAL AND METHODS:A qualitative study was conducted, utilizing content analysis to analyze data obtained through interviews with 18 midwifery students. Participants were selected using purposive sampling. Data collection involved semi-structured and in-depth individual interviews, continuing until data saturation was achieved. Data analysis was performed, resulting in the extraction of main categories and subcategories.RESULTS:The qualitative study yielded two main categories and nine subcategories. The main categories consisted of constructive feedback, encompassing subcategories such as teacher qualifications, teacher personality, consideration of student traits, appropriate feedback, physical presence of the teacher, and positive feedback from others. The second main category was destructive feedback, which included subcategories such as negative feedback from others and inappropriate feedback during delivery, as well as the perception of inappropriate teacher behavior from the students’ point of view.CONCLUSION:The findings of this study highlight the importance of multidimensional clinical feedback in the learning processes of midwifery students. Constructive feedback was perceived as a key factor in enhancing learning, whereas destructive feedback was found to diminish the quality of learning.

Introduction

Midwifery is a crucial field of medical science that plays a vital role in the health of both the mother and the baby. Due to the high-risk and unique nature of their job in labor and maternity departments, the abilities and performance of midwives can be impacted.[1] Therefore, it is crucial to emphasize the quality of clinical education during their training. Research has shown that enhancing the clinical performance of midwifery students contributes to their competence in labor and maternity departments.[2] Various contemporary educational methods at the bedside have proven effective in improving the clinical competence, self-efficacy, and overall performance of nursing and midwifery students.[34] One fundamental element of bedside teaching and learning is the utilization of feedback.[5] Feedback provides comprehensive and detailed information that encourages learners to reflect on their performance, focusing on what they have done and the potential outcomes.[6] Therefore, feedback is considered the foundation of bedside education.[7] The purpose of feedback is to help learners identify their strengths and areas for improvement, facilitating their progress in the learning process without judgment or rejection.[8] For learners, feedback strengthens successful learning, identifies and corrects learning errors, while for teachers, it provides insights to enhance education and recommend individual or group remedial actions for learners.[8] Feedback in education not only stimulates critical thinking, addresses deficiencies, and promotes individual growth but also enhances problem-solving abilities and group discussions.[9] Furthermore, providing feedback and encouragement has a positive impact on improving students’ documentation behavior.[10] Haghani et al.’s study (2014) revealed that multiple factors influence the impact of feedback on overall performance, with learners themselves being the most significant factor.[11] Evidence suggests that the majority of students are dissatisfied with the feedback they receive during clinical training, considering it to be highly ambiguous.[12] Similarly, Ziaee et al.’s study (2015) demonstrated that although feedback is considered an important element in midwifery education, it is not optimally provided, particularly for students in higher years. While instructor empowerment can influence students’ perceptions of feedback adequacy, it is insufficient on its own.[13] This is because, even if trainers are capable of providing feedback, it will be fruitless if learners are incapable of receiving and utilizing it or lack the desire to do so.[9] Additionally, Haghani’s[14] study (2014) revealed that the principles of providing feedback in midwifery education are not consistently followed in terms of content, method, and skills. Considering the aforementioned factors, gaining a deep understanding of midwifery students’ experiences during their internship, especially in specialized and sensitive departments such as labor and delivery, can inform the formulation and implementation of effective educational feedback. This study aims to explore midwifery students’ experiences of feedback at the bedside in the labor and delivery wards in 2022.

Material and Methods

Study design and setting

The present qualitative study used content analysis to examine midwifery students at North Khorasan University of Medical Sciences from March to October2022.

Study participants and sampling

The participants were selected through purposive sampling, and in-depth and semi-structured interviews were conducted with 18 individuals using open-ended questions. Sampling continued until data saturation was achieved. The inclusion criteria involved having communication and interview skills and being in the 6th semester or above. To ensure maximum diversity, participants were selected from a wide range of ages and academic semesters.[678]

Data collection tools and technique

Data were collected through in-depth interviews. Initially, the researcher identified participants through targeted visits to various hospitals where the students were interning. Eligible midwifery students were invited to participate in the study. Once they agreed to participate, written informed consent was obtained. The time and location of the interviews were determined based on the participants’ preferences. Before conducting the interviews, the purpose of the study and the interview methodology were explained. The researcher also reassured them about their voluntary participation and the confidentiality of their information. The interviews took place in a private room at the internship hospitals or dormitories at a prearranged time, and a tape recorder was used to record the interviews, accommodating the participants’ preferences. The interviews began with open-ended questions, such as “Describe your experiences of how the professor provided feedback during the management of natural childbirth. Have you encountered any positive or negative reactions from the professor or others during childbirth management?” The interviews lasted between 45 and 90 minutes and continued until no new data emerged, eliminating the need for additional interviews. The interviews were recorded in a Microsoft Word file, and OneNote software was used for data analysis. The inductive analysis method proposed by Graneheim was applied, and the codes were subsequently organized into subclasses based on their similarities. Finally, several similar classes were grouped into final classes. To ensure the reliability of the data, this study employed the four criteria suggested by Lincoln and Guba. These criteria included conducting in-depth interviews at different times and locations, ensuring maximum diversity in terms of age and academic semester, engaging in peer review, and leveraging the experiences of the research team for data validation. To establish verifiability, the text of several interviews, codes, subcategories, and extracted categories were shared with research colleagues and experts in the fields of obstetrics and reproductive health who were familiar with qualitative research analysis but did not participate in the study.[15] They were asked to evaluate the accuracy of the data coding process. Transferability was ensured by sharing the extracted codes with three qualified midwifery students who were not involved in the study. Their assessments of the similarity between the study results and their own experiences were examined to achieve appropriate agreement.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the ethics committee of North Khorasan University of Medical Sciences approved this study under the code of ethics IR.NKUMS.REC.1401.104. Participants were thoroughly informed about the study’s purpose and methodology and provided written consent. They were reassured that their information would be kept confidential and that the audio files of their interviews would be securely stored. Furthermore, they were given the choice to withdraw from the study at any time.

Subcategories, categories, main categories

Constructive feedback

One of the main categories extracted was constructive feedback. The students said that feedback is important. It helps them gain confidence in delivery. The professor’s scientific and practical qualification and sufficient experience played an important role in receiving feedback for the students, so that one of the participants said about the professor’s practical training during childbirth:

“Maybe I can fill in a few more delivery statistics, but the training that I received during childbirth was included in it. They tell me that I will never forget stress and busyness, and this is important. Trainings during childbirth are very important and stronger” (participant number seven).

The teacher’s personality is effective at giving feedback. One of the participants said:

“my teacher’s presence and the way he spoke to Mom was good and excellent. I did not expect this good behavior from the teacher. I expected more.” Because there is a stressful situation, he should come quickly and only be stressed and convey this to his mother, but thank God it was the other way around” (Participant No. 12).

Paying attention to the students’ characteristics was necessary. It helped the professor give appropriate feedback. Participant number 3 mentioned that:

“once one of the children came with our group.” We even made fun of her giving birth, but the professor encounter with him very patiently.

The professor’s moral character was important in midwifery students. As participant number 2 said,

“But my professor did not argue and behaved gently and said that you will learn.”

The time and place for feedback by the professor was very important; the participants said that impacted their experience of natural childbirth. For example, participant number 1 said,

“the professor should tell us our problems in the next birth. They should show us our mistake.”

The participants thought that the teacher’s mere presence can cause a positive experience. They also thought it could cause positive feedback. One of them said,

“I kept thinking that I am not alone, the teacher is with me. I calmed down and tried not to think about it until the time came.” (Participant No. 15).

Finally, positive feedback from patients and midwives in the delivery department creates good experiences for students. In this case, one of the participants mentioned,

“One shift I was at night, even though my shift was over, I stayed at the hospital until 10 am until my patient gave birth. He did not see me as a student. He used to say that if they polled me, I would say that you were the best midwife, I love you very much” (interview number nine).

Destructive feedback

Another main class extracted was destructive feedback. Destructive feedback can also affect students’ experiences in the delivery department.

The participants were unhappy that they received inappropriate feedback from the professor during delivery. One of the participants mentioned “

… but in general, during childbirth, we children feel that they are stressed and if we do a small thing wrong, they fight a lot. That’s why I was very stressed. So that I don’t do something wrong. “(Participant No. 15).

Participant number 16 stated that

the teacher’s inappropriate behavior was also very important from view point of student’s because it made them feel disappointed in themselves when they looked at them in a certain way for not doing something correctly or not knowing.”

Another destructive feedback was the negative feedback of the people around me in the maternity ward. In this case, one participant said,

…. “But I am saddened by the ward staff’s feedback. I feel that my teacher did not teach me this, that I do not know. The staff I know as the second person who pointed out my mistake” (Participant number 6).

Discussion

This qualitative study aimed to understand the experiences of midwifery students regarding feedback from professors in the labor and delivery department. The participants in this study mentioned the presence of both constructive and destructive feedback in this sensitive and specialized area. These feedbacks from clinical professors can have an impact on the learners. They stated that the professor’s competence, personality, attention to the students’ characteristics, appropriate feedback, physical presence of the professor, and positive feedback from others in the environment all have a positive and effective influence on the learning of midwifery students in the clinical setting, especially in the labor department. In other words, constructive feedback provides information and constructive criticism about performance, which helps improve the quality of midwifery care. Providing constructive feedback in the field of midwifery enhances the quality of care, improves practical skills and self-efficacy of midwifery students, and fosters better communication and collaboration between students and professors. In other words, constructive feedback helps midwives and midwifery students identify their strengths and weaknesses in the process of providing midwifery care. By receiving positive and negative feedback, they can enhance the quality of their services and gain a better understanding of patients’ problems and needs. Additionally, constructive feedback helps midwifery students improve their practical skills, gain more self-confidence, and provide midwifery services with greater assurance. While negative feedback from surrounding individuals, inappropriate feedback during childbirth, and unprofessional behavior from professors can lead to further issues and problems, it is important to address these concerns and find appropriate solutions. Negative feedback from others can range from unpleasant comments to harsh and unnecessary criticism. This type of feedback can significantly impact a student’s self-confidence and morale. Inappropriate behavior from professors may include disrespect, humiliation, insults, or a lack of support towards students. Such behavior can have detrimental effects on students’ self-confidence and academic performance. In line with this, a study by Monadi Ziyarat (2015) demonstrated that providing feedback can enhance cognitive learning and the development of clinical skills among nursing students.[12] Therefore, clinical professors play a crucial role in the education of students in the clinical setting. Factors related to the professor, such as their personality, communication skills, and academic and practical qualifications, are particularly important. The results of Aliafsari-Mamaghani’s study (2019) indicated that communication skills of instructors sometimes have a greater impact than their practical skills and theoretical knowledge on the clinical learning of students. Clinical educators have a fundamental role in fostering cooperation among those involved in the clinical education of students. When clinical instructors possess strong communication skills, students can benefit more from their clinical experiences and have a smoother and more effective learning process within the hospital.[16] Additionally, Ziaei’s study (2016) revealed that 72.5% of students received regular feedback, with most feedback being provided during procedures (75.83%), on an individual basis (60%), verbally (81.66%), and with detailed explanations (61.7%). Qualitatively, students rated the delivery and usefulness of their feedback with an average score of 2.6 ± 0.5 and 2.7 ± 0.75, respectively, on a four-point Likert scale. Most students selected the “sometimes” option when answering qualitative items. In all three areas investigated, third-year students rated their received feedback more positively compared to fourth-year students.[13]

Limitation and recommendation

One of the strengths of our research was the qualitative design and data collection through semi-structured interviews. The qualitative approach allowed us to collect data more comprehensively and in-depth on the factors influencing the clinical feedback of midwifery students in the delivery room. However, one limitation of this study was the small number of midwifery students involved in managing natural childbirth in the presence of a professor. This was due to the low rate of low-risk natural births and the potential occurrence of emergency events during the third and fourth stages of labor, such as bleeding or dystocia. As a result, the students’ experiences and the feedback they received from the teacher, staff, and others may differ. While this study provides valuable insights into midwifery students’ feedback in the delivery room, it is important to note that the findings are limited to a small sample of Iranian midwifery students. Although a small sample is acceptable for an in-depth exploratory study, the results cannot be generalized.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the results of this study revealed that constructive feedback included five subcategories: teacher competence, teacher personality, attention to students’ personality traits, appropriate feedback, and physical presence of the teacher. On the other hand, destructive feedback included three subcategories: negative feedback from others, inappropriate feedback, and the teacher’s inappropriate behavior during childbirth, as perceived by the student. Understanding students’ experiences with feedback from professors can provide a platform for professors to pay special attention to them during clinical education, which can positively impact midwifery students’ performance and perspective.
