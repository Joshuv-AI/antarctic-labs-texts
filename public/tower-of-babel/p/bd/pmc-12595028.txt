Soil physicochemical status under Eucalyptus globulus plantations in the Northwestern highlands of Ethiopia
Authors: Rahel Zegeye, Kassie Getnet, Feven Azene Tebekew, Aminat Seid Ahmed, Parshotam Datt Sharma, Tuma Ayele Yadda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the highlands of Ethiopia, croplands and grazing lands have been converted into Eucalyptus globulus (E. globulus) plantations to meet demand for construction materials, fuelwood and higher income sources. However, the impact of these plantations on soil physicochemical properties remains debated among researchers. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the status of soil physicochemical properties under the E. globulus plantations in the northwestern highlands of Ethiopia. A total of 30 composite soil samples were collected from two soil depths (0–20 and 20–40 cm) across three (E. globulus plantation, cropland and grazing land) land use types with five replications each. Changes in soil properties under different land uses and soil depths were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance in SAS 9.4 software. Soil properties such as moisture content, organic matter and total nitrogen were significantly higher (≤ 0.05) in E. globulus plantations compared to cropland and grazing land. While low content of clay, pH, available phosphorus, exchangeable bases (Ca, Mg, K, and Na) and cation exchange capacity were recorded under E. globulus plantations than cropland. Therefore, the findings of this study would have an invaluable role in providing information for policy-makers on sustainable land use management, particularly to reduce the expansion of E. globulus plantations on fertile soils and mitigating the acidity of soil in the study area.Keywords: E. globulus plantation, Land use types, Soil depth, Soil physicochemical propertiesSubject terms: Biogeochemistry, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Eucalyptus plantation was introduced to East African countries during the second half of the 19th century to rehabilitate degraded lands and to alleviate shortages of firewood and building materials1. It has spread throughout the country as a crucial component of farming systems due to their high demand for construction purposes, fuelwood, and income generation1–6. Hence, a substantial portion of productive cropland has been converted into eucalyptus plantation woodlots in the highlands of Ethiopia7–11. Eucalyptus plantation rapidly increased from time to time due to its fast-growing nature3,6,12. Its area coverage was about 148,000 ha in the year of 2000 and increased to 506,000 ha by 200913. Of the total planted area, 58% was covered by community plantations14. However, the higher nutritional requirements of the fast-growing eucalyptus plantation were most likely accelerating the chemical degradation of the soil15,16. It significantly reduces the readily available nutrients and water reserves in the watershed as a result of its efficient water abstraction ability due to a deep and dense root network17,18. Furthermore, soil hydrophobicity was induced by the decomposition of eucalyptus plantation litter, which reduced infiltration and water content5,19. Litterfall and litter decomposition are the major sources of nutrients in the forest ecosystem, but the litter quality of eucalyptus plantations is low12. Eucalyptus plantation leaf litter contains relatively higher phenolic compounds, resulting in a low decomposition and mineralization rate20.

There were concerns about the negative impacts of eucalyptus plantations on the soil properties and environment in the highlands of Ethiopia1,19,21,22. The impacts include soil fertility reduction, loss of water and the disruption of understory plantations due to the allopathic effects that inhibit undergrowth regeneration1,12,19. Studies by McIntosh et al.23 also indicated that soil organic matter (OM) content was lower in eucalyptus plantations compared to native vegetation. Other studies by Zerga et al.24 and Molla et al.9 showed increased soil acidity and decreased base saturation in the eucalyptus tree, which reduce soil fertility and productivity. Conversely, a few studies indicated that eucalyptus plantations have a positive effect on soil fertility and crop productivity25,26. For instance, soil chemical properties such as pH, total nitrogen (TN), available phosphorus (AP), cation exchange capacity (CEC) and OM were higher in eucalyptus plantations than other land uses8,27–29.

The effects of Eucalyptus globulus (E. globulus) plantations on the soil’s physicochemical properties and the environment remain to be debated among researchers due to site-specific factors such as topography, soil type, climate, and management practices19,22,26,30–32. E. globulus plantations are currently being significantly increased by changing cropland and grazing lands in the highlands of Ethiopia3,19. Farmers prefer E. globulus plantations for their growth performance, commercial purpose, and not required management practices compared to other exotic and native species1,3,6. However, these plantations affect the soil’s physicochemical properties9,19. Consequently, several studies8,14,19,22,27,29,31,33,34 have been conducted to investigate the effects of E. globulus plantations on soil physicochemical properties in various regions of Ethiopia. Although the effect of eucalyptus plantations on soil resources is unclear, various studies have produced different conclusions. The decision-makers were challenged to implement effective policies to reduce the spread of E. globulus plantations due to the absence of conclusive quantitative evidence. This suggests that additional scientific investigation is required to fill the evidence gap regarding the effects of this species on soil physicochemical properties. Therefore, the objective of this study was to assess the soil physicochemical status under E. globulus plantations compared with grazing land and cropland in the northwestern highlands of Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Description of the study site

This study was conducted in Wonka Kebele (the lowest administrative unit of Gozamn district), Amhara region, in the northwestern highlands of Ethiopia (Fig. 1). The altitude is 2974 m.a.s.l, and it is located 300 km from Addis Ababa. It is situated between 10° 1′ 46″, 10° 35′12″ N latitudes, 37° 23′ 45″, and 37° 55′ 52″ E longitudes. The study area covers about 2270 ha (Fig. 1).

According to Gozamn District Agriculture and Rural Development Office (GDARDO)35, the annual mean rainfall of the study area is 1628 mm and the rainy season from May to October. The maximum and minimum temperatures are 25 °C and 11 °C, respectively, besides the study area belongs to the midland agroecological zone. The major cultivated crops in the study area are wheat (Triticum aestivum), barley (Hordeum vulgare), potato (Solanum tuberosum), maize (Zea mays), and teff (Eragrostis teff). Nitrogen and phosphorus fertilizers were applied annually to the cropland to enhance soil fertility and yield35. Historically, the area was originally covered by natural forest before it was cleared and converted into cropland for agricultural use. Besides, the dominant plantation species is E. globulus, a fast-growing perennial tree that remains green throughout the year. The conversion from natural forest to cropland and later to eucalyptus plantations. Grazing land use was primarily characterized by the presence of grasses, which provides the necessary forage for livestock, forming the main source of sustenance for grazing animals. Based on the report of GDARDO35, the soil types include Nitisols, Vertisols, Cambisols, Phaeozems, Acrisols, and Leptosols. Nitisols are the dominant soil in the study area.

Site selection and soil sample preparation

A field survey was conducted in the study area in order to identify representative sampling locations that satisfied our objective of investigating how eucalyptus plantations affect the soil’s physicochemical properties. Thus, E. globulus plantations, grazing land, and cropland use types were selected in the study area. A cluster sampling procedure was used to classify different land use types in the study area. The woodlots of the eucalyptus plantation were selected since they constituted the majority of the plantations in the study area. The E. globulus plantation was 25 years old, with tree heights from 15 to 20 m, a tree density of 2000 m2/ha, and a diameter at breast height from 22 to 72 cm. Soil samples were collected in the eucalyptus tree canopy at a distance of 2 m from the tree trunk. This approach was selected to capture the influence of eucalyptus litter fall, root interactions, and shading on soil physicochemical properties. The E. globulus plantation does not apply any cultural treatments like fertilizer, and there is no rotation in the study area. Cropland use includes cultural treatments such as fertilization and crop rotation. Grazing land use served as the control group since it included grasses and no human interventions, but animals grazed it, whereas cropland and E. globulus plantations disturbed by humans. The three land use types were also similar in terms of agroclimatic zone, soil type, topography and slopes. Soil sampling was performed by using a cluster sampling design. A cluster of 100 × 100 m and five cluster centroids, each of 20 × 20 m, were established. Thus, 1 kg of composite soil sample was collected by auguring for disturbed soil samples. A total of 30 composite soil samples (three land use types x two soil depths x five replications) were collected for soil physicochemical analysis. Likewise, 30 undisturbed soil samples were collected by using a core sampler for determining the bulk density of soils. Soil samples in the field were packed in plastic bags, recorded and standardized with a sampling code and transported to a soil laboratory. In addition, the soil samples were crushed and oven-dried at 105 °C for 24 h in the laboratory to determine moisture content. This temperature is sufficient to remove all gravitational and hygroscopic water. Whereas air-drying was used for soil chemical analysis to preserve soil nutrients and chemical properties. For the analysis of the physicochemical properties of the soil, a 2 mm sieve was used, whereas a 0.5 mm sieve was used for analyzing total nitrogen and organic carbon36.

Soil laboratory analysis

Soil particle size distribution was analyzed by using the hydrometer method37, whereas soil bulk density (BD) was determined by using the core sampler38. Besides, soil moisture content (MC) was determined by using the gravimetric method39. The pH of the soil was determined by using a glass electrode pH meter with a 1:2.5 soil and water40. Soil organic carbon (OC) content was determined following a wet digestion method40, and soil organic matter (OM) was obtained by multiplying the OC content by 1.724. In addition, total nitrogen (TN) content was estimated using the Kjeldahl digestion and distillation method41. Available phosphorus (AP) content was determined using the Olsen method42, while cation exchange capacity (CEC) was extracted by using a 1 N ammonium acetate solution at pH 7.043. Besides, calcium (Ca) and magnesium (Mg) contents were determined using an Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometer (AAS), whereas potassium (K) and sodium (Na) were analyzed using a Flame Photometer44.

Statistical analysis

Changes in soil properties under different land use types and soil depths were analyzed by two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) following the General Linear Model (GLM) procedure available in statistical software (SAS) 9.4. This statistical method was selected to analyze the interactions between different land use types and soil depths to determine how each factor affects soil properties. The treatments were arranged in a factorial randomized complete block design (RCBD) format with land use types and soil depths. Before analyzing the data, used SAS to test for the normality of the data distributions. A comparison of the means was performed using a least significant difference (LSD) test at 0.05.

Results and discussion

Effects of E. globulus plantations on soil physical properties

Soil texture

Table 1 showed that the clay content under the E. globulus plantation (64.70%) was lower than in the cropland (71.90%). This could be E. globulus plantation may have an effect on the texture of the soil (clay) due to the high nutrient uptake by the roots. The result agrees with the findings of Molla et al.9 showed that the lower clay content was recorded under plantations of E. globulus than grazing land and croplands in the highlands of Ethiopia. In addition, silt was higher at a 0–20 cm depth (20.2%) than at a 20–40 cm depth (16.6%), but clay was higher at a 20–40 cm depth (73.0%) than at a 0–20 cm depth (67.1%) (Table 1). The higher clay content in the subsurface soil compared to surface soil could be due to illuviation processes by the action of water movement and pedogenic process that migrated from the surface soil to the subsurface soil9. Similar findings have been reported by Eyayu et al.45 and Bitew et al.32 who found the overall mean values of silt and sand to be higher on the surface than in the subsurface, and those of clay to be higher in the subsurface than in the surface layer in northwest Ethiopia. Likewise, Yeshaneh46 and Sonaimuthu et al.47 stated that clay content increased with soil depth and sand content decreased with increased soil depth.

Bulk density, total porosity, and soil moisture content

The lower BD was recorded under E. globulus plantations than cropland and grazing lands (Table 1). The lowest BD in the plantation might be due to reducing raindrop impacts on the soil as well as high content of OM. Furthermore, it could be that the tree’s root exudates reduce soil compaction. While, the highest BD in grazing land use and cropland uses could be due to animal trampling effect and exposure to direct effect of the temperature9. The higher soil BD in croplands were linked to lower OM content and increased soil compaction19. The 0–20 cm soil depth had a significantly lower value of BD (1.08 g cm− 3) compared to the 20–40 cm soil depth (1.34 g cm− 3) (Table 1). The BD was lower on surface soil than in subsurface soil due to the high OM in the surface soils9,32. The higher BD of the soil is declining nutrients, while the lower BD in the soil becomes improved, it is better for plant growth48. Moreover, higher BD can decrease water retention and impede root growth49. In addition, a higher TP was recorded under E. globulus plantations than for cropland use (Table 1). This might be because eucalyptus plantations contain litterfall that increases the OM and TP of the soil. The TP was significantly affected by soil depth (Table 1). The surface soil had a significantly higher TP (59.2%) than the subsurface soil (49.4%) (Table 1). This might be due to the presence of OM in the surface layer of the soil. Because of soil OM that decrease compaction from the surface soil and the decrease soil OM in the subsurface layer, the BD increased with soil depth32,50,51. This was due to the high pore space because of higher OM content and biological activity. The lower BD implies greater pore space and improved aeration and creates a better environment for biological activity47,52. Soil MC was significantly (p < 0.05) affected by land use types and soil depth (Table 1). The higher soil MC (23.6%) was recorded under the E. globulus plantation than grazing land (18.7%) and cropland (20.3%) (Table 1). This might be due to high ground cover that could decrease the soil temperature and increase water retention in the soil. Table 1 shows that the subsurface (20–40 cm) soil has a significantly higher MC (22.7%) than the surface soil (19.8%). The lower soil MC might be due to the higher surface temperature and increased evaporation compared to in the subsurface soil. As reported by Karim et al.53 the soil MC under canopy cover was higher than bare land, which indicated that canopy cover decreased the rate of evaporation and increased the soil water-holding capacity54.

Effects of E. globulus plantations on soil chemical properties

Soil pH

Land use types had a significant effect on soil pH (p < 0.05) as shown in Table 2. A lower soil pH was recorded under E. globulus plantations (4.07) than under cropland (5.08) and grazing land (5.39) (Table 2). Through their root systems, eucalyptus trees can release specific organic acids into the soil and high OM contributed to acidification44. Moreover, the lower soil pH in the eucalyptus plantation was associated with the depletion of basic cations55. Thus, as per the rating of Tadesse et al.56 the soil under the eucalyptus plantation was strongly acidic than other land use types. Other studies showed that the lower soil pH was recorded under E. globulus plantations compared to grazing land and cropland in the central region and highlands of Ethiopia33,57.The relatively lower content of pH under the eucalyptus plantation could lead to higher bioavailability of Fe, Al, and H, which reach toxic levels in plants32,58,59. Besides, the higher soil acidity under the eucalyptus plantation could be due to the higher uptake of basic cations and the release of H ions in the soil. These results was confirmed by the findings of Temesgen et al.34, Läuchli & Grattan60 who found more acidic soil under the eucalyptus plantation than under grazing land. As reported by Nsabimana et al.61 the higher nutrient uptake consumed by eucalyptus plantations and biomass was the main reason for the acidity of the soil under the eucalyptus plantation. Moreover, the higher exchangeable acidity under the eucalyptus plantation contributed to the decomposition of OM, which releases organic acids and intensifies soil acidity62.

Organic matter, total nitrogen, and available phosphorus

The E. globulus plantation had a significantly higher soil OM content (4.87%) compared to cropland (2.80%) and grazing land (4.41%) (Table 2). This could be due to the eucalyptus plantation offers more biomass to be decomposed into the soil. The continuous leaf litterfall and its decomposition, the lack of tillage, as well as the reduced soil erosion were the factors contributing the build-up of OM in the soils. On the contrary, the continuous tillage of cropland enhanced the depletion of OM in the soil. This result was corroborated by the findings of Yitaferu et al.27 and Liang et al.63 who found that a higher OM content was recorded in eucalyptus plantations compared to cropland. Lower soil temperatures under eucalyptus plantations decreasing the rate of decomposition could also be a factor in increasing OM input64. In addition, eucalyptus plantation ages, its litter and roots growth increases the soil OM65,66. The soil OM content increased from the surface (0–20 cm) soil to the subsurface (20–40 cm) (Table 2), due to a excessive source of OM found in the surface soil19,67. Land use types had a significant (p < 0.05) effect on TN and AP (Table 2). A higher TN content was recorded under E. globulus plantations (Table 2). This could be related to the high litterfall and biomass, which increase the amount of TN. Studies by Duguma et al.68 also showed that TN was higher in the soils of eucalyptus woodlots than in grazing land and cropland. The lower TN content under cropland was due to the amounts of N lost through soil erosion27; OM is the source of N and largely governs the distribution of TN found by Karim et al.53 besides E. globulus plantations contain a high amount of OM through litterfall69–71. The capacities of eucalyptus species to extend nutrient cycling in the soil also increase the contents of TN66. Compared to cropland (2.49 mg kg− 1) and grazing land (2.8 mg kg− 1), the E. globulus plantation had a significantly lower content of AP (1.24 mg kg− 1) (Table 2). This could be because AP was fixed in acidic soil under E. globulus plantations as a lower pH was recorded in the study area. The bond of AP with Al and Fe becomes stronger, limiting the availability of AP in the soils32,72–74. Besides, the study was confirmed by Chanie et al.50 who found a very low range of AP recorded in a eucalyptus woodlot than other land use types.

Exchangeable bases, percent base saturation and cation exchange capacity

Table 3 shows that land use types and soil depth had a significant (p < 0.05) effect on exchangeable bases (Ca, Mg, K, and Na), percent base saturation (PBS) and CEC. Exchangeable bases were lower in E. globulus plantations compared to cropland and grazing land (Table 3). The fast-growing nature of E. globulus plantations largely absorbs basic cations and releases hydrogen ions9,75. The findings of Leite et al.15 who found lower concentrations of Ca, Mg, Na, and K in E. globulus plantations than in cropland verified this result. Likewise, the plantation of eucalyptus had lower exchangeable cations compared to other land use types8,19. Studies by Chen et al.76, Berthrong et al.77, Mekonnen et al.26 also stated that basic cations were lower in eucalyptus plantations than in cropland, which largely absorbs basic soil nutrients. Moreover, CEC and PBS were lower under E. globulus plantations than cropland and grazing lands (Table 3). The lower CEC and PBS under E. globulus plantations could be due to the lower clay and basic cation recorded in the current study. Similar studies have indicated a lower CEC and PBS were recorded under E. globulus plantations compared with the soil from cropland and grazing land30. As per ratings by Hazelton & Murphy78 the CEC was lower under E. globulus plantations than under cropland and grazing land, while PBS and basic cations were very low under eucalyptus plantations.

Study limitations and future directions

While the study provides valuable insights into the impacts of E. globulus plantations on soil physicochemical properties, it has some limitations. The analysis was based on a single-season sampling approach, which may not reflect seasonal variability in soil conditions. Moreover, potential site history effects were not considered, which could have influenced soil characteristics independently of land use types. Lastly, biological and microbiological soil indicators were not included, though they play a crucial role in assessing overall soil health. Future research should incorporate seasonal soil sampling methods, historical land use records and soil biological indicators to offer a more comprehensive and dynamic understanding of eucalyptus plantation effects over time. Long-term experiments would also help evaluate aggregate impacts on soil health and ecosystem functioning.

Conclusions

This study explores the effects of Eucalyptus globulus (E. globulus) plantations compared to grazing and croplands on soil physicochemical properties, and shows significant implications for sustainable land management decisions. Hence, the results revealed that E. globulus plantations reduce basic cations and release organic acids into the soil. Thus, soil pH, exchangeable bases (Ca, Mg, K, and Na) and CEC declined, while improving acidic cations than grazing and croplands. Moreover, clay, bulk density, pH, CEC, moisture content and percent base saturation increased at a soil depth of 20–40 cm, while sand content, organic matter, available phosphorus and total nitrogen decreased at a depth of 0–20 cm in all land use types. Hence, the findings of this study provide invaluable insights for policy-makers on sustainable land use management, with particular emphasis on site selection for plantations, limiting the expansion of E. globulus on fertile lands and mitigating soil acidity in the study area.
