RNase L regulates the antiviral proteome by accelerating mRNA decay, inhibiting nuclear mRNA export, and repressing transcription
Authors: J Monty Watkins, Cameron J Douglas, Renee Cusic, Ciaran P Seath, James M Burke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYRibonuclease L (RNase L) is an antiviral endoribonuclease that triggers widespread degradation of cellular mRNAs. Here, we show that RNase L-mediated decay of cellular mRNA is a conserved response to flaviviruses, including Zika virus (ZIKV), dengue virus serotype 2 (DENV-2), and West Nile virus (WNV). Quantitative mass spectrometry shows that RNase L downregulates proteins with short half-lives involved in cell-cycle progression, cellular metabolism, and protein synthesis. However, mRNAs encoding antiviral proteins that are induced by interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) initially evade degradation by RNase L, allowing for the synthesis of critical antiviral proteins. At later times during the antiviral response, RNase L dampens ISG protein synthesis by triggering a block in nuclear mRNA export and repressing transcription. These findings show that RNase L regulates the antiviral proteome through an intricate balance of mRNA decay and nuclear RNA biogenesis, which is crucial for preventing overproduction of pro-inflammatory proteins.

INTRODUCTION

Ribonuclease L (RNase L) is a latent endoribonuclease constitutively expressed in most human cell types.1 RNase L activates upon condensation of oligoadenylate synthetase 3 (OAS3) on viral double-stranded RNA (dsRNA), which initiates the synthesis of pppA2′p5′A2′p5′A (2-5A).2–5 2-5A binds to the N-terminal ankyrin repeat domain (ARD) of RNase L, which allows for complementation of its C-terminal RNase domain via homodimerization.6 RNase L cleaves single-stranded RNA (ssRNA) at UN ^N motifs in viral and host RNAs,7,8 which antagonizes the replication of numerous and diverse viruses.9–14 RNase L has been widely thought to suppress viral replication by cleaving viral RNA, cleaving ribosomes to arrest translation, and/or generating RNA fragments that induce the type I interferon (IFN) response, autophagy, and the inflammasome.15–18

More recently, RNase L was shown to trigger widespread degradation of cellular mRNA.19–21 The loss of cellular mRNA was shown to be the primary means by which RNase L reduces bulk translation, as RNase L-cleaved ribosomes were shown to be translationally competent.20 Importantly, this allows for the translation of mRNAs that encode proteins critical for the antiviral response, such as type I IFNs (i.e., IFN-beta; IFN-B1), which evade RNase L-mediated mRNA decay.19,22–24 In addition, the rapid degradation of cytosolic mRNA by RNase L causes RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) to translocate from the cytosol to the nucleus, which coincides with defects in nuclear RNA processing and inhibition of nuclear-cytoplasmic mRNA export.25 The inhibition of nuclear mRNA export occurs at later times post-activation of RNase L and reduces type I and type III IFN production.23 How RNase L-mediated mRNA decay and its secondary effects broadly alter the proteome during the antiviral response has not been investigated.

In this article, we characterize how RNase L alters the cellular proteome by quantitative mass spectrometry (MS) following dsRNA lipofection. These analyses show that RNase L downregulates the homeostatic cellular proteome over the course of the innate antiviral response by accelerating mRNA decay, inhibiting export, and repressing transcription. The mRNAs encoded by interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) are induced to high levels during the initial phase of the RNase L response due to their ability to evade RNase L-mediated mRNA decay, thus allowing for the synthesis of these key antiviral proteins. However, RNase L-mediated inhibition of nuclear mRNA export and repression of RNAPII dampen ISG protein production over the course of the antiviral response. These findings establish how RNase L alters the cellular proteome to regulate cellular processes and inflammation in response to viral infection.

RESULTS

RNase L degrades mRNAs during viral infections and has minimal impact on apoptosis

The activation of RNase L in response to lipofection of poly(I:C), a synthetic viral dsRNA mimic, initiates widespread decay of cellular mRNA.19,20 Whether this occurs in response to viral infection, and what the frequency of this response is in individual cells, has not been thoroughly characterized. Therefore, we infected parental (wild-type [WT]) or RNase L-knockout (RL-KO) A549 cells with dengue virus serotype 2 (DENV-2), Zika virus (ZIKV), or West Nile virus (WNV) and assessed human GAPDH mRNA and total poly(A)+ RNA cellular mRNA levels by single-molecule RNA in situ hybridization (smRNA-FISH). We also co-stained for viral RNA.

We observed an RNase L-dependent reduction in cytosolic GAPDH mRNA levels (~100-fold) in the majority of A549-WT cells (66%–81%) infected with DENV-2, ZIKV, or WNV, similar to poly(I:C) lipofection (Figures 1A and 1B). The loss of GAPDH mRNA corresponded to a reduction in cytosolic poly(A)+ RNA levels (Figures 1A–1C). These data indicate that flaviviruses frequently trigger RNase L-mediated decay of cellular mRNA. Remarkably, viral RNA was relatively abundant in cells that activated RNase L-mediated mRNA decay, though RNase L generally reduced viral RNA loads (Figures 1A–1D). This suggests that RNase L preferentially targets cellular mRNA and/or that viral RNAs evade, either directly or in effect, RNase L-mediated RNA decay.

Cells remain viable following RNase L-mediated decay of cellular mRNA

RNase L has been shown to initiate apoptosis.15,26,27 Therefore, we sought to determine how long cells remain viable after initiating RNase L-mediated mRNA decay. To examine this, we took advantage of the fact that the initiation of RNase L-mediated mRNA decay causes poly(A)-binding protein C1 (PABPC1) to assemble into RNase L-induced bodies (RLBs) and translocate to the nucleus.28 Therefore, we performed live-cell imaging of PABPC1 tagged with mRuby2 (mRuby2-PABPC1) following either poly(I:C) lipofection or ZIKV infection (Figures 2A–2C).

As expected, PABPC1 assembled into RLBs and translocated from the cytosol to the nucleus following lipofection of poly(I:C) in WT cells (Figures 2A and 2B).28 In contrast, RL-KO cells formed cytosolic PABPC1-positive stress granules as the result of protein kinase R (PKR) activation (Figures 2A and 2B). We observed that most WT cells (>90%) initiated RNase L-dependent PABPC1 translocation by 2 h post-lipofection of poly(I:C) (Figures 2B–2D), with these cells completing PABPC1 translocation by 3 h post-activation (Figures 2B–2E). Thus, RNase L completes degradation of cellular mRNA in less than 3 h post-activation, consistent with previous estimates that RNase L completes degradation of cellular mRNAs within an hour.19,29 Remarkably, many of these cells lived for several hours after completing RNase L-mediated decay of cellular mRNA (Figures 2B–2E), indicating that RNase L activation is not immediately lethal to cells.

Similar analyses following ZIKV infection showed that RNase L-dependent translocation of PABPC1 from the cytosol to the nucleus initiated between 18 and 24 h post-infection (p.i.) (Figures 2C and 2D). The rate of PABPC1 translocation to the nucleus following RNase L activation, as determined by RLB assembly, in response to ZIKV was similar to the rate observed in response to poly(I:C), whereby PABPC1 completed translocation within ~3 h (Figure 2E). These data indicate that RNase L activates in individual ZIKV-infected cells between 18 and 24 h p.i. and degrades nearly all cellular RNA within 3 h of activation. Nevertheless, ZIKV-infected cells remained viable for several hours following RNase L activation, with some cells remaining viable over 24 h post-activation of RNase L (Figure 2C).

We next examined cell viability and apoptotic signaling in WT and RL-KO cells following either poly(I:C) lipofection or ZIKV infection. We performed live-cell imaging of cells expressing mRuby-2-PABPC1 and quantified cell death by apoptotic cell morphology (implosion of the nucleus) and the activation of caspase-3/7 using a fluorescent reporter (Figures 3A, 3B, S1, and S2). We observed similar rates of cell death and caspase-3/7 activation between WT and RL-KO cells in response to poly(I:C) or ZIKV (Figures 3C and 3D). Notably, the decrease in viable cells coincided with an increase in caspase-3/7 activity, suggesting that cell death is initiated by apoptotic signaling in response to dsRNA and ZIKV. Importantly, in cells that activated RNase L, apoptotic signaling did not initiate for several hours (~12 h) following RNase L activation (Figure 3E; Video S1). Moreover, caspase-3/7 activity typically initiated simultaneously (±1 h) with cell implosion in WT and RL-KO cells (Figure 3E; Videos S1 and S2). Combined, these data show that RNase L activation does not initiate apoptotic signaling or cell death during the antiviral response.

RNase L broadly alters the cellular proteome during the innate immune response

Because most cells remain viable following RNase L-mediated mRNA decay, we sought to examine how RNase L-mediated mRNA decay broadly remodels the proteome over the course of the innate immune response. To do this, we performed quantitative MS in WT and RL-KO A549 cells under mock conditions or following poly(I:C) lipofection (8 or 16 h). We chose to analyze cells at 8 h as most cells have initiated both the RNase L and the RLR-MAVS-IRF3 pathways.23 We chose 16 h post-poly(I:C) because this is when type I and type III IFN secretion peaks and cell viability begins to decline (Figure 3C).23 Across all treatments, we observed 6,933 unique proteins. We did not observe substantial differences between the cellular proteome in WT and RL-KO cells under mock conditions (Figure S3A), and we observed only minor alterations to the proteome at 8 h post-lipofection of poly(I:C), with little overlap with the 16-h time point (Figures S3B and S3C).

At 16 h post-lipofection of poly(I:C), we observed substantial differences in the proteome between WT and RL-KO cells (Figure 4A). We conducted two-sample t tests (false discovery rate [FDR] < 0.05) of WT and RL-KO cells to identify proteins that were significantly up- or downregulated in an RNase L-dependent manner at 16 h post-lipofection of poly(I:C) (Figure 4B; Table S2). As expected, ISG proteins (i.e., ISG15, IFIT1, MX1, and DDX58) were significantly upregulated in both WT and RL-KO cells following poly(I:C) lipofection. We observed 447 proteins significantly downregulated in an RNase L-dependent manner and 274 proteins upregulated in an RNase L-dependent manner (Figure 4C; Table S2).

We considered the possibility that the downregulation of proteins by RNase L is due to them having short half-lives, thus leading to their more rapid reduction following the degradation of cellular mRNAs by RNase L. Indeed, cross-referencing the downregulated proteins with a published database of protein half-lives30 showed that proteins with short half-lives were downregulated by RNase L in comparison to proteins with long half-lives, which were largely unaffected by RNase L (Figure 4D). These data suggest that loss of protein synthesis plays a significant role in the function of RNase L, whereby the arrest of protein synthesis via mRNA decay broadly downregulates the cellular proteome, which is correlated with the inherent stability of specific proteins rather than the activation of a specific protein degradation mechanism.

We performed Gene Ontology (GO) analyses on proteins up- or downregulated in WT and RL-KO cells (Figure 4E). Both cell lines downregulated proteins involved in cell cycle and upregulated proteins involved in innate immunity to viral infection. Notable processes enriched among RNase L-downregulated proteins involved regulation of cellular stress and ribosome biogenesis. A particularly important finding was that RNase L selectively regulated transcriptional activity by TRRUST analysis.31 The activity of several transcription factors, including STAT1, MYC, AR, and CREB5, was downregulated by RNase L, whereas proteins controlled by NFKB1, NFKBIA, SP1, IRF1, and RELA were upregulated in both WT and RL-KO cells (Figure 4F).

RNase L dampens the production of proteins encoded by ISGs

Although the activation of RNase L results in the degradation of nearly all cellular mRNAs, the mRNAs induced by immediate-early genes (i.e., IFN-B1) have been shown to accumulate to abundant levels, which is critical for the synthesis of key antiviral proteins.19,20 However, it is currently unknown whether the mRNAs encoded by ISGs similarly evade RNase L-mediated mRNA decay and how RNase L broadly alters ISG protein synthesis.

RNase L downregulates ISG proteins

To investigate how RNase L-mediated mRNA decay affects induction of ISG proteins, we plotted the differential (mock vs. pI:C) between WT and RL-KO cells at 8 and 16 h post-lipofection of poly(I:C) (Figure 5A). We observed an increase (2- to 32-fold) in a modest number of proteins involved in antiviral defense in both WT and/or RL-KO cells, such as GBP1, IFIT3, IFIT1, ISG15, OASL, IFNL1, and IFNL3, at 8 h post-lipofection of poly(I:C) (Figures 5A and 5B). At 16 h post-lipofection of poly(I:C), both WT and RL-KO cells induced many antiviral proteins, including IFITs, MX1, ISG15, OASL, GBP1, ISG20, OAS2, IFIH1 (MDA-5), and DDX58 (Rig-I) (Figures 5A and 5B).

Importantly, nearly all antiviral proteins were higher in RL-KO cells in comparison to WT cells at both 8 and 16 h post-lipofection of poly(I:C), though the difference was more pronounced at 16 h (Figures 5C, S4A, and S4B). Western blot analyses corroborated our MS data, showing that GBP1, IFIH1, IFIT1, IFIT2, MX1, OAS2, OASL, and PPP1R15A (GADD34) increased in both WT and RL-KO cells following lipofection of poly(I:C), yet they were all lower in WT cells in comparison to RL-KO cells (Figure 5D). Notably, we did not readily detect some antiviral proteins (GBP1, IFITs, MX1, and OAS2) at 8 h post-lipofection of poly(I:C) in either WT or RL-KO cells (Figure 5D), consistent with their lower levels and lack of detection in our MS analyses.

ISG-encoding mRNAs evade RNase L-mediated mRNA decay and are translated

Over 90% of A549 cells activate RNase L-mediated mRNA decay by 6 h post-lipofection of poly(I:C) (Figures 2B–2D). However, based on our analyses, as well as previous analyses of type I/III IFN secretion,23 antiviral protein synthesis occurs between 8 and 16 h. Therefore, we wanted to determine if the mRNAs encoding ISGs evade RNase L-mediated mRNA decay. To assess this, we lipofected WT or RL-KO cells with poly(I:C) for 8 h and performed smRNA-FISH for the mRNAs encoding IFIT2, ISG15, OASL, or MX1 and GAPDH mRNA.

In mock-treated WT and RL-KO cells, the mRNAs encoding IFIT2, ISG15, OASL, or MX1 were poorly expressed (<10 copies), with most cells containing no detectable mRNAs (Figure 5E). However, these mRNAs were induced in cells following lipofection of poly(I:C), with individual cells expressing between 10 and 1,000 copies. Importantly, WT cells that lacked GAPDH mRNA contained abundant levels of these ISG-encoding mRNAs. Average IFIT2 and ISG15 mRNA levels were comparable between WT and RL-KO cells (Figures 5E and 5F), whereas MX1 and OASL mRNA levels were lower, on average, in WT cells in comparison to RL-KO cells. These data indicate that the mRNAs encoding ISGs variably evade RNase L-mediated mRNA decay, either directly or in effect.

To confirm that the protein synthesis of ISGs occurs in cells with activated RNase L, we performed smRNA-FISH for OASL and GAPDH mRNAs and co-stained for OASL protein by immunofluorescence assay (IFA). Consistent with our MS and western blot analyses, we did not observe OASL protein in cells under mock conditions (Figure S5A). However, at 8 h post-lipofection of poly(I:C), we observed high OASL protein levels in many WT cells (Figure 5G). Importantly, most of these cells contained high levels of OASL mRNA while lacking GAPDH mRNA (Figures 5G and S5B). On average, OASL protein levels were only slightly lower (~2-fold) in WT cells in comparison to RL-KO cells (Figure 5H), which was comparable to the 2-fold lower OASL mRNA levels in WT cells. Notably, OASL mRNA and protein levels were positively correlated in both WT and RL-KO cells (Figure S5C). Similar results were obtained for IFIT2 (Figure S5D). These data demonstrate that ISG mRNAs effectively evade RNase L-mediated mRNA decay, which is sufficient for their translation.

RNase L-dependent nuclear retention of ISG mRNAs dampens ISG protein synthesis

We next addressed how RNase L limits ISG protein production at late times (16 h) post-lipofection of poly(I:C). Previous studies showed that RNase L triggers a block in canonical nuclear export of IFNL1 mRNA between 8 and 16 h post-lipofection of poly(I:C), resulting in a reduction in IFNL1 protein synthesis. Therefore, we examined if ISG proteins are reduced due to the retention of their mRNAs in the nucleus.

We observed that some ISG-encoding mRNAs (IFIT2, OAS3, and OASL) are retained in the nucleus at 16 h post-lipofection of poly(I:C), whereby the average levels of these mRNAs in the nucleus of WT cells were 5-fold higher in comparison to RL-KO cells (Figure 6A). However, we did not observe an increase in the mean nuclear levels of many ISG mRNAs (IFIT1, IFIT3, IFIT5, ISG15, and OAS2). This suggests that these mRNAs are not frequently or robustly retained in the nucleus (Figure 6A). Consistent with this notion, WT cells co-stained for ISG15 and OASL mRNAs displayed nuclear retention of OASL mRNA but not ISG15 mRNA (Figure S6A). These data suggest that RNase L differentially affects mRNA export of mRNAs encoded by ISGs, as previously observed for IFNL1 and IFNB1 mRNAs.23

Among the group of mRNAs that did not display a mean increase in nuclear levels (IFIT1, IFIT3, IFIT5, and MX1), we did observe a subset of cells that retained high levels of these mRNAs in the nucleus, which was not observed in RL-KO cells (Figure 6A). However, many WT cells also displayed low nuclear levels of these mRNAs, particularly IFIT1 and IFIT3, in comparison to RL-KO cells. These data suggest that RNase L activation can inhibit the export of these mRNAs from the nucleus, but that an additional RNase L-dependent mechanism might simultaneously act to reduce nuclear mRNA levels (further addressed in the following section).

To determine if the nuclear mRNA export reduced protein levels at 16 h post-lipofection of poly(I:C), we performed co-IFA for OASL mRNA and IFA for OASL protein (Figure 6B). These analyses demonstrated that WT cells contained significantly lower OASL protein than RL-KO cells (Figures 6B and 6C), and cells displaying nuclear retention of OASL mRNA contained the lowest OASL protein levels (Figures 6B–6D). We observed similar results for both IFIT2 and IFIT3, whereby nuclear retention of their mRNAs correlated to a reduction in their protein levels (Figures S6B–S6G). Notably, IFIT3 expression was low in both RL-KO cells and WT cells maintaining an export block. The low levels of IFIT3 in RL-KO cells may be explained by the condensed mRNAs we observe, which is beyond the scope of this paper (Figures S6B–S6D). These data support that RNase L-mediated inhibition of nuclear export dampens the protein synthesis of ISGs at later times post-poly(I:C).

RNase L activation leads to the repression of transcription

Our data show that total ISG mRNA levels are reduced at late times (16 h) post-lipofection of poly(I:C) in an RNase L-dependent manner, due in part to accelerated mRNA decay and nuclear mRNA export inhibition. However, we considered the possibility that RNase L could trigger a reduction in RNAPII-mediated transcription. The rationale for this hypothesis is that the translocation of RBPs from the cytosol to the nucleus in response to MHV-68 SOX (muSOX)-mediated mRNA decay inhibits RNAPII-mediated transcription.32 Moreover, we observed a reduction in many ISG proteins despite the lack of their encoding mRNAs being retained in the nucleus. Last, many WT cells, but not RL-KO cells, displayed a reduction in nuclear IFIT1 and IFIT3 mRNAs (Figure 6A), suggesting that RNase L could limit transcription of these mRNAs. Therefore, we examined if RNase L activation reduces RNAPII-mediated transcription and whether this would affect the transcriptional induction of immediate-early antiviral genes or ISGs.

To test whether RNase L activation could repress transcription, we measured transcriptional activity in WT and RL-KO cells by pulsing them with 5-ethynyl uridine (EU). We quantified EU incorporation by fluorescence microscopy using Alexa Fluor 488 (EU-click-488). We also performed IFA for PABPC1 as a marker for RNase L activation, as PABPC1 translocates to the nucleus upon RNase L activation. In both WT and RL-KO cells, incorporation of EU (EU+) resulted in intense nuclear fluorescence upon click chemistry of EU-click-488 in comparison to cells lacking EU (EU−), and treatment with actinomycin D reduced EU-click-488 staining (Figures 7A, 7B, and S7A). These data demonstrate that EU-click-488 fluorescence is dependent on active transcription.

In WT cells, we observed a progressive reduction in EU staining following the lipofection of poly(I:C) (Figures 7A–7C). At 4 and 8 h, EU staining was reduced by 25% and 50%, respectively. By 12 h, it was reduced by greater than 60%. This reduction was RNase L dependent, as EU staining was significantly higher in RL-KO cells (Figures 7A–7C). Moreover, EU staining in WT cells containing nuclear PABPC1 (RNase L active) was lower than in WT cells displaying cytosolic PABPC1 (RNase L inactive) (Figures S7A and S7B). These data support the notion that RNase L activation correlates with a reduction in transcription, particularly at late times post-dsRNA stress (12 h). We note that RL-KO cells displayed a reduction (~25%) in EU staining throughout the time course (Figures 7A–7C), suggesting that an additional innate immune response to dsRNA represses transcription. Notably, these cells tended to contain PABPC1-positive stress granules (Figures S7B and S7D), suggesting that the PKR pathway, the integrated stress response (ISR), and/or stress granules also repress transcription.

ISGs are induced despite repression of RNAPII transcription at early times

Three observations indicate that antiviral genes—immediate-early genes, such as type I IFNs and ISGs—are induced despite global repression of transcription mediated by RNase L. First, our data showed that cells with activated RNase L contain ISG-encoding mRNAs (Figure 5E). Second, IFIT2 mRNA was abundant in cells that activated RNase L (reduced cytosolic poly(A)+ RNA) and displayed transcriptional repression (reduced EU) at 8 h post-lipofection of poly(I:C) (Figures 7D and S8). Importantly, we typically observed 2–3 nuclear structures that intensely stained (~10-fold higher than individual mRNA molecules) with IFIT2 probes (Figures 5D and S9A). These foci co-stained with probes targeting the IFIT2 intron and 3′ end down-stream of gene (DOG) RNAs (Figures S9B and S9C), indicating that these are nascent IFIT2 pre-mRNAs being transcribed and processed at the IFIT2 genomic locus, consistent with previous studies characterizing IFN-B1 and IFN-λ gene induction.19 Thus, the presence of these IFIT2 transcriptional foci in cells with reduced EU staining suggests that the IFIT2 gene is undergoing active transcription despite bulk repression of transcription. Notably, the reduction of nuclear EU staining by RNase L is not due to the degradation of nuclear RNA, as poly(A)+ RNA was not reduced in the nucleus in cells that activated RNase L. This is consistent with previous studies showing that RNase L is localized to the cytosol and does not degrade nuclear RNA.21,25,33 Third, consistent with the notion that IFIT2 mRNA is transcribed despite repression of global transcription, metaanalyses of RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) from WT and RL-KO 6 h post-lipofection of poly(I:C) showed that mRNAs encoding antiviral proteins, including IFIT2 as well as IFN-B1, IFNL1, RSAD2, and OASL, are induced to comparable levels in WT and RL-KO cells (Figure 7E).19 These data show that ISGs can be transcribed, at least initially, in cells that have initiated RNase L-dependent repression of global transcription.

RNase L-mediated repression of RNAPII dampens ISG mRNA production at late times

We considered the possibility that RNase L-dependent repression of RNAPII-mediated transcription could eventually shut off ISGs and that this could contribute to the broad reduction in ISG protein synthesis. Two observations support this hypothesis. First, we observed a reduction in total ISG15 mRNA from 8 to 16 h poly(I:C) in WT, but not RL-KO, cells (Figure 7F). Second, we assessed transcriptional output in cells by measuring the integrated density of nascent transcripts at the genomic locus of ISG15. Typically, cells stained for ISG15 mRNA by smRNA-FISH contained two intense foci in the nucleus (Figures 7G and S9A). These foci were not observed in mock-treated cells (Figure S10A), consistent with the ISG15 gene being transcriptionally silent under homeostatic conditions, and their intensity reduced following actinomycin D treatment (Figure S10B). Thus, these data indicate that these foci represent nascent ISG15 transcripts at the ISG15 genomic loci, similar to our above observations of IFIT2 (Figures S9A–S9C). We term these “ISG15 transcription foci.” Importantly, the intensity of ISG15 transcription foci reduced from 8 to 16 h post-lipofection of poly(I:C) in WT cells (Figure 7G). The reduction in the ISG15 transcription focus intensity was more pronounced in WT cells than in RL-KO cells, though RL-KO cells also displayed a marked reduction in transcription focus intensity, consistent with the observation that cells can also reduce transcription in an RNase L-independent manner in response to poly(I:C) (Figures 7A–7C). Overall, these data support that RNase L promotes transcriptional silencing of ISGs at late times during the innate antiviral response.

DISCUSSION

In this article, we investigated how RNase L alters the cellular proteome during the innate antiviral response. Combined with previous studies,19,20,25 our data support a model whereby RNase L activation results in the rapid decay of cellular mRNA, which eventually leads to a block in mRNA export and repression of transcription (Figure 7H). These processes serve as a molecular timer that shuts down cellular protein synthesis in a hierarchical manner, whereby translation of basally expressed mRNAs is immediately arrested via their rapid decay, altering homeostatic functions. Simultaneously, transcriptionally induced mRNAs encoding antiviral proteins evade the effects of RNase L-mediated degradation and are consequently translated. However, subsequent inhibition of nuclear mRNA export and repression of transcription mediated by RNase L reduces antiviral protein synthesis, providing a crucial regulatory mechanism that can dampen potentially damaging inflammation.

Our data solidify the idea that RNase L-mediated degradation of cellular mRNA is a frequent response to viruses that trigger RNase L activation, such as flaviviruses (Figures 1A–1C). By monitoring PABPC1 localization using live-cell imaging, we observed that the activation of RNase L is delayed until 18–24 h p.i. with ZIKV (Figures 2C and 2D). However, once activated, RNase L degrades cellular mRNA to completion within individual cells within ~3 h (Figure 2E), resulting in most cells lacking basally expressed mRNAs by 24 h p.i., consistent with previous observations.34,35 Remarkably, most of these cells survive for an additional 24 h. Moreover, RNase L-KO cells initiated apoptotic signaling and died at a rate comparable to that of cells that activated RNase L in response to ZIKV (Figures 3A–3D). Combined, these data demonstrate that neither the activation of RNase L nor the degradation of cellular mRNA is immediately cytotoxic to cells. Because these observations contrast with many studies proposing that RNase L initiates apoptosis signaling cascades,24,27 we will further investigate stress response and apoptotic pathways initiated by RNase L activation in additional biologically relevant contexts.

Our proteomic analyses demonstrate that RNase L-mediated mRNA decay leads to the downregulation of over 447 proteins (Figures 4A–4C). Importantly, these data revealed that RNase L also represses antiviral protein synthesis, particularly at late times post-lipofection of poly(I:C) (Figures 5A–5H). This is an important finding because RNase L is typically thought to promote the type I IFN response by generating cleavage fragments that activate Rig-I-mediated MAVS signaling,16 though subsequent studies demonstrated that cells with intact or robust RNase L responses dampen the protein synthesis of type I IFNs.36 Combined with our previous studies and data herein, we propose that RNase L shuts down cellular processes in a stepwise manner that differentially alters the ability of cells to synthesize antiviral proteins. During the early phase of the RNase L response, ISG-encoded mRNAs can be transcribed, exported to the cytosol, and translated, as demonstrated for OASL, IFIT2, and IFIT3 (Figures 5E–5H and S6B–S6E). Thus, similar to mRNAs encoded by immediate-early genes (IFN-B1),19 ISG-encoding mRNAs evade RNase L-mediated mRNA decay, in effect, as RNase L cannot greatly increase the decay rate of these inherently unstable and highly induced mRNAs. During the mid-phase of the RNase L response, nuclear export of some ISG mRNAs is inhibited in an RNase L-dependent manner (Figure 6A), which represses their translation (Figures 6B and 6C). During the late phase of the RNase L response, RNase L represses RNAPII-mediated transcription (Figures 7A–7C). While ISGs are initially transcribed despite this broad reduction in RNAPII-mediated transcription (Figures 7D and 7E), the transcription of ISGs reduces at late time post-lipofection of poly(I:C) (Figures 7F and 7G). Thus, if ISG mRNAs are not induced before RNase L arrests mRNA export and RNAPII-mediated transcription, their ability to be translated is nearly abolished via multiple RNase L-mediated shutoff mechanisms (Figures 6C and 6D). These observations reveal that the activation kinetics of the RNase L response relative to the type I IFN response likely determines how strongly RNase L represses the ISG response. Moreover, this could potentially be context dependent, as viruses differentially trigger and/or antagonize these pathways. Future work will focus on understanding the relative kinetics of these pathways in response to diverse viruses and how this impacts the ISG response.

Our data herein support a mechanism by which RNase L dampens host gene expression, which is the repression of transcription (Figures 7A–7C). Consistent with our findings, Mao et al. showed that RNase L limits RNAPII transcription and occupancy across the genome.37 The ability of RNase L-mediated mRNA decay in the cytosol to regulate nuclear RNA biology is not unprecedented, as it was previously shown to repress nuclear mRNA export and alter nuclear RNA processing.23,25 Thus, mass decay of cytosolic mRNAs signals the cell, via yet-to-be-determined mechanisms, to repress host gene expression via inhibition of RNAPII-mediated transcription, perturbation of nuclear RNA processing, and inhibition of nuclear mRNA export. This function likely serves to reinforce the ability of RNase L to suppress the homoeostatic proteome and autoregulate inflammatory responses. It is notable that, despite bulk reduction in RNAPII-mediated transcription at 8 h post-lipofection of poly(I:C), ISGs such as IFIT2 were being actively transcribed based on the presence of abundant mRNA and nascent mRNAs at the genomic locus (Figure 7D). These observations suggest that the repression of RNAPII-mediated transcription by RNase L is hierarchical with respect to the kinetics by which genes are transcriptionally silenced, whereby constitutively expressed genes are preferentially repressed while stress-response genes maintain induction during the initial response. Based on studies of mu-SOX-mediated mRNA decay,32,38,39 we suspect that the influx of RBPs into the nucleus interferes with RNAPII-mediated transcription and/or RNA processing.23 An alternate model is that the downregulation of transcription factors by RNase L is responsible for the decrease in transcription (Figure 4F). Future work will aim to address the mechanism of RNase L-mediated repression of RNAPII and how this impacts viral and host gene expression.

Last, we identified hundreds of proteins that are differentially regulated in an RNase L-dependent manner during the innate antiviral response (Figures 4B and 4C). Many proteins were downregulated by RNase L due to their inherently short half-life (Figure 4D), and they function in regulating homeostatic activities such as cell division and protein synthesis (Figure 4E), thus providing another means by which RNase L shuts down cellular functions. In addition, RNase L regulated proteins that have context-specific antiviral activities, such as cell-mediated cytotoxic immune responses, complement system, barrier immunity, and immune checkpoints. Future studies will aim to identify the key factors regulated by RNase L that impact antiviral immunity and tumor suppression in biologically relevant contexts.

Limitations of the study

A small fraction of the cells with activated RNase L undergo apoptosis over the course of poly(I:C) lipofection, which could reduce the magnitude of effect that RNase L has on protein levels at the 16-h time point.

STAR★METHODS

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Cell culture

A549 cells were cultured in DMEM +10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) + penicillin/streptomycin (P/S). Passaging used 0.25% trypsin. Cells were cultured at 37°C with 5% CO2. Knockout cell lines were verified by western blot. Cells were tested for mycoplasma via regular microscopy with Dapi counterstain.

Generation and quantification of viral stock

Viral stocks were produced in Vero cells. Once the cells were >80% confluent, cells were infected at 0.1 MOI. Supernatants were harvested at either 48 or 72 h. After collecting the supernatant, dead cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 500g for 5 min. The remaining media was syringe filtered using a 0.45 μM filter.

METHOD DETAILS

Western blots

Cell lysates were harvested at the indicated time points post-infection by trypsinization. Lysates were resuspended in water, then mixed with 2x SDS lysis buffer, 1x BME, and heated to 95°C for 10 min. Samples were resolved on Bolt Bis-Tris Plus Mini Protein Gels, 4-12% (Invitrogen, NW04120BOX) and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween(TBST) and then probed with the indicated primary antibody overnight at 4°C. Blots were washed 3x and probed with the designated secondary antibody for 4 h at 4°C. Blots were visualized using SuperSignal West Femto Chemiluminescent Substrate.

Viral infections

Cells were plated 1 day before infection and grown to 60-80% confluency. Viruses were added in serum-free media at indicated MOIs. After 1 h, media was removed, cells were washed 1x with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and fresh media was added.

smFISH probe labeling

The smFISH probes were labeled with Atto-488, Atto-550, or Atto-633 using 5-Propargylamino-ddUTP-ATTO-488 (Axxora: JBS-NU-1619-488), 5-Propargylamino-ddUTP - ATTO-550 (Axxora: JBS-NU-1619-550), or 5-Propargylamino-ddUTP-Atto633 (Axxora: JBS-NU-1619-633). Oligos were labeled by adding 16 μM oligos, 2 μL of 10 mM ddNTPs, 1x TDT buffer, and 1.5 μL of terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (Thermo Fisher Scientific: EP0161) in a 50 μL total reaction volume. Oligos were then incubated overnight at 37°C and precipitated with 400 μL ethanol and 50 μL 3M Sodium acetate, pH 3.2. Oligos were then incubated at −20°C overnight and centrifuged at 20,000xg for 30 min. The supernatant was then aspirated, and the pellet was washed twice with 75% ethanol. Pellet was allowed to dry, then resuspended into 80 μL RNase free water. Oligo d(T)30-Cy3 were purchased from IDT.

Microscopy

Cells were plated onto glass coverslips in 12-well plates, then virally infected. After the indicated time of infection, the media was removed, cells were washed 1x in DPBS and then fixed in 500 μL 4% paraformaldehyde for 12 min. To permeabilize the cells, paraformaldehyde was then removed, and 1 mL 75% ethanol was added. Cells were then stored at 4°C for at least 2 h before beginning staining protocol. For dual staining of immunofluorescence and smFISH, cells were washed 2x in PBS, then incubated in 500 μL primary antibody in PBS for 4 h at 4°C. Cells were washed 2x in PBS, then secondary antibodies were added for 2 h at 4°C. Cells were washed 2x, then fixed in 4% pfa for 10 min. Cells were washed 3x in PBS, then washed in buffer A (filter-sterilized 2x SSC with 10% formamide) for 5 min. smFISH probes were then added to a hybridization chamber containing a wet paper towel with parafilm on top. 50 μL of smFISH probes diluted 1:100 in hybridization buffer (0.45 μm filtered 10% dextran sulfate, 10% formamide, 1x nuclease-free SSC, diluted in nuclease-free water) were dropped onto the parafilm. Glass slips were then flipped onto the smFISH probes. Hybridization chambers were sealed with parafilm and incubated overnight at 37°C. The next day, slips were washed 2x in Buffer A, then once in 2x SSC. Slips were then mounted on slides with Vectashield + DAPI (VectorLabs: H-1000-10) and dried. Images were taken on a Nikon Eclipse Ti2 with a 100x, 1.45 NA oil objective or a 20x air objective.

EU-click-488

Cells were plated onto glass coverslips in 12-well plates, then virally infected or transfected at indicated timepoints. A 2 mM working solution of EU (Thermofisher: C10329) was prepared and added to the cells 1 h prior to fixation. Cells were washed 1x in DPBS, and then fixed in 500 μL 4% paraformaldehyde for 12 min. To permeabilize the cells, paraformaldehyde was then removed, and 1 mL 75% ethanol was added. Cells were then stored at 4°C for at least 2 h before beginning staining protocol. 500 μL of Click-iT® reaction cocktail was added to the cells for 30 min at room temperature. Cells were then washed and mounted on slides for imaging.

smFISH quantification

smFISH dots were quantified using FIJI (ImageJ) on maximum intensity projections of z-stacks. Cells were circled manually to create ROIs. Intensity thresholds were set to quantify single mRNA, and particles between sizes 0-2 were counted using the Analyze Particle function. The step-by-step guide for smFISH quantification is explained in Cusic et al.29

Live-cell imaging

Cells were plated onto glass 96-well plates at 20,000 cells per well 24 h prior to transfection or infection. 100x stock solution of caspase-3/7 detection reagent (Thermofisher: C10423) was added to wells 30 min prior to initiation of imaging. Cells were then imaged Nikon Eclipse Ti2 20x air objective. Quantification of cell viability was done manually. For each biological replicate, a single field of view with approximately 50 cells was manually analyzed. At each timepoint, the number and percentage of healthy cells was quantified. For caspase-3/7 quantification, the percentage of cells with nuclear fluorescent signal was quantified.

SP3 peptide cleanup

Frozen cell pellets were lysed with SDS lysis buffer: 50 mM HEPES pH 8.5, 1% SDS, protease inhibitor, and 500 U/mL Pierce Universal Nuclease (ThermoFisher #88700). Samples were homogenized with 20 strokes of a Dounce homogenizer and then incubated at 4°C for 30 min. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 20,000 rcf for 10 min and the supernatant was transferred to a new tube. Samples were normalized to 1 mg/ml. 20 μg of protein from each sample was prepared for LC/MS analysis using Sera-Mag Carboxylate SpeedBeads (Cytiva Life Sciences, Cat. #45152105050250 and Cat. #65152105050250) following the manufacturer protocol. In brief, samples were reduced and alkylated with 10 mM DTT and 22.5 mM iodoacetamide. Samples were loaded onto Sera-Mag Carboxylate SpeedBeads at 1:20 protein:bead ration. 100% Ethanol was used to bind protein to bead, followed by 80% Ethanol washes. Proteins were digested at 37°C overnight with Pierce Trypsin Protease MS-Grade (Thermo Scientific #90057). Trypsinized peptides were then cleaned up with Sera-Mag Carboxylate SpeedBeads, using acetonitrile to bind and subsequently wash samples. Peptides were eluted with 2% DMSO in water and sonicated for 1 min. Eluted peptides were split into two technical replicates per sample and submitted to The Herbert Wertheim UF Scripps Institute for Biomedical Innovation and Technology’s mass spectrometry and proteomics core and run on a Bruker timsTOF Pro2.

Mass spectrometry data analysis

Peptide digests were acidified with TFA to 0.1% (v:v) and desalted using 2 μg capacity ZipTips (Millipore, Billerica, MA) according to manufacturer instructions. Following drying under vacuum, peptides were re-solubilized in 20 mL of 0.1% formic acid (FA) to a final concentration of 100 ng/μL. Samples were analyzed on a nanoElute (plug-in V2.1.60.0; Bruker, Germany) coupled to a Bruker TimsTOF Pro 2 mass spectrometer (Breman, Germany), equipped with a CaptiveSpray source and a 20 μm zero dead volume (ZDV) Sprayer. Peptides (corresponding to 400 ng) were separated on a reverse-phase C18 column (10 cm X 75 μm X 1.9 μm, Bruker PepSep Ten Series). The column temperature was maintained at 50°C using an integrated Bruker Column Toaster (Bremen, Germany). The column was equilibrated using 4 column volumes before loading samples in 100% buffer A (99.9% Fisher Optima® LC/MS water, 0.1% FA), with both steps performed at 800 bar. Samples were separated at 500 nL/min using a linear gradient from 3% to 30% buffer B (99.9% Fisher Optima® LC/MS acetonitrile, 0.1% FA) over 17.90 min before ramping to 95% buffer B (0.5 min) and sustained at 95% buffer B for 2.4 min (total separation method time 20.7 min). The Bruker TimsTOF Pro 2 was operated in DIA-PASEF mode using Tims Control v. 5.0.2. Settings for the MS method were as follows: Mass Range 100 to 1700m/z, 1/K0 Start 0.6 V.·/cm2 End 1.4 V·s/cm2, TIMS Ramp and accumulation time 75ms, Capillary Voltage 1700V, Dry Gas 3 l/min, Dry Temp 200°C, DIA-PASEF settings: 18 MS/MS scans (50m/z windons, 0.21 1/K0 windows, total cycle time 0.74), mass range 300 to 1200, and CID collision energy 20eV (at 0.60, 1/K0) to 65eV (at 1.60, 1/K0). The analysis was performed at The Herbert Wertheim UF Scripps Institute for Biomedical Innovation & Technology, Mass Spectrometry and Proteomics Core Facility (RRID:SCR_023576). Data processing information is included in the quantification and statistical analysis section.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Statistical analyses

p values were derived using Student’s t test (Excel). *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001 unless otherwise noted in the figure legend. Statistical tests used, values of n, and representation of n can be found in individual figure legends.

Mass spectrometry quantification

Data were processed via DIANN 1.8.1. Parameters set as follows: trypsin/P digestion, 3 missed cleavages, 3 max. variable modifications, N-term M excision, Ox(M), Ac(N-term) and C carbamidomethylation. Peptide length range was 7-30, precursor charge range 1-4, m/z range 300-1800, and fragment ion range 200-1800. Mass accuracy and MS accuracy were both set to 10. The following settings on the algorithm were checked: “Use isotopologues”, “MBR”, “No shared spectra”, “Heuristic protein inference”. Precursor FDR was set to 1%. A spectral library was used generated via DIANN from all known human proteins (In-Silico specral library). Resulting matrix.pg file was opened in Perseus (v2.0.7.0). Intensities were inputted as “main”, the rest of the descriptors are categorical. Data were then transformed (Log2). Data were annotated as by sample, grouping all replicates together. Missing values were imputed with Perseus default settings. Normalization was performed via median subtraction. Following this process, a volcano plot was generated utilizing a t test for statistical significance. The resulting volcano plots were plotted in GraphPad Prism 10 for final figures. Gene Ontology was conducted using Metascape, and the data were plotted using ggplot in R. TRRUST analysis was conducted using Metascape.
