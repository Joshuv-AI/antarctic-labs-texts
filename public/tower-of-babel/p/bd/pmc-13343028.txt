Incidence and predictors of immediate postoperative haemodynamic instability among adult surgical patients in Ethiopia, 2024: a multicentre prospective observational study
Authors: Getachew Mekete Deress, Abdurehman Seid Mohammed, Shimelis Seid Tegegne, Habtie Bantider Wubet, Temesgen Birlie Asmare, Kumlachew Geta Belete, Negesse Zurbachew Gobezie, Kaletsidk Dessalegn Mossie, Temesgen Dessie Mengistu, Worknew Ebabu Mengistu, Denberu Eshetie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to find the incidence and predictors of postoperative haemodynamic instability among adult surgical patients in the postanaesthesia care unit (PACU).Study designA multicentre prospective observational study was conducted from 1 June to 30 September 2024.SettingThe study was conducted at three hospitals in the South Gondar Zone, north-west Ethiopia.ParticipantsThe study included 404 elective surgical patients undergoing surgery who were in the PACU. All adult patients who were fit, stable and underwent elective surgery under anaesthesia and were admitted to the PACU for monitoring and stabilisation were included in the study. Patients who were directly admitted to an intensive care unit or ward after an operation and those who refused to give consent were excluded from the study.Outcome measuresThe study investigates the incidence and predictors of immediate postoperative haemodynamic instability in adult surgical patients.ResultThe incidence of postoperative haemodynamic instability in the PACU was 47.8% (95% CI 43.1% to 52.5%). Multivariable logistic regression analysis shows that American Society of Anesthesiologists class III patients (adjusted OR (AOR): 3.04, 95% CI 1.48 to 6.25), patients with preoperative anxiety (AOR: 2.9, 95% CI 1.73 to 4.78), preoperative use of beta-blocker medication (AOR: 1.9, 95% CI 1.11 to 3.32), patients who experienced intraoperative haemodynamic instability (AOR: 1.8, 95% CI 1.2 to 3.14) and patients with a procedure lasting more than 4 hours (AOR: 3.7, 95% CI 3.26 to 9.67) were significantly associated with haemodynamic instability.ConclusionThe incidence of postoperative haemodynamic instability in this study was high. Our findings highlight the urgent need for hospital quality improvement initiatives and healthcare professionals assigned to the PACU should monitor the patients continuously. We also recommend that future researchers monitor the patients for 24 hours.Keywords: Patient-Centered Care, Adult anaesthesia, Adult surgery

Introduction

Surgery plays a crucial role in healthcare across the globe, greatly aiding in the prevention, diagnosis and treatment of diverse medical conditions.1 Annually, 230 million non-cardiac surgeries are undertaken worldwide, with 500 000–900 000 of these patients experiencing major postoperative complications after surgery.2 3

Haemodynamic stability refers to the condition in which the circulatory system operates effectively to maintain sufficient blood flow and pressure, delivering oxygen and nutrients to the body’s tissues and organs.4 5 Maintaining haemodynamic stability is vital for overall health and represents a critical goal in various medical situations, especially for acutely ill or injured patients.6

Haemodynamic instability (HDI) occurs when the circulatory system fails to maintain adequate blood flow, blood pressure and oxygen delivery to the body’s tissues and organs.7 This indicates a disruption in the normal balance and regulation of the cardiovascular system. It is a serious condition that can lead to organ damage and even death if not properly addressed.8 9

Induction of anaesthesia is generally considered safe, largely due to advancements in patient monitoring and pharmaceuticals during the last decades.10 11 Despite significant advances in perioperative anaesthesia, complications in the postanaesthesia care unit (PACU) remain common.12 Complications in the PACU generally significantly contribute to increased morbidity and mortality of the patients.13

It is an independent risk factor for long-term patient morbidity and mortality. Patients with severe haemodynamic events had more than 4-hour stays in the PACU.14 Untreated acute postoperative HDI triggers a dangerous cascade of events that can lead to organ damage and severe complications. Minimising these adverse consequences requires early identification, appropriate treatment and continuous monitoring of vital signs.15

In patients admitted to PACU, postoperative complications are common and approximately represent 4.25%–37.3% of all cases, with severity ranging from moderate to catastrophic.16 17 The most frequently encountered PACU complications were respiratory, cardiovascular, hypothermia, pain, postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) and central nervous system‐related adverse events.12 The prevalence of cardiovascular complications in the postoperative period was 81%.18 Hypotension, hypertension (HTN), bradycardia and tachycardia are common incidents in the PACU. It is more commonly associated with anaesthetic drugs, intraoperative blood loss or postoperative HDI.19 20

The incidence of HDI in the postanaesthesia care unit was 21.1%–56.5%.16 In a study conducted in Libya, the incidence of HTN, hypotension, tachycardia and bradycardia was 12%, 8%, 25% and 1%, respectively.21 In a study conducted in Gondar, the incidence of HDI was 59.47% of patients who had developed HDI in the PACU. About 27.34%, 21.82%, 13.67% and 15.35% of patients had experienced tachycardia, bradycardia, hypotension and HTN, respectively, in the PACU.22 These data suggest that there are significant regional differences in the incidence of HDI in the PACU, with Gondar reporting higher rates across several parameters compared with Libya. These differences could be influenced by various factors, including patient demographics, surgical procedures, anaesthesia techniques and postoperative care practices. Due to this incidence variation, we need to explore the underlying causes of these discrepancies and to identify strategies for improving haemodynamic stability in the PACU across different settings.

The determining factors affecting haemodynamic stability in the PACU are anaesthesia type, operation type, age, history of medication, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status and chronic disease. Anaesthetics, different medications, intraoperative blood loss, surgeon expertise and urgency of the case are common factors that affect haemodynamic stability in the PACU.2 18 23

While existing studies on PACUs in Ethiopia have contributed valuable insights, they often suffer from significant limitations that hinder the generalisability of their findings. Many of these studies are single-centre designs, which restrict their ability to capture the variability in staffing levels, equipment availability and patient outcomes across different healthcare settings within the country. Additionally, a lack of prospective data limits the understanding of how real-time factors influence PACU performance and patient safety. In contrast, the current multicentre, prospective study design aims to address these gaps by encompassing a broader range of PACU contexts across multiple facilities, thereby providing a more comprehensive and representative analysis of the challenges and disparities faced in Ethiopian PACUs. This approach not only enhances the validity of the findings but also facilitates the identification of best practices that can be implemented to improve patient care in this critical setting.

Methods

Study area, study design and study period

A multicentre prospective observational study was conducted at hospitals in the South Gondar Zone, Ethiopia, from 1 June to 30 September 2024. According to Ethiopia’s 2021 statistical report, the South Gondar Zone has a total population of 2 631 566, with 1 331 888 males and 1 299 678 females. The zone is served by eight government hospitals, including seven primary hospitals and one comprehensive specialised hospital. These hospitals are Nefas Mewucha Primary Hospital (NMPH), Wogeda Primary Hospital, Ebinat Primary Hospital, Mekane Eyesus Primary Hospital, Andabet Primary Hospital (APH), Debre Tabor Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (DTCSH), Dr. Ambachew Memorial Hospital and Addis Zemen Primary Hospital. For this study, three institutions (DTCSH, AZPH and NMPH) were selected. These hospitals have operating rooms that provide both elective and emergency services, offering diagnosis and treatment for a variety of procedures. Before collecting the data, the study was registered on Research Registry, using the unique registration number researchregistry11636.

Source and study population

The source population included all adult surgical patients aged 18 and older who underwent elective surgery at the study hospital. The study population consisted of adult patients aged 18 and older who underwent elective surgery and met the inclusion criteria during the study period.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All adult (≥18 years) patients who underwent elective surgery under anaesthesia and were admitted to the PACU for monitoring and stabilisation were included in the study. Patients who were directly admitted to an intensive care unit or ward after an operation and those who refused to give consent were excluded from the study.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

The sample size was determined using a single population proportion formula, considering the following assumptions: a 5% marginal error, a 95% CI and the incidence of haemodynamic events in the PACU reported at the University of Gondar, which was 59.47%,22 and considering a 10% non-response rate. Finally, the sample size would be 407. The study participants were selected with a non-probability consecutive sampling technique from patients waiting for surgical interventions under anaesthesia.

Operational definitions

HDI (change) is a life-threatening condition in which the circulatory system fails to deliver sufficient blood flow and oxygen to the body. It is characterised by abnormal BP, HR and tissue perfusion. If a patient has at least one physiological parameter that changes from baseline, including systemic HTN, hypotension, tachycardia or bradycardia alone or in combination, then the patient has experienced HDI.22 23 7

ASA physical status classification: developed by the ASA task force, which classifies patients according to their physical status (systemic well-being).24

ASA class I: normal healthy patient except for the surgical complaint he had.

ASA class II: a patient with a mild systemic disease without substantive functional limitation.

ASA class III: a patient with a severe systemic disease with substantive functional limitations.

ASA class IV: a patient with severe systemic disease that constantly threatens life.

ASA class V: the moribund patient who is not expected to survive without the operation.

Baseline values of vital signs: these refer to the initial set of measurements of a patient’s essential physiological parameters, taken when they are in a relatively stable state and before any specific intervention or event occurs that might influence those parameters.25

HTN: defined as an increase in mean arterial pressure (MAP) by 20% from the baseline MAP or systolic arterial pressure (SAP) ≥140 mm Hg for longer than 5 min.19 25

Hypotension: defined as a decrease in MAP by 20% from the baseline MAP or MAP<60 mm Hg and SAP<90 mm Hg for longer than 5 min.25 26

Tachycardia: an increase in HR, ≥20% from baseline value or HR≥100 for adults for longer than 5 min.15 17 25 27

Bradycardia: a decrease in HR, ≥20% from the baseline value, HR<60 beats per minute for adults for longer than 5 min.15 25 27

Respondents with State-Trait Anxiety Involuntary Scale (STAIS) scores of 44 or greater were considered anxious preoperatively.28

Respondents with an STAIS score of less than 44 were considered not anxious preoperatively.28

A variance inflation factor (VIF) value less than 5 is considered acceptable, while values between 5 and 10 indicate moderate multicollinearity and values greater than 10 suggest problematic multicollinearity.29

Data collection tools and procedures

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire and checklist in English. It was taken from previous studies after a careful review of the literature5 7 8 15 17 28 and translated into Amharic. The data collection procedure included a chart review, a patient interview using a structured questionnaire and direct observation of the patient’s vital signs on the monitoring. The data included in the questionnaire encompassed sociodemographic information, clinical patient characteristics and various surgical and anaesthetic variables. Preoperative data for patients include age, sex, BMI, nil per oral (NPO) hours, medical history and medication usage history. During the intraoperative period, BP, HR, temperature and oxygen saturation were recorded. Anaesthetic and surgical factors, including surgery urgency, surgical duration, type of anaesthesia, estimated blood loss during the procedure, timing of the surgery, intraoperative fluid administration and HDI during the procedure, were documented in a questionnaire. At admission to the PACU postoperatively, haemodynamic variables (HR, systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP)) were measured using a non-invasive monitoring device. Peripheral oxygen saturation was also monitored. Pulse oximeters: devices that measure blood oxygen levels and pulse rate without needing to draw blood. Glucometer: devices that monitor glucose levels in the blood. Thermometers: devices that measure body temperature. ECG monitors: devices that are a quick, painless, non-invasive test that records the electrical signals of the heart using electrodes placed on the skin. BP monitors: non-invasive devices like the Omron series that measure BP without needing to insert anything into the body. Postoperative complications, including pain, PONV, respiratory adverse events and agitation, were assessed and documented. The patient’s chart was reviewed to collect demographic, anaesthesia and surgical-related data. The assessment of the patient’s haemodynamic status began within the first 5 min of arrival at the PACU. Data on haemodynamic variables (HR, BP) were collected every 10 min of the first hour of the PACU stay and every 15 min of the second hour of the PACU stay as per PACU monitoring standards.18 26

Data quality assurance

To ensure data quality, a pretest of the questionnaire was conducted with 37 patients (10% of the sample size) from hospitals not involved in the main study. This aimed to assess the accuracy, completeness and clarity of the information. Necessary corrections will be based on feedback from the questionnaire for the main study. Data collectors received training and orientation regarding the study’s objectives and relevance, along with clarifications on each item included in the study. The completeness and consistency of the collected data were regularly monitored, and any incomplete data were excluded. During data collection, each question was reviewed by the investigator to ensure it was complete and appropriate. It was internally consistent and checked by Cronbach’s alpha coefficient (α=0.82).

Data entry, analysis and interpretation

The collected data were coded and entered into EpiData software (V.7) for error correction before analysis using SPSS V.26. Descriptive statistics were conducted and presented with frequency, percentage, median, mean and SD. The association between independent and dependent variables was determined at a 95% CI using χ2 tests, along with bivariate and multivariate binary logistic regression, and presented as crude and adjusted ORs. To assess the relationship between the different predictor variables and the dependent variables, initial bivariate relationships between each independent variable and outcome were examined using a binary logistic regression model. Independent variables with a p value <0.2 at this stage were included in a multivariable logistic regression model to control for potential confounding factors. After adjusting for their effect on the outcome, variables with a p value <0.05 within a 95% CI were considered significant determinants. Multicollinearity among selected independent variables was checked with the VIF test, which was acceptable (VIF=1.87).

Patient and public involvement

No patients or the public were directly involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Result

A total of 404 study participants were involved in this research, achieving a response rate of 99.3%. The majority of participants were female (267, 66.09%). The median age of the participants (IQR) was 45 years (23–80), while the mean age was 46.21 years, with an SD of 13.5. Most participants, 210 (52%), were classified as ASA I. The median NPO hours (IQR) for the participants was 12 (9–14) hours. Regarding medical history, 45 (11.14%) participants had a history of cardiovascular-related diseases, and 74 (18.31%) had a history of respiratory-related diseases (table 1).

Discussion

According to this study, the incidence of HDI among elective surgical patients in the PACU was 193 (47.8%). Approximately 148 (36.63%), 98 (24.26%), 76 (18.81%) and 63 (15.59%) of patients experienced HTN, tachycardia, bradycardia and hypotension, respectively. This issue may be related to the low staff-to-patient ratio in the PACU, which hampers early identification of HDIs and timely patient stabilisation. This challenge arises from limited resources. Similar to other developing countries, our PACU experiences a high patient flow, making it difficult to organise the unit according to established protocols. Distinguishing critically ill patients who require close monitoring from stable ones is often challenging. Consequently, both stable and unstable surgical patients tend to have prolonged stays in the PACU. Prolonged exposure to the high-stress environment of the PACU increases the risk of HDIs, even among stable surgical patients.

This study closely aligns with the research conducted by Wolde et al, which reported an incidence of HDI at 53.8%.7 In contrast, studies conducted in the UK (58%)15 showed a higher incidence of postoperative HDI compared with the findings of this study. This variation may arise from the frequent use of intra-arterial invasive monitoring, which is more effective at detecting HDI in the PACU. However, because of the limited availability of invasive tracking and arterial catheters in our study area, we were unable to evaluate invasive monitoring for beat-to-beat BP measurement.

Higher ASA classes correlate with an increased risk of postoperative HDI due to underlying comorbidities, reduced physiological reserve, increased surgical stress, anaesthetic sensitivity, challenges in fluid management and the need for intensified monitoring. In this study, patients who were in ASA class III were 3.04 times more likely to develop HDI in the PACU than patients in ASA class I. This result was in line with the research conducted by Abebe et al,22 Wolde et al7 and Meyer et al.30 It is a fact that the ASA physical status classification system provides valuable insights into patients’ overall health status and perioperative risk, with higher ASA classifications correlating with increased complication rates. This might be attributable to the fact that ASA class III patients have more comorbidities.

Preoperative anxiety is an inherited response characterised by feelings of tension, apprehension, nervousness, fear, discomfort and high autonomic activity to unforeseen and eventually disgusting conditions of the preoperative period.31 It is common for patients to become anxious before surgery, but the extent of anxiety levels varies from patient to patient and fluctuates over time, starting before the surgery and persisting until the postoperative period.32 This research finding revealed that preoperative anxiety was found to be significantly associated with HDI in the PACU. Patients who had preoperative anxiety were 2.9 times more likely to develop HDI in the PACU than patients who did not have preoperative anxiety. This result was in line with the study done by Kim et al in 2010 in Korea.33 The study done by Raocharernporn et al in 2017 in Bangkok.34 The study done by Farbood et al in 2020 in Iran.35 The study done by Bayrak et al in 2019.36 A study done by Ahmetovic-Djug et al in 2017.37 Unlike our study, research by Goulart et al in Brazil on how anxiety affects BP and HR after surgery found no statistically significant differences in HR, SBP and DBP based on anxiety levels.38 This possible reason may be that increased morbidity among anxious patients is associated with the development of cardiovascular lesions caused by health-related behaviours and direct effects on myocardial perfusion, due to the release of stress hormones such as cortisol and epinephrine. This physiological response can cause increased HR, elevated BP and changes in vascular resistance, all of which may contribute to HDI during the postoperative period. Additionally, anxious patients may have increased muscle tension and altered respiratory patterns, further complicating their recovery. Psychological factors, such as fear of surgery or anticipation of pain, can also exacerbate physiological responses, leading to greater instability in the PACU. Overall, the interplay between psychological stress and physiological responses can significantly impact haemodynamic stability in patients with preoperative anxiety.

The use of beta-blockers in the preoperative period was a strong predictor of postoperative HDI in our study. Similarly, studies done in Libya21 and the UK15 showed that the patients who were on beta-blockers preoperatively were a predictor of postoperative bradycardia, which results in postoperative HDI. This possible reason could stem from the use of beta-blockers before surgery, which reduces cardiac sensitivity to haemodynamic changes that may lead to HDI or compromise the patient’s stress response mechanism to adverse events contributing to HDI.

This research finding revealed that neuraxial anaesthesia was not significantly associated with postoperative HDI. This finding aligns with the study conducted in the USA, which stated that the choice of anaesthesia did not contribute to HDI. In contrast to this finding, the study conducted by Wolde et al7 and Abebe et al22 revealed that a patient who took neuraxial anaesthesia was significantly associated with postoperative HDI. This discrepancy might be attributed to the study setting or period and could be related to the quality of service in the PACU and also it might be the difference in adjuvant medications, dosages and type of local anaesthetic agent used to reduce the incidence of hypotension associated with regional anaesthesia could be one possible explanation.

Intraoperative HDI was significantly associated with HDI in the PACU. Patients who experienced intraoperative HDI were 1.8 times more likely to develop postoperative HDI in the PACU than those who did not experience intraoperative HDI. This result was in line with the research conducted by Abebe et al,22 Ahmadi et al39 and Wolde et al.7 This possible reason could be that during surgery, fluctuations in BP and HR can occur due to factors such as anaesthesia effects, fluid shifts, surgical stress and pain. These changes can lead to a compromised cardiovascular response that persists into the recovery phase. Additionally, intraoperative interventions, including fluid management and medication administration, may not adequately stabilise haemodynamics, resulting in residual instability. The cumulative impact of these factors can predispose patients to further complications in the PACU, where they may experience inadequate perfusion, altered vital signs or increased risk of adverse events.

Duration of surgery was found to be significantly associated with HDI in the PACU. Patients who had a surgical duration of 4 hours or more were 3.7 times more likely to develop HDI in the PACU than patients who had surgical procedures with less than 4 hours. This result was consistent with research conducted in Libya,21 Iran,18 and research conducted by Wolde et al7 and Abebe et al.22 All agreed that procedures lasting 4 hours or longer resulted in HDI in the PACU. This possible reason could arise from patients being exposed to increased amounts of anaesthetic medications, hypothermia, blood loss, fluid shifts and tissue stress as the procedure lengthens. This leads to heightened inflammation and a greater risk of complications. Consequently, the outcome is often hypotension, HTN or arrhythmias in the PACU.

Conclusion and recommendations

In general, this study revealed that the incidence of postoperative HDI in the PACU was high. ASA class III patients, patients with preoperative anxiety, patients who are on beta blocker medication preoperatively, individual patients who experienced intraoperative HDI, and patients undergoing procedures lasting more than 4 hours were predictors of HDI in the PACU.

Therefore, healthcare professionals assigned to the PACU should monitor the patients continuously, and we also recommend that future researchers monitor the patients for 24 hours and use invasive monitors. Interdisciplinary communication among anaesthesia providers, surgeons and PACU staff can facilitate comprehensive patient care. Additionally, standardised assessment tools and individualised care plans can help manage patients more effectively. By prioritising staff education, training and implementing evidence-based protocols, healthcare teams can improve patient outcomes and safety in the PACU.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The strength of our study lay in the multicentre data collection, which was crucial for generalising the results. Given the limited research on HDI in our country, this study could serve as a valuable baseline for future researchers. Some patients missed preoperative screening for coexisting medical conditions due to a lack of standard investigations, and this study revealed that HDI exclusively occurred in the PACU, and failed to detect any types of postoperative complications or HDI change experienced by patients after being discharged from the PACU. Observational study design is primarily limited by an inability to establish causal relationships due to a lack of control over variables, making it susceptible to confounding bias. Additionally, we used a non-invasive monitoring device that might not accurately reflect haemodynamic parameters.
