Pathogenesis of central serous choroidopathy: A novel hypothesis
Authors: Rohan Chawla
Published: 2024

Central serous retinopathy (CSC) is a common cause of visual disability in young individuals, particularly males. The incidence of CSC is estimated to vary from 9.9 to 27 per 100,000 in males and from 1.7 to 15 per 100,000 in females.[1] To date, the exact pathogenesis of CSC has not been fully understood. This manuscript details a possible hypothesis regarding pathogenesis of CSC. First, let us examine the established facts.

This disease was earlier referred to as central serous retinopathy, but the name was changed to choroidopathy when evidence from imaging studies revealed that the primary abnormality appeared to be in the choroid. Researchers have demonstrated increased choroidal thickness with larger lumen choroidal vessels and multiple areas of diffuse leakage from the choriocapillaris on indocyanine green angiography (ICG) in cases of CSC.[2] Dilated larger choroidal vessels and a thickened or pachychoroid with thinner inner choroidal layers have also been demonstrated using optical coherence tomography.[3] All of these findings suggest that the primary abnormality lies in the choroidal vasculature. Wide-field ICG studies have also revealed dilated choroidal veins, which have been referred to as varicosities by Spaide et al.[4] They have proposed a theory of venous overload choroidopathy as a cause of CSC. Anomalous anastomosis of these choroidal veins draining into the vortex veins has also been demonstrated.[5] These findings further indicate increased perfusion of the choroid. It also know that therapies that reduce choroidal flow, such as photodynamic therapy (PDT), are the most successful in treating this disorder.[6] Recent studies have also suggested that some cases of CSC may respond to drugs such as eplenerone,[7] intravitreal injection of steroids,[8] and intravitreal injection of certain anti-VEGF agents,[2] even in the absence of a choroidal neovascular membrane.

There are various studies on the risk factors of CSC, and most of them suggest that an imbalance between the sympathetic and parasympathetic systems,[9] type A personality,[10] hypertension,[11] and the intake of systemic steroids[12] are probable risks factors. Small eyes with reduced uveo-scleral outflow may also be a risk factor for CSC. In such cases, partial thickness sclerectomy may permit the choroidal vessels to dilate and reduce intraluminal pressure, aiding in the resolution of CSC.[13]

From all the above observations, it may be reasonable to conclude that in most cases of CSC, there is an increase in intraluminal pressure and flow volume in the medium to larger choroidal vessels, which presses on the overlying choriocapillaris, causing pressure-induced or ischemic damage to the choriocapillaris. This, in turn, leads to changes in the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). A breach in the outer retinal barrier results in an acute presentation of CSC, with some of the fluid overload from the choroidal space entering the subretinal space. More diffuse RPE damage leads to chronic CSC, with further movement of fluid into the retina. Continuous high flow in choroidal arterioles translates into increased flow in the choroidal veins, which would then become engorged and tortuous, appearing as varicose veins, as described by Spaide.

How can we connect the anatomical changes, risk factors, and possible therapeutic options described above to form a single hypothesis that explains most of these phenomenon. To do so, we must first understand the physiology of choroidal blood flow, as published in the literature.

The choroid is a highly vascular tissue with a very high flow rate. Blood flow in the choroid, at 1–2 ml/min, is the highest among all body tissues.[14] Choroidal blood flow in the submacular region is 10 times higher than in the retinal periphery.[14] It was once believed that choroidal flow is not regulated; however, evidence suggests that regulated choroidal flow is essential for maintaining a healthy retinal pigment epithelium and outer retinal function and that disturbances in flow can disrupt the function of these tissues.[15] It has been suggested that choroidal flow is unlikely to be affected by local metabolite concentrations and is instead under neural and myogenic control.[15] The choroid is innervated by both sympathetic and parasympathetic nerves.[15] Stimulation of the sympathetic nerves supplying the choroid has been shown to cause choroidal vasoconstriction in animals.[16] Sympathetic stimulation is said to cause vasoconstriction of the choroidal vessels to reduce overperfusion in response to increased blood pressure or in fight-or-flight situations.[16]

An imbalance between sympathetic and parasympathetic tone has previously been documented in cases of CSC.[9] Stress, Type A personality, and hypertension—conditions associated with heightened sympathetic tone—are all risk factors for CSC.[2] Does this imply that sympathetic vasoconstriction of the choroidal vasculature is the main culprit behind CSC? But then in today’s world, who does not experience stress, and many people have hypertension, yet not all of them develop CSC. Thus, the presence of a higher sympathetic tone cannot be the sole explanation. Thus, what is the missing link? Let us explore further.

A study conducted in 2003 demonstrated that following exercise, despite an increase in overall ocular perfusion pressure, some mechanism helps maintain choroidal blood flow close to its basal value.[17] Another experimental study carried out in 2005 has shown that, after exercise, there is a significant rise in choroidal flow in cases of chronic CSC, whereas choroidal blood flow increased only minimally in normal individuals.[18] These findings indicate that, following exercise, the physiological sympathetic neural response mentioned above is activated in normal individuals, leading to choroidal vasoconstriction. This response reduces choroidal blood flow, maintaining it close to its basal value. In cases of CSC, however, sympathetic neural regulation fails to act effectively, and thus the choroidal vessels fail to constrict. Consequently, choroidal flow does not reduce but rather increases as blood pressure and ocular perfusion pressure increase following exercise. Thus, it can be hypothesized that the primary defect in individuals with CSC is an inherent abnormality in the sympathetic innervation of the choroidal vascularity, which fails to regulate choroidal flow. Prolonged increased pressure in ocular circulation due to hypertension and stress leads to sustained, unregulated increased choroidal flow, resulting in an increase in the caliber of choroidal vessels. This, in turn, damages the overlying choriocapillaris and retinal pigment epithelium. A breach in the outer retinal barrier, in the presence of increased choroidal flow, results in acute CSC. Chronic damage ultimately leads to widespread RPE dysfunction and its sequelae. The primary pathology is thus hypothesized to be an abnormal neurovascular regulation of choroidal flow due to a defect in the sympathetic innervation of the choroid. This abnormality in choroidal sympathetic innervation could be congenital or acquired. Previous studies have suggested that an anomaly in the regulation of choroidal flow could be a reason behind CSC, although it was not specifically linked to anomalous sympathetic neural regulation.[14] Males with an inherently thicker choroid,[19] larger choroidal vessel size, and greater vessel density[20] may thus be more predisposed to the secondary effects of such anomalous neurovascular regulation. A study in humans has also shown that vigorous physical activity is a risk factor for CSC.[21] Thus, if we consider the primary defect in patients with CSC to be an abnormal sympathetic innervation of the choroidal vessels that fails to reduce choroidal flow under various situations, many aspects fall into place. This hypothesis could explain the entire cascade from acute CSC to pachychoroid, changes seen in chronic CSC, and response to PDT, which reduces choroidal flow.

Let us examine how the theory of abnormal neurovascular regulation of choroidal flow, due to an anomaly in the sympathetic innervation of the choroid, can explain other known aspects of CSC. Systemic corticosteroids are known to cause or worsen CSC. Systemic administration of steroids can increase systemic blood pressure[22] and enhance end-organ blood flow. In a study of apparently normal individuals, no changes in choroidal thickness were observed in most participants following the administration of systemic steroids. However, one participant showed increased choroidal thickness after systemic steroid administration and subsequently developed CSC.[23] According to our hypothesis, most normal individuals would have intact sympathetic neurovascular regulation, which mitigates the increase in vascular flow induced by systemic steroids. In contrast, an individual with a defective sympathetic neurovascular regulatory response would be unable to reduce choroidal flow effectively and may therefore develop CSC.

Further, a recent study has shown improvement in chronic CSC following intraocular steroid injection.[8] This improvement may not be due to an anti-inflammatory effects of steroids, as proposed by the authors of the study. If the anti-inflammatory effects were the mechanism of action, systemic steroids should also lead to CSC resolution. According to our proposed hypothesis, there is no significant inflammatory component; rather, the primary issue is an abnormal neuroregulatory mechanism. Local steroids likely cause local choroidal vasoconstriction, leading to reduced choroidal flow and resulting in an improvement in CSC. The reduction in choroidal thickness has also been documented by the authors of the same study. High concentrations of local steroids can cause vasoconstriction by potentiating the effects of available catecholamines or by altering the transcription of adrenergic receptor genes.[24] A similar choroidal vasoconstriction or reduced choroidal permeability may be involved in the action of anti-VEGF agents. Eplenerone, a mild anti-hypertensive, might benefit some patients with CSC due to its ability to lower blood pressure in those who are borderline hypertensives. Another reported intervention that has been helpful in some cases of CSC is partial thickness sclerectomy.[13] This procedure may help by allowing larger choroidal vessels to distend outwards, thereby reducing pressure on the overlying choriocapillaris.

Some individuals develop acute CSC only once in their lifetime and never thereafter. How does the theory of abnormal neurovascular regulation explain this phenomenon? Perhaps, in such individuals, the degree of abnormal neurovascular regulation is partial or incomplete. Choroidal flow may be upregulated but not to a severe extent. Following some event in life such as an acute stressful episode, over-exercise, or aggravation by the intake of systemic steroids, choroidal flow temporarily increases, leading to a breach in the integrity of the outer retinal barrier and acute CSC. Over time, as the RPE heals and choroidal flow returns to baseline levels, CSC resolves. In these individuals, the choroidal flow may never again exceed the critical level necessary to cause CSC. Conversely, individuals with significant neurovascular regulation abnormalities may experience recurrent CSC attacks, as observed in clinical practice. Those with nearly complete neurovascular regulation abnormalities may progress to chronic CSC.

If we assume the presented hypothesis to be correct, we can extrapolate the following:

One such test could be an exercise-induced choroidal flow assessment.

Based on an extensive literature review and our experience in treating these patients with various therapies over time, we hypothesize that an inherent neurovascular regulatory defect, primarily involving the sympathetic innervation of the choroid, can explain the pathogenesis of CSC. However, this hypothesis needs to be experimentally validated.
