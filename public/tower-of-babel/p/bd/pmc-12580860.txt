Canonical and Non‐Canonical Functions of Histone H3K4 Methylation Modifiers in Cancer
Authors: Takayuki Hoshii
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHistone H3K4 modifications are altered in the regulation of gene expression and in multiple cellular processes during cancer development and progression. Understanding the roles of H3K4‐modifying enzymes will provide novel insights into therapeutic tools for cancer treatment. H3K4‐modifying enzymes catalyze the addition or removal of covalent modifications with specific substrate preferences. Although these proteins are evolutionarily conserved between yeast and mammals, their protein domains and functions are not identical, and they exhibit signs of evolutionary divergence. Recent studies have indicated that H3K4‐modifying enzymes play non‐canonical roles via non‐histone substrates or non‐catalytic functions. Here, we discuss both canonical and non‐canonical functions of H3K4‐modifying enzymes and their potential benefits for therapeutic applications in cancer therapy.Keywords: cancer, demethylase, H3K4, methyltransferase, non‐canonical

Introduction

In eukaryotic cells, genomic DNA is organized into chromatin, where nucleosomes consist of approximately 150 base pairs wrapped around a histone octamer (H2A, H2B, H3, or H4). Histones undergo post‐translational modifications, including methylation, acetylation, phosphorylation, and ubiquitination, dynamically regulating chromatin architecture, gene expression, and other DNA‐templated processes.

The methylation of lysine 4 on histone H3 (H3K4) is a well‐characterized epigenetic marker associated with transcriptionally active chromatin [1]. It exists in mono‐, di‐, and tri‐methylated forms, each enriched at distinct genomic locations. H3K4 tri‐methylation (H3K4me3) localizes to the promoters of active genes, whereas H3K4 mono‐methylation (H3K4me1) marks enhancers [2]. These modifications are regulated by histone lysine methyltransferases (KMTs) and demethylases (KDMs), which maintain the epigenetic balance.

Aberrant regulation of H3K4 methylation has been implicated in cancers. Genetic alterations, including mutations, translocations, and copy number variations in H3K4‐modifying enzymes, have been reported in diverse tumors. Dysregulated H3K4 methylation leads to inappropriate gene expression, affecting cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and other cancer‐related pathways. These enzymes have emerged as biomarkers and therapeutic targets in oncology.

Importantly, the functions of H3K4 methylation modifiers extend beyond their canonical roles in histone methylation. In this review, we define canonical functions as their enzymatic activities toward histone H3K4, while non‐canonical functions encompass both (1) catalytic activities toward non‐histone substrates and (2) regulatory or scaffolding roles that do not depend on their catalytic domains. These non‐canonical activities often critically contribute to cancer progression and therapy resistance, highlighting the multifaceted nature of these proteins.

Here, we provide a comprehensive overview of both canonical and non‐canonical functions of histone H3K4 methylation modifiers in cancer, with particular emphasis on their diverse, context‐dependent activities and their significance in cancer biology and therapy.

H3K4 Methylation Patterns and Their Functional Implications

H3K4me1 is enriched in enhancers, whereas H3K4me3 occurs at the transcriptional start sites (TSS) of active genes. Di‐methylation (H3K4me2) is observed at both (Figure 1A). All three modifications are associated with transcriptional activity, with H3K4me3 as the most well‐established marker of active expression.

(B) Illustration of a bivalent promoter marked by both H3K4me3 (active) and H3K27me3 (repressive), indicating a poised transcriptional state. (C) Illustration of broad H3K4me3 domains in oocytes. Beyond canonical distribution, non‐canonical H3K4me3 patterns were identified (Figure 1B). Bivalent promoters harboring both H3K4me3 and the repressive marker, H3K27me3, maintain genes in a poised state during early embryogenesis [3]. Non‐TSS‐containing broad H3K4me3 domains in immature mouse oocytes must be removed for normal embryonic development [4]. Furthermore, broad H3K4me3 peaks extending across gene bodies enhance transcriptional elongation [5]. H3K4me3 has also been reported to regulate the RNA polymerase II pause‐release [6]. Thus, its function depends on chromatin context.

The H3K4M mutant, in which lysine 4 is replaced with methionine, blocks lysine methylation, demonstrating the biological role of H3K4 methylation. This mutation suppresses enhancer activation during adipose and muscle development [7], and has revealed the role of H3K4 methylation in bivalent promoters during hematopoiesis [8]. Unlike H3K27M and H3K36M, H3K4M is not associated with cancer, suggesting distinct cellular effects or incompatibility with transformation. H3K4M mimics H3K4me2 and binds strongly to H3K4 demethylases, potentially interfering with proteins recognizing H3K4 methylation [9]. Thus, findings using H3K4M should be interpreted cautiously and ideally validated with other mutations in H3K4‐modifying enzymes.

H3K4 methylation patterns are regulated by methyltransferases and demethylases, which show both redundant and non‐redundant functions (Figure 2). Therefore, understanding their individual roles and interactions is essential for uncovering novel mechanisms of transcriptional regulation, particularly in the context of cancer epigenetics.

H3K4 modifiers, and their canonical/non‐canonical functions. Methyltransferases and demethylases regulate H3K4 methylation states. Each enzyme exhibits distinct substrate specificities and preferentially catalyzes the addition or removal of specific methylation marks. Some of these also show non‐canonical roles, by catalyzing non‐histone substrates, regulating other proteins by non‐catalytic action, or acting as a scaffold for multiple subunits.

All H3K4 methyltransferases encode the enzymatic SET domain. KMT2 family proteins form the catalytic WRAD complex with WDR5, RbBP5, ASH2L, and DPY30 (Figure 3). Yeast has only Set1, responsible for all H3K4 methylation states. Surprisingly, yeast cells lacking Set1 activity and thus devoid of H3K4 methylation remain viable [10]. Loss of H3K4 methylation in yeast primarily leads to defects in gene silencing rather than gene activation, suggesting different functional significance from higher eukaryotes.

Functional divergence of Set1 homologs in mammals has been partly demonstrated. KMT2A/B primarily catalyze H3K4me2, KMT2C/D produce H3K4me1, and SETD1A/B mediate H3K4me3 in vitro (Figure 2) [11, 12]. This division of labor suggests that H3K4 methylation is not a simple transcriptional switch but a multifaceted epigenetic system with context‐specific roles in development, homeostasis, and diseases, including cancer.

H3K4 Methyltransferase

KMT2A/B

KMT2A (MLL1) and KMT2B (MLL2) are homologs of the drosophila trithorax (Figure 3). KMT2A is well characterized due to its essential roles in embryonic development and cancer. KMT2A regulates the expression of homeobox (Hox) genes and vertebrate development [13]. KMT2A/B methylates histone H3K4 as well as the non‐histone substrate, Borealin (K143), which plays a critical role in chromosome segregation [14]. KMT2A/B inhibition or Borealin at K143 mutation impairs the liquid–liquid phase separation of the chromosome passenger complex, leading to chromosome instability and aneuploidy in hepatocellular carcinoma [14].

Although the catalytic function of KMT2A is considered important, knock‐in mice lacking the SET domain of Kmt2a are viable, whereas conventional Kmt2a knockout mice are embryonic lethal [15, 16]. These observations indicate that the non‐catalytic functions of KMT2A are essential for embryogenesis. Notably, these same non‐catalytic functions are hijacked in leukemogenesis: KMT2A rearrangements (KMT2A‐r) produce oncogenic fusion proteins that lack the catalytic SET domain but retain the N‐terminal region containing the menin‐binding motif (MBM), which is required for leukemic transformation [17]. Collectively, these findings underscore that non‐catalytic mechanisms play fundamental roles not only in development but also in leukemia pathogenesis. Accordingly, menin inhibitors represent a promising therapeutic strategy against KMT2A‐r leukemia; as of 2024, the FDA has approved revumenib for this indication [18].

Several studies have shown that KMT2B has non‐redundant functions and is broadly required for global H3K4 methylation [19, 20]. Interestingly, a study reported an essential non‐catalytic role of KMT2B in the chromatin tethering of bivalent genes in mESC [21]. Further studies will provide new insights into the role of KMT2B in cancer development.

KMT2C/D

KMT2C (MLL3) and KMT2D (MLL4) are drosophila trithorax‐related (TRR) homologs that are frequently mutated in multiple cancers [22, 23, 24]. While KMT2D is a tumor suppressor in non‐Hodgkin's lymphoma [25], KMT2D has also been identified as a tumor promoter in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [26]. In contrast, KMT2C is widely known as a tumor suppressor in various cancers, including AML. Thus, these two enzymes play both redundant and non‐redundant roles in cancer development. Although the non‐canonical roles in cancer remain unclear, Kmt2c/d double knockout mouse embryonic stem cells (mESC) show the reduction of alkaline phosphatase activity, a classical marker for undifferentiated cells, whereas the Kmt2c/d‐∆SET mESC do not, thus these results indicate that the non‐enzymatic functions of KMT2C/D in stem cell maintenance [27]. Importantly, the conserved UTX stabilization domain (USD) of TRR was sufficient to rescue TRR‐null associated lethality in drosophila, and the KMT2D USD was sufficient to rescue gene expression in KMT2D‐deficient/KMT2C‐mutated HCT116 colon cancer cells. Thus, the non‐canonical function of KMT2C/D is to stabilize UTX in the nucleus [28]. KMT2D also interacts with the BAP1/ASXL2 histone deubiquitinase complex via multiple PHD finger domains at its N‐terminus [29]. Therefore, as with KMT2A/B, future studies are required to understand the potential roles of the N‐terminal complex of KMT2C/D in canonical and non‐canonical functions in both stem and cancer cells.

SETD1A/B

SETD1A (KMT2F) and SETD1B (KMT2G) are homologs of yeast Set1 and drosophila dSet1, and primarily mediate H3K4me3 (Figures 2 and 3). SETD1A is upregulated in multiple cancers; however, mutations are rarely detected [30, 31, 32]. In contrast, SETD1B is frequently mutated in B‐cell lymphoma [33].

Recent studies have shown the non‐redundant, non‐canonical functions of SETD1A/B (Figure 3). SETD1A catalyzes YAP K342me1, prevents its nuclear export, and promotes tumorigenesis in a lung cancer model [30]. Although SETD1A is indispensable in AML, its catalytic SET domain is dispensable (Figures 3 and 4) [34]. The FLOS domain of SETD1A functions as a scaffold, interacting with the BuGZ/BUB3 complex, BOD1L, and cyclin K [34, 35, 36]. Both the BuGZ/BUB3 complex and BOD1L are kinetochore‐associated factors, and BOD1L recruits SETD1A to chromatin [35]. The SETD1A‐cyclin K complex, together with CDK12, regulates RNA polymerase II elongation; thus, cyclin K–CDK12 inhibitors are promising for SETD1A‐dependent cancers [34]. Cyclin K and BOD1L, but not BuGZ/BUB3, interact specifically with SETD1A, not SETD1B, supporting a functionally distinct non‐canonical SETD1A complex.

Non‐redundant functions of SETD1A/B in leukemia. SETD1A/B forms a canonical complex called COMPASS. The non‐enzymatic function of SETD1A promotes the transcriptional elongation of genes associated with DNA repair and mitochondrial respiration. In contrast, SETD1B maintained broad H3K4 peaks on MYC because of its sustained expression in AML cells.

In contrast, SETD1B interacts with BOD1, a cytoplasmic protein that promotes breast cancer progression [37]. Loss of either SETD1B or BOD1 induces the expression of genes involved in fatty acid metabolism, although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. In SETD1B‐deficient breast cancer cells, global H3K4me3 levels are unaffected, and the SET domain of SETD1B appears dispensable. Thus, the non‐catalytic function is a common feature of SETD1A/B in cancer cells.

Accumulated evidence indicates that SETD1B regulates broad H3K4me3 peaks and that its catalytic activity is essential for neurons, spermatogenesis, oogenesis [38, 39, 40]. Our group showed that the catalytic function of SETD1B is required for cytokine‐independent growth and MYC expression, whereas BOD1 is dispensable in MLL‐rearranged leukemia (Figure 4) [41]. These findings suggest BOD1/BOD1L acts as a molecular switch of SETD1A/B between catalytic and non‐catalytic functions. In mESC, SETD1A/B are functionally redundant for the formation of broad H3K4me3 peaks [42]. In contrast, no additive effects were observed upon deletion of the SET domain of SETD1A and knockdown of SETD1B in a leukemia model [41]. Although SETD1B acts canonically across cell types, SETD1A's enzymatic activity is cell type–specific, possibly explaining distinct roles of each protein depending on the cellular context [43]. The context‐dependent utilization of SETD1A/B in different cancers remains unexplored and is an important issue for future research.

SMYD

SET and MYND domain‐containing proteins 1–3 (SMYD1–3) may also function as H3K4 methyltransferases. SMYD1 (KMT3D) possesses H3K4 di‐methylation activity and is essential for muscle fiber maturation [44, 45]. SMYD2 (KMT3C) was initially identified as an H3K36 methyltransferase; however, it also catalyzes H3K4 methylation. SMYD2 is markedly upregulated in various cancers and contains multiple non‐histone substrates, including p53 (K370), ERα (K266), and EZH2 (K307) [46, 47, 48]. SMYD3 (KMT3E) is also essential in various cancers, including colorectal, hepatocellular, and oral squamous cell carcinoma [49, 50, 51]. SMYD3 catalyzes the methylation of H3K4, H4K5, and H4K20, as well as non‐histone substrates such as VEGFR1 (K831), HER2 (K175), and IRF3 (K39) [52, 53, 54]. Although SMYD3 inhibitors have been developed, no molecules have been reported for clinical use, and their effects on the methylation of both H3K4 and non‐histone substrates have not yet been evaluated [55, 56]. The utilization of these inhibitors in cancer models may also promote the identification of non‐catalytic roles of SMYD family proteins. Moreover, the potential redundancy between SMYD proteins and KMT2, the H3K4 methyltransferases, remains an important topic for future investigation.

H3K4 Demethylase

KDM1

KDM1A (LSD1) was the first histone demethylase identified [57]. KDM1A demethylates H3K4me1/2, H3K9me1/2, and H4K20me1/2 (Figure 2). H3K9me1/2 is associated with transcriptional repression. Thus, KDM1A regulates both repression and activation, with its inhibition showing context‐dependent effects. KDM1A also targets non‐histone substrates, demethylating and stabilizing several key regulatory proteins, including DNMT1 [58], E2F1 [59], and HIF‐1α [60] (Figure 5A). KDM1A also interacts with p53, specifically demethylating K370me1/2, thereby repressing 53BP1‐dependent activity [61]. KDM1A also demethylates β‐catenin at K180, enhancing its transcriptional activity and promoting muscle stem cell self‐renewal [60], raising interest in the KDM1A/β‐catenin axis in cancer. Beyond its enzymatic functions, KDM1A also exhibited non‐catalytic activity (Figure 5B). For example, KDM1A destabilizes tumor suppressors FBXW7 [62] and p62 [63], while stabilizing ERRα [64]. KDM1B (LSD2), a paralog of KDM1A, also demethylates H3K4me1/2. KDM1B is upregulated in cancers and functions as an E3 ubiquitin ligase promoting O‐GlcNAc transferase degradation, thereby inhibiting lung cancer cell growth [65]. These findings indicate the enzymatic and non‐enzymatic importance of KDM1A/B in cancer biology.

Non‐canonical functions of KDM1A demethylase. (A) Non‐histone targets of KDM1A‐mediated demethylation. (B) Non‐catalytic function of KDM1A. (C) KDM1A acts as a scaffold to recruit a transcription repressive complex in a GFI‐dependent manner. A KDM1A inhibitor blocked this interaction.

The aberrant expression or activity of KDM1A is associated with oncogenic transcription, especially in hematological malignancies and neuroendocrine tumors. KDM1A inhibitors including ORY‐1001 (ladademstat) and IMG‐7289 (bomedemstat) are currently under clinical investigation. The function of KDM1A in hematopoietic malignancies extends beyond its demethylase activity. A pivotal mechanism is its function as a transcriptional corepressor through interaction with the transcriptional repressor GFI1 (Figure 5C). KDM1A forms a complex with GFI1 and the CoREST co‐repressor complex, anchoring it to the chromatin and repressing differentiation‐associated genes [66]. In this context, KDM1A acts more as a scaffold than as an enzyme. Understanding the dual roles of KDM1A as a demethylase and a transcriptional scaffold is essential for optimizing therapies.

KDM5

The KDM5 family (KDM5A–D) specifically removes H3K4me2/3. KDM5 proteins are tightly regulated under physiological conditions, but their dysregulation is implicated in various diseases, particularly cancer. KDM5A is frequently overexpressed in acute lymphoblastic leukemia, neuroblastoma, and prostate cancer, repressing tumor suppressor genes [67]. KDM5B functions as an oncogene in breast cancer and melanoma, promoting tumor growth and therapeutic resistance by maintaining a dedifferentiated state [68, 69]. Conversely, KDM5B also acts as a tumor suppressor in MLL‐ and NUP98‐rearranged AML. Similarly, KDM5C is oncogenic in some cancers but suppressive in others, including clear cell renal carcinoma [70, 71]. Recently, our group and others demonstrated the tumor‐suppressive function of KDM5C in mouse AML models [41, 72]. Strong tumor suppressor activity was observed for KDM5C, but not for KDM5B, in MLL‐rearranged AML with RAS activation, suggesting that cellular context or signaling influences dependency on specific KDM5 members. Unlike others, KDM5D, located on the Y chromosome, shows tissue‐restricted expression and is less studied. However, emerging evidence suggests a role for sex‐specific tumor invasiveness and metastasis [73].

Unlike KDM1A, no direct biochemical evidence supports specific non‐histone substrates for KDM5 demethylases. Although peptide library‐based screening has predicted candidates, further validation is required to establish their physiological relevance [74]. Beyond demethylase activity, KDM5 proteins act as scaffolds in chromatin regulation. Yeast KDM5 (Jhd2) interacts with polyadenylation machinery to regulate 3′UTR length [75]. This function appears to be conserved in human KDM5B in breast cancer cells. In Drosophila, KDM5 promotes mitochondrial gene expression via its PHD motif, independent of demethylase activity [76]. In mammals, KDM5A enhances TNC expression and promotes breast cancer invasion in a demethylase‐independent manner [77]. These findings highlight the broad functional repertoire of KDM5 proteins beyond histone demethylation.

Conclusions

While current therapeutic strategies focus on blocking enzymatic activity, alternative approaches, such as targeted protein degradation, allosteric inhibitors, or protein–protein interaction blockers, may be more effective for enzymes with critical non‐catalytic functions. In our study, targeted degradation of SETD1A using the dTAG system demonstrated strong tumor‐suppressive effects in leukemia cells, highlighting the promise of this strategy [78]. The development of small‐molecule degraders is expected to further accelerate progress in this area. At the same time, the field of H3K4 methylation biology is rapidly evolving, driven by emerging technologies and the growing appreciation of the complexity of chromatin‐associated proteins. Addressing these challenges will not only uncover novel regulatory paradigms but also pave the way for more precise and effective epigenetic therapies for cancer.
