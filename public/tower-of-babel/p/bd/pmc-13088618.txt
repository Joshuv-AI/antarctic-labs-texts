Independent risk factors and predictive model for high hospitalization costs in children with Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia
Authors: Xianyao Wang, Hachao Zhou, Lingling Jiang, Haipeng Lin, Wenshan Zhong, Ruiling Ma, Zhiwei Xiao, Shaofen Lin, Jing Lin, Yutao Guo, Mingxiang Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP) can impose substantial healthcare burden in children. Because retrospective severity definitions can be heterogeneous, we used total hospitalization cost as a complementary high–resource-use phenotype and aimed to identify admission-time predictors of high cost and develop an early prediction model.MethodsWe retrospectively enrolled 402 hospitalized children with MPP at Shantou Central Hospital, China (Jan–Dec 2024). A Gaussian mixture model (GMM) was fitted to total hospitalization cost to derive a data-driven threshold for a high-cost (high–resource-use) subgroup. Using variables available within the first 24 h of admission, we developed a logistic model including age, Tmax, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and radiographic consolidation, and then evaluated an age × consolidation interaction. Discrimination was assessed by ROC AUC; age-stratified analyses were used to interpret joint age–consolidation patterns.ResultsA data-driven cost threshold identified a high-cost subgroup (> 7,139 RMB; 41/402, 10.2%), defined a posteriori from the observed hospitalization-cost distribution. High-cost cases had longer length of stay, more frequent intensive management (bronchoalveolar lavage and systemic corticosteroids), and a higher prevalence of consolidation. The four-variable model showed good discrimination (AUC 0.790), which improved after adding the age × consolidation interaction (AUC 0.838). Inflammatory markers were higher in consolidation-positive patients, with the largest differences observed in younger children; correspondingly, the interaction model identified younger children with consolidation as having the highest predicted high-cost risk.ConclusionsIn this single-center retrospective cohort, hospitalization cost operationalized a high–resource-use phenotype in pediatric MPP. An admission-time model incorporating an age × consolidation interaction may support early risk stratification and resource planning in similar settings; external validation is needed before clinical use.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06691-z.Keywords: Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia, Pediatric, Hospitalization cost, Gaussian mixture model, Pulmonary consolidation

Introduction

Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP) is one of the leading causes of community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) in children and accounts for a considerable proportion of pediatric respiratory hospitalizations worldwide [1]. Although most cases follow a mild and self-limited course, a subset of patients develop severe or refractory MPP (SMPP/RMPP), characterized by persistent fever, progressive pulmonary consolidation, extrapulmonary complications, and poor response to macrolide therapy [2–4]. These severe forms often require prolonged hospitalization, adjunctive corticosteroid or immunoglobulin therapy, and even intensive care unit (ICU) admission, imposing a substantial clinical and economic burden on families and healthcare systems [5–7]. Epidemiological data indicate that MPP follows cyclical epidemic patterns, with epidemic seasons often accompanied by surges in severe (SMPP) and refractory (RMPP) cases, underscoring the need for early identification and risk stratification of children at high risk for severe disease during outbreak periods [1, 6, 8].

Although recent evidence-based guidelines in China have proposed more standardized criteria for severe and refractory MPP [9], definitions used in clinical practice and research remain heterogeneous worldwide and are not yet fully harmonized across countries and studies. For instance, some studies define refractory MPP as persistent fever and radiologic deterioration after seven or more days of macrolide therapy, whereas others adopt shorter thresholds (72–120 h) or emphasize clinical and radiologic worsening despite ongoing macrolide treatment [10–13]. Moreover, interpretation of pediatric chest radiographs and clinical judgment can be subjective, with notable inter-observer variability even under standardized reading protocols, which further contributes to variability in case inclusion and disease classification [14–16]. In contrast, hospitalization cost represents an objective and quantifiable indicator integrating multiple aspects of disease severity and resource utilization—such as length of stay, diagnostic testing, advanced therapies (e.g., corticosteroids, IVIG), and the need for intensive care—and aligns with established pediatric resource-intensity frameworks assessing disease burden [17–19]. Additionally, recent real-world cost analyses from Chinese pediatric cohorts demonstrate a strong correlation between total hospitalization expenses and disease severity, supporting the feasibility of using cost as an outcome for clinical prediction modeling [20–21]. Accordingly, we conceptualized total hospitalization cost as a high–resource-use phenotype that complements heterogeneous severity definitions, while acknowledging setting-specific influences. Therefore, we aimed to develop an admission-time prediction model to identify children at risk of high hospitalization costs and to support early resource planning in a single-center cohort.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective observational cohort study was conducted at Shantou Central Hospital (Guangdong, China). Pediatric patients (< 14 years of age) clinically diagnosed with pneumonia and hospitalized between January and December 2024 were screened for Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) using targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) of oropharyngeal swabs.

During this period, 2,503 specimens were submitted, and MP was detected in 607 cases (24.25%), of which 417 (16.67%) had an estimated concentration > 1 × 10⁶ copies/mL. To reduce potential misclassification from prior or convalescent MP detection, we defined the analytic cohort using prespecified microbiological/serological criteria (including EC > 1 × 10⁶ copies/mL), and included 402 hospitalized children in the final analysis (230 boys and 172 girls) (Fig. 1). All study variables were complete in the final analytic cohort (n = 402). Variables with missingness were checked; the final analytic cohort included only patients with complete data for the prespecified predictors (complete-case analysis).

All demographic, clinical, laboratory, and treatment data were retrieved from the hospital’s electronic medical record system. The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee of Shantou Central Hospital (Approval No. [2025,119]), and the requirement for informed consent was waived because of its retrospective design.

Clinical data collection and variable definitions

Clinical data were extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical records. Candidate predictors were prespecified as admission-time variables and included demographic characteristics (age and sex), clinical features at presentation (duration of fever, duration of cough, and peak body temperature [Tmax]), and laboratory and hospitalization indicators (white blood cell count, platelet count, C-reactive protein, neutrophil and lymphocyte percentages, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, lactate dehydrogenase [LDH], tNGS results, M. pneumoniae antibody titers, and total hospitalization costs). Tmax was defined a priori as the highest documented body temperature within the first 24 h after admission and was extracted from nursing temperature records. LDH was defined as the first laboratory result obtained within the first 24 h after admission and was extracted from the laboratory information system. Pulmonary consolidation was determined from the first chest imaging performed within the first 24 h after admission. To minimize information leakage, all predictors were restricted to data available within the first 24 h of admission and prior to major inpatient interventions (e.g., bronchoalveolar lavage or escalation to systemic corticosteroids).

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics and group comparisons. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using an independent-samples t test (two groups) or one-way ANOVA (≥ 3 groups), with Bonferroni adjustment for multiple comparisons where applicable. Non-normally distributed data are presented as median (interquartile range) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test (two groups) or the Kruskal–Wallis test (≥ 3 groups), with Dunn’s post-hoc test and Bonferroni adjustment when appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as counts (percentages) and were compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Cost distribution and cut-off. Total hospitalization cost showed a right-skewed, long-tailed distribution, indicating potential latent cost strata. We fitted Gaussian mixture models with 1–4 components and compared models using the Bayesian information criterion (BIC). Models with ≥ 2 components yielded substantially lower BIC than the single-component model, whereas improvements beyond two components were modest. Given our clinical objective of distinguishing ordinary- from high-cost patients, we adopted a two-component Gaussian mixture model (K = 2) to approximate low- and high-cost subgroups. Extreme high-cost cases were retained (no trimming or winsorization). The high-cost cut-off was defined as the intersection point of the two weighted component density curves, yielding a threshold of 7,139 RMB; costs were dichotomized at this value for subsequent analyses. To assess robustness to the definition of high-cost hospitalization, we performed prespecified sensitivity analyses using alternative binary outcomes defined by the 90th percentile (top decile) and 75th percentile (top quartile) of total hospitalization cost. In addition, total cost was analyzed as a continuous outcome using a Gamma generalized linear model with log link (Gamma-log GLM). These sensitivity analyses used the same admission-time predictors as the primary model (and, where applicable, the age × consolidation interaction term).

Univariable screening and primary multivariable model. Each admission-time clinical variable was examined by univariable logistic regression against the high-cost outcome. Guided by clinical plausibility and univariable results, the prespecified multivariable model included four early-warning predictors—age (months), Tmax, LDH, and pulmonary consolidation (0/1)—with continuous predictors kept continuous. Discrimination was quantified by ROC AUC. Uncertainty and internal validation were assessed using 1,000 nonparametric bootstrap resamples, providing 95% CIs for AUC and for sensitivity/specificity at the Youden’s J–optimal operating point, and yielding calibration metrics (calibration intercept/slope; Hosmer–Lemeshow test where appropriate) and the Brier score. Clinical utility was evaluated by decision-curve analysis across clinically plausible threshold probabilities. Given 41 high-cost events, model complexity was constrained and EPV was tracked (EPV = 10.3 for the prespecified 4-predictor model; EPV = 8.2 for the interaction-augmented model). As a sensitivity analysis for potential overfitting, we additionally fitted an L2-penalized (ridge) logistic regression model with the same predictors and the age × consolidation interaction; the penalty strength was selected by cross-validation (Supplementary Table S3).

Interaction analysis and link to prediction. Because consolidation was more frequent in older children, whereas overall hospitalization cost decreased with age, we evaluated effect modification between age and consolidation. Using the same early-available predictors, continuous hospitalization costs were modeled using a Gamma generalized linear model (log link) including age, consolidation, and their interaction (age × consolidation). From this model, we derived (i) marginal predicted costs by age according to consolidation status, and (ii) the age-specific cost ratio curve, CR(age) = predicted cost (consol = 1) / predicted cost (consol = 0), with 95% confidence intervals. To assess the incremental predictive value of the interaction for the binary high-cost outcome, we compared two nested logistic models—a base model (age, Tmax, LDH, consolidation) and the same model plus the age × consolidation term—using the DeLong test for the difference in AUC and a likelihood-ratio test. Age was mean-centered (Age_c = Age − 59.2 months) before constructing the interaction (Age_c × consolidation). Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (maximum VIF = 1.62). Full parameter estimates and collinearity diagnostics for both models are provided in Supplementary Table S1. The age × consolidation interaction was prespecified as a plausible effect modifier and evaluated by comparing nested models.

Software and reproducibility. Descriptive statistics and group comparisons were performed in SPSS 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY). Predictive modeling and sensitivity analyses—including Gaussian mixture modeling for cost cut-off selection, logistic regression (with interaction term), internal validation by nonparametric bootstrap (AUC and calibration metrics including calibration intercept/slope and Brier score), decision-curve analysis, ridge-penalized logistic regression, and Gamma generalized linear modeling with log link for continuous-cost analyses—were implemented in Python 3.11 (NumPy 1.26, pandas 2.2, SciPy/Matplotlib, statsmodels 0.14, scikit-learn 1.4) with a fixed random seed. Figures were finalized in GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. A detailed analysis pipeline and code-availability statement are provided in the Supplementary Appendix.

Results

Seasonality of MPP admissions and cost profile

Monthly admissions peaked in July (n = 77), with secondary peaks in June and August (both n = 50), and the lowest volumes in March and December (both n = 11). Median monthly hospitalization costs ranged from ~ 3,600 to 4,750 RMB, with higher medians in October (~ 4,750 RMB) and December (~ 4,743 RMB), whereas high-volume July had only a moderate median (~ 3,822 RMB). Cost dispersion (IQR) was widest in March and December (upper quartiles ~ 7,972 and ~ 6,582 RMB), indicating greater month-to-month variability in resource intensity. Overall, admission volume and per-case cost were not tightly coupled, suggesting that severity mix and care patterns differ by month (Fig. 2). Across the full cohort, mean hospitalization cost was 4,766 RMB (SD 3,654), and the median was 3,990 RMB (IQR 3,142–5,103).

Stratification and clinical parameter differences based on hospitalization cost

Cost stratification (Gaussian Mixture Model, GMM)

Based on the two-component GMM described in the Methods, the intersection of the two weighted component density curves yielded a data-driven cut-off of 7,139 RMB (Fig. 3). Using this threshold, 41/402 children (10.2%) were classified as high-cost (> 7,139 RMB) and 361/402 (89.8%) as ordinary-cost (≤ 7,139 RMB), providing the binary outcome for subsequent univariable comparisons and multivariable prediction analyses.

Continuous parameters

Compared with the ordinary-cost group, the high-cost group was younger (median 48.0 vs. 60.0 months; P = 0.0172), had higher peak temperature (Tmax: 39.8 vs. 39.2 °C; P = 0.0007), and higher LDH (median 410 vs. 325 U/L; P < 0.0001). Length of stay was markedly longer (12.0 vs. 7.0 days; P < 0.0001). Distributions of fever duration, cough duration, BMI, WBC, neutrophil/lymphocyte ratios (including N/L), platelets, CRP, and normalized MP reads did not differ significantly between groups (all P ≥ 0.19) (Table 1).

Multivariable logistic regression (independent factors for high hospitalization cost)

Guided by clinical plausibility and the observed univariable associations (Tables 1 and 2), we excluded in-hospital management variables (e.g., bronchoalveolar lavage, systemic corticosteroids) and variables not suitable for baseline prediction (e.g., length of stay), and pre-specified a parsimonious admission-time predictor set. Four variables were independently associated with high hospitalization cost: Age (months): OR = 0.9834 (95% CI 0.9711–0.9959, p = 0.0093). Each additional month corresponds to ~ 1.7% lower odds; per 12 months, ~ 18–20% lower odds. Peak temperature (Tmax, °C): OR = 1.6000 (95% CI 1.0044–2.5487, p = 0.0479), indicating ~ 60% higher odds per + 1 °C. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH, U/L): OR = 1.0047 (95% CI 1.0019–1.0075, p = 0.0011); per + 100 U/L, ≈ 1.60-fold higher odds. Pulmonary consolidation (present vs. absent): OR = 5.4904 (95% CI 2.6101–11.5495, p < 0.0001), ≈ 5.5-fold higher odds with radiographic consolidation (Table 3).

In summary, older age was associated with lower odds of high hospitalization cost, whereas higher peak temperature (Tmax), elevated LDH, and radiographic consolidation were associated with higher costs, consistent with greater clinical severity and resource utilization.

Predicting high hospitalization cost in hospitalized children with Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection: univariable effects and multivariable ROC performance

The multivariable logistic model showed the highest discrimination for high hospitalization cost (AUC 0.790, 95% CI 0.708–0.853). Among individual predictors, LDH had the greatest discrimination (AUC 0.695, 95% CI 0.598–0.789; Youden’s J cut-off ≈ 410 U/L), followed by pulmonary consolidation (AUC 0.683, 95% CI 0.596–0.760), Tmax (AUC 0.661, 95% CI 0.564–0.753; J cut-off ≈ 39.6 °C), and age (AUC 0.613, 95% CI 0.516–0.703; J cut-off ≈ 24 months) (Fig. 4). At the Youden’s J–optimal operating point, the multivariable model achieved sensitivity 0.707 and specificity 0.759 (cut p ≈ 0.11; TP/FP/TN/FN = 29/87/274/12). The corresponding J-point sensitivities/specificities for single predictors were: LDH 0.537/0.848, consolidation 0.585/0.781, Tmax 0.561/0.751, and age 0.439/0.781.

For bedside use, we additionally report two clinically oriented probability thresholds from the multivariable ROC curve, given a high-cost prevalence of 10.2% in this cohort: a sensitivity-prioritized cutoff of p ≥ 0.051 (sensitivity 90.2%, specificity 53.5%, PPV 18.0%, NPV 98.0%) and a specificity-prioritized cutoff of p ≥ 0.225 (sensitivity 56.1%, specificity 91.4%, PPV 42.6%, NPV 94.8%).

Given that pulmonary consolidation is clinically more prevalent among older children, whereas age itself showed a protective association with high hospitalization cost in our data, yet consolidation was positively associated with high cost, we posited that age might modify the effect of consolidation on cost, raising a plausible interaction hypothesis. We then examined the association between age (in months) and consolidation status: a Mann–Whitney U test indicated a significant age difference between groups (U = 12,356, P = 0.0027), with patients exhibiting consolidation being older (median 72.0 months, IQR 36.0–84.0) than those without consolidation (median 48.0 months, IQR 24.0–84.0), supporting a positive association between the two.

On this clinical and statistical basis, we fitted a Gamma regression with a log link (Gamma–log GLM) including age (continuous), consolidation, and their interaction, adjusting for other covariates. This had two aims: (i) to depict age-specific cost trajectories stratified by consolidation and assess whether slopes differ by stratum; and (ii) to quantify the age-dependent cost ratio

and identify the crossing where CR = 1, indicating the age at which the relative impact of consolidation on cost diminishes to equivalence. Accordingly, we generated two figures: (Fig. 5) predicted cost versus age with 95% CIs by consolidation status, and (Fig. 6) the CR(age) curve with 95% CIs, marking the CR = 1 crossing at approximately 135.5 months as a clinically interpretable reference. These visualizations corroborate the ROC-based interaction analysis: at younger ages, consolidation corresponds to higher predicted costs and larger cost ratios; with increasing age, this effect attenuates and approaches parity—providing intuitive evidence of effect modification of the consolidation–cost relationship by age.

Adding the age × consolidation term improved discrimination (AUC 0.790 [95% CI 0.708–0.853] to 0.838 [95% CI 0.768–0.900]; ΔAUC = 0.048, 95% bootstrap CI 0.011–0.088; DeLong P = 0.014) and improved overall model fit (likelihood-ratio χ² = 12.14, df = 1, P = 0.00049), indicating that the interaction captures information beyond additive main effects (Fig. 7). Full parameter estimates for both models are provided in Supplementary Table S1. For external validation and bedside calculation, the full interaction-model equation (including the intercept) with variable coding/scaling is reported in the Supplementary Appendix; a two-panel nomogram stratified by consolidation status is shown in Supplementary Figure S1.

Comparison of ROC curves before and after adding the age × consolidation interaction

In the interaction model, the association between age and high-cost risk varied by consolidation status. Among children with consolidation, younger age corresponded to a substantially higher predicted probability of high hospitalization cost (> 7,139 RMB), whereas the excess cost associated with consolidation attenuated with increasing age. Consistently, the cost-ratio curve CR(age) approached 1 at approximately 135 months, indicating near-disappearance of the consolidation-related cost increment in older children. Overall, these findings support effect modification by age and show that incorporating the interaction improves discrimination while enabling an age-specific interpretation of economic burden. Model calibration for both the base and interaction models is shown in Supplementary Figure S2. Across deciles of predicted risk, observed event rates generally increased with predicted probabilities; calibration intercept/slope (bootstrap 95% CIs) and Brier scores indicated acceptable calibration and good overall probabilistic accuracy, with only minor deviations in the lowest-risk deciles where predicted probabilities were close to zero (Supplementary Figure S2). In penalized-regression sensitivity analyses, ridge logistic regression yielded highly similar discrimination (AUC 0.839, 95% bootstrap CI 0.765–0.898) and consistent coefficient directions, supporting model stability despite the low event count (Supplementary Table S3). Decision-curve analysis (Supplementary Figure S3) suggested that using the interaction model to guide a risk-based strategy provides net benefit across a broad range of threshold probabilities compared with default “treat-all” and “treat-none” approaches, supporting potential clinical utility for admission-time risk stratification and resource planning.

Sensitivity analyses for the high-cost definition

Findings were robust to alternative outcome definitions. When high-cost hospitalization was redefined using the top decile and top quartile of total hospitalization cost, the direction of associations for key predictors (notably Tmax and LDH) remained consistent (Supplementary Table S2). Moreover, when total cost was analyzed as a continuous outcome using a Gamma-log GLM, Tmax and LDH remained positively associated with higher costs and the age × consolidation interaction persisted in direction, supporting that predictor–cost relationships were not driven by a single dichotomization threshold (Supplementary Table S4). Finally, to address right-skewness and potential influence of extreme values on mixture-based thresholding, we refit the Gaussian mixture model on log-transformed costs and back-transformed the intersection cut-off; the resulting classification showed high agreement with the primary raw-cost GMM definition with low posterior overlap/uncertainty, supporting robustness of the cost-phenotype derivation (Supplementary Figure S4 and Supplementary Table S5). Although no trimming/winsorization was applied, refitting the GMM on log(cost)—which down-weights extreme values on the raw scale—yielded a similar cost-phenotype classification with high agreement to the primary definition, indicating limited sensitivity to the single extreme outlier.

Age–consolidation interaction and its combined impact on high-cost hospitalization

To specifically examine the interaction between pulmonary consolidation and age, we stratified the 402 hospitalized pneumonia patients by age group (< 3 years, 3–5 years, and ≥ 6 years) and by the presence or absence of lung consolidation on imaging, yielding six subgroups. We compared key clinical indicators (peak temperature Tmax, white blood cell count WBC, C-reactive protein CRP, lactate dehydrogenase LDH), treatment interventions (bronchoalveolar lavage BAL, intravenous steroid use), and outcome measures (length of stay LOS, total hospital cost) across these subgroups. All values are summarized as median and interquartile range (IQR); for LDH and cost, mean ± standard deviation (SD) are also provided to reflect their skewed distributions (Table 4). The frequency of consolidation increased with patient age: only about 13% of children < 3 years had evidence of consolidation, compared with ~ 26% in those aged 3–5 years and ~ 32% in those ≥ 6 years (Table 4).

We observed clear age-related gradients in inflammatory markers, with both Tmax and CRP increasing across age bands in absolute terms. For example, median CRP rose from 2.7 mg/L in < 3-year-olds without consolidation to 15.3 mg/L in ≥ 6-year-olds without consolidation, and from 12.1 to 17.7 mg/L among those with consolidation (Table 4). However, the increment associated with consolidation differed by age: in infants and toddlers, consolidation was associated with a large jump in CRP and fever (e.g., CRP 2.7 vs. 12.1 mg/L; Tmax 38.6 vs. 39.5 °C in < 3-year-olds), whereas in older children the CRP and Tmax gaps between consolidation-negative and consolidation-positive patients were progressively smaller (e.g., CRP 15.3 vs. 17.7 mg/L; Tmax 39.4 vs. 39.5 °C in ≥ 6 years). This pattern suggests that younger children exhibit a more pronounced inflammatory “contrast” between non-consolidated and consolidated pneumonia, whereas in older children high inflammatory levels are more uniformly present regardless of consolidation status.

The presence of consolidation was consistently associated with a more severe illness course in all age categories. Consolidation-positive patients had longer hospital stays and higher hospital costs than their age-matched counterparts without consolidation (Table 4). Among children < 3 years old, those with consolidation had a median LOS of 10 days versus 7.5 days in those without consolidation, and their median hospital cost was 5,899 vs. 4,210 RMB. Similarly, in both the 3–5-year and ≥ 6-year groups, cases with consolidation showed higher median LOS and cost compared with those without consolidation. LDH levels were also generally higher in consolidation-positive than in consolidation-negative patients within each age band, consistent with more extensive tissue injury in pneumonias with lobar consolidation (Table 4). Notably, the youngest consolidation-positive group (< 3 years) had the highest mean cost, partly driven by several very high-cost cases.

Therapeutic interventions also showed age- and consolidation-related patterns. As expected, no child without consolidation underwent BAL, whereas a minority of consolidation-positive patients did, with the highest BAL frequency in the youngest age group and a gradual decline with age (23.1% in < 3 years, 13.5% in 3–5 years, and 9.4% in ≥ 6 years; Table 4). Intravenous corticosteroid therapy was substantially more common in consolidation-positive than in consolidation-negative patients across all age strata (e.g., 76.9% vs. 44.0% in < 3 years; 70.3% vs. 50.5% in 3–5 years; 67.9% vs. 49.1% in ≥ 6 years). Within the consolidation-positive subgroup, steroid use tended to be highest in the youngest children and slightly lower in older age bands, indicating that intensive interventions (BAL and steroids) were most frequently applied in younger patients once consolidation was present. Overall, these findings indicate that the combination of younger age and pulmonary consolidation is associated with a more pronounced inflammatory contrast, more intensive treatment, and greater economic burden, which is further reflected in the distribution of high-cost hospitalizations across age–consolidation subgroups (Tables 4 and 5).

Discussion

This study used total hospitalization cost in children with Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP) as the starting point and developed a cost-based risk stratification, followed by multivariable analysis and ROC modelling using admission-time clinical variables. The aim was to identify inpatients most likely to follow a high–resource-use trajectory and to evaluate the early warning value of simple admission indicators. In our setting, total inpatient cost is a routinely captured, objective endpoint that integrates the intensity of care delivered and may correlate with overall clinical burden. In practice, higher costs often accompany longer hospital stays and more intensive management, including combination antibiotic regimens, systemic corticosteroid therapy, ICU admission, mechanical ventilation, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL), management of complications, and slower clinical recovery—features more commonly observed in severe pediatric pneumonia and refractory MPP [21–24]. Prior pediatric pneumonia studies similarly suggest that severe cases incur substantially higher direct medical costs than non-severe cases, with marked cost escalation when complications or ICU care are required across both high- and middle-income settings [21, 25–29].

At the same time, hospitalization cost is not a direct readout of guideline-defined physiologic severity and can be shaped by local pricing, payment arrangements, and institutional practice patterns. Importantly, in our hospital, escalation to high-intensity interventions is guided by the overall clinical picture and guideline-based severity assessment rather than any single admission indicator (e.g., consolidation alone does not mandate bronchoscopy or systemic corticosteroids). Therefore, we interpret “high cost” primarily as a high–resource-use phenotype and acknowledge that residual confounding related to treatment propensity and institutional practice may remain.

Based on this rationale, we first used a Gaussian mixture model (GMM) to characterize the right-skewed distribution of total hospitalization costs and to derive a data-driven definition of a high-cost subgroup. The resulting cut-point (7,139 RMB), obtained at the intersection of the two fitted component density curves, reflects the charging structure and cost distribution of this cohort and is not intended as a universal “high-cost” threshold across institutions. Rather, it represents a cohort-specific, reproducible framework that can be re-applied and recalibrated in other settings to identify local high–resource-use subgroups.

Using this GMM-derived definition, we then examined whether the high-cost subgroup aligned with greater clinical burden and resource utilization. Consistent with a higher disease-burden phenotype, the high-cost group had a markedly longer length of stay, and radiographic consolidation, BAL, and systemic corticosteroid use were more frequent. Importantly, the high-cost group also exhibited admission-relevant features previously reported to be associated with more intense inflammation and tissue injury in severe or refractory MPP, including younger age and higher Tmax (within 24 h of admission) and LDH (Table 1 and 2). Taken together, these findings support interpreting high hospitalization cost as a high–resource-use phenotype that complements traditional severity classifications by capturing length of stay, procedures, treatment intensity, and complications-related care—thereby providing a clinically meaningful outcome for early risk prediction in the context of value-based care. With this cohort-specific definition, we built a four-variable logistic regression model with age, Tmax, LDH, and pulmonary consolidation to predict high hospitalization cost. The model showed good discrimination (AUC = 0.790), and after inclusion of an age × consolidation interaction term, the AUC rose to 0.838, while retaining a parsimonious predictor set [30, 31].

All four predictors are biologically plausible. LDH is a widely used marker of tissue injury and systemic inflammation, and elevated LDH has been repeatedly associated with severe or refractory MPP and greater pulmonary involvement [30, 32–34]. Tmax is likewise an independent predictor: higher peak fever was more common in the high-cost group, consistent with observations that children with severe or refractory MPP typically present with higher fever burdens than milder cases [30, 31, 34]. Radiographic consolidation is a classic feature of severe pediatric pneumonia, particularly when multilobar or extensive, and has been repeatedly associated with complicated disease, higher resource utilization, and poorer outcomes [31, 34, 35]. In our data, consolidation remained strongly associated with high cost across age strata, indicating that children with consolidation at any age tend to consume greater medical resources.

An important finding of this study is that the effects of age and pulmonary consolidation on hospitalization cost are not independent but interact. In our cohort, radiographic consolidation was present in all age groups and became more frequent with increasing age (from about 13% in children < 3 years to over 30% in those ≥ 6 years), in line with previous MPP reports showing that lung consolidation is common in school-aged children and often associated with more severe and prolonged disease courses [35–37]. At the same time, when age was analysed as a continuous variable, higher age was independently associated with a lower risk of high hospitalization cost. This pattern is consistent with broader pediatric CAP data indicating that younger children, particularly infants and toddlers, are at higher risk of severe pneumonia, adverse outcomes, and intensive resource use [9, 38–41]. Given this seemingly paradoxical pattern—age increasing the likelihood of consolidation, while age and consolidation show opposite individual associations with severity and high-intensity care—we introduced an age × consolidation interaction term, together with age-stratified and six-subgroup analyses (three age bands × presence/absence of consolidation), to examine their combined impact on hospitalization cost.

The stratified analyses showed that age influences not only the absolute levels of inflammatory response and cost, but also the incremental effect associated with the presence of consolidation. Overall, higher age was associated with higher absolute Tmax and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels, reflecting a more vigorous inflammatory response in more immunologically mature hosts. However, when comparing children with and without consolidation within each age stratum, a gradient pattern emerged: in the < 3-year group, the differences in CRP and peak temperature between consolidation-positive and consolidation-negative patients were the largest; in the 3–5-year and ≥ 6-year groups, these differences progressively diminished. In other words, among infants and toddlers, once consolidation develops, the immune response rises much more steeply relative to their non-consolidation peers—manifested as larger “jumps” in inflammatory markers—accompanied by longer hospital stays and higher costs; in older children, the relative increment attributable to consolidation is more modest. Consistently, across age strata, LDH was generally higher in consolidation-positive than consolidation-negative patients, in keeping with previous studies linking elevated LDH to more severe lung parenchymal injury and more extensive pulmonary involvement in pneumonia and acute lung injury [42, 43].

In the multivariable model, both younger age and radiographic consolidation remained significant predictors of high hospitalization cost, and adding the age × consolidation interaction term clearly improved model discrimination. The interaction results indicate that, among children with consolidation, predicted high-cost risk is highest in infants and young children and decreases with age, consistent with the age-gradient patterns in inflammatory markers and length of stay described above. In other words, the incremental impact of pulmonary consolidation on hospitalization cost and treatment intensity is substantially greater in younger children than in school-aged children: once infants and young children develop consolidation, they tend to cluster on a high-cost, high-intervention trajectory, whereas school-aged children with consolidation still belong to a higher–resource-use stratum, but the additional increase in resource consumption is comparatively modest. Together, these findings highlight the phenotype of “younger age plus consolidation” as a particularly high-risk, high–resource-use subgroup. This heterogeneity is not captured by the traditional severe/non-severe dichotomy and is not explicitly reflected in current MPP guidelines, underscoring the importance of explicitly accounting for age–consolidation interaction in future risk-stratification tools. Clinically, the model can be applied within the first 24 h of admission to estimate the probability of high-cost hospitalization and support early triage and resource planning. A two-panel nomogram (Supplementary Figure S1) enables bedside calculation, and clinicians may select sensitivity- or specificity-prioritized thresholds depending on whether the priority is to minimize missed high-cost cases or to limit false positives.

This study has limitations. The high-cost definition and the numeric threshold are inevitably shaped by regional economic context, insurance/charging structures, and local practice patterns, and thus primarily reflect our center’s current management and payment environment. In addition, this single-center cohort was assembled in 2024—a period of increased M. pneumoniae activity reported in several regions—so the observed seasonal pattern and case-mix may not generalize to non-epidemic years. Accordingly, external validation and, if needed, recalibration across centers and across years are warranted before broader application.

In summary, this study links GMM-based stratification of hospitalization costs with a parsimonious admission-time logistic model incorporating an age × consolidation interaction, integrating resource use and clinical features within a single analytical framework. In this context, high hospitalization cost is treated as a high–resource-use phenotype that may complement heterogeneous severity definitions when interpreted alongside clinical findings and prior evidence. The model highlights infants and young children with pulmonary consolidation as a subgroup with particularly elevated predicted risk. From an operational perspective, a higher predicted probability of high resource use at admission may support planning—anticipating longer length of stay, closer monitoring, earlier senior review, proactive family communication, and better alignment of staffing and bed management. Importantly, the model is intended to inform risk estimation and resource planning rather than to mandate specific therapies; external validation and prospective evaluation are needed before clinical implementation.
