Early rupture of an anterior communicating artery pseudoaneurysm after transsphenoidal surgery: A case report and clinical implications
Authors: Satoshi Shitara, Masayuki Yamagishi, Hisao Hirai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Intracranial pseudoaneurysm after transsphenoidal surgery (TSS) is rare, particularly in the anterior communicating artery (AcomA).Case Description: A 47-year-old female underwent extended TSS for a giant pituitary adenoma with gross-total resection. She developed a subarachnoid hemorrhage on the night of surgery. Angiography revealed a ruptured AcomA pseudoaneurysm, which was successfully treated with coil embolization. The patient achieved good recovery (modified Rankin Scale 1).Conclusion: Early postoperative hemorrhage after TSS should prompt early vascular imaging, as timely endovascular treatment can be life-saving.

INTRODUCTION

Intracranial pseudoaneurysm is a rare but potentially fatal complication following transsphenoidal surgery (TSS). Most vascular injuries involve the internal carotid artery (ICA) because of its proximity to the sellar region. In contrast, pseudoaneurysms arising from the anterior cerebral artery (ACA) or anterior communicating artery (AcomA) are extremely uncommon. We report a rare case of early rupture of an AcomA pseudoaneurysm following extended TSS and discuss its surgical implications.

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 47-year-old female presented with progressive visual disturbance. Neurological examination revealed bitemporal hemianopsia, and endocrinological evaluation was unremarkable.

Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated a 41-mm sellar and suprasellar mass consistent with a giant pituitary adenoma, compressing the optic chiasm and displacing the AcomA complex superiorly. Preoperative 3D computed tomography angiography showed no vascular abnormalities [Figure 1].

Preoperative and postoperative imaging findings. (a) Contrast-enhanced coronal magnetic resonance imaging demonstrating a giant pituitary adenoma with suprasellar extension. (b) Preoperative 3D computed tomography angiography showing no evidence of an aneurysm in the anterior communicating artery region. (c) Postoperative axial computed tomography scan obtained after neurological deterioration, demonstrating diffuse subarachnoid hemorrhage.

The tumor showed marked suprasellar extension beyond the tuberculum sellae, requiring an extended endoscopic transsphenoidal approach to achieve adequate exposure and safe dissection of the suprasellar component. Extended endoscopic TSS was performed, achieving gross-total resection without intraoperative cerebrospinal fluid leakage or significant bleeding. No unusual arterial bleeding, need for packing, or bipolar coagulation near the AcomA complex was encountered during surgery. No apparent vascular injury was recognized intraoperatively [Figure 2].

Endovascular treatment and postoperative imaging findings. (a) Magnified three-dimensional rotational angiography of the left internal carotid artery before coil embolization, demonstrating an irregularly shaped aneurysmal lesion arising from the anterior communicating artery. (b) Right internal carotid artery angiography immediately after coil embolization, demonstrating preserved visualization of the distal left A2 segment. (c) Plain radiograph obtained after completion of coil embolization, showing the coil mass at the aneurysm site. (d) Three-dimensional rotational angiography of the left internal carotid artery obtained 4 days after embolization, demonstrating complete obliteration of the pseudoaneurysm with preservation of the left A2 segment. (e) Plain computed tomography scan obtained after ventriculoperitoneal shunt placement, demonstrating no ventricular enlargement. (f) Plain computed tomography scan demonstrating the shunt catheter inserted through the right occipital horn.

On the night of surgery, the patient developed sudden impaired consciousness. Computed tomography revealed diffuse subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) with acute hydrocephalus. Emergency external ventricular drainage was performed.

Digital subtraction angiography identified a 6-mm pseudoaneurysm arising from the AcomA. During coil embolization, contrast extravasation was observed, indicating marked fragility of the aneurysmal wall. Endovascular coil embolization was successfully performed, achieving complete obliteration. Follow-up angiography confirmed durable occlusion [Figure 2].

The postoperative course was complicated by hydrocephalus requiring ventriculoperitoneal shunt placement. The patient gradually improved and was discharged with a modified Rankin Scale score of 1. Postoperatively, visual symptoms gradually improved during follow-up. At 6-month follow-up, she remained neurologically stable without recurrence [Figure 2].

DISCUSSION

Intracranial pseudoaneurysm is a rare but potentially fatal vascular complication following TSS. Although vascular injury occurs in approximately 0.4–1.1% of cases, the vast majority involve the cavernous or paraclinoid segments of the ICA, reflecting their close anatomical proximity to the sellar region.[1,7] In contrast, pseudoaneurysms involving distal anterior circulation vessels, such as the ACA and AcomA, are exceedingly rare, with only a limited number of cases reported in the literature.[2-5,8-10]

The present case is notable for several reasons. First, the pseudoaneurysm arose from the AcomA, which is an uncommon location compared with the ICA. Most vascular complications during TSS occur within the cavernous sinus, where the ICA is vulnerable to direct injury during sellar exposure or tumor resection.[11] In contrast, injury to the AcomA requires manipulation beyond the sellar confines, typically in the suprasellar cistern. This risk is particularly relevant in cases of giant pituitary adenomas requiring extended TSS, where tumor extension into the suprasellar space necessitates dissection near the optic apparatus and anterior circulation vessels.[8]

Second, the pseudoaneurysm ruptured acutely on the night of surgery, resulting in SAH. Most reported pseudoaneurysms following TSS present in a delayed fashion, typically days to weeks after surgery, reflecting gradual weakening of the arterial wall following partial injury.[5,8] Delayed presentations are thought to result from progressive disruption of the intima and media, leading to pseudoaneurysm formation and eventual rupture. In contrast, the early rupture observed in the present case suggests more substantial intraoperative vessel wall injury, possibly involving full-thickness damage with temporary tamponade that subsequently failed in the early postoperative period [Figure 3].

Several mechanisms may explain the AcomA pseudoaneurysm formation in the present case. Giant pituitary adenomas frequently exhibit significant suprasellar extension and may compress, displace, or adhere to adjacent vascular structures, including the ACA-AcomA complex.[6] During extended TSS, tumor debulking and capsule dissection may transmit traction forces to adherent vessels or their perforators. In addition, blind dissection in a narrow operative corridor may result in mechanical or thermal injury to the arterial wall. Even minimal injury can lead to pseudoaneurysm formation, as disruption of the intimal and medial layers allows blood to dissect into surrounding tissue, forming a fragile cavity prone to rupture.[12]

An alternative explanation is that a preexisting small saccular aneurysm may have been masked by compression from the giant adenoma and became hemodynamically apparent after tumor decompression. Although preoperative vascular imaging showed no aneurysm, small lesions may be overlooked, particularly on magnetic resonance angiography. However, the extremely early postoperative rupture, absence of definite preoperative radiological findings, and close anatomical relationship between the tumor and AcomA complex suggested the possibility of surgically related arterial wall injury and pseudoaneurysm formation. Because definitive histopathological confirmation was unavailable, the distinction between pseudoaneurysm and ruptured preexisting aneurysm could not be fully established.

The clinical presentation of pseudoaneurysms depends largely on their anatomical location. ICA pseudoaneurysms commonly present with delayed massive epistaxis due to their proximity to the sphenoid sinus.[11] In contrast, pseudoaneurysms of the ACA or AcomA are located intradurally and more frequently present with SAH, as observed in the present case.[2,9] This distinction is clinically important, as SAH occurring in the early postoperative period may initially be attributed to other causes, such as residual tumor bleeding or operative manipulation, potentially delaying appropriate vascular evaluation.

Prompt diagnosis and treatment are essential to prevent catastrophic rebleeding. Digital subtraction angiography remains the gold standard for diagnosis and allows for simultaneous therapeutic intervention. Endovascular treatment, including coil embolization, stent-assisted coiling, or flow diversion, has emerged as the preferred treatment modality due to its minimally invasive nature and high success rate.[2,9] In selected cases, parent vessel sacrifice or surgical intervention may be required. Early intervention is particularly critical in pseudoaneurysms, which lack a true vessel wall and carry a higher risk of rupture compared with true aneurysms.[2,9] In the present case, intraoperative contrast extravasation during coil embolization suggested marked fragility of the aneurysmal wall, a characteristic feature of pseudoaneurysms. This finding supported the possibility of surgically related pseudoaneurysm formation rather than rupture of a stable preexisting saccular aneurysm.

The early rupture observed in the present case suggests that the pseudoaneurysm may have formed intraoperatively as a result of partial arterial wall injury, with temporary tamponade by surrounding tumor or arachnoid structures. Following tumor removal, loss of this mechanical support and restoration of arterial pulsatile stress may have contributed to rapid expansion and early rupture. This mechanism is consistent with the fragile structure of pseudoaneurysms, which lack a true arterial wall and are prone to early rupture once structural support is lost.

This case highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for vascular injury in patients who develop acute neurological deterioration following extended TSS, particularly in cases involving giant pituitary adenomas with suprasellar extension. Early vascular imaging should be considered when unexpected postoperative hemorrhage occurs, even in the early postoperative period. Careful surgical technique, awareness of vascular anatomy, and prompt recognition of complications are essential to minimize morbidity and mortality associated with this rare but life-threatening condition. To our knowledge, early rupture of an AcomA lesion on the night of surgery following extended TSS has rarely been reported, highlighting the importance of early vascular evaluation in such cases.

CONCLUSION

AcomA pseudoaneurysm is a rare but life-threatening complication after extended TSS. Early postoperative neurological deterioration should prompt early vascular imaging, and timely endovascular treatment can result in favorable outcomes.
