Autophagy in the liver
Authors: Elijah Burks, Jenna Copes, Kelly Nguyen, Albert Tawil-Brown, Shengmin Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe liver plays a dynamic role in maintaining whole-body homeostasis through its control of nutrient metabolism, detoxification, and immune regulation. Autophagy, a conserved lysosomal degradation pathway, is central to these functions, enabling hepatocytes to adapt to fluctuations in nutrient availability, hormonal signals, and cellular stress. Hepatic autophagy is tightly regulated by nutrient and energy-sensing pathways, including AMPK, mTOR, the coordinated actions of insulin and glucagon, and transcriptional regulators TFEB, FOXO proteins, PPAR isoforms, FXR, and NRF2. Epigenomic mechanisms, chromatin remodeling complexes, and post-transcriptional regulators, such as microRNAs (miRNAs), RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), and liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS), further refine autophagy gene expression and autophagosome formation. In physiological conditions, autophagy maintains hepatocyte integrity by supporting lipid, carbohydrate, and protein turnover and by clearing damaged or excess organelles through selective pathways such as mitophagy, lipophagy, pexophagy, ER-phagy, and xenophagy. Autophagy dysfunction contributes to the development of various liver diseases, including metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD), cholestatic liver disease, liver fibrosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Understanding the diverse regulatory networks governing hepatic autophagy, along with the roles of autophagy in liver homeostasis, provides new opportunities for therapeutic intervention. This review summarizes existing findings on the role of autophagy in the liver, focusing on recent advances in the regulation of hepatic autophagy. It also highlights unresolved mechanisms and discusses how targeting autophagy may offer novel strategies for treating liver diseases.KEYWORDS: Autophagy, macroautophagy, liver homeostasis, liver disease, metabolism

Introduction

Obtaining energy and processing nutrients are essential for human survival; equally important is the ability to remove and recycle cellular components. Autophagy is a conserved catabolic pathway in which cytoplasmic materials are degraded by lysosomes and repurposed to support cellular maintenance and metabolic balance [1]. Three major forms of autophagy have been described: microautophagy, chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA), and macroautophagy.

Microautophagy involves the direct engulfment of cytoplasmic material by invaginations of the late endosomal or lysosomal membrane. This process can target selective substrates, including proteins with the pentapeptide motif (KFERQ) and nonselective bulk cytoplasmic content, and does not require the formation of autophagosomes. In addition to its degradative function, microautophagy contributes to immune surveillance by facilitating the delivery of endogenous antigens to lysosomal compartments for MHC class II presentation [2,3].

CMA is a selective process in which specific cytosolic proteins are recognized by molecular chaperones and directly translocated across the lysosomal membrane for degradation. Unlike other forms of autophagy, CMA does not involve vesicle formation. Instead, proteins containing a KFERQ-like motif are recognized by heat-shock proteins (HSPs) such as HSPA8/HSC70 and delivered to the lysosomal membrane for import and degradation [2].

In this review, we focus specifically on macroautophagy, hereafter referred to as autophagy. Autophagy is characterized by the sequestration of cytoplasmic cargo within double-membraned vesicles known as autophagosomes, which subsequently fuse with lysosomes to enable cargo degradation (Figure 1) [2,4]. Autophagy can occur in a nonselective manner, termed bulk autophagy, or through selective mechanisms that target specific substrates such as damaged organelles, protein aggregates, or invading pathogens. Beyond serving as the terminal degradative compartments, lysosomes function as a central regulatory hub for autophagy through nutrient-sensing pathways, particularly the mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1)-transcription factor EB (TFEB) axis, which coordinates autophagosome formation with lysosomal biogenesis and cellular metabolic status [2]. By recycling intracellular macromolecules and organelles, autophagy plays a fundamental role in maintaining cellular and organismal homeostasis under both basal and stress conditions. Following degradation, breakdown products are released back into the cytosol to support biosynthesis and energy production, particularly during nutrient deprivation [5].

Brief overview of the autophagy process and its key regulators.

Autophagy is a tightly regulated, multistep catabolic process comprising initiation, nucleation, elongation, and maturation. Autophagy initiation is regulated by the Unc-51-like autophagy-activating kinase 1 (ULK1) complex and autophagy-related gene 9 (ATG9). Phagophore nucleation requires the coordinated activity of class III phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3KC3), the DedA superfamily, and the ATG2-PROPPINs complex, which together promote membrane expansion and sequestration of cytoplasmic cargo. Subsequent phagophore elongation is driven by ATG conjugation systems, ultimately resulting in the formation of a double-membrane autophagosome. Autophagy is activated in response to physiological stressors such as fasting, exercise, and hypoxia. Under these conditions, the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) assists autophagy as a quality-control system that clears damaged proteins, lipids, and organelles [6]. In the liver, autophagy is especially important due to the hepatocyte’s role in metabolism, detoxification, and nutrient sensing. Hepatic autophagy regulates lipid, carbohydrate, and protein turnover; maintains mitochondrial, ER, and peroxisome quality; and contributes to immune homeostasis. Dysregulated autophagy has contributed to the pathogenesis of various liver diseases, including metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD), cholestatic liver disease, liver fibrosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [7–9].

Significant progress has been made in defining the core autophagy machinery, liver-specific mechanisms regulating autophagy, and the diverse roles of autophagy in liver diseases. In this narrative review, we summarize current findings on hepatic autophagy based on literature searches conducted in the Web of Science and PubMed databases for studies published through 2025. Preference was given to research conducted in the 21st century, except for foundational studies. Autophagy-related terms and section-specific keywords were used throughout the search process. Priority was given to studies published in English, including recent primary research, seminal mechanistic reports, and clinically relevant investigations related to hepatic autophagy regulation and liver disease. Reference lists from peer-reviewed publications were also examined and prioritized for inclusion. Non–peer-reviewed studies, preprints, and opinion pieces were excluded from the review. EndNote reference management software was used to organize and compile citations. We highlight recent advances in the regulation of hepatic autophagy, including epigenomic mechanisms, RNA-binding proteins, alternative splicing, microRNA-mediated regulation, and liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS), and discuss their relevance to liver physiology, disease pathogenesis, and therapeutic development.

Regulation of autophagy in the liver

Autophagy in the liver is governed by an integrated regulatory network that links nutrient availability, hormonal cues, and metabolic stress to changes in autophagic activity. In this section, we will discuss how nutrient and energy-sensing pathways, along with transcriptional regulators, modulate autophagy-related gene (ATG) expression and the autophagy process. We will also present recent evidence on post-transcriptional modulation of autophagy, including epigenomic mechanisms and LLPS.

Nutrient and energy-sensing pathways

Autophagy is tightly regulated by nutrient and energy availability, making nutrient-sensing pathways essential for maintaining hepatic metabolic balance. Because the liver responds rapidly to changes in feeding, fasting, and hormonal signaling, hepatocytes rely on these pathways to determine when cellular components should be recycled to sustain energy production and when autophagy should be suppressed to favor nutrient storage and anabolic growth. Insulin, glucagon, and the core regulators AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and mTOR have all been shown to contribute to the coordinated regulation of autophagy in the liver (Figure 2).

Insulin and glucagon

Cell proliferation and autophagy regulation are highly dependent on the availability of nutrients and growth factors, which determine cellular energy levels. Insulin and glucagon are two key hormones that regulate metabolism and blood glucose homeostasis through opposing effects on liver autophagy. In rats, malnourishment was associated with elevated basal insulin and reduced glucagon levels, which activated the mTORC1 pathway and suppressed hepatic autophagy [10]. Insulin further stimulates the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT) pathway, inhibiting forkhead box O (FOXO) transcription factors and enhancing mTORC1 activity, thereby reinforcing autophagy inhibition [10,11].

Conversely, during fasting, glucagon promotes hepatic autophagy to mobilize nutrients. Ruan et al. demonstrated that glucagon activates a calcium-dependent O-GlcNAc signaling pathway in hepatocytes, where calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII) phosphorylates O-GlcNAc transferase (OGT), which subsequently modifies and activates the unc-51-like autophagy-activating kinase 1 (ULK1) complex, enhancing AMPK-mediated phosphorylation and autophagic flux [12]. Together, these mechanisms illustrate how insulin and glucagon act in opposition, with insulin suppressing and glucagon stimulating autophagy, to maintain energy balance under fed and fasting conditions.

AMPK

AMPK functions as a cellular energy sensor that detects low-energy states characterized by elevated AMP levels, prompting the initiation of adaptive metabolic responses to restore ATP homeostasis. Other activators of AMPK include nutrient starvation and exposure to metabolic toxins, allowing cells to balance nutrient supply with energy demand [13]. Glucose availability is a primary determinant of cellular energy status. Under glucose deprivation, AMPK can be activated through an AMP/ADP-independent mechanism that senses reduced levels of the glycolytic intermediate fructose-1,6-bisphosphate. In this pathway, aldolase acts as a metabolic sensor linking glucose availability to AMPK activation [14].

Autophagy is strongly induced during nutrient deprivation, and AMPK serves as a central positive regulator of this process. AMPK promotes autophagy by phosphorylating ULK1 at Ser1548, thereby facilitating autophagosome initiation [15]. In parallel, AMPK inhibits mTORC1, a key regulator of cell growth and a major suppressor of autophagy, via phosphorylation of mTORC1 regulatory components [16,17]. Suppression of mTORC1 signaling permits activation of transcriptional regulators such as TFEB, which coordinates the expression of genes involved in autophagy and lysosomal biogenesis [14,18,19]. Through these mechanisms, AMPK enhances catabolic processes, including glucose utilization and fatty acid β-oxidation, in part through autophagy-dependent substrate mobilization [20]. These pathways are particularly active in the liver, highlighting the tissue-specific importance of AMPK in modulating hepatic autophagy. The degradation products generated by AMPK-driven autophagy are subsequently released into the cytoplasm, where they support biosynthesis and energy production during metabolic stress [21].

mTOR

mTOR is a serine/threonine kinase that regulates cellular metabolism, growth, and autophagy through two distinct complexes, mTORC1 and mTORC2 [22].

mTORC1 is a nutrient-sensitive complex that promotes anabolic metabolism while inhibiting autophagy by integrating intracellular and extracellular cues [23]. Growth factors such as insulin activate mTORC1 through PI3K-AKT signaling. Activated AKT inhibits the tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC) and proline-rich AKT substrate of 40 kDa (PRAS40), leading to Ras homolog enriched in brain (RHEB)-dependent activation of mTORC1 [22]. Conversely, energy stress activates AMPK, which inhibits mTORC1 by phosphorylating regulatory-associated protein of mTOR (RAPTOR), while hypoxia further suppresses mTORC1 through the regulated in development and DNA damage response 1 (REDD1)-mediated activation of TSC2 [22]. Amino acid availability also tightly regulates mTORC1 by activating Rag GTPases to recruit mTORC1 to the lysosomal surface for activation by RHEB, whereas amino acid deprivation induces hepatic autophagy to replenish intracellular amino acid pools [22,24,25]. Upon its activation, mTORC1 inhibits autophagy by suppressing the ULK kinase complex through phosphorylation of ULK1 and ATG13, thereby preventing autophagosome initiation under nutrient-rich conditions [26]. Additionally, the TOR-autophagy spatial coupling compartment (TASCC) facilitates localized mTORC1 activation by autophagy-derived amino acids, enabling coordinated protein degradation and synthesis [27]. In a mouse ALD model, chronic ethanol feeding robustly activated mTORC1, whereas mTORC2 activity, assessed by AKT Ser473 phosphorylation and the expression of rapamycin-insensitive companion of mTOR (RICTOR), was unchanged or modestly reduced, indicating preferential engagement of mTORC1 in certain liver diseases [28].

mTORC2 functions as a key mediator of insulin-dependent nutrient signaling in the liver. mTORC2 phosphorylates AKT at Ser473 in response to insulin, enabling downstream regulation of hepatic glucose and lipid metabolism. Liver-specific deletion of mTORC2 leads to constitutive gluconeogenesis, impaired glycolysis, and reduced lipogenesis, indicating defective sensing of the fed state. Mechanistically, mTORC2-mediated AKT activation promotes glucokinase expression and sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1c (SREBP1c) processing, thereby coordinating glucose utilization with lipid synthesis [29].

Emerging evidence suggests that mTORC2 activity is temporally regulated during nutrient stress. In fibroblasts, prolonged starvation and sustained autophagy suppress mTORC1 while permitting reactivation of mTORC2, as indicated by restored AKT Ser473 phosphorylation. Genetic or pharmacologic inhibition of autophagy prevents mTORC2 reactivation, suggesting that autophagy may be an upstream regulator of mTORC2 signaling. These findings position mTORC2 as a stress-adaptive signaling node that links prolonged nutrient deprivation to AKT-dependent transcriptional responses rather than acute anabolic growth [30].

Transcriptional regulators

Autophagy is modulated not only by upstream nutrient and hormone signaling, but also by a series of transcription factors, including TFEB, FOXO proteins, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) isoforms, farnesoid X receptor (FXR), and nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2). TFEB

TFEB regulates lysosomal function and autophagy and is a member of the microphthalmia/transcription factor E (MiT/TFE) family of basic helix-loop-helix transcription factors. TFEB coordinates the expression of genes involved in autophagosome formation, lysosomal biogenesis, and substrate degradation, thereby integrating cellular clearance with metabolic adaptation [31]. Genome-wide studies have identified a TFEB-controlled transcriptional network, often referred to as the CLEAR (Coordinated Lysosomal Expression and Regulation) gene program, which includes numerous autophagy and lysosome-related genes essential for maintaining cellular homeostasis [31].

TFEB activity is tightly regulated by nutrient and energy status through post-translational modifications that control its subcellular localization. Under nutrient-rich conditions, mTORC1 phosphorylates TFEB at key serine residues, promoting the interaction of TFEB with 14-3-3 proteins and retention in the cytoplasm at the lysosomal surface. In contrast, nutrient deprivation, lysosomal stress, or inhibition of mTORC1 leads to TFEB dephosphorylation and nuclear translocation, where it activates transcription of autophagy and lysosomal genes [31]. In addition to mTORC1, TFEB is modulated by other signaling pathways, including AMPK, AKT, extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK), and calcium-dependent calcineurin signaling, highlighting TFEB as a convergence point for multiple metabolic and stress-responsive pathways [31].

In the liver, TFEB plays a critical role in metabolic regulation beyond its canonical function in autophagy. Hepatic TFEB activity is induced during fasting and contributes to lipid metabolism by promoting lipophagy and regulating transcriptional programs involving peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator-1α (PGC-1α) and PPARα, thereby enhancing fatty acid oxidation and mitochondrial function [31]. Dysregulation of TFEB signaling has been implicated in liver pathologies, including MASLD and ALD, where impaired TFEB nuclear localization correlates with defective autophagy and lipid accumulation. Conversely, experimental activation of TFEB in murine models improves hepatic lipid clearance and attenuates liver injury, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target [31].

FOXOs

The FOXO transcription factors link nutrient and hormonal cues to gene programs that regulate fasting adaptation and autophagy [32]. When insulin levels fall, reduced AKT phosphorylation allows FOXO proteins to accumulate in the nucleus, activating genes involved in gluconeogenesis, oxidative stress resistance, and autophagy. Liver-specific FOXO1 knockout studies demonstrate that FOXO1 mediates insulin’s suppression of hepatic glucose production and regulates glucose utilization during fed-fasted transitions [33], while deletion of FOXO1/3/4 disrupts fasting responses and alters metabolic gene networks [34]. Under cellular stress, FOXO activity is further enhanced by sirtuin 2 (SIRT2), and FOXO directly interacts with ATG7 to stimulate autophagosome formation, linking FOXO signaling to autophagy machinery [35,36]. Together, these findings establish FOXO1/3/4 as key transcriptional regulators that activate hepatic autophagy in response to nutrient deprivation and metabolic stress.

PPARs

PPARs are ligand-activated nuclear receptors that regulate lipid metabolism, inflammation, and autophagy in a tissue and isoform-specific manner [37]. Among the three major isoforms (PPARα, PPARδ, and PPARγ), PPARα is most highly expressed in metabolically active, oxidative tissues, including the liver, kidneys, heart, and intestines [37]. PPARα is activated during fasting, when circulating fatty acids rise, and directly induces the expression of multiple ATGs, including microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (Lc3), Atg7, Beclin 1 (Becn1), and peroxisomal biogenesis factor 14 (Pex14), thereby promoting autophagic flux and lipid degradation through lipophagy [38]. Pharmacological activation of PPARα is sufficient to induce autophagy even in the fed state, whereas fasting-induced autophagy is significantly blunted in PPARα-deficient mice, demonstrating that PPARα is required for the full autophagic response to nutrient deprivation [38]. In contrast, during the refed state, FXR suppresses hepatic autophagy, in part by competing with PPARα for binding to shared response elements within autophagy gene promoters and recruiting transcriptional corepressors [38,39]. This reciprocal regulation established a temporal switch, whereby PPARα promotes autophagy during fasting, while FXR represses autophagy upon nutrient repletion. Together, these studies indicate PPARα as a key transcriptional activator of autophagy under physiological fasting conditions and highlight nuclear receptor competition as a mechanism for fine-tuning hepatic autophagy across metabolic states.

In addition to PPARα, evidence also shows that PPARδ regulates hepatic autophagy under conditions of increased energy demand. Activation of PPARδ enhances autophagic flux in hepatocytes and mouse liver, as demonstrated by LC3-II formation, autophagosome maturation, and lysosomal degradation of lipid droplets. Mechanistically, PPARδ promotes autophagy through activation of AMPK and suppression of mTOR signaling, thereby coupling autophagy to fatty acid oxidation. Importantly, genetic or pharmacologic inhibition of autophagy abolishes PPARδ-mediated lipid clearance, indicating that autophagy is required for PPARδ-driven metabolic adaptation [40].

In contrast, PPARγ is most abundant in adipose tissue but is also expressed in hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), where it plays a major antifibrotic role. Activation of PPARγ reduces HSC activation and fibrosis by suppressing TFEB-mediated autophagy and altering lipid droplet dynamics. In high-fat and high-cholesterol diet murine models, PPARγ agonists such as rosiglitazone reduce liver fibrosis and improve liver function through this HSC-specific autophagy mechanism [41].

Together, the distinct expression patterns and regulatory actions of PPARα, PPARδ, and PPARγ highlight their isoform-specific contributions to hepatic metabolism, autophagy, and liver disease.

FXR

FXR is a bile acid-activated nuclear receptor highly expressed in the liver and intestines, where it regulates bile acid, lipid, and glucose metabolism. The expression of cholesterol 7 α-hydroxylase (CYP7A1), the rate-limiting enzyme in the classic pathway of bile acid synthesis, is tightly regulated by FXR through induction of the small heterodimer partner (SHP) in the liver. FXR is also the main regulator of fibroblast growth factor 19 (FGF19 in humans; the mouse homolog is FGF15) in the intestine [42,43]. Activation of FGF15/19 has also been shown to inhibit TFEB, thereby reducing autophagy [43]. In contrast, CYP7A1 itself negatively regulates the mTOR pathway, promoting autophagy when FXR activity is low [44].

FXR can repress autophagy through direct transcriptional mechanisms. FXR binds to the promoters of several key ATGs and inhibits their expression during the fed state [38,39]. FXR competes with PPARα for binding to shared DR1 motifs on these promoters, exerting opposing effects on autophagy. Moreover, bile acid-activated FXR disrupts the cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB)/CREB-regulated transcription coactivator 2 (CRTC2) complex, forming a repressive transcriptional complex that silences autophagy genes such as Ulk1 and Tfeb, establishing an FXR-CREB axis that acts as a nutrient-sensitive switch to inhibit autophagy independently of mTOR signaling [39]. Finally, FXR has been shown to suppress autophagy by inducing the expression of RUBICON (RUBCN), a negative regulator of autophagosome maturation [45]. Together, these findings demonstrate that FXR suppresses autophagy through multiple coordinated mechanisms, integrating bile acid signaling, transcriptional repression, and lysosomal inhibition in the liver.

NRF2

NRF2 is a transcription factor responsible for regulating the body’s antioxidant and anti-inflammatory responses. Under homeostatic conditions, NRF2 undergoes ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation mediated by the Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1)-Cullin 3 (CUL3) E3 ubiquitin ligase complex; however, under oxidative stress, KEAP1 is modified to prevent the degradation of NRF2 [46]. This allows NRF2 to induce antioxidant and detoxification genes through interaction with antioxidant response elements. NRF2 activity can be regulated by sequestosome 1 (P62/SQSTM1), a classic autophagy substrate. P62 competes with NRF2 for the KEAP1 binding site, thereby preventing NRF2 degradation; this effect is enhanced when P62 is phosphorylated at Ser351 [46]. Intriguingly, NRF2 can also induce P62 expression and further enhance the NRF2-P62 positive feedback loop [47].

NRF2 activation has been linked to enhanced autophagy in several studies. NRF2 directly modulates the expression of key ATGs [48]. In the liver, autophagy is induced following drug-induced liver injury (DILI), likely through NRF2 activation [49,50]. The expression levels of NRF2 are upregulated in patients with MASLD and in diet-induced obese mice. Nrf2-depletion promotes hepatic lipid accumulation in mice following high-fat diet feeding, partially due to attenuated autophagic flux and impaired fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes [51]. Taken together, these findings suggest a bidirectional relationship between NRF2 signaling and autophagy in the liver under stress conditions.

DNA methylation

DNA methylation is an important epigenetic mechanism that often occurs within promoter regions, where it can repress transcription of ATGs [54]. In the liver, promoter hypermethylation has been shown to directly suppress autophagy. In a mouse model of MASLD, Kupffer cells displayed increased DNA methylation at the promoter regions of Lc3b, tgAtg5, and tgAtg7 [55]. This epigenetic modification was primarily driven by elevated expression of DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1). Hypermethylation of these promoters led to reduced expression of ATG genes and impaired autophagic flux. In contrast, pharmacological inhibition or genetic knockdown of DNMT1 restored autophagy in Kupffer cells.

Additional evidence from non-hepatic systems further supports a central role for DNA methylation in the regulation of autophagy. Promoter hypermethylation-mediated silencing of LC3A variant 1 suppresses autophagy and accelerates gastric cancer progression [56]. Conversely, hypomethylation of the BECN1 promoter enhances autophagic activity in breast cancer cells [57]. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that DNA methylation of ATG promoters is a conserved mechanism for modulating autophagic flux across diverse physiological and pathological contexts.

Histone modifications

Histone modifications are covalent chemical alterations of histone proteins that influence gene expression by modulating chromatin structure, thereby regulating the accessibility of DNA promoter regions to transcription factors [58]. Histone modifications play critical roles in the pathogenesis of liver diseases, such as MASLD [59]. Well-studied modifications include histone methylation and acetylation. Several more recently identified modifications have also been reported, including lactylation, citrullination, crotonylation, succinylation, SUMOylation, propionylation, butyrylation, 2-hydroxyisobutyrylation, and 2-hydroxybutyrylation [58]. Here, we will focus primarily on how histone methylation and acetylation are involved in the regulation of autophagy in the liver.

Methylation/Demethylation

Histone methylation and demethylation are modifications in which one or more methyl groups are added to or taken from lysine (K) or arginine (R) residues of histones [60]. Evidence has shown that histone methylation is a key regulator of autophagy, with specific repressive marks suppressing transcription of ATGs [54]. A previous study showed that the downregulation of cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) expression contributes to homocysteine-induced autophagy and liver injury [61]. This downregulation of CFTR is regulated by the interaction between DNMT1 and enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2), which regulate DNA methylation and histone H3 lysine 27 trimethylation (H3K27me3), respectively. In fatty acid-induced hepatocellular lipotoxicity, activated mTORC1 increases the levels of the euchromatic histone lysine methyltransferase 2 (EHMT2 or G9a) and suppresses autophagy [62]. Mechanistically, this decrease in autophagy is associated with elevated levels of the EHMT2-mediated repressive histone H3 lysine 9 dimethylation (H3K9me2) mark and reduced RNA polymerase II occupancy at the promoter regions of Becn1 and Atg7. Coactivator-associated arginine methyltransferase 1 (CARM1) regulates histone H3 demethylation at arginine 17 (H3R17me2). Genome-wide analyses reveal that CARM1 exerts transcriptional co-activator function on ATG and lysosomal genes through TFEB [63,64]. Besides methyltransferases, several histone demethylases have been reported to be involved in autophagy regulation [65]. In mouse models, a previous study showed that lysine-specific demethylase 3A (KDM3A) plays an important role in starvation-induced autophagy in the liver by reducing levels of histone H3 lysine 9 dimethylation (H3K9me2) during fasting [66]. Together, current evidence suggests that histone methylation and demethylation play critical roles in regulating autophagy in the liver.

Acetylation/Deacetylation

Histone acetylation and deacetylation are post-translational modifications involving the addition or removal of acetyl groups on histone lysine residues, thereby modulating chromatin compaction and transcriptional accessibility [60]. Histone acetylation is regulated by two classes of enzymes: lysine acetyltransferases (KATs), which catalyze the transfer of an acetyl group to a lysine residue, and lysine deacetylases (KDACs), which catalyze the removal of acetyl groups. KDACs are further divided into two subgroups: Zn2+-dependent histone deacetylases (HDACs) and NAD+-dependent SIRTs [67]. Classical HDACs require a catalytic Zn2+ ion in their active site. Zn2+ coordinates the carbonyl oxygen of the acetyl-lysine substrate and activates a water molecule that performs a nucleophilic attack to hydrolyze the acetyl group from lysine residues. In contrast, sirtuins use NAD+ as a co-substrate for deacetylation. Both histone acetylation and deacetylation have been shown to regulate the expression of ATG genes and contribute to the development of human diseases [68–71].

Although limited studies on the interaction of KATs and autophagy in the liver have been reported, several studies have shown evidence of autophagy regulation by KDACs in liver diseases. A previous study showed that HDAC9 is highly expressed in mice and patients with MASLD and is positively correlated with liver enzyme levels and serum lipid concentrations in patients with MASLD [72]. Silencing HDAC9 reduced the severity of fibrosis and inflammation and restored autophagy. Mechanistically, HDAC9 was shown to decrease histone H3 lysine 9 acetylation (H3K9ac) at the promoter region of ATG4B, a key autophagy regulator, thereby suppressing ATG4B transcription and impairing autophagy in MASLD. Histone acetylation has been well studied in HCC [73]. HDAC inhibitors have been shown to promote epithelial–mesenchymal transition in HCC via AMPK-FOXO1-ULK1 axis-mediated autophagy [74], indicating a link between histone acetylation and autophagy in HCC.

In addition to HDACs, SIRTs have also been shown to contribute to liver diseases by modulating autophagy, although not directly through their roles in histone deacetylation. For example, SIRT1 restores impaired autophagy through deacetylation of BECN1 [75] in ALD and LC3 in HepG2 cells [76]. Overexpression of SIRT2 [77], SIRT3 [78], and SIRT7 [79] can also modulate autophagy. However, whether SIRTs induce or suppress autophagy in the liver is highly context-dependent, and the underlying mechanisms require further investigation.

Post-transcriptional regulation of ATGs

MicroRNAs

miRNAs are small, non-coding RNAs that regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally by binding complementary sequences (typically in the 3′ untranslated regions) of target mRNAs, causing translational repression or mRNA degradation. In the liver, this layer of control helps cells rapidly tune autophagy by adjusting ATG abundance and key nutrient/energy-sensing pathways in response to metabolic cues and stress [80].

In addition to directly targeting ATGs, miRNAs can regulate autophagy indirectly by acting “upstream” on signaling nodes that feed into the autophagy machinery. For example, in HSCs, miR-16 suppresses autophagy by inhibiting G protein alpha subunit 12 (Gα12), a factor that promotes c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK)-dependent ATG12-ATG5 conjugation. When miR-16 levels are reduced, autophagy increases, HSC activation is enhanced, and fibrosis is promoted, indicating cell-type-specific miRNA-autophagy coupling in the liver [81]. Beyond canonical cytoplasmic repression, non-canonical nuclear miRNA activity has also been described. In macrophages, intranuclear miR-204-3p activates ULK1 transcription, boosts vacuolar protein sorting 34 (VPS34) complex activity, and improves autophagic flux, thereby damping inflammatory signaling and slowing steatohepatitis progression [82].

It was also proven that autophagy activation in HSCs is linked to vesicular release of miR-29a, and that inhibiting autophagy reduces miR-29a secretion and alters collagen-related outputs, connecting autophagy status to extracellular miRNA signaling during fibrogenic activation [83]. Another study found hepatocyte miR-33 deletion affected AMPK-ULK1 signaling and increased autophagy-associated markers while improving MASLD/MASH features in vivo, reinforcing that miRNAs can shift hepatic nutrient/energy signaling into or away from an autophagic state [84].

Finally, miRNAs can disrupt the coordination between autophagy initiation and completion by simultaneously targeting pathway initiation signals and lysosomal fusion capacity. In ALD models, miR-155 suppresses mTOR signaling (via mTOR/RHEB) while also reducing lysosomal membrane proteins (LAMP1/2), which are required for autophagosome-lysosome fusion. This dual inhibition results in the accumulation of LC3-II and P62, consistent with impaired autophagic flux, and diverts undegraded cargo toward exosome release. Together, these findings support the existence of a miRNA-controlled autophagy-lysosome-exosome axis that may amplify inflammatory signaling in liver disease [85].

RNA-binding proteins

RBPs form ribonucleoprotein complexes that regulate all stages of RNA metabolism, including splicing, stability, localization, and translation. Through these functions, RBPs exert broad control over gene expression programs, and dysregulation of RNA processing has been increasingly linked to altered autophagy and disease pathogenesis [86,87]. Emerging evidence suggests that RBPs not only serve as selective cargo for autophagic degradation but also act as direct modulators of autophagy initiation and flux by regulating the expression of ATGs (Figure 5).

Several studies have demonstrated the role of RBPs in liver cells and liver diseases. The RBP human antigen R (HuR, encoded by the ELAVL1 gene) is required for efficient autophagosome formation in liver cells by binding to the 3′ untranslated regions of ATG5, ATG12, and ATG16 mRNAs, thereby facilitating their translation. HuR expression positively correlates with ATG protein levels in HCCs, suggesting that aberrant HuR-mediated ATG translation may contribute to dysregulated autophagy in liver cancer [88]. In MASLD, lipotoxicity induces upregulation of the methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3), leading to increased N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification of autophagy-related transcripts. METTL3 suppresses autophagic flux through stabilization of RUBCN, a negative regulator of autophagosome-lysosome fusion. Knockdown of METTL3 restores autophagy and enhances lipid droplet clearance, linking RNA methylation to autophagy inhibition in metabolic liver disease [89]. In HSCs, the RBP tristetraprolin (TTP, encoded by ZFP36) modulates autophagy at the level of mRNA stability. TTP binds AU-rich elements within the 3′ untranslated region of ATG16-like 1 (ATG16L1) mRNA, promoting its decay and suppressing autophagy. In this context, inhibition of autophagy enhances ferroptosis resistance and contributes to stellate cell activation and liver fibrosis, identifying TTP-dependent ATG16L1 regulation as a pathogenic axis [90].

Emerging evidence has shown that alternative RNA splicing plays a critical role in the development of liver diseases [91–96]. RBPs involved in pre-mRNA splicing also contribute to autophagy regulation, as demonstrated in studies using cancer cells [97–99]. In animal models and human patients with MASLD/MASH, the expression of the spliceosome component ubiquitin-specific peptidase 39 (USP39) is downregulated in the liver. Hepatocyte-specific deletion of USP39 in mice leads to increased lipid accumulation, spontaneous steatosis, and impaired autophagy [100]. Mechanistically, USP39 regulates the alternative splicing of several Atgs and heat shock transcription factor 1 (Hsf1), resulting in their reduced expression. These findings indicate that USP39-mediated alternative splicing is required to sustain autophagy and lipid homeostasis in the liver.

Liquid–liquid phase separation

LLPS is a biophysical process in which biomolecules separate from the surrounding cytoplasm or nucleoplasm to form dynamic condensates without membranes that concentrate specific proteins and RNAs [101,102]. These condensates enable rapid molecular exchange, spatial organization, and reversible assembly in response to cellular conditions. Beyond its established roles in transcriptional regulation and nuclear organization, LLPS has emerged as a fundamental mechanism coordinating multiple stages of autophagy, including initiation, elongation, and selective cargo clearance [103–105]. LLPS in autophagy initiation and phagophore assembly

Autophagy initiation occurs at the phagophore assembly site (PAS), which behaves as a liquid-like condensate formed through LLPS of core autophagy proteins. A seminal study demonstrated that the autophagy-initiating ULK1 (Atg1 in yeast) complex undergoes phase separation to form dynamic droplets both in vitro and in vivo; mutations or phosphorylation events that impair LLPS disrupt PAS formation and block autophagosome biogenesis [106]. Phase separation allows rapid and reversible organization of the PAS in response to nutrient availability, providing a mechanistic explanation for how autophagy can rapidly switch on or off during fasting and refeeding [106,107].

A recent study in yeast further revealed that early PAS droplets selectively enrich the Atg12-Atg5-Atg16 E3 ligase complex through specific multivalent interactions, promoting efficient Atg8 lipidation while simultaneously limiting Atg4-mediated de-lipidation [108]. This dual function establishes the PAS as a catalytic hub that both accelerates autophagosome membrane expansion and protects newly lipidated Atg8 from premature removal. Together, these studies establish LLPS as an active organizer of autophagy initiation rather than a passive consequence of protein clustering.

Cargo-driven phase separation in selective autophagy

LLPS also governs selective autophagy by organizing initiation hubs directly on cargo surfaces. Autophagy biogenesis factors and cargo engage in multivalent, low-affinity interactions that drive phase separation into dynamic condensates, which subsequently mature into sites of phagophore nucleation [109]. Importantly, excessively strong receptor–cargo interactions inhibit phase separation and block autophagy, highlighting the need for finely tuned interaction strengths. These initiation hubs form not only in yeast, but also in mammalian cells, where they establish physical connections with the ER to support phagophore biogenesis [109]. These findings indicate that LLPS acts as a conserved trigger for both bulk and selective autophagy across species.

Among selective autophagy receptors, P62 is one of the best-characterized LLPS-driven factors. P62 forms ubiquitin-dependent liquid droplets in vivo with hallmark liquid properties, including fusion and fluorescence recovery after photobleaching [110]. Polyubiquitin chains, particularly K63-linked chains, contribute to P62 phase separation through multivalent P62-ubiquitin interactions, thereby concentrating ubiquitinated cargo for autophagic degradation. Post-translational modifications, including phosphorylation, modulate P62 phase behavior, while disease-associated P62 mutations disrupt condensate formation and cargo segregation [110].

LLPS and autophagy in the liver

Dysregulated LLPS has emerged as a contributor to altered lipid metabolism in the liver. In steatotic liver disease, LLPS of lipid droplet-associated proteins, such as hydroxysteroid 17-β dehydrogenase 13 (HSD17B13), together with stress granule components, including Ras GTPase-activating protein-binding protein 1 (G3BP1), coordinate lipid storage and cellular stress responses. Impairment of these condensates promotes lipid buildup and inflammation [111,112].

The interaction between LLPS and autophagy in the context of liver dysfunction has also been studied in transgenic mice with autophagy deficiency. P62 condensates can activate antioxidant signaling by sequestering KEAP1, thereby stabilizing NRF2 and inducing cytoprotective gene expression [113,114]. In the liver, disruption of autophagy leads to pathological accumulation of P62 condensates, excessive NRF2 activation, organelle buildup, and hepatomegaly [114,115]. Genetic uncoupling of P62-KEAP1 interactions suppresses NRF2 hyperactivation and markedly ameliorates liver injury under autophagy-deficient conditions, demonstrating the in vivo relevance of LLPS-regulated P62 signaling in liver pathology [115].

Collectively, these studies suggest that LLPS can be a critical mechanism in the autophagy process and in liver diseases associated with dysregulated autophagy. Further investigation of LLPS and autophagy in the liver may reveal potential therapeutic targets for liver diseases.

Roles of autophagy in the liver under physiological conditions

Autophagy is essential for maintaining liver homeostasis under physiological conditions by supporting metabolic flexibility and cellular quality control. In hepatocytes, autophagy regulates the turnover of lipids, proteins, and organelles, enabling adaptation to changing nutrient availability and preventing the accumulation of damaged cellular components. Through these functions, hepatic autophagy integrates intracellular recycling with whole-body energy balance and metabolic homeostasis [116].

Lipid metabolism

Lipids are essential macromolecules that serve as long-term energy reserves and support cellular and metabolic homeostasis. In the liver, lipid turnover is tightly regulated by autophagy, particularly under conditions of nutrient deprivation. During starvation, autophagy is activated to deliver intracellular proteins, organelles, and lipid stores to lysosomes for degradation, thereby generating alternative energy sources [117]. Triglycerides stored within lipid droplets are sequestered into autophagosomes and degraded in lysosomes to release free fatty acids for β-oxidation, a process termed macrolipophagy [117]. Disruption of autophagy during starvation leads to hepatic lipid accumulation and increased steatosis, underscoring its essential role in maintaining intracellular lipid balance [117].

Beyond its role in energy mobilization, autophagy is critical for preserving hepatic lipid homeostasis and preventing pathological structural changes. In a murine study using siRNAs against Atg5 or tgAtg7 to inhibit autophagy, impaired lipophagy resulted in HSC activation, depletion of intracellular lipid droplets, and progression toward liver fibrosis [118]. These findings highlight that proper autophagic regulation is required not only for lipid turnover, but also for maintaining liver architecture and preventing fibrotic disease.

Recent studies have identified selective lipophagy receptors that mediate lipid droplet recognition by the autophagy machinery. The lipid transfer protein oxysterol-binding protein-related protein 8 (ORP8) localizes to lipid droplets and facilitates their encapsulation by autophagosome membranes, functioning as a lipophagy receptor in hepatocytes [119]. ORP8 activity is regulated by AMPK-dependent phosphorylation, which enhances its interaction with LC3 and GABA type A receptor-associated proteins (GABARAPs). Genetic deletion of ORP8 or disruption of its LC3/GABARAP-binding capacity leads to lipid droplet and triglyceride accumulation, demonstrating its essential role in hepatic lipid metabolism [119]. Similarly, ATG14 has been implicated as an additional selective factor that contributes to lipid droplet recognition and degradation during lipophagy [120].

In addition to direct lipid droplet degradation, autophagy regulates hepatic lipid metabolism through transcriptional control of fatty acid oxidation pathways. During fasting, autophagy promotes the degradation of nuclear corepressor 1 (NCOR1) through interaction with GABARAP family proteins, thereby relieving repression of PPARα target genes. When autophagy is impaired, NCOR1 accumulates, resulting in reduced PPARα activity and diminished hepatic β-oxidation [121]. Together, these mechanisms demonstrate that autophagy coordinates lipid storage, mobilization, and transcriptional regulation to maintain hepatic lipid homeostasis under physiological conditions.

Carbohydrate metabolism

The liver is a central organ for maintaining systemic glucose homeostasis, coordinating glucose uptake, storage, and release to meet whole-body energy demands. Hepatic glycogen serves as the primary storage form of glucose and provides a critical energy reserve during fasting and acute metabolic stress [122]. Disruption of hepatic glucose and glycogen metabolism is a hallmark of insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes, underscoring the importance of tightly regulated carbohydrate turnover under physiological conditions [122,123].

In the fasted state, glucagon signaling promotes glycogen breakdown to maintain blood glucose levels. Ultrastructural and biochemical studies on newborn rats demonstrated that glucagon stimulates autophagic degradation of glycogen in hepatocytes, particularly in the immediate postnatal period when energy demand is high, and nutrient availability is limited [124]. These studies demonstrated the presence of glycogen within autophagic vacuoles alongside an increase in lysosomal α-glucosidase activity, supporting a role for lysosomal pathways in glycogen degradation during starvation [124].

Beyond bulk autophagy, hepatic glycogen can be selectively degraded through glycophagy, a specialized form of autophagy dedicated to glycogen turnover. A key breakthrough in understanding glycophagy came with the identification of starch-binding domain-containing protein 1 (STBD1) as a proposed glycogen cargo receptor [125]. STBD1 binds preferentially to poorly branched or structurally abnormal glycogen and facilitates its delivery to lysosomes, suggesting a quality-control function that prevents the accumulation of aberrant glycogen species associated with glycogen storage diseases [126]. This selective routing of glycogen to lysosomes led to the formal designation of the pathway as “glycophagy.”

Emerging evidence indicates that glycophagy is dynamically regulated by nutrient- and glucose-sensing pathways. Glucose deprivation activates AMPK, a central energy sensor, which has been shown to interact bidirectionally with glycogen metabolism and autophagy, linking cellular energy status to glycogen flux [123]. More recent mechanistic studies, including work in non-mammalian vertebrate liver models, suggest that glycophagy is regulated by AKT-FOXO1 signaling in response to glucose availability, providing a molecular framework connecting insulin signaling, transcriptional control, and autophagic glycogen degradation [127]. Although these studies were performed outside mammalian systems, the conserved nature of AKT-FOXO1 signaling supports their relevance to hepatic carbohydrate metabolism.

Physiological relevance of glycophagy is further supported by disease models in which glycogen-directed autophagy is impaired. Intrauterine growth-restricted rat models with hyperinsulinemia exhibit defective hepatic glycogen autophagy, leading to abnormal glycogen accumulation and impaired glucose homeostasis [10]. Similarly, lysosomal enzymes such as acid α-glucosidase are essential for completing glycophagic flux, and defects in their maturation or trafficking compromise lysosomal glycogen degradation [128].

Collectively, these studies establish glycophagy as a physiologically relevant pathway that complements classical glycogenolysis to regulate hepatic carbohydrate metabolism. By selectively targeting glycogen for lysosomal degradation, autophagy enables hepatocytes to rapidly mobilize glucose during fasting and stress while maintaining glycogen quality and metabolic flexibility. Although the molecular machinery of glycophagy is still being defined, growing evidence suggests that dysregulation of this pathway contributes to metabolic disease, highlighting the importance of autophagy in hepatic carbohydrate homeostasis under physiological conditions [129].

Protein metabolism

Proteins are essential macromolecules that provide structural support, catalyze biochemical reactions, and regulate diverse physiological processes. In the liver, autophagy, specifically microautophagy, plays a central role in protein turnover, particularly during nutrient deprivation, by supplying amino acids to maintain systemic energy balance [116,130,131].

Hormonal and nutrient cues tightly regulate hepatic protein autophagy. Insulin acts as a dominant suppressor of autophagy through activation of the PI3K-AKT-mTORC1 signaling pathway, whereas fasting, amino acid deprivation, or insulin deficiency suppresses mTORC1 activity and promotes autophagic proteolysis [17,132]. In streptozotocin-treated rats, which exhibit markedly reduced circulating insulin, autophagic degradation of endogenous hepatic proteins increases by approximately 70% within 24 hours. This enhanced proteolysis is accompanied by expansion of autophagic vacuoles and elevated lysosomal hydrolase content, indicating robust activation of the lysosome-autophagy system [132]. Administration of insulin reverses these effects, underscoring its critical inhibitory role in hepatic protein autophagy.

At the cellular level, amino acid deprivation triggers structural remodeling of the rough ER in hepatocytes, leading to its sequestration and degradation through autophagy. In insulin-deficient and fasted states, ribosome-free regions of the RER invaginate to form autophagosomes that subsequently mature into autolysosomes, resulting in coordinated degradation of membrane-bound and luminal proteins [132]. This process contributes to both protein catabolism and remodeling of hepatic secretory capacity during prolonged nutrient stress.

Quantitative studies in perfused rat liver further demonstrate that autophagy accounts for a substantial fraction of hepatic proteolysis during graded amino acid deprivation, establishing autophagy as a primary pathway for protein degradation under physiological fasting conditions [133]. More recent work has shown that liver autophagy contributes directly to the maintenance of circulating amino acid pools, reinforcing its systemic metabolic role beyond intracellular quality control [134].

In addition to bulk protein degradation, selective forms of protein autophagy have been described. Autophagy contributes to mitochondrial protein turnover, accounting for approximately one-third of mitochondrial proteolysis under basal conditions, highlighting its importance in maintaining organelle proteostasis [135]. Specialized pathways such as proteophagy, the selective autophagic degradation of proteasomes, have also been identified in mammalian cells, including mechanisms that can operate independently of ATG5 and ATG7 [136,137]. Although these pathways aren’t well characterized in hepatocytes under physiological conditions, they underscore the versatility of autophagy in regulating protein quality and abundance.

Collectively, these findings establish hepatic autophagy as a central regulator of protein metabolism, integrating hormonal signals, nutrient availability, and cellular stress to control protein turnover. By supplying amino acids for energy production and gluconeogenesis, protein autophagy enables the liver to buffer systemic metabolic demands during fasting while preserving intracellular homeostasis under physiological conditions.

Organelle turnover

As discussed above, a central function of autophagy is to prevent the accumulation of toxic byproducts by recycling cellular components for biosynthesis and maintaining cellular homeostasis. Beyond its role in macronutrient metabolism, autophagy also preserves cellular function by selectively removing damaged, excess, or unnecessary organelles. This organelle-specific turnover occurs through distinct forms of selective autophagy, including mitophagy, which clears damaged mitochondria; lipophagy, which degrades lipid droplets; pexophagy, which mediates peroxisome turnover; reticulophagy/ER-phagy, which eliminates excess or stressed endoplasmic reticulum; and xenophagy, which removes intracellular pathogens [138]. These selective autophagy pathways are not liver-specific but rather represent broadly conserved cellular quality-control programs. Nevertheless, they can be important in the liver because of its high metabolic, detoxification, immune, and secretory demands.

Mitophagy

Given the central role of mitochondria in ATP production and hepatic metabolism, mitophagy is essential for maintaining mitochondrial quality control and cellular energy balance. Mitophagy is activated under stress conditions such as hypoxia, mitochondrial depolarization, and excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, all of which can occur in liver diseases including MASLD, ALD, and DILI.

The best-characterized mechanism is the PTEN-induced kinase 1 (PINK1)/Parkin RBR E3 ubiquitin protein ligase (PARKIN)-dependent pathway, which senses mitochondrial membrane depolarization. Under mitochondrial stress, PINK1 accumulates on the outer mitochondrial membrane and recruits PARKIN, an E3 ubiquitin ligase that ubiquitinates outer mitochondrial membrane proteins, thereby promoting recognition by autophagy receptors, LC3-positive autophagosome formation, and subsequent lysosomal degradation of damaged mitochondria. Through this pathway, mitophagy removes damaged or superfluous mitochondria, limits excessive ROS production, prevents the accumulation of oxidized mitochondrial proteins and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), and preserves hepatic metabolic homeostasis. The role of mitophagy in liver diseases has been comprehensively reviewed in previous publications [116,139].

In addition to the PINK1/PARKIN-dependent pathway, PINK1/PARKIN-independent mitophagy pathways also contribute to mitochondrial homeostasis. These include receptor-mediated mechanisms involving proteins such as BNIP3, NIX, FUNDC1, FKBP8, and BCL2L13, as well as alternative mitochondrial quality-control processes, including mitochondria-derived vesicles, mitochondrial-associated degradation, mitochondrial spheroid formation, mitocytosis, and autophagic secretion of mitochondria. These pathways may be particularly important under metabolic, hypoxic, or oxidative stress conditions.

Impaired mitophagy has been implicated in chronic liver diseases by allowing dysfunctional mitochondria to accumulate, thereby enhancing mitochondrial ROS production, mtDNA release, and downstream inflammatory signaling. In ALD, acute ethanol exposure can induce PARKIN-mediated mitophagy as an adaptive protective response, whereas chronic alcohol exposure impairs lysosomal function and may compromise mitochondrial clearance. In DILI, particularly acetaminophen-induced liver injury, PINK1/PARKIN-mediated mitophagy removes damaged mitochondria and limits hepatotoxicity, whereas loss of this pathway exacerbates liver injury. Defective mitophagy can also promote activation of innate immune pathways, including NLRP3/AIM2 inflammasome signaling and cGAS-STING activation, through increased mitochondrial ROS production and cytosolic or extracellular mtDNA release, leading to caspase-1 activation and production of IL-1β and IL-18. Collectively, these findings suggest that mitophagy serves as a critical checkpoint linking mitochondrial quality control, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses in liver disease, and pharmacological enhancement of mitophagy may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for MASLD, ALD, and DILI.

Pexophagy

Peroxisomes perform key metabolic functions, including β-oxidation of long-chain fatty acids, bile acid synthesis, and detoxification of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). Their quality is maintained through pexophagy, which eliminates damaged or excess peroxisomes. Autophagy can be induced by ROS, such as superoxide (O2•−) and H2O2 under nutrient-deprived conditions, linking oxidative stress to peroxisomal turnover [140]. Pexophagy reduces cellular ROS burden by removing nonfunctional peroxisomes and downregulating their numbers when oxidative demand is low [138]. The first direct evidence of mammalian pexophagy showed that Atg7-deficient mouse livers failed to degrade excess peroxisomes following peroxisome proliferation, while wild-type hepatocytes efficiently cleared them [141]. These findings confirm that autophagy is essential for peroxisome turnover and hepatic metabolic balance.

Lysophagy

Lysophagy functions similarly to other forms of selective autophagy but specifically targets damaged lysosomes to prevent the leakage of degradative enzymes and cellular injury. Upon lysosomal membrane damage, galectin-3 and LC3 are recruited to compromised lysosomes, initiating their removal [142]. Although studies on lysophagy remain limited, this mechanism is critical to maintaining lysosomal integrity and ensuring efficient cellular waste clearance.

Reticulophagy (ER-phagy)

The ER plays a vital role in protein synthesis, lipid metabolism, and detoxification. Reticulophagy (ER-phagy) removes excess or damaged ER to preserve protein-folding capacity and prevent stress-induced damage. ER stress, often triggered by elevated ROS or excessive protein synthesis, activates ER-phagy to eliminate misfolded or aggregated proteins, a process particularly relevant in alcohol-induced liver stress [138]. In mammals, ER-phagy facilitates the removal of excess hepatic ER after withdrawal of the hepatic mitogen 1,4-Bis[2-(3,5-dichloropyridyloxy)]benzene (TCPOBOP), with P62 identified as a receptor mediating this process [143]. In yeast, starvation and rapamycin treatment induce ER-phagy, in which ER whorls are engulfed and delivered to lysosomes via invagination of the vacuolar membrane, notably independent of the core autophagy machinery [144,145].

Xenophagy

Xenophagy targets invading pathogens or foreign entities such as iron complexes (ferritinophagy) for lysosomal degradation. In the liver, this process is crucial for innate immune defense, clearing intracellular bacteria and modulating inflammatory responses [146]. Xenophagy is characterized by key autophagy markers, including LC3 accumulation, P62 degradation, and colocalization of autophagosomal and lysosomal proteins, which can be distinguished from LC3-associated phagocytosis. Core autophagy proteins such as ATG5, ATG7, and ATG16L1 are essential for pathogen clearance, highlighting xenophagy’s role in maintaining hepatic immune homeostasis [146].

Roles of autophagy in the liver under disease conditions

Autophagy is finely tuned by metabolic and stress-related changes. Under physiological conditions, these pathways maintain hepatic homeostasis by balancing protein and organelle turnover, lipid metabolism, and detoxification. However, persistent metabolic stress, inflammation, or toxic insults can disrupt these pathways, leading to autophagy dysfunction that contributes to disease pathogenesis [147]. The following section will discuss alterations in autophagy across several common liver diseases and explore potential therapeutic strategies targeting autophagy.

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease

MASLD, formerly termed nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), represents the most prevalent cause of chronic liver disease worldwide. It encompasses a clinical spectrum from simple steatosis to steatohepatitis, fibrosis, cirrhosis, HCC, and hypertension [148]. The disease arises from metabolic imbalance and excessive lipid deposition within hepatocytes, triggering oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and ER stress. As mentioned previously, autophagy serves as a key homeostatic mechanism that regulates metabolic balance in the liver. Rare and low-frequency ATG7 loss-of-function variants are shown to promote MASLD progression by impairing autophagy and facilitating ballooning and inflammation in human patients [149]. Under conditions of nutrient overload, key metabolic pathways involved in the autophagy process can be altered, including AMPK and mTORC1 [150].

Existing evidence has demonstrated transient activation of autophagy following acute lipid accumulation, whereas prolonged metabolic stress suppresses autophagic flux [151]. Human liver biopsies from MASLD patients display elevated LC3 and P62-positive aggregates, reduced mitophagy markers, and upregulation of ER stress signatures, all consistent with impaired autophagic clearance [151]. Mechanistically, this dysfunction involves downregulation or degradation of core proteins (ATG5, ATG7, BECN-1), increased RUBCN expression that blocks autophagosome-lysosome fusion, and dysregulation of nutrient-sensing pathways characterized by hyperactive mTORC1 and suppressed AMPK signaling.

In experimental models, pharmacologic or genetic activation of autophagy via agents, such as rapamycin, metformin, spermidine, or Tat-Beclin-1 restores lipid homeostasis, reduces inflammation and improves liver histology. Conversely, hepatocyte-specific loss of autophagy or mitophagy genes exacerbates steatosis and fibrosis [151]. Although no clinical trials have yet targeted autophagy directly in MASLD, several human therapeutics with indirect pro-autophagic effects, including resmetirom, green-tea catechins, and statins, have demonstrated metabolic and histological benefits [148]. Together, these findings underscore the critical role of autophagy in maintaining hepatic metabolic balance and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target for MASLD.

Alcohol-associated liver disease

ALD encompasses a progressive spectrum of hepatic injury from steatosis to alcoholic hepatitis, fibrosis, cirrhosis, and HCC driven by chronic ethanol consumption and its toxic metabolites. Ethanol metabolism generates acetaldehyde and ROS, promoting oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage, and protein aggregation. Autophagy in ALD has been extensively investigated in the past two decades [150,152–154]. In general, autophagy functions as a key defense mechanism by clearing damaged organelles and aggregated proteins; however, its regulation depends on the duration and extent of exposure [153]. Acute ethanol treatment transiently activates autophagy, evidenced by increased ATG expression and LC3 puncta formation, whereas chronic exposure impairs lipophagy and autophagic flux through lysosomal dysfunction and decreased nuclear TFEB levels. In experimental models, activation of autophagy via mTOR inhibition (rapamycin, Torin-1) or TFEB overexpression restores flux, reduces steatosis, and protects against apoptosis. Conversely, autophagy inhibition (tgAtg7 knockdown, chloroquine, or 3-methyladenine) aggravates lipid accumulation and hepatocyte death.

Although no clinical trials have yet evaluated autophagy modulators in ALD, preclinical evidence consistently supports targeting the mTOR-TFEB axis to restore lysosomal function and mitigate ethanol-induced liver injury.

Liver fibrosis

Liver fibrosis is characterized by excessive deposition of extracellular matrix in the liver. It represents a wound-healing response to chronic liver injury; however, if left untreated, it can progress to cirrhosis and HCC [155]. Depending on the etiology, collagen-producing myofibroblasts can originate from HSCs and/or portal fibroblasts (PFs) [156–158].

Dysfunction of autophagy in the liver can contribute to the progression of liver fibrosis [147,159,160]. In HSCs, autophagy can be induced by several factors, including hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF1α), transforming growth factor β1 (TGFβ1), and high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) [161]. Autophagy is activated in HSCs from mouse models following liver injury, and inhibition of autophagy decreases fibrogenesis in JS1 cells, an immortalized mouse HSC line [118]. Deletion of Atg7 in mouse HSCs attenuates liver fibrosis induced by chronic injury, a mechanism associated with autophagy-mediated lipid metabolism [118]. This study also suggests that autophagy-mediated lipid metabolism is required for HSC activation. Evidence further shows that ER stress activates HSCs in an autophagy-related manner [162], highlighting a critical role for HSC autophagy in liver fibrogenesis.

In contrast to autophagy in HSCs, autophagy in other liver cell types appears to play a protective role in hepatic fibrogenesis. Macrophage-specific deletion of Atg5 aggravates carbon tetrachloride (CCl4)-induced liver fibrosis [163]. Higher levels of interleukin (IL)-1α and IL-1β, as well as increased recruitment of inflammatory cells, are also observed in mouse livers with Atg5 deletion in macrophages [163]. Impaired autophagy caused by deletion of Atg7 in endothelial cells does not affect liver homeostasis but amplifies liver fibrosis without increasing liver injury following CCl4 treatment [164]. A recent study shows that autophagy is deficient in CD4+ T cells from patients with advanced fibrosis, and that loss of autophagy following genomic deletion of Atg5 in T cells is associated with the emergence of pathogenic IL-17A+ IFN-γ+ Th17 cells that drive liver fibrosis in mice [165]. Deletion of Atg5 genes in hepatocytes also leads to liver injury and fibrosis [166].

Several compounds, including quercetin, curcumin, oroxylin A, and DHA, modulate autophagy-related pathways and exhibit antifibrotic activity in animal models [155]. While no approved therapies directly target autophagy, ongoing preclinical work supports its therapeutic potential for reversing or preventing hepatic fibrogenesis.

Cholestatic liver disease

Cholestasis is a condition in which bile formation or flow is impaired, causing fatigue, pruritus, and jaundice [167]. Cholestatic liver disease (CLD) may occur in a variety of clinical scenarios, with etiologies that vary by the anatomical location of the defect and the acuity of presentation [167]. CLD imposes a significant healthcare burden, yet treatment options remain limited [167,168]. The main acute causes of cholestasis include alcohol-associated hepatitis (AH), common bile duct stenosis and cholangitis, DILI, sepsis, pregnancy, and total parenteral nutrition. Chronic causes include primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC), primary biliary cholangitis (PBC), secondary sclerosing cholangitis, biliary atresia, and genetic disorders. Notably, acute cholestasis can progress to a chronic condition, while chronic cholestasis may present with acute flare-ups [167,169]. Early biochemical evidence of cholestasis includes elevated serum levels of alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT), followed by the development of hyperbilirubinemia [167]. Autophagic flux is suppressed by bile acids in hepatocytes [170]. Dysregulated autophagy is observed in CLD [171]. In models of human cholestasis, autophagy can be impaired in an FXR-dependent manner, partly through induction of the protein RUBCN [45]. Although experimental evidence has shown the potential link between autophagy and cholestatic liver injury [171], whether and how autophagy contributes to CLD remains largely unclear.

Liver cancer

Dual roles of autophagy in cancer have been described [172–175]. Autophagy acts as a tumor suppressor by preventing the accumulation of damaged proteins and organelles, whereas in tumor cells, it is activated in response to cellular stress and/or the increased metabolic demands associated with rapid cell proliferation. Interestingly, defective autophagy in the host has been shown to promote the release of the arginine-degrading enzyme arginase I (ARG1) from the liver and to enhance the degradation of circulating arginine, which is essential for tumor growth [176]. This study suggests that autophagy outside tumor cells may also contribute to tumor growth through systemic effects.

Loss of ATGs, such as ATG5 and ATG7, in the liver leads to spontaneous tumorigenesis in mice [9,166,177,178]. Although the accumulation of P62 and the sustained activation of NRF2 are central drivers of tumorigenesis caused by autophagy deficiency in mouse livers, several other signaling pathways, such as HMGB1, Yes-associated protein (YAP), and mTOR, have also been shown to contribute [179]. Interestingly, loss of hepatic ATG5 prevents mice from developing diethylnitrosamine (DEN)-induced HCC [9]. Similar observations have been reported in liver-specific TFEB-deleted mice following combined DEN and ethanol treatment [180]. These findings suggest that autophagy is critical for the development of HCC once hepatocarcinogenesis has been initiated. Given the complexity of autophagy in liver cancer, whether and how autophagy should be targeted for liver cancer treatment requires further investigation.

Viral hepatitis

Autophagy is broadly recognized as a double-edged sword during viral infection. It can restrict viral replication by initiating innate antiviral responses, selectively degrading immune components associated with viral particles, and coordinating viral antigen presentation [181,182]. However, many viruses can also exploit autophagy to support their life cycle. In viral hepatitis, autophagy plays a complex and context-dependent role, functioning both as a host defense mechanism and as a pathway exploited by hepatitis viruses to support their replication.

For hepatitis C virus (HCV), multiple studies demonstrate that the virus relies heavily on autophagy machinery. Autophagy is required for the initiation of HCV RNA replication [183]. HCV induces autophagy to facilitate its replication, persistence, and evasion of innate immunity [184,185]. Autophagy also supports later stages of the viral life cycle. Inhibition or knockdown of autophagy components blocks the release of infectious HCV particles, in part by disrupting exosome-mediated viral egress [186].

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) also activates autophagy machinery to promote its envelopment, hijacking autophagic membranes to support virion maturation without fully inducing autophagic degradation [187]. Collectively, current evidence indicates that autophagy is involved in the pathogenesis of both HBV and HCV infections. Although autophagy may restrict viral infection under certain conditions, the life cycles of both viruses are supported by autophagy activation. Therefore, targeting autophagy as a therapeutic strategy in viral hepatitis will require precise modulation to avoid unintended enhancement of viral propagation.

Drug-induced liver injury

Autophagy plays a critical role in DILI, with most studies focusing on acetaminophen (APAP) overdose. APAP is a common pain reliever. Although it is generally safe at recommended doses, intentional or unintentional overdoses occur due to its wide availability [188]. APAP overdose can cause severe liver injury and is the leading cause of acute liver failure (ALF) in the United States, accounting for approximately 50% of all reported cases [188]. APAP undergoes metabolism by the cytochrome P450 system, producing the reactive metabolite N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine (NAPQI). The increased levels of highly reactive NAPQI deplete hepatic glutathione content and react with protein sulfhydryl groups, forming NAPQI-protein adducts. The formation of NAPQI-protein adducts, particularly mitochondrial adducts, is critical for APAP-induced liver injury [188,189].

Autophagy, particularly mitophagy, plays a critical protective role in APAP-induced liver injury. In mouse models, APAP induces autophagy, likely by suppressing mTOR signaling and promoting ROS production in hepatocytes. Pharmacological inhibition of autophagy worsens hepatotoxicity, whereas induction by rapamycin protects against liver injury [190]. Activation of autophagy-lysosomal pathways also facilitates the removal of APAP protein adducts, indicating that autophagy is essential for preventing APAP-induced liver injury [191]. APAP overdose additionally induces mitochondrial spheroid formation and mitochondrial dysfunction, and co-deletion of PINK1 and PARKIN impairs mitophagy, elevating serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and mortality [192–194]. Defective mitophagy due to optineurin (OPTN) deficiency disrupts mitochondrial homeostasis and exacerbates hepatocyte necrosis, whereas OPTN overexpression is protective [195]. Autophagy in non-parenchymal liver cells also contributes to APAP-induced liver pathology. Activation of ATG16L1 in peritoneal macrophages enhances phagocytosis and a reparative phenotype via autophagy-ROS-MerTK signaling, promoting IL-10-CXCR2-dependent hepatocyte proliferation, suggesting that macrophage autophagy plays a critical role in recovery from APAP-induced liver injury [196]. Taken together, these findings highlight the promising role of autophagy in ameliorating APAP-induced liver injury and suggest that targeting autophagy may be a feasible therapeutic approach for APAP overdose.

Conclusions and perspectives

From its discovery in the 1960s to Ohsumi’s Nobel Prize in 2016, research on autophagy has revealed its essential role in maintaining hepatic homeostasis. In the liver, autophagy integrates signals from nutrient- and energy-sensing pathways, such as AMPK and mTOR, with transcriptional regulators (TFEB, PPARs, FOXOs, FXR, and NRF2), epigenomic mechanisms, and chromatin remodeling complexes. These intersecting layers coordinate metabolic adaptation, organelle turnover, and immune regulation. Disruption of autophagy at any of these regulatory levels contributes to liver pathologies. Although considerable progress has been made, gaps remain in defining liver-specific regulatory pathways and in understanding the contributions of emerging mechanisms, such as LLPS. Moreover, the complexity of autophagy presents both challenges and opportunities for therapeutic targeting. Modulating upstream regulators, such as mTOR, AMPK, or FXR, holds therapeutic potential; however, systemic effects and cell-specific differences complicate liver-targeted intervention. Future studies should prioritize spatial and single-cell mapping of autophagy across hepatic cell types to identify more precise therapeutic targets and develop safer, more effective autophagy-based treatments for liver disease. Finally, utilizing the autophagy process as a drug-design approach [197], e.g., autophagy-targeting chimeras (AUTACs), shows promise for liver disease therapy.
