Real-world implementation of electronic patient-reported outcome measures (ePROMs) in routine oncology practice: a scoping review
Authors: K. Liao, Q. Liu, Y. Wang, K.H. Law, N. Takemura, D.H.Z. Lui, Y.-K. Lo, C. Faivre-Finn, T. Nuamek, J. Price, K.Y. Ho, J. Yorke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRoutine collection of electronic patient-reported outcome measures (ePROMs) enhances symptom monitoring and improves outcomes in cancer care. We evaluated published reports of real-world ePROM implementation in oncology, focusing on approaches to implementation, types of ePROMs used, completion rates, factors associated with ePROM completion, and impacts on patient and health care process/system-level outcomes. We also aimed to propose a classification of ePROM programmes.Materials and methodsWe systematically searched Medline, Embase, CINAHL Complete, and Scopus for articles describing routine ePROM implementation and evaluation. Data charting captured characteristics of the study, study population, characteristics of routinely collected ePROMs, and evaluation of their routine use. We also present a new classification of ePROM programmes into three levels: asynchronous, clinician-supervised, and real-time clinician-supervised, based on clinical pathway integration.ResultsOf 10 384 identified manuscripts, 50 met the inclusion criteria, reporting on 39 real-world ePROM programmes. Nearly all ePROM programmes (97.4%) collected data on symptom burden. The median overall completion rate was 61% (interquartile range 47%-76%), with substantial variability over time and across hospitals. Completion was associated with sociodemographic, clinical, and treatment-related factors. Most ePROM programmes (61.5%) were classified as asynchronous models, with five (12.8%) clinician-supervised models and three (7.7%) real-time clinician-supervised models, while seven (17.9%) lacked sufficient detail. The most frequently reported outcomes were fewer hospitalisations or emergency department visits (10.3%) at the patient level and improved symptom identification (17.9%) at the process/system level.ConclusionePROMs are increasingly embedded in routine oncology care, demonstrating benefits at both patient and system levels. However, most current programmes adapt asynchronous models with limited rapid clinician interaction.

Introduction

Patients with cancer frequently experience symptoms related to the disease or treatment-related toxicities.1 Effective symptom management is a cornerstone of high-quality cancer care, and it is well established that symptoms are most accurately reported by patients themselves.2 Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) are defined as measurements of the patient’s health status, such as health-related quality of life (HRQOL) and symptoms, reported directly by the patient, without interpretation by health care professionals.3 PROMs have been collected in clinical trials as treatment outcomes, complementing the information gathered from more conventional clinical and survival metrics in cancer research.4 A growing body of literature has shown that routinely collected electronic PROMs (ePROMs) benefit patients with cancer from various aspects, such as better symptom management, better communication between patient and clinician, and additional prognostic value in predicting clinical and survival outcomes.5, 6, 7 However, respondent burden may reduce ePROM completion, leading to disparities in data quality and limiting their utility for regulatory decision-making and clinical care.8

A 2015 scoping review demonstrated that PROMs were increasingly used in routine cancer care, facilitated by the use of electronic systems for data collection. Consistent with earlier studies, it is suggested that the routine use of PROM could enhance communication about symptoms and quality of life, while also highlighting several implementation barriers.7 However, although the review focused on ‘routine’ PROM use, all but one included study, by Seow et al.,9 were conducted in a research setting. In 2022, the European Society of Medical Oncology guidelines recommended the adoption of ePROMs in routine oncology practice, citing benefits such as improved physical function, symptom control, HRQOL, reduced hospitalisations, better overall survival, greater patient satisfaction, and cost-effectiveness.10 In recent years, several cancer centres have pioneered the real-world implementation and evaluation of ePROMs in routine cancer care in different ways.11, 12, 13, 14 Categorising the real-world ePROM programmes according to their level of integration into the care pathway may offer a practical framework to inform future implementation strategies and support wider adoption. Such a classification also aligns with established implementation science theories, such as the Non-Adoption, Abandonment, Scale-up, Spread, and Sustainability and the Normalisation Process Theory frameworks, which emphasise that the uptake of health care technologies depends on their fit with existing clinical workflows.15,16

To our knowledge, no comprehensive, up-to-date information is available on the extent to which ePROMs have been implemented in routine cancer care, how they have been used, and the impact on clinical practice and patient outcomes. Such insights are essential to guide the wider adoption of ePROMs in routine oncology care, develop a categorisation system based on their integration within the care pathway, and examine completion rates, disparities in completion, and clinical implications. Specifically, the objectives are to (i) identify published reports describing and evaluating the routine implementation of ePROMs in routine oncology care; (ii) describe which and how ePROMs were collected; (iii) assess ePROM completion rates and associated factors; (iv) evaluate the impact of routine ePROM collection on patient and health care process/system outcomes; and (v) propose a new framework for categorising the level of ePROM clinical integration.

Methods

The aim of our scoping review was to describe the landscape relating to the implementation of ePROMs in oncology. We designed the review in line with recommendations of the Joanna Briggs Institute17 and registered a scoping review protocol on Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/KE3V4). The review follows the principles outlined in Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR).18

Database search strategy

We systematically searched four databases, including Medline, Embase, CINAHL Complete, and Scopus, on 7 November 2024, and updated the search on 28 May 2025. The search syntax combined keywords and MeSH or Emtree terms, including ‘ePROMs,’ ‘remote monitoring,’ and ‘cancer’. We developed the search terms informed by the PROMs-Cancer Core Framework, a person-focused framework for PROM use in routine cancer care and revised based on a librarian’s suggestion19 (see full search syntax in Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100717). To complement the electronic search, we manually searched the reference lists of all included studies and relevant reviews.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for assessing the relevance of studies were structured according to the Population, Concept, and Context framework, recommended by the Joanna Briggs Institute review guidance.17 We focused on reports of real-world implementation [i.e. evaluation of routine ePROM use and not as part of a research project such as a randomised controlled trial (RCT) or a cohort study], including descriptive quantitative analysis, secondary analysis of routinely collected ePROM data, qualitative analysis, descriptive implementation report, and framework-guided implementation report. The reports were included if (i) the population contained adults diagnosed with cancer whether actively on treatment or not; (ii) any generic, cancer-specific ePROMs or their components were collected; (iii) the ePROMs were collected as part of routine clinical care; (iv) an original study published in English with full-text available; and (v) published after 2014, as the previous systematic review indicated that ePROMs were mostly collected and reported in a research setting instead rather than part of routine care before that.7 However, one article included in the previous scoping review,7 which reported the real-world implementation of the Edmonton Symptom Assessment System,9 met the inclusion criteria and was therefore included in the present scoping review (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100717).

Screening and study selection

After removing duplicates from the database searches, two reviewers independently screened all titles and abstracts. One author (KL) screened all titles, and three authors (QL, YW, and KHYL) each reviewed one-third of the articles. For identified reports, the full-text articles were retrieved and assessed independently and in duplicate by three authors (KL, QL, and YW). Disagreements were discussed with a senior author (JY), if necessary. Reasons for exclusion were recorded during the full-text screening stage only. Both titles and abstracts and full-text screening were conducted using Rayyan (https://www.rayyan.ai/).

Data charting

We developed a structured form to aid data charting and recording, including items related to the characteristics of the study, study population, characteristics of routinely collected ePROMs, and evaluation of the routine use of ePROMs. The evaluation of the routine use of ePROMs included the accessibility of the ePROM programmes and the impact of the implemented ePROM programmes on patient-level outcomes (e.g. symptoms, survival, hospitalisation, and treatment discontinuation) as well as process/system-level outcomes (e.g. patient–clinician communication, symptom identification, and clinical decision-making). The categorisation of patient-level and process/system-level outcomes was informed by the previous scoping review.7

Specific data items and their definitions are presented in Supplementary Table S3 (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100717). Three authors (KL, QL, YW) tested the data charting form by independently charting data from two randomly selected eligible studies and then charted data independently and in duplicate. Discrepancies in the charted data were resolved through discussion, and a senior author (JY) was consulted if needed. All the data were charted in Excel spreadsheets.

Collating, summarising, and presenting the results

Data were analysed in Excel and reported as frequencies to indicate the extent to which items were reported when appropriate. We used a deductive approach to summarise the qualitative content recommended by Pollock et al.20 and presented the results of characteristics, factors associated with ePROM completion, and the impact of the ePROM programme on the programme level.

ePROM, electronic patient-reported outcome measure.

The characteristics of an asynchronous model involve clinicians’ review of ePROMs at the next outpatient consultation, with no requirement from clinicians to review or respond to the submitted ePROMs within a preset timeframe. Patients do not receive any clinician feedback upon completion, but may receive automated messages based on their responses.

The characteristics of a clinician-supervised model involve stratification of patients into different levels of risk for treatment toxicities or other outcomes using ePROMs and other clinical factors. This informs clinical decisions such as the necessity, timing, and mode (face-to-face/telephone) of the next consultation, with or without tailored advice and self-management support provided via automated messages. The clinical team must respond within a specified timeframe, usually before the next consultation.

The characteristics of a real-time, clinician-supervised model involve two major features: a real-time alert system and time-specified responses from the clinical team. In addition to risk stratification, alerts are triggered when patient risk exceeds a predefined threshold, requiring the clinical team to respond to the patients within a specific timeframe, depending on the hospital policy.

Levels of clinical integration and clinician interaction

Table 4 summarises the extent to which ePROMs were integrated within the care pathway and the level of clinician interaction with the ePROM programme. Among the 39 programmes, 24 (61.5%) were asynchronous models, five (12.8%) were clinician-supervised models, and three (7.7%) were real-time clinician-supervised models, while seven (17.9%) did not provide sufficient detail for classification. System feedback mostly included risk identification (n = 11, 28.2%), and responsible clinicians were the primary recipients of the feedback (n = 19, 48.7%). Patient-facing feedback was delivered mainly through in-person discussion (n = 9, 23.1%) or automated messages (n = 6, 15.4%). Feedback on ePROM responses was most often given during subsequent consultations (n = 10, 26.3%) or in real-time (n = 6, 15.4%). ePROM data were primarily used to inform symptom management (n = 13, 33.3%), referrals (n = 8, 20.5%), and treatment adjustment or planning (n = 7, 17.9%). Discussion

This scoping review evaluated reports of real-world ePROM implementation in routine oncology practice. We found that since 2014, ePROMs have been increasingly integrated into routine oncology care around the world. Symptom burden was the most frequently evaluated outcome through routine use of ePROMs, with HRQOL also commonly assessed. As part of this scoping review, we developed the ePROM-CIP, a new classification of real-world ePROM programmes based on the degree of interaction with the clinical pathway. Most ePROM real-world programmes are asynchronous models, whereas the research evidence base, particularly RCTs, predominantly integrate real-time clinician-supervised models for timely clinical decision-making.70,71 This discrepancy likely reflects the greater time, resources, and financial investment needed to implement real-time clinician-supervised ePROM programmes in real-world settings. The ePROM completion rates varied across hospitals and over time, with disparities associated with sociodemographic, clinical, or disease characteristics. Although the impact of patient-level outcomes was underinvestigated, available evidence indicated potential benefits at both individual and health care process or system levels in oncology care.

To our knowledge, no classification was previously available to categorise how ePROM programmes operate in routine clinical practice. We therefore propose the ePROM-CIP to provide a practical guide for clinicians and cancer centres to plan and tailor resource allocation.72 In practice, the choice between different levels of integration involves trade-offs. For example, although asynchronous models require fewer operational resources, their success depends heavily on digital health literacy and patient engagement.73 The clinician-supervised models may appear more resource-intensive, but could reduce health inequities and improve adoption, especially when supported by reimbursement mechanisms.74 Overall, the most appropriate level of integration depends on local context, such as health system structure, goals, and the capacity of the cancer centres and clinicians.

This work also highlights that ePROM programmes implemented into routine care more often adopt asynchronous models, in contrast to the real-time, clinician-supervised models commonly used in research settings.70,71 This likely reflects the need to ensure scalability and sustainability within constrained resources in routine care. This shift also illustrates the tension in digital oncology integration between maintaining implementation fidelity of the ePROM symptom monitoring programmes and pragmatically adapting delivery models to fit local clinical workflows, staffing, and digital infrastructure. Purposeful changes to how ePROMs are delivered may also enhance acceptability and better fit the local context.75 Nevertheless, these programmes usually retain core components such as remote symptom monitoring to maintain fidelity with the underlying intervention.

Most ePROM programmes employed instruments with adapted items from adverse event item inventories such as PRO-CTCAE and adapted versions of the CTCAE,13,30,46,48,51,54,55,60,61,76 reflecting a primary focus on identifying treatment-related toxicities. These ePROMs may be particularly well-suited for routine implementation, as they can provide actionable information on both symptom severity and interference with daily activities. By contrast, despite it being the main outcome measure used in clinical trials and available guidance for clinical application of the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) suite, the EORTC Quality of Life Questionnaire-Core30 is not predominantly used in routine cancer care.7 One possible explanation is that the EORTC Quality of Life Questionnaire-Core 30, with its 30 items that are often used in conjunction with additional disease-specific modules, was not developed for clinical use or for one-to-one clinical consultations and may be perceived as burdensome in routine settings and challenging to apply at the individual level.67

In addition, despite more than a decade since the last scoping review in 2015, we have shown that there has been little advancement in the standardisation of routine ePROM collection, with a persistent wide variety of instruments used across health care providers.7 This heterogeneity highlights the trade-off between standardisation and local adaptation, where ePROM fragmentation risks limiting the comparability of outcomes across cancer centres. Although the International Consortium for Health Outcomes Measurement has developed five core sets of ePROMs for measuring outcomes in oncology, including localised and advanced prostate cancer, colorectal cancer, lung cancer, and nonmetastatic breast cancer,77, 78, 79, 80, 81 they were primarily designed for research and benchmarking purposes rather than routine oncology care, and there is no study developing core sets specifically for routine implementation. Future studies should explore the development of core sets of ePROMs for routine use in specific cancer groups, cancer stages, and treatment pathways, balancing the trade-off between clinical utility and the risk of questionnaire fatigue.

Our findings align with a 2020 review of ePROM use in the research context, which identified several barriers to ePROM use, including the time required to complete ePROMs, patient inability to complete them, perceived irrelevance, concerns about the impact on the health care provider–patient relationship, and privacy issues.82 The median completion rate of 60.7% suggests moderate uptake in routine care, compared with the 80%-90% completion rates frequently reported in RCTs.71,83 However, in this review, the substantial variability in completion rates across sites and the observed decline over time highlight significant challenges regarding long-term sustainability. In addition, our review highlights that real-world implementation also faces the challenge of digital exclusion and disparities in ePROM completion. Lower completion among older patients, ethnic minorities, and those with lower socioeconomic status, language barriers, or higher comorbidity burden raises concern that ePROM programmes may inadvertently reinforce existing health care disparities if implemented without adequate support. To achieve effective and equitable ePROM integration in routine practice, efforts should focus on addressing the disparities through resource redistribution and targeted support strategies. For example, additional assistance from caregivers and clinical staff should be considered to collect ePROMs from patients facing greater barriers.62,84,85 In addition, providing ePROMs in the patient’s first language can further improve the ePROM completion.86

The impact of the routine collection of ePROMs includes monitoring patients’ general health, response to treatment or symptom management, and supporting clinical decision-making.87, 88, 89, 90 Consistent with previous reviews evaluating the effect of ePROMs as an intervention in the research context, our review indicates that routine use of ePROMs can improve both patient-level and process/system-level outcomes.7,91,92 However, in this review, the majority of ePROM programmes implemented in routine oncology care have yet to formally evaluate their impact. We also identified a notable gap that existing evaluations have largely prioritised the process/system-level over patient-level outcomes, so that many real-world ePROM programmes are currently lacking robust evidence of durable patient-level benefit. Future studies should therefore assess the impact on patient-level outcomes, including treatment modification, treatment completion, quality of life, time-to-progression, and overall survival. Additionally, real-world cost-effectiveness analyses are also required to support the sustainable implementation of ePROM programmes in routine oncology care settings.

This scoping review has several limitations. First, we systematically reviewed published peer-reviewed reports on the implementation of ePROMs in routine oncology practice. However, ePROM programmes may be implemented into routine practice without any reports in peer-reviewed journals. Also, non-English papers were excluded. Thus, we may underestimate the scope of ongoing ePROM programmes in routine practice and overlook implementations in non-English speaking countries. Second, the proposed ePROM-CIP is aimed at providing a pragmatic classification of how ePROM programmes differ, specifically in terms of clinical integration, rather than a comprehensive implementation framework. Consequently, although the ePROM-CIP effectively categorises operational workflows, it may not capture all dimensions of the overall ‘complexity’ of ePROM programmes, including cancer centres’ digital maturity, reimbursement models, staffing, data governance, and patient engagement. Future research might consider developing a more comprehensive classification, incorporating the broader organisational and systemic dimensions. This could potentially help clinicians and cancer centres better use their resources to plan their ePROM implementation. Third, although we synthesised factors associated with ePROM completion, the included reports did not indicate whether ePROM programmes were offered equally across sociodemographic groups, nor did they report the proportion of participants from populations that are typically underrepresented or digitally excluded. Future evaluations of real-world ePROM programmes should examine whether systematic differences exist in both access to and uptake of these programmes across sociodemographic groups. Fourth, our review did not specifically focus on ePROM programmes implemented in palliative settings, where symptom monitoring and care priorities may differ from other clinical pathways. Therefore, our findings may not fully capture how ePROMs are used in palliative oncology. Future research should examine routine ePROM use specifically in palliative oncology care.

Conclusion

This scoping review demonstrates that the routine collection of ePROMs in oncology care is rapidly expanding and holds significant promise for transforming patient management and outcomes. The findings support their systematic use to monitor symptoms and improve outcomes. Our proposed classification of ePROM programmes, ePROM-CIP, provides a practical framework to help clinicians plan and tailor implementation strategies. Future research should prioritise optimising patient engagement, addressing disparities in ePROM completion rates, developing core ePROM sets for routine implementation, balancing comprehensiveness with the risk of questionnaire fatigue, and evaluating the impact of routine ePROM use in real-world practice.
