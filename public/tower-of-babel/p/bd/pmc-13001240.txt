Fusobacterium nucleatum promotes tumor progression driven by histone lactylation through increasing GLUT1 expression in colorectal cancer
Authors: Shizhen Zhou, Kai Wang, Hao Wang, Qinggang Yuan, Xinxin Yu, Lei Li, Linsen Shi, Qiongyuan Hu, Lixiang Liu, Chao Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe gut microbiota plays a significant role in the incidence and progression of colorectal cancer（CRC）, with Fusobacterium nucleatum (F. nucleatum) identified as a contributor to the advancement of this malignancy. However, the precise mechanisms underlying its action remain unclear.MethodsThis study uses FISH technology to measure F. nucleatum in tumors and WB to examine histone lactylation, exploring their connection. HCT116 CRC cells were treated with lactic acid, Oxamate, 2-DG, and F. nucleatum supernatant to analyze histone lactylation and cell behaviors like proliferation, migration, and invasion. The research also screened F. nucleatum supernatant for compounds that increase tumor cell lactylation and assessed their effects on cell viability using CCK-8, EdU, and transwell assays. Additionally, GLUT agonists and inhibitors were used to demonstrate that formic acid in the supernatant elevates GLUT1 expression.ResultsTumors characterized by elevated nuclear levels demonstrate increased histone lactylation. Modulating histone lactylation levels in tumor cells through human intervention can substantially impact their proliferative, migratory, and invasive capacities. Our study identified that F. nucleatum enhances the expression of GLUT1 in tumor cells via its metabolite, formic acid, leading to increased lactate production and histone lactylation. This process ultimately augments the stemness of tumor cells.ConclusionsF. nucleatum enhances the expression of GLUT1 in colorectal cancer cells through the production of formic acid, which subsequently elevates histone lactate levels and facilitates tumor progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-07968-8.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Fusobacterium nucleatum, Histone lactylation, Lactate, Formic acid, GLUT1

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the third most prevalent malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally [1, 2]. Despite notable advancements in surgical interventions and therapeutic strategies for CRC, the 5-year relative survival rate for patients has remained stagnant over recent decades [3–5]. Consequently, there is a pressing need to elucidate the molecular mechanisms governing the onset and progression of CRC tumors.

One significant aspect of CRC pathophysiology is the increased glycolytic metabolism observed in tumors, which leads to elevated lactate production and creates an acidic tumor microenvironment [6, 7]. Recent research has highlighted the importance of lactate-induced lactylation of histone lysine residues as an epigenetic modification [8–10]. This modification has been shown to directly enhance chromatin gene transcription, and play a facilitative role in the progression of various tumors, particularly colorectal cancer.

Recent research has demonstrated that metabolites derived from the gut microbiota are crucial in facilitating communication between the microbiota and tumor cells. Numerous studies have established that Fusobacterium nucleatum (F. nucleatum) are pathogenic agents in colorectal tumors and are implicated in the regulation of tumor initiation and progression [11–13]. Metagenomic analyses have identified several metabolites produced by F. nucleatum, including succinate, formate, and propionate [14, 15]. Notably, it has been reported that F. nucleatum enhances the stemness, invasion, and metastasis of CRC cells through its metabolite formate [11, 14]. Nevertheless, the association between F. nucleatum and histone lactylation in colorectal cancer remains unclear.

In this study, we performed an extensive analysis of F. nucleatum and histone lactylation within tumor tissues of colorectal cancer patients, identifying a significant positive correlation between F. nucleatum levels and histone lactylation in tumors. Our findings indicate that F. nucleatum elevates the expression of glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) in tumor cells through the production of the metabolite formic acid, which subsequently enhances glycolytic activity and lactate production. This process further augments histone lactylation in tumor cells, thereby facilitating the progression of colorectal cancer.

Results

The presence of F. nucleatum in CRC is correlated with histone lactylation

To examine the abundance of F. nucleatum and the level of histone lactylation modification in colorectal cancer patients, we collected 40 pairs of colorectal cancer tissues along with corresponding adjacent normal tissues for analysis (Fig. 1A). Basic clinical information for all patients is provided in Table S1. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) was employed to assess the presence of F. nucleatum in the tumor samples (Fig. 1B). Based on the detection of F. nucleatum, patients were categorized into two groups for subsequent histone lactylation modification analysis. Our findings indicate that histone lactylation in F. nucleatum-positive tumor tissues was evident in patients across both early (stages I-II) and late (stage III) TNM stages (Fig. 1C–D). Consequently, we hypothesize that the presence of F. nucleatum in tumors is associated with histone lactylation in tumor tissues.

The presence of F. nucleatum in CRC is correlated with histone lactylation. (A) Quantification of F. nucleatum and histone lactate levels in tumor samples from colorectal cancer patients. (B) Representative fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) images illustrating the presence of F. nucleatum in tumor tissues, using an Fn-specific probe. (C-D) analysis of the impact of F. nucleatum on histone lactate levels across different stages of colorectal cancer, assessed via Western blotting (WB). Data with error bars are presented as the means±SD. Statistical significance was calculated using unpaired two-tailed t test. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ns: no significance

Histone lactylation is positively correlated with the progression of CRC

To elucidate the impact of tumor histone lactylation on colorectal cancer, we assessed the concentration and levels of lactate in both tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissues. In alignment with previous research, our findings indicate a significant increase in lactate content within tumor tissues (Fig. 2A). Concurrently, the level of histone lactylation in tumor tissues was observed to be elevated compared to normal tissues (Fig. 2B–D). We categorized patients based on T and N stages and discovered that advanced stages were associated with higher levels of histone lactylation (Fig. 2E). Notably, patients with advanced TNM staging exhibited increased tumor histone lactylation, whereas histone lactylation levels in normal tissues showed no correlation with tumor staging (Fig. 2F). These findings demonstrate a significant association between tumor histone lactylation and tumor malignancy.

Histone lactylation can enhance tumor cell activity

To investigate histone lactylation’s impact on colorectal cancer, we performed in vitro experiments on tumor cell lines. Initially, we assessed lactate production and histone lactylation in common colorectal cancer cell lines under normal conditions, selecting HCT116 for further study (Figure S1A-C). By modifying lactate levels in the culture medium, we regulated histone lactylation, confirming that lactic acid supplementation enhances lactylation (Fig. 3A). Testing HCT116 cells under varying lactate concentrations revealed that lactate boosts cell proliferation, migration, and invasion (Fig. 3B–E). To elucidate this, we inhibited glycolysis to reduce lactate production and histone lactylation, finding that HK2 inhibitor (2-DG) and lactate dehydrogenase inhibitor (oxamate) decreased histone lactylation and reduced tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion (Fig. 3F–J, Figure S2A-E).

The culture medium of F. nucleatum enhanced histone lactylation in tumor cells

To investigate whether F. nucleatum can indeed augment histone lactylation in tumor cells and to identify the specific components involved, we conducted co-culture experiments using inactivated F. nucleatum cells and their culture medium with HCT116 cells. The findings indicated that the culture medium of F. nucleatum (Fn CM) significantly increased the cells’ capacity to produce lactate (Fig. 4A). Similarly, cells treated with the Fn CM exhibited a marked increase in histone lactylation levels, whereas inactivated F. nucleatum cells demonstrated no such effect (Fig. 4B). Moreover, our study revealed that Fn CM can enhance the proliferation, migration, and invasion capabilities of tumor cells (Fig. 4C–E)

Nucleatum enhances tumor cell activity by producing formic acid

Based on the aforementioned experiments, F. nucleatum primarily influences the histone lactylation levels in HCT116 cells through its metabolites. We subsequently investigated several key metabolites, including Formic Acid (FA), Acetic Acid (AA), and Succinic Acid (SA). It was observed that only FA significantly enhances the histone lactylation levels in HCT116 cells (Fig. 5A–F). Additionally, we evaluated the impact of FA, AA, and SA on the proliferation, migration, and invasion of HCT116 cells. The findings indicated that all three metabolites significantly influenced the proliferation of HCT116, with FA demonstrating a more pronounced effect (Figs. 5G–5J).

FA can enhance the histone lactylation of HCT116 by up-regulating GLUT1

Based on the aforementioned experiments, FA exhibits the most potent capability in enhancing tumor biological behavior and upregulating histone lactylation; however, its underlying mechanism remains unclear. Subsequently, we assessed the mRNA levels of key glycolysis-associated enzymes in both the FA-treated and control groups. The findings revealed that the mRNA levels of SLC2A1 and LDHA were elevated in the FA group compared to the control group (Fig. 6A). Corresponding results were observed at the protein level (Fig. 6B). Subsequently, we detected the expression levels of these transcription factors in HCT116 cells treated with FA. As shown in Figure S3, among the tested candidates, Nrf2 exhibited a marked upregulation upon FA stimulation, suggesting that Nrf2 is a potential target transcription factor mediating the FA-induced GLUT1 expression. We then employed the GLUT1 inhibitor WZB-117 [16, 17]. By comparing lactic acid levels and histone lactylation among the three groups, it was demonstrated that WZB-117 could reverse the FA-induced upregulation of lactic acid and histone lactylation (Fig. 6C-D). Furthermore, WZB-117 was also capable of reversing the FA-mediated proliferation, migration, and invasion of HCT116 cells (Fig. 6E–H).

Discussion

In this study, we identified a novel association between F. nucleatum and histone lactylation in colorectal cancer, elucidating a previously unrecognized mechanism by which F. nucleatum facilitates tumor progression. Our findings demonstrate that F. nucleatum metabolism leads to the production of formic acid, which subsequently upregulates the key enzyme GLUT1 involved in glycolysis within tumor cells. This upregulation results in increased lactate production, which enhances histone lactylation and ultimately augments tumor cell activity.

Colorectal cancer, akin to other malignant neoplasms, demonstrates the “Warburg effect,” wherein cancer cells preferentially engage in glycolysis for energy production, even in the presence of oxygen [18]. This glycolytic activity is markedly elevated compared to that of normal colorectal cells, resulting in a substantial increase in lactate production. This phenomenon is a characteristic feature of tumor cells, particularly in colorectal cancer. Historically, lactic acid was regarded merely as a byproduct of glycolytic metabolism. However, Faubert et al. demonstrated its potential to be reutilized as a primary carbon source within the mitochondrial tricarboxylic acid cycle. Zhang et al. further elucidated that lactate can function as an epigenetic regulator by modifying histone lysineresidues.

Both intracellular and extracellular lactate contribute to the stimulation of histone lysine lactylation [19–22]. This post-translational modification of histones can influence chromatin structure by altering nucleosome interactions or recruiting non-histone proteins, thereby modulating gene expression and cellular functions [8]. Recent studies have highlighted the role of lactylation in various pathological conditions, including tumorigenesis [22], heart failure [21], and sepsis [23]. Emerging research underscores the correlation between histone lactylation and the initiation and progression of melanoma, pancreatic ductal carcinoma, renal cancer, non-small cell lung cancer, colorectal cancer, and other malignancies, as well as its facilitation of tumor immune evasion [24–27].

The lactylation score in gastric cancer patients inversely correlates with immune cell infiltration and response to immune checkpoint inhibitors [28]. H3K18la facilitates melanoma progression by inducing YTHDF2 gene expression [29]. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, histone H3K18 lactylation modulates spindle assembly checkpoint genes TTK and BUB1B, influencing tumor cell proliferation and migration [24, 30]. H3K18la also enhances the oncogenic transformation of LCN2 and c-Myc, promoting breast and bladder cancer [31, 32]. In liver cancer, non-responders exhibit significantly higher H3K18la levels compared to responders, which upregulates B7–H3 expression and suppresses CD8+ T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity [33]. The identification of histone lactylation offers insights into the Warburg effect and the role of lactate in tumor immunity.

Our study indicates that the level of histone lactylation in colorectal cancer is contingent upon lactate concentration; as lactate levels rise, there is a corresponding increase in histone lactylation, which subsequently enhances the proliferation, migration, and invasion capabilities of tumor cells.

For the first time, we have identified a correlation between intratumoral F. nucleatum and histone lactylation within tumor cells using clinical samples. Prior research has demonstrated that F. nucleatum contributes to the initiation and progression of colorectal cancer by promoting tumor cell proliferation, suppressing anti-tumor immune responses, inducing localized inflammatory reactions, and causing cellular DNA damage.

Our findings reveal that the F. nucleatum metabolite, formic acid, upregulates the expression of GLUT1, thereby enhancing the glucose uptake capacity of tumor cells. This process supplies ample substrates for glycolysis, resulting in increased lactate production and subsequently elevating histone lactylation levels in tumor cells.

Methods

Human samples

To examine alterations in F. nucleatum levels and histone lactylation in colorectal cancer (CRC) patients, tissue samples were collected from Nanjing drum tower hospital. All human experiments followed the Helsinki Guidelines. The mean age of the CRC patients was 59.3 years. Additional clinical and pathological characteristics of the patients are detailed in Table S1.

Cell culture

The CRC cell lines HCT116, RKO, and SW480 were procured from the Type Culture Collection of the Chinese Academy of Sciences in Shanghai, China. The cell lines were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, 11875500BT), supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Celligent, CG0430B) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution (Gibco, 15,140–122). Upon reaching 80% confluency, HCT116, RKO, and SW480 cells were seeded onto various plate types and subjected to distinct experimental conditions. Pharmacological agents, including Lactate (0, 1, 10 mM), Oxamate (0, 5, 10 mM), FA (2 mM), AA (2 mM), SA (0.5 mM), WZB117 (10 μM), and 2-DG (0, 1, 2.5 mM), were dissolved in PBS as required. Prior to cell stimulation, all in vitro experiments incorporated a 12-hour starvation period using serum-free culture medium.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from the cell samples using TRIzol reagent (Vazyme, R411-01-AA). Subsequently, cytoplasmic and nuclear RNA fractions were isolated employing the Cytoplasmic & Nuclear RNA Purification Kit (Norgen Biotek, Torold, ON, Canada) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The isolated RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the HiScript® III RT SuperMix (+gDNA wiper) (Vazyme, R223-01) for quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis. The qRT-PCR was conducted on an ABI StepOne Plus instrument utilizing PowerUp™ SYBR® Green (Applied Biosystems, USA), with Actin used as the internal control for normalization purposes. The primers used for qRT-PCR are detailed in the Reagents and Tools table.

Western blotting (WB)

Tumor tissues were homogenized using three steel balls in a lysis buffer (Solarbio, R0010) supplemented with 1% phosphatase inhibitor (MCE, HY-K0023) and 1% protease inhibitor (MCE, HY-K0010), at a ratio of 200 µl of buffer per 20 mg of tissue. Protein extraction was performed via centrifugation at 14,000 g for 20 minutes. Similarly, cell samples were treated with 200 µl of lysis buffer per 1 × 10^6 cells and subjected to centrifugation at 14,000 g for 20 minutes. Protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA assay kit (Beyotime, P0010). Subsequently, 25 µg of protein per lane was resolved on 8 and 12% polyacrylamide gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (Biofroxx, 9048–46-8) in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (Biosharp, 9005–64-5), followed by incubation with specific primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. On the following day, secondary antibodies were applied, and detection was performed using an imaging system. Detailed information on all antibodies used is provided in the Reagents and Tools table.

Cell growth and proliferation assays

The rate of cell proliferation was evaluated using CCK-8 cell proliferation assays. Seven independent replicates of HCT116 cells, subjected to various experimental conditions, were cultured in 96-well plates. At designated time intervals, a 10% CCK-8 solution (Dojindo Laboratories, CK04) was introduced to each well, followed by a 2-hour incubation period. Absorbance readings were subsequently taken at 450 nm. Additionally, 5-Ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) assays were conducted in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol, utilizing the EdU Cell Proliferation Assay Kit.

Scratch assay

To evaluate the migratory characteristics of HCT116 cells, an in vitro scratch assay was conducted. A linear incision was made through the center of a monolayer of cells that had been cultured for 24 hours. Subsequently, Lactate, Oxamate, FA, AA, SA, WZB117, and 2-DG were administered for an additional 24 hours respectively, and the cells were observed using an inverted microscope. The cell migration capacity was quantified by calculating the wound-healing rate, expressed as a percentage (distance migrated/original wound distance × 100%).

Transwell invasion and migration experiments

Transwell inserts were positioned within a 24-well culture plate, with the insert referred to as the upper chamber and the culture plate as the lower compartment. The cells were initially digested in a serum-free medium, after which the cell density was adjusted to 1 × 106 cells/mL, and the suspension was inoculated into the upper chamber. Subsequently, RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. For the Transwell invasion assays, the upper membrane was precoated with 40 µL of Matrigel (BD Biosciences, USA). After 24 hours, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and washed with PBS. The cells were then stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Solarbio, China), and representative images were captured at 100× and 200× magnification using an optical microscope.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was conducted according to established protocols. The procedure involved the rehydration of tumor tissue slides, antigen retrieval, blocking, and incubation with primary antibodies. This was followed by the application of secondary antibodies and staining using an IHC kit (Beyotime, P0603) with a DAB peroxidase substrate. Comprehensive information regarding all pertinent antibodies is available in the Reagents and Tools table.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) assay

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumor sections were utilized for fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) analysis employing the bacterial DNA FISH Assay Kit (D-0015, FOCO Biology, China). The hybridization process was conducted at 37 °C over a period of two days using an F. nucleatum probe (5′-CTTGTAGTTCCGCYTACCTC-3′, labeled with FITC) at a concentration of 10 μg/mL in the hybridization buffer. Subsequently, the slides were rinsed with sterile double-distilled water, air-dried in the dark, and mounted using a mounting medium containing DAPI (ab104139, Abcam, UK). Fluorescence microscopy was performed to capture images, utilizing a BX63F microscope.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The data presented in this study are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), unless stated otherwise, and are further detailed in the relevant sections of the methodology and figure legends. Statistical comparisons between the two groups were performed using Student’s t-test for parametric data, the Mann-Whitney U-test for nonparametric data, and the log-rank test for survival analysis. p-values were calculated using either SPSS version 26.0 or GraphPad Prism version 9.0, with significance levels indicated as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ns for non-significant results.
