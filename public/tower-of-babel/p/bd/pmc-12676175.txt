Systematic Echocardiographic Screening Protocol for Cardiac Amyloidosis Detection

Cardiac amyloidosis is a progressive infiltrative cardiomyopathy affecting 1% to 4% of patients older than 65 with heart failure.1 Despite Food & Drug Administration approval of targeted therapies for transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy in 2019, diagnostic pathways remain fragmented, with median diagnosis time from symptom onset of about 2 years.2 Transthyretin amyloidosis disproportionately affects African Americans, with the Val142Ile variant present in approximately 1 in 25 individuals, yet detection rates remain inadequate.3

Traditional diagnostic approaches rely on physician recognition during clinical encounters, missing opportunities for early detection during routine echocardiography. Studies demonstrate that cardiac amyloidosis often masquerades as hypertensive heart disease or age-related changes, with typical red flags including heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, carpal tunnel syndrome, and bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome preceding cardiac symptoms by years.4,5 Our institution observed delayed diagnoses and missed cases, particularly during noncardiac hospitalizations where echocardiography was performed for incidental purposes, representing a critical missed opportunity for early intervention.

Project Rationale

Preimplementation clinical observations at our institution revealed a pattern consistent with the published literature on cardiac amyloidosis underdiagnosis. Despite performing hundreds of echocardiograms annually on patients meeting established screening criteria (age ≥65 years, interventricular septal thickness [IVS] ≥1.2 cm), institutional detection rates were functionally zero over the preceding 12 months. This stark discrepancy with published prevalence estimates of 1% to 6% in similar elderly populations highlighted a critical care gap requiring systematic intervention.

Preimplementation analysis revealed several critical barriers: inconsistent application of strain imaging, absence of standardized screening criteria at the point of image acquisition, and lack of systematic risk-stratification protocols. Echocardiography technologists, who acquire the majority of diagnostic images, lacked structured guidance for identifying suspicious patterns. Additionally, communication pathways between imaging and clinical teams were fragmented, leading to delayed or missed referrals for confirmatory testing.

The high prevalence of undiagnosed cardiac amyloidosis, combined with the availability of disease-modifying therapies, created an urgent need for systematic early detection protocols that could be implemented across diverse clinical encounters.

Project Description

We implemented a comprehensive screening protocol over 7 months (November 2024–May 2025) using continuous quality improvement methodology. This initiative was specifically designed to improve the identification of high-risk patients by echocardiography technologists at the point of image acquisition, addressing the fundamental problem that official echocardiographic reports consistently failed to flag suspicious cases. The implementation was rolled out systematically, with ongoing refinements based on real-time feedback and outcomes data throughout the study period.

Our aim targeted 100% screening of eligible patients undergoing echocardiography, establishing age-specific criteria (≥65 years generally, ≥60 years for African Americans) and IVS ≥1.2 cm. These criteria were derived from the published literature demonstrating increased prevalence in these populations and established echocardiographic parameters associated with infiltrative cardiomyopathy.6

Five key innovations were initiated:

1.Daily technologist-driven screening: Echocardiography technologist champions conducted a systematic review of patient lists using automated criteria, communicating through TEAMS-based platform in real time. Unlike traditional physician-driven protocols, this approach empowered technologists as first-line identifiers.

Risk-Stratification Protocol

Patients meeting initial criteria underwent comprehensive assessment incorporating pro–B-type natriuretic peptide levels, troponin T values, electrocardiographic findings, hypertension history, and review of prior echocardiograms when available to assess progression of septal wall thickness. They were then classified according to risk as low, intermediate, high, and very high, with high-risk/very high–risk patients prompting direct team communication recommending PYP scanning and serum light-chain analysis.

Project Outcome, Impact, and Future Directions

Between November 2024 and May 2025, a total of 82 consecutive patients meeting the systematic echocardiographic screening criteria (age ≥65 years or ≥60 years for African Americans, IVS ≥1.2 cm) were evaluated.

Baseline patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The mean age was 73.1 ± 11.3 years, with 50.0% male. The majority were White (47.6%), followed by Black (24.4%) and other/Asian ethnicities (15.9%). Hypertension was present in 70.7%, diabetes mellitus in 28.0%, dyslipidemia in 41.5%, and coronary artery disease in 26.8% of patients. Preserved ejection fraction (≥50%) was observed in 82.3%, while 17.7% had reduced ejection fraction. Importantly, 69.5% of patients were admitted for noncardiac reasons, reflecting the strength of this protocol in identifying amyloidosis risk during incidental imaging opportunities. By design, 80.5% met the age-based screening trigger, and 89% had IVS ≥1.2 cm.

In the intermediate-risk group, 100% were flagged by echocardiography suspicion alone, with 1 patient (3.8%) showing an equivocal PYP scan but no confirmed diagnosis.

In the low-risk group, rare positives emerged: 1 equivocal PYP, 1 positive CMR, and 1 positive biopsy. Ultimately, 1 patient (6.2%) in this group was diagnosed with cardiac amyloidosis, indicating that most confirmed cases originated from the high-risk tier.

Across the entire cohort, 8 patients had positive/equivocal PYP scans, 2 had positive CMR, and 2 had a positive biopsy. After integrating confirmatory work-up, 6 patients (7.3%) received a final diagnosis of cardiac amyloidosis, demonstrating markedly improved detection compared to baseline institutional performance (historically near zero) (see Table 2).

These findings represent a 7.3% confirmed diagnostic rate (6/82) among all screened patients, dramatically exceeding preimplementation baseline detection of near-zero cases annually. Importantly, our protocol identified high-risk patients and notified their physicians with PYP scan recommendations, but we did not have control over who ultimately received testing. Of the 40 patients identified as high risk, only 14 (35%) actually received PYP scans, yet 6 of these 14 scans (42.8%) were positive or equivocal, demonstrating the high diagnostic yield when testing was performed. It should be noted that a significant number of patients labeled as high risk did not undergo testing for various reasons, including treating physician or patient choice and discharge to hospice.

Future Directions

Next steps include automated adherence monitoring, regional network expansion, and integrated genetic testing protocols for family cascade screening optimization.

Discussion

Our systematic approach addresses critical gaps in cardiac amyloidosis detection through multifaceted innovation. The 7.3% confirmed diagnosis rate among screened patients aligns with published prevalence estimates of 1% to 6% in at-risk elderly populations with heart failure, validating the effectiveness of our screening criteria.4,7 This yield compares favorably to other screening initiatives reported in the recent literature, where systematic approaches in heart failure clinics achieved 3% to 8% detection rates.8

Our technologist-empowered model represents a paradigm shift by recognizing that diagnostic opportunities often occur at image acquisition rather than interpretation.

Implementation Challenges and Solutions

This quality improvement project was designed to enhance the identification of high-risk patients by echocardiography technologists who could benefit from further amyloidosis evaluation. The execution of the protocol was inherently dependent on technologist motivation and proactivity, which naturally varied month to month. Despite the 7-month study period, hundreds of echocardiograms were performed, and many suspicious cases were likely missed owing to the voluntary rather than mandated nature of this screening enhancement initiative.

Early implementation faced several barriers, including technologist workflow integration concerns, physician skepticism regarding technologist-driven protocols, and information technology limitations. Solutions included dedicated champion identification, physician engagement through presentations, and development of streamlined electronic health record integration. Staff turnover required ongoing education reinforcement, addressed through standardized orientation protocols and peer-mentoring systems.

The predominance of cases identified during noncardiac admissions highlights the importance of universal screening beyond traditional heart failure presentations. These findings challenge current practice paradigms that focus amyloidosis screening primarily on specialized heart failure clinics, missing opportunities in general medical wards where elderly patients frequently undergo echocardiography for various indications.

Educational intervention targeting frontline technologists proved essential, with sustained engagement achieved through positive reinforcement cycles created by informing staff of patient outcomes. The holographic visual aid system provided constant point-of-care reminders, addressing “out of sight, out of mind” phenomena plaguing screening protocols.

Cost-effectiveness considerations strongly favor early detection, with economic modeling suggesting potential savings in heart failure hospitalizations, emergency department visits, and diagnostic procedures.1,2 The protocol's scalability and minimal resource requirements make broad implementation feasible across diverse health care settings, particularly important given the aging population and increasing amyloidosis awareness.

Limitations

The methodology of this single-center quality improvement initiative, while reflective of real-world implementation conditions, presents inherent limitations. The protocol's dependence on technologist engagement, though providing valuable insights for sustainable implementation, resulted in variable month-to-month execution and likely missed cases among the hundreds of echocardiograms performed during the study period. The absence of formal preimplementation data collection limited our ability to quantify baseline detection rates, though institutional experience and published literature suggested functionally zero detection prior to protocol implementation.

Conclusions

This systematic screening protocol demonstrates that low-cost, multifaceted approaches can transform cardiac amyloidosis detection across diverse hospital encounters. The identification of both wild-type disease in elderly populations and hereditary variants requiring family screening underscores comprehensive clinical value. Technologist empowerment combined with structured communication pathways creates sustainable detection improvements that extend beyond traditional cardiology encounters, achieving a 7.3% diagnosis rate that dramatically exceeds baseline institutional performance.
