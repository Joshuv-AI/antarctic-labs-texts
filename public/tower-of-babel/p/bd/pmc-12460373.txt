Higher butanol titer and selectivity in electro-fermentation experiments with Clostridium acetobutylicum ATCC 824 are due mainly to methyl viologen rather than electrode polarization
Authors: Edouard Beudy, Sandra Menir, Michel Laurent, Fadhel Ben Chaabane, Théodore Bouchez, Hélène Velly
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractButanol selectivity is crucial for the development of an industrial process targeting butanol as the main product of acetone-butanol-ethanol (ABE) fermentation by solventogenic Clostridia. This study evaluated electro-fermentation (EF), with an electron carrier, methyl viologen (MV), as a strategy to modify solvent production in Clostridium acetobutylicum ATCC 824 under optimized batch fermentation conditions. Cathodic EF was performed with or without 0.5 mM MV in single-compartment reactors with two-chamber and a negative potential was applied to the cathode. The greatest differences in final products concentrations were observed with the addition of MV compared to the control and EF fermentations. The fermentation with MV only was slowed down and resulted in 11% more butanol (control: 14.2 g.L−1, MV: 15.8 g.L−1) and half as much acetone, increasing the butanol/acetone (B/A) ratio by 137% from 1.93 to 4.57 g C.g C−1. The pH profile was also modified with a final pH 0.5 unit higher. Conversely, EF did not appear to alter product formation significantly, even if slight differences were observed under our conditions. In addition, the reductase activity (RA) was measured by flow cytometry and showed that EF alone affected the intracellular redox state compared to the control fermentation, as did the addition of MV. However, electrode polarization had no effect on the extracellular oxidoreduction potential (ORP) profiles.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00449-025-03212-7.Keywords: Clostridium acetobutylicum, Butanol, Electro-fermentation, Methyl viologen, Redox, Selectivity

Introduction

Climate change is one of the most pressing challenges facing humanity in the 21st century. In 2019, the transport sector, which relies mainly on fossil fuels, was responsible for 15% of greenhouse gas emissions [1]. To reduce this impact, the search for sustainable alternatives is crucial and biofuels offers a promising alternative to fossil fuels [2]. n-Butanol (referred to as butanol in this study) can be produced by fermentative processes and used as a biofuel. Compared to ethanol, butanol has physico-chemical properties closer to gasoline and is more compatible with existing infrastructures (engines and pipelines) [3]. It also has important other industrial applications as a solvent or a chemical intermediate used in the production of plastics, raisins or paints [4–6].

Butanol is the primary product of the acetone-butanol-ethanol (ABE) fermentation produced at a molar ratio of 3:6:1 [7] by solventogenic Clostridia, anaerobic Gram-positive bacteria. Solvents are synthesized through a biphasic metabolism consisting of multiple redox reactions (Fig. 1). During the initial acidogenic phase, cells predominantly produce acids, which are re-assimilated in the solventogenic phase to form solvents. Throughout this process, sugars act as both a carbon and electron sources.

From an industrial perspective, shifting selectivity toward a target metabolite such as butanol is preferable to improve the process efficiency. Since acetone is a poor biofuel, various strategies have been employed to increase the butanol to acetone ratio, noted B/A, by altering metabolic and electron flow pathways [8]. These methods include using more reduced substrates [9, 10], decreasing hydrogen production through redox mediators [11–15] or hydrogenase inhibition [16–21], modifying the extracellular redox potential by gas sparging [22, 23] or addition of reduced molecules [24], and using genetic engineering approaches [25, 26].

Redox compounds such as methyl viologen (MV, 1,1′-dimethyl-4,4′-bipyridinium dichloride) offer a promising approach to enhancing butanol selectivity during fermentation. MV improves butanol over acetone production during Clostridium acetobutylicum fermentation [12]. Studies have shown that the addition of few mM of MV in the culture medium can induce metabolic shifts, decreasing acids and acetone concentrations, the growth rate and hydrogen production, but significantly increasing butanol production and yield [11–13]. The redox potential of the oxidized forms is E(MV+/MV2+) = − 440 mV/SHE [27], which is close to that of ferredoxin, a key electron transport protein in solventogenic Clostridia [28]. MV can thus be reduced by the pyruvate ferredoxin oxidoreductase (Pfor) instead of ferredoxin during pyruvate cleavage. Reduced MV can subsequently serve as a preferred substrate for ferredoxin NAD(P) oxidoreductase (Fnor) rather than hydrogenase (Hyd) to reduce redox coenzymes [12, 29] (Fig. 1). These reduced coenzymes are then used in many metabolic reactions, particularly in alcohol synthesis to restore the balance between oxidized and reduced forms (Fig. 1).

Electro-fermentation (EF) is another innovative approach to modify carbon and electron flows in microbial fermentation processes. EF generally takes place within a bioelectrochemical system including two chambers, an anodic and a cathodic compartment, separated by an ion exchange membrane [31]. A working electrode (WE) is placed in the fermentation compartment, while a counter electrode (CE) is set in the other. A potentiostat controls the potential difference applied between these electrodes. To promote the formation of reduced compounds, such as alcohols, a negative potential is applied to the WE, which acts as a cathode [31], providing an additional source of electrons that can alter the metabolism of the cells.

For Clostridia fermentations, the potential applied to the WE is set more negatively than the typical extracellular oxidoreduction potential (ORP) measured during a solventogenic Clostridia fermentation [12, 22, 32] to favor reduction reactions. In most cases, this potential is maintained around − 500 mV/SHE to provide a sufficiently reduced environment while avoiding excessive water electrolysis. The cathode can theoretically provide electrons to reduce redox coenzymes, which have redox potentials of E°(NAD+/NADH) = − 320 mV/SHE and E°(NADP+/NADPH) = − 315 mV/SHE [33].

Studies have shown that under certain conditions, direct electron transfer from the WE to these coenzymes may can be achieved, increasing butanol production [34] and shifting selectivity by favoring butanol formation over acetone [35]. However, Gram-positive bacteria such as Clostridia are generally less electroactive due to their cell wall [36], which limits the electron transfer from the WE to the cells. To facilitate this transfer, artificial electron carriers can act as redox shuttles. They should have intermediate redox potentials that allow them to be reduced at the cathode and subsequently transfer electrons to the oxidized coenzymes. Previous studies have explored redox shuttles such as neutral red [35, 37, 38] and methyl viologen [11, 39], and demonstrated promising improvements in ABE solvent production, productivity, and selectivity.

As mentioned later in Table 2, most of the studies on Clostridia EF, with or without electron carriers, have focused on Clostridium beijerinckii and have reported little ABE solvent production. In this work, the cathodic EF of Clostridium acetobutylicum ATCC 824 was investigated with and without the addition of electron carriers in culture conditions already optimized for high ABE solvent production (> 18 g.L−1). The aim was to determine if electrode polarization could further enhance fermentation performance under these conditions. In addition, the redox state of the cells was analyzed to better understand the mechanisms involved.

Materials and methods

Microorganism and cultivation conditions

The strain used in this study was Clostridium acetobutylicum ATCC 824. It was stored as spores suspension in 20% (v.v−1) glycerol solution at -80°C. Precultures 1 were carried out in CALAM medium [40] composed of (per liter): 250 g boiled and mashed potatoes; 10 g glucose; 2 g (NH4)2SO4; 2 g CaCO3. The medium was autoclaved at 121 °C for 20 min.

Precultures 1 were cultivated in 15 mL tubes containing 10 mL CALAM medium, boiled for 10 min to eliminate traces of O2. A volume of 250 µL of the spores suspension, shocked in boiling water for 1 min, was added to the medium. Precultures 1 were incubated in a Bactron II anaerobic chamber (Shel Lab, Cornelius, OR, USA) for 24 h at 34 °C.

Precultures 2 and fermentations in bioreactors were carried out in a modified GAPES medium [41] composed of (per liter): 80 g glucose; 2.5 g yeast extract; 1 g KH2PO4; 0.6 g K2HPO4; 1 g MgSO4 7H2O; 6.6 mg FeSO4 7H2O; 0.1 g p-aminobenzoic acid; 2.9 g ammonium acetate; 0.05 g antifoam Biospumex 36K (PMC Ouvrie, Carvin, France). For electron carrier fermentation, 0.5 mM of MV was added in the culture medium. The medium was sterilely filtered at 0.2 µm. Precultures 2 were prepared by inoculating 50 mL of overnight reduced GAPES medium in 125 mL flasks with 10% (v.v−1) of precultures 1 and were incubated during 72 h at 34°C in the chamber. Electro-fermenters were inoculated at 10% (v.v−1) with 24 h precultures 2.

Electro-fermentation set-up

The electro-fermenters used in this study were two-chamber single-compartment reactors (Fig. 2) designed by INRAE (Antony, France) and built by Belleville SA (Fontenay–Sous–Bois, France). They were based on modified 0.5 L Duran glass bottles with four ports, which served as the cathodic compartment, sealed with a glass plate also with four ports. The anodic compartment was an L-shaped glass tube inserted through one of ports of the plate. The two compartments were separated by a CMI-7000 cation exchange membrane (Membranes international, Ringwood, NJ, USA).

Single-compartment reactor with two chambers for cathodic EF. SCE saturated calomel electrode, WE working electrode, CE counter electrode, CEM cation exchange membrane

A three electrodes system was used during the experiments. The working electrode was a 4 cm2 stainless steel grid (RS PRO, London, UK) connected to a 316L stainless steel rod, placed centrally in the cathodic chamber facing the membrane. The CE was a platinum electrode (XM150 Metal Electrode, Radiometer Analytical, Villeurbanne, France) placed at 20 cm of the WE, and the reference electrode was a saturated calomel electrode (Red Rod Reference Electrode, Radiometer Analytical) placed next to the WE.

To ensure anaerobic conditions, the airtightness of the system was checked prior to autoclaving. The electro-fermenters were sterilized at 121 °C for 20 min with the WE only. The electrodes were connected to a potentiostat (VMP3, BioLogic, Seyssinet-Pariset, France) monitored by the EC-lab software (BioLogic).

After autoclaving, the main compartment was aseptically filled with 450 mL of sterile GAPES medium and the CE tube was filled with 70 mL of a 32.4 mM KCl solution to match the GAPES medium conductivity (4.17 mS.cm−1). The reactors were placed on magnetic stirrers (200 rpm) in a heating chamber at 34 °C. The culture medium was sparged with sterile N2 overnight.

pH and ORP were monitored using a pH Sensor InPro3253i/SG/225 (Mettler-Toledo, Urdorf, Switzerland). For EF, the WE was polarized at -510 mV/SHE after inoculation, while no polarization was applied in classical fermentation. All potentials in this study are expressed in mV/SHE. The N2 sparging was stopped 30 min after inoculation. Samples can be taken through tubing connected to a non-return valve and analyzed using the methods described in the following section. One biologic replicate was performed for each condition in this study.

In the EF experiments, the number of electrons emitted from the cathode was determined and the EF coefficient ηEF was calculated according to Moscoviz et al. [31] as follows:ηEF=Qe-Qproduct, where Qe- is the charge transferred through the electric circuit and obtained by integrating the intensity measured over the time, and Qproduct is the total charge released when the products formed are fully oxidized.

Analytical methods and calculations

The bacterial growth was estimated by measuring the optical density (OD) at 600 nm with a M4 UV–Vis spectrophotometer (VWR, Radnor, PA, USA). For chromatographic analyses, the supernatant was centrifuged at 4500 × g for 15 min (Centrifuge 5804, Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany), filtrated at 0.2 µm and diluted with an internal standard (1-propanol, 0.55 g.L−1).

Residual glucose concentration was quantified by injecting 20 µL of filtered sample in a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with a MetaCarb 87P column (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) coupled to a Refractomax 521 RI Detector (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). Ultrapure water served as the mobile phase with a flow rate of 0.4 mL.min−1. The column was maintained at 85 °C in a 50-min run. A calibration curve ranging from 0.15 g.L−1 to 2.50 g.L−1 was prepared using external standards at the beginning of each analysis sequence.

Acid concentrations were determined by injecting 10 µL of filtered sample in an HPLC with an Aminex HPX-87H column (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) equipped with a RID-20A refractometer (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) and an SPD 20-A UV–Vis Detector set at 210 nm (Shimadzu). The mobile phase was 5 mM sulfuric acid at a flow rate of 0.6 mL.min−1. The column temperature was set at 60 °C in a 60 min run. A calibration curve ranging from 0.05 g.L−1 to 1.00 g.L−1 was prepared using external standards at the beginning of each analysis sequence.

Solvent concentrations were evaluated by injecting 1 µL of filtered sample into a gas chromatography using a Rt-Q-BOND column (30 m length, 0.32 mm internal diameter, 10 µm film thickness, Restek, Bellefonte, PA, USA) coupled with a flame ionization detector. Helium was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.6 mL.min−1. The column was heated gradually from 50 to 200 °C over 16 min. Standard solutions containing ethanol, acetone, 2-propanol, 1-propanol, and butanol at 0.5 g.L−1 were analyzed every ten samples to verify the validity of the analyses.

Metabolite concentrations were reported in g.L−1 or g C.L−1; the yields were expressed in g C.g Csubstrate−1 to better reflect the carbon flows, taking into account the acetate added to the culture medium; the B/A ratios were expressed in g C.g C−1, except in Table 2 where they are reported in g.g−1 for comparison with literature data.

Reductase activity measurement

Reductase activity (RA) of the cells was determined using a CyFlow Space flow cytometer (Sysmex, Kobe, Japan) equipped with a 488 nm wavelength emission laser and four light detection filters. Forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC) detectors provided information on cell size and granularity, respectively; green fluorescence intensity (GFI) was captured using a band-pass filter (530 ± 15 nm); red fluorescence intensity was collected with a high-pass filter (> 630 nm). Fluorescence data were analyzed using the Flomax software (Sysmex).

Cells were stained immediately after sampling using BacLight™ RedoxSensor™ Green Vitality Kit (Thermo Fisher), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Samples were diluted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) to achieve a cell concentration suitable for cytometer analysis. For the RA assay, 10 µL of 0.1 mM RedoxSensor™ Green (RSG) and 10 µL of 1.5 mM propidium iodide (PI) were added to 1 mL cell suspension, followed by incubation at 37 °C in the dark for 10 min. For the negative control assay, 2 µL of 5 mM carbonyl cyanide 3-chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP) were added to 1 mL of the diluted cell suspension and incubated for 5 min at room temperature before the addition of RSG and PI. Stained cells were analyzed immediately after incubation. The GFI is expressed in arbitrary unity (AU).

Results and discussion

MV slowed down metabolism while the presence of electrode had no impact on fermentation kinetics

Four batch fermentation conditions were tested to evaluate the effect of EF and the addition of methyl viologen (MV) as an electron carrier on fermentation kinetics of Clostridium acetobutylicum ATCC 824. A control open-circuit fermentation (OC) was compared to a fermentation with the addition of 0.5 mM MV (OC + MV), a fermentation with the working electrode polarized at − 510 mV/SHE (EF), and a fermentation combining an identical polarized electrode with the addition of 0.5 mM MV (EF + MV). The kinetics of growth, glucose consumption, acids production and solvents production are shown in Fig. 3. Detailed kinetics of acids and solvents productions can be found in the supplementary information (Figs. S1 and S2, respectively). Metabolite concentrations are expressed in g C.L−1 throughout this section.

Kinetics of growth (noted OD), glucose consumption, acids and solvents production for the control fermentation with open circuit (a), the electro-fermentation (b), the fermentation supplemented with 0.5 mM MV (c) and the electro-fermentation with 0.5 mM MV (d)

The addition of MV slowed the kinetics of glucose consumption and products formation compared to the control condition, whereas EF with or without MV showed no effect on these kinetics. The experiments in all conditions followed the typical biphasic metabolism of the strain [7].

During the first 6 h of fermentation, the control culture was in the acidogenic phase: cells consumed glucose to produce mainly acids, accompanied by significant cell growth as indicated by an increase of the OD. Despite acetate in the initial medium, the concentrations of acetate and butyrate increased (Fig. S2), reaching a total acid concentration of about 2.3 g C.L−1 at 6 h of fermentation (Fig. 3). After 6 h, solventogenesis began, with the cells continuing to assimilate glucose, reconsuming acids, and beginning to produce solvents. The control culture then entered the stationary phase: OD increased until 24 h and stabilized around 7 units at 30 h. With the addition of MV at a low concentration (0.5 mM), the OD increased less rapidly at the beginning of the fermentation (Fig. 3c). Between 24 and 30 h, the glucose consumption rate and solvent productivity were maximum, reaching 0.92 g C.L−1.h−1 and 0.65 g C.L−1.h−1, respectively, for control condition (Fig. 3a). The addition of MV resulted in a decrease of sugar consumption, with both the maximum glucose consumption rate and the solvent productivity decreasing by 31% to 0.63 g C.L−1.h−1 and 0.45 g C.L−1.h−1, respectively (Fig. 3c). After 30 h, in the control condition, glucose assimilation and solvent production decreased drastically. Solvent concentration exceeded 12 g C.L−1, and the butanol concentration became toxic to the cells [7, 42]. However, the acid concentration increased slightly from 30 h (Fig. 3a). Acetate was no longer consumed; butyrate was still produced and a small fraction did not appear to be reconsumed to form butanol (Fig. S1). With MV, glucose was still consumed between 30 and 47.5 h, resulting in significant solvents production (4 g C.L−1) with no butyrate formation (Fig. 3c). Overall, glucose consumption, solvents production and acids reassimilation were slowed down throughout the fermentation with MV addition. These decreases were consistent with the metabolic perturbations induced by MV toxicity [43]. Peguin et al. observed that MV decreased the growth rate and increased the lag phase [12]. In their study, the batch fermentation of C. acetobutylicum ATCC 824 at a controlled pH of 4.5 was 50% slower and biomass was divided by more than half with addition of 1 mM MV. Rao and Mutharasan also reported a slower solvents formation when 0,1 g.L−1 of MV was added in the batch fermentation of C. acetobutylicum NRRL B591 [15]. In our study, the OD started to decrease after 30 h in all conditions. In fact, at the end of the stationary phase, cells aggregated to form flocs, making the OD of the liquid phase sample less representative of the actual biomass present in the bioreactors. The fermentation was finished after 47.5 h when glucose was no longer consumed, and solvents began to evaporate (Fig. 3).

For EFs, with the WE polarized at − 510 mV/SHE, the kinetics of growth, glucose consumption, acid production, and solvent production were indistinguishable from those observed without MV (Fig. 3b) and with MV (Fig. 3d). The detailed kinetics of acid and solvent production (Fig. S1 and S2) were also unchanged. This suggests that under our culture conditions, which allowed rapid growth and high solvent production (near-optimal conditions), EF did not provide a significant benefit to the cells. These results contrasted with some previous literature reports which showed that EF can influence the fermentation kinetics of solventogenic Clostridia, under less favorable conditions, especially when solvent productivity was lower than that presented in our study under control condition [35] or when minimum media were used [34, 37], leading to decreased solvent production compared to our results. Molognoni et al. reported a 21% increase in butanol + acetone productivity when the WE was polarized at − 600 mV/SHE reaching a maximum of 0.14 g.L−1.h−1 [35], while fermentation EF in our study (Fig. S1b) achieved more than three times this productivity. He et al. reported a higher growth rate of C. beijerinckii IB4 under EF conditions (− 500 mV/SHE) with or without an electron carrier (neutral red), which also resulted in an increase of solvent productivity and glucose consumption rate as well [37]. Similarly, Engel et al. showed that C. acetobutylicum DSM 792 exhibited twice the glucose consumption rate with EF (− 400 mV/SHE) compared to control fermentation without electron carrier [34]. These comparisons suggest that EF could be more beneficial under conditions where fermentation is limited by sub-optimal growth or metabolic activity, as it could help to overcome these constraints. However, when optimal or near-optimal conditions are already achieved, the impact of EF may be marginal. In this context, it would be interesting to investigate the role of EF under more challenging conditions, such as fermentations using industrially relevant substrates, which typically result in lower fermentation performance than glucose-based media.

Effects of MV on the final performances

MV affected the metabolism of C. acetobutylicum ATCC 824 by redirecting the carbon flow toward butanol at the expense of acetone. With the addition of 0.5 mM MV, the butanol concentration increased by 11%, from 14.2 g.L−1 without MV to 15.8 g.L−1 (Fig. 4a), and the butanol yield increased by one third (Table 1). On the other hand, the acetone concentration decreased by half from 7.7 to 3.6 g.L−1, as did the acetone yield. MV significantly changed the B/A ratio, which increased from 1.93 to 4.57 g C.g C−1 (Table 1). A higher butanol yield naturally resulted in a lower glucose consumption at the end of the fermentation due to the butanol toxicity [7, 42]. With MV, glucose consumption decreased by 16%, followed by a 10% decrease of total solvent concentration (Fig. 4a), while the solvent yield increased by 6% (Table 1). The ethanol concentration remained unchanged. The final acid concentration decreased by 21% with MV, mainly due to a 40% decrease in butyrate concentration, while acetate and lactate concentrations were not affected (Fig. 4c).

Several research teams have demonstrated similar effects of MV on sugar consumption, solvents production and solvents selectivity in C. acetobutylicum fermentation. Kim & Kim indicated that the addition of 2 mM MV to C. acetobutylicum ATCC 4259 fermentation had minimal impact on glucose consumption but changed butanol/acetone concentrations (g.L−1) from 5.5/2.2 in the control condition to 6.4/1.9 with MV [11]. Peguin et al. reported ABE concentrations (g.L−1) of 1.6/11.2/1.0 with 1 mM MV, compared to 5.2/10.0/1.0 without MV in batch fermentation of C. acetobutylicum ATCC 824 carried out at a controlled of 4.5 [12]. In their study, glucose assimilation and the solvent production decreased by 10% and 15%, respectively. Using ten times less MV (0.1 mM), Al-Shorghani et al. observed no effect of MV on final glucose consumption (50 g.L−1) and solvents concentrations (15 g.L−1) in batch fermentation of C. acetobutylicum YM1 [13]. However, they also found altered ABE concentrations (control: 3.3/10.6/1.3 g.L−1, with MV: 2.0/12.5/0.9 g.L−1).

Impact of electro-fermentation on fermentation performances

The effects of EF on final performances were also investigated with and without MV. Under the conditions of this study, fermentation performances were only slightly affected by EF. Acetone and butanol production showed slight variations, but with no significant differences with or without EF. The butanol and acetone concentration and consequently, the selectivity for butanol over acetone were almost identical (Fig. 4b and Table 1).

Moreover, the solvent yield was not affected by the polarization of the electrode without MV (Table 1). With MV, the glucose consumption and the amount of solvent produced were identical to the fermentation with MV alone, which also resulted in an equivalent solvent yield.

In the literature, several research teams aiming at improving the performance of solventogenic Clostridia have observed an increased butanol concentration and B/A ratio with EF with or without electron carriers at a working potential between − 400 and − 600 mV/SHE. However, it should be emphasized that these reports did not achieve high solvent concentrations and therefore toxic butanol titers attained in this study. The results obtained here and those reported in the literature are summarized in Table 2. The following part aims to provide a brief review of the performances of solventogenic Clostridia focusing on solvents concentration and selectivity (B/A ratio), in order to better understand the impact of cathodic EF on the metabolism of these strains.

Kim & Kim were the first to study EF of C. acetobutylicum (strain ATCC 4259) with the addition of 2 mM MV [11]. Compared to this study, they reported a solvent concentration half as low. However, they observed an 8% increase in butanol concentration and a 24% increase in B/A ratio between the fermentation and the EF both with MV in a complex culture medium (yeast extract: 4 g.L−1) using glucose as the carbon source. Similarly, Peguin et al. obtained comparable results with C. acetobutylicum ATCC 824 in a minimal medium supplemented with a lower MV concentration (1 mM) and a higher applied potential to the WE, ranging from − 454 to − 654 mV/SHE during fermentation [44]. Their study showed a 7% increase in the molar yield of butanol during EF with MV compared to fermentation with MV alone. The maximum butanol concentration they achieved (approximately 13.5 g.L−1) remained obtained here. Surprisingly, Engel et al. observed a 60% increase in butanol concentration without electron carrier, by polarizing the WE at − 400 mV/SHE during fermentation of C. acetobutylicum DSM 792 [34]. However, the fermentations were performed in a poorly buffered unregulated minimal culture medium, with a glucose concentration of 45 g.L−1. It seems likely that the performance of the control fermentation was limited by a phenomenon called “acid crash” [45], resulting in a final total acid concentration of 12.8 g.L−1 and an ABE solvent concentration not exceeding 6 g.L−1. All glucose was consumed during EF, but this strain can consume nearly 70 g.L−1 of glucose under batch conditions [46], thus ending the fermentation when the substrate is depleted. The authors noted that polarizing the WE at -400 mV /SHE thermodynamically favored the reduction of redox coenzymes below the NAD+/NADH and NADP+/NADPH redox potentials. The probable reduction could contribute to initiate the solventogenesis. However, they did not observe any effect of EF on the B/A ratio, which actually decreased under their conditions [34]. This suggests that EF may counteract an acid crash occurring in their control fermentation but did not affect solvent selectivity. Zhang et al. observed very low solvent concentrations and glucose consumption with C. beijerinckii NCIMB 8052 [38, 39]. Although this species can produce less ABE solvents (about 16 g.L−1) [47, 48] than C. acetobutylicum, Zhang et al. measured only 1 g.L−1 of butanol produced and 7 g.L−1 of glucose consumed after several days under the control condition in two studies [38, 39]. With EF, they achieved threefold [39] and fourfold [38] higher butanol production. They also observed an increase in the B/A ratio, although the results should be interpreted with caution due to the low solvent production in their control conditions. With the addition of electron carriers such as Neutral Red (NR) [38] and Methyl Viologen [39], the same authors showed that EF increased the butanol concentration (less than 6 g.L−1 maximum) and improved the B/A ratio up to 4.69 g.g−1 with NR (Table 2). Under similar experimental conditions, He et al. also found a slight increase of 4% in the B/A ratio with EF without NR, and a 15% increase with EF and NR with the strain C. beijerinckii IB4, a mutant of NCIMB 8052. Nevertheless, they reported higher ABE and butanol concentrations, around 14 and 10 g.L−1, respectively [37] (Table 2). Interestingly, Molognoni et al. recently demonstrated a significant improvement in the B/A ratio with EF of C. beijerinckii NCIMB 8052 without an electron carrier [35]. By polarizing the WE at − 600 mV/SHE, this ratio doubled to 7.71 g.g−1 with a 47% increase in butanol concentration, despite it did not exceed 6 g.L−1. Finally, Mogollón et al. studied C. saccharoperbutylacetonicum N1-4 under EF conditions (− 400 mV/SHE) [49]. This strain is known for its high ABE solvent production and has a metabolism close to that of C. acetobutylicum [50]. The authors observed an increase in both butanol and acetone concentrations, with the B/A ratio increasing by 9% [49].

In this study, EF appears to not have significant effect on the metabolism of solventogenic Clostridium under culture conditions that induce high ABE solvent production, as also observed by He et al. [37] However, EF significantly improved Clostridia fermentation performances with lower solvent concentrations production under specific experimental conditions, in particular improving both solvent titers and butanol selectivity. The most promising prospect is that EF could help to counteract the acid crash phenomena under certain conditions, a complex issue with certain solventogenic Clostridia.

Exploring the potential redox mechanisms involved

In this study, several methods were used to investigate the intracellular and extracellular redox mechanisms at play when the electrode was polarized, with or without MV.

Analysis of the reductase activity

The redox state of the cells under the four fermentation conditions was analyzed by flow cytometry. Cellular enzymes with a RA are involved in various processes, including some that play a role in the electron transport chain [51]. Intracellular RA was measured using RSG. This fluorochrome is an indicator of the metabolic state of cells [52]. RSG emits a green fluorescence after reduction by bacterial reductases, thus the fluorescence intensity correlates with the RA of the cells. The GFI over the fermentations is shown in Fig. 5.

The reductase activity of the cells over the fermentation in the 4 conditions

EF and MV both stimulated the RA, although MV had a greater effect on the performance of the fermentation of C. acetobutylicum ATCC 824. Interestingly, the electrode polarization resulted in a higher RA during the fermentation compared to the control condition. The GFI profile of EF condition was the same as the two fermentations with MV from 6 h of fermentation, suggesting that EF could influence the redox state of the cells.

For all conditions, the RA was high in the first hours of the fermentation and then decreased to zero at the end. Therefore, the RA was higher during acidogenesis, whereas it slowed down during solventogenesis. This observation could be correlated to the increased concentration of reduced coenzymes, at the beginning of the batch fermentation of solventogenic Clostridia, which later decreased when NAD(P)H was used to produce alcohols (ethanol and butanol) [53, 54].

In the fermentations with MV (OC + MV and EF + MV), the RA was higher during the first 6 h of culture. This suggested that MV stimulated the RA during acidogenesis. MV can be reduced by pyruvate-ferredoxin oxidoreductase [55] and preferentially serves as a substrate for NAD(P)-ferredoxin oxidoreductase rather than hydrogenase [12, 29] (Fig. 1). Consequently, the concentration of reduced coenzymes (NAD(P)H) increased, that may be associated with a higher RA. These results were consistent with the fermentation performances detailed in the previous section and provided first insights into the intracellular redox state of C. acetobutylicum ATCC 824.

pH and ORP profiles

The pH and ORP profiles confirmed the influence of MV on the fermentation of C. acetobutylicum ATCC 824 but did not show a significant impact of EF. These profiles are presented in the Fig. 6.

PH (a) and ORP (b) profiles monitored over the fermentation in the 4 conditions

Focusing on pH, the profiles were different between fermentations with and without MV but not with EF (Fig. 6a). With MV, the pH decreased during the first 10 h of fermentation and then increased (acidogenesis then solventogenesis [7]) to exceed 5.2 units by the end. Without MV, the pH followed the same pattern but decreased after 20 h and then oscillated, stabilizing at around 4.7 units. The 0.5 unit difference in final pH can be attributed to residual acid concentrations. In fact, acid concentrations were lower with MV with or without EF compared to those without MV (Fig. 4a).

Regarding the ORP, there were slight differences between the four conditions, and the profile was consistent with those reported in the literature for batch fermentations of solventogenic Clostridia [12, 22, 24, 32]. At the beginning of the fermentation during acidogenesis, bacteria rapidly reduced the medium due to their reductive metabolism, causing the ORP to drop to an average value of − 270 mV/SHE over the fermentation (Fig. 6b). This reduction was caused by glucose consumption, which increased the reducing power [32]. The medium remained reduced as long as glucose was consumed, and gas was produced. At the end of fermentations, the broth was oxidized due to potential oxygen entry into the reactors. ORP variations mirrored pH changes according to Nernst’s law [56]: as pH increases, ORP decreases, and vice versa. This could explain the 15 mV difference observed between fermentations with MV and without MV between 24 and 36 h of culture. Consequently, the environment was slightly more reductive in the presence of MV during the solventogenesis phase and could facilitate the production of reduced molecules, particularly butanol. Moreover, at equivalent pH, after 1.5 h of fermentation, the ORP reached − 325 mV/SHE with MV (OC + MV and EF + MV) compared to − 300 mV/SHE in the other conditions (OC and EF). This difference probably corresponded to the reduction of MV by bacteria. On the other hand, the ORP measured in the EF conditions was nearly identical to that measured without the polarized electrode. Polarizing the electrode to − 510 mV/SHE did not alter the extracellular redox potential, suggesting that it may have little effect on the intracellular redox potential and consequently on the NAD(P)H/NAD(P)+ ratio. Research teams have controlled the extracellular ORP to induce a redox imbalance and improve the B/A ratio during batch cultures of C. acetobutylicum using alternative strategies such as adding DTT, a reducing agent [24] or air sparging [22, 23].

Therefore, while polarizing the electrode to − 510 mV/SHE did not significantly affect the extracellular redox potential, it is crucial to consider the electron balance to understand the effects of reduction at the cathode surface during EF.

Electron balance

By analyzing the chronoamperometric profiles (data not shown), the amount of electrons transferred through the system and emitted at the surface of the stainless steel WE can be calculated. The number of electrons present in the products can also be quantified. In EF, 2.96 mmol of electrons were emitted without MV, while 5.46 mmol were emitted with MV, indicating that reduction reactions occurred on the cathode surface. However, these electrons represented only a small fraction of those available in the final products. EF coefficients were 0.08% and 0.16% for conditions without and with MV, respectively. Similar results have been reported by several research teams working with solventogenic Clostridia [34, 49, 57, 58]. With MV, the release of electrons at the cathode surface was increased, leading to a higher reduction current. These electrons may either directly reduce redox coenzymes NAD+ and NADP+ without MV or reduce MV itself. These reductions were thermodynamically favorable at the electrode surface, and the reduced MV can subsequently generate reducing power. However, water can also be reduced to H2 with the potential applied to the cathode. Finally, the amount of electrons was insufficient to fully explain the difference in butanol production: 27.5 mmol and 90.7 mmol of additional electrons for butanol formation without and with MV, respectively. It appears that even a small amount of electrons can significantly affect redox metabolism and lead to metabolic perturbations, but the effects are not yet understood [34, 49, 57]. However, MV directly interacted with the metabolism and was the main cause of the changes in the B/A ratio.

Conclusion

In this study, fermentations of the ABE solvent-producing Clostridium acetobutylicum ATCC 824 were performed with or without EF (− 510 mV/SHE) and 0.5 mM MV in two-chambers single-compartment reactors. The results showed that butanol selectivity over acetone was more influenced by the presence of MV rather than by EF. The kinetics were slowed with MV, but no change was observed with EF. Butanol concentration and the B/A ratio were increased by 11% and 137% with the addition of MV but not significantly affected with the polarization of the electrode. Therefore, under the conditions of this study, fermentation of C. acetobutylicum ATCC 824 with MV achieved a high butanol concentration, (15.8 g.L−1) and yielded an increased selectivity for butanol over acetone with a B/A ratio of 4.57 g C.g C−1. RA analysis confirmed the effect of MV on the metabolism. Ongoing experiments aim to further characterize the redox state of the cells using several methods, with results to be presented in an upcoming study.
