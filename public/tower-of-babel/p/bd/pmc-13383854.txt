Primary signet-ring carcinoma of the urinary bladder: A case report and mini review of literature
Authors: Binyam Mohammedbirhan Berhe, Birhanu Kassie Reta, Haftom Guesh Girmay, Daniel Kassie Molla, Tariku Mulatu Bore, Melaku Abay Muluneh, Seydu Worku
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary signet ring cell carcinoma (SRCC) of the urinary bladder is an exceptionally rare and aggressive malignancy. We report a case of a 60-year-old man presenting with painless gross hematuria, urinary frequency, and dysuria. Imaging and cystoscopy revealed a bladder lesion, while histopathology demonstrated diffuse signet ring cell infiltration with detrusor muscle invasion, confirming muscle-invasive disease. Extensive investigations, including upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy, chest radiography, and prostate biopsy, excluded secondary malignancies. Despite the lack of immunohistochemistry, diagnosis was established through clinicopathologic correlation. This case highlights the importance of thorough evaluation for accurate diagnosis and appropriate management.Keywords: Urinary bladder, Signet ring cell carcinoma, Muscle-invasive bladder cancer, Hematuria

Introduction

Primary adenocarcinoma of the bladder accounts for less than 2% of bladder malignancies, with the signet ring cell subtype being exceedingly rare. Histologically, it's characterized by mucin-filled tumor cells displacing the nucleus peripherally, giving it a classic signet ring appearance.1, 2, 3, a, b

A major diagnostic challenge is distinguishing primary bladder signet ring cell carcinoma from metastatic involvement, most commonly from the gastrointestinal primaries. This distinction is critical, as it directly influences management and prognosis.4, 5, 6

Case presentation

A 60-year-old male presented with a 3-month history of painless gross hematuria, associated with urinary frequency and dysuria. There was no history of weight loss, abdominal pain, altered bowel habits, or prior malignancy. He has no history of cigarette smoking. On physical examination, no palpable abdominal masses or lymphadenopathy was noted.

Laboratory investigations revealed mild anemia while renal function tests were within normal limits. Urinalysis confirmed hematuria without evidence of infection. Ultrasound examination demonstrated focal thickening of the urinary bladder wall. Further evaluation with a contrast-enhanced CT scan showed an irregular area of wall thickening involving the posterior bladder wall without definitive evidence of regional or distant metastasis. Urine cytology was done and the report revealed presence of numerous atypical cells.

Cystoscopic examination revealed an ulcerative lesion on the posterior wall of the bladder, and multiple biopsies were obtained. Microscopic examination (Fig. 1) revealed diffuse infiltration of the bladder wall by malignant cells exhibiting abundant intracytoplasmic mucin displacing the nuclei to the periphery, consistent with signet ring cell morphology. The tumor cells were arranged in diffuse sheets and singly dispersed infiltrative patterns within a desmoplastic stroma. Importantly, tumor infiltration into the detrusor muscle (muscularis propria) was identified, confirming muscle-invasive disease (at least pT2). No conventional urothelial carcinoma component was identified in the sampled tissue.

As part of the workup for possible primary sites, esophagogastroduodenoscopy, colonoscopy, chest radiographs and prostatic core needle biopsy were done, and all were non-revealing. The patient was counselled regarding the diagnosis of muscle-invasive bladder cancer and the need for definitive treatment, including radical cystectomy with or without systemic chemotherapy. At the time of reporting, no surgical intervention had been performed; the patient refused further management and went home against medical advice.

Discussion

Primary signet-ring cell carcinoma (SRCC) of the bladder is an exceptionally rare and aggressive cancer that is usually diagnosed at an advanced stage. While bladder adenocarcinomas make up only about 2% of epithelial bladder tumors, the SRCC subtype is even more uncommon, accounting for just 0.24% of all primary bladder cancers.1 The majority of patients are in their seventh to eighth decade of life with male preponderance. Approximately 25% of patients show distant metastasis, and about 50% of cases present with locally advanced disease at the time of diagnosis.2 Signet-ring cell carcinoma is far more common outside the urinary bladder and is mostly seen in the gastrointestinal tract. Hence, the possibility of metastasis should always be considered, and a thorough workup is needed to rule out a primary elsewhere.3,4

The histopathogenesis of primary mucin-producing adenocarcinomas, including signet-ring cell carcinomas, remains unclear because the normal bladder contains neither columnar nor mucus-secreting glandular epithelium. Many investigators proposed that the metaplastic potential of the urothelium has two distinct patterns. Progressive invagination of hyperplastic epithelial buds into the lamina propria (Von Brunn's nest) leads to the formation of cystitis cystica. Subsequent metaplasia of the urothelial lining within these cysts to columnar mucin-producing cells results in the production of cystitis glandularis, which is a premalignant lesion. Alternatively, cuboidal or columnar metaplasia of the surface epithelium can occur without downward invagination, with chronic bladder irritation and infection being the predisposing factors of these changes. Most of these tumors are mucin-secreting, but the passage of mucus during micturition is uncommon. Two-thirds of the tumors were mucin-secreting, in most of which the site of the deposition was extracellular (interstitial). Less commonly, mucin is secreted within the lumen of the acini, and infrequently, excessive intracellular mucin displaces the nucleus to a peripheral crescent, giving the cells a signet-ring appearance.4

The histologic diagnosis was based on the presence of signet ring cells filled with cytoplasmic mucus containing vacuoles compressing and displacing the nucleus into a peripheral crescent alongside the cell wall. The component signet ring cells are variable; it is >75% in almost half the cases.5

The usual clinical presentation does not differ significantly from that of other bladder malignancies.6 Hematuria is the most commonly described clinical presentation, which is also the presentation of our patient. However, other forms of presentation were also described, such as dysuria, daytime urinary frequency and urinary incontinence or retention.7 Signet-ring cell carcinomas of the bladder were most often infiltrative and diffusely involving the majority of the bladder. Radiological features are often nonspecific. Therefore, cystoscopic evaluation is extremely important. It is essential to distinguish this carcinoma from metastases, as different therapeutic strategies are often necessary.4,6

Primary signet-ring cell carcinoma of the urinary bladder shares identical histologic features with tumors arising in organs such as the gastrointestinal tract, breast, lung, gallbladder, and prostate. Consequently, comprehensive evaluation is essential to rule out a metastatic origin. In this case, assessment of the gastrointestinal tract with esophagogastroduodenoscopy and colonoscopy didn't reveal any primary lesion elsewhere.4,6

A chest radiograph should also be performed to exclude a primary pulmonary tumor. Additionally, prostate adenocarcinoma, another malignancy that may involve the bladder through direct extension and mimic primary bladder adenocarcinoma, should be ruled out by transrectal needle biopsy of the prostate.17 In our case the chest radiograph and prostate biopsy were non-revealing.

In the evaluation of signet-ring cell carcinoma (SRCC) involving the urinary bladder, excluding a metastatic primary tumor from an extravesical site is essential. The gastrointestinal tract represents the most common primary source of metastatic SRCC to the bladder. Immunohistochemical markers, including CK7, CK20, CEA, EMA, and CDX2, have limited value in distinguishing primary urinary bladder SRCC from metastatic gastrointestinal SRCC because their expression profiles frequently overlap. In contrast, the absence of nuclear β-catenin staining, together with the lack of an identifiable gastrointestinal lesion on endoscopic examination, supports a primary bladder origin. Membranous localization of β-catenin staining is also considered supportive of primary urinary bladder SRCC.12 Although immunohistochemistry can aid in differentiating primary from secondary tumors, it was not available in this case. Nevertheless, the combination of histopathologic morphology and thorough clinical, radiologic, and endoscopic evaluation allowed for a confident diagnosis.

Treatment modalities for signet ring cell carcinomas include surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy. Surgical options range from transurethral resection to radical cystectomy with urinary diversion. Most of the reported cases have undergone radical cystectomy with urinary diversion. But because most of these cancers were diagnosed at an advanced stage, the prognosis was very poor.18

Conclusion

This case highlights a rare presentation of primary SRCC of the urinary bladder with detrusor muscle invasion. Comprehensive evaluation, including negative upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy, is essential to exclude secondary involvement. Even in the absence of immunohistochemistry, careful clinicopathologic correlation can support the diagnosis. Early recognition is critical, as these tumors often present as muscle-invasive disease with aggressive behavior.
