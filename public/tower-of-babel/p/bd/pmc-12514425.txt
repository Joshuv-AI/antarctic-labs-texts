Risk Factors and Prognostic Significance of Severe Postoperative Complications After Curative Resection for Remnant Gastric Cancer: A Multicenter Retrospective Study
Authors: Kensuke Kudou, Mitsuhiko Ota, Kippei Ogaki, Yasue Kimura, Yuta Kasagi, Naomichi Koga, Hirofumi Hasuda, Hirotada Tajiri, Tetsuro Kawazoe, Yasuo Tsuda, Tomonori Nakanoko, Koji Ando, Eiji Oki, Tomoharu Yoshizumi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundRemnant gastric cancer (RGC) is associated with higher surgical difficulty and poorer long‐term outcomes than primary gastric cancer. However, the impact of postoperative complications on prognosis in RGC remains unclear.MethodsThis retrospective, multicenter cohort study included 126 patients who underwent curative surgery for RGC across five institutions between 2007 and 2024. Postoperative complications were evaluated using the Clavien–Dindo (CD) classification. Patients were stratified into two groups based on the presence of severe complications (CD grade ≥ 3a). Logistic regression analysis was used to identify risk factors for complications, and Cox proportional hazards models were applied to determine prognostic factors for overall survival (OS) and recurrence‐free survival (RFS).ResultsSevere postoperative complications occurred in 15.9% of patients. Multivariate analysis identified smoking (OR = 8.28, p = 0.0048), operative time ≥ 300 min (OR = 3.52, p = 0.0448), and blood transfusion (OR = 4.82, p = 0.0380) as independent risk factors for severe complications. Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated significantly poorer 5‐year OS (32.0% vs. 65.6%, p = 0.002) and RFS (33.6% vs. 64.3%, p < 0.001) in patients with CD grade ≥ 3a complications. CD grade ≥ 3a and pathological Stage II or higher were independent prognostic factors for both OS and RFS.ConclusionsSevere postoperative complications are associated with poor long‐term outcomes in patients undergoing curative surgery for RGC. Identifying and mitigating modifiable risk factors, such as smoking and operative invasiveness, may help improve surgical and oncological outcomes in this challenging population.Keywords: postoperative complication, prognosis, remnant gastric cancer

Introduction

Remnant gastric cancer (RGC) is defined as gastric cancer that arises in the residual stomach following gastrectomy due to benign or malignant disease. Previous reports have shown that the incidence of RGC was ~2%–3% [1]. Radical gastrectomy remains the standard curative treatment for RGC [2]. However, surgery for RGC is often technically challenging due to anatomical alterations, intra‐abdominal adhesions, and the frequent need for combined resection of adjacent organs. These factors contribute to the increased technical difficulty of surgery for RGC, which is believed to be associated with higher rates of postoperative complications and mortality.

Moreover, several studies have demonstrated that long‐term outcomes after radical gastrectomy for RGC are poorer than those for primary gastric cancer [3, 4]. However, the underlying reasons for the poor prognosis of RGC and its relationship with postoperative complications remain unclear.

Previous studies have investigated the impact of postoperative complications on long‐term prognosis in patients with various gastrointestinal cancers, such as esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, primary gastric cancer, and colorectal cancer, and have reported that complications are associated with poorer survival [5, 6, 7]. Although this association has not been clarified in the context of RGC, if postoperative complications are indeed independent predictors of poor prognosis, the relatively high complication rates observed after RGC surgery could be a potential contributor to the poor outcomes in these patients.

Therefore, in the present study, we focused on postoperative complications in patients who underwent curative surgery for RGC. Given the limited number of RGC cases per institution, we conducted a multicenter analysis to identify risk factors for postoperative complications and to evaluate their prognostic significance.

Methods

Patients

This retrospective, multicenter cohort study included patients from five institutions: Kyushu University, Kyushu Cancer Center, Kyushu Medical Center, Iizuka Hospital, and Kyushu Central Hospital. Patients from four institutions (excluding Kyushu University) who underwent surgery for RGC between October 2013 and October 2024 were included, along with patients from Kyushu University between July 2007 and October 2022. A total of 135 cases were reviewed. After excluding 7 patients who underwent palliative or noncurative surgery for Stage IV disease and 2 patients who underwent simultaneous surgery for synchronous double cancer, 126 patients were included in the final analysis.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kyushu University (Approval Number 24156‐00) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (revised in Fortaleza, Brazil, October 2013), available at https://www.wma.net/what-we-do/medical-ethics/declaration-of-helsinki/. Informed consent was obtained from all patients. For patients who could not be contacted directly, an opt‐out approach was adopted through institutional websites.

Statistical Analyses

Differences in characteristics between the groups were evaluated by using Fisher's exact test or the unpaired t‐test. Survival curves were plotted using the Kaplan–Meier method. Differences were analyzed using the log‐rank test. Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed using logistic regression analyses to identify risk factors for postoperative complications and the Cox proportional hazards model to identify independent prognostic factors. All p values were two‐sided, with p < 0.05 being statistically significant. For the Cox regression models, covariates with p < 0.1 in univariable analyses were included in the multivariable analysis to reduce the risk of overfitting, given the limited number of events. For transparency, the results of the full model including all candidate covariates are provided in the Supporting Information S1 and S2: Tables 1 and 2.

All statistical analyses were performed using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), a graphical user interface for R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) [8]. EZR is a modified version of R Commander, which is designed to add statistical functions frequently used in biostatistics.

Results

Patient Characteristics According to Severe Postoperative Complications

Patients were divided into two groups based on the presence or absence of severe postoperative complications (Clavien–Dindo (CD) classification grade ≥ 3a). Patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The CD ≥ 3a group had significantly higher proportions of male sex (95.0% vs. 74.5%, p = 0.044) and smokers (80.0% vs. 49.1%, p = 0.014). The mean C‐reactive protein (CRP) level was also significantly higher in this group (p = 0.006).

Operative Outcomes

The CD ≥ 3a group had significantly longer mean operative times (369.2 vs. 291.3 min, p = 0.001) and longer postoperative hospital stays (45.4 vs. 14.5 days, p < 0.001). In terms of details of postoperative complications, the CD ≥ 3a group showed higher rates of pancreatic fistula and postoperative bleeding, whereas respiratory complications and surgical site infections were more common in the CD < 3a group. Concomitant organ resections included the pancreas (n = 3), spleen (n = 4), colon (n = 2), liver (n = 2), gallbladder (n = 9), and diaphragm (n = 1). The frequency of multivisceral resection did not differ significantly between patients with and without severe complications (Table 2).

Adjuvant Chemotherapy

Adjuvant chemotherapy was administered in 21 patients (16.7%). There was no significant difference in the proportion of patients receiving adjuvant chemotherapy between the CD ≥ 3a and CD < 3a groups (25.0% vs. 15.1%, p = 0.439). The median time to initiation of adjuvant therapy was comparable between the two groups (45.0 vs. 49.0 days, p = 0.552) (Table 2).

Risk Factors of Postoperative Complications

To identify risk factors for severe complications (CD ≥ 3a), univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed. In univariate analysis, smoking (odds ratio [OR] = 4.150, p = 0.0161), body mass index (BMI) ≥ 25 (OR = 3.590, p = 0.0401), pathological Stage II or higher (OR = 3.290, p = 0.0237), operative time ≥ 300 min (OR = 4.100, p = 0.0113), and blood transfusion (OR = 3.360, p = 0.0356) were significantly associated with complications.

Multivariate analysis identified smoking (OR = 8.280, p = 0.0048), operative time ≥ 300 min (OR = 3.520, p = 0.0448), and blood transfusion (OR = 4.820, p = 0.0380) as independent risk factors for severe postoperative complication (Table 3).

Postoperative Prognosis Based on Severe Postoperative Complications

The median follow‐up duration for the entire cohort was 36 months (interquartile range: 1–123 months), during which 38 patients died. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated that 3‐year overall survival (OS) rates were 48.0% in the CD ≥ 3a group and 76.9% in the CD < 3a group, while the 5‐year OS rates were 32.0% and 65.6%, respectively (p = 0.002) (Figure 1a). Similarly, the 3‐year recurrence‐free survival (RFS) rates were 33.6% in the CD ≥ 3a group and 75.4% in the CD < 3a group, while the 5‐year RFS rates were 33.6% and 64.3%, respectively (p < 0.001) (Figure 1b).

Kaplan–Meier survival curves comparing (a) overall survival (OS) and (b) recurrence‐free survival (RFS) between patients with (Clavien–Dindo classification [CD] grade ≥ 3a) and without (CD grade < 3a) severe postoperative complications. Patients with CD grade ≥ 3a complications demonstrated significantly worse OS (p = 0.002) and RFS (p < 0.001).

Prognostic Factors for OS and RFS

To identify prognostic factors, Cox regression analyses were performed. Pathological Stage II or higher (hazard ratio [HR] = 2.437, p = 0.0152), and CD grade ≥ 3a (HR = 2.256, p = 0.0344), were identified as independent prognostic factors of poor OS in multivariate analysis (Table 4). For RFS, pathological Stage II or higher (HR = 3.671, p = 0.0002), intraoperative blood loss ≥ 500 mL (HR = 1.996, p = 0.0414), and CD grade ≥ 3a (HR = 3.373, p = 0.0004) were significant in univariate analysis; multivariate analysis confirmed Stage ≥ II or higher (HR = 2.852, p = 0.0033) and CD ≥ 3a (HR = 2.634, p = 0.0082) as independent prognostic factors (Table 5).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated risk factors for postoperative complications and their impact on long‐term prognosis in patients undergoing curative surgery for RGC. Smoking, prolonged operative time (≥ 300 min), and blood transfusion were identified as independent risk factors for complications. Furthermore, severe complications (CD grade ≥ 3a) were independently associated with poor long‐term prognosis.

Previous reports have shown complication rates for RGC ranging from 10% to 47% [3, 9, 10, 11], and postoperative mortality rates from 0% to 13% [12, 13, 14]. In our study, the overall complication rate was 31.7%, and surgery‐related mortality was 1.6%. Although the complication rate was relatively high, the rate of severe complications (CD grade ≥ 3a) was 15.9%. This might reflect our strict definition of complications, which included minor issues such as delirium and dumping syndrome.

Studies by Park and colleagues and Kwon and colleagues identified multivisceral resection, prolonged operative time, BMI ≥ 25, and transfusion as risk factors for complications after RGC surgery [9, 15]. Our results were consistent regarding operative time and transfusion; however, BMI ≥ 25 and multivisceral resection were not significant. Notably, smoking emerged as a novel independent risk factor.

The surgical difficulty in RGC is often influenced by the initial gastrectomy procedure and anastomosis type. Dense adhesions—more pronounced in open than laparoscopic or robotic procedures—may complicate the surgery, particularly in cases involving gastroduodenostomy (adhesions to the liver and pancreas) or gastrojejunostomy (adhesions to the jejunum and colon). These factors increase the risk of intraoperative injury, prolong operative time, increase blood loss, and may necessitate transfusion. Such surgical stress may contribute to complications such as pancreatic fistula, ileus, or bleeding, which were relatively frequent in our series. Respiratory complications were also relatively frequent, potentially due to the high prevalence of smoking.

The negative impact of postoperative complications on long‐term prognosis has been reported in various cancers. Saeki and colleagues demonstrated that the development of postoperative complications was an independent disease‐specific poor prognostic factor after curative resection for patients with less‐advanced esophageal squamous cell carcinoma [5]. Yu and colleagues showed that postoperative complications had a significant negative impact on the long‐term survival in gastric cancer patients, even if the tumor is resected curatively [6]. McArdle and colleagues have reported that the development of an anastomotic leakage was associated with worse long‐term survival after potentially curative resection for colorectal cancer [7]. However, similar studies focusing on RGC are scarce.

The mechanism underlying the impact of complications on prognosis remains unclear. One hypothesis is immunosuppression: surgical stress may impair natural killer cells and cytotoxic T lymphocytes [16, 17]. Another involves inflammation: neutrophils activated by infection may promote metastasis through neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), and NET inhibition has been shown to suppress cancer recurrence [18, 19]. Besides, the inflammatory cells in tumor tissues induce tissue remodeling and angiogenesis [20]. In addition, delays in adjuvant chemotherapy due to postoperative complications may also negatively affect prognosis [6, 21].

Moreover, patients with severe complications tended to have lower albumin levels and higher CRP values, suggesting impaired nutritional and inflammatory status. These baseline conditions may have predisposed patients both to developing severe complications and to experiencing poor long‐term outcomes, and should be considered a potential source of unmeasured confounding.

There are several limitations to this study. Although data were collected from multiple institutions, RGC remains a rare disease, and the total number of cases included was still relatively small. Furthermore, the retrospective nature of the study may have introduced inherent biases. In addition, tumor biology may have confounded both the development of severe complications and survival outcomes. In our cohort, five patients were clinically diagnosed as stage IVA, but some of them were down‐staged to pathological Stage II or III after resection, suggesting that clinical staging may overestimate disease extent. Although clinical stage IVA was not included in the multivariable models because of the small number of such cases (n = 5), it is possible that locally advanced disease, such as pT4b tumors requiring pancreas‐involving resections, may have predisposed patients to complications like pancreatic fistula and contributed to poor prognosis. This potential influence of tumor biology should be recognized as an important limitation of our study.

Conclusions

In this multicenter cohort study, we identified smoking, prolonged operative time, and blood transfusion as independent risk factors for severe postoperative complications following curative surgery for RGC. Moreover, severe complications were independently associated with poorer long‐term survival. These findings highlight the need for meticulous perioperative management and strategies to reduce surgical invasiveness to improve both short‐ and long‐term outcomes in patients with RGC.
