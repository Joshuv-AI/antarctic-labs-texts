Bioactive stem cell-laden 3D nanofibrous scaffolds for tissue engineering
Authors: Ozra Natouri, Abolfazl Barzegar, Abbas Nobakht, Afsaneh Bagheri, Farhad Eslami, Saeed Jafarirad, Nastaran Tohidifar, Behzad Nikzad
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents biomimetic nanoscaffolds composed of electrospun polycaprolactone-collagen (PCL-Coll) nanofibers, loaded with bioactive Arnebia euchroma (AE) extract and stem cells, to develop cell-based tissue engineering constructs. The incorporation of AE extract, known for its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, into the PCL-Coll nanofibers resulted in nanoscaffolds denoted as PCL-Coll/AE0, PCL-Coll/AE5, PCL-Coll/AE10, and PCL-Coll/AE15, corresponding to AE extract concentrations of 0.0, 5.0, 10.0, and 15.0 wt%, respectively. The PCL-Coll/AE nanoscaffolds exhibited high porosity values of 62.81 ± 4.78 %, 59.9 ± 2.10 %, 52.44 ± 2.66 %, and 46.32 ± 1.35 %, respectively, thereby providing an optimal 3D environment for cell attachment and proliferation. Analysis of the AE extract revealed the presence of abundant shikonin, a key bioactive compound, as indicated by its characteristic absorption bands at 490, 520, and 560 nm. FE-SEM data confirmed the homogeneous immobilization of AE compounds within the 3D nanofiber scaffolds, with fibers averaging 316 ± 137 nm in diameter, falling within the range of natural collagen fibers. To evaluate the structural integrity of the fully stem cell-laden scaffolds, we assessed cell growth, cell attachment within the PCL-Coll/AE nanoscaffolds, the cytoprotective effects of AE extract, and the expression levels of stemness-related genes, including Nanog, Rex1, Sox2, Oct4, Klf4, and C-Myc. Real-time PCR assays indicated that key indicators of stemness were upregulated, with average increases from 120 ± 15 % in PCL-Coll/AE0 to 250 ± 30 % in PCL-Coll/AE15 over a two-week period. These upregulations promote cell proliferation and facilitate cell cycle progression. Data indicate that increasing the concentration of bioactive AE extract within the scaffolds enhanced cell adhesion on the scaffold surface and improved cell viability after 7 days of culture, with viability rising from 109 ± 6.15 % in PCL-Coll/AE0 to 145.5 ± 10.11 % in PCL-Coll/AE15. Cytoprotective assays against oxidative stress induced by H₂O₂ revealed relative cell viability for PCL-Coll/AE0, PCL-Coll/AE5, PCL-Coll/AE10, and PCL-Coll/AE15 as 42.78 ± 5.85 %, 49.29 ± 6.79 %, 64.98 ± 3.32 %, and 78.68 ± 3.09 %, respectively. These results correlate with the antioxidant potency of AE extract compounds, particularly shikonin and its derivatives. Therefore, the cell-laden biomimetic PCL-Coll/AE15 scaffolds, which demonstrate sustained stemness features and a continuous local release of bioactive AE compounds, represent a promising candidate for tissue engineering applications.Keywords: Stem cell-laden nanoscaffolds, Biomimetic electrospun nanofiber, Tissue regeneration, Arnebia euchroma, Stemness-related genes

Introduction

Soft tissue regeneration, especially for complex 3D structures such as skin, cardiovascular tissue or fat, poses significant challenges in tissue engineering [1]. Many limitations are associated with the application of soft tissue engineering techniques. These include issues with cell survival, mechanical obstacles such as scaffold stability and accessibility, evaluation of the microenvironment composition, the potential to induce malignant transforms, and challenges with cell migration and depletion [2]. There are many factors for successful tissue regeneration, and scaffolds play an important role in engineering. Scaffolds are critical for providing structural support, mimicking the extracellular matrix (ECM), and guiding cell behavior during tissue regeneration [3]. The design and characteristics of scaffolds directly influence the regeneration process by facilitating mass transfer to the tissue core, offering appropriate mechanical properties, and interacting with cells and growth factors [4,5]. Scaffolds designed to mimic the in vivo stem cell microenvironment, when combined with stem cell technologies, offer a highly promising approach for inducing stem cell-derived tissue formation [6]. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have garnered significant interest in wound healing and regenerative medicine (skin wound healing [7], bone regeneration [8,9], cardiac repair [10]) due to their ability to secrete paracrine growth factors and cytokines [11], differentiate into specialized cell types, and modulate the immune response [12]. Stem cell research is a prominent topic in biomaterials, and an ideal graft should meet two criteria: directing stem cell differentiation and preserving stemness pluripotency [13,14]. Scaffold nanotopography, characterized by features on the nanometer scale, has been shown to influence both directed stem cell differentiation and the maintenance of stemness properties, critical for tissue regeneration [15]. Despite the many therapeutic benefits, a significant limitation of using stem cells with tissue scaffolds is the absence of biomimetic 3D niche microenvironments crucial for their stemness properties of long-term survival and functionality. Researchers are developing artificial stem cell niches, such as hydrogels [16], microspheres [17], and nanofibers [18], to mimic a naturalmicroenvironment for seeding stem cells.

Electrospun nanofibers (NFs) of polycaprolactone (PCL) blended with collagen are promising for soft tissue regeneration due to their biomimetic structure of ECM, promoting cell adhesion and proliferation, while also offering biocompatibility and mechanical support from the combination of materials [19,20]. However, further optimization of the scaffold microenvironment might be necessary to enhance cell functionality beyond adhesion and proliferation. This research investigates the potential of biomimetic scaffolds loaded with Arnebia euchroma (AE) extract to enhance stem cell proliferation and stemness for improved tissue regeneration applications. For centuries, the root of AE (Boraginaceae family) has been employed as a traditional remedy for various ailments, such as macular eruptions, measles, sore throat, carbuncles, and burns [21,22]. Shikonin, a fundamental constituent of AE, has been confirmed to possess more beneficial, which include anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antibacterial, antiparasitic, antifungal, and antiviral effects, as well as wound healing properties [23].

We hypothesize that integrating the ECM-mimicking properties of PCL-collagen nanofibers with the bioactive benefits of AE extract will generate a conducive biomimetic microenvironment for developing stem cell-laden scaffolds in tissue engineering. This study suggests that such ECM-mimicking bioactive nanoscaffolds could significantly enhance tissue regeneration by promoting cell survival, proliferation, and maintenance of stemness. This is achieved through the activation of key stemness factors, including Nanog, Rex1, Sox2, Oct4, Klf4, and C-Myc, which synergistically preserve stem cell identity, support proliferation, accelerate cell cycle progression, and reinforce the pluripotency of stem cells for tissue engineering applications.

Material and methods

Materials

We sourced the A. euchroma root from Darudarman Salafchegan (https://darudarmanco.com/en/about-us), who holds all associated collection data (location, methodology, permits). Glutaraldehyde (GA), DMSO, H2O2 and MTT were supplied by Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). Ethanol (EtOH), Acetic acid (AA), Formic acid (FA), n-Hexane, Diethyl ether and PCL (Mw = 80,000) were from Merck Chemical Co (Germany). PBS, DMEM, FBS, Trypsin, and Penicillin/Streptomycin were supplied from Gibco (Invitrogen, NY, USA). Rat tail-derived Collagen type I (Purity: 98 %) was obtained from Fannavaran Nano-Meyar Shaya Co (Tabriz, Iran). Adipose Derived Mesenchymal Stem Cells (ADSCs) was purchased from Royan Cell Bank of Iran. Double-distilled water was used in all experimental procedures. All reagents and chemicals used in the examine were of analytical gradе and wеrе еmployеd without extra purification.

Arnebia euchroma (AE) extraction procedure

A. euchroma roots were ground into powder and macerated in n-hexane for 72 h at room temperature. The extract was filtered (Whatman filter paper, pore size: 2.5 μm) and concentrated using a rotary evaporator (IKA, model RV 10 Digital, Germany) and desiccator to yield a red, oily AE extract. Fabrication and optimization of bead-less NFs

To optimize bead-free nanofiber (NF) formation, we investigated the influence of solution viscosity and solvent composition. PCL/Collagen (PCL-Coll) solutions were prepared at various concentrations (7.5, 10, 12.5, and 15 wt%) in different solvent mixtures (v/v) of acetic acid (AA) and formic acid (FA) (60:40, 70:30, 80:20, 90:10). Following the optimization process, the PCL-Coll solution was prepared under the same conditions. Subsequently, the AE extract was added into the solution at unique concentrations of 0.0, 0.5, 10, and 15 wt%. The developed four special samples have been labeled as PCL-Coll/AE0, PCL-Coll/AE5, PCL-Coll/AE10, PCL-Coll/AE15 nanoscaffolds, respectively. These resulting solutions were electrospun using a constant flow rate (0.65 ml/h), drum speed (360 rpm), and a voltage range of 23–25 kV. The needle-to-collector distance was adjusted between 14 and 16 cm to achieve bead-free NFs.

Characterization and mechanical testing

The morphology and surface features of the electrospun NFs were examined using a field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, MIRA3 FE-SEM, Tescan, Czech). Functional group analysis of the NFs was performed using Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR, Tensor 27, Bruker, Germany) in the wavenumber range of 400–4000 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1.

The mechanical properties of the scaffolds were evaluated using a universal tensile machine (model 1446, Zwick, Germany) equipped with a 5-kN load cell. Dog-bone shaped samples (width: 5 mm, gauge length: 20 mm) were tested at a controlled crosshead speed of 10 mm/min. The approximate thickness of the samples was 0.1 mm.

NFs porosity, permeability, swelling, and degradation

The porosity of the scaffolds was determined using the liquid displacement method with ethanol. After measuring the dry weight (Wd) and volume (V), the NFs were soaked in ethanol and weighed again (Ws) following ethanol removal. The porosity was calculated using Eq. (2).

Water vapor permeability was assessed using the Water Vapor Transmission Rate (WVTR) method. NFs were sealed onto a glass tube with a known area and filled with water. The weight loss after 24 h at 37 °C was used to calculate WVTR using Eq. (3).

To evaluate the swelling and degradation rates of the NFs, all 4 different scaffolds (22 cm2) were weighed (Wi) and immersed in PBS (37 °C, shaking). At set time points, samples were retrieved, blotted dry (Whatman filter paper), weighed again (Ws), and swelling percentage calculated using Eq. (4).

To achieve the desired degradation within 1–7 days, samples were oven-dried (40 °C) to constant weight (Wd). Degradation rate was calculated using Eq. (5). Morphology and structural changes were monitored by SEM analysis.

In-vitro release of AE extract

The release profile of AE extract from the scaffolds was monitored in PBS at 37 °C under shaking (150 rpm). Pre-weighed scaffolds (20–25 mg) were immersed in 20 ml of the release medium. At predetermined time points, samples of the medium were collected and replaced with fresh PBS to maintain volume. The amount of released AE extract was quantified by UV–vis spectrophotometry at the λmax (predetermined using a calibration curve) and calculated using the established calibration.

Nanoscaffolds and stem cell culture/viability

For cell culture, sterilized circular NF scaffolds were prepared by immersing them in 70 % ethanol (2 min), followed by three PBS washes and UV irradiation (20 min/side). Each well of a 24-well plate was filled with 1 ml of culture medium (DMEM, 1 g/L glucose, 10 % FBS, 1 % Pen/Strep) and seeded with ADSCs (predetermined density) in a humidified incubator (37 °C, 5 % CO2). Culture media was replaced every 48 h.

Cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay. Briefly, after a defined culture period, the medium was removed, and 100 μL of MTT solution (0.5 mg/mL in PBS, pH 7.4) was added per well. Following incubation (37 °C, 4 h), the medium was removed, formazan crystals were dissolved in DMSO, and the absorbance at 580 nm was measured using an ELISA plate reader for viability quantification.

Stem cell-laden scaffolds assessment

To assess the efficacy of our fabricated biomimetic nanoscaffold as a cell-based scaffold for tissue regeneration, we examined the attachment and morphological characteristics of ADSCs on the scaffold. Stem cell-laden 3D scaffolds, similar to those used here, have previously demonstrated the ability to promote the formation of granulation tissue, facilitate angiogenesis, and enhance collagen deposition, which are all crucial processes for successful tissue regeneration [24]. For this aim, ADSCs were seeded on PCL-Coll/AE0, PCL-Coll/AE5, PCL-Coll/AE10, PCL-Coll/AE15 nanoscaffolds at a density of 2×104 cells per well. At first, the NFs were shaped into circular forms to ensure compatibility with 24-well cell culture plates. Subsequently, the NFs were disinfected by immersing in a 70 % v/v EtOH solution for 2 min, followed by 3 repeated washes with sterile PBS. Sterilization of the NFs was achieved by subjecting both sides to 20 min of UV light exposure. Each well was filled with 1 ml of culture media, allowing the nanofiber scaffolds to hydrate for 24 h under standard conditions (humidified atmosphere, at 37°C, and 5 % CO2). After 7 days, the samples were washed twice with PBS and fixed using glutaraldehyde (2.5 % v/v) for 1 h. Then, the cellular included NFs were washed with PBS. An EtOH gradient (30 %, 50 %, 70 %, 80 %, 90 %, and 99 % v/v) changed into used to dehydrate the cell-protected NFs, and the samples were allowed to air dry at 4 °C. Finally, the SEM was used to imaging mesenchymal stem cell-laden PCL-Coll/AE scaffolds.

Cell protective capacity of AE-loaded PCL-Coll/AE NFs

ADSCs were cultured on the NFs for 48 h to assess the cytoprotective capacity of AE-loaded PCL-Coll mats under oxidative stress conditions. Following this, the samples underwent a 4-h treatment with 1 % H2O2 at 37 °C. After the incubation, the MTT assay was used to determine the relative viability of the cells at the scaffolds. All other conditions were also similar to the cell viability assessment test.

Assessing the expression levels of SRGs in stem cell-laden scaffolds

Real-time RT-PCR was performed to assess the expression levels of stemness-related genes (SRGs) in different stem cell-laden scaffolds from previous sections. A list of six SRGs was obtained from the previously published paper [25] and used to screen the effects of NFs scaffold on the stemness properties of ADSCs. Multipotent adult stem cells are a good choice for cell therapy because they possess proliferative potential with stemness properties to produce cytokines and growth factors with wound healing tissue engineering applications [7,11]. Different stem cell-laden scaffolds were achieved by culturing ADSCs on various types of NF mats (PCL-Coll/AE0, PCL-Coll/AE5, PCL-Coll/AE10, PCL-Coll/AE15) for 7 and 14 days. Total RNA was extracted using RNX-plus reagent (CinnaGen, Iran) following the manufacturer's instructions. For cDNA synthesis, 1g of general RNA turned into opposite transcribed using the Prime Script RT reagent package (Takara Bio, USA) in step with the producer's pointers (cDNA synthesis kits - Takara Bio, 2020). qRT-PCRs were performed using RealQ Plus 2 × Master Mix Green High ROX™ (Amplicon, Denmark) on Bio-Rad software. The expression levels of Nanog, Rex1, Sox2, Oct4, Klf4, and C-Myc genes had been normalized to the home tasks gene (β-actin) and in comparison with the untreated cells. The comparative 2−ΔΔCt approach was used to determine the expression level of every mRNA molecule. Oligo (Macrogen, Seoul, South Korea) synthesized the qPCR primers, and their sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1. The qPCR protocol consisted of one denaturation cycle at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 amplification cycles at 95 °C for 30 s, 54–64 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 30 s, and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. All experiments have been executed in triplicate.

Results and discussion

Arnebia euchroma (AE) extract

Hexane was chosen as the solvent for AE extract preparation due to its effectiveness in extracting shikonin from Arnebia plants [26]. Therefore, we have used hexane as a typical maceration solvent for achieving the AE extract containing rich shikonin. Maceration time was optimized, and the maximum yield was achieved after 72 h (Fig. 1). The obtained extract exhibited strong absorption in the visible region (450–600 nm), characteristic of shikonin [27] with its three peaks at 490, 520, and 560 nm (Fig. 1). Shikonin is a valuable pharmaceutical compound, and its incorporation into the nanofibers is expected to enhance cell growth and proliferation. Therefore, by complexing the AE extract with NFs, we expect that the resulting nanoscaffolds will demonstrate outstanding performance in promoting cell growth and proliferation.

Absorbance of the maceration medium of AE for determination of the optimal maceration time during time intervals (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 24, 48, 72 and 168 h).

Fabrication of biomimetic nanoscaffolds

Electrospinning with optimized parameters produced continuous, bead-free PCL-Coll nanofibers (Supplementary Fig. S1). Increasing polymer concentration and using an 80:20 AA/FA solvent ratio improved fiber uniformity. The shape of the PCL/Coll nanofibrous scaffold can intently resemble that of the native extracellular matrix (ECM) and be considered as a suggestion for biomimetic nanoscaffolds. The addition of AE extract (PCL-Coll/AE) up to 15 wt% maintained a porous nanofiber structure with minimal impact on diameter uniformity (Fig. 2). Data demonstrate that all the samples are bead-free, porous and, exhibit continuous nanofibers. Notably, the final average diameter (316 ± 137 nm) falls within the range of natural collagen fibers (50–400 nm) [28]. These biomimetic scaffolds with ECM-like features offer a promising microenvironment for cell growth and hold potential for tissue regeneration (explored later).

FE-SEM micrographs of the optimized NFs (scale bars: 10 μm) and the corresponding diameter histogram and statistical data. The images of electrospun polymer solutions and fabricated NFs collected on an aluminum foil also presented.

Nanoscaffold characterization (FT-IR and mechanical properties)

FT-IR analysis (Fig. S2) confirmed the successful incorporation of AE extract into the PCL-Coll nanofibers (PCL-Coll/AE). Within the functional group region of the PCL-Coll/AE frequency range, a broad peak was observed at 3432.62 cm−1, likely resulting from a combination of the extract's phenolic hydroxyl groups and collagen's N-H stretching band. The AE extract exhibited a distinct and strong band at 1734.98 cm−1, associated with the carbonyl group of naphthazarin, which experienced a slight shift upon encapsulation in the PCL-Coll nanofiber. The absorption peaks related to C-O stretching were observed at 1111.84, 1104.39, and 1103.06 cm−1 for AE, PCL-Coll, and PCL-Coll/AE, respectively. Frequencies of 2946.74 and 2916.09 cm−1 observed in the PCL-Coll and AE spectra corresponded to the asymmetrical stretching vibration of methyl and methylene groups. Likewise, frequencies of 2866.99 and 2848.02 cm−1 were associated with the symmetrical stretching vibration of the same groups. In the PCL-Coll/AE spectrum, these frequencies shifted to 2938.99 and 2865.48 cm−1. New peaks and slight shifts in existing peaks indicated interactions between the extract and the polymer matrix. Also, the observed shifts in the FT-IR spectra indicate new chemical and physical interactions between the polymer matrix and the extract.

The mechanical properties of the nanofibrous scaffolds were assessed in Fig. 3a. The addition of AE increased the maximum stress before fracturing (Fig. 3b), suggesting improved strength. However, there's no major difference in the final tensile strength between PCL-Coll/AE10 and PCL-Coll/AE15. Regarding elongation at break, the observed trend in the values indicates that the inclusion of AE leads to a decrease in the flexibility of the nanofiber groups (Fig. 3c). Therefore, the inclusion of AE extract weakens the PCL-Coll nanofibrous mats by diminishing polymer interactions, resulting in reduced chain entanglement and increased segmental mobility.

The stress-strain curves of PCL-Coll and AE-loaded PCL-Coll NFs (a), the nanofibers' tensile strength (b), and the elongation at break (c).

Nanoscaffolds porosity and swelling behavior

Well-designed porous scaffolds with swelling properties provide ample opportunity for cell migration, proliferation, and the exchange of oxygen and nutrients through interconnected pores which are encountered in tissue engineering applications [29]. The porosity of the scaffolds decreased with increasing AE content (Fig. 4a), ranging from 62.8 % (PCL-Coll/AE0) to 46.3 % (PCL-Coll/AE15). However, all scaffolds even, those with high concentrations of AE extract, exhibit a substantial level of mesh porosity for cell activity and nutrient exchange. The water vapor transmission rate (WVTR) followed a similar trend (Fig. 4b), indicating reduced water permeability with higher AE concentration. This suggests tighter structures with fewer diffusion paths for water molecules.

One-way ANOVA with Tukey post hoc analysis. n = 3; ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01 and ∗∗∗p < 0.001.

Moreover, the swelling ratios at equilibrium manner have been achieved (Fig. 4c) to be 312.13 ± 8.99 %, 288.67 ± 6.02 %, 251 ± 6.24 %, and 210 % for PCL-Coll/AE0, PCL-Coll/AE5, PCL-Coll/AE10 and PCL-Coll/AE15. The data indicates that all samples exhibited water adsorption rates exceeding 200 %. However, the inclusion of the concentrated bioactive AE extract led to a decrease in swelling values. This can be attributed to the hydrophobic nature of the AE extract, primarily due to the presence of the highly hydrophobic shikonin compound. Therefore, all of the abovementioned physical properties of nanoscaffolds, including mechanical resistance, porosity, WVTR and swelling assays, clearly indicate that there are inherent physically limitations in incorporating concentrated bioactive AE extract to fabricate highly bioactive nanoscaffold.

In vitro degradation of nanoscaffolds

The weight loss of the scaffolds over seven days was examined and presented in (Fig. 5a). The degradation of NFs follows a logarithmic pattern, characterized by an initial rapid release of biopolymers due to surface erosion. The initial weight loss of PCL-Coll nanofibers was mainly attributed to the leaching of collagen, as previously reported by Dulnik et al. [30]. This is subsequently succeeded by a more gradual bulk erosion originating from the material's core region. The degradation rate decreased with increasing AE content, likely due to reduced porosity and the hydrophobic nature of the extract. Fig. 5b shows the sustainability of nanofiberous scaffolds during in vitro degradation. The results revealed that after seven days of incubation nanoscaffolds in PBS, the majority of fibers in the matrix became swollen, and the fibers in all samples partially adhered to one another. Notably, the matrix of PCL-Coll/AE0 turned into fragments consisting of short fibers. In contrast, no significant morphological changes were observed in PCL-Coll/AE15. This is attributed to the hydrophobic compounds present in the extract, which helped maintain the fibrous structure of the NFs.

In vitro release of bioactive compounds

The bioactive compounds releasing profile is very important using scaffold in biological and nanomedicine application [31]. The released amount of bioactive AE molecules is depicted in Fig. 6. The release process followed a consistent trend in all samples. During the initial stage, samples loaded with AE exhibited an initial burst release. This phenomenon can be attributed to the diffusion of weakly bound AE molecules on the fiber surfaces. Following the initial burst, the release rate became more gradual. As the fibers swelled, a change in the release mechanism happened. Consequently, the release profile became flatter. Over 300 h–51 μg/ml were released from PCL-Coll/AE15, noting that higher AE content in the samples resulted in faster and more extensive release, likely due to a higher concentration gradient.

Stem cell characteristics in cell-laden PCL-Coll/AE nanoscaffolds

Our study investigated the potential of biomimetic nanoscaffolds laden with adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) for tissue regeneration. Stem cell-laden three-dimensional scaffolds that support cell viability can promote tissue regeneration by forming granulation tissue, collagen deposits, and enhanced angiogenesis [24]. To assess the completely cell-laden scaffold with cellular structural integrity, we evaluated their biocompatibility (cytotoxicity), cell growth (proliferation capacity), and adhesion properties. The results are presented in Fig. 7. Data indicated no statistically significant differences in ADSCs viability between PCL-Coll/AE scaffolds and the control group on the initial day of cell culture. However, the PCL-Coll/AE0 group exhibited significantly higher cell viability than the control group (116.41 ± 4.56 %). The increase in cellular growth on PCL-Coll/AE0 NFs on the first day can be potentially attributed to the nanofibrous unique morphology with biomimetic potency. The thinner fibers and higher porosity of the PCL-Coll/AE0 NFs may facilitate better cell penetration and colonization, thereby promoting enhanced cell proliferation. Moreover, the scaffolds containing bioactive AE extract exhibited a substantial increase in cell viability by the 7th day. Fig. 7a illustrates that the proliferation of ADSCs for a week is contingent upon the presence of the nanoscaffold as well as its integration with varying concentrations of bioactive AE extract. Increasing the concentration of bioactive AE extract within the scaffolds leads to increased cell viability after 7 days of cell culture rising from 109 ± 6.15 % in PCL-Coll/AE0 to 145.5 ± 10.11 % in PCL-Coll/AE15. These findings suggest that bioactive AE extract can enhance cell proliferation and viability within these scaffolds. The upregulation of pluripotent stem cell markers, associated with cell proliferation and cell cycle progression, further supports this conclusion (see section 3.8). The similar data also showed that genes related to reproduction and embryonic development in mouse embryonic stem cells, such as Oct4 and Nanog, were significantly upregulated after treatment with high concentrations of antioxidants from bamboo leaves [32].

Cytoprotective assays against H2O2 in Fig. 7b confirmed cell viability data. The relative viability of ADSCs for PCL-Coll/AE0, PCL-Coll/AE5, PCL-Coll/AE10 and PCL-Coll/AE15 was measured as 42.78 ± 5.85 %, 49.29 ± 6.79 %, 64.98 ± 3.32 %, and 78.68 ± 3.09 %, respectively. The results showed a significant increase in relative cell viability with increasing concentrations of AE extract in the scaffolds under oxidative stress conditions. The bioactive nanoscaffolds' ability to protect the viability of ADSCs against oxidative stress directly correlates with the antioxidant potency of AE extract compounds, particularly shikonin and its derivatives. The use of antioxidant compounds such as shikonin and alkanin has gained significant attention due to their potential to neutralize reactive oxygen species (ROS) and prevent cellular damage. These compounds possess numerous organic properties, such as wound restoration, anti-inflammatory, anticancer, and antimicrobial consequences [33]. Previous studies have demonstrated that AE root extracts possess antioxidant properties primarily attributed to their abundant polyphenols content particularly derivatives of shikonin [34,35]. Thus, we introduce bioactive nanoscaffolds, particularly those containing higher concentrations (10 % and 15 %) of AE extract, which can promote cell viability, proliferation, and protection against oxidative stress. This paves the way for developing effective stem cell-laden biomimetic 3D scaffolds for tissue repair applications. These findings, supported by the observed good cell adhesion and proliferation on the scaffold surface (Fig. 7c), demonstrate the promise of these bioactive scaffolds, particularly those with higher AE content, for promoting cell viability, proliferation, and protection against oxidative stress.

Stemness behavior of developed cell-laden PCL-Coll/AE nanoscaffolds

Stem cells have self-renewal and differentiation potentials with tissue engineering and regenerative properties, which are critically regulated by the expression levels of SRGs. Also, the stemness can diminish with age or ex vivo cultivation, limiting their applications. The SRGs are responsible for the finely tuned balance between keeping stem cell quiescent and undifferentiated, and allowing them to differentiate into different cell lineages in the process of tissue repair [36]. Thus, the expression of SRGs in ADSCs is crucial for maintaining their regenerative potential in tissue engineering, which was analyzed using qRT-PCR in biomimetic cell-laden PCL-Coll/AE nanoscaffolds. As shown in Fig. 8, the qRT-PCR results indicate an upregulation in the expression levels of all tested SRGs in ADSCs cultured on PCL-Coll/AE, which was dependent on both time and the concentration of bioactive AE compounds.As expected, the expression of all the selected SRGs exhibited a significant increase in ADSCs cultured on PCL-Coll/AE15 particularly after 14 days, with average SRGs expression increasing from 120 ± 15 % in PCL-Coll/AE0 to 250 ± 30 % in PCL-Coll/AE15. Previous studies have established that Nanog, Sox2, Oct4, Klf4, and C-Myc play crucial roles in tissue engineering by reprogramming adult cells into induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), which possess the self-renewal and differentiation capabilities similar to embryonic stem cells (ESCs). Oct4 and Sox2 work together to preserve stem cell identity, while Klf4 supports cell proliferation and C-Myc enhances cell cycle progression. Nanog further reinforces pluripotency, ensuring the cells remain undifferentiated [37]. By utilizing these factors, iPSCs can be derived from a patient's own cells, overcoming ethical concerns associated with ESCs and minimizing the risk of tissue rejection. Thus, iPSCs, through the action of these transcription factors, hold immense potential for advancing tissue engineering and regenerative medicine. Commonly, with aging and prolonged ex vivo culture, the expression of stemness-related genes declines, leading to a reduction in stem cell functionality and regenerative capacity [36]. This study emphasizes the importance of understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying stemness preservation and developing strategies to maintain stemness, ultimately enhancing the therapeutic potential of stem cell-laden nanoscaffolds in regenerative medicine.The nano-topography of scaffold surfaces has emerged as a critical factor influencing stemness preservation. The observed enhancement in ADSC proliferation and the induction of stemness-related gene expression on PCL-Coll/AE nanosacffolds can be partly attributed to the unique nano-topography of the scaffolds surface that incorporated with bioactive AE compounds. Moreover previous studies have highlighted the significant role of maintaining a balanced redox state in preserving stemness by upregulation of Oct4 and Nanog [32]. Excessive generation of ROS disrupts the equilibrium of redox homeostasis in stem cells, resulting in oxidative stress, compromised stem cell characteristics, and diminished stemness. Antioxidant systems, comprising enzymes and herbal metabolites, play an important position in defending against excessive ROS, thereby contributing preserving of cell redox homeostasis [38]. In this regard, AE extract renowned for its antioxidant compounds, emerges as a valuable complement to well-designed bioactive nanofibrous scaffolds, facilitating the preservation of stem cell stemness by inducing the expression of SRGs. It is expected that these efforts in introducing biomimetic stem cell-laden scaffolds blended with natural AE extract will enable functional repair of typical tissue injuries and regeneration.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study introduces bioactive and biomimetic stem cell-laden electrospun nanofibers, which have great potential for use in soft tissue engineering. These bioactive nanoscaffolds contain bioactive components, mainly shikonin derivatives, known for their beneficial effects on stem cell-based tissue regeneration. By incorporating these bioactive components into the nanofibers, resulted in stimulating in vitro proliferation and growth of ADSCs. Additionally, the bioactive nanoscaffolds demonstrated good cytoprotective activity, which helped preserve the stemness properties of ADSCs. This was evident through the increased expression of SRGs. Finally, the usage of PCL-Coll/AE nanofibers combined ADSCs indicates promise in developing robust and sustainable regenerative scaffolds which can enhance the tissue remodeling for soft tissue repair. Further investigations are warranted to explore the particular application of these stem cell-laden nanofibers in tissue engineering and regenerative medicinal contexts.
