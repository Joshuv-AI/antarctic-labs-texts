Exploring medical students' reflections and well-being during clinical training: a qualitative study of “peer reflecting teams”
Authors: Tine Lass Klitgaard, Trine Lignell Guldberg, Jette Kolding Kristensen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMedical students face significant demands, particularly during the transition to clinical training, which is associated with increased stress, anxiety, and a loss of established support systems. In this context, reflection is recognised as a key mechanism for promoting learning, professional identity formation, and well-being. However, engaging in meaningful reflection often requires structured support. This study introduces the concept of Peer Reflecting Teams and explores how medical students experience participation in such groups, and how junior residents, as near-peer facilitators, shape these reflective processes.MethodsA qualitative design was developed, including field observations of meetings (9 h) and five group interviews with both medical students (n = 16) and junior residents (n = 4). The data were analysed using an inductive thematic approach.ResultsThe participating medical students described the transition from university to clinical placements as a valuable yet abrupt and overwhelming experience. Analysis of the group interview data identified four key themes: [1] experiencing a safe and supportive space; [2] fostering a sense of community by sharing experiences; [3] developing strategies for navigating difficult clinical situations; and [4] the essential role of the near-peer facilitator in guiding and enriching the reflective process. All together, these themes illustrate how participation in peer-facilitated reflection groups not only offers a sense of community with peers, but also supports medical students’ professional identity formation and equips them to better respond to the complex demands of clinical practice.ConclusionsThis study highlights the importance of providing medical students with a dedicated space to reflect on their clinical experiences. Structured Peer Reflecting Team sessions support medical students’ well-being, professional identity formation, and strategies to better respond to demands in clinical situations. Universities should actively facilitate opportunities for medical students to engage in such reflective practices during clinical placements to enhance both learning outcomes and well-being.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07905-1.Keywords: Medical education, Medical student, Clerkship, Clinical training, Well-being, Reflecting groups, Peer learning, Professional identity formation, Qualitative research

Introduction

Medical education is considered to be one of the most academically and emotionally demanding training programmes compared to any other profession [1, 2], and is known to be a stressful and difficult time. A meta-analysis shows that the global prevalence of anxiety among medical students is 33,8% [1], and research indicates that medical students face significant levels of psychiatric disorders and burnout compared to their non-medical counterparts [3–5].

One of the most stressful times during medical school is the transition from preclinical, university-based learning to clinical learning [6]. On the one hand, students learn about and from patient care [7] and gain experience in managing patients and becoming part of a community of practice [8]. They develop a sense of themselves as future doctors and work across different clinical contexts [7, 9]. On the other hand, this learning takes place in the context that is primarily designed as a workplace, where students’ expectations, roles and responsibilities can be challenged [10, 11]. Studies have continuously highlighted an environment characterised by fragmentation, inadequate supervision, workplace stress, peer competition, and isolation as some of the key stressors [12–14]. Furthermore, previous support systems, both formal (e.g., student services) and informal (e.g., friends), may become less available because of the clinical rotations [15].

In this challenging context, reflective activities have been recognised as essential components of medical education. They not only facilitate students’ learning from clinical experiences but also play a vital role in the development of their professional identity and overall well-being [16–19]. Reflecting on practice has been identified as a key mechanism for fostering resilience in the face of the demands of the medical profession [20]. However, the overwhelming pressure to assimilate vast amounts of new information often prevents medical students from engaging in such reflection [21]. As such, several studies highlight the need for scheduled and structured approaches to encourage reflective habits [16, 21–23].

Group-based reflection, particularly in peer settings, has been shown to promote reflective practice among medical students by offering a space to share experiences, explore emotional responses, and develop coping strategies for clinical training [12, 18]. A recent review supports this, finding that group reflection enhances engagement foster belonging, and reduces isolation [18]. Students valued the protected time to reflect and the trusting group environment [24], and participation has also been linked to reduced feelings of loneliness and imposter syndrome [25]. The facilitator’s role appears central to the success of these groups. Several studies emphasise the importance of skilled facilitation in creating a safe and supportive learning environment [26, 27]. Gold et al. describe a model where psychiatry residents facilitated reflection groups for medical students, creating a near-peer dynamic perceived as highly beneficial. The authors recommend exploring this model in other clinical settings [25].

While existing research highlights the value of peer reflection and facilitator’s role, few studies have explored how peer reflections is experienced during clinical rotations, particularly from both the student and facilitator perspectives. There remains a need to understand not only how medical student experience participation in reflections during clinical training, but also how junior residents, in their role as near-peer facilitators, support and shape these reflective processes. In the present study, we designed a concept for Peer Reflecting Teams where medical students met in groups with junior residents as facilitators. The research questions were: How do medical students experience participation in peer-facilitated reflective teams during their clinical rotations; what meanings do both medical students and junior residents as facilitators ascribe to these experiences in relation to the medical students’ development as doctors; and what role do the junior residents play as facilitators in these groups?

Context

The medical program at Aalborg University is structured to integrate theoretical learning with clinical practice, consisting of a three-year bachelor’s degree followed by a three-year master’s degree. The bachelor’s programme primarily focuses on building theoretical knowledge in medical sciences through case-based learning, lectures, and clinical exercises. Students work in small groups, engaging in collaborative problem-solving and hands-on clinical practice, including short rotations at hospitals. The master’s programme builds on the skills and knowledge gained during the bachelor’s phase, with an increased focus on clinical practice. Students participate in more extensive hospital rotations and in primary care settings, where they further develop their clinical competencies. This means that the master students in the fourth and fifth year spend almost all their time in a clinical setting, learning alongside experienced doctors. Typically, the students attend the morning report before being assigned to the doctors they are to follow for the rest of the day [28]. During clinical rotations, students are divided into smaller teams and rotate between departments every 3 to 6 weeks. Each rotation includes a variety of learning activities, such as bedside teaching, symposia, and project work, all the while learning how to navigate the hospital environment.

Methods

The intervention – Reflecting teams

All medical students in their fourth year were invited to participate in Peer Reflecting Teams (PRT). The opportunity to join was highlighted by the junior residents during a shared teaching session, where the purpose and format of the PRT were introduced and students were encouraged to sign up. The PRT meetings were held in the late afternoon (in their spare time), and participation was on a voluntary basis. However, after the first meeting, the students committed themselves to continue. Approximately 50% of the students have participated in the years 2021 to 2023.

The groups were organised for one semester, during which the medical students met monthly in groups of five to eight people, led by a junior resident. The medical students were all currently engaged in their clinical placements, but in different departments at the hospital. The meetings followed a structured format that encouraged sharing and reflection. Although no formal reflective model was used, the sessions followed a structured format. This included a ‘quick round’ in which the students shared what was on their minds (both positive and challenging experiences), followed by further questioning and the selection of two to three cases for deeper reflection where peers reflected on the situation while the facilitator moderated the discussion. The presenter then rejoined the conversation and was encouraged to share what they found meaningful. This format aimed to support reflection in a safe and structured way.

The junior residents facilitating the group sessions, were all at the beginning of their specialty training, most of them working as year-one junior resident. Junior residents were recruited through email invitations distributed by educational coordinators within hospital departments, who were asked to forward the information to junior residents in their departments. The residents were chosen as facilitators of the team sessions, both because they were considered easily approachable for the students, but also to act as role models for the students. Before starting, the junior residents participated in an introductory course run by a psychologist with expertise in supervising doctors. The session covered key elements such as creating psychological safety, guiding group discussions, managing emotional content, and balancing professional and personal perspectives. Emphasis was placed on supporting students’ reflections while maintaining a non-judgemental and exploratory approach. A summary of the training content is provided in Appendix 1. The psychologist also conducted a midterm follow-up session and were available for emergent situations. The aim was to provide the junior residents with the knowledge and tools to guide and supervise the groups.

Study design

To explore the participating medical students’ and junior residents’ experiences with the Peer Reflecting Teams, we designed a qualitative study using both observations and group interviews as methods. The combination of these methods gave an opportunity to both explore the setting, interactions and the experiences of the participating students and junior residents.

Data generation

The first author, trained as an anthropologist, conducted both the observations and group interviews. She did not know any of the participating medical students or junior residents beforehand. To gain knowledge of the concept of Peer Reflecting Teams, we decided to perform preliminary observations (nine hours) prior to engaging in the group interviews. The observations were designed to provide the research team with insights into the initiative and, more specifically, to inform and refine the development of the interview guides (Appendix 2). During the observations, attention was given to the structure of the sessions, the role of the facilitator, the nature of the topics discussed, and the group dynamics, and field notes were written. For example, questions about emotional safety, peer relationships, and facilitator involvement were shaped by recurring patterns observed during the sessions. Prior to the observations, the junior residents contacted their respective groups to inform the students about the study, including its background, purpose, and research questions. They asked students in advance to provide consent for the researcher to attend one of their sessions. At the beginning of each session, the researcher repeated the study purpose and ethical considerations, and all participants were asked to provide written informed consent before the observation began.

All fourth-year medical students who had participated in PRT during the semester in which the data was generated (n = 79) and all junior residents (n=8) who had facilitated groups were contacted by email. The email included information about the study, its purpose, and scope, and invited them to respond if they were interested in participating. In total, we conducted five group interviews: four with medical students (n=16) and one with the junior residents (n=4) who facilitated the groups (see Table 1). All junior residents and medical students received an email with information about the study, including its purpose and scope, and were invited to respond if they were interested in participating. When selecting among those who expressed interest, we aimed to ensure variation across clinical placements, gender, and group size to strengthen the diversity of perspectives. We assessed that the data collected provided sufficient information power for a meaningful thematic analysis [29]. Before the group interview, the participants were informed about the project, and all provided their written informed consent to participate. The interview groups were mixed across the peer reflecting teams. All group interviews were audio recorded and transcribed. The group interviews were conducted in Danish, and the analysis was based on the original transcripts. All quotes and personally identifiable information were pseudonymised, and in this article, we identified each participant with a number. Quotations were translated into English only at the stage of manuscript preparation, with the first author responsible for translation and co-authors reviewing for accuracy.

Analysis

Qualitative studies adhere to an iterativ model, where the processes of data generation, analysis, and writing are fluidly interconnected [30]. Our analytical approach was guided by a thematic reflective analysis as outlined by Clarke and Braun [31]. Initial coding and theme development were conducted by the first author and discussed in depth with the last author to support reflexivity and interpretive rigour. As last author holds a faculty leadership position, she was not involved in the data collection and only accessed anonymised excerpts from the group interviews during the analysis and manuscript writing to maintain confidentiality. Together, the first and last author explored emerging themes and nuances in interpretations. This process was repeated as preliminary themes evolved and were subsequently grouped into broader conceptual categories. These categories were informed by patterns and similarities in the data, such as descriptions of the interactions, the reflection with peers, and the role of the junior resident as facilitator. In the next phase, the first author re-read all group interview transcripts to ensure a comprehensive grasp of the material and to apply the framework consistently. This resulted in a shared set of coded data that formed the analytical foundation for writing the results section.

The research team was interdisciplinary, consisting of both an anthropologist and medical doctors, and the team members had experience with both pre- and postgraduate education, contributing to both a nuanced methodological approach and strengthening of the analytical process.

Results

The participating medical students described the transition from being a university student to joining a busy clinical department during their clinical rotation as a great learning opportunity, but also as overwhelming and abrupt. During the analysis we identified four themes: (1) Experiencing a safe space; (2) Fostering community by sharing experiences; (3) Developing strategies for navigating difficult clinical situations; and (4) The role of the near-peer facilitator.

Experiencing a safe space

When the participants were asked to describe the Peer Reflecting Team (PRT) to an outsider, the words ‘safe space’, ‘breather’ and ‘act like a release’ were commonly used. The medical students described their experience of the transition from university to clinical rotation as a sudden and challenging shift. ‘There is a world of difference’ between being an undergraduate medical student at the university and becoming part of a work community where the workflow is intense, tasks are assigned, and expectations are to be met. The facilitators described how they as medical students missed a space where they could talk to peers about the challenges they faced:

We [as medical students] did talk with the other students, but it would have been nice to have a dedicated space for it… Somewhere you knew was meant for those conversations. Facilitator3.

On rotations, the medical students accompanied the doctors and often did not see their peers during the day. Thus, the PRT became a place where they could meet and discuss their experiences:

For me, it was a liberating place. It was safe for me to come there and talk about it, any way I wanted to. Medical student5.

Because I’ve certainly felt that when you [participate] you get the opportunity to hear that you’re not the only one sitting here with these problems. Or who finds everything difficult. So yeah, it’s just a space where you can express how you feel. Where it’s confidential. Medical student11.

The medical students described feeling genuinely understood and listened to both with their peers and the facilitator. When participating in the PRT, there was a special framework that fostered confidentiality, and there was time for listening, sharing, and reflecting on both their own and others’ experiences. During the observations, it became evident that this included both practical challenges (for example, finding their way or deciding where to put their lunch box), concrete situations (for example, encountering a neighbour as a patient during a ward round), and that some of the reflections also were about collaboration with hospital staff, especially doctors and nurses:

I think I would also describe it as a safe space where you can talk about your experiences and you’re allowed to say ‘oh, I was in this ward where this doctor was just really annoying to me’, and you know it’s not going to go any further […] that you have a safe space where you can talk about your experiences… the little things that take up a lot of space in the beginning… ‘how do I find exactly where I’m supposed to be?’ and ‘oh no, I’ve lost a doctor and what do I do?’ […] and you have a [facilitator] who can also tell you that… everything is going to be fine. Medical student1.

The participants expressed that there seemed to be a development in reflection themes. In the first meeting, they typically reflected on specific events and situations, but later, they began to reflect on their role as medical students and their thoughts about it, including feelings of inadequacy.

However, some of the medical students expressed that – despite the confidentiality – they found it hard to share experiences and difficulties. This emphasised the importance of the short turn-taking process at the beginning of the meetings, where all participants were encouraged to share a case or reflections from their clinical rotations:

Everyone is asked from the very beginning… if there is something they need to get off their chest. Because otherwise, you might end up keeping it inside. If something is weighing on you, it can also be hard to express. At least for some people. […] It’s definitely hard to say no when asked directly. Medical student12.

For example, we have someone in our group who doesn’t talk very much […] this [participating in the reflecting team] can make it possible for her to share her part. Medical student15.

Both medical students and facilitators discussed the importance of the number of participating students in the groups. Too few participants made the setting vulnerable, while too many made it difficult to ensure that everyone had enough time to share their experiences. Both medical students and facilitators estimated that the ideal number of participants in the team was five-six.

Fostering community by sharing experiences

The medical students described how, in the clinic, they felt “small” and “uninformed” compared to the doctors. They observed a high level of professionalism, with things just “running smoothly,” but they did not feel a part of it. The medical students described how they often felt alone with clinical tasks or decisions they felt unprepared for. This created a sense of uncertainty and reinforced a feeling of not belonging:

And then it’s easy to start feeling like a misfit. Medical student4.

When you start as a graduate student and is thrown into the hands of those senior doctors… […] you might find yourself thinking, “I’m just being overly sensitive,” “I just need to pull myself together,” and “it’s all just nonsense.” Medical student5.

These experiences contributed to feelings of inadequacy and self-doubt. Several participants described how they initially internalised these feelings and were unsure whether their reactions were legitimate. Although most medical students spoke with family or friends about their experiences, many pointed out that it was not equivalent to talking to peers. The lack of shared knowledge about the hospital setting made it difficult for people outside the clinical environment to fully understand the challenges students were facing:

They [medical students] found it challenging… first of all, knowing who they could share things with due to confidentiality. But also, the fact that people at home—partners, family—have other occupations… So they [family, friends] don’t understand what you’re exposed to or what you experience… That’s why it’s good to have a space where you can meet with peers. Facilitator2.

The PRT offered a space where they could share experiences with others with similar experiences. This often led to moments of recognition and reassurance. Several medical students described how listening to peers talk about similar difficulties helped them normalise their own reactions and feel less isolated:

It’s nice to have a space where you’re allowed to say that you find it difficult and then realise that most others actually feel the same way [several: “yes”]. It makes you feel less alone… and that’s comforting. Medical student5.

That’s why it’s so helpful to have things like peer reflecting teams, where you can talk with everyone else and realise that, well, if that’s the standard, then we’re all inadequate… you can unite around that. It’s an experience many people have here. Medical student4.

That’s exactly what I feel is the very best thing about peer reflecting teams — that you hear you’re not alone, really. Medical student11.

When presenting and discussing cases in the PRT, it often led to shared recognition, and participants expressed that many of their peers could relate to the situation. This mutual understanding helped reframe their own experiences and reduced the sense of solitude.

However, some participants experienced some disconnection when their peers were in different clinical rotations. For example, differences in terminology, routines, and types of experiences could make it difficult to fully relate to each other. One medical student described how, when some students talked about surgery, others were more focused on navigating ward routines. At the same time, some pointed out how this variation could in fact be beneficial, as it encouraged a more general level of reflection and allowed them to compare experiences across contexts. Despite these differences, all medical students emphasised that many of the underlying challenges were shared. These included the abrupt transition to clinical practice, balancing academic requirements with clinical duties, and handling expectations from clinical colleagues and oneself.

Developing strategies for navigating difficult clinical situations

The participants emphasised that the transition to clinical rotations included a new environment and a new way of learning. They described how, during their rotations, they were placed in situations and given responsibilities they did not feel ready for. However, they found that reflecting during group meetings helped them navigate these challenges:

And I think that was the first time it really dawned on me—this is actually an option! I don’t have to do everything I’m asked to do. But it’s like there’s this ingrained mindset, that… if someone believes I can handle a task, then surely I must be able to. And everyone around the table was saying, “You should never have to take that on as a student”, “You’re not responsible for such an important test, right?” That moment stood out to me because both students and the facilitator reassured me that it’s okay to say no. Since then, I’ve gotten much better at setting boundaries and having the confidence to say, “I’m not the one who should take on this responsibility.” And that’s okay. I think that was the biggest eye-opener for me. Medical student5.

In this, the PRT provided them with the opportunity to discuss topics and situations in depth with the group members, and thus they learned not only from their own experiences but also from those of others. This allowed them to develop strategies for handling similar situations in the future, including practical tools to handle difficult situations:

[…]Aas much as you get something back from others by sharing, “Okay, this was tough,” […] the others [medical students] gain just as much from hearing about this experience, because they’ve either been through it themselves or will encounter it later on… because for the next three years at least, we’re going to be working with some doctors here and there who have unhelpful input. Medical student11.

I definitely think that for my part, I’ve gained very concrete tools for handling a grumpy doctor who’s having a bad day and decides to take it out on me [others laugh]. There are plenty of examples of that… it’s common, and it’s something we discuss it while we’re there. You pick up strategies on how to deal with it. But also in terms of burnout – how to handle it, move forward, get back on track, and find motivation again. Medical student4.

One student shared how, in the past, a confrontation with an irritated doctor would have deeply affected them. However, through the reflections in the PRT, he was able to handle the situation better as his team had previously discussed a similar situation:

I think that in the past, I would have let it affect me a lot. I would have spent the rest of the day feeling like a HUGE apology and gone straight home to hide under the covers as soon as I could. But… because we had talked about it so much, it didn’t really affect me that much. Medical student3.

Thus, reflecting on both their own and others’ experiences and stories gave the medical students strategies for reacting to and coping with difficult situations. This not only involved how to handle a situation in the moment but also how to avoid self-blame afterward: “You react the way you do, but afterwards… there is not the same sense of self-blaming” (Medical student1). Participating in the peer reflections had taught them to be more mindful of their reactions, set boundaries, and speak up when needed:

As a brand-new student, you just don’t dare to say no to a doctor. If a doctor tells you to go somewhere, you just say “okay”, you know… So, it was really great to get those concrete tools to handle situations like that… also, just understanding how you personally react in a given situation and realising that it’s actually okay… no matter who you are, it’s okay; that’s just how it is. Medical student4.

These aspects were also emphasised by the facilitators as being crucial for the medical students’ personal growth, as these skills are essential for their future work as doctors:

And I actually think it’s a really good skill to try to introduce a bit early… that people become able to sense their own boundaries… And I just believe that they will become better colleagues in the long run if they already now learn to put words to the things that I think we try to articulate in our groups. I can certainly see that the ones I think are the easiest to work with are those who are in touch with that side of themselves. Facilitator1.

The role of the near-peer facilitator

When sharing their experiences of the concept, the medical students reflected on the role of the facilitator, the junior resident, describing their role as ‘essential’ in guiding discussions and ensuring that everyone had the opportunity to speak. At the first meeting, the facilitator introduced the principles and played a more directive role in structuring the session. However, as the meetings progressed, the facilitators’ role became more peripheral, and the medical students took the lead on managing the sessions.

Furthermore, the medical students valued how the facilitator sometimes contributed with their own experiences as both medical students and junior residents:

She brings a perspective that the rest of us haven’t thought about because she knows what being a doctor is like […] I mean, I feel she can contribute because she has actually been through what we’re going through right now […] she has worked with so many doctors and experienced so many situations that she can think from their [doctors’] perspective too, because we’re sitting here thinking from the perspective of us as students, and find it frustrating how the doctors are the way they are. But she can just come and say… and contribute with their perspective, for example, how really, really busy they are because they have all this, they need to get done. Medical student10. And it adds something more when it’s one of them [junior residents] who says that it’s not okay… rather than just us, seventh-semester students, all thinking that it’s rubbish. It really makes a difference when someone who is actually a doctor says it. Medical student13.

In the quotes above, it is clear how the junior resident’s role is more than just facilitating the reflections and getting the medical students involved. The junior residents can also contribute to the reflective discussions by drawing on their roles as medical graduates and the experience they have gained along the way. The junior resident plays an important role in helping the medical students as a mirror who is further along in their medical education. In addition, the junior resident can contribute to the discussions by offering a perspective based on their experience as a doctor, which the medical students experience as adding depth and nuance to the reflections.

Discussion

In this study, we explore how medical students experience participation in peer-facilitated reflective teams during their clinical rotations, what meanings both medical students and facilitators ascribe to these experiences in relation to the medical students’ development as doctors, and what role junior residents play as facilitators in these groups. Our analysis shows that the participating medical students describe the clinical rotations as a profound educational opportunity, yet also as abrupt and, at times, overwhelming transition that presents numerous challenges, including new environments, increased responsibilities, and the pressure to meet high expectations. In these rotations, the Peer Reflective Teams (PRTs) functioned as a dedicated space where medical students could reflect on their experiences in a safe environment. The participants consistently described the PRT as a “safe space”, a term that encapsulates the essence of their experiences.

Central to this, is the experience of shared reflections and peer support. The medical students frequently expressed how hearing others articulate thoughts and emotional responses similar to their own not only brought relief but also helped them reframe their own experiences. This is consistent with findings in the literature suggesting that group reflection provides a valuable space for discussing emotional responses and developing coping strategies [12, 18]. In our study, the medical students, explained how listening to peers speak about difficulties helped normalise their own responses and reduced feelings of isolation and thereby mitigated any feelings of inadequacy or self-doubt. This process of mutual recognition resonates with Gold et al. (2019), who found that participation in student reflection groups enhanced emotional well-being and helped mitigate feelings of impostorism and loneliness [25]. Similarly, Leung and Peisah (2023), in their systematic review of group reflective practice, highlight how such groups can reduce isolation, strengthen collegiality, and help students handle the emotional dimensions of clinical work [18].

The significance of peer support during the transition to clinical placements can also be understood through learning theories, which highlight how social interaction and support facilitate learning and adaptation. As Lave and Wenger (1991) argue, learning occurs through engagement in social practice, where newcomers gradually gain legitimacy and belonging [32]. For medical students, opportunities to participate meaningfully in clinical communities are often limited, especially when they frequently rotate. Nevertheless, it is evident that they still need to belong to a community of practice, as a peripheral position can contribute to feelings of isolation, uncertainty, and self-doubt. In this context, the PRT may be understood as a form of community within an emerging community of practice, where students begin to develop their professional identity not only through participation in clinical work but also through structured reflection with peers. This sense of belonging and recognition contributes positively to their well-being and helps mitigate the stress associated with the transition into clinical practice.

Another important finding from this study is how the PRTs equipped the participating medical students with strategies to navigate challenging situations in the clinical environment. The medical students described how the reflections enabled them to reflect on their own responses to difficult situations, taught them how to set boundaries and handle interpersonal tensions. For instance, several participants explained how, prior to the PRT, they felt obliged to accept tasks from senior staff without questions. Through structured reflection with peers and facilitators, they developed the confidence to recognise and communicate their own limitations. This aligns closely with the Professional role outlined in the CanMEDS framework [33], particularly in relation to self-awareness, emotional regulation, and the development of a reflective mindset. According to the CanMEDS, professionalism encompasses both a commitment to ethical patient care and a responsibility to one’s own well-being and continuous development. The PRT provided medical students with a structured and safe space to explore these aspects in practice. Initially, students used the sessions to reflect on concrete and practical challenges—such as navigating unfamiliar hospital environments or dealing with uncomfortable clinical interactions. Over time, however, their reflections deepened and shifted focus to uncertainty about their role, feelings of inadequacy, and the complexities of interprofessional collaboration. This progression reflects a core CanMEDS expectation: the ability to monitor and regulate one’s thoughts, behaviours, and emotions for optimal performance and well-being, as well as building resilience [33]. Other studies also emphasise the crucial role of reflection in learning from experience and guiding future actions [16]. This aligns with the understanding of reflection on clinical and nonclinical experiences as having an impact on medical students identity formation [17, 23]. By pausing to reflect, students became more emotionally aware and better equipped to respond to the complex demands of clinical practice.

Finally, our findings highlight the pivotal role played by the near-peer facilitator—typically a junior resident —in supporting the success of the PRT sessions. Students described the facilitator as “essential” in establishing structure, encouraging participation, and fostering a safe environment for reflection. Initially, the facilitator adopted a more active, directive role, particularly in introducing the concept and guiding the first session. Over time, however, their involvement became more peripheral, allowing students to take increasing ownership of the discussions. This gradual shift reflects a supportive pedagogical strategy, in which autonomy is handed over to learners as their confidence and group cohesion grow. However, some students may struggle with the structure of the reflections, and ensuring that discussions move beyond mere sharing to in-depth reflection on actions and learning is essential [24]. Educators should therefore provide clear guidance on structuring PRT sessions to secure their potential.

In line with previous research, the presence of a skilled facilitator appears to be key to cultivating a psychologically safe space [26, 27]. Notably, medical students in our study appreciated the dual role of the facilitator—not only as a moderator of the discussions, but also as a near-peer who could contribute relevant clinical perspectives based on recent personal experience. Their ability to share relatable insights, bridge the gap between student and doctor mindsets, and validate students’ concerns from a more experienced standpoint was described as particularly valuable. As one participant noted, “it really makes a difference when someone who is actually a doctor says it,” underscoring the authority and credibility that near-peer facilitators bring to the reflective space. This mirrors the model described by Gold et al., in which psychiatry residents facilitated student reflection groups, creating a near-peer learning environment that was both accessible and impactful [25]. Our findings support the relevance of this model beyond psychiatry, suggesting that near-peer facilitation can enhance reflective learning in other clinical contexts by balancing guidance with relatability.

Limitations

While this study offers important insights into medical students’ experiences with Peer Reflecting Teams, several limitations should be considered. First, the group interview format may also have influenced the data in ways that are difficult to control. Group dynamics, including the presence of dominant voices or perceived hierarchies among participants, may have shaped which perspectives were voiced and which were silenced. However, we also observed several instances where participants actively nuanced each other’s statements and introduced divergent perspectives, even when they did not fully agree. This suggests that the group setting, while carrying certain limitations, also enabled a degree of dialogic depth and collective meaning-making that enriched the data.

Second, while the interviews and observations provided insight into the medical students’ subjective experiences and perceptions, they do not offer access to their actual clinical practice or demonstrate whether the expressed reflections and learning intentions translate into long-term behavioural change or professional development. Future studies could address this limitation by employing longitudinal designs or by incorporating perspectives of clinical supervisors.

Third, it is important to note that participation in the Peer Reflecting Teams was voluntary. As such, those who took part had actively chosen to engage in reflective practice, which may indicate a particular openness or interest in such methods. Including the perspectives of students who opted not to participate could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the perceived value and limitations of this approach.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study underscores how the Peer Reflecting Teams (PRT) serve as an important support for medical students navigating the complexities of clinical rotations by providing a safe space for reflection, fostering community, and equipping students with strategies for managing challenges. As the medical students transition from university to clinical placements, they often encounter a range of emotional and professional challenges. Providing a dedicated space for reflection where they can share their experiences with peers, not only offers a sense of community with peers, but also supports medical students’ professional identity formation and equips them to better respond to the complex demands of clinical practice. PRTs provide a framework that encourages students to process difficult situations and develop strategies for navigating challenging clinical environments. Further research is warranted to explore the long-term impact of PRT participation on medical students’ well-being and professional identity formation. In addition, dedicated studies should examine the junior residents’ experiences of acting as facilitators, including how they perceive the role, the challenges they encounter, and the potential personal or professional benefits they may gain.
