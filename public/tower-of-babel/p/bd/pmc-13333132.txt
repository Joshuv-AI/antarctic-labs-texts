Associations of Gestational Weight Gain Up to 24 Weeks with Pregnancy Outcomes: A Chinese Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Jie Ma, Decui Cheng, Ming Zhu, Dongmei Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough inadequate or excessive gestational weight gain (GWG) has been associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes, most studies in China have applied the Institute of Medicine (IOM) guideline, which may not be optimal for Chinese women. Evidence on early-mid pregnancy weight gain and perinatal outcomes remains limited.MethodsThis single-center retrospective cohort study included singleton live births delivered at Shanghai General Hospital between January and December 2021. GWG24 was calculated as measured weight at approximately 24 gestational weeks minus self-reported pre-pregnancy weight and categorized according to the 2022 Chinese GWG guideline. Logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate associations between GWG24 and pregnancy outcomes.ResultsAmong 976 women, 122 (12.5%) were below-range, 490 (50.2%) were within-range, and 364 (37.3%) were above-range GWG24. Compared with within-range GWG24, below-range GWG24 was associated with higher odds of small for gestational age (SGA; adjusted OR 3.00, 95% CI 1.46–6.14) and lower odds of large for gestational age (LGA; adjusted OR 0.44, 95% CI 0.23–0.84). Above-range GWG24 was not significantly associated with SGA or LGA. Maternal outcomes included GDM, gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, caesarean delivery, and PPH; neonatal outcomes included SGA, LGA, macrosomia, and low birthweight.ConclusionBelow-range GWG24, classified according to the 2022 Chinese guideline, was associated with higher odds of SGA and lower odds of LGA. GWG24 may help identify women at increased risk of adverse fetal growth outcomes.

Introduction

Weight management before and during pregnancy is a cornerstone of prenatal care, as both pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) and gestational weight gain (GWG) are associated with adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes. In contemporary evidence syntheses, GWG below recommendations has been associated with higher risks of small for gestational age (SGA), low birth weight, and preterm birth, whereas GWG above recommendations has been associated with higher risks of large for gestational age (LGA), macrosomia, cesarean delivery, and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy.1,2 Earlier large systematic reviews and individual participant data meta-analyses similarly showed that GWG outside recommended ranges is common and clinically meaningful across populations.3–5

The 2009 Institute of Medicine/National Academy of Medicine (IOM/NAM) recommendations have been widely used in research and clinical practice; however, their applicability to Asian populations remains debated because they were developed primarily from North American populations and may not adequately reflect differences in body composition and metabolic risk across ethnic groups.6 In China, the Chinese Nutrition Society released local recommendations in 2021, followed by the 2022 Standard of Recommendation for Weight Gain during Pregnancy Period, providing BMI-specific guidance for gestational weight gain in Chinese women.7 Recent Chinese cohort data also suggest that Chinese-specific GWG recommendations may perform differently from the 2009 NAM guideline when evaluating offspring outcomes, supporting the rationale for population-tailored standards.7–10

Compared with total GWG, evidence on gestational-age-specific or trimester-specific weight gain remains relatively limited, especially in Chinese populations. This is clinically important because weight gain status identified by mid-pregnancy can still be modified through nutritional counseling and lifestyle interventions, and targeted interventions have been shown to reduce GWG and improve selected maternal and neonatal outcomes.11,12 Recent studies in Chinese populations and international cohorts have also highlighted the value of trimester-specific or gestational-age-specific GWG assessment rather than relying exclusively on total pregnancy weight gain.9,10

Therefore, GWG is not only a reflection of maternal physiological adaptation during pregnancy but also a clinically relevant indicator of fetal growth. However, most previous studies have focused on total GWG at delivery, which is influenced by gestational duration and may be less useful for timely intervention during pregnancy. In addition, widely used GWG recommendations have largely been derived from Western populations, and their applicability to Chinese women remains uncertain. Given that weight gain status by mid-pregnancy may still be modified through nutritional counseling and lifestyle intervention, evaluating gestational weight gain up to 24 weeks may help identify women at increased risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes and provide an opportunity for timely clinical assessment. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the associations of GWG up to 24 gestational weeks with pregnancy outcomes in a Chinese cohort using the 2022 Chinese guideline.

Methods

Study Population

This retrospective cohort study abstracted electronic medical records of women who delivered at the Department of Obstetrics, Shanghai General Hospital between January and December 2021. The study sample comprised consecutive eligible deliveries during the study period. Maternal anthropometrics, obstetric history, delivery information, and maternal and neonatal outcomes were obtained from routine prenatal visits, delivery records, and discharge summaries. Routine antenatal care at Shanghai General Hospital typically begins at 6–13 gestational weeks and includes scheduled visits at 11–13 weeks, around 24 weeks, 28 weeks, 31–32 weeks, and then every two weeks until delivery.

Women were eligible if they were aged ≥18 years, had a singleton birth, delivered at ≥28 gestational weeks, and had birthweight >1000 g. Among 1000 women meeting these criteria, 21 were excluded because of missing pre-pregnancy BMI or missing weight data required to calculate GWG24, and 3 were excluded because of fetal congenital malformation or stillbirth. The final analytic cohort included 976 women. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai General Hospital; informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design.

BMI and Weight Gain

Pre-pregnancy weight (kg) and height (cm) were self-reported at the first prenatal visit. Weight at approximately 24 gestational weeks was routinely measured in the clinic using a calibrated electronic scale (precision 0.1 kg). Weight at ~24 weeks was defined as the measurement closest to 24+0 gestational weeks within a ±1-week window. Pre-pregnancy BMI (kg/m2) was calculated as pre-pregnancy weight divided by height squared (m2). Gestational weight gain up to 24 weeks (GWG24, kg) was calculated as measured weight at ~24 weeks minus pre-pregnancy weight.

GWG24 was categorized using the 2022 Chinese guideline (Standard of Recommendation for Weight Gain during Pregnancy Period). Pre-pregnancy BMI was classified as underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), normal (18.5–23.9 kg/m2), overweight (24.0–27.9 kg/m2), and obese (≥28.0 kg/m2). The guideline recommends first-trimester gain of 0–2.0 kg and BMI-specific weekly gain in the second/third trimesters; therefore, the recommended GWG24 range was derived as: (first-trimester recommended range) + (recommended weekly gain range) × (24−13) weeks. The resulting GWG24 recommended ranges were 4.07–8.16 kg (underweight), 2.86–7.28 kg (normal), 2.42–6.07 kg (overweight), and 1.65–5.30 kg (obese). Women were classified as within-range if GWG24 fell within the BMI-specific recommended range, below-range if below, and above-range if above.

Outcomes

Maternal outcomes included gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), gestational hypertension, preeclampsia (PE), caesarean delivery, and postpartum hemorrhage (PPH). Neonatal outcomes included large for gestational age (LGA), small for gestational age (SGA), macrosomia (birthweight >4000 g), and low birthweight (birthweight ≤2500 g).

LGA and SGA were defined as birthweight above the 90th percentile and below the 10th percentile, respectively, for gestational age at birth according to WHO birthweight-for-gestational-age standards. PPH was defined as blood loss >500 mL after vaginal delivery or >1000 mL after caesarean delivery.

All women underwent a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) at 24–28 gestational weeks. GDM was diagnosed when any plasma glucose value met or exceeded the following thresholds: fasting 5.1 mmol/L, 1-hour 10.0 mmol/L, or 2-hour 8.5 mmol/L. Gestational hypertension was defined as new-onset hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg) on two occasions at least 4 hours apart after 20 gestational weeks. PE was defined as hypertension with proteinuria (≥0.3 g protein in a 24-hour urine collection) or other end-organ involvement (eg, thrombocytopenia, renal insufficiency, impaired liver function, pulmonary edema, or new-onset headache/visual symptoms).

Data Cleaning and Handling of Implausible Values

Prior to analysis, key anthropometric variables were screened for plausibility using pre-defined clinical ranges. Values outside plausible ranges were considered likely data-entry errors and were set to missing to reduce undue influence on derived measures and model estimates. The variables screened included maternal height, pre-pregnancy weight, weight around 24 gestational weeks, weight at delivery, and neonatal birthweight. For the primary analysis, BMI used for stratification and BMI-specific GWG24 categorization was recalculated from recorded height and pre-pregnancy weight (BMI) rather than relying on the original BMI field. The plausibility ranges used and the number of values recoded as missing are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Variables included in the multiple imputation model are summarized in Supplementary Table S2.

Ethics Statement

This retrospective cohort study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Shanghai General Hospital, Shanghai, China (Approval No. Yuanlun Kuai [2023] 478; protocol No. 20231228013607034). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All patient data were de-identified prior to analysis and handled confidentially in accordance with institutional policies and ethical standards.

Statistical Analyses

Maternal characteristics were summarized across GWG24 groups. Categorical variables are presented as n (%) and continuous variables as mean ± SD (or median [IQR] where appropriate). Group comparisons used chi-square tests (or Fisher’s exact tests when expected counts were small) for categorical variables and one-way ANOVA (or Kruskal–Wallis tests) for continuous variables.

Missing values in key continuous variables were handled using multiple imputation with chained equations under a missing-at-random assumption (five imputed datasets). The imputation model included the exposure (GWG24), primary outcomes (SGA and LGA), and covariates used in regression models. Binary outcomes coded as 1/2 were recoded to 1/0 (1 = event, 0 = non-event). Associations between GWG24 group and outcomes were assessed using logistic regression with within-range GWG24 as the reference category. The primary adjusted model included maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI (continuous; BMI), and parity; estimates were pooled across imputations using Rubin’s rules and reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). In BMI-stratified comparisons with zero events in either the comparison or reference group, Fisher’s exact tests were reported instead of unstable adjusted logistic estimates. As a sensitivity analysis, prenatal visit count during pregnancy was additionally included. Two-sided P <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study applying the 2022 Chinese GWG guideline, below-range gestational weight gain up to 24 gestational weeks (GWG24) was independently associated with higher odds of SGA and lower odds of LGA compared with within-range GWG24. In contrast, above-range GWG24 was not significantly associated with SGA or LGA after adjustment. These findings underscore the potential value of assessing GWG status by mid-pregnancy (around 24 weeks), a clinically relevant time point at which nutritional counseling and fetal growth surveillance may still be optimized when indicated.

Previous studies have consistently shown that inadequate total GWG is associated with SGA and low birth weight, whereas excessive total GWG is associated with LGA and metabolic complications.13,14 A large systematic review reported that 23% of pregnancies had GWG below and 47% above the IOM-recommended ranges. However, the IOM guideline was developed primarily from North American cohorts, and its generalizability across populations has been questioned.3 Differences in body composition and metabolic risk profiles mean that Asian women may experience obesity-related risks at lower BMI levels than White populations, and population-specific BMI cutoffs and GWG targets may therefore be more appropriate.15 Several Asian countries have proposed local GWG recommendations.5 Our study extends this literature by evaluating early-mid pregnancy weight gain using the 2022 Chinese guideline, which incorporates BMI cutoffs and recommended gains tailored to Chinese women, and by focusing on GWG24 rather than total GWG.

Asian populations may exhibit different body composition and metabolic risk profiles compared with Western populations, supporting the rationale for Chinese-specific BMI and GWG recommendations. In addition, gestational weight gain assessed at approximately 24 weeks may represent a clinically relevant and potentially modifiable time window because nutritional counseling and lifestyle intervention can still be implemented before late pregnancy.

Interpretation of Categorical Findings

The categorical (below/within/above) GWG24 classification is designed to reflect clinically meaningful thresholds rather than assuming a strictly linear association across the full GWG24 spectrum. Accordingly, excessive risk may concentrate among women with clearly insufficient GWG24, such that categorical comparisons detect a strong association even when the overall dose-response pattern is not strictly linear. This pattern is compatible with a threshold or non-linear relationship between early-mid pregnancy weight gain and fetal growth restriction. In sensitivity analyses additionally adjusting for prenatal visit count (a proxy for healthcare utilization and pregnancy risk status), effect estimates for SGA and LGA were materially unchanged, supporting the robustness of the main findings.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths of this study include the use of the 2022 Chinese GWG guideline, detailed clinical data enabling calculation of GWG24 near 24 gestational weeks, and the focus on a mid-pregnancy exposure window that is less confounded by gestational duration than total GWG. Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered. First, pre-pregnancy weight was self-reported at the first prenatal visit, which may introduce recall or reporting bias and lead to exposure misclassification. Women with higher pre-pregnancy BMI may be more likely to under-report body weight, which could result in differential exposure misclassification across BMI strata. However, because pre-pregnancy weight was recorded before pregnancy outcomes occurred, any resulting bias would likely be non-differential with respect to outcome status. Second, residual confounding cannot be excluded given the retrospective design and limited covariate availability. Third, this was a single-center study and generalizability to other regions or healthcare settings may be limited. Finally, we evaluated short-term perinatal outcomes and did not assess longer-term maternal or offspring cardiometabolic outcomes.

In addition, pregnancies complicated by stillbirth or major fetal malformations were excluded from the analysis. Although this approach improved cohort homogeneity, it may have introduced selection bias and may limit the generalizability of our findings to all pregnancies.

Clinical Implications

Our findings suggest that assessment of GWG24 may help identify women at increased risk of adverse fetal growth outcomes during mid-pregnancy. Because weight gain remains modifiable at this stage of gestation, GWG24 assessment may provide useful information to support clinical evaluation and counseling. However, the present observational study does not establish that interventions based on GWG24 improve pregnancy outcomes. Further prospective and interventional studies are needed to evaluate the clinical utility of this approach.

Conclusion

In this single-center retrospective cohort, gestational weight gain up to 24 weeks classified according to the 2022 Chinese guideline was associated with fetal growth outcomes. Below-range GWG24 was associated with higher odds of SGA and lower odds of LGA relative to within-range GWG24. These findings suggest that GWG24 may help identify women at increased risk of adverse fetal growth outcomes. Further multicenter prospective studies are warranted to confirm these findings and evaluate their clinical relevance.
