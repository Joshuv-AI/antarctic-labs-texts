Dynamic control of NMDA receptor Ca2+ permeability by endogenous and synthetic modulators
Authors: Mae G. Weaver, Gabriela K. Popescu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceNMDA receptors conduct Ca2+-rich excitatory currents, which are essential for learning and memory, and are culprits in severe neuropsychiatric conditions. Drugs acting on the NMDA receptor are presently tested for their ability to control the total current, which assumes that the amount of Ca2+ in the fluxed current is constant for a given receptor complex. We demonstrate here that the Ca2+ content of the NMDA receptor current varies in real time over a wide range in response to allosteric modulators of endogenous or synthetic origin. These results reveal an unappreciated layer of regulation for processes driven by NMDA receptor Ca2+ fluxes and call for preclinical testing of all NMDA receptor–targeted drugs with allosteric mechanisms for their effect on Ca2+ permeability.

Most of the fast excitatory transmission in the mammalian central nervous system reflects the activation of ligand-gated channels of the ionotropic glutamate receptor family (iGluRs) (1). These receptors are large transmembrane proteins that respond to glutamate by opening an intrinsic cation-selective pore (2). In physiological conditions, iGluRs pass net inward currents consisting of Na+ or a mix of Na+ and Ca2+ at negative membrane potentials and net outward K+ currents at positive potentials. The observation that iGluR currents reverse direction around 0 mV indicates that each of the physiologically permeant ions, Na+, Ca2+, and K+, passes through open iGluR channels with similar ease (3). However, on closer inspection, members of the iGluR family display distinct reversal potentials (Erev). These differences, even if small, are of outsized importance because they reflect differences in the relative permeability (PCa/PNa) of divalent (Ca2+) versus monovalent (Na+ and K+) cations. As such, variations in the relative permeability of iGluRs translate in distinct glutamate-elicited Ca2+ transients and initiate specific signaling cascades in the postsynaptic cell.

Based on their Ca2+ permeability, iGluRs fall into two classes: those with no or low Ca2+ permeability (PCa/PNa, 0 to 1), represented by AMPA and kainate receptors, and those with high Ca2+ permeability (PCa/PNa, 2 to 8), represented by NMDA receptors. Further, within each iGluR branch, Ca2+ permeability varies with the molecular composition of the receptor complex (2, 4). All iGluRs are tetramers of homologous subunits. AMPA and kainate receptors assemble as homo- or heterotetramers of GluA1–4 and GluK1–5 subunits, respectively, and specific auxiliary subunits. NMDA receptors assemble as dimers of heterodimers, each containing an obligatory GluN1 subunit (N1) and an N2(A–D) subunit. For AMPA receptors, Ca2+ permeability lies on a relative continuum based on the identity of the receptor complex (pore-forming and auxiliary subunits) (4). For NMDA receptors, whose activity is independent of auxiliary subunits, Ca2+ permeability is determined by the identity of the N2 subunits, with higher values for N2A,B-containing receptors, and smaller values for N2C,D-containing receptors (5).

In addition to much higher Ca2+ permeability than AMPA and kainate receptors, NMDA receptors also have larger unitary currents (40 to 50 pS) and exceptionally long activations (50 ms to 1 min), which collectively produce substantially larger Ca2+ transients (6, 7). Consistent with the critical significance of Ca2+ fluxes in cellular physiology, several mechanisms control the amplitude and duration of NMDA receptor currents over a wide temporal scale. Regulated expression and trafficking of seven N1 splice variants and four N2 subunits can change both the amplitude and the ionic composition of the excitatory current passed by NMDA receptors in the span of minutes to hours. Such changes occur during the normal development and maturation of synapses, and have been associated with specific physiological or pathological synaptic states (8). On a faster, seconds-to-minutes timescale, cellular activities can modify existing receptor complexes to affect the amplitude and ionic composition of their currents. One such mechanism involves changes in the phosphorylation status of existing receptors, which mediates behavioral responses to emotional stress (9, 10). Finally, on a timescale of milliseconds to seconds, which is the dynamic range of NMDA receptor operation, their response amplitude and kinetics are highly sensitive to fluctuations in the interstitial concentration of several inorganic cations (H+, Zn2+, Mg2+, and Ca2+) and organic molecules (polyamines, steroids). Understanding how small diffusible ligands control the NMDA receptor responses is fundamental to developing effective pharmacological interventions for a variety of neuropsychiatric conditions (11–13).

Depending on the location of their binding site on NMDA receptors, rapid-acting modulators fall into two categories. Allosteric modulators bind outside the pore, where they modify the gating reaction, and therefore change the channel open probability (Po). Depending on whether they enhance or depress the glutamate-elicited currents, such ligands are referred to as positive (PAMs) or negative (NAMs) allosteric modulators, respectively. In contrast, channel blockers bind in the pore, where they interfere directly with the flow of ions, and therefore change channel conductance. It has been widely assumed that neither allosteric modulators nor blockers change the NMDA receptor Ca2+ permeability, and therefore the ionic composition of their currents is fixed for a receptor assembly (2, 11). However, even if disparate, several observations suggest otherwise. In one example, mild acidosis reduced the Ca2+ permeability and unitary conductance of N1/N2A receptors (14). This is surprising because receptor protonation, which is widely accepted as a physiological mechanism of controlling receptor Po, requires the N-terminal domain (NTD) of the receptor, and this domain lies at a considerable distance from the pore (15). In a separate example, synthetic allosteric modulators with binding sites closer to the pore, within the external vestibule (EU1622 and EU1794), also reduced Ca2+ permeability and unitary conductance of NMDA receptors (16–18). These examples call into question the presently held dogma that the NMDA receptor Ca2+ permeability is determined by the receptor’s primary structure, and therefore, is invariant on the timescale of receptor activation.

To test whether the Ca2+ permeability of NMDA receptors can fluctuate rapidly and to evaluate its dynamic range, we measured the effect of Ca2+ on the reversal potential of glutamate-elicited currents in the presence of several fast-acting modulators with established mechanisms and structural sites of action. Using single-molecule recordings, whole-cell electrophysiology, and patch-clamp fluorometry, we show that allosteric modulators, whether endogenous or synthetic, can control the Ca2+ permeability of recombinant and neuronal NMDA receptors in real time. Further, we show that the change in the relative proportion of Ca2+ to Na+ in the fluxed current reflects specifically a change in the ability of NMDA receptors to pass Ca2+, whereas their ability to pass Na+ remains unaffected. These results demonstrate that the NMDA receptor–mediated Ca2+ flux can be altered rapidly and specifically by endogenous and synthetic allosteric modulators, producing depolarizing currents whose Ca2+ content varies over a wide dynamic range.

Materials and Methods

Reagents.

All electrophysiology reagents were from Sigma-Aldrich and of >99% purity. Ifenprodil hemitartrate (99%) was from ThermoFisher Scientific (J60932.MF) and GNE-9278 from Tocris (901230–11–3). They were prepared as 100,000X and 2,000X stock solutions in anhydrous dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), respectively (Invitrogen, D12345). All tissue culture reagents were from ThermoFisher Scientific or Corning.

Cell Culture and Protein Expression.

Human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293 cells (ATCC CRL-1573) were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 10 mM MgCl2, with 5% CO2 at 37° C. Cells were transfected with cDNA plasmids encoding rat GluN1-1a (U08261.1) or rat GluN1-1b (U08263.1) with rat GluN2A (AF001423.1) or rat GluN2B (M91562.1) and GFP at a 1:1:1 ratio using polyethenimine. NTD-lacking proteins (ddNTD-2A and ddNTD-2B) were encoded by the constructs described previously (19). The homomeric AMPA receptor construct GluA2flip(Q)(L504Y) was chosen due to its ability to produce non-desensitizing currents (20) and was transfected with GFP at a 1:1 ratio.

Primary rat cortical neurons were purchased from ThermoFisher (A1084001). Cells were plated on nitric acid washed, Poly-D-Lysine (0.1 mg/mL; Millipore Sigma, Massachusetts) coated coverslips at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells/cm2, and maintained in minimal essential medium (MEM; Gibco, California) supplemented with, 0.6% glucose, 10% horse serum, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, and 2 mM Glutamax and with 5% CO2 at 37° C. After 12 h in culture, the growth medium was replaced with Neurobasal Medium (Gibco, California) supplemented with 2% B27, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 2 mM Glutamax.

Whole-Cell Current Recordings.

Whole-cell currents were recorded from HEK293 cells at 16 to 36 h posttransfection, with borosilicate pipettes (2 to 6 MΩ) filled with intracellular solution containing (in mM) 135 CsCl, 33 CsOH, 2 MgCl2, 10 HEPES, and 10 EGTA, at pH 7.4 (CsOH). Extracellular solutions contained (in mM) 150 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 0.02 EDTA, 0.1 glycine, 10 HEPBS (pH 8), or HEPES (pH 7.2 or 6.5) and were supplemented with 0 or 1 glutamate and 0.2 to 10 CaCl2 to produce the free Ca2+ concentrations indicated. For experiments using nanomolar Zn2+ concentrations, ZnCl2 was buffered according to the methods described previously (21, 22). Briefly, we replaced EDTA and HEPBS in our extracellular solution with 10 mM tricine supplemented with 2.6 µM ZnCl2 to produce a free Zn2+ concentration of 10 nM.

Whole-cell currents from cortical neurons were recorded between days 16 to 19 after plating, with borosilicate pipettes (3 to 7 MΩ) filled with intracellular solution containing (in mM) 130 K-gluconate, 4.0 ATP-Mg, 0.3 GTP-Na, 1 CaCl2, 10 HEPES, and 10 EGTA, set at pH 7.3, with osmolarity ~250 mOsm. Extracellular solutions contained (in mM) 140 NaCl, 5 KCl, 10 glucose, 0.1 glycine, 10 HEPBS (pH 8) or HEPES (pH 7.2), with osmolarity ~290 mOsm, supplemented with 0 or 1 NMDA and 0.2 or 2 CaCl2, as indicated. All currents were amplified, low-pass filtered at 2 kHz (Axopatch 200B; 4-pole Bessel), sampled at 5 kHz (Digidata, 1440A), and stored as digital files with pClamp 10.7 software (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA).

Single-Molecule Current Recordings.

Cell-attached unitary currents were recorded from HEK293 cells at 16 to 36 h posttransfection with borosilicate pipettes (12 to 25 MΩ) filled with either a Na+- or a Ca2+-based extracellular solution as described previously (23, 24). Briefly, the Na+ solution contained (in mM), 150 NaCl, 5 KCl, 10 HEPBS (pH 8) or HEPES (pH 7.2), 1 EDTA, 0.1 glycine, and 1 glutamate set to pH 8 or pH 7.2 with NaOH. In the Ca2+ solution, we replaced 150 NaCl with 75 mM CaCl2 and set the pH using Ca(OH)2. When recording unitary Ca2+ currents, cells were bathed in a KCl-based solution containing (in mM), 142 KCl, 1.8 CaCl2, 1.7 MgCl2, and 10 HEPES set to pH 7.4 to collapse the cellular resting membrane potential (10, 25). All other experiments were conducted using standard PBS. Inward currents were recorded after applying +100 mV through the recording pipette. Currents were amplified and low-pass filtered at 10 kHz, sampled at 40 kHz (PCI-6229, M Series card, National Instruments, Austin, TX), and written into digital files with QuB acquisition software. All recordings were processed offline to isolate bursts produced by individual receptors and to correct baseline deviations and contaminating electrical noise. Idealization was done using the segmental-k-mean (SKM) algorithm in QuB with a 0.025 ms dead time to estimate unitary amplitudes and mean open and closed event durations. Unitary amplitudes were used to calculate unitary chord conductance.

Results

Modulation of NMDA Receptor Ca2+ Permeability By Extracellular pH.

To determine whether changes in external proton concentrations affect the Ca2+ permeability of iGluRs, we examined the PCa/PNa of representative members of this family at two external proton concentrations, pH 8 and pH 7.2. We tested recombinant NMDA receptors corresponding to the adult (N2A-containing) and juvenile (N2B-containing) receptors, recombinant Ca2+-permeable AMPA receptors (GluA2(Q)), and endogenous NMDA receptors expressed in dissociated rat hippocampal neurons (DIV 16–19). We estimated PCa/PNa with the high monovalent cation method (28), by measuring the magnitude of the change in the reversal potential (ΔErev) upon adding 2 mM Ca2+ to an external solution that initially contained only Na+ as a permeant ion (26).

In HEK293 cells expressing the adult recombinant N1-1a/N2A, we found that adding 2 mM Ca2+ in the external solution shifted the Erev from −0.12 ± 2.0 (ENa) to 4.1 ± 2.3 (ECa) in low proton concentrations (pH 8), and from −2.0 ± 4.9 to 0.49 ± 5.3 in physiological proton concentrations (pH 7.2) (Fig. 1 A and B). We used the Lewis relationship to calculate PCa/PNa values of 7.4 ± 2.1 (n = 6) at pH 8 and 4.0 ± 1.4 (n = 9) at pH 7.2 (Materials and Methods) (Fig. 1C). These values agree well with previous measurements for this receptor type in physiological conditions (30) and replicate the reported inhibitory effect of acidosis on Ca2+ permeability (14). Further, we found that external pH produced changes of similar magnitude on the PCa/PNa of recombinant N1-1a/N2B and of neuronal NMDA receptors. In contrast, for Ca2+-permeable AMPA receptors consisting of GluA2(Q) subunits, we measured PCa/PNa values of 1.5 ± 0.5 regardless of external pH (Fig. 1 B and C and Table 1). These results demonstrate that NMDA receptors, but not AMPA receptors, display fast, reversible pH-dependent Ca2+ permeability.

The N-Terminal Domain Mediates the pH Dependence of Ca2+ Permeability.

Previous research showed that NMDA receptors' Po varies with fluctuations in external H+ and Ca2+ concentrations (31, 32). Moreover, even though typical concentrations for these cations in the central nervous system lie around 50 nM for protons (pH 7.3) (33) and 1.2 mM for Ca2+ (34), both fluctuate with activity and with metabolic and pathological states (35, 36). Specifically at glutamatergic synapses during activity, fast (millisecond) acidic transients are followed by slower (hundreds of ms) alkalinization (37, 38). Therefore, we aimed to increase the resolution and range of our PCa/PNa measurements by covering a wider range of H+ and Ca2+ concentrations. We measured Erev of N1-1a/N2A receptor currents with external Ca2+ concentrations in the 0.2 to 10 mM range (Fig. 2A), and derived PCa/PNa values by fitting the Lewis equation to data obtained in three pH values (Fig. 2B). This approach revealed that PCa/PNa varied over the entire range of Ca2+ and H+ tested, with the following calculated values: 6.7 ± 0.7 (n = 27) at pH 8, 4.3 ± 0.4 (n = 24) at pH 7.2, and 2.6 ± 0.1 (n = 19) at pH 6.5 (Fig. 2B and Table 1). These results show that the relative Ca2+ permeability of NMDA receptors is malleable and can vary over a wide range, in a pH-dependent manner.

The structures responsible for the inhibitory effects of protons on the gating kinetics (Po) of NMDA receptors lie within the NTD (11, 39). The activity of receptors lacking the NTD layer (ddNTD) is pH-insensitive, and the activity of receptors with natural variation in the NTD of the N1 subunits, a or b variants, has distinct pH sensitivities (EC50): pH 7.2 for N1-1a and pH 6.7 for N1-1b (39). To learn whether in addition to mediating the effect of protons on gating kinetics (Po), the NTD also mediated the effect of protons on PCa/PNa, we measured the magnitude of Ca2+-induced shifts in Erev over a range of Ca2+ concentrations and pH values for NMDA receptors with distinct NTD structures. We found that the PCa/PNa of ddNTD-2A receptors hovered in the 4.5–5.2 range, independently of external pH (Fig. 2C). Further, the PCa/PNa of receptors containing the N1-1b variant was lower than those of receptors containing the N1-1a variant at the lowest proton concentration tested (4.7 ± 1.0, pH 8, n = 21) and was less sensitive to changes in pH. This result suggests that the positively charged peptide encoded by exon 5, which is absent in the N1-1a subunit but present in the N1-1b subunit, reduces the receptor PCa/PNa by itself while also reducing the potency of external protons to modulate PCa/PNa. We obtained similar results for N2B-containing receptors (Fig. 2D and Table 1) suggesting that the NTD-mediated modulation of Ca2+ permeability is a conserved feature of NMDA receptors.

Negative Allosteric Modulators Acting at the NTD Decrease Ca2+ Permeability.

Because the NTD of NMDA receptors is distal to the pore-forming transmembrane domain, the effects of the exon 5-encoded peptide and of protons on PCa/PNa must occur through an allosteric mechanism rather than by direct interactions with the pore or by interfering with permeating cations. Several gating modifiers reduce NMDA receptor activity by binding to and changing the structure of the NTD (11). Among the most studied are Zn2+, which reduces the activity of 2A-containing receptors with high potency (EC50, 17 nM) (22), and the synthetic compound ifenprodil (IFN), which reduces the activity of N2B-containing receptors (EC50, 0.3 µM) (40). We measured the effect of these two modulators on the PCa/PNa of NMDA receptors in low proton concentrations (pH 8) and compared it with the effect measured for Mg2+, which is a physiological pore blocker and reduces currents by interfering directly with the flow of permeating ions.

We found that when rigorously buffered, Zn2+ (10 nM, pH 8) reduced the PCa/PNa of N1-1a/N2A receptors from 9.2 ± 2.2 to 3.9 ± 1.7 (n = 10), whereas Mg2+ (1 mM, pH 8) had no effect on PCa/PNa (7.7 ± 2.3 vs. 8.1 ± 3.4, n = 8), as reported previously (30) (Fig. 3A and Table 1). Similarly, IFN (0.3 μM, pH 8) reduced the PCa/PNa of N1-1a/N2B receptors from 8.8 ± 2.1 to 3.2 ± 1.4 (n = 6), whereas Mg2+ (1 mM, pH 8) had no effect (9.2 ± 2.5 vs. 8.4 ± 3.6, n = 6) (Fig. 3B and Table 1). Together, these results demonstrate that NTD ligands, whether endogenous (H+, Zn2+) or synthetic (IFN), exert powerful inhibitory effects on the receptor’s relative Ca2+ permeability. This effect is separate from their well-described inhibitory effects on gating kinetics and is not shared by channel blockers.

Bidirectional Modulation of Ca2+ Permeability By Allosteric Ligands.

To date, the only other reported examples of synthetic allosteric modulators that can change PCa/PNa of NMDA receptors are the synthetic compounds EU1622-14 and EU1794-4 (16–18). Unlike H+, Zn2+, and IFN, which are NTD ligands, these organic compounds bind to residues in the extracellular vestibule of the NMDA receptor, and both decrease the channel’s unitary conductance and PCa/PNa. This observation indicates that extracellular sites other than the NTD can also control the receptor’s relative Ca2+ permeability. To test whether other allosteric modulators that bind in the external vestibule can also affect PCa/PNa, we tested GNE-9278, a synthetic modulator that binds to residues in the pre-M1 linker of the GluN1 subunit and increases Po (EC50, 10 µM) (41). Surprisingly, we found that in physiological conditions (pH 7.2) GNE-9278 (50 μM) increased the PCa/PNa of N1-1a/N2A receptors from 4.1 ± 1.2 (n = 6) to 5.9 ± 1.7 (n = 9) (Fig. 3C and Table 1). However, repeating these experiments in low proton concentrations (pH 8) showed that GNE-9278 had no effect on PCa/PNa. Therefore, we conclude that GNE-9278, rather than modulating the Ca2+ permeability directly, does so by preventing the inhibitory effect of protons.

Together, these results demonstrate that allosteric modulators, whether negative (H+, Zn2+, IFN), or positive (GNE-9278), in addition to controlling the amplitude and kinetics of the total excitatory current generated by NMDA receptors by controlling their Po, also control the ionic composition of the fluxed current. This observation is consequential because it implies that the amplitude of the Ca2+ current can fluctuate separately from the amplitude of the total current. Given that presently, drugs targeting ion channels are only evaluated on their ability to modulate the total current, their effects on the Ca2+ signal remain unknown. This practice may have obscured the large and prevalent effects of allosteric modulators on Ca2+ permeability, despite their assiduous investigation over more than four decades.

Allosteric Modulators Change the Ca2+ Content of the NMDA Receptor Current.

To estimate how the changes in PCa/PNa, which we estimated from measured changes in reversal potentials, translate into changes in the fraction of Ca2+ in the fluxed current, we used patch-clamp Ca2+ fluorometry. This method records simultaneously the amplitude of the net inward current (I, nA) with the whole-cell patch-clamp technique, and the intensity of the intracellular fluorescence (ΔF, RFU) in cells filled with a Ca2+-sensitive dye (Fig. 4A). With this method, we calculated the total charge transferred in response to glutamate application, by integrating the recorded current amplitude over time (QT). We also estimated the charge carried specifically by Ca2+ (QCa) by calibrating, in separate experiments, the change in fluorescence intensity (ΔF) relative to the total charge transferred when external Na+ is replaced with an impermeant ion (NMDG), such that Ca2+ is the only inwardly passing ion (Fig. 4B). With this calibration, we calculated the fraction of charge carried by Ca2+ in the total current (Pf) (42).

Using this method, we examined currents elicited with glutamate from N1-1a/N2A receptors at pH 8 and found that of the total charge, Ca2+ carried 35 ± 7.5% (n = 7). This value is twofold higher than that we and others measured at pH 7.2, which was 16 ± 4.6% (n = 5) (14, 29) (Fig. 4C). Converting the measured Pf values to PCa/PNa with Eq. 4, we calculated corresponding values for PCa/PNa of 7.7 ± 1.6 for pH 8, and 3.5 ± 1.0 for pH 7.2, which agree well with PCa/PNa values we estimated in similar conditions from Erev measurements (Figs. 1–3 and Table 1). Therefore, both methods show that allosteric modulators can change the fraction of Ca2+ in the NMDA receptor current in real time and over a substantial range.

Allosteric Modulators Change PCa/PNa by Specifically Altering the Channel’s Permeability to Ca2+ but Not to Na+.

We have shown previously that phosphorylation of a single intracellular residue increases the relative Ca2+ permeability of N1-1a/N2B receptors by specifically increasing their Ca2+ but not Na+ conductance. To examine whether the NTD ligands investigated here operate with a similar mechanism, we recorded unitary currents from NMDA receptors with the cell-attached patch-clamp technique, in conditions where the only inwardly permeating ions were Ca2+ or Na+ (Fig. 5A).

As reported previously, unitary Ca2+ currents generated by an NMDA receptor display two conductance levels, a larger main level (im), and a sublevel (is) (Fig. 5 A and B) (32, 43). For N1-1a/N2A receptors, we found that at pH 8, these were 21 ± 3 pS and 11 ± 3 pS (n = 8), respectively, in good agreement with previous reports (32). Adding Zn2+ (10 µM) reduced both amplitude levels to 12 ± 1.5 pS and 6.2 ± 1.2 pS (n = 7), respectively. Similarly, increasing the proton concentration from pH 8 to pH 7.2 reduced both amplitude levels to 16 ± 1.6 pS and 5.0 ± 2.0 pS (n = 5) (Fig. 5C). In contrast, neither Zn2+ nor H+ changed the Na+ current amplitude, for which we measured a single conductance level of 61 ± 8.0 pS (Fig. 5C). For N1-1a/N2B receptors, we measured unitary Ca2+ currents with main and subconductance levels of 23 ± 1.4 pS and 12 ± 1.6 pS (n = 7), respectively. Adding IFN (0.3 µM) reduced Ca2+ conductances to 15 ± 2.2 pS and 6.9 ± 1.7 pS (n = 7) (Fig. 5C). Similarly, increasing the proton concentration from pH 8 to pH 7.2, reduced both Ca2+ conductance levels to 17 ± 6.2 pS and 5.1 ± 2.5 pS (n = 7) (Fig. 5C). However, neither IFN nor pH changed the Na+ conductance, for which we measured a single conductance level of 58 ± 7.7 pS (Fig. 5C). These results demonstrate that gating modulators with binding sites distant to the permeating pore, reduced specifically the unitary Ca2+ current and had no effect on the unitary Na+ current.

To examine the effect of allosteric modulators on the dwell times and relative occupancies of the two Ca2+-conducting levels, we separated from these records short bursts of activity with no overlapping openings. Results showed that both H+ and Zn2+, or IFN, produced a small (~20%) but statistically significant increase in the receptor’s main-level open dwell time, with no detected change in the sublevel dwell time (Fig. 5B). Further, neither of the NTD ligands examined changed the relative occupancies of the two open levels.

Collectively, the results reported here demonstrate that rather than an invariant biophysical property of each receptor subtype, the composition of the ionic current fluxed by NMDA receptors, specifically its Ca2+ content, fluctuates with moment-by-moment changes in their chemical environment and can vary over a wide dynamic range.

Discussion

We examined how fluctuations in the extracellular fluid composition affect the NMDA receptor–mediated Ca2+ flux. The current dogma assumes that the Ca2+ permeability of NMDA receptors is controlled solely by the receptor’s primary structure. In contrast, our results show that gating modulators, such as H+, Zn2+, IFN, and GNE-9278, but not channel blockers, such as Mg2+, alter the relative Ca2+ permeability of NMDA receptors over a wide dynamic range (PCa/PNa = 2.6 to 8.2), corresponding to a fractional Ca2+ current, Pf = 12 to 42%. This observation suggests that the total charge transferred by receptors can vary independently from the amount of Ca2+ in their ionic current and that these biophysical properties may be controlled by separate mechanisms.

In addition to their overarching responsibility as principal mediators of excitatory transmission in the central nervous system, the ability of iGluRs to pass Ca2+ plays essential roles in brain function and dysfunction and has been under assiduous investigation. The values reported in the literature for NMDA receptor relative Ca2+ permeability vary over a wide range, PCa/PNa = 1.1 to 10.5, while values for their fractional Ca2+ permeability are tighter, Pf = 10 to 15% (28, 29, 44, 45). Most likely, this variability reflects the diverse experimental approaches and conditions used by different groups over the years (2, 5, 26). Collectively, these results led to the currently accepted view that the Ca2+ permeability of iGluRs is controlled solely by the subunit composition of the receptor assembly (4, 5). To date, a singular study reports evidence that an endogenous negative allosteric modulator, acidosis, reduces NMDA receptor fractional Ca2+ permeability (Pf) of 11.3% to 7.6% when the pH drops from 7.3 to pH 6.5 (14). More recently, two new synthetic allosteric modulators, EU1622-14 and EU1794-4, were found to reduce the receptor’s relative Ca2+ permeability (16–18). Aside from these instances, where diffusible gating modifiers changed NMDA receptor Ca2+ permeability, there is, to our knowledge, only one other mechanism that can control NMDA Ca2+ permeability of a given receptor protein: direct posttranslational modification by protein kinase A (46). Although the evidence remains indirect, results show that the Ca2+ content of excitatory postsynaptic currents can be modified in real time in response to adrenergic (47, 48) and dopaminergic stimulation (49), and following ifenprodil treatment (47). Our finding that NMDA receptor ligands can produce rapid, reversible, and bidirectional changes in the receptor’s Ca2+ permeability integrates these disparate findings into a developing paradigm, which postulates that the NMDA receptor Ca2+ permeability is under dynamic allosteric control.

In the brain, the extracellular concentrations of H+ and Zn2+ fluctuate during normal synaptic transmission and vary with pathological conditions. Generally, the acid–base balance of the cerebrospinal fluid is under homeostatic control, with a pH around 7.3 (33). Although the exact magnitude, polarity, and duration of the pH variations that accompany normal neurotransmission remain under vigorous investigations, it is well accepted that neuronal activity produces local pH transients, which in turn modulate neuronal excitability and firing, and correlate with changes in cognition and emotion (38, 50, 51). During high-frequency neuronal firing the pH can vary transiently by as much as 0.05 to 0.20 units (52, 53), and persistent acidification of 0.5 to 1.6 pH units was reported during and after an ischemic stroke or hypoxic event (54, 55). Postmortem and imaging studies revealed decreased pH values with normal aging, and a decrease by 0.2 pH units in brains of patients with schizophrenia (56, 57). Overall, growing evidence indicates that acidification may be a common occurrence in psychiatric disorders (58). It will be important to evaluate whether the reduction in NMDA receptor Ca2+ permeability that accompanies acidification is neuroprotective or pathological. Similarly, it is well accepted that extracellular Zn2+ concentrations fluctuate during normal synaptic transmission, and dysregulation of Zn2+ homeostasis associates with severe pathologies (59, 60). Specifically, animals modified to express NMDA receptors insensitive to Zn2+ inhibition showed altered pain processing, suggesting a specific role for this modulation in pain perception (61). It is unclear whether these in vivo effects of Zn2+ reflect a reduced Ca2+ influx or a general decrease in total current. Other endogenous ligands, such as neurosteroids and polyamines, modulate NMDA receptors with allosteric mechanisms. It remains to be determined whether they also affect the receptor’s Ca2+ permeability and whether this regulation has physiological significance.

Notably, the lowest PCa/PNa value we observed, which was 2.6 for N1-1a/N2A receptors in high H+ concentrations (pH 6.5), was nearly twofold larger than that observed for Ca2+-permeable AMPA receptors (PCa/PNa, 1.5) and represents a substantial Ca2+ enrichment over the value expected for a nonselective cation-permeable channel (PCa/PNa, 1). To account for the results presented here, we propose that the fractional Ca2+ content of NMDA receptor currents rather than fixed for each receptor is dynamic and under bidirectional modulation. The range of Ca2+ permeabilities we establish here for NMDA receptors (PCa/PNa 2.6 to 8.2), together with the range reported recently for non-NMDA receptors (PCa/PNa, 0 to 1) (4) indicates distinct mechanisms of Ca2+ handling in these two receptor classes. Because NMDA receptors always enrich the Ca2+ content of their current, relative to the external solution, they likely have intrinsic mechanisms that select for Ca2+. In contrast, non-NMDA receptors, which pass currents with a reduced Ca2+ content relative to the external solution, may have intrinsic mechanisms that select against Ca2+. Importantly, for NMDA receptors, the entire range of permeability is available to any receptor and is under allosteric control, whereas for non-NMDA receptors, Ca2+ permeability appears to be a fixed property of each receptor assembly.

The mechanism by which allosteric modulators control the Ca2+ content of NMDA receptor currents is likely to involve conformational changes in or around the external Ca2+ binding site. Ample evidence suggests that the Ca2+ permeability of NMDA receptors relies on a Ca2+-binding site located externally to the pore (43, 62, 63). Likely this site resides within the highly acidic DRPEE sequence, which is adjacent to the gate-forming residues on the M3 transmembrane helix of N1 subunits (64, 65). One possibility is that allosteric modulators, which work by influencing the probability of the M3 helix being in its bent position (66, 67), also affect the conformation of the external binding site for Ca2+, and thus its affinity for Ca2+ or its ability to funnel Ca2+ effectively into the channel. It remains to be determined whether the effect of modulators on Po and their effect on Ca2+ permeation are correlated. Our results show that NTD ligands, such as H+, Zn2+, and IFN, which are NAMs, also reduce Ca2+ permeability. GNE-9278, a PAM that acts through residues situated in the linker region, between the agonist-binding domain and transmembrane domain, increases NMDA receptor Ca2+ permeability at pH 7.2 but not at pH 8, suggesting that rather than modulating Ca2+ permeability, GNE-9278 interferes with the pH effect. Given that the phosphorylation state of intracellular residues can also affect the receptor’s Ca2+ permeability, we conclude that the allosteric control of Ca2+ permeability is not specific for a particular receptor region but is a general feature of the NMDA receptor’s operation. Whether external ligands and intracellular phosphorylation change Ca2+ permeability with similar or distinct mechanisms remains to be determined.

NMDA receptors have been pursued assiduously over more than four decades as pharmacologic targets for a wide variety of severe neuropsychiatric conditions (68). Despite a rich complement of regulatory sites and increasing number of pharmacologic tools, few ligands have proved effective and safe in patients (69). A huge barrier to drug development has been the ubiquitous involvement of NMDA receptors with neurotransmission and cellular plasticity across the central nervous system and therefore the need for judicious targeting of specific receptor populations and functions. Our results suggest that preclinical evaluation of new drug candidates should include reporting their effects on the NMDA receptor Ca2+ flux, which presently is not standard practice. Similarly, an increasing number of variations in the primary structure of NMDA receptors identified in patients have been designated pathogenic (70, 71). With few exceptions, the functional evaluation of these receptors does not include Ca2+ permeability assays (72). Our results suggest that information on the Ca2+ permeability of disease-related variants will be necessary when matching patients with available therapies.
