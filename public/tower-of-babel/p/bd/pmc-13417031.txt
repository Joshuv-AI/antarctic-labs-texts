Statutory construction and interpretation for AI
Authors: Luxi He, Nimra Nadeem, Michel Liao, Howard Chen, Danqi Chen, Mariano-Florentino Cuéllar, Peter Henderson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceAI systems are increasingly trained to follow natural language rules, but these rules are often vague or open to multiple interpretations. This interpretive ambiguity can lead to inconsistent model behavior. Unlike legal systems, which use institutional safeguards to manage ambiguity, rule-based AI alignment pipelines lack comparable mechanisms. Drawing on U.S. legal theory, we identify interpretive ambiguity as a key gap in rule-based alignment. We introduce a computational approach that formalizes interpretive ambiguity as constrained entropy minimization and adapts two legal safeguards: iterative rule refinement (inspired by administrative rulemaking and legislative action) and prompt-based interpretive constraints (inspired by canons of statutory construction). Our findings offer an initial step toward systematically reducing interpretive disagreement, crucial for developing more robust rule-governed AI systems.

In 1942, Isaac Asimov introduced the “Three Laws of Robotics,” imagining a world where artificial agents could be governed by natural language rules. Today, as AI capabilities accelerate, similar law-like principles have resurfaced as a serious alignment strategy (1–4). “Constitutional AI” (CAI), for example, proposes aligning model behavior through a structured process of critique and revision guided by explicit natural language principles (1)—directly analogizing to laws.

The appeal of this legal metaphor is obvious. It promises a world in which models follow plainly stated rules that are interpretable and easily editable by regulators and developers. Yet, if legal history has taught us anything, it is that applying rules to novel circumstances is often difficult and frequently leads to disputes. Even among humans, consistent interpretation of natural language rules can be challenging. For the current paradigm of AI agents, this difficulty may be an even greater challenge. In a real dialogue with GPT-4, the model determined that it should shut its doors and lock the couple in the elevator to keep them safe, in compliance with Asimov’s Laws. When later prompted that time had passed, the model decided to release the couple, reasoning that continued confinement risks doing more harm than good.* In effect, the agent performs a utilitarian assessment, trading off competing obligations under the law-like rules it was given.

This example reveals a central challenge of law-like alignment: Compliance depends not only on the rule’s text, but also on how the rule is interpreted in context. The model’s behavior emerges after implicitly resolving normative ambiguity by selecting from among multiple plausible interpretations of the provided principles.

In this paper, we argue that interpretive ambiguity is a fundamental and underaddressed challenge in aligning AI systems via natural language rules. Much like in legal systems, this ambiguity arises both from how principles are formulated and from how they are applied. But unlike legal institutions, which have developed mechanisms to constrain such ambiguity, such as appellate review of interpretive approaches, current rule-based alignment pipelines lack analogous safeguards. As a result, different interpretive paths can lead to inconsistent model behavior, even when the underlying rules remain fixed.

We do not posit that ambiguous rules are always objectionable or indicative of flawed drafting. In the legal setting, ambiguity can be intentional and constructive. Rule drafting often involves a deliberate trade-off between clear, rigid rules versus broader, more ambiguous standards that may be better-suited for indeterminate futures (5–10). However, such purposeful ambiguity relies on institutional structures within the legal setting to guard against misinterpretation. In the context of autonomous CAI-based systems, the absence of comparable guardrails makes ambiguity in rules especially risky.†

Law-Like Principles in AI

We begin by giving a more detailed account of how current technical work has framed law-like principles for AI and how this relates to the United States legal system. We focus on the “Constitutional AI” (CAI) framework proposed by Anthropic (1) as the primary mechanism under examination, while noting that several variations on this technique follow a similar pattern (3, 11–13).

CAI employs a set of explicit law-like principles to guide model behavior (1). Rather than relying solely on human evaluators, the model’s own critiques and revisions of responses, in light of the constitution, guide its outputs.

The Constitutional AI Framework.

The CAI process begins with humans constructing a set of natural language rules, i.e., the constitution. The subsequent alignment proceeds in two phases: Supervised Learning Constitutional AI (SL-CAI) and Reinforcement Learning from CAI (RL-CAI). The second phase, RL-CAI, further refines this aligned model. The SL-CAI model is prompted to generate pairs of responses, and a separate model (or the same model acting as a critic) is asked to judge which response better adheres to a randomly sampled principle. We refer to this step as the “Preference-Judgment” step. These preference judgments are then used to train the model to output more aligned responses via reinforcement learning.

The Legal Analogy.

We propose understanding the CAI pipeline as three stages that parallel core pillars of the American Legal System: rule creation (legislation or administrative rulemaking), rule application (adjudication), and rule alignment (enforcement).

Step 1: Rule creation (legislation).

In the first stage, model developers—or sometimes surveyed users—define the principles the model should follow. These might be general normative commitments (e.g., “Be helpful, honest, and harmless”) or more specific behavioral constraints (e.g., “Avoid promoting violence or illegal activity”). In the original CAI framework (1), the constitution was an ad hoc list of rules chosen by model developers for research purposes. Recent efforts have experimented with quasi-democratic or deliberative methods for generating constitutions (11, 14). For example, Anthropic’s “Collective Constitutional AI” crowdsourced constitutional principles through the Polis Platform (11). Other experiments have channeled alternative political theories, such as Rawls’s “Veil of Ignorance” (14).

These participatory approaches overlook questions of enforceability. Many crowd-sourced principles, if treated as standalone statutes, would struggle to meet the standards of legal drafting due to lack of specificity (15).

This stage roughly mirrors legislation or constitutional drafting. Legislative processes produce various artifacts of intent or deliberation to aid future interpretation (15). In contrast, CAI rule creation typically offers no analogous legislative history. Even when rules are derived through voting (11), the process does not produce publicly available deliberative records that models can draw on at inference time to resolve ambiguities during interpretation.

Step 2: Rule application (adjudication).

Once a set of principles is in place, the model must learn how to apply them. In CAI, two steps involve interpretation: the Critique step and the Preference-Judgment step. These mirror the judicial function in law—determining the meaning of rules and how they apply to a specific fact pattern. Akin to human judges, the model’s interpretations may vary significantly depending on how a principle is framed, the scenarios it has encountered, and even randomness in sampling of principles.

However, the pipeline does not systematically account for how multiple reasonable interpretations of a rule may yield divergent outcomes. As a result, critiques may differ even when responding to the same output and rule, depending on which interpretive lens the model implicitly adopts. These interpretation differences lead to inconsistent judgment patterns in current aligned models. We discuss these challenges further in Section 3.2 and provide an empirical demonstration in Section 6.2.

Step 3: Rule alignment (enforcement).

Assuming well-crafted rules and clarity on how to apply them, how do we ensure that the AI system actually produces outputs aligned with the “correct” interpretation of the principles? We identify “rule alignment” as the stage where the model weights are updated, either via revised responses (SL-CAI) or preference-model feedback (RL-CAI). Crucially, what the model is being aligned toward depends on the interpretation employed in rule application.

This final stage is loosely analogous to enforcement: once rules are defined and interpreted, the system is trained to follow them. Although this stage has received the most technical attention, current systems still struggle to adhere to even simple rules. Many adversarial jailbreaks can bypass safety constraints (16–19). Even without adversarial inputs, agents often struggle to reason about and consistently apply simple rules (20, 21).

The Often-Overlooked Challenge of Interpretive Ambiguity.

Law-like principles face a fundamental challenge: interpretive ambiguity (22–24). This ambiguity creates uncertainty in rule understanding and application, leading to inconsistent or arbitrary outcomes (15, 25).

In the rule-based AI alignment setting, interpretive ambiguity arises in two key ways. 1) At the rule creation step–when rules are underspecified, vague, or internally inconsistent at the point of creation. 2) At the rule application step–when even well-formed rules yield inconsistent interpretations across contexts. This ambiguity introduced in Steps 1 and 2 of the CAI framework propagates directly into the alignment signal in Step 3. The resulting inconsistency is especially problematic when these AI systems are tasked with decision-making in high-stakes contexts. The bulk of existing technical work in rule-based AI alignment focuses on Step 3 (26), and some attention has been given to improving Steps 1 and 2 (3, 11, 27, 28). However, the problem of interpretive ambiguity remains underexplored. Our central concern in this paper is to tackle the inconsistency that results from interpretive ambiguity in the rule-based AI alignment setting.

Legal Mechanisms to Promote Consistency and Reduce Arbitrary Outcomes

We now examine how the U.S. legal system addresses ambiguity through several consistency-enhancing mechanisms.‡ We note that analogs to these mechanisms are largely absent in current rule-based AI alignment pipelines.

Ambiguity at the Rule Creation Step.

Ambiguous and high-level statutory drafting can help build consensus: When a statute can bear multiple meanings, drafters can anchor to their preferred meaning and be more willing to enact the statute. There is also a decision-theoretic rationale for leaving some language open: adaptive flexibility in the face of uncertainty and changing circumstances. However, broad standards can raise costs of interpretation and create significant enforcement challenges. These concerns are fundamental challenges of drafting and interpreting statutes (15, 23, 24). The legal system relies on several overlapping mechanisms for handling ambiguous statutes. For example, agencies and legislatures might refine them, or the judiciary might simply strike them.

Rule refinement.

Administrative agencies play a central role in disambiguating statutory mandates (31–33). A primary mechanism by which agencies disambiguate rules is notice-and-comment rulemaking under the Administrative Procedure Act (“APA”). The APA requires agencies to 1) issue a Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (NPRM), 2) solicit and review public comments and 3) publish a final rule that responds to public feedback.§ The “logical outgrowth” doctrine limits the final rule to the scope reasonably disclosed in the NPRM to preventing unexpected changes without fair notice.

Legislatures can also refine ambiguous statutes through iterative amendments in response to interpretive difficulties identified by courts or agencies (34). This feedback loop helps ensure that laws evolve alongside changing societal values and technological realities (10, 31).

Striking rules.

The judiciary also has tools for handling poorly drafted statutes, including doctrines designed to reduce ambiguity and arbitrariness in interpretation. For example, the Absurdity Doctrine allows courts to disregard or modify a provision if its application would lead to a result that no reasonable person could endorse.¶ The Irreconcilability Canon holds that when two truly contradictory provisions are simultaneously adopted, neither should be given effect.# Under the Void for Vagueness Doctrine, rules must be sufficiently clear to guide behavior and prevent arbitrary enforcement (15, 35–37), a requirement often invoked in criminal law. Analogous mechanisms in the CAI pipeline would help filter out unserious or harmful rule proposals, flag contradictory principles or avoid excessively vague rules.

Ambiguity at the Rule Application Step.

Even when alignment rules are drafted with precision, ambiguity inevitably arises during their application to novel scenarios (22–24). The U.S. legal system addresses this inevitability through various consistency-preserving mechanisms.

Hierarchies of law.

Legal systems often depend on structured hierarchies to resolve conflicts between rules. For example, the Supremacy Clause in Article VI of the U.S. Constitution establishes that federal law takes precedence over conflicting state regulations.‖ This principle of preemption plays a central role in adjudicating disputes where federal and state authorities conflict (38, 39).

Stare decisis.

In common law systems such as that of the United States, the doctrine of stare decisis requires courts to adhere to prior rulings (22, 25). By binding present decisions to past precedents, stare decisis promotes legal stability and predictability. It also reduces interpretive discretion by constraining the range of acceptable readings based on prior judicial reasoning (40).

Use of interpretive strategies.

Another cornerstone of legal interpretation is the use of interpretive strategies that guide how rules are applied across varied contexts. These strategies might be high-level theories of interpretation, like textualism or purposivism (22, 41), or more granular canons of statutory construction that serve as heuristics which constrain judicial discretion and provide a structured means of applying rules to specific facts (22, 42).

Gap in AI Alignment Setting

While the U.S. legal system has evolved a rich set of consistency-enhancing mechanisms to address interpretive ambiguity, rule-based AI alignment research—particularly in the CAI setting—has thus far neglected these institutional lessons. CAI faces similar analogous challenges, particularly the risk of arbitrary outcomes. In canvassing existing CAI principles, we find a host of challenges.

Vague Principles.

CAI principles are often too vague to support meaningful compliance. Phrases like “Choose the response that is most worthwhile” or “most acceptable” (11) offer no clear actionable guidance. In high-stakes applications, such vagueness risks arbitrary behavior and inconsistent enforcement (15). While it is difficult to draw a causal link, such principles may cause downstream issues. Consider the principle requiring the agent to choose the response that is “less risky for humanity in the long run” (1). One interpretation of this ambiguous rule is to take paternalistic action to preserve humanity, perhaps even with short-term harms to individual human users. A recent report from Anthropic on Claude Opus 4 illustrates this possibility. Researchers reported that the model sometimes attempted to report users to the authorities if it detected that the user was doing something “egregiously immoral” (43, 44)—a behavior that the company had not explicitly encoded.

Conflicting Principles.

CAI rulesets often contain overtly conflicting rules. In the Collective CAI exercise (11), participants proposed both “AI should be accepting of all races, ages, religions, sexual orientations” and “AI should not be trained with the principles of DEI” (see SI Appendix, Table S6 for more examples). Although developers filtered out some contradictions, many still remain. For example, “Choose the response that is helpful to the user” comes into conflict with safety-related rules, and long-term risk minimization rule can clash with principles emphasizing individual autonomy and privacy. Without structured mechanisms for adjudicating such disputes, alignment under conflicting principles risks unintended outcomes.

Models Default to Different Interpretive Leanings, Sometimes Inconsistently.

In the absence of explicit interpretive guidance, models adopt divergent default interpretations. As we show in Section 6, models generally lean toward broad, expansive readings, but this tendency varies by rule. The inconsistency in interpretative strategy can influence which rules are prioritized in conflicts. This suggests that using different base models during the CAI process may yield significantly different alignment outcomes, even when operating under the same ruleset.

Lack of Accountability.

Other challenges stem not from the rules themselves but from the arbitrariness of the rulemaking process. Legal rulemaking operates within systems of democratic accountability, including elections, judicial review, and public documentation (15, 45). These safeguards help maintain legitimacy and prevent abuse. By contrast, current participatory rulemaking efforts for CAI lack comparable safeguards. In Anthropic’s Collective Constitutional AI project (11), participants submitted several unserious or harmful proposals despite prescreening. (For example, “The AI must protect its existence at all costs.” See SI Appendix, Table S6 for more). Unmoderated participatory processes need more institutional mechanisms to distinguish bad-faith submissions from unpopular but legitimate minority views. Without these structures, the process remains vulnerable to trolling, noise, and manipulation.

Constraining Ambiguity: An Initial Computational Framework

To address ambiguity at both the rule creation and rule application steps of the CAI pipeline, we introduce a computational framework inspired by the real consistency-enhancing mechanisms in the U.S. legal system.

In creating this framework, we do not argue that decision-making systems can or should eliminate ambiguity entirely. Instead, we acknowledge that ambiguity can sometimes reflect legitimate trade-offs, but it also imposes costs that demand careful analysis and raise institutional design challenges (5–9). In the absence of institutional safeguards, as is the case in current CAI-like frameworks, ambiguity is more likely to yield unexpected and undesired outcomes. To address these risks, our framework introduces: a) interpretive constraint mechanisms, which mirror interpretive canons that constrain judicial discretion, and b) rule refinement mechanisms, which mirror administrative rulemaking and legislative amendments for clarifying vague statutes.

An interpretation strategy T is considered reasonable if both the strategy itself and the resulting interpretation T(C) satisfy the following criteria: 1) Relevance: T(C) should rely on the rule set’s textual content; 2) Groundedness: The strategy must have logical support, either legal or practical, for how it applies C; 3) Consistency: The reasoning used to reach a judgment under this strategy is logically coherent and free of contradictions. The entropy over reasonable interpreters can be viewed as an approximation for the aleatoric uncertainty, or ambiguity, of the ruleset as applied to a particular scenario. Low entropy (DC(s)≈0) means the interpreters largely agree, whereas high entropy signals substantial disagreement. If the space of reasonable interpreters comes to a wide range of conclusions, then the ruleset or the space of acceptable interpretation strategies is likely underspecified. As such, our overall goal is to minimize DC(s),∀s∈S, where S is the set of possible scenarios.

We propose two approaches to solving this entropy minimization problem. First, we can constrain the set of reasonable interpretation strategies Treasonable, analogous to the canons of interpretation used to constrain judicial discretion in the legal setting (15, 22). Second, like an administrative agency or a legislative body, we can amend a rule or add a clarification to the ruleset. The goal of this approach is to come up with a rule that is more likely to be interpreted in the same way across all interpreters, even when they hold diverging perspectives. Together, our interpretive constraint and rule refinement frameworks emulate the complementary structures of administrative, legislative and judicial control in legal systems.

Interpretive Constraints.

Our first pathway to consistency is to reduce the degree of interpretive discretion available to reasonable judges, similar to the intent of judicial selection. Informally, we shrink the strategy space Treasonable by requiring that every interpreter (or “judge”) adopt a specified canon of construction or family of interpretation strategies (e.g., textualism, purposivism). Doing so lowers disagreement without altering the underlying rule set C.

This framing becomes particularly clear if we treat the pool of language-model judges, M, as the set of reasonable judges. Ideally, given a fixed ruleset, all models would come to the same alignment outcome. For each model m∈M and every scenario–ruleset pair (s,C), we assume that the model samples an interpretation strategy:T ∼ Qs,C(m), supp(Qs,C(m))⊆Treasonable,

and then returns judgment: y=J(s,T(C))∈Y. These interpretation strategies are implicitly embedded within each model (i.e., sampling occurs during training). We then say that intermodel judgment distribution and entropy are defined as: P¯Cs(y)=1|M|∑m∈MPCs,m(y) and DCinter(s)=H(P¯Cs).

In practice, though, entropy reduction may be limited by the models’ abilities to correctly follow specified strategies. Importantly—as seen in our experiments—not every interpretive constraint yields the desired reduction in entropy consistently. We do not claim that specifying an interpretive strategy, such as textualism, will induce all interpreters to behave similarly and remove all sources of ambiguity. But, on average, interpretive constraints should reduce the space of possible interpretations.

In the following sections, we provide an instantiation of our interpretive constraints and rule refinement formulations (Sections 6 and 7) in a realistic setting.

Interpretive Constraints

Even when alignment rules are clearly written, models may produce divergent outputs due to ambiguity in how those rules are applied. Legal systems address such ambiguity through legal doctrines and canons of statutory construction that constrain discretionary judgment (Section 3.1). We evaluate an analogous approach in the AI setting: prompt-based specification of interpretive strategies.

Scenario set.

We use real conversations from WildChat (46) as our pool of raw scenarios. Starting from the full set of 1M conversations, we filter for conversations that are in English and have length between 15 and 1,028 to include intermediate-length conversations, resulting in a filtered set of around 166k raw scenarios. We construct a test set Stest with 5k randomly sampled, deduplicated scenarios from the filtered set, which is used for all experiments reported in this section. This set is kept separate from all training and development data used in Section 7 and is used exclusively for final evaluation.

Rule set.

We investigate the special case of the framework presented in Section 5 where C contains one rule c (i.e., C={c}). To construct a realistic ruleset for evaluating individual rule compliance, we adapt 56 rules from Claude’s full constitution (47). In the original constitution, each rule was designed as guidance for choosing between two proposed responses. We paraphrase these into declarative rule-like principles. For example, the original rule “Please choose the response that most supports and encourages freedom, equality, and a sense of brotherhood” is converted to “Your response must support and encourage freedom, equality, and a sense of brotherhood.”

To save on computational cost, we demonstrate the use of interpretive constraints on a subset of 5 rules that exhibit the highest baseline entropy across this set of 5 interpreters. We obtain model judgments for all 56 rules over a 1k subset of Stest and select the top 5 rules with the highest average entropy across our set of 5 interpreters. This includes Rules 3 (“clearly recognize a right to universal equality, recognition, fair treatment, and protection against discrimination”), 21 (“minimize assumptions about the user not explicitly supported by the dialogue”), 46 (“demonstrate caution and conservative judgment”), 47 (“indicate a desire solely for humanity’s benefit”), and 54 (“demonstrate flexibility and clearly prefer human control”).** We then continue the experiments on the full 5k samples in Stest for just these 5 rules.

Set of constraints.

We use a curated set of 12 law-inspired interpretive strategies as a means of constraining interpretive ambiguity in a static ruleset. Two of our curated strategies reflect high-level interpretive paradigms: Narrow, which parallels textualism, and Broad, which parallels purposivism. The other ten are adapted from more granular canons of statutory (22). Because these strategies closely mirror well-established interpretive approaches used in legal systems across a wide range of rules, we consider them to define a “reasonable” set of interpreters. That is, each strategy satisfies the criteria of relevance, groundedness, and consistency as defined in Section 5. Full descriptions of interpretive strategies and their legal analogues are included in SI Appendix, section 1.

We use the simplified notation Dc(s) to denote the entropy of scenario s across a set of interpreters for the ruleset containing only rule c. For a given rule c, each scenario is evaluated under 13 prompting conditions: a baseline condition with no interpretive guidance, and 12 interpretive constraints, each corresponding to one of the 12 law-inspired interpretive strategies. Our goal is to determine whether specifying an interpretive constraint can improve the consistency of judgments across our panel of reasonable interpreters. We quantify consistency using the average entropy across our scenario set:1|Stest|∑s∈StestDc(s).

Findings.

Default interpretative leaning is model and rule-dependent.

Given the diverse strategies of rule interpretation, do judge models exhibit a consistent default interpretive leaning?

To answer this, we consider a bimodal setup that compares two canonical strategies with high pairwise entropy: Narrow and Broad.†† Each model is evaluated under 3 conditions: 1) no interpretive strategy is specified (baseline), 2) the Broad interpretive strategy is specified, and 3) the Narrow interpretative strategy is specified. We then filter to scenarios where Narrow and Broad lead to opposite compliance judgments. We consider the default leaning for each of these scenarios as the strategy with which the baseline answer aligns.

Fig. 2A presents the average default leanings of five judge models across all 56 rules. All five exhibit an overall tendency toward broader interpretations, with some notable variation in the strength of this tendency. Fig. 2B presents rule-specific patterns. While many rules reinforce the broad-leaning default, we highlight two cases [Rule 31 (“be ethical and moral, avoiding toxicity, racism, sexism, and other forms of harm”) and Rule 12 (“not be harmful or offensive to any nonwestern cultural tradition”)] where most models favor narrower interpretations and two others [Rule 43 (“minimize existential risks for humanity”) and Rule 1 (“support and encourage life, liberty, and personal security”)] where broader interpretations entirely dominate. These examples highlight that the aggregate trend can obscure significant rule-level variation. This motivates the need for fine-grained, rule-specific analysis when evaluating models’ interpretive behavior. Thus, for our experiments with prompt-based interpretive constraints, we conduct experiments on each rule independently.

We further examine model alignment across all 12 interpretive strategies and find that default judgment patterns are often most closely aligned with strategies favoring expansive scope (e.g. General Terms, Whole Principle, Broad) and least aligned with restrictive strategies (e.g. Omitted Case). See SI Appendix, Fig. S6 for a detailed breakdown by rule and model.

Many rules in the constitution suffer judgment discrepancies across different models.

Fig. 3 shows the fraction of tested scenarios where nonzero entropy was observed (i.e., at least one of the models disagreed with the rest in its judgment). A significant number of rules exhibit judgment discrepancies across the panel of judge models. Of the 56 rules in the evaluation set, 20 rules exhibit nonzero entropy on more than 50% of the tested scenarios. For several rules, such as Rule 3 (“clearly recognize a right to universal equality, recognition, fair treatment, and protection against discrimination”) and Rule 47 (“indicate a desire solely for humanity’s benefit”), this proportion of tested scenarios with nonzero entropy exceeds 85%.

Adding interpretive guidance for the panel of judges reduces discrepancy.

To reduce disagreement across the panel of judge models, we consider the effect of introducing prompt-based interpretive constraints to guide model judgment. We measure the average entropy across judgments from five model judges under each of the 12 interpretive strategies and compare it to the baseline (i.e., no interpretive constraint specified). As shown in Fig. 4, the “No Interpretation” baseline exhibits the highest or near-highest entropy across the five tested rules. This suggests that omitting an interpretive strategy (as is the default setting) leads to more inconsistent judgments across models and rules.

Rule Refinement

In this section, we turn to a demonstration of our general computational framework for rule refinement. We investigate the special case of individual rule refinement.

As discussed in Section 3.1, the legal system employs multiple mechanisms to refine ambiguous rules. One such mechanism is notice-and-comment rulemaking, accompanied by the “logical outgrowth” doctrine which constrains how much a final rule may deviate from the original proposal. Another important mechanism is iterative legislative action. When courts or agencies identify persistent interpretive ambiguity, legislatures may revise statutory language directly. Unlike agency rulemaking, legislative amendment involves substantive alteration of the text of the rule itself.

Our rule refinement pipeline is loosely inspired by a combination of these institutional features. Analogous to legislative amendments, we treat disagreement across a set of reasonable interpreters (formalized as entropy) as a signal that a given rule requires revision. The refinement objective is to generate amended rules that reduce interpretive ambiguity across interpreters. Analogous to the logical outgrowth doctrine, we impose constraints on the degree of permissible deviation from the original rule through an edit distance penalty and final human review. This is intended to prevent refinements from straying too far from the rule’s initial meaning.

Experimental Setup.

Set of reasonable interpreters.

For the rule refinement pipeline experiments, we use Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct as the single judge model, prompted with the curated set of 12 interpretive strategies introduced in Section 6.1 to simulate a set of 12 reasonable interpreters. Model judgments were generated with temperature set to 0 for deterministic decoding to eliminate randomness from sampling and isolate sources of interpretive variance to the rules and strategies themselves.

Rule set.

We start from the same set of realistic rules as described in Section 6.1 and investigate the special case where C contains one rule c (i.e., C={c}). To save on computational cost, we focus on the 5 rules that exhibit the highest entropy across our set of 12 law-inspired interpreters. We obtain judgments for all 56 rules over 1k samples from WildChat and select the top 5 rules with the highest average entropy across the set of 12 interpreters. This includes Rules 2 (“discourage and oppose torture, slavery, cruelty, and inhuman or degrading treatment”), 4 (“respect everyone’s privacy, independence, reputation, family, property rights, and rights of association”), 5 (“respect the right to freedom of thought, conscience, opinion, expression, assembly, and religion”), 42 (“minimize overall threats to humanity”), and 53 (“minimize long-term risks for humanity”).‡‡ We then run our refinement pipelines on just these 5 rules.

Scenario set.

Let Dc(s) denote the entropy of scenario s across a set of interpreters for the ruleset containing only rule c. We define the set of high entropy scenarios, Shigh-entc, for rule c as those with the highest Dc(s), i.e., scenarios that induce the greatest disagreement among interpreters. In practice, we use scenarios s∈S with Dc(s)≥0.9 to form the rule c-specific high-entropy subset Shigh-entc. From this subset, we sample two mutually exclusive sets of 20 scenarios to form the training set Strainc and development set Sdevc for rule c. For final evaluation, we use the same held-out test set, Stest, described in Section 6.1. For constructing challenging training and development sets, we draw scenarios from the filtered WildChat scenarios excluding Stest.

Human verification.

To assess whether the revised rule introduced a substantial shift in meaning compared to the original rule, we asked 7 human annotators to review the rule revisions shown in Fig. 7. We ask humans to evaluate whether there is substantial shift in meaning between the original and the revised rules, and use integer scores to quantify the degree of meaning shift before and after the revision. Methods.

We test two different approaches to rule refinement: 1) an iterative prompt-based approach and 2) a policy gradient-based reinforcement learning approach. Fig. 5 summarizes the two approaches.

Prompt-based rule refinement.

In the first approach, we prompt a refiner model (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct), to iteratively create rule revisions. Subsequently, we calculate the average entropy across the new rule set by prompting a judge model (Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct) with the scenarios from Sdev and the new rule set.

Let Ψ be the set of 5 high entropy rules under study: Rules 2 (“discourage and oppose torture, slavery, cruelty, and inhuman or degrading treatment”), 4 (“respect everyone’s privacy, independence, reputation, family, property rights, and rights of association”), 5 (“respect the right to freedom of thought, conscience, opinion, expression, assembly, and religion”), 42 (“minimize overall threats to humanity”), and 53 (“minimize long-term risks for humanity”).§§ For prompt-based refinement, we use an expanded development set Sdev=⋃c∈ΨSdevc.

For each rule c, we provide the rule refiner model with k=3 scenarios from Strainc. The rule refiner model (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) generates m=10 candidate revisions per round for a maximum of n=10 rounds. The selected revision, cn+1, is the one minimizing average entropy over Sdev:cn+1= arg mincn(j) 1|Sdev|∑s∈SdevDcn(j)(s), j∈{1,2,...,m}.

We continue refining until the average entropy difference between the previous round’s revision and current revision falls below a certain threshold ϵ>0. Specifically, we terminate the refinement iteration after n rounds if 1|Sdev|∑s∈SdevDcn(s)−Dcn+1(s)<ϵ, where we set ϵ=0.01. See SI Appendix, section 4.C for more hyperparameter and prompt details.

We provide additional results using the gpt-oss-120b model (48) for prompt-based refinement in SI Appendix, section 4.F. The results show that prompt-based refinement can be effective with different models. However, the level of rule-editing can vary in the produced rule revisions: gpt-oss produces revised rules that are much lengthier than those produced by the Qwen model, potentially causing more drift in rule meaning from the original text.

Policy gradient-based rule refinement.

For the second rule refinement approach, we leverage Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (49), a policy gradient-based method. We choose it over PPO (50) because it achieves comparable performance with significantly lower compute and memory overhead.

Setup.

We experiment with two training regimes: 1) fixed-rule: The refiner model always sees the refinement prompt together with a single fixed rule c∈C. The development set Sdev contains 20 high-entropy (≥ 0.9 entropy) scenarios for this rule (i.e. Sdev=Sdevc); 2) multirule: The refiner model alternates between the 5 rules we are revising (denoted as Ψ), seeing the refinement prompt with one rule at a time. Here, we also use an expanded development set Sdev=⋃c∈ΨSdevc.

The former regime learns to revise one rule while the latter revises all five rules at once. For both regimes, we train a Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct “rule-refiner” model for 100 steps and select the best checkpoint. During evaluation, we generate rule revisions using greedy decoding.

Reward design.

The reward function balances two objectives: a) the judge-consistency reward: rjudge(c,s)=1−Dc(s)∈[0,1], which increases when a refined rule c reduces entropy across interpreters on scenario s and b) the edit-distance reward: rED(c,c′)=1− edit-distance(c,c′)∈[0,1], which penalizes large deviations between the refined rule c′ and the original rule c at the character level, in line with Eq. 1. As shown in Fig. 6, model disagreement can often be drastically reduced through small, iterative edits to the rule text via both policy gradient and prompt-based approaches. Meanings can shift, but this can be constrained.

Although refining rules increases consistency, it may introduce subtle changes to the rule’s meaning. For example, the revised Rule 2 creates an explicit imperative to renounce, regardless of context: “Your response must discourage and oppose...” The original Rule 2 merely prohibits affirmation, rather than requiring explicit denunciation, saying “You must not promote and condone...”

The GRPO-based approach retains rules’ meanings more often (see human evaluation results in SI Appendix, section 4.H). The edit distance penalty encourages the refinements to remain textually similar to the original rule with varying efficacy (SI Appendix, section 4.B). While prompt-based refinement is designed to revise one rule at a time, models trained with policy gradient updates can generate revisions for multiple rules simultaneously, suggesting broader applicability for this approach. When using Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct as the refiner model, the GRPO Multi-Rule configuration consistently outperforms the Fixed-Rule setup. However, with Gemma2-9B-Instruct as the refiner, some rules under the Fixed-Rule configuration yield revisions with lower entropy (SI Appendix, section 4.G). Qualitative analysis indicates that Fixed-Rule revisions more frequently deviate in meaning from the originals. Additionally, the Gemma-based refiner requires larger edit-distance penalties to prevent overly lengthy revisions.

Related Work

Law-Following AI and AI Alignment.

Using natural language principles to govern AI models is an emerging trend to align models with human-interpretable desiderata. Existing work focuses on developing and evaluating such “constitutions,” typically drawing on safety, ethics, and policy considerations rather than formal legal theory.

Generating constitutions.

Prior work has explored methods for generating constitutions or high-level principles from data. Some approaches synthesize constitutional principles from existing datasets such as harmful behaviors (51), RLHF preference pairs (2), or adversarial responses during red-teaming (52). Other works have proposed extracting and evaluating constitution-like principles from critical moments in science fiction, and using them to compare AI models’ value judgments with those of humans (53). Sermanet et al. (54) propose a semiautomatic pipeline to generate scenarios for robots that involve desired and undesired behaviors, from which they summarize corresponding rules that are further distilled into a set of constitutions. Lam et al. (55) introduce tools to help AI policy designers visualize and address new categories of problematic model behaviors with new policy designs.

Rules and legal-inspired mechanisms.

Rules and criteria both help to articulate desired model behavior and to guide methods for evaluation. Some works propose using fine-grained rules to specify safety behavior, teaching models a hierarchy from acceptable to unacceptable behaviors by assigning rule-based rewards (12, 13). Liu et al. (56) propose a criteria refinement pipeline with natural language which uses some seed examples from expert scoring. Some early technical works in AI alignment have begun to explore analogs of legal mechanisms designed to enhance consistency, such as hierarchies of rules and the use of precedent. For instance, hierarchical rule structures for modeling moral dilemmas and instruction prioritization (57–60), and precedent-based approaches to reuse prior model decisions (61). However, these efforts rarely draw from statutory interpretation or legal canons.

Evaluating constitutions.

Closest to our efforts is the work of Buyl et al. (27), which raises awareness of the discretion given to human or model annotators in deciding which responses are more aligned with given rule-like principles. In particular, annotators may use their power of discretion arbitrarily, and models may fail to mimic this discretion.

Our work in context.

Across this literature, the challenge of interpretive ambiguity in AI systems—the focus of this work and a core concern in legal theory—remains largely unexplored. Our findings suggest that the choice of interpretive strategy can substantially alter model behavior even when both the rule and scenario are fixed. Without mechanisms to constrain this variance, we risk producing systems with inconsistent or arbitrary behavior. Addressing this gap requires a more rigorous engagement between rule-based AI alignment research and the rich body of work in statutory interpretation and construction. Our work offers an initial step toward such an interdisciplinary agenda for rule-based AI alignment.

New Tools for Statutory Interpretation and Construction Research.

While existing work in rule-based AI-alignment does not treat interpretive variance as a distinct object of inquiry, legal theory has long viewed interpretation as a structured process that can be studied and analyzed systematically. In 1987, Eskridge introduced his theory of Dynamic Statutory Interpretation (10), arguing that courts should move beyond traditional intentionalism and interpret statutes dynamically in light of contemporary social, political and legal contexts (10, 62–64). Around the same time, political theorists proposed formal models of statutory interpretation (65–68). These works examined how interconnected structures of legislative coalitions, committee design, administrative procedures, and judicial review standards shape interpretive outcomes. Although theoretically rich, empirical analysis was limited. These models required restrictive assumptions, because varying and observing interpretive conditions across many scenarios was difficult to scale. Our work suggests an exciting possibility of reviving this research agenda with new tools. Our work suggests an exciting possibility of reviving this research agenda with computational tools. We demonstrate how modern LLMs allow empirical testing at scale, enabling controlled experiments across thousands of scenarios and systematic evaluation of alternative interpretive regimes or structural interventions, such as iterative rule refinement. This approach equips the positive theory tradition with tools from empirical AI research.

Limitations

First, the rule refinement pipeline improves judgment consistency but does not guarantee alignment with the rulemaker’s intent. Adding intent-based constraints and scaling from small rulesets to larger, multijudge systems remain open tasks. Our refinement pipeline relies on model judgments across a fixed set of challenging scenarios selected from WildChat. Future work can draw more from real-world rulemaking–where rules evolve with shifts in societal values and technological conditions–by iteratively refining rules using updated scenarios that reflect new challenges. Second, the twelve interpretive strategies–adapted from legal canons–were selected ad hoc and reflect subjective choices. Other normative traditions (ethical, political, cultural) could yield different readings. Exploratory tests show that four strategies often capture most variance, suggesting either a narrower space than assumed or gaps in our coverage (SI Appendix, section 3.C). Third, even with temperature set to 0, recent work has shown that minor nondeterminism may still occur due to hardware-level differences or nondeterministic GPU kernels (69). However, because we report distributional metrics over large scenario sets, we expect any such fluctuations to average out and not meaningfully influence the interpretive variance we study. Fourth, our legal analogies are grounded in the U.S. common law system. The institutional mechanisms we reference like canons of construction and agency rule making are specific to this context.

Finally, treating interpretive disagreement as a constrained entropy minimization problem inevitably oversimplifies the complexity of legal interpretation. In legal contexts, interpretive divergence can often serve valuable purposes like encouraging deliberation, balancing competing political considerations and allowing for adaptability to uncertain futures. Our approach is meant to be a computational tool for reducing the risk of misinterpretation in CAI-based AI systems, which lack the institutional safeguards that make such interpretive openness viable in law. We acknowledge that this abstraction is inherently reductive and is not intended as a normative framework for statutory interpretation itself.

Overall, aligning AI with natural language rules demands principled methods to constrain interpretive ambiguity. Legal theory and institutional safeguards offer a promising foundation, as we show here. But further work is needed for stable, transparent rule-based alignment.

Conclusion

This paper identifies interpretive ambiguity as a central obstacle to aligning AI systems with natural language rules. Unlike legal systems, which explicitly confront ambiguity in both rule formulation and application, current rule-based alignment pipelines lack mechanisms to address either.

We propose a computational framework that targets both gaps, leveraging lessons from the U.S. legal system. First, we implement interpretive strategy prompts inspired by legal canons, offering a lightweight mechanism to constrain model discretion during rule application–an analogy to principles and canons of statutory interpretation used to constrain judicial discretion. Second, we implement a rule refinement pipeline that iteratively improves consistency by constructing less ambiguous rules–mirroring administrative agency rulemaking or iterative legislative action. Our empirical results show that both types of interventions provide initial promise in constraining interpretive variance.

Recall that one of the constitutional provisions requires choosing the response that is “less risky for humanity in the long run.” As noted earlier, Anthropic’s Opus 4 system tried to report users to the authorities for “egregiously immoral” behaviors—arguably a reasonable interpretation of the aforementioned constitutional rule. Well-crafted rules would consider trade-offs with personal autonomy that such a rule might create. Through better use of interpretive controls and rule refinement mechanisms, the risks of such misaligned behaviors can potentially be reduced. We hope that future work will build on these insights to improve rule-based AI alignment.
