Drug Use Patterns and Characteristics by Drug Type Among Drug Abusers: Findings From the Survey of Drug Abusers in Korea 2021
Authors: Hae-ryoung Chun, Na-rae Lee, Hyeon Woo Yim, Hyunsuk Jeong, Younghoon Chon, Seung-Yup Lee, Hae Kook Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough substance use disorders are increasingly recognized in the Republic of Korea (South Korea), empirical data on the prevalence and characteristics of drug use among treatment-seeking populations remain limited. This study examined the rate and temporal patterns of major psychoactive substance use—specifically sedatives/hypnotics/tranquilizers (sedatives), amphetamines, and marijuana/cannabis (cannabis)—across three timeframes and explored the associated sociodemographic and clinical factors.MethodsData were obtained from 540 adults with a history of substance use, recruited from medical and rehabilitation institutions as part of the 2021 Survey of Drug Abusers in Korea. Sociodemographic and clinical factors—including Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th edition (DSM-5)–based substance use disorder (SUD) diagnosis, self-recognition of addiction, depression, anxiety, and Addiction Severity Index (ASI) scores—were assessed. Group comparisons were conducted using χ2 and t-tests. Multivariable logistic regression identified factors associated with sedative, amphetamine, and cannabis use across past 30-day, past-year, and lifetime periods. Multiple linear regression examined associations between each substance and ASI composite domains. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.ResultsAmong the 540 individuals with a history of substance use, the lifetime rate of use was highest for amphetamines (71.9%), followed by cannabis (52.8%) and sedatives (38.1%). Sedative use remained relatively consistent across timeframes (30-day: 21.9%; 1-year: 28.9%; lifetime: 38.1%), suggesting patterns of chronic or concurrent use. Across all three substance groups, participants exhibited poorer mental health, including elevated levels of depression, anxiety, or psychiatric severity. Amphetamine and sedative users were more likely to be female, older, and of lower socioeconomic and educational status, with greater impairment in psychiatric, family, and social functioning, as well as higher self-recognition of addiction. In contrast, cannabis users were predominantly younger males with higher socioeconomic status but elevated anxiety levels. Institutional affiliation also differed by substance type: cannabis users were more frequently associated with rehabilitation centers, whereas amphetamine users were more often treated in medical institutions.ConclusionFindings highlight the coexistence of shared psychosocial vulnerabilities and substance-specific risk profiles in Korea. Integrated, multidimensional interventions—linking psychiatric care, social support, and economic protection—are needed for amphetamine and sedative users. For younger cannabis users, early intervention and relapse prevention are essential. Strengthening coordination between medical and rehabilitation systems under Korea’s Judicial–Treatment–Rehabilitation Linkage Model may reduce stigma and enhance recovery-oriented care for individuals with SUD.Keywords: Substance-Related Disorders, Drug Users, Amphetamines, Cannabis, Sedatives, Korea

INTRODUCTION

The prevalence of illicit drug use and non-medical use of prescription drugs has significantly increased worldwide, with the number of drug users estimated to have reached 296 million in 2021—a 23.3% increase from 2011.1 In Canada and several U.S. states, the legalization of cannabis has reduced public awareness of its potential risks, contributing to its emergence as the most commonly used substance.2 Republic of Korea (South Korea) is also experiencing a significant rise in drug-related offenses. In 2022, the total number of drug offenders reached 18,395, marking a 13.9% increase from the previous year and remaining above 16,000 for four consecutive years.3 The growing use of non-face-to-face distribution methods, such as international mail and social networking services, has further accelerated drug trafficking through channels that present relatively low detection risk.3

Illicit drug use contributes not only to social issues but also to serious physical and mental health problems. For instance, methamphetamine, a representative stimulant, is associated with both psychiatric and medical comorbidities.4 Cannabis use may induce panic, anxiety, depression, or even psychosis.5 These substances can also lead to dependency or withdrawal symptoms, exacerbating users’ overall health conditions. Prolonged drug use has significant adverse effects on health and well-being, ranging from acute toxicity to chronic conditions.6 Some consequences, such as overdose and hepatitis C, are directly attributable to drug use, while others, including psychiatric disorders and cancers, are associated with increased risk.7,8 SUD also contributes to interpersonal problems and may lead to neglect of familial and occupational responsibilities.6

Recent international studies have begun to explore the differences in user characteristics among users of sedatives, stimulants, and cannabis. These studies highlighted the importance of substance-specific approaches for prevention and intervention. For example, research has found that co-users of cannabis and sedatives tend to have lower educational attainment and higher rates of alcohol and tobacco use.9 Other studies report that users of cannabis tend to be younger and unmarried, while stimulant users often have stronger cravings and a higher prevalence of psychiatric comorbidities.10 However, there is still a lack of research directly comparing the characteristics and contextual factors associated with different types of substances among drug users in Korea. Previous Korean studies generally involved much smaller sample sizes, which limited the generalizability of their findings.10 Few studies have examined variations in user characteristics across key drug types, such as sedatives, amphetamines, and cannabis, which are known for their differing patterns of use and accessibility.

Therefore, this study analyzed nationally collected data on substance use in Korea to investigate how users of sedatives, amphetamines, and cannabis differ in terms of sociodemographic attributes, mental health status, and usage persistence across multiple timeframes. These findings are expected to inform differentiated strategies for addiction intervention and policy development.

The aim of this study was to examine the rates and temporal patterns of sedative, amphetamine, and cannabis use (past 30 days, past 1 year, and lifetime) among adults with a history of substance use or treatment for SUD, and to identify sociodemographic and clinical factors associated with each substance type.

METHODS

Data sources and participants

This study used cross-sectional data from the 2021 Survey of Drug Abusers in Korea, collected between September 2021 and May 2022. A total of 540 adults completed both self-administered questionnaires and assessments using the Addiction Severity Index (ASI). The ASI was administered by trained interviewers through a semi-structured format to evaluate problematic use of substances consumed illegally or misuse of prescription drugs.

Participants were recruited from medical institutions (n = 332) and rehabilitation centers affiliated with the Korean Association Against Drug Abuse (n = 208). Among those recruited from medical institutions, 199 were voluntary patients treated at general psychiatric hospitals or referred through probation offices, and 133 were individuals referred to treatment-protection facilities via the probation system. Of the rehabilitation center participants, 114 were mandated to participate under an educational or conditional-prosecution order, and 94 were voluntary registrants who joined community-based rehabilitation programs.

Most participants were individuals diagnosed with or treated for SUD. However, some participants recruited from rehabilitation centers may not have received a formal medical diagnosis, as inclusion in these programs was based on participation in educational or rehabilitation activities rather than physician evaluation. Measures

Rate of drug use

In this study, the ASI was used to assess experience with each type of substance and calculated as composite scores (these areas are medical, employment, drug use, legal, family/social relationships, and psychiatric problems) to provide a quantitative evaluation of the severity of drug use. Participants were surveyed regarding their use of various psychoactive substances across three timeframes: the past 30 days, the past 12 months, and their lifetime. All substance categories and classifications used in this study followed the predefined framework of the ASI. The substances included alcohol, opioids/analgesics (e.g., fentanyl and tramadol), sedatives and hypnotics (e.g., zolpidem, ketamine, and anxiolytics), amphetamine-like psychostimulants (including methamphetamine and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder [ADHD] medications), cannabis (natural, synthetic, and liquid forms), hallucinogens (e.g., ecstasy), and other substances such as inhalants, herbal intoxicants, and over-the-counter drugs of abuse.

In this study, individuals were classified as multiple substance users if they reported using two or more substances, including alcohol, on the same day, in accordance with the ASI guidelines. Alcohol use was included based on the reported frequency and regularity of use, regardless of whether the individual had experienced intoxication, as the ASI does not differentiate between these categories.

The ASI distinguishes barbiturates from sedatives/hypnotics/tranquilizers but does not include a separate category for anesthetics. Historically, barbiturates were considered a major public health and regulatory concern and were classified as an independent category under the Controlled Substances Act in the United States.11 This historical context explains why barbiturates remain a distinct category within assessment tools such as the ASI. Accordingly, ketamine and propofol were classified under sedatives/hypnotics/tranquilizers based on their pharmacological mechanisms—N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor antagonism for ketamine12,13,14 and γ-aminobutyric acid receptor modulation for propofol15,16—both of which induce sedative-hypnotic effects. Clinically, these agents are used as sedative-hypnotic anesthetics for the induction and maintenance of sedation. Because the ASI lacks a dedicated “anesthetic” category, this classification was made in accordance with the instrument’s predefined framework, and these substances were included as subcategories under sedatives/hypnotics/tranquilizers. Regarding ADHD medications, methylphenidate was grouped under amphetamines for analysis because of its strong pharmacological similarity to amphetamine-type stimulants (ATSs). Both methylphenidate and amphetamines inhibit the reuptake of dopamine and norepinephrine, thereby enhancing psychomotor activation.17 In the WHO Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification system (N06BA), methylphenidate is listed alongside amphetamines and methamphetamines as a centrally acting sympathomimetic agent. Under the Korean Narcotics Control Act, it is also designated as a psychotropic substance categorized with amphetamine analogs. In Korea, short-acting methylphenidate is not approved for use in adults under current regulations, and its use is therefore considered equivalent to the use of illicit stimulants. Empirical evidence indicates that the nonmedical use and misuse of methylphenidate have been increasing in Korea, particularly among individuals with SUDs and students.18 Therefore, this classification reflects both pharmacological similarity and real-world patterns of misuse observed in the Korean context.

In contrast, Concerta (a long-acting methylphenidate formulation) was excluded because it is primarily used for therapeutic purposes under medical supervision and carries a substantially lower risk of misuse compared with short-acting formulations. Previous studies have also shown that extended-release formulations are associated with a lower abuse potential.19,20

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics

Demographic variables included age, sex, educational attainment, marital status, monthly income, and living arrangements (cohabitation). Clinical indicators included self-recognition of addiction, 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) and 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder scale (GAD-7) scores for depression and anxiety, and ASI scores across six domains: physical, economic, legal, drug use, interpersonal relationships, and psychiatric status. DSM-5 criteria for substance use disorders21 was used to assess whether an individual’s substance use met the threshold for clinically significant problematic use.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics. The rate of substance use by drug type is presented as a frequency and percentage across the three timeframes. The χ2 and independent t-tests were used to examine differences in characteristics between users and non-users of sedatives, amphetamines, and cannabis. The results were stratified by use in the past 30 days, years, and lifetime. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify sociodemographic, clinical, and substance use–related factors associated with the use of sedatives, amphetamines, and cannabis. Separate models were estimated for each substance type across three time frames: past 30 days, past year, and lifetime. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for each variable included in the model. We conducted multiple linear regression analyses to examine the association between lifetime substance use and six ASI composite scores. The models were adjusted for lifetime use of other substances, sex, age, cohabitation, marital status, income level, economic support, education level, self-recognition of addiction, type of treatment institution, depression (PHQ-9), anxiety (GAD-7), and the presence of DSM-5 SUD. All predictors were entered simultaneously, and regression coefficients (β) with 95% CI were estimated. All analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA), and statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Ethics statement

This study received ethical review and approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the National Center for Mental Health (IRB No. BNH-2021-12), Seoul, Korea. Informed consent was submitted by all subjects when they were enrolled.

RESULTS

Amphetamines, including methamphetamine and prescription stimulants such as Adderall, showed the highest overall prevalence (71.9% lifetime, 34.6% past year, 10.6% past 30 days). Cannabis ranked second (52.8% lifetime, 23.5% past year, 4.3% past 30 days), followed by sedatives/hypnotics/tranquilizers (38.1%, 28.9%, 21.9%, respectively). Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics

Among the total sample (N = 540), 77.4% were male, and the mean age was 38.9 ± 12.6 years. Most participants were living with others (68.9%) but were unmarried (83.5%), and 52.2% reported a monthly income of less than 0.5 million KRW. Additionally, 54.3% expressed a need for financial support, indicating substantial economic instability. Educational attainment was generally low, with 71.3% having completed middle or high school education. A total of 59.1% self-identified as being addicted, and 60.7% met the DSM-5 criteria for SUD. Mental health burdens were also high, with 44.1% screening positive for depression (PHQ-9 ≥ 10) and 52.8% for anxiety (GAD-7 ≥ 5). Scores on the ASI showed the highest severity in the economic (mean = 0.62) and psychiatric (mean = 0.61) domains.

Differences by substance type

Significant differences were observed across drug types. Sedative use was more common among females (49.2%, P = 0.004), younger participants, and those with low income and educational attainment. Sedative users also had significantly higher rates of depression and anxiety across all timeframes (depression: 46.6% in lifetime users, P < 0.001; anxiety: 43.9%, P = 0.004). Amphetamine users tended to be older, with the highest lifetime use observed among individuals in their 50s (95.1%, P < 0.001), and had lower educational levels, with 83.6% having completed middle school or less (P < 0.001). They also showed significantly elevated legal problems, higher DSM-5 SUD scores, and greater psychiatric severity, including a lifetime depression rate of 79.4% (P = 0.001) and anxiety rate of 76.8% (P = 0.006). In contrast, cannabis users were generally younger (69.9% in their 20s, P < 0.001), more educated, and more likely to have used rehabilitation services (64.4% vs. 45.5%, P < 0.001). However, significantly higher rates of depression and anxiety among cannabis users were observed only in the past 30-day group (6.7% and 7.0%, respectively, both P = 0.001), and not in past-year or lifetime users.

Participants classified as SUD based on DSM-5 SUD criteria (score ≥ 2) were significantly more likely to have used all three substances: sedatives (44.5% lifetime), amphetamines (75.0%), and cannabis (56.7%) (all P < 0.001). Institutional affiliation also differed: sedative and amphetamine users were more frequently associated with medical institutions, whereas cannabis users were more often linked to rehabilitation centers (P = 0.006 for past-year cannabis users; P < 0.001 for lifetime use).

ASI differences by substance type

Table 3 presents the mean composite scores of the ASI domains according to sedative, amphetamine, and cannabis use across three timeframes (past 30 days, past year, and lifetime). Both sedative and amphetamine users showed significantly higher ASI scores in the drug and psychiatric domains across all timeframes (P < 0.05). In addition, sedative users demonstrated consistently higher scores in the relationship domain at all timepoints (P < 0.05). In contrast, cannabis users exhibited significant differences only in the psychiatric and relationship domains, and these associations were limited to the past 30 days and past year. No ASI domains were significantly associated with cannabis use across all timeframes. Detailed results corresponding to Table 3 are provided in Supplementary Tables 2, 3, 4.

DISCUSSION

This study examined patterns of sedative (prescription drug misuse), amphetamine, and cannabis use among individuals in Korea with a history of substance use or treatment for SUD, focusing on temporal patterns of use and associated sociodemographic and clinical characteristics. Overall, the findings demonstrate the coexistence of shared psychosocial vulnerabilities across substance groups and substance-specific profiles that have important implications for tailored intervention strategies.

Amphetamines, cannabis, and sedatives are among the most frequently misused substances in Korea. In this study, substantial discrepancies were observed between lifetime and recent (past-year or past-30-day) use of amphetamines and cannabis, whereas sedative use remained relatively consistent across all timeframes. This pattern suggests that sedative misuse tends to be chronic or recurrent, reflecting the difficulty of cessation and the possibility of substitution from illicit stimulants or cannabis to prescription tranquilizers during recovery. Previous research has reported that benzodiazepines are often misused to alleviate withdrawal symptoms, manage anxiety, or serve as substitutes when illicit drugs are unavailable,22,23 and that sedative-hypnotic misuse and benzodiazepine-related overdoses have increased globally.24,25 These findings, together with the higher ASI drug use severity observed among sedative users across lifetime, past-year, and past 30-day use categories, suggest that sedative misuse may be part of a broader pattern of polysubstance use rather than a distinct clinical subgroup. Among individuals with severe addiction or co-occurring mental health problems, sedatives are likely to be used as adjunctive means to alleviate withdrawal symptoms, anxiety, or sleep disturbances.26,27

Across all groups, participants commonly exhibited poor mental health, as indicated by higher levels of depression, anxiety, or ASI psychiatric severity. Participants who used sedatives within the past 30 days exhibited higher depressive symptoms, whereas those who used amphetamines showed greater impairment in the ASI psychiatric domain. Cannabis users also demonstrated poorer psychiatric functioning in both lifetime and past-year analyses, and those who had used cannabis in the past 30 days reported higher anxiety levels. Given that ASI composite scores reflect the severity of problems during the past 30 days, the persistence of poor psychiatric status even among lifetime or past-year cannabis users suggests sustained mental health difficulties, possibly related to ongoing or residual effects of substance use. Although concurrent use of other substances cannot be excluded, these findings indicate that a history of substance use itself may be associated with increased psychiatric vulnerability.

These results highlight a shared psychosocial vulnerability among individuals with SUD, which may underlie the elevated risk of polysubstance use.28,29 Such commonalities emphasize the need for integrated screening and intervention strategies that address core psychiatric comorbidities alongside substance use treatment.30 Recent evidence also shows that the use of benzodiazepines and opioid medications is strongly associated with ongoing psychiatric or psychological follow-up and with ADHD symptomatology.31 The higher prevalence of ADHD among benzodiazepine or opioid users further supports the need for coordinated, psychiatry-linked treatment models that integrate mental health management into rehabilitation and recovery for all individuals with SUD.

In this study, cannabis users tended to be younger, more highly educated, predominantly male, and exhibited relatively less socioeconomic disadvantage, and they were more likely to be affiliated with rehabilitation institutions than with medical facilities. Despite its illegal status, cannabis use in Korea has been reported to occur more frequently among individuals with overseas exposure or experimental and recreational use patterns.10 However, previous studies have suggested that early exposure to cannabis may increase the risk of progression to polysubstance use, underscoring the importance of early identification and intervention. In this context, educational- or rehabilitation-focused interventions alone may be insufficient for some cannabis users within the Korean treatment system, and selective medical and psychiatric assessment may be warranted, particularly for individuals with identified mental health vulnerabilities.

Amphetamines are known to have a higher addiction potential than cannabis, and consistent with this, amphetamine users in the present study were more likely to acknowledge being addicted. Previous Korean studies have also reported higher rates of psychiatric history among stimulant users compared with cannabis users,10 and preexisting psychiatric disorders have been identified as a risk factor for developing SUD.32 In line with these findings, amphetamine users in this study exhibited greater dependence severity, psychiatric burden, and socioeconomic instability, indicating a need for more intensive and continuous medical management.

In this study, lifetime sedative users and recent amphetamine users exhibited significantly impaired family and social functioning, along with higher proportions of women and older adults. Long-term benzodiazepine use has been associated with aggression, poor impulse control, and emotional instability, which can exacerbate interpersonal conflicts.33,34 Female users of ATSs face systemic barriers to accessing treatment despite their high prevalence,35 and women living in economically deprived areas are more frequently prescribed sedatives such as diazepam.36 Consistent with previous research, low income and lack of social support are strongly associated with greater addiction severity, particularly among users of central nervous system depressants and stimulants.37 These findings highlight the need for multidimensional and gender-sensitive approaches that integrate clinical treatment with social and economic support to improve outcomes in high-risk groups.

In this study, amphetamine and cannabis users showed higher ASI legal status scores than sedative users, reflecting greater exposure to legal issues or perceived legal burdens rather than direct criminal behavior. In Korea, where both substances are illegal, such legal and social pressures may exacerbate stigma and hinder treatment engagement and recovery. Although community-based mental health infrastructure—including rehabilitation centers and suicide prevention services—has expanded, early and integrated intervention systems for individuals with substance use problems remain limited.38

In response, Korea has implemented a nationwide Judicial–Treatment–Rehabilitation Linkage Model, a government-led initiative providing personalized treatment and social rehabilitation programs for individuals with substance use offenses under conditional non-prosecution or probation. This program facilitates referral to addiction rehabilitation centers before release from correctional facilities or the end of probation, representing an important step toward integrated recovery support. However, the long-term success of this model will depend on strengthening coordination between rehabilitation facilities and medical institutions. Establishing a stepwise, integrated management system—from initial screening and treatment to rehabilitation—and forming multidisciplinary committees composed of rehabilitation professionals, psychiatrists, and clinical experts could ensure individualized, evidence-based interventions tailored to patient needs.

This study has several limitations. First, due to its cross-sectional design, causal relationships between the variables cannot be established. Second, the reliance on self-reported data introduces potential recall and social desirability biases. In particular, given the legal restrictions and social stigma surrounding substance use in Korea, participants may have under-reported their drug use behaviors or related symptoms, even though they were recruited from treatment and rehabilitation settings. Future research should incorporate longitudinal designs and objective biological measures to validate self-reports and capture changes in substance use behaviors over time. Third, participants were primarily recruited from treatment and rehabilitation settings, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of drug users in Korea. Future studies that include community-based or general population samples are needed to enhance external validity. Fourth, substance classification was based on the ASI framework, and interpretation of substance-specific results should take into account that its categories do not necessarily correspond to legal or clinical diagnostic classifications.

Despite these limitations, this study had several strengths. It included a relatively large clinical sample of individuals who use illicit substances in Korea, which is significant given the difficulties in recruiting this population due to stigma, legal risks, and limited accessibility. The large sample size improves the generalizability and clinical relevance of our findings. To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the most comprehensive studies in Korea to examine the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of substance users.

This study identified distinct sociodemographic and clinical characteristics across sedative, amphetamine, and cannabis users in Korea, revealing both shared psychosocial vulnerabilities and drug-specific profiles. Amphetamine and sedative users showed greater psychiatric burden, socioeconomic instability, and addiction awareness, indicating the need for long-term, integrated care combining psychiatric treatment, social support, and economic protection. In contrast, younger cannabis users—often managed through education-only programs—require early psychiatric and preventive interventions to reduce relapse and transition to stimulant or polysubstance use. Strengthening coordination between medical and rehabilitation systems is essential, particularly under Korea’s Judicial–Treatment–Rehabilitation Linkage Model, to ensure continuity of care and reduce stigma. Developing multidisciplinary, substance-tailored strategies will be critical to improving treatment accessibility and promoting recovery-oriented addiction care in Korea.
