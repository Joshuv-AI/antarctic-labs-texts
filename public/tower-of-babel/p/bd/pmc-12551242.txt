Dietary mineral intake was correlated with serum HDL-C in patients with type 2 diabetes
Authors: Wenmin Li, Yingying Shi, Deyi Xu, Haofan Yang, Wenhao Zheng, Liang Wang, Lin Xu, Tao Lei, Jun Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to examine the association between dietary mineral intake and serum lipid profiles in patients with type 2 diabetes.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, daily mineral intake was assessed using a validated dietary questionnaire administered to 149 participants. Daily energy and minerals intakes were calculated using 2002 China Food Composition Database, and reference values were based on the Dietary Reference Intakes (DRIs) from the 2013 Chinese Dietary Guidelines. Partial correlation and multivariable linear regression analysis were performed to examine associations between daily mineral intake and serum lipid parameters.ResultsCompared with the DRIs for Chinese residents, daily intake of calcium, zinc, potassium, and dietary fiber was significantly lower in both men and women (all P < 0.001), whereas sodium, iron, and iodine intake were higher. Partial correlation analysis indicated that daily intake of calcium, iron, zinc and selenium was positively associated with serum high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) (P < 0.05), while dietary iodine intake was negatively associated with HDL-C (r= -0.181, P = 0.040). Multivariable linear regression analysis showed that dietary intakes of calcium, iron, iodine, zinc, and selenium were significantly associated with HDL-C after adjusting for covariates (all P < 0.05). No significant associations were observed between dietary mineral intake and total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, or triglyceride.ConclusionsPatients with type 2 diabetes had largely suboptimal dietary mineral intake. Higher intakes of dietary calcium, iron, zinc, and selenium were positively associated with serum HDL-C, suggesting that maintaining mineral balance may benefit lipid homeostasis in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-025-01163-6.

Background

Diabetes has emerged as a global epidemic, with recent report indicating a prevalence of 13.7% in China [1]. Numerous studies have shown that dietary interventions can significantly reduce the incidence of diabetes and all-cause mortality [2, 3]. Among patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), diet quality has been strongly linked to metabolic disorders such as hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia, and excessive weight gain [4, 5]. As a result, lifestyle interventions that incorporate both diet and exercise have become key strategies in diabetes management [6]. Despite this, deficiencies in dietary mineral intake remain prevalent worldwide [7, 8]. For example, a study in Italy found that only about 20% older adults with T2DM met the recommended dietary intakes for minerals such as calcium, magnesium, and selenium [7]. However, few studies have examined dietary mineral intake status among diabetic patients in China.

Recently studies have indicated that mineral deficiency was associated with dyslipidemia [9–14]. Specifically, serum zinc concentration has been positively associated with serum levels of triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), and low - density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), while negatively associated with high - density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) [15–18]. Similarly, serum ferritin concentrations have been linked to dyslipidemia; however, the relationship between dietary iron intake and lipid profiles remains controversial [19, 20]. Some studies have suggested that iron supplementation has no significant effect on plasma HDL-C level [20–22], while others have reported a positive association with triglycerides [19, 23, 24]. In areas with excessive iodine intake, dietary iodine levels have been observed to reduce serum HDL-C levels [25, 26]. However, data on the association between dietary mineral intake and lipid profiles in patients with T2DM in China, especially in coastal cities such as Shanghai, remain limited. This cross-sectional study aimed to examine the association between daily mineral intake and lipid parameters in patients with type 2 diabetes.

Methods

Study design and patients

Patients aged 20 years and older were recruited from the Department of Endocrinology and Metabolism at Putuo Hospital, Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, between February 1 and April 25, 2024. T2DM was diagnosed according to the 1999 World Health Organization (WHO) criteria [27]. Patients were excluded if they had: (1) severe hepatic or renal insufficiency; (2) other forms of diabetes, such as type 1 diabetes mellitus or gestational diabetes; (3) malignancy; (4) cognitive impairment or psychiatric disorders; or (5) conditions preventing completion of questionnaires. A total of 149 patients with T2DM who provided complete clinical and dietary data were included in this cross-sectional study. Informed written consent was obtained from all participants. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Putuo Hospital, Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Approval No. PTEC-A-2024-40(S)-1) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Questionnaires and evaluation of mineral intake

The participants’ intake of 35 major food groups and beverages over the preceding 72 h was assessed using a previously validated food frequency questionnaire (see Supplementary Table 1) [28]. Daily energy and minerals intakes were calculated using the 2002 food composition database [29]. The 2013 Chinese Dietary Guidelines (CDGs) were used to evaluate whether the participants’ daily mineral intakes met the recommended levels outlined in the Dietary Reference Intakes (DRIs) [30].

Anthropometric and biochemical measurements

Height and weight were measured with patients barefoot and wearing light clothing. Body mass index (BMI, kg/m2) was calculated by dividing weight in kilograms by the square of height in meters [31]. Venous blood samples were collected after an overnight fast of at least 10 h. Glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) was measured using high-performance liquid chromatography (HLC-732G11; Tosoh, Tokyo, Japan). Lipid profiles (including TG, TC, LDL-C, and HDL-C), hepatic function markers (alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, and γ-Glutamyl transpeptidase), renal function markers (blood urea nitrogen, serum creatinine, and uric acid), and blood glucose were analyzed using an automated biochemical analyzer (Siemens Atellica CH 930, New Jersey, USA).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables were presented as number (percentage). Differences in means were examined using the Student’s t test (Tables 1 and 2), while differences in proportions were analyzed using the Chi-square test. Differences between patients’ daily mineral intakes and DRIs were analyzed using Medcalc statistical software (version 20.0). Partial correlation analysis was performed to examine the association between dietary mineral intake and serum lipid profiles, adjusting for covariates including gender, age, duration of diabetes, smoking status, alcohol consumption, BMI, dietary fiber intake, daily energy intake, metformin usage, insulin therapy, and lipid-lowering treatment. Multivariable linear regression models were used to examine the association between HDL-C and daily intake levels of each mineral, adjusting for gender, age, duration of diabetes, smoking status, alcohol consumption, BMI, dietary fiber intake, and daily energy intake (Model 2). Model 3 further adjusted for HbA1c, proportion of energy from fat, metformin usage, insulin therapy, and lipid-lowing treatment addition to the covariates in Model 2. All the analyses were performed using IBM SPSS statistics for Windows, version 26.0 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.).

Results

Clinical characteristics of the patients

Table 1 presents the clinical characteristics of the 149 patients included in the study, comprising 79 males and 70 females. The mean age of participants was 68 years. The duration of diabetes was shorter in men compared to women, while glycosylated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) and serum creatinine levels were significantly higher in men (P < 0.05). Additionally, women had higher serum LDL-C levels than men. Other parameters, including BMI, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), γ-glutamyl transpeptidase, fasting blood glucose, postprandial blood glucose, TC, TG, HDL-C, serum urea nitrogen, uric acid, glomerular filtration rate, proportion of lipid lowering treatment, insulin therapy, and metformin usage, were comparable between men and women.

Status of dietary mineral intake in the participants

As shown in Table 2, the total energy intake was significantly higher in men than in women (P < 0.05), whereas the proportions of energy derived from carbohydrates, fats, and proteins was similar between men and women. No significant sex differences were observed in dietary mineral intake. Compared to the DRIs, however, both men and women consumed significantly higher amounts of sodium, iron, and iodine (all P < 0.05). Specifically, sodium intake exceeded the DRI by 65.4% (2315 mg/d vs. 1400 mg/d), iron by 30.8% (15.7 mg/d vs. 12 mg/d), and iodine by 79.2% (215 mg/d vs. 120 mg/d). In contrast, intake of calcium, potassium, zinc, and dietary fiber was significantly substantially below the DRIs in both groups (all P < 0.05), with calcium lower by 54.9% (451 mg/d vs. 1000 mg/d), potassium by 11.35% (1773 mg/d vs. 2000 mg/d), zinc by 24.88% (9.39 mg/d vs. 12.5 mg/d), and dietary fiber by 53.5% (9.3 mg/d vs. 20 mg/d). Furthermore, daily selenium intake was significantly lower than the DRI in men (P < 0.05).

Correlation between mineral intake and lipid profiles

As shown in Table 3, partial correlation analysis showed that daily intakes of calcium, iron, iodine, zinc, and selenium were significantly associated with HDL-C levels (all P < 0.05). Specifically, dietary calcium, iron, zinc, and selenium intakes were positively correlated with HDL-C (P < 0.05), while iodine intake was negatively correlated with HDL-C (r = -181, P = 0.040). No significant associations were found between daily mineral intake and TC, TG, or LDL-C levels. Furthermore, HDL-C was not significantly correlated with sodium or potassium intake.

Discussion

This study provides important insights into the relationship between dietary mineral intake and lipid profiles in patients with T2DM. First, compared with the DRIs for Chinese residents, both men and women had substantially higher intakes of sodium, iron, and iodine, but markedly lower intakes of calcium, potassium, zinc, and dietary fiber. Second, daily intake of calcium, iron, iodine, zinc, and selenium were significantly associated with serum HDL-C levels.

The observed excessive sodium and reduced potassium intake reflect dietary shifts in rapidly urbanizing regions of China, where patterns have shifted toward higher consumption of fats, proteins, and sodium, alongside lower potassium intake [11, 32]. On the other hand, studies in Europe have shown that only a small proportion of individuals adhering to the Mediterranean diet meet the recommended intake for calcium and selenium [7]. In our study, the intake of calcium and selenium was also insufficient, reinforcing evidence of the global prevalence of inadequate mineral intake. While China’s longstanding policy of universal salt iodization has successfully achieved iodine sufficiency [33, 34], excessive iodine intake has been observed in coastal regions [35], a pattern also reflected in our findings.

The association between mineral intake and lipid metabolism has been examined in previous studies. Firstly, Zinc deficiency has been associated with dyslipidemia [14] and clinical trials have shown that zinc supplementation can significantly reduce serum TG, TC, and LDL-C levels while increasing HDL-C level in patients with T2DM [15, 16, 36]. Consistent with these findings, our study observed a positive association between dietary zinc intake and HDL-C after adjusting for potential confounders. Notably, zinc’s role in lipid homeostasis may be further enhanced when combined with selenium supplementation, as suggested by other studies [17]. Secondly, the association between iron intake and lipid profiles remain controversial. While some studies have suggested that higher dietary iron intake is associated with adverse lipid profiles (e.g., elevated TG and reduced HDL-C), others have found no significant impact of iron supplementation on HDL-C levels [19–23]. Our study observed a positive association between iron intake and HDL-C, highlighting the need for further research to confirm this association. Thirdly, a meta-analysis showed that higher dietary calcium intake was associated with lower blood TG and LDL-C, as well as higher HDL-C concentrations [37]. Similarly, we also observed a positive relationship between dietary calcium intake and HDL-C. Finally, the role of iodine in lipid metabolism is complex and findings across studies have been inconsistent. Research from Korea suggests that dietary iodine is inversely associated with TG and positively correlated with HDL-C in postmenopausal women [38]. Conversely, excessive iodine intake has been linked to reductions in HDL-C and apoA1 levels in certain populations [39]. In our study, excessive iodine intake was negatively associated with HDL-C, supporting previous evidence of an inverted U-shaped relationship between iodine exposure and lipid abnormalities [39, 40]. Further studies are needed to better understand the underlying mechanisms of these associations.

A key strength of this study lies in its detailed assessment of dietary mineral intake and comprehensive analysis of its associations with serum lipid profiles in patients with T2DM. Utilizing a validated food frequency questionnaire ensured reliable dietary data, while multivariable linear regression models enabled adjustment for potential confounders. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between dietary mineral intake and lipid profiles; thus, multi-center longitudinal studies are warranted to validate the causal association between dietary mineral balance and lipid homeostasis. Second, the reliance on self-reported dietary intake data may introduce recall bias, although efforts were made to minimize this by selecting appropriate participants and, when necessary, involving supervisors to assist. Third, the relatively small sample size may limit the power to detect associations of mineral intake with TC, TG, and LDL-C. Finally, the single-center setting may restrict the generalizability of the findings to broader populations, especially those outside urban areas or in different regions of China. Larger-scale studies and mechanistic investigations are needed to enhance the applicability of these findings.

Conclusions

This study highlights significant disparities in dietary mineral intake among patients with T2DM in China, revealing clear deviations from the DRIs. Importantly, this study demonstrated significant associations between dietary mineral intake and serum HDL-C, suggesting that these minerals may play a crucial role in lipid homeostasis.
