Left pulmonary vein anatomical variation in Turner syndrome. Benefits of three‐dimensional visualization for surgical planning
Authors: Gabrielle Drevet, Valentin Soldea, François Tronc
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 41 year‐old female with a medical history of Turner syndrome underwent a chest computed tomography (CT) scan which revealed a varicose left pulmonary vein and an endobronchial tumor of the left lower lobe. As venous drainage of each lobe seemed to be respected, surgical resection was considered. During surgical exploration, the absence of fissure and a unique venous trunk was observed. Surgical resection was aborted as only pneumonectomy was possible in this context. Endobronchial resection was performed. To better understand this particular anatomy, a three‐dimensional (3D) reconstruction was performed a posteriori. This technique is already commonly used in the preoperative planning of pulmonary segmentectomy. Here, we have shown its interest in a lung malformative context.

CASE

A 41 year‐old female with a medical history of Turner syndrome underwent a chest computed tomography (CT) scan which showed a varicose left pulmonary vein (Figure 1a–c) and an endobronchial tumor of the left lower lobe (Figure 1d). Bronchoscopy allowed the diagnosis of a carcinoid tumor. Angiography revealed a varicose left upper lobar vein with an abnormal course (Figure 1e). As the venous drainage of each lobe appeared to be respected, surgical exploration was undertaken. Absence of fissure was noted (Figure 2a) and mediastinal exploration showed a unique pulmonary vein. The dissection of the “fissure” revealed a varicose vein crossing the fissure (Figure 2b) and draining into a unique venous trunk. Only pneumonectomy was then possible. Surgery was therefore aborted and endobronchial resection with cryotherapy was performed. The final pathological analyses confirmed a typical carcinoid. The patient is free from recurrence 10 months after the procedure. Reconstruction (Visible Patient, Strasbourg, France) was performed after surgery to better understand this unusual anatomy. This confirmed an anomaly in the connection of the superior pulmonary vein, which runs almost entirely through the lung and drains into a unique “inferior” vein (Figure 2c,d).

COMMENT

The presence of vascular anomalies in patients with Turner syndrome is known, particularly partial anomalous pulmonary venous return. 1 However, there are less than 100 cases reported in the literature. Most frequently, this syndrome is associated with aortic coarctation and dissection. 2 Congenital anomalies of pulmonary veins are underestimated mainly because they are not systematically investigated. In fact, in our patient the anomaly was discovered incidentally and was initially diagnosed as an arteriovenous malformation, highlighting the difficulty to correctly assess lung vascular congenital anomalies. Common venous trunk prohibited conventional lobectomy. Pneumonectomy was deemed a too heavy surgery for a carcinoid tumor. Direct anastomosis of the tortuous vein to the left atrium was not considered because of a high risk of postoperative complications. To better understand this particular anatomy, a three‐dimensional (3D) reconstruction was performed a posteriori. This technique is already commonly used in the preoperative planning of pulmonary segmentectomy. 3 Here, we have also shown its interest in a lung malformative context.
