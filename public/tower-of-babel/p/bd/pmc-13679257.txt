Central microglia–adenosine-related signaling impairs bone–tendon interface repair via sympathetic ADRB2 activation
Authors: Tingmo Huang, Liyang Wan, Ruixue Du, Liyue Zeng, Wenjie Li, Feifei Yuan, Hongbin Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBone–tendon interface (BTI) injuries pose a major clinical challenge because surgical repair often fails to restore the native enthesis and its structural and mechanical integrity. Although local repair mechanisms have been extensively studied, whether central neuroimmune circuits contribute to BTI healing remains unclear. Here, using a murine rotator cuff injury model, we provide evidence that BTI injury engages a sensory–central–sympathetic regulatory axis that contributes to impaired repair. BTI injury activated sensory afferent signaling and was associated with microglia-mediated neuroinflammation in the hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus (PVN), reduced PVN neuronal activity, and increased central adenosine-related signaling. Chemogenetic activation of PVN microglia suppressed PVN neuronal activity, enhanced sympathetic-associated changes, and impaired BTI healing, whereas microglial inhibition produced the opposite effects. Metabolomic, microdialysis, and pharmacological analyses identified extracellular adenosine as a microglia-associated signaling mediator, likely involving A1R-expressing PVN neurons. Downstream, increased sympathetic signaling at the healing interface was associated with elevated β2-adrenergic receptor (ADRB2) activity and reduced osteogenic and chondrogenic factor expression. Local ADRB2 blockade improved molecular, structural, and histological indices of BTI repair, supporting ADRB2 as a peripheral effector node of this neuroimmune–sympathetic pathway. Together, these findings suggest that central microglia–adenosine-related signaling contributes to BTI repair impairment through sympathetic ADRB2 activation. Introduction

The bone-tendon interface (BTI), or enthesis, is a specialized transitional tissue that links tendon to bone. Its graded architecture—tendon, non-calcified fibrocartilage, calcified fibrocartilage, and bone—disperses mechanical load and maintains joint stability [1, 2]. Injuries to the BTI, including rotator cuff tears, anterior cruciate ligament ruptures, and Achilles tendon injuries, are common in orthopedics. In the United States alone, roughly 4.5 million patients sustain rotator cuff injuries each year, generating healthcare costs exceeding $474 million [3]. Despite surgical advances, BTI repair frequently yields biomechanically inferior fibrovascular scar, and postoperative re-tear rates for rotator cuff repairs can reach 25–75% [4–6]. The region’s intrinsic hypocellularity and poor vascularity constrain regeneration, underscoring the urgent need for new therapies to improve healing quality.

Emerging evidence indicates that the central nervous system can modulate peripheral tissue repair. The hypothalamus is a pivotal neuroendocrine and autonomic hub regulating metabolism, stress responses, and bone homeostasis [7–9]. Within it, the paraventricular nucleus (PVN) projects directly to sympathetic preganglionic neurons, positioning it as a key driver of sympathetic nervous system output [7, 10, 11]. The sympathetic system in turn influences BTI healing, as shown by dynamic changes in local norepinephrine (NE) levels and β2-adrenergic receptor (ADRB2) activity at repair sites [12, 13]. These observations raise the possibility that the PVN serves as an upstream node that modulates sympathetic tone and, thereby, BTI repair outcomes. Sensory neurogenic signals are also increasingly recognized as mediators that convey peripheral tissue damage to central circuits [14–16]. Yet the specific afferent pathways from the BTI to the PVN, and the detailed central mechanisms by which the PVN orchestrates healing, remain poorly defined.

Neuroinflammation—a central immune response involving glial activation and pro-inflammatory cytokine release—is tightly linked to neuronal activity [17–19]. Microglia, the CNS’s resident immune cells, rapidly respond to environmental perturbations and, upon activation, shift toward pro- or anti-inflammatory states, releasing factors that shape synaptic plasticity and neuronal excitability [20–23]. Peripheral insults such as trauma can disrupt the blood-brain barrier and elicit neuroinflammatory responses in discrete brain nuclei, contributing to various diseases [23–25]. Importantly, PVN neuroinflammation can alter peripheral sympathetic tone, affecting disease progression in hypertension and metabolic disorders [26, 27]. Whether central neuroinflammation within the PVN participates in regulating BTI healing, however, has not been explored.

Here, we hypothesize that BTI injury triggers a multi-axis pathway: sensory afferents initiate microglia-mediated PVN neuroinflammation, which modulates PVN neuronal activity through specific signaling mechanisms; this, in turn, elevates peripheral sympathetic tone that impairs BTI healing by suppressing osteochondrogenic signaling via ADRB2. Using chemogenetic, metabolomic, electrophysiological, and pharmacological approaches in a murine rotator cuff injury model, we sought to investigate this neuroimmune–sympathetic regulatory axis. Our findings provide evidence for a previously underappreciated central neuroimmune mechanism that may contribute to musculoskeletal repair outcomes and suggest potential therapeutic targets for improving BTI healing by modulating specific nodes of this integrated pathway.

Results

BTI injury alters neuronal activity in the hypothalamic PVN region

Previous work implicates sympathetic innervation in BTI healing, evidenced by altered levels of neurotransmitters such as norepinephrine and neuropeptide Y at the repair interface [4, 12, 13]. However, the upstream brain nuclei that regulate sympathetic tone remain unclear. We therefore used retrograde tracing with pseudorabies virus (PRV) injected at the supraspinatus tendon insertion to identify central regions involved in post-injury BTI modulation (Fig. 1a). Among several brain regions examined, the PVN—a key autonomic and neuroendocrine hub [7–9]—showed the strongest viral labeling (Fig. 1b-d), suggesting a potential role in BTI repair.

We next assessed neuronal activation patterns across various brain regions following BTI injury using c-Fos immunohistochemistry (Supplementary Fig. S2). Among the regions analyzed, the PVN exhibited marked differences in the number of c-Fos-positive (c-Fos+) cells. The count of c-Fos+ neurons within the PVN showed pronounced variations across different weeks post-injury (wpi). At 4 wpi, a notable reduction in c-Fos+ neurons was observed compared with sham group (Fig. 1e). By 8 wpi, however, the number of c-Fos+ neurons in the PVN did not differ from that in sham animals (Fig. 1f). Western blot analysis further confirmed a decrease in c-Fos protein expression in the PVN after injury, with the most pronounced reduction occurring at 4 weeks (Fig. 1g, h). To directly assess neuronal excitability, we performed in vivo electrophysiological recordings in the PVN. Spike raster plots and local field potential (LFP) traces (Fig. 1i) revealed dynamic alterations in PVN neuronal activity following BTI injury. Multi-unit spike histograms and quantitative firing-rate analyses consistently demonstrated sustained suppression of PVN neuronal activity after injury, with mean firing rates significantly lowered, particularly at 4 wpi (Fig. 1j, k). Spectral analysis further showed time-dependent changes in neuronal oscillations: low-frequency oscillations (30–80 Hz) were significantly decreased at 4 wpi but returned toward baseline by 8 wpi (Fig. 1l, m). Together, these data indicate that BTI injury induces time-dependent alterations in PVN neuronal activity, with the most prominent suppression observed at 4 wpi.

BTI injury triggers microglia-mediated neuroinflammation in the PVN

To investigate the mechanisms underlying PVN neuronal suppression, we extracted RNA from PVN tissues and performed high-throughput RNA sequencing. Principal-component analysis (PCA) of the transcriptomic data showed clear separation between the 4 wpi and sham groups, indicating distinct transcriptional profiles (Fig. 2a). Partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) further confirmed the distinct clustering (Fig. 2b). We identified 461 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) at 4 wpi, of which 326 were up-regulated and 135 were down-regulated (Fig. 2c). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that these DEGs were significantly associated with immune and inflammatory processes, including “immune system process,” “innate immune response,” and “inflammatory response” (Fig. 2d, e). Gene-set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of the entire transcriptome corroborated these findings, showing significant enrichment of GO terms such as “immune system process,” “innate immune response,” and “immune response” in the 4 wpi group compared with the sham group (Fig. 2f). Because central inflammation has been shown to regulate neuronal activity in various physiological and pathological contexts [17–19], these results suggest that neuroinflammation may be a key mediator of the BTI-induced alterations in PVN neuronal activity.

As the resident immune cells of the central nervous system, microglia play a pivotal role in inflammatory responses [20–23]. In Cx3cr1-CreERT2;Rosa26-tdTomato reporter mice, a marked increase in tdTomato-labeled microglia was observed in the PVN after injury, peaking at 4 weeks post-operation and declining to baseline by 8 weeks (Fig. 2g, h). Western blot analysis indicated that the activated microglia predominantly exhibited a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, as evidenced by elevated expression of CD86 and iNOS. This M1 polarization was already detectable at 2 weeks, peaked at 4 weeks, and returned to sham levels by 8 weeks post-surgery. In contrast, the anti-inflammatory M2 markers CD206 and Arg1 remained unchanged throughout the 2-, 4-, and 8-week postoperative periods (Fig. 2i, j). Consistent with these findings, qRT-PCR analysis showed significant up-regulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α) in the PVN, with the most pronounced increase occurring at 4 weeks after injury (Fig. 2k). These findings indicate that BTI injury induces a temporally defined microglia-mediated neuroinflammatory response in the PVN, peaking during the intermediate healing phase.

Notably, the temporal accumulation of microglia in the PVN coincided with the reduction in the neuronal activity marker c-Fos. Correlation analysis revealed a significant negative relationship between the post-injury increase in microglial number and c-Fos expression (Pearson’s r = − 0.89, p < 0.05; n = 6 mice per time point) (Fig. 2l), suggesting that microglial expansion may contribute to the suppression of PVN neuronal activity after BTI injury.

We also examined the spatial and temporal characteristics of microgliosis after rotator-cuff injury by comparing microglial responses in several brain regions: the PVN and central amygdala (CeA)—regions implicated in autonomic regulation—as well as the primary motor cortex (M1) and lateral hypothalamus (LH) as control regions unrelated to autonomic function. Microglial density in the PVN was significantly elevated at 2 and 4 weeks post-surgery compared with the other regions examined (Supplementary Fig. S3a, b). Moreover, PVN microglia displayed more pronounced morphological alterations after injury, including enlarged somata and reduced process complexity, as confirmed by Sholl analysis (Supplementary Fig. S3c-f). These results underscore the region-specific nature of PVN neuroinflammation in the context of BTI repair.

Microglial activation suppresses the excitability of PVN neurons

To determine whether PVN microglial activity functionally influences neuronal excitability, we employed a chemogenetic strategy. Cre-dependent adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors encoding either the excitatory designer receptor hM3D(Gq) (rAAV-MG1.2-EF1a-DIO-hM3D(Gq)-EGFP) or the inhibitory receptor hM4D(Gi) (rAAV-MG1.2-EF1a-DIO-hM4D(Gi)-EGFP), which are DREADDs (Designer Receptors Exclusively Activated by Designer Drugs), were stereotaxically delivered into the PVN of Cx3cr1-CreERT2 mice. Following viral injection, tamoxifen was administered intraperitoneally for five consecutive days to induce Cre-mediated recombination, as previously described [28] (Fig. 3a). EGFP fluorescence was confined to the PVN, confirming localized viral expression (Fig. 3b). Three weeks later, co-localization of EGFP with the microglial marker Iba1 verified successful targeting of microglia (Fig. 3c). Both viral constructs exhibited > 90% specificity for microglial infection within the PVN (Fig. 3d).

To determine whether DREADD manipulation altered the activation state of PVN microglia, we next examined microglial morphology and pro-inflammatory cytokine expression after CNO administration. Chemogenetic activation of PVN microglia with hM3D(Gq) increased microglial soma size and reduced process complexity, whereas chemogenetic inhibition with hM4D(Gi) attenuated these activation-associated morphological changes (Supplementary Fig. S4a, b). Consistently, hM3D(Gq) activation upregulated the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, whereas hM4D(Gi) inhibition reduced their expression (Supplementary Fig. S4c). These findings indicate that DREADD manipulation modulated PVN microglial morphology and was accompanied by corresponding changes in the local pro-inflammatory cytokine profile.

Administration of clozapine-N-oxide (CNO) to activate the chemogenetic receptors produced bidirectional changes in neuronal activity. Immunofluorescence and western blot analyses showed that, compared with controls, the hM4D(Gi) group displayed a significant increase in the number of c-Fos+ neurons and in c-Fos protein expression, whereas the hM3D(Gq) group showed marked decreases in both measures (Fig. 3e-h). Electrophysiological recordings corroborated these molecular changes. Spike raster plots and LFP traces revealed that chemogenetic manipulation bidirectionally reshaped PVN neuronal activity patterns (Fig. 3i). Multi-unit spike histograms and quantitative firing-rate analysis indicated that microglial inhibition (hM4D(Gi) + CNO) significantly increased both spike counts and mean firing rates, whereas microglial activation (hM3D(Gq) + CNO) strongly suppressed these parameters (Fig. 3j-l). Spectral analysis further demonstrated that microglial inhibition elevated oscillatory power in the 30–80 Hz band, whereas microglial activation reduced it (Fig. 3m, n).

Together, these results demonstrate that microglial activity bidirectionally modulates PVN neuronal excitability, as assessed by both molecular (c-Fos) and electrophysiological metrics, indicating that microglia play a regulatory role in shaping neuronal responses within the PVN.

PVN microglial activity impairs BTI healing by modulating peripheral sympathetic tone

We next examined the functional consequence of this central microglia-neuron circuit on peripheral BTI healing. Although PVN neuroinflammation is known to influence peripheral sympathetic activity [26, 29], its effect on sympathetic tone specifically at the BTI site remains unexplored. To address this, we manipulated PVN neuroinflammation chemogenetically and assessed local sympathetic tone at the healing interface. ELISA measurements showed that NE content in plasma and at BTI healing sites was significantly lower in the hM4D(Gi) group and markedly higher in the hM3D(Gq) group compared with controls (Fig. 4a). Consistent with this, at the BTI site, the protein level of tyrosine hydroxylase (TH), the rate-limiting enzyme in NE synthesis, was reduced in the hM4D(Gi) group and elevated in the hM3D(Gq) group (Fig. 4b, c). Profiling of sympathetic activity across multiple time points further revealed that both NE content (Supplementary Fig. S5a) and TH protein levels (Supplementary Fig. S5b, c) were increased after injury, peaking at 4 weeks post-operation. To further determine whether BTI injury and PVN microglial manipulation were associated with activation of central sympathetic premotor circuits, we examined neuronal activation in the rostral ventrolateral medulla (RVLM), a key brainstem region involved in sympathetic regulation. BTI injury increased c-Fos expression in the RVLM at 4 wpi (Supplementary Fig. S5d, e). Moreover, chemogenetic activation of PVN microglia further enhanced RVLM c-Fos expression, whereas chemogenetic inhibition of PVN microglia reduced this response (Supplementary Fig. S5f, g). Together with the changes in NE and TH levels at the BTI healing site, these findings suggest that PVN microglial activity is associated with enhanced peripheral sympathetic tone, at least in part through recruitment of downstream sympathetic regulatory circuits involving the RVLM.

Notably, ADRB2 has been identified as the predominant adrenergic receptor at the BTI healing interface [12]. We therefore hypothesized that microglia‑driven neuroinflammation in the PVN elevates peripheral sympathetic tone, leading to increased norepinephrine which, through ADRB2, suppresses the expression of osteochondrogenic factors and thereby impedes BTI healing. Our data support this hypothesis. Immunofluorescence analysis showed fewer ADRB2⁺ cells at the healing site in the hM4D(Gi) group, and significantly more in the hM3D(Gq) group, compared with controls (Fig. 4d, e). Western blotting further confirmed that suppressing PVN microglial activity decreased, whereas activating microglia increased, ADRB2 protein levels at the interface (Fig. 4b, c).

We then evaluated the quality of BTI healing across groups. Western blot analysis showed that suppression of PVN inflammation (hM4D(Gi)) up-regulated the osteogenic factor Sp7 and the chondrogenic factor Sox9 at the healing site, whereas inflammatory activation (hM3D(Gq)) strongly down-regulated their expression (Fig. 4f, g). Micro-CT-based three-dimensional reconstruction and analysis of subchondral bone microstructure demonstrated greater bone mass in the hM4D(Gi) group and reduced bone mass in the hM3D(Gq) group relative to controls (Fig. 4h). Quantitative microarchitectural parameters indicated a more favorable healing interface in the hM4D(Gi) group, with significantly higher bone mineral density (BMD), bone volume fraction (BV/TV) and trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and lower trabecular separation (Tb.Sp); the hM3D(Gq) group displayed the opposite pattern (Fig. 4i).

Histological assessment supported these findings. H&E and toluidine blue/fast green (TB&FG) staining revealed more mature tendon-to-bone integration in the hM4D(Gi) group and less mature integration in the hM3D(Gq) group compared with controls (Fig. 4j, k). Using a modified bone-to-tendon insertion maturation score, we found that histological scores in the hM3D(Gq) group were significantly lower than those in both the control and hM4D(Gi) groups at 4 weeks post-operation (Fig. 4l), indicating delayed or inferior morphological healing. Furthermore, quantification of proteoglycan deposition (integrated optical density, IOD) showed significantly higher levels in the hM4D(Gi) group and lower levels in the hM3D(Gq) group (Fig. 4m). Biomechanical testing demonstrated that the hM4D(Gi) group exhibited significantly greater failure load, ultimate strength and stiffness than controls, whereas the hM3D(Gq) group showed the opposite trend (Fig. 4n, o). No significant difference in cross-sectional area was observed among groups (Fig. 4o). To further assess the temporal relationship between central neuroimmune changes and functional BTI recovery, we performed time-series biomechanical testing after RCI. Failure load, ultimate strength, and stiffness gradually improved over time but remained only partially restored at 8 wpi compared with the sham/non-RCI baseline (Supplementary Fig. S6a–c). These results indicate that biomechanical recovery of the repaired BTI lags behind the normalization of central neuroimmune and sympathetic markers.

Metabolomic profiling identifies adenosine as a microglia-associated signaling mediator

Metabolites, as small molecules capable of diffusing freely or being transported between cells, serve as important mediators of intercellular communication [30, 31]. To identify key signaling molecules that might link microglial activation to altered neuronal activity in the PVN, we performed non-targeted metabolomic profiling of PVN extracellular fluid. PVN tissues from control, hM4D(Gi), and hM3D(Gq) groups were analyzed by high-throughput LC-MS/MS. PCA revealed that chemogenetic manipulation of microglia markedly reshaped the metabolic profile of the PVN (Fig. 5a). Differential metabolites (P < 0.05, fold-change > 2, VIP > 1) among the three groups were further analyzed by K-means clustering (Fig. 5b) and hierarchical clustering (Fig. 5c). Among the top-ranking metabolites, adenosine showed an abundance pattern inversely related to microglial status: its levels were significantly lower in the hM4D(Gi) group and higher in the hM3D(Gq) group compared with controls (Fig. 5c). Given the well-established role of adenosine as an endogenous modulator of neural activity, vascular function, and immune regulation [32–35], these results suggest that adenosine may play a crucial role in mediating microglia-dependent regulation of PVN neuronal excitability after BTI injury.

To gain insight into the potential source of the observed adenosine changes, we performed KEGG pathway enrichment analysis on the differential metabolites. This analysis highlighted significant enrichment in pathways related to “Nucleotide metabolism” and “Purine metabolism” (Fig. 5d, e). Adenosine is an intermediate in nucleotide metabolism, primarily generated through the dephosphorylation of ATP to ADP, AMP, and finally adenosine. Its cellular concentration is tightly linked to nucleotide turnover, often rising under conditions of increased ATP consumption [36]. These findings suggest that the elevated adenosine in the PVN following microglial activation may originate from enhanced ATP degradation, warranting further investigation into its specific role in BTI healing.

To independently validate the association between PVN microglial manipulation and extracellular adenosine levels, we measured its extracellular concentration in the PVN using microdialysis coupled with high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Fig. 5f). Consistent with the metabolomics data, inhibition of microglia (hM4D(Gi)) significantly lowered extracellular adenosine levels, whereas microglial activation (hM3D(Gq)) increased them relative to controls (Fig. 5g). Together with the DREADD-induced changes in microglial morphology and pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, these findings suggest that extracellular adenosine accumulation is associated with the microglia-related pro-inflammatory response in the PVN.

Adenosine primarily signals through the A1 (A1R) and A2A (A2AR) receptors in the brain [37, 38]. Western blot analysis showed that the PVN predominantly expressed A1R protein, with minimal A2AR expression (Fig. 5h, i). Chemogenetic modulation bidirectionally regulated A1R abundance: A1R levels were reduced in the hM4D(Gi) group and elevated in the hM3D(Gq) group, while A2AR expression remained unchanged. Immunofluorescence confirmed that A1R almost exclusively colocalized with NeuN+ neurons, with minimal expression in GFAP+ astrocytes or Iba-1+ microglia (Fig. 5j, k), suggesting that A1R-expressing neurons may participate in the response to increased central adenosine signaling. Furthermore, adenosine levels in the PVN were significantly elevated after BTI injury, peaking at 4 weeks and declining by 8 weeks (Supplementary Fig. S7a). In parallel, A1R protein expression in the PVN increased significantly at 4 weeks post-injury, whereas A2AR levels remained low and unchanged (Supplementary Fig. S7b-e).

Central adenosine-related signaling contributes to PVN neuronal suppression and impaired BTI healing

To clarify the impact of central adenosine-related signaling on BTI injury-induced healing impairment, we included a sham/non-RCI reference group (Ref.) as a physiological baseline in the intracerebroventricular intervention experiment. This group allowed us to distinguish baseline PVN neuronal activity, sympathetic tone, ADRB2 expression, and native BTI structural and biomechanical properties from injury-induced changes. Compared with sham/non-RCI mice, RCI mice receiving ACSF showed reduced PVN neuronal activity, increased sympathetic tone, elevated ADRB2 expression at the healing interface, and impaired structural and biomechanical properties of the repaired BTI.

We then examined whether modulation of central adenosine-related signaling altered these injury-induced changes. Mice received intracerebroventricular administration of adenosine or the adenosine receptor-modulating xanthine derivative doxofylline, with ACSF serving as the vehicle control (Fig. 6a). Adenosine was used to enhance central adenosine signaling, whereas doxofylline was used to attenuate adenosine-related signaling. Doxofylline administration partially restored PVN c-Fos expression, whereas adenosine administration further reduced c-Fos expression (Supplementary Fig. S8a–d). These results suggest that central adenosine-related signaling, likely involving A1R-expressing neurons in the PVN, contributes to reduced PVN neuronal activity after BTI injury.

We next assessed whether central modulation of adenosine-related signaling affected sympathetic tone and BTI repair outcomes. Compared with ACSF-treated RCI controls, doxofylline reduced sympathetic-related changes at the BTI healing site, as reflected by decreased NE levels (Fig. 6b), lower ADRB2 expression (Fig. 6c, d), and reduced TH protein expression (Fig. 6e, f). Western blot analysis further showed that doxofylline increased the expression of the osteogenic factor Sp7 and the chondrogenic factor Sox9 at the BTI healing interface (Fig. 6g, h). Consistent with these molecular changes, micro-CT imaging demonstrated improved subchondral bone formation in the doxofylline-treated group (Fig. 6i, j). Histological evaluation showed better matrix organization, higher histological scores (Fig. 6k, l), and increased proteoglycan deposition (Fig. 6m, n). Biomechanical testing further demonstrated greater failure load, ultimate strength, and stiffness after doxofylline treatment compared with ACSF-treated RCI controls (Fig. 6o).

Conversely, adenosine administration aggravated these injury-induced changes. Adenosine increased NE, ADRB2, and TH levels; reduced Sp7 and Sox9 expression; decreased subchondral bone mass; impaired histological integration; reduced proteoglycan deposition; and weakened biomechanical properties of the repaired BTI (Fig. 6b–o).

Collectively, these findings suggest that central adenosine-related signaling bidirectionally influences PVN neuronal activity, sympathetic-associated changes, and BTI repair outcomes after injury. Together with the predominant expression of A1R in PVN neurons, these data support A1R as a likely receptor component, while not excluding the involvement of other adenosine receptor subtypes or additional central circuits.

Local ADRB2 signaling contributes to impaired BTI healing downstream of sympathetic activation

To establish a causal link from central microglial activation to peripheral ADRB2-mediated healing impairment, we performed a rescue experiment. We first confirmed that chemogenetic activation of PVN microglia (hM3D(Gq)) elevated sympathetic tone and ADRB2 expression at the BTI site, leading to poor healing (Fig. 4). We then asked whether local blockade of ADRB2 could reverse these detrimental effects. Accordingly, following rotator cuff repair, we administered the selective ADRB2 antagonist ICI 118,551 directly to the BTI healing site of hM3D(Gq) mice (Fig. 7a). qRT-PCR analysis at 4 weeks post-injury revealed that ADRB2 blockade significantly increased the mRNA levels of osteogenic (Runx2, Ocn) and chondrogenic (Sox9, Aggrecan) factors compared with vehicle-treated hM3D(Gq) controls (Fig. 7b). Western blot analysis confirmed elevated protein expression of the key factors Sp7 and Sox9 at the healing enthesis after ICI 118,551 treatment (Fig. 7c, d). Immunofluorescence staining further revealed a marked increase in Sp7+ and Sox9+ cells at the healing interface in ICI 118,551-treated mice compared with vehicle controls (Fig. 7e, f).

We next examined whether this molecular rescue translated to improved structural and functional healing. Micro-CT analysis demonstrated that local ADRB2 antagonism significantly improved subchondral bone microarchitecture in hM3D(Gq) mice, yielding higher BMD, BV/TV, and Tb.Th, and lower Tb.Sp (Fig. 7g, h). Histologically, ICI 118,551-treated hM3D(Gq) animals exhibited higher histological maturity scores (Fig. 7i, k) and greater proteoglycan deposition (IOD) at the healing interface compared with vehicle-treated hM3D(Gq) controls (Fig. 7j, l).

To further determine whether local ADRB2 signaling also contributes to impaired healing after conventional BTI injury without chemogenetic activation of PVN microglia, we performed an additional experiment in standard RCI mice. Following rotator cuff repair, mice received local administration of ICI 118,551 or vehicle at the BTI healing site (Supplementary Fig. S9a). Compared with vehicle-treated RCI mice, local ICI 118,551 treatment increased the mRNA expression of osteogenic markers, including Runx2 and Ocn, and chondrogenic markers, including Sox9 and Aggrecan (Supplementary Fig. S9b). Western blot analysis further showed increased protein expression of Sp7 and Sox9 after ADRB2 blockade (Supplementary Fig. S9c, d). Micro-CT analysis demonstrated improved subchondral bone microarchitecture in ICI 118,551-treated mice, as reflected by higher BMD, BV/TV, and Tb.Th and lower Tb.Sp (Supplementary Fig. S9e, f). Histological analysis showed improved insertion maturation after local ADRB2 blockade, with higher histological scores in the ICI 118,551-treated group than in the vehicle-treated group (Supplementary Fig. S9g, h). TB&FG staining further revealed increased proteoglycan deposition at the healing interface, as quantified by IOD (Supplementary Fig. S9i, j). These findings suggest that local ADRB2 signaling contributes to impaired BTI repair not only under experimentally enhanced PVN microglial activation but also in the standard RCI model.

Sensory afferent input is an important upstream contributor to PVN neuroinflammation after BTI injury

Having established that BTI injury induces microglial activation and neuroinflammation in the PVN, we next aimed to identify the afferent signal that conveys peripheral injury information to the brain. Based on the well-documented role of sensory-nerve-mediated interoceptive signaling in driving central adaptations after peripheral tissue damage [14–16, 39], we hypothesized that sensory input from the injury site is essential for initiating this central response. To test this, we performed local sensory denervation by injecting capsaicin—a neurotoxin that selectively ablates nociceptive C-fibers—directly into the BTI after rotator cuff repair (Fig. 8a).

Successful impairment of local sensory innervation was confirmed by a pronounced reduction in calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) immunoreactivity, a marker of sensory nerve terminals, at the BTI healing site compared with vehicle-injected controls (Fig. 8b, c). Remarkably, this local sensory ablation substantially attenuated BTI injury-induced microgliosis in the PVN. Capsaicin-treated mice showed a significant decrease in tdTomato+ microglial density (Fig. 8d, e) and a shift toward a less-activated morphological phenotype (Fig. 8f-h) relative to vehicle-treated injured animals. Furthermore, the molecular signature of central neuroinflammation was markedly suppressed by capsaicin treatment. Western blot analysis revealed that injury-induced up-regulation of the M1 markers CD86 and iNOS was significantly blunted, whereas the M2 markers CD206 and Arg1 remained unchanged (Fig. 8i, j). Consistently, qRT-PCR analysis of PVN tissue showed that capsaicin treatment abolished the BTI injury-evoked increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL‑1β, IL‑6, TNF‑α) (Fig. 8k). Concomitantly, the microglia-driven rise in extracellular adenosine in the PVN after injury was also significantly reduced by capsaicin (Fig. 8l).

We then examined whether alleviation of PVN neuroinflammation by capsaicin restored neuronal activity (Supplementary Fig. S10a). Immunofluorescence analysis showed that capsaicin largely reversed the BTI injury-induced suppression of PVN neurons: the number of c-Fos+ cells, which was markedly decreased after injury, became significantly higher than in control animals (Supplementary Fig. S10b, c). Western blotting confirmed that c-Fos protein levels in the PVN were elevated in capsaicin-treated mice compared with the injury-alone group (Supplementary Fig. S10d, e). In vivo electrophysiological recordings further demonstrated that the mean firing rate of PVN neurons (Supplementary Fig. S10f, g) and the power of low-frequency LFP oscillations (Supplementary Fig. S10h, i), both depressed at 4 wpi, were restored to control levels after capsaicin treatment.

Discussion

The present study provides evidence for a previously underappreciated neuroimmune mechanism by which peripheral BTI injury engages central circuits that may influence musculoskeletal repair. We show that BTI injury activates sensory afferent signaling, induces microglia-mediated neuroinflammation within the hypothalamic PVN, and is associated with reduced PVN neuronal excitability and increased central adenosine-related signaling. This central response coincides with enhanced sympathetic tone and increased ADRB2 signaling at the healing interface, where osteogenic and chondrogenic programs are suppressed (Fig. 9). Together, these findings suggest that a sensory–central–sympathetic regulatory axis contributes to impaired BTI repair and provide a conceptual framework for brain–body communication in musculoskeletal regeneration.

Proposed sensory–central–sympathetic regulatory axis contributing to impaired BTI repair. BTI injury may engage sensory afferent signaling, which is associated with PVN microglial neuroinflammation, extracellular adenosine accumulation, and reduced PVN neuronal activity. These central changes coincide with enhanced sympathetic-associated signaling and increased local ADRB2 activity at the healing interface, where osteogenic and chondrogenic gene expression is suppressed, thereby contributing to impaired BTI repair

Although these findings support the involvement of a sensory–central–sympathetic regulatory axis in BTI repair, this pathway should not be interpreted as an exclusive or fully linear mechanism. Altered PVN neuronal activity may contribute to the peripheral healing phenotype, but the present data do not establish PVN neuronal firing as the sole or direct mediator of impaired repair. Changes in norepinephrine levels at the healing interface may also reflect broader autonomic activation, stress-related responses, or systemic neuroimmune regulation. In addition, because adenosine and doxofylline were administered intracerebroventricularly, their effects may involve multiple central regions beyond the PVN, including additional autonomic, neuroendocrine, and inflammatory circuits. PVN neuroinflammation may therefore influence BTI healing through integrated mechanisms involving sympathetic output, hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis modulation, and systemic inflammatory mediators. Future studies using projection-specific recordings, cell-type-specific manipulation, and region-restricted pharmacological approaches will be required to define the precise contribution of PVN neuronal circuits to BTI repair.

A central question is how a localized peripheral injury is communicated to the brain to elicit a central neuroimmune response. Our data support sensory nerve input as an important upstream contributor to this process. Local capsaicin-mediated impairment of sensory nerve terminals at the BTI markedly attenuated PVN microgliosis and the associated neuroinflammatory signature, suggesting that sensory afferent signaling is involved in relaying injury-related information to this autonomic nucleus. Although humoral factors and systemic inflammatory mediators may also contribute, these findings extend the established roles of sensory nerves in nociception and neurogenic inflammation [40–42] by suggesting that they may also participate in engaging central neuroimmune circuits that influence peripheral tissue repair.

Microglia, the resident immune cells of the CNS, are activated by diverse insults [43–45] and release pro-inflammatory mediators that can substantially influence neuronal function, contributing to the pathophysiology of numerous neurological disorders [20, 46, 47]. In the present study, peripheral BTI injury was associated with a temporally defined microglial response within the PVN, a key autonomic regulatory nucleus [7, 10, 11, 48], characterized by microglial accumulation, morphological activation, and increased expression of pro-inflammatory markers. Chemogenetic manipulation further supported a functional involvement of PVN microglial activity in BTI repair: inhibition of PVN microglia improved repair outcomes, whereas activation of PVN microglia worsened them. Thus, PVN neuroinflammation may represent an important central contributor to musculoskeletal repair outcomes. Given that PVN neuroinflammation has previously been implicated in cardiovascular and metabolic diseases [29, 49–51], our findings extend its potential relevance to orthopaedic tissue repair and suggest that central neuroimmune regulation may participate in the broader biology of tissue regeneration.

It should be noted that PVN neuroinflammation after BTI injury may be temporally dynamic rather than uniform throughout the healing process. In the present study, microglial accumulation, pro-inflammatory marker expression, extracellular adenosine elevation, neuronal suppression, and increased sympathetic tone were most prominent at approximately 4 weeks after injury and declined toward baseline by 8 weeks. This temporal pattern suggests that the reduced PVN neuronal activity observed at 4 wpi may represent a later suppressive or compensatory phase of neuroinflammatory signaling, potentially associated with adenosine accumulation. However, this does not exclude the possibility that earlier inflammatory responses may exert different, or even opposite, effects on specific PVN neuronal populations, including transient excitation of sympathoregulatory neurons. Because c-Fos staining and multi-unit electrophysiological recordings reflect overall neuronal activity within the recorded PVN region, they may not fully capture cell-type-specific or projection-specific temporal changes. Future studies using cell-type-resolved and projection-specific recordings will be needed to define the temporal evolution of PVN neuroimmune–neuronal interactions after BTI injury.

A major issue in neuroimmunology is how activated microglia shape neuronal activity. Our metabolomic and microdialysis analyses identified extracellular adenosine as a microglia-associated signaling mediator whose levels changed in parallel with PVN microglial manipulation. Adenosine is a well-established endogenous neuromodulator that can regulate neuronal excitability through multiple adenosine receptor subtypes, including Gi-coupled A1R [52–54]. In our study, A1R was the predominant adenosine receptor detected in PVN neurons, and pharmacological modulation of central adenosine-related signaling altered PVN c-Fos expression. These findings support the involvement of adenosine-related signaling, likely involving A1R-expressing PVN neurons, in the regulation of PVN neuronal activity. However, because adenosine is not A1R-specific and doxofylline is not a canonical highly selective A1R antagonist, these pharmacological results should be interpreted as evidence for central adenosine-related modulation rather than as definitive proof of an exclusively A1R-mediated mechanism.

Although our additional analyses showed that DREADD manipulation altered PVN microglial morphology and pro-inflammatory cytokine expression in parallel with changes in extracellular adenosine levels, the precise mechanism linking microglial activation to adenosine accumulation remains unresolved. The enrichment of purine and nucleotide metabolism pathways suggests that enhanced extracellular ATP turnover or altered purine metabolism may contribute to increased adenosine production. However, we did not directly identify the cellular source of adenosine or the enzymatic pathway responsible for its accumulation. Therefore, we refer to adenosine as a microglia-associated signaling mediator rather than definitively as microglia-derived adenosine. Future studies using cell-type-specific metabolic tracing or manipulation of ATP-degrading enzymes will be required to clarify this mechanism.

Together, these findings support a functionally linked cellular communication process in which BTI injury is associated with PVN microglial activation, extracellular adenosine accumulation, and reduced PVN neuronal activity. This process is likely to involve A1R-expressing PVN neurons, although the contribution of other adenosine receptor subtypes or additional central circuits cannot be excluded. These observations are consistent with previously proposed models in which microglia regulate neuronal activity through adenosine-related signaling [55]. Our study extends this concept to the context of peripheral musculoskeletal repair and suggests that central adenosine-related neuroimmune signaling may contribute to BTI healing outcomes.

The relationship between reduced overall PVN neuronal activity and increased sympathetic tone should be interpreted with caution. The PVN is a heterogeneous autonomic and neuroendocrine nucleus containing multiple neuronal populations, including presympathetic, neuroendocrine, glutamatergic, GABAergic, and peptidergic neurons. Therefore, reduced total c-Fos expression or multi-unit activity does not necessarily indicate uniform suppression of all PVN output pathways. One possible explanation is that adenosine-related signaling preferentially suppresses inhibitory neuronal populations or neurons that normally restrain sympathetic output, thereby indirectly disinhibiting downstream sympathetic circuits. Alternatively, PVN microglial inflammation may alter the balance among distinct neuronal subtypes and projection-defined circuits, resulting in enhanced sympathetic tone despite reduced overall neuronal activity. Future studies using cell-type-specific recording, projection-specific tracing, and selective manipulation of PVN-to-brainstem circuits will be required to identify the neuronal subpopulations responsible for the sympathetic and healing phenotypes.

We further clarified the efferent pathway by which central events may influence peripheral repair. The PVN is a major upstream regulator of sympathetic nervous system (SNS) outflow [56, 57]. Our additional analysis of RVLM c-Fos expression provides further support for the involvement of central sympathetic regulatory circuits after BTI injury. The RVLM is a major premotor sympathetic region, and increased neuronal activation in this area is consistent with enhanced sympathetic outflow. The observation that PVN microglial activation increased, whereas PVN microglial inhibition reduced, RVLM c-Fos expression suggests that PVN neuroinflammation may influence peripheral sympathetic tone partly through downstream PVN–brainstem autonomic pathways. However, the RVLM should not be interpreted as the sole downstream mediator, as the PVN may regulate sympathetic output through multiple hypothalamic and brainstem routes. Consistent with this interpretation, manipulating the microglia–adenosine-related axis in the PVN altered NE levels at the healing BTI, suggesting that this central response is associated with changes in local sympathetic tone. Importantly, we identified ADRB2 as a key peripheral mediator of this detrimental sympathetic signal. Local ADRB2 antagonism at the BTI site rescued the suppression of osteogenic (Runx2, Ocn) and chondrogenic (Sox9, Aggrecan) factors and improved structural and functional healing without directly manipulating central activity. Moreover, the beneficial effect of local ADRB2 blockade was observed not only in hM3D(Gq)-mediated PVN microglial activation but also in the standard RCI model. This finding suggests that ADRB2 signaling contributes to injury-associated repair impairment after BTI injury, rather than being only a downstream consequence of experimentally enhanced central microglial activation. Nevertheless, because local ADRB2 blockade may act on multiple cell types within the healing interface, future studies using cell-type-specific ADRB2 deletion will be needed to define the precise peripheral target cells responsible for this effect. Future projection-specific tracing, recording, and manipulation studies will also be required to define the precise PVN-to-RVLM circuitry involved in BTI repair.

An important temporal observation is that normalization of central neuroimmune and sympathetic markers did not necessarily indicate complete functional recovery of the repaired BTI. Although PVN microglial activation, extracellular adenosine levels, neuronal activity, and local NE levels largely returned toward sham levels by 8 wpi, biomechanical testing showed that failure load, ultimate strength, and stiffness remained only partially restored. This delayed functional recovery suggests that early-to-intermediate activation of the sensory–PVN microglia–adenosine-related signaling–sympathetic ADRB2 axis may have persistent consequences for matrix maturation, mineralization, and mechanical integration at the healing interface. Therefore, interventions targeting this axis may need to be applied during the active early-to-intermediate healing window to achieve long-term improvements in BTI repair.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the mechanism linking PVN microglial activation to extracellular adenosine accumulation remains unresolved. Although metabolomic analysis suggests involvement of purine and nucleotide metabolism, we did not determine whether increased adenosine primarily reflects extracellular ATP degradation, altered nucleoside transport, or other metabolic pathways. Second, the pharmacological interpretation of the adenosine-related experiments requires caution. Because adenosine and doxofylline were administered intracerebroventricularly, their effects may involve central regions beyond the PVN; moreover, adenosine can activate multiple receptor subtypes, and doxofylline should not be considered a canonical highly selective A1R antagonist. Third, although local capsaicin treatment supports the involvement of sensory afferent input, the specific sensory neuron subtypes, molecular mediators, and potential contributions of humoral or systemic inflammatory signals remain to be defined. Fourth, the PVN contains heterogeneous neuronal populations, and our c-Fos and multi-unit electrophysiological analyses do not resolve the specific neuronal subtypes or projection-defined circuits responsible for sympathetic regulation. Finally, this study was performed in male mice, and future work should examine sex differences and validate the relevance of this pathway in large animal models and human patients.

In summary, our findings support a functionally linked sensory–central–sympathetic regulatory axis that contributes to impaired BTI repair. BTI injury engages sensory afferent signaling and PVN microglial neuroinflammation, which are associated with extracellular adenosine accumulation, altered PVN neuronal activity, enhanced sympathetic-related signaling, and increased ADRB2 activity at the healing interface. These changes coincide with reduced osteogenic and chondrogenic programs and compromised structural and functional repair. Rather than defining a fully linear or exclusive pathway, our study provides a framework for understanding how peripheral musculoskeletal injury may interact with central neuroimmune and autonomic circuits to influence tissue healing. Targeting specific nodes in this regulatory axis, such as modulating PVN microglial activity, central adenosine-related signaling, or local ADRB2 signaling at the injury site, may provide potential therapeutic strategies for improving BTI repair.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals

All animal procedures were approved by the Laboratory Animal Committee of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (Approval No. CSU-2023-0118). Male C57BL/6 and Cx3cr1-CreERT2 mice (8–10 weeks old) were obtained from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. Animals were maintained under standard housing conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle and given ad libitum access to food and water.

Generation of Cx3cr1-tdtomato reporter mice and tamoxifen induction

To enable specific labeling and tracking of microglia, Cx3cr1-CreERT2 transgenic mice (Shanghai Model Organisms) were crossed with Ai14 (Rosa26-tdTomato) reporter mice (Stock No. 007914, The Jackson Laboratory). The resulting Cx3cr1-CreERT2;Ai14 offspring (hereafter referred to as Cx3cr1;tdTomato mice) express the red fluorescent protein tdTomato in a Cre- and tamoxifen-dependent manner, selectively in microglia.

For tamoxifen induction, 8- to 10-week-old Cx3cr1;tdTomato mice were administered tamoxifen (75 mg/kg body weight, intraperitoneally; MedChemExpress) once daily for five consecutive days. Tamoxifen was dissolved in corn oil (10 mg/mL) and prepared fresh prior to each injection. Mice were allowed a washout period of at least 2 weeks after the final tamoxifen injection before undergoing any subsequent experimental procedures, to ensure complete recombination and stable tdTomato expression in microglia.

Rotator cuff injury model

A murine rotator cuff injury and repair model was generated as previously described [58]. Mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital (0.6 mL/20 g; Sigma-Aldrich). A longitudinal incision was made on the anterolateral shoulder, followed by deltoid splitting to expose the supraspinatus tendon. The tendon was secured with a 6 − 0 PDS suture (Ethicon) and transected at its insertion on the greater tuberosity. After removal of enthesis cartilage using a scalpel, a 1-mm bone tunnel was drilled through the humeral head, and the tendon was reattached to its native footprint through the tunnel. Wounds were closed in layers. Mice were allowed free cage activity and administered penicillin G prophylactically for 3 days. The surgical workflow is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S1. For the sham operation group, the same skin incision and deltoid splitting were performed to expose the supraspinatus tendon, but the tendon was left intact without transection, bone tunnel drilling, or repair. The wound was then closed in layers, and mice received identical postoperative care and prophylactic penicillin G for 3 days.

Retrograde viral tracing

To map central regions innervating the BTI, pseudorabies virus (PRV-CAG-EGFP, 1 µL; Braincase) was injected into the supraspinatus insertion under stereomicroscopic visualization. After 4–5 days, mice were euthanized, and brains were harvested for histological analysis and fluorescence imaging to identify PRV-labeled neurons.

Stereotaxic surgery and chemogenetic manipulation

Chemogenetic modulation of PVN microglia was performed using stereotaxic AAV injections as previously described [59]. Isoflurane-anesthetized mice were secured in a stereotaxic apparatus (RWD). AAV vectors (rAAV-MG1.2-EF1a-DIO-hM3D(Gq)-EGFP or rAAV-MG1.2-EF1a-DIO-hM4D(Gi)-EGFP; 5.08 × 10¹² vg/mL, 150 nL) were bilaterally injected into the PVN (AP − 0.76 mm, ML ± 0.26 mm, DV − 4.74 mm) using a Micro 4 injector (WPI). Tamoxifen (75 mg/kg, i.p.; MedChemExpress) was administered for 5 consecutive days to induce Cre activity. Three weeks after injection, clozapine-N-oxide (CNO; 5 mg/kg, i.p.; BrainVTA) was applied to activate DREADDs. Microglia-specific viral expression was confirmed by immunohistochemistry.

Immunohistochemistry

After deep anesthesia, mice were transcardially perfused with saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Brains were extracted and post-fixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4 °C, then cryoprotected by sequential immersion in 20% and 30% sucrose solutions. Coronal Sect. (16 μm thick) containing the PVN were cut on a cryostat and mounted onto slides. For immunohistochemistry, sections were incubated in blocking buffer (5% normal serum, 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by an overnight incubation at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-cFos (Synaptic Systems, #226017), anti-Iba1 (Wako, #019-19741), anti-GFAP (Abcam, ab4674), anti-NeuN (Abcam, ab104224), anti-A1R (Abcam, ab82477), anti-A2AR (Affinity, #DF4850), anti-ADRB2 (Sigma-Aldrich, SAB4500577), and anti-CGRP (Abcam, ab81887). Fluorescent secondary antibodies were applied for 2 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a Zeiss AxioImager M2 microscope equipped with an Apotome.2 system (Zeiss, Solms, Germany), and fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). Antibodies used in this study are detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

Western blot analysis

Brains were rapidly removed, and PVN tissue was dissected on ice. Samples were homogenized in lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay (Beyotime #P0010). Equal protein amounts were resolved via SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore). After blocking, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with antibodies against c-Fos ( 1:1000, #2250, Cell Signaling Technology), CD86 (1:1000, #91882S, Cell Signaling Technology), CD206 (1:1000, ab64693, Abcam), iNos (1:1000, #18985-1-AP, Proteintech), Arg-1 (1:1000, #16001-1-AP, Proteintech), A1R (1:1500, ab82477, Abcam), A2AR (1:1000, #DF4850, Affinity), ADRB2 (1:1000, SAB4500577, Sigma-Aldrich), Sp7 (1:1000, ab209484, Abcam), Sox9 (1:1000, ab185966, Abcam), and β-actin (1:5000, #66009-1, Proteintech). Following incubation with secondary antibodies conjugated to horseradish peroxidase for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using ChemiDoc™ MP (Bio-Rad) and quantified in ImageJ. The relative protein expression levels were normalized to β-actin.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA from PVN and BTI tissues was extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen). cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg RNA using the GoScript™ Reverse Transcription System (Promega). qRT-PCR was conducted on an ABI PRISM 7900HT using GoTaq® qPCR Master Mix (Promega). Cytokine genes (IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α) and osteochondrogenic markers (sox9, runx2, ocn, aggrecan) were analyzed. GAPDH served as the internal control. Relative expression was calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

RNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

PVN tissue was dissected for RNA-seq. RNA quantity and purity were assessed using NanoDrop ND-1000, and integrity was evaluated with the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100. cDNA libraries were constructed using SuperScript™ II Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate paired-end reads. After quality filtering, clean reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome. Gene expression quantification, differential expression analysis, GSEA, and GO/KEGG enrichment analyses were performed to determine biological pathways associated with transcriptional changes.

In vivo electrophysiological recording

A 16-channel microwire array (KD-MWA-16, Kedou) was implanted into the PVN and grounded using cerebellar screws (RWD). Electrodes were fixed with dental cement. After 5–7 days recovery, mice were habituated for 30 min and connected to a Cereplex M amplifier (Blackrock Microsystems). Signals were sampled at 1000 Hz, low-pass filtered at 250 Hz, and local field potentials (LFPs) were recorded for 30 min. Single-unit spikes were simultaneously acquired with a 300–3000 Hz band-pass filter. Frequency spectra and power spectral density of the LFPs, together with spike-time histograms and firing-rate summaries, were analyzed using NeuroExplorer.

Metabolomic analysis

PVN metabolites were quantified using high-performance ion exchange chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (HPIC-MS/MS) with a system from Shanghai Biotree Biotech Co., Ltd. Processed data were analyzed by principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) to identify metabolic alterations. Metabolites with VIP > 1 and p < 0.05 were considered significantly changed. KEGG (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/) and MetaboAnalyst (http://www.metaboanalyst.ca/) databases were used for pathway enrichment.

Microdialysis and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)

Microdialysis coupled with HPLC with electrochemical detection (HPLC‑ECD) was used to monitor adenosine levels in the PVN according to a previous experimental protocol [60]. Briefly, under isoflurane anesthesia, a guide cannula (CMA7) was stereotaxically implanted into the PVN and fixed with dental cement. After 4–5 days of recovery, a microdialysis probe (CMA7) was inserted and perfused continuously with sterile artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) at 1 µL/min. Following a 180‑min equilibration period, dialysates were collected every 30 min. Adenosine concentrations were determined by reverse‑phase HPLC‑ECD using an ODS column (150 mm × 3.2 mm) and a mobile phase consisting of 150 mM NaH₂PO₄, 4.8 mM citrate, 3 mM SDS, 50 µM EDTA, 8 mM NaCl, 11.3% methanol and 16.7% acetonitrile (pH 5.6) at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. Detection was performed with a coulometric detector (Decade Lite, Antec Scientific).

Intracerebroventricular cannulation and drug administration

A guide cannula targeting the third ventricle (AP − 1.8 mm, DV − 5.0 mm) was implanted following rotator cuff injury [61]. After 7 days recovery, mice received continuous infusion of adenosine or doxofylline (10 µg/day) in ACSF using a CMA402 pump for 21 days. Implant placement was verified anatomically post-experiment.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Norepinephrine (NE) levels in the plasma and BTI repair site were quantified using an ELISA kit (E-EL-0047c, Elabscience). Regenerated BTI tissue (~ 20 mg) was harvested as previously described [58]. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a Multiskan Mk3 microplate reader, and each sample was tested in triplicate.

Micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT)

Subchondral bone formation was evaluated using a vivaCT-80 scanner (Scanco). Supraspinatus tendon-humerus (SSTH) complexes samples were fixed in 4% PFA overnight and scanned at 10.4-µm voxel size, 55 kVp, 0.36° rotation, and 400-ms exposure. The acquired Micro-CT data for each specimen were processed using a 3-dimensional Gaussian filter and a global threshold to differentiate bone voxels from surrounding soft tissues and bone marrow. A region of interest (ROI) was defined following established criteria [62]. Subsequently, morphological parameters of the newly formed bone—bone mineral density (BMD), bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th) and trabecular spacing (Tb.Sp)—were quantified.

Histological staining

After micro-CT, SSTH samples were decalcified, embedded, and sectioned at 5 μm in the sagittal plane through the greater tuberosity and supraspinatus tendon. Sections were stained with hematoxylin & eosin (H&E) or toluidine blue & fast green (TB&FG). Histological examination was conducted using light microscopy (CX31, Olympus, Germany). H&E assessed general healing morphology; TB&FG quantified proteoglycan content via integrated optical density (IOD). Two blinded observers performed semiquantitative scoring using a validated BTI healing system [63].

Biomechanical testing

Mechanical properties (failure load, ultimate strength, and stiffness) of the repaired BTI were assessed using an Instron 5942 testing system. Prior to testing, the width and thickness of the supraspinatus tendon insertion site were measured using a caliper under a tensile load of 0.1 N, and the cross-sectional area was calculated accordingly [64]. The tendon was securely clamped using sandpaper, while the humerus was firmly held in a vice grip. Testing was conducted at room temperature with specimens preconditioned to 0.1 N and then subjected to failure loading at a rate of 0.03 mm/s. A consistent gauge length was maintained throughout the testing procedure. Data points were excluded if the tendon slipped out of the grip or if failure did not occur at the BTI site.

Local pharmacological inhibition of ADRB2

Local pharmacological inhibition of ADRB2 was performed using the selective ADRB2 antagonist ICI 118,551 hydrochloride (HY-13951, MedChemExpress), as previously described [65]. ICI 118,551 was dissolved in sterile saline and administered locally at a dose of 10 mg/kg. This local ADRB2 blockade strategy was used in two experimental settings. First, to determine whether ADRB2 blockade could rescue the impaired healing phenotype associated with PVN microglial activation, hM3D(Gq) mice that underwent rotator cuff injury and repair received local peri-injury injections of ICI 118,551 or vehicle. Second, to examine whether ADRB2 signaling also contributes to impaired healing under conventional injury conditions, standard RCI mice without PVN viral injection or hM3D(Gq)-mediated microglial activation were treated with local ICI 118,551 or vehicle. Beginning on postoperative day 7, mice received daily peri-injury injections of ICI 118,551 or an equal volume of vehicle control at the BTI healing site under isoflurane anesthesia for 21 consecutive days. The total injection volume was 10 µL per mouse, and injections were performed using a 33-gauge Hamilton syringe under direct visualization.

Local sensory denervation by capsaicin

To ablate local sensory nerve terminals at the injury site, capsaicin (HY-10447, MedChemExpress) was used. A stock solution of capsaicin was prepared in a vehicle of 10% ethanol and 10% Tween 80 in saline, and then diluted with saline to a working concentration of 2 µg/µL, according to a previous report [66]. Under isoflurane anesthesia, mice in the denervation group received a total of four BTI injections of capsaicin (10 µg in 5 µL each): 48 h before surgery and immediately, 1 week, and 2 weeks after rotator cuff repair. Control mice received an equal volume of the vehicle solution. To minimize systemic discomfort, all mice were pretreated with analgesic (buprenorphine, 0.1 mg/kg, s.c.) 30 min prior to capsaicin or vehicle injection.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software, with results presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Comparisons between two groups were performed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was utilized, followed by Bonferroni or Newman-Keuls post hoc tests. Additionally, two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc testing was applied for multiple comparisons. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
