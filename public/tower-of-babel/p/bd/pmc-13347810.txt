Clustering of countries based on national essential medicines lists: cross-sectional study
Authors: Adelaide Buadu, Moizza Zia Ul Haq, Camila Heredia, Amal Rizvi, Aine Workentin, Nav Persaud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNational essential medicines lists (NEMLs) guide medicine selection and procurement and are key tools for promoting equitable access. While countries adapt their lists to national priorities, underlying medical needs are broadly similar across settings; however, factors beyond epidemiological need influence which medicines are included on national lists. We assessed whether countries can be empirically grouped based on their NEML content and examined how these groupings relate to geography, economic status and selected health system characteristics.MethodsWe assessed NEMLs from 158 WHO Member States and selected non-Member States and territories. Countries were clustered using k-means analysis applied to principal components derived from binary medicine-inclusion data. Cluster profiles were characterised using ORs and compared across WHO region, gross domestic product (GDP) per capita, health expenditure, life expectancy and population size.ResultsFour medicine-based clusters were identified. Cluster 1 (66 countries, 41.8%), largely from the African Region and the Region of the Americas, emphasised medicines for infectious and neglected diseases, vaccines and antisera. Cluster 2 (65 countries, 41.1%) included countries from multiple regions listing a heterogeneous mix of older, off-patent medicines for symptomatic management and selected chronic conditions. Cluster 3 (18 countries, 11.4%), primarily from the European, Eastern Mediterranean and Region of the Americas, prioritised medicines for chronic noncommunicable diseases. Cluster 4 (9 countries, 5.7%), predominantly from the European Region, included newer, higher cost medicines. Cluster membership was significantly associated with WHO region (χ², p<0.001), but clusters did not correspond exclusively to any single region or income group, with substantial within-cluster heterogeneity in GDP and health expenditure.ConclusionCountries can be grouped according to the medicines prioritised on their NEMLs. These clusters reflect patterns in medicine selection and related health system policies that cut across traditional geographic and economic classifications and provide a complementary framework for comparative health system analysis and policy benchmarking.

Introduction

Access to essential medicines remains a central global health challenge, with persistent inequities in availability, affordability and use across and within countries. Despite this challenge, countries are often categorised by geographic region or economic activity, such as gross domestic product (GDP), but for specific policy issues like access to medicines, other factors may be more relevant.1 2 National health systems are frequently compared within WHO regions or income bands. These groupings are sometimes appropriate: certain health conditions cluster regionally, and specific interventions are more readily available in higher income settings.3 4 However, many health challenges—particularly non-communicable diseases such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease and chronic respiratory conditions—are globally prevalent and addressed with broadly similar clinical interventions such as medicines.5 Classifications based primarily on geography or income may obscure important similarities and differences in how countries organise and prioritise healthcare delivery.57

National essential medicines lists (NEMLs) guide medicine selection, procurement, reimbursement and prescribing, aiming to promote equitable access to safe, effective and cost-effective treatments.8 9 The WHO Model List of Essential Medicines serves as a global resource, which countries are encouraged to adapt according to local epidemiology, health system capacity and resource constraints.8 10 As of the most recent reporting, 158 WHO Member States and selected non-Member States and territories have established an NEML.8 11 National lists vary in composition and scope in ways that are not well explained by differences in health needs.12 13

Previous analyses have examined alignment with the WHO Model List or compared medicine listing in NEMLs or availability across countries stratified by income or region, or documented variation in the composition of NEMLs across countries.1 14 15 While informative, such approaches assume that geography or income primarily drives medicine selection. In this study, we examined whether countries can be empirically grouped based solely on the medicines included in their NEMLs. We then assessed how these medicine-based clusters relate to commonly used country classifications, including WHO region, economic grouping, health expenditure, life expectancy, health access and quality and population size. By doing so, we test whether conventional geographic and economic classifications may obscure meaningful structure in medicine selection across countries. By focusing on medicines as the organising principle, this analysis provides a complementary perspective on global health system variation and informs discussions on essential medicines policy, health system convergence and equity in access to care.8 9 16 Identifying such groupings could reveal unexpected patterns of health system organisation and highlight countries whose medicine selection practices diverge from their economic or regional peers.1 13

Methods

Data sources and identification of NEMLs

We compiled 158 NEMLs from WHO Member States and selected non-Member States and territories. Of these, 11 were reimbursement lists.

In May 2023, we systematically identified NEMLs through official government and WHO country office sources, supplemented by consultation with national and regional experts where necessary. We included inpatient and outpatient lists across all levels of care, excluding non-list prescribing guidelines. Similar approaches have been described previously.17 For each country, we used the most recent edition of the NEML identified. Reporting of this study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (see online supplemental appendix S1).

Medicines were abstracted using International Nonproprietary Names (INNs). Combination products were separated into individual components, and salts were ignored to ensure consistency across lists. Medicines were mapped to Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) codes using automated web-based matching, followed by manual review by two researchers. Random validation checks against source documents ensured accuracy.18 19

Country characteristics

Country-level indicators were compiled to compare with medicine-based clusters. Life expectancy at birth and GDP per capita were obtained from the Central Intelligence Agency World Factbook,20 population size from the United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs,21 and per-capita health expenditure from the WHO Global Health Observatory.22 Healthcare Access and Quality (HAQ) Index scores were obtained from the Global Burden of Disease Study.23 These indicators were selected to provide broad demographic, economic and health-system comparisons across medicine-based clusters. For countries where data was missing from the abovementioned sources (eg, Somalia, Democratic People’s Republic of Korea), analyses used data from alternative sources where available.

Pre-processing of medicine variables

Medicines that were universally listed or nearly invariant across countries (SD<0.1) were excluded, as they do not contribute meaningfully to between-country differentiation and may distort multivariate analyses.24 Of the 2084 medicines initially identified, 320 (15.4%) were excluded.

Dimensionality reduction

Because the dataset contained nearly two thousand binary medicine-inclusion variables, dimensionality reduction was required prior to clustering. Principal component analysis (PCA) was selected due to its interpretability and computational efficiency for high-dimensional datasets and its widespread use in comparative health system and policy research. PCA was applied to the binary medicine-inclusion matrix to identify latent dimensions representing underlying patterns in how countries tend to select similar groups of medicines. In this context, these latent dimensions reflect shared therapeutic priorities and medicine-selection tendencies that are not directly observable from individual medicine listings alone but emerge from recurring coinclusion patterns across countries. Although PCA was applied to binary data, this approach has been widely used to summarise high-dimensional inclusion patterns in health systems analyses.2527

The first 10 principal components were retained based on inspection of the scree plot and cumulative variance explained (figure 1), consistent with standard practice.28 Visual inspection of the scree plot indicated an elbow after the early components, beyond which additional components contributed only marginal gains in explained variance. Retaining 10 components was, therefore, chosen as a pragmatic cut-off that preserved the dominant structure in the data while reducing noise and computational complexity prior to k-means clustering. Component scores were retained for each country and used as input for clustering.

Scree plot of eigenvalues after principal component analysis (PCA). The plot shows the eigenvalues of the principal components used in the PCA, with the first few components capturing the largest variance in the medicine-based dataset. The plot was used to determine the number of components to retain for clustering analyses.

Cluster analysis

Clustering was performed using the k-means algorithm applied to the first 10 PCA components, a method commonly used in comparative health system analyses due to its scalability and interpretability.29 30 Solutions with k=3–6 clusters were evaluated for interpretability, coherence and stability, and a four-cluster solution was selected. Interpretability referred to whether clusters demonstrated clinically and policy-relevant medication profiles. Coherence referred to the internal consistency of medicine-selection patterns within clusters. Stability referred to the reproducibility of clustering patterns across repeated k-means initialisations and alternative cluster solutions. In practice, interpretability was assessed through inspection of whether resulting clusters reflected clinically meaningful and policy-relevant medicine groupings; coherence was evaluated by examining the consistency and distinctness of medicine-selection patterns within each cluster; and stability was assessed by repeating k-means with multiple random initialisations and comparing the consistency of cluster assignments across runs and across values of k.

The four-cluster solution provided the best balance between interpretability, internal coherence and cluster separation. Three-cluster solutions merged clinically distinct medication profiles, whereas five and six-cluster solutions fragmented clusters into smaller groups with limited additional interpretive value. Similar higher level clustering patterns were observed across alternative solutions, supporting the robustness of the selected four-cluster structure.

Cluster validation and separation

Cluster separation and internal consistency were evaluated prior to substantive interpretation. Euclidean distances between cluster centroids and within-cluster sums of squares were examined to assess internal homogeneity31 (detailed validation metrics are reported in online supplemental appendix S2). Differences across clusters in the principal component space were formally tested using multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA), followed by univariate analyses of variance (ANOVAs) for individual components and Tukey-adjusted post-hoc comparisons.32 33 These analyses were used to validate the separation of the final four-cluster solution rather than to determine the number of clusters. Full statistical results for these analyses are provided in online supplemental appendix S3.

Identification of defining medicines

To characterise cluster-specific medicine profiles, defining medicines were identified using ORs derived from 2×2 contingency tables comparing listing within and outside each cluster. ORs were calculated as (a×d)/(b×c), following standard epidemiologic methods.34 35 In this context, the OR expresses how much more likely a medicine is to be included in countries within a given cluster compared with countries outside that cluster.

Medicines were classified as cluster-defining if they were listed by at least one country within the cluster and had an OR ≥20. Medicines listed exclusively within a single cluster were considered defining even when ORs were infinite. The OR approach was used to identify medicines disproportionately associated with specific clusters.36 A conservative threshold was selected pragmatically to focus attention on medicines strongly characteristic of each cluster and reduce inclusion of weakly discriminating medicines.

Comparison with country characteristics

After cluster validation, medicine-based clusters were compared across country-level indicators. Continuous variables were analysed using one-way ANOVA with post-hoc pairwise comparisons where appropriate. Model fit was summarised using R². Categorical variables were analysed using χ2 tests of independence, with effect sizes reported using Cramér’s V.32 In addition, cluster-specific outlier analyses were conducted to identify countries whose characteristics deviated from dominant cluster patterns. For continuous variables, outliers were identified using standard distributional criteria within clusters, whereas for categorical variables, including WHO region, outliers were defined based on under-representation within clusters (≤10% of cluster membership).

Statistical software

All analyses were conducted using Stata/MP V.19 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, Texas).37 Data cleaning, dimensionality reduction, clustering and statistical comparisons were performed within this environment.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting or dissemination of this study.

Results

We included 158 NEMLs from WHO Member States and selected non-member states and territories, including 11 reimbursement lists. Lists ranged in year from 2005 to 2023, and many lists (71 lists, or 44.9%) were released in 2020 or later. Of the 2084 medicines initially identified, 320 (15.4%) were excluded from the cluster analysis due to minimal variation across countries (SD<0.1). Among the remaining 1764 medicines, the number listed per country varied widely, ranging from 39 to 932, with a median of 346 (IQR 277–446) and a mean of 383 (SD 169). Only 33 medicines (1.9%) were listed in at least half of countries, while the majority appeared in fewer than half of NEMLs, resulting in a right-skewed distribution (skewness 1.05).

Each of the 158 countries was assigned to one of four clusters based solely on the medicines listed in their NEML. Cluster 1—defined by the inclusion of anti-parasitic medicines, treatments for neglected diseases, and antisera—included 66 countries (41.8%), mostly from the African and Region of the Americas, with relatively low per-capita health spending. Cluster 2—defined by older medicines used for symptomatic management as well as treatments for chronic non-communicable diseases—included 65 countries (41.1%), mainly from the European and African regions, with a wide range of per-capita health spending. Cluster 3—defined by selected medicines for chronic non-communicable diseases—included 18 countries (11.4%), primarily from the European, Eastern Mediterranean and Region of the Americas, with high per-capita health spending. Cluster 4—defined by newer, higher cost medicines—included nine countries (5.7%), predominantly from the European region, with high per-capita health spending. Medicine listing by cluster

The number of medicines included in each country’s list varied across clusters. Cluster 1 had a median of 394 medicines (IQR 348–440), cluster 2 had 261 medicines (IQR 204–305), cluster 3 had 574 medicines (IQR 506–654) and cluster 4 had 767 medicines (IQR 748–824). Cluster 1 countries had lists most similar to the WHO model Essential Medicines List (EML), while cluster 4 countries were least similar (median differences: 312 for cluster 1, 386 for cluster 2, 494 for cluster 3 and 646 for cluster 4) (see online supplemental figure 1 for the distribution of differences). The distribution of differences varied significantly across clusters (Kruskal-Wallis χ²(3)=99.4, p<0.001).

Cluster-defining medicines were identified as those at least 20 times more likely to be listed in a given cluster than in the other three. Cluster 1 had 223 cluster-defining medicines, cluster 2 had 17, cluster 3 had 94 and cluster 4 had 233. Of 567 cluster-defining medicines, 116 were listed exclusively by countries in a single cluster (12 in cluster 1, none in cluster 2, 21 in cluster 3, 83 in cluster 4). The therapeutic profiles of cluster-defining medicines differed substantially across clusters, with cluster 1 emphasising infectious disease and public-health medicines, cluster 2 older symptomatic therapies, cluster 3 chronic disease therapies and cluster 4 newer oncologic and biologic medicines (table 2). Online supplemental appendix S4 provides the full list of cluster-defining medicines by cluster, with ORs.

Geographic distribution

The geographic distribution of countries differed across medicine-based clusters (Pearson χ (15)=62.7, p<0.001; Cramér’s V=0.36). Cluster 1 included primarily countries from the African Region (48%), followed by the Region of the Americas (20%) and the Eastern Mediterranean Region (15%). Cluster 2 included countries from the Western Pacific (25%), African (22%), European (22%) and Region of the Americas (20%). Cluster 3 included mainly European Region countries (44%), while cluster 4 consisted predominantly of European Region countries (78%) (table 1; figure 2).

Global distribution of medicine-based clusters. World map showing 158 countries coloured by their assigned medicine-based cluster (clusters 1–4). This figure illustrates the geographic spread of countries across clusters, highlighting the predominance of African countries in cluster 1 and a large share of European countries in clusters 3 and 4.

Several countries were clustered with few other countries from the same WHO region, that is, these countries were geographic outliers within their cluster. For example, cluster 1—predominantly composed of African Region countries—included small numbers of countries from the European, South-East Asia, and Western Pacific Regions, while cluster 3 included isolated representation from the African and South-East Asia Regions. Among the 21 countries identified as geographic outliers, 13 (61.9%) had per-capita health spending levels closer to the median of their WHO region than to that of their medicine-based cluster (suggesting that health spending alone did not fully explain their medicine-based clustering).

Economic characteristics

Per-capita health expenditure was lowest in cluster 1 and highest in cluster 4 (mean spending US$240 for cluster 1, US$867 for cluster 2, US$1029 for cluster 3 and US$2878 for cluster 4; figure 3B). Expenditure differed significantly across clusters (one-way ANOVA F(3154)=16.77, p<0.001; R²=0.25). Tukey tests indicated significant differences between cluster 1 and clusters 2–4, and between cluster 4 and clusters 2–3 (p<0.05).

Distribution of country characteristics across medicine-based clusters. Boxplots show distributions of (A) GDP per capita, (B) health expenditure per capita, (C) life expectancy at birth and (D) population size across the four medicine-based clusters. GDP, gross domestic product.

Within-cluster variation was most pronounced in clusters 1 and 2: cluster 1 included very low-spending countries such as Madagascar and Burundi alongside higher-spending countries such as Saudi Arabia and Costa Rica, while cluster 2 included low-spending countries such as Eritrea and the Gambia and high-spending countries including Sweden, Japan and the Republic of Korea. Cluster 3 showed limited dispersion, with Malta identified as a high-spending outlier and Algeria as a low-spending outlier, while no health-expenditure outliers were observed in cluster 4. Among health-spending outliers within clusters (n=25), most (80%) belonged to clusters in which their WHO region accounted for less than 40% of membership, indicating that neither geographic region nor health spending alone fully explains cluster membership (see online supplemental figure 2).

GDP per capita also differed (F(3154)= 27.21; p<0.001; R²=0.35; figure 3A), with substantial heterogeneity in clusters 1 and 2, which included low-income and high-income countries (eg, Burundi and Somalia alongside Saudi Arabia and Trinidad and Tobago in cluster 1; Niger and Sierra Leone alongside Japan, Sweden and Iceland in cluster 2). Full GDP distributions, outlier analyses, and World Bank income classifications are provided in online supplemental appendix S5.

Life expectancy and population size

Life expectancy at birth differed across clusters (F(3154)= 12.55; p<0.001; R²=0.196; figure 2C): cluster 1, 69.6 years; cluster 2, 73.3 years; cluster 3, 76.4 years; cluster 4, 79.4 years. Cluster-level differences were also reflected in individual countries, with several low-life and high-life-expectancy outliers observed within clusters (see online supplemental appendix S4 for detailed analyses and outlier identification). A similar pattern was observed for the HAQ Index (see online supplemental appendix S4).

Population size did not differ across clusters (F(3,154)= 0.31; p=0.815) (figure 3D), with wide variation within clusters, including very large countries (India, China, Brazil) and microstates (Cabo Verde, Niue, Tuvalu). Full population distributions and identified outliers are provided in online supplemental appendix S5. Figure 3 presents Boxplots of these country characteristics across clusters.

Discussion

Interpretation of main findings

Countries can be meaningfully grouped based on the medicines prioritised in their NEMLs. These empirically derived groupings suggest that conventional geographic and economic classifications may mask meaningful structure in medicine selection, with important implications for policy benchmarking and cross-country comparison. Medicine-based clustering reveals patterns not fully explained by geography or health spending, although the four clusters differ in regional representation and median health expenditure. Cluster 1 lists are most similar to the WHO Model EML, whereas cluster 4 emphasises newer, higher cost medicines. These findings suggest that while NEML content aligns broadly with WHO regional and economic patterns, medicine-selection priorities may diverge from these expectations in some settings.

Medicine-based clustering may provide a practical framework for policy learning among countries facing similar pharmaceutical prioritisation decisions despite differing geography or income level. Clustering based on NEML content provides a framework for countries to assess whether their medicine lists align with national objectives and values. Countries within the same cluster could use these groupings for benchmarking, collaborative horizon scanning, shared formulary development or joint procurement initiatives. For example, several European Region countries in cluster 1—such as Armenia, the Republic of Moldova and Uzbekistan—selected medicines closely aligned with the WHO Model EML despite higher GDP per capita and health spending compared with other cluster 1 countries. Similarly, Australia and Oman were geographic outliers within cluster 4, which otherwise consisted predominantly of European Region countries. Their inclusion in cluster 4 suggests convergence in medicine-selection patterns across geographically distinct high-income health systems with similar capacities to adopt newer and higher cost therapies. Countries that appear as geographic or economic outliers within a cluster may wish to reassess whether deviations from regional norms reflect deliberate policy choices, local population needs or external pressures influencing medicine selection.

Countries in cluster 2 were characterised by shorter, heterogeneous NEMLs; the median number of listed medicines was 261, substantially lower than the overall median. For example, Sri Lanka and Bangladesh listed 188 and 253 medicines, respectively. This profile may warrant closer scrutiny of selection processes to ensure alignment with population health needs. Outliers across clusters may indicate either policy innovation or misalignment, providing opportunities for targeted technical assistance, peer learning and future WHO Model List revisions.

Overall, NEMLs exhibit substantial heterogeneity. Outlier analyses suggest that countries diverging from regional norms often had health spending closer to their regional median than to their assigned cluster, whereas countries whose per-capita health spending fell outside the cluster-specific 10th–90th percentile range were frequently grouped with clusters under-represented in their region. These observations may reflect differences in therapeutic prioritisation, approaches to adopting newer medicines and broader pharmaceutical policy and decision-making processes (such as formulary design and reimbursement frameworks), which likely shape how countries translate the WHO Model List into NEMLs.

Comparison with previous literature

Previous studies of essential medicines policy have largely focused on alignment with the WHO Model List, availability of tracer medicines or comparisons stratified by income or region.1416 Our findings extend this literature by showing that countries cluster empirically based on what they list, revealing cross-cutting patterns that are not fully captured by conventional geographic or economic groupings. Clusters differed in medicine profiles, from infectious and neglected diseases to chronic or specialised conditions, reflecting health system priorities, governance, financing and political decision-making rather than a uniform progression. These patterns align with prior work on how national policy and institutional capacity shape essential medicines decisions.12 13 38

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. We compiled a large, standardised NEML database, applied robust multivariate methods and combined internal validation with external comparisons across multiple country characteristics, capturing meaningful health-system variation that is not fully explained by geography or income. The use of a stringent OR threshold to define cluster-specific medicines improves interpretability by emphasising strongly characteristic medicines.

Several limitations should be noted. NEMLs reflect policy intent rather than actual availability, procurement or use of medicines; inclusion on a list does not guarantee access in practice. A further limitation is that the function of NEMLs may differ across settings. In many low and middle-income countries, NEMLs serve as explicit prioritisation tools, whereas in several high-income countries—particularly in Europe—medicines are defined through reimbursement lists or publicly funded benefit packages. These are conceptually related but incorporate additional considerations such as health technology assessment and reimbursement policy. As a result, clustering among high-income countries may partly reflect coverage and reimbursement processes rather than essential medicines prioritisation alone.

Variables directly reflecting governance and medicine-selection processes—such as frequency of NEML revision, linkage to standard treatment guidelines, committee structure, procurement mechanisms, reimbursement policies and implementation processes—were not consistently available across countries and therefore could not be systematically incorporated into the analysis. As a result, country-level comparisons focused primarily on broad demographic, economic and health-system indicators. Future work could examine whether institutional and governance-related factors explain additional variation in medicine-based clustering.

All medicines were treated equally, without weighting by clinical importance, level of care or therapeutic redundancy. Analyses were conducted at the INN level and therefore did not account for differences in formulations, strengths or indication-specific listings. As a result, potentially important distinctions between products may not have been captured. While some formulation differences may be clinically minor (eg, tablet vs capsule formulations), others may substantially affect indications, pricing, procurement or feasibility of use, particularly for biologics, paediatric formulations and oncology medicines. Some cluster-defining medicines were listed by very few countries, resulting in small cell counts and potentially unstable OR estimates, and the magnitude of these ORs should, therefore, be interpreted with caution. PCA was applied to binary data, which, while common in similar applications, may not capture all nuances of colisting patterns. Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes assessment of how clusters evolve over time in response to epidemiological or policy changes.

Conclusions and future research

Countries can be meaningfully grouped based on the medicines they prioritise, revealing medicine-based clusters that only partially overlap with traditional geographic and economic classifications. These clusters reflect distinct health system orientations, from infectious disease–focused to advanced specialty care. This approach offers a novel lens for comparative analysis, with applications in policy benchmarking, peer learning and global health planning. Future research could examine how NEMLs evolve over time, link cluster membership to health outcomes or access measures and integrate data on medicine availability, pricing and reimbursement to better understand how policy intent translates into real-world access.
