Emission rates of respirable crystalline silica and dust from engineered stone fabrication
Authors: Saisattha Noomnual, Zheyuan Zhuang, Rachael M Jones
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEngineered stone, a composite material containing high levels of crystalline silica, has led to a surge in silicosis among workers who perform cutting, grinding, and other finishing tasks. To improve our understanding of respirable crystalline silica (RCS) and respirable dust exposures and exposure determinants for these tasks, this study extracted data from previously published studies and (i) calculated the emission rate of respirable dust and RCS (mass per time) from experimental simulation studies, (ii) assessed whether control technology or task are determinants of emission rate, and (iii) applied a 2-zone mathematical model to estimate RCS exposures using the calculated emission rates and evaluated the model predictions against task-duration exposure measurements. Emission rates were calculated from 4 published experimental simulation studies, and regression analysis showed that emission rates of RCS were positively associated with slab crystalline silica content and negatively associated with the use of control technologies. Near-field RCS concentrations predicted by model tended to underestimate exposures, but the distribution overlapped with field-based exposure measurements. The model performance is reasonable, though further simulation studies or field-based studies with detailed exposure determinant data can be used to improve emission rate estimates and model performance. The emission rates calculated in this study can be used to reconstruct past exposures for epidemiologic and risk analyses, and assist with attribution of silicosis and lung cancer to work-related causes. A multifaceted approach is necessary to ensure that employers are providing workplaces that eliminate hazardous exposures to RCS and other components of engineered stone.

Respirable crystalline silica exposures from processing of engineered stone countertops continues to be a global occupational health challenge. This study calculates emission rates of respirable crystalline silica and dust from cutting and grinding tasks, with and without controls. These emission rates enable exposure modeling and reconstruction for some high-exposure tasks, like dry cutting, that have not been well characterized in the field.

Introduction

Engineered stone (ES), also known as artificial stone, is a manufactured composite material that is widely used for countertops. Although the composition of ES varies, most products comprise small quartz-containing particles bound with resin and mixed with pigments and other materials to achieve the desired appearance (Ramkissoon et al. 2024). The crystalline silica content (CSC) of ES often exceeds 50% (Kumarasamy et al. 2022), which means that respirable crystalline silica (RCS) is released into the air during cutting, grinding, and polishing the stone. RCS exposures have been found to exceed regulatory occupational exposure limits, particularly when tasks are performed with hand tools or without water suppression (Salamon et al. 2021; Surasi et al. 2022; Weller et al. 2024). Unsurprisingly, given the exposure levels, workers in this industry have experienced high rates of silicosis, characterized by disease onset at early age and rapid progression (León-Jiménez et al. 2020; Hoy et al. 2022; Fazio et al. 2023). The burden of this preventable occupational disease in Australia led that country to ban the use, supply, and manufacture of ES, defined as an artificial product containing at least 1% crystalline silica by weight (Kirby 2024). In the United States, the California Department of Industrial Relations updated the Respirable Crystalline Standard to require the use of wet methods and powered air-purifying respirators for “high-exposure trigger tasks,” such as grinding ES that contains >0.1% crystalline silica by weight, among other provisions (8 California Code of Regulations §5204).

There has been a growing literature about occupational exposures associated with ES countertop processing—the cutting, grinding, and polishing slabs to the desired size and shape—that has included field-based exposure monitoring and laboratory experimentation. The subject has warranted literature reviews, but these have been limited to the qualitative synthesis of exposure data (Mandler et al. 2023; Ramkissoon et al. 2024). Few exposure data are known to have been collected during tasks performed without engineering controls, which are the highest exposure tasks, because by the time exposure monitoring became more broadly implemented in this industry, many shops had already implemented some type of controls, and information on work conditions during exposure monitoring has been limited in many studies. Glass et al. (2022) demonstrated that the proportion of time spent processing ES and time spent working without water suppression systems were strongly correlated with silicosis risk, which affirms an exposure–response relationship in the absence of measured exposures for some high-exposure tasks.

The overall goal of this study is to describe the emission rate of RCS and respirable dust (RD) from ES during processing activities, and how the emission rates vary among potential exposure determinants. The specific objectives are to (i) calculate the emission rate of RD and RCS (mass per time) from experimental simulation studies, (ii) identify whether control technology or task are determinants of emission rate, and (iii) apply a 2-zone mathematical model to estimate RCS exposures using the calculated emission rates and evaluate the model predictions against task-duration exposure measurements. The emission rate and mathematical model demonstrated will enable reconstruction of past exposures for epidemiologic or risk analyses and inform the selection of control strategies.

Method

Literature review

A scoping literature review was implemented to identify articles that contain data related to the emission of RCS or RD or could be used to evaluate exposure model predictions (Tricco et al. 2018). The PubMed and Web of Science databases were searched using the string: (“artificial stone” OR “engineered stone”) AND (silica OR silicosis OR quartz). Searches were first performed in both databases on 21 December 2022 and updated several times through 10 January 2025. Identified references were imported into Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia). In addition, NIOSH Health Hazard Evaluation reports were searched in March 2025.

Inclusion criteria were: (i) airborne contaminant concentrations were measured during experimental simulation or in workplaces and (ii) the study involved cutting, grinding, polishing, or other aerosol-generating activities involving ES. Exclusion criteria were: (i) no original quantitative measurements of airborne contaminants were reported, (ii) study did not involve crystalline silica-containing ES, and (iii) study was not about ES processing. Titles and abstracts were screened against the criteria by 2 investigators, and disagreements were resolved by discussion. Full-text articles were similarly screened. Included articles were not formally reviewed for quality as there are not standard criteria to quality review of this body of exposure science literature, and such assessment was not required for the study objectives. Data extracted from the included studies included citation information, work activity, tools used, control technologies in place, stone type, silica content of ES, emission rates and measured concentrations of RCS and RD, number of samples/replicates, sampling methods, task duration, and size and ventilation rates of experimental chambers or work areas.

Calculation of emission rates

The emission rate, denoted G, was defined as the mass of RCS or RD released into the air per unit time (mg/min). Emission rates were determined from experimental simulation studies. The approach differed depending upon the way data were reported. Briefly, when the concentration of material in air was reported, G was calculated using the 2-zone model at steady state:

G=CN×βQQ+β , where CN is the time-averaged contaminant concentration (mg/m3) measured in a personal breathing zone sample or in the outflow of a small experimental enclosure representing the maximum sustained exposure level for a specific task; Q is the volumetric airflow rate in the experimental chamber (m3/min); and β is the interzonal flow (m3/min) and was calculated as follows: β=πr2×s, where s is airspeed (m/min) (Nicas 2009). One study reported an emission rate normalized to the volume of stone removed, so G was obtained by multiplying the normalized emission rate by the volume of stone removed (Thompson and Qi 2023). Two studies performed experiments in a room-scale unventilated tent and reported neither air exchange rates nor airspeeds (Cooper et al. 2015; Johnson et al. 2017): the air change rate was represented by a uniform distribution over the range of 0.5 to 2/h (Nazaroff 2021), and s was represented by a uniform distribution over a range of 0.6 to 6 m/min corresponding to the calm indoor environment (Baldwin and Maynard 1998; Wilson et al. 2007). Details of the calculations are available in Supplementary material.

Some experimental simulation studies reported exact results for each sample while others reported summary statistics for N samples. When only summary statistics were reported, random sampling was performed to identify N sample values that most closely reproduced the reported summary statistics. Say, the reported summary statistics were mean and standard deviation, {x^,σ^}, and the ith set of randomly selected N samples from a normal distribution with those parameters yields {xi^,σi^}, then the best set of N samples are those that minimize |(x^−xi^)+(σ^−σi^)|. If the summary statistics reported were the geometric mean and geometric standard deviation, the same analyses were performed using those parameters and samples drawn from a lognormal distribution. These sampled N values were then used to calculate G values when the exact original results were not originally reported, and in statistical analyses.

Statistical analyses

In addition to developing summary statistics for G, random effects regression was applied to determine whether G for RCS or RD is the same across the potential determinants of exposure, or not. The random effect was used to represent clustering of results from the same study as there was potential for systematic variation between studies. Control technologies were coded as a series of indicator variables for wetted blade (WB, on-tool water spray), water curtain spray (WC), sheet-flow wetting (SW), and on-tool local exhaust ventilation (LEV). When 2 controls were used together, this was reflected in the 2 corresponding indicator variables. Activity was categorized as cutting or grinding. CSC of the ES was classified as ≤70% or >70%, but use of this variable was limited to graphical assessment owing to uncertainty about this variable. In particular, CSC was measured in bulk dust, which may not correspond directly to the bulk material, or the source of information was not reported. When processing ES, workers have been observed to frequently alternate between activities such as sawing, grinding, or polishing every 5 to 10 min (Phillips et al. 2013), and to perform grinding or cutting continuously for a few minutes followed by moving and measuring stone slabs (Qi and Echt 2021). Though, Weller et al. also reported that some workers performed a single primary activity for 5 to 8 h of a work shift (Weller et al. 2024). Herein, the model was simulated with a task duration (T) of 15 min, but with RCS emission occurring during the first 5 min only to reflect alternation of activities, but other scenarios can be modeled.

A sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the impact of model parameters G, Q, and S on the time-weighted average (TWA) concentration of RCS or RD in the near field, CN, using a point-wise approach (Gottschalk et al. 2010). The mean of each input parameter was calculated and used to calculate CN. The response of time-averaged CN was then assessed by reducing each input parameter by 10% from the mean value. The proportional change in the output variable was divided by the proportional change in the input parameter to compute a sensitivity ratio. This ratio was multiplied by the standard deviation of the input parameter's distribution. Finally, the individual sensitivity values were summed to calculate the total sensitivity, and the percentage contribution of each input parameter to the total sensitivity was computed.

Exposure model evaluation

Modeled CN values were compared with task-duration RCS concentrations measured in the workplaces. Comparisons were not made to shift-duration exposures because that required additional assumptions about worker time–activity patterns, and the focus of this analysis was to evaluate model predictions using the calculated emission rates. Task-duration exposure data were identified through the literature review, and the distribution of exposure data were extracted by activity and control technology from included articles. When multiple studies had exposure data for the same activity and control technology, the lognormal distributions of exposure data were combined via bootstrapping with equal weight applied to each study.

Three methods were used to compare modeled and measured exposures: qualitative graphical comparison, percent difference analysis, and logarithm mean bias (LMB). LMB is a log-scale measure of average bias between predicted and observed values, where positive values indicate overprediction and negative values indicate underprediction that providing a balanced view of model performance across the wide range of values that occur with lognormally distributed data (Ghim et al. 2018). Since the modeled concentrations were the output of a Monte Carlo simulation, the comparisons were based on the Monte Carlo simulation outputs.

Results

Literature review

A total of 303 peer-reviewed articles were identified through the search. After removal of 126 duplicates, 150 titles and abstracts were screened. Twenty-eight articles underwent full-text review, and 16 were excluded, leaving 12 items for data extraction. Reasons for exclusion included: not reporting airborne contaminant concentrations (n = 11), no original data (n = 4), and not in English (n = 1). As the authors were completing the analyses herein, we became aware of 2 additional studies that met our inclusion criteria (DeVaughn et al. 2025; Soo et al. 2025). Three NIOSH Health Hazard Evaluations were identified and were the only studies to report task-duration exposure measurements, so these were used for model evaluation (Qi and Echt 2016, 2021). Among the included peer-reviewed articles, 4 were experimental simulations and were used to determine emission rates (Cooper et al. 2015; Johnson et al. 2017; Carrieri et al. 2020; Thompson and Qi 2023), while the remaining studies reported shift-duration exposure measurements in workplaces.

The sensitivity analysis reveals that G is the dominant factor influencing RCS CN when no controls are used, accounting for 89% and 90% of the total sensitivity for cutting and grinding, respectively, and G is positively correlated with CN (Table S6). Airspeed and air exchange rate contribute less than 10% to the total sensitivity, and, as expected, are negatively correlated with CN. In contrast, airspeed becomes the most influential parameter when controls are used, accounting for approximately 60% of the total sensitivity for cutting and grinding.

Figure S5 and Table 5 compare the distributions of modeled RCS concentrations in the near-field and personal task-duration exposure measurements measured in workplaces: Only data for controls are presented because no task-duration exposure measurements were identified without controls. Qualitatively, the exposures measured in the field fall within the distribution of modeled values, but the model tends to underestimate RCS concentrations for grinding and other tasks, with percent differences of 76% and 79%, respectively. The corresponding LMBs are −1.46 for grinding and −1.58 for other tasks.

Discussion

While the burden of silicosis and disease severity among ES processing workers has led to significant efforts to measure workplace exposures in this emergent industry (summarized in Tables S4 and S5), gaps in knowledge remain about exposures owing to the inability to measure exposures in all combinations of exposure determinants, and lack of information about potential exposure determinants associated with exposure measurements. For example, there are no field-based exposure measurements explicitly associated with dry activities, only experimental simulations (Carrieri et al. 2020; Thompson and Qi 2023). This may reflect that more workplaces are implementing controls, but it is a limitation for exposure reconstruction as this high-intensity exposure has not been well characterized. Mathematical modeling is a tool that can help to bridge such knowledge gaps (Nicas and Jayjock 2002). To that end, this study calculated emission rates for RCS and RD from published experimental simulation studies involving ES processing activities and utilized those emission rates to assess potential exposure determinants and model exposures using a 2-zone model.

Emission rates for RCS and RD were calculated from 4 studies (Table 2), and differences were observed in the emission rates among the studies (Figs. S1 and S2). This highlights the influence of experimental conditions on study findings that can arise from the design of the experimental chamber, the tools and control technology used, and the task itself, in addition to random variability. Variability in tools, activities and environments among workplaces can be wide, leading to variability in exposures (Surasi et al. 2022) (Table S4). Interstudy differences in experimental simulation may capture some of the variability encountered in workplaces, but can make it a challenge to isolate the effect of potential exposure determinants. Nonetheless, this study was able to describe the effect of control technologies on the emission rates of RCS and RD (Fig. S3; Tables 4 and S3).

A specific aspect of experimental study design relevant to the calculation of emission rates is the location at which the RCS or RD concentration were measured. For example, measurement in the breathing zone of a worker does not indicate that well-mixed conditions have been met in the experimental chamber. Therefore, the 2-zone model was used to calculate emission rates, and owing to the fine nature of respirable silica and the workers’ proximity to the emission source, a localized steady state was assumed to be reached rapidly after emission began. Consequently, the time-averaged concentration measured in the workers’ breathing zone, representing a steady-state near-field concentration, provides a valid and representative measure of the sustained exposure level for a specific activity during the sampling period. However, the different measurement locations used to calculate the emission rates are one contributor to uncertainty in this analysis. Calculations of the emission rates required assumptions about the ventilation and air speed conditions in the test chambers, which introduced additional uncertainty into the estimates.

RCS concentrations predicted by the 2-zone model are relatively similar to task-duration exposures measured in workplaces (Fig. S5). During processing activities with controls, the geometric mean RCS concentrations for both cutting and grinding were 4- to 6-fold higher than the geometric mean values measured in the field studies (Table 5). Jayjock et al. (2011) provided several examples where 2-zone model predictions were within 2-fold of measured values, though this is not a strict criterion for evaluation of model performance.

A strength of the modeling application demonstrated is the use of Monte Carlo simulation, which enabled pooling of emission rates across studies in addition to incorporating variability and uncertainty in model parameters to calculate exposure concentrations. The method also enables sensitivity analysis, which indicated that G is a key determinant of RCS concentrations in the near field when controls are absent, and S is a key determinant of RCS concentrations in the near field when the controls are present. This difference is likely the result of the fact that when the emission rate is low, the influence of other variables can become more obvious.

There are several next steps to improve the emission rate estimates and the accuracy and precision of the resulting exposure model estimates. Semple et al. (2003) have suggested that calibrating emission rates based on field data can be used to improve model accuracy, which is a potential strategy for emission rates associated with processing ES. Additional experimental simulation studies could be performed to fill in gaps about different combinations of activities and control strategies, as well as explore the influence of different control technology types within each broader category of controls. Further, field-based exposure measurement studies should improve the collection of information about potential exposure determinants and model parameters so that the data have increased utility in complementary research and greater interpretability.

A combination of engineering controls, regulatory measures, and education for employers and workers is essential to effectively reduce RCS exposure associated with ES if the product cannot be eliminated from workplaces. The potential impact of wet methods, such as on-tool water systems or water curtains that flood the cutting surface, on RCS exposures have been demonstrated in this analysis, experimental studies, and field-based exposure measurements (Tables 5, S1, and S4) when used alone or with LEV systems. The statistical analyses suggest that wet methods and on-tool LEV significantly reduce RCS and RD emission rates (Tables 4 and S3). Both wet-based and LEV control strategies require ongoing maintenance, such that employers will need to maintain their systems and perform ongoing exposure monitoring to ensure effective control functioning. Uncertainty about the adequacy and long-term effectiveness of control technologies contributed to the new Cal/OSHA requirement for use of powered air-purifying respirators regardless of measured exposure levels. In contrast, Australia eliminated this hazard by banning ES (Hore-Lacy et al. 2025).

To illustrate the real-world implications of this work, modeled task-duration exposures were considered in a hypothetical work scenario to estimate 8-h TWA exposures. Consider a worker who performs grinding tasks—grinding for a few minutes then moving and measuring stone slabs—over an 8-h shift with 1.5 h of breaks and 6.5 h of active work (Qi and Echt 2016). In the absence of controls, the modeled geometric mean 15-min task-duration RCS exposure is 0.71 mg/m3 (Table 5) and yields an 8-h TWA of 0.58 mg/m3 ([0.71 mg/m3 × 6.5 h]/8 h= 0.58 mg/m3). And in the presence of controls, the modeled geometric mean 15-min task-duration RCS exposure is 0.02 mg/m3 (Table 5) and yields an 8-h TWA of 0.02 mg/m3. The 8-h TWA exposure estimate with controls is relatively consistent with field-based exposure studies, which report mean 8-h RCS TWA of 0.02 to 0.09 mg/m3 when wet methods have been implemented (Weller et al. 2024; DeVaughn et al. 2025; Soo et al. 2025); and is about 40% of the OSHA RCS Permissible Exposure Limit (PEL) of 0.05 mg/m3. In contrast, the estimated 8-h TWA exposure in the absence of controls is nearly 12 times higher than the OSHA PEL. Studies have found lifetime exposures to RCS of as little as 0.4 mg/m3-years to be associated with increased risks of lung cancer (Ge et al. 2020; Rey-Brandariz et al. 2023), indicating that increase cancer risks persist, even in the presence of controls, among workers who pursue this career.

Silicosis will continue to be diagnosed in growing numbers among ES workers in California and worldwide, owing the latency of the disease; and lung cancer diagnoses can also be anticipated. Therefore, it is important to continue to implement workplace-based strategies to limit exposures if ES products cannot be banned, and work to ensure that appropriate medical care and social services are provided to workers suffering from these preventable work-related diseases.

Conclusions

The emission rates of RCS and RD calculated in this study from cutting and grinding ES with various control technologies can be used to reconstruct exposures among workers. While exposure levels modeled from the calculated emission rates align well with measured task-duration exposures, improvements may be possible with additional data about emissions and determinants of exposures. The findings affirm that engineering controls may reduce workers’ exposures during ES processing tasks, but that risks remain for workers with repeated exposures over months and years. Effective retrospective exposure assessment is needed to ensure that silicosis and lung cancer among workers is correctly attributed to their work with ES.
