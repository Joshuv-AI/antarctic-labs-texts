Indirect Comparison of Nipocalimab Versus Efgartigimod and Rozanolixizumab in the Treatment of Generalized Myasthenia Gravis
Authors: Saiju Jacob, Mahmoud Hashim, Brian Hutton, Kavita Gandhi, Suzy Van Sanden, Rafal Slowik, Christopher Drudge, Antoine C El Khoury, Mi Jun Keng, Sumeet Singh, Sindhu Ramchandren, Nils Erik Gilhus
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNipocalimab, efgartigimod, and rozanolixizumab (the last two cyclically dosed) are approved neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) blockers for treating generalized myasthenia gravis (gMG). No trials have directly compared these therapies; hence, indirect treatment comparisons (ITCs) were conducted to evaluate their relative efficacy.MethodsMatching-adjusted indirect comparisons (MAICs) and Bucher ITCs were used to compare nipocalimab vs. efgartigimod and rozanolixizumab for changes from baseline (CFB) in Myasthenia Gravis Activities of Daily Living (MG-ADL) total score observed in their phase 3 registration trials. Bucher ITCs used the relative treatment effect vs. placebo. As there was considerable cross-trial heterogeneity, including uncertainty in using the placebo arm as a common comparator, active treatment arms were used in unanchored MAICs. MG-ADL CFB was compared between trials (1) at multiple timepoints to evaluate onset of action and disease control over time, and (2) using area under the curve (AUC) as a measure of cumulative effect normalized per week of follow-up.ResultsIn both Bucher ITCs and MAICs, nipocalimab had a comparable MG-ADL CFB at week 1 vs. the other FcRn blockers. In MAICs, MG-ADL CFB was significantly greater with nipocalimab vs. efgartigimod at week 8 sustained up to 24 weeks (p < 0.05), and vs. rozanolixizumab at week 10 sustained up to 14 weeks (p < 0.05); results numerically favored nipocalimab in corresponding Bucher ITCs. Using normalized AUC, MG-ADL CFB with nipocalimab was significantly greater in MAICs (p < 0.05) and numerically greater in Bucher ITCs vs. the other FcRn blockers.ConclusionsSustained disease control is an important consideration in managing chronic diseases with fluctuating symptoms such as gMG. Study results showed that nipocalimab provided a comparable onset of action and consistent and sustained disease control that was numerically or statistically significantly greater (depending on ITC method) when compared with the symptom-based cyclic FcRn blockers efgartigimod and rozanolixizumab.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-026-03543-1.Keywords: Nipocalimab, Rozanolixizumab, Efgartigimod, Generalized myasthenia gravis, Indirect treatment comparison, Matching-adjusted indirect comparison

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Head-to-head clinical trials are not available evaluating the relative efficacy of neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) blockers (nipocalimab, efgartigimod, and rozanolixizumab) in generalized myasthenia gravis (gMG), so indirect treatment comparisons (ITCs) are an important means of evaluating their relative efficacy.Conducting ITCs in gMG comes with inherent challenges related to differences in the design and populations of available trials. The use of multiple approaches to ITCs and the outcomes being compared can help address these challenges. What was learned from the study? Nipocalimab provided a comparable onset of action and consistent and sustained disease control that was numerically or statistically significantly greater (depending on ITC method) vs. symptom-based cyclic FcRn blockers.On the basis of unanchored matching-adjusted indirect comparisons, nipocalimab had greater reductions in change from baseline in Myasthenia Gravis Activities of Daily Living total score at multiple timepoints (week 8 to week 24 for comparison vs. efgartigimod, and week 10 to week 14 for comparisons vs. rozanolixizumab); results numerically favored nipocalimab in corresponding Bucher ITCs.

Introduction

Generalized myasthenia gravis (gMG) is a chronic autoimmune disease of the neuromuscular junction (NMJ). Patients with gMG experience muscle weakness due to impaired neuromuscular transmission, caused by antibodies to proteins found at the NMJ [1]. Muscle weakness can lead to myasthenic crisis, which requires immediate intensive-care hospitalization and can be fatal [2]. Patients with gMG may experience unpredictable fluctuations in muscle weakness that affect their daily activities and reduce their quality of life [3]. The clinical and economic burden of gMG is substantial [3, 4].

Sustained disease control is the key goal of gMG treatment [5]. Patients with gMG typically start on conventional standard of care (SOC) therapies, with other advanced therapies added to SOC if disease control is inadequate [6–8]. These add-on therapies include the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) blockers nipocalimab, efgartigimod, and rozanolixizumab. Nipocalimab was assessed in the phase 3, double-blind, placebo-controlled randomized controlled trial (RCT) VIVACITY-MG3 (NCT04951622) [9]. This trial showed that nipocalimab plus SOC provided clinically meaningful and statistically significant improvement and sustained efficacy vs. placebo plus SOC over the 24-week treatment period, while demonstrating an acceptable safety profile [9].

The VIVACITY-MG3 trial supported the approval of nipocalimab for the treatment of gMG by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in April 2025 and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) in November 2025 [10, 11]. Efgartigimod and rozanolixizumab were assessed in the phase 3 registration RCTs ADAPT (NCT03669588) [12] and MycarinG (NCT03971422) [13], respectively. These therapies have been approved by the FDA and EMA for use in gMG [14–17].

Whereas nipocalimab is administered on a continuous basis every 2 weeks, the FDA and EMA labels for efgartigimod and rozanolixizumab specify a symptom-based cyclic schedule with subsequent treatment cycles being administered as needed according to clinical evaluation [14–17]. This is reflective of the dosing schedules in the phase 3 registration RCTs for these therapies: nipocalimab every 2 weeks for 24 weeks (VIVACITY-MG3) [9]; up to three 4-week cycles of efgartigimod over 26 weeks with a cycle repeated as needed no sooner than 8 weeks after the start of the previous cycle (ADAPT) [12]; and one 6-week cycle of rozanolixizumab followed by an 8-week observation period (MycarinG) [13].

No head-to-head RCTs comparing nipocalimab, efgartigimod, and rozanolixizumab for gMG are available; in each case the phase 3 registration trial was placebo controlled, with all patients receiving background SOC regardless of the treatment group. In the absence of head-to-head trials, comparative efficacy can be assessed by conducting an indirect treatment comparison (ITC) to compare relative treatment effects between trials. ITC methods are commonly used to support health technology assessment (HTA) reviews, guideline development, and clinical decision-making [18, 19].

The selection of appropriate ITC methods should be informed by the available evidence and tailored to the challenges encountered in a particular disease and treatment space. A systematic literature review and ITC feasibility assessment was previously undertaken by the authors to evaluate the technical possibility of conducting ITC analyses for gMG treatments using trials of add-on therapies to conventional SOC [20]. Three key challenges for conducting ITCs were identified along with mitigation strategies. First, ITCs of change from baseline (CFB) outcomes restricted to the primary assessment timepoint reported in each trial may produce results that may be poorly representative of the treatment effect since it is not stable over time for all treatments (e.g., using only the primary assessment timepoint for cyclical treatments does not represent disease control when off treatment). This can be partially mitigated by conducting ITCs of CFB at multiple timepoints and analyzing cumulative treatment effects. Multiple timepoint analyses can highlight differences in efficacy onset and consistency of disease control which are relevant to patients and prescribing physicians in managing a chronic disease such as gMG with fluctuating symptoms, whereas an area under the curve (AUC) approach can compare the net treatment effect over time that may be less patient relevant and more appropriate for economic modeling purposes. Second, populations differ between trials in gMG. This can be mitigated by conducting matching-adjusted indirect comparisons (MAICs) to adjust for imbalances in baseline patient characteristics between trials [21, 22]. Third, there is considerable uncertainty as to whether the assumptions underlying anchored ITCs related to a common comparator (placebo) are valid given between-trial differences in placebo characteristics including route of drug administration, dosing schedules, and background therapies as well as unexplained differences in absolute response rates in placebo arms across trials. If these assumptions are invalid, then anchored ITCs may not be appropriate. This can be mitigated by conducting unanchored MAICs, which do not use placebo arms as an anchor and adjust for baseline imbalances in patient characteristics of active arms. Bucher ITCs can also be considered to supplement MAICs [23]; although limited in this setting by requiring placebo arms as an anchor, they are a simple and widely used ITC method. Taken together, multiple approaches to ITCs and the outcomes being compared are advised to ensure a robust understanding of the comparative efficacy of gMG therapies. The importance of considering multiple ITC approaches has also been highlighted in other disease areas [24].

The objective of this study was to evaluate and compare disease control over time with nipocalimab vs. efgartigimod and rozanolixizumab for the treatment of gMG. This was accomplished by conducting ITCs to understand comparative effectiveness under the specific assumptions of these analyses. Based on a previously published feasibility assessment [20], multiple approaches to ITCs and the outcomes being compared were undertaken.

Methods

Overview

Two pairwise ITC methods, MAIC and Bucher ITC, were used to estimate the relative treatment effect of nipocalimab 15 mg/kg (maintenance dose) vs. efgartigimod 10 mg/kg and rozanolixizumab 7 mg/kg and 10 mg/kg in adults with gMG [21–23]. Unanchored ITC methods were preferred because of the considerable uncertainty as to whether the assumptions underlying anchored comparisons related to a common comparator (placebo) were valid. To address concerns regarding the comparability of the placebo arms between trials, the MAICs were unanchored, i.e., active treatment arms with background SOC therapies were compared directly without use of the placebo arms as an anchor. For these MAICs, patients in VIVACITY-MG3 were weighted so the characteristics of the weighted patient population matched those of the comparator trial population. In addition, Bucher ITCs were conducted to generate comparative efficacy estimates anchored via placebo, without population adjustment. Although these analyses were anchored, they are potentially more biased than unanchored MAICs because of cross-trial differences in placebo arm characteristics and response.

This study used only data from previously conducted clinical trials, so institutional review board approval was not required.

Data Sources

The ITCs were conducted using the phase 3 registration trials VIVACITY-MG3 (nipocalimab), ADAPT (efgartigimod), and MycarinG (rozanolixizumab 7 mg/kg and 10 mg/kg) [9, 12, 13, 25]. Records for these trials were identified via a systemic literature review [20]. Key characteristics of these trials are summarized in Table 1. The MAICs used individual patient data (IPD) for VIVACITY-MG3 and aggregate data for the other FcRn blocker trials; only aggregate trial data informed the Bucher ITCs. Outcome data up to week 24 were used for comparisons of nipocalimab and efgartigimod to align with the maximum timepoint of the VIVACITY-MG3 trial. For comparisons with rozanolixizumab, data up to the maximum timepoint of the MycarinG trial (14 weeks) were used.

For all ITCs, trial populations were aligned on MG-associated autoantibody. The anti-acetylcholine receptor antibody-positive (AChR+) subgroups of VIVACITY-MG3 and ADAPT were used for comparisons of nipocalimab and efgartigimod, as the primary analysis in ADAPT was for this subgroup. For comparisons of nipocalimab and rozanolixizumab, the subgroups of patients in VIVACITY-MG3 who were either AChR+ or anti-muscle-specific tyrosine kinase antibody-positive (MuSK+) were used to align with the eligibility criteria for the MycarinG rozanolixizumab trial.

Outcomes

We examined the outcome change from baseline (CFB) in Myasthenia Gravis Activities of Daily Living (MG-ADL) score. This outcome is commonly and consistently reported for RCTs evaluating therapies in gMG. Reported observed data were used for all three trials to ensure comparability. Two approaches were used for MG-ADL CFB: (1) a multiple timepoint approach to determine the CFB at multiple timepoints of interest to evaluate the onset of treatment effect and disease control over time, and (2) an AUC approach to compare cumulative treatment effect over time, normalized per week of follow-up. Details of each approach are provided in the supplementary material.

MAIC Methods

Analyses were conducted using R and SAS based on the methods developed by Signorovitch et al. [21] and as implemented by Technical Support Document 18 from the Decision Support Unit of the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence [22]. The supplementary material provides details of the statistical methods underlying the MAICs. For the MAICs, patient-level data from the nipocalimab arm of VIVACITY-MG3 that matched with other FcRn blocker trials on type of MG autoantibody were reweighted to adjust for imbalances in baseline characteristics that were identified as important prognostic factors (PFs) and were available for both VIVACITY-MG3 and the comparator trial.

A comprehensive and transparent approach to identifying important PFs for adjustment in MAICs is critical to minimize bias introduced by cross-trial differences in trial populations; however, there is a lack of clear guidance on this. A de novo multistep selection process involving a systematic literature review (SLR) and clinical expert elicitation exercise was developed and used to identify important PFs in gMG, described in detail in the supplementary material.

It was necessary to balance between adjusting for multiple baseline patient characteristics so trial populations became more similar (thus reducing bias) and not reducing the effective sample size (ESS) so far as to end up with uncertain results. The following baseline patient characteristics identified as important PFs were adjusted for in the MAICs: % AChR+ (comparisons with rozanolixizumab only since efgartigimod data were for a fully AChR+ population), time since diagnosis, % female, geographic region (comparisons with rozanolixizumab only), history of corticosteroids, history of nonsteroidal immunosuppressant therapy, MG-ADL score, and Quantitative Myasthenia Gravis score.

Estimates of the comparative efficacy of nipocalimab vs. the other FcRn blockers were derived using the estimated MG-ADL CFB for nipocalimab in the reweighted VIVACITY-MG3 trial and the estimated MG-ADL CFB for the other FcRn blocker based on published aggregate data for its trial.

Bucher ITC Methods

Although unanchored ITC methods were preferred because of the uncertainty related to assumptions underlying anchored comparisons, Bucher ITCs were also conducted as an alternative ITC approach. All analyses were conducted based on the methods developed by Bucher et al. [23]. Estimates of the comparative efficacy of nipocalimab vs. each other FcRn blocker were estimated by comparing the treatment effect vs. placebo based on the published summary-level data from the VIVACITY-MG3 trial, and the treatment effect vs. placebo based on the published summary-level data from the comparator trial.

Results

Adjustment Details of MAICs

Baseline characteristics of patients with gMG before and after adjustment in MAICs are shown in the supplementary material. After adjustment, the ESS for the nipocalimab group of VIVACITY-MG3 (initial sample size of 63 patients with AChR+ and 75 patients with AChR+/MuSK+) was 40, 43, and 39 (corresponding to reductions of 36.5%, 42.7%, and 48.0% from the initial sample size) for comparisons with efgartigimod, rozanolixizumab 7 mg/kg, and rozanolixizumab 10 mg/kg, respectively.

ITCs Using Normalized AUC

Results of the pairwise ITCs comparing nipocalimab vs. efgartigimod and rozanolixizumab (7 mg/kg and 10 mg/kg) regarding mean MG-ADL CFB (normalized AUC) are shown in Figs. 2, 3, and 4. In the unanchored MAICs, the mean difference in MG-ADL CFB (normalized AUC) was statistically significantly greater with nipocalimab vs. efgartigimod, rozanolixizumab 7 mg/kg, and rozanolixizumab 10 mg/kg (p < 0.05). For the Bucher ITCs, the differences in MG-ADL CFB between nipocalimab and efgartigimod, rozanolixizumab 7 mg/kg, and rozanolixizumab 10 mg/kg were non-significant, with nipocalimab being numerically favored. All treatments were better than placebo and had a similar effect size based on normalized AUC (Fig. S1).

Discussion

This study assessed the comparative efficacy between nipocalimab and other approved FcRn blockers for gMG in the absence of direct head-to-head comparative trials. ITCs of relevant RCTs were performed using the outcome of CFB in MG-ADL. The validity of ITCs depends on the homogeneity of included trials. Prior to conducting the analyses, we undertook a thorough feasibility assessment to evaluate the similarity of trials [20].

Cross-trial heterogeneity was identified as a key challenge when conducting ITCs in gMG, as RCTs differed with respect to their populations, treatment strategies and dosing schedules, and outcome assessment definitions and timepoints. The use of MAICs permitted adjustment for cross-trial heterogeneity, to address observed heterogeneity in potential PFs. While the Bucher ITCs are typically used when an anchor is available, in this case results should be interpreted with caution due to potential bias from differences in the placebo arms and baseline characteristics between trials. Unanchored MAICs may be less biased than Bucher ITCs (and other anchored analyses) in this setting because they do not use placebo arms.

An unanchored approach was used to address observed differences in the administration of placebo across trials, such as dosing frequency, route of administration, and symptom-based cyclic vs. continuous dosing, as well as differences in background SOC therapies and placebo response. As a result of these differences, there was considerable uncertainty as to whether the assumptions underlying anchored comparisons related to a common comparator were valid. Observed differences between the Bucher ITC and unanchored MAIC results underscore the importance of critically considering the comparability of placebo arms across trials.

We conducted ITCs at multiple timepoints and using a normalized AUC approach. Multiple timepoint analyses can highlight differences in efficacy onset and the sustainment and consistency of disease control, which are relevant to patients and prescribing physicians in managing a disease such as gMG that is chronic but characterized by fluctuating symptoms. An AUC approach can compare the net treatment effect over time, which may be less patient relevant and more appropriate for use in economic modeling. In the unanchored MAICs for MG-ADL CFB based on the normalized AUC, the CFB was greater with nipocalimab vs. the two other FcRn blockers. In the corresponding Bucher ITCs, nipocalimab demonstrated comparable efficacy to the other FcRn blockers.

Although the AUC approach allowed a comparison of the net treatment effect using a single measure, it does not capture onset of effect and disease fluctuations observed with cyclically dosed regimens. Notably, ITCs based on AUC could be conducted using data digitized from published MG-ADL CFB figures, making this approach valuable in situations where IPD are not available. A similar AUC approach has been used previously for several ITCs [25, 27, 28]. The multiple timepoint approach was a useful way of discerning disease control over time. Early onset of action is important for assuring patients and healthcare professions of the treatment effectiveness. Rapid onset of action was comparable across the FcRn blockers. However, the disease control over time was significantly greater with nipocalimab vs. the other FcRn blockers. While sustained disease control is the treatment goal, a lack thereof can lead to symptom fluctuations and exacerbations with negative impacts to patient quality of life.

Strengths and Limitations

This study reports a comprehensive ITC analysis comparing efficacy of three approved FcRn blocker gMG therapies. The use of pairwise ITC methods permitted the alignment of trial populations on MG autoantibody, and MAICs improved the comparability of trial populations while achieving a reasonable ESS. The multiple timepoint approach permitted comparisons of efficacy onset and disease control over time, while the normalized AUC approach allowed for the use of a single summary measure of the net treatment effect.

Previously published ITCs generally used outcomes measured at a single timepoint and methods such as network meta-analysis that do not involve adjusting for cross-trial heterogeneity [29–32]. Previous ITCs including nipocalimab have typically used only phase 2 RCT data [29, 31], a less robust approach compared to our use of phase 3 registration RCTs for all treatments being compared. In our previous ITC feasibility assessment, we observed more pronounced within-trial imbalances in baseline patient characteristics in phase 2 RCTs compared to phase 3 registration RCTs [20]. Although published ITCs have used MG-ADL response, we chose to compare MG-ADL CFB for consistency, given variability in response definitions across trials.

A multistep process using several data sources including a SLR and clinical expert input was undertaken to identify important PFs for adjustment in MAICs. Although two anchored MAICs have been published in gMG, one comparing efgartigimod (using ADAPT) and ravulizumab (using the CHAMPION-MG trial), and the other comparing efgartigimod (ADAPT) and rituximab (BeatMG), the authors of each study acknowledged that selection of covariates for adjustment was based solely on the stratification variables used for subgroup analyses in ADAPT and these covariates were not verified as treatment effect modifiers [28, 33].

Despite the robust approach employed to identify PFs, unanchored MAICs require that all imbalances in PFs are adjusted for. As in any comparative analysis using non-randomized data, the potential for residual unaddressed heterogeneity impacting MAICs after adjustment must be acknowledged, especially because not all clinically relevant PFs were reported in the RCTs used for our ITC analyses. There were characteristics identified as relevant PFs that were not matched on or adjusted for in MAICs, which was done to preserve the ESS and because some characteristics were not available for the trials being compared. However, we were able to account for multiple important factors in the MAIC analyses, which were identified by leveraging multiple sources of evidence. The ITC analyses may also have been impacted by unobserved or unmeasured confounding. Many of the ITC effect estimates were relatively imprecise, in part a function of the limited sample sizes of the underlying trials. There was also a risk of multiplicity issues given the multiple timepoint analyses; however appropriate analytical methods were followed, and we also conducted complementary analyses based on AUC.

For the ADAPT efgartigimod trial, dropout rates likely affected the analyses at later timepoints (i.e., after week 20) as well as AUC analyses. In ADAPT, patients who required re-treatment after week 18 could switch to open-label treatment in a follow-on trial, which meant that fewer patients were available at later timepoints [12, 25] (Fig. 1). Given the impact of this switch, an unanchored MAIC approach was particularly appropriate at later timepoints. Outcome data for ADAPT beyond 10 weeks were only available in a G-BA report.

Dosing schedules differed across trials included in the ITCs, e.g., cyclical dosing in ADAPT, consecutive treatment and observation periods in MycarinG, and continuous dosing in the other trials. Future studies are needed to examine the association of observed differences in disease control with factors such as dosing schedule and mode of administration differences. Our ITCs used estimation methods for efficacy CFB outcomes such as multiple timepoints and AUC; this gave a more holistic view of efficacy onset, consistency of disease control, and net benefit over time. These methods did not depend on the availability of IPD.

Conclusions

ITC methods are commonly used to compare efficacy in the absence of head-to-head clinical trials. We used multiple approaches to estimate comparative efficacy for nipocalimab and two other FcRn blockers indicated for the treatment of gMG. Concerns regarding the poor comparability of placebo arms across trials due to differences in their characteristics and placebo response were addressed by conducting unanchored MAICs. Taken together, results of unanchored MAICs and Bucher ITCs showed that nipocalimab provided a comparable onset of action and consistent and sustained disease control that was numerically or statistically significantly greater (depending on ITC method) when compared with symptom-based cyclic FcRn blockers. Head-to-head trials are needed to validate our findings by directly comparing both consistency and sustainment of disease control between FcRn blockers.
