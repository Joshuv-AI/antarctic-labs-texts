Clinical validation of a novel catheter–tissue contact algorithm in atrial fibrillation ablation using pulsed field ablation

Pulsed field ablation (PFA) is increasingly being adopted for atrial fibrillation (AF) ablation.1 For long-term success and minimizing complications, the role of PFA catheter contact with atrial tissue cannot be overemphasized. Abbott EnSite contact index™ module is a proprietary algorithm that gauges tissue contact based on impedance increase to guide ablation. However, the clinical use of this algorithm has not been validated in vivo. In this study, we sought to define a threshold percentage increase in impedance from baseline using the EnSite contact index™ module that accurately correlates with catheter–atrial tissue contact.

Data from 25 consecutive patients undergoing first-time AF ablation between April and November 2025 using EnSite mapping with the contact index™ module and the Boston Scientific Farawave™ PFA catheter were retrospectively reviewed. For this study, atrial tissue capture was used as a surrogate for atrial tissue contact. Atrial tissue capture was defined as consistent tissue capture using sequential unipolar pacing from each of the Penta splines at 10 mA and 2 ms in flower position on the left atrial (LA) posterior wall and in basket position in the pulmonary vein (PV) using an inferior vena cava indifferent electrode.

The study cohort consisted of 15 males (60%) and 10 females (40%). All of the patients were of white race. The mean age was 69 ± 10 years. The median CHA2DS2Vasc score was 3 (2–4). The cohort had a mean body mass index of 31.1 ± 6 kg/m2, left ventricular ejection fraction of 58% ± 9%, and LA size of 4.3 ± 0.6 cm. 20 patients (80%) had paroxysmal AF and only 5 (20%) had persistent AF.

A total of 371 data points (185 in flower and 186 in basket configuration) were collected, each including baseline unipolar aggregate penta-spline blood pool impedance, increase in individual spline impedance with tissue contact, unipolar pacing capture from individual Farawave™ catheter spline, catheter configuration (basket or flower), individual spline number, and bipolar electrograms (EGM) amplitude (mV) from the individual spline. Baseline impedance in the LA was 160 ± 21 Ω. With LA tissue contact, the median increase in impedance was 19% (10%–33.5%). Baseline bipolar voltage (mV) was higher on the LA posterior wall than in the PVs (1.63 [0.35–2.23] vs 0.98 [0.23–1.13], P = .0098). Using multiple logistic regression analysis, percentage increase in a spline impedance from baseline (Ω; odds ratio [OR]: 1.43, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.27–1.66, P < .0001) and baseline spline bipolar voltage (mV; OR: 4.77, 95% CI: 2.32–13.68, P < .0001) were the only independent predictors of atrial tissue capture.

Additionally, receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) analysis identified a ≥17% increase in impedance from baseline as the optimal threshold (Youden Index 0.71) for predicting atrial tissue capture, yielding a specificity of 100%, positive predictive value of 100%, sensitivity of 71%, and negative predictive value of 51%. Overall, the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was 0.94 (95% CI: 0.91–0.96, P < .0001), indicating excellent discriminatory performance of percentage impedance increase from baseline in predicting unipolar atrial tissue capture used as a surrogate for tissue contact (Figure 1). ROC analysis was also performed for bipolar EGMs, which showed a 0.67 mV voltage as an optimal cutoff for atrial capture (Youden Index 0.52) with a specificity/positive predictive value of 87% and 94%, respectively. However, the AUC was 0.83 (95% CI: 0.7807–0.8884, P < .0001), suggesting inferior discriminatory performance. Despite the mechanism of PFA-induced myocardial injury being non-thermal electroporation, effective catheter–atrial tissue contact remains pivotal for achieving durable homogeneous lesion formation during AF ablation. Prior experimental and clinical studies have demonstrated that lesion depth, transmurality, and durability during PFA are influenced by electrode–tissue proximity, orientation, and local impedance characteristics.2,3

Additionally, inadequate catheter–atrial tissue contact during PFA can also increase procedural complications. It can increase the risk of blood pool–dominant energy delivery, exposing circulating erythrocytes to high-intensity electric fields and predisposing to intravascular hemolysis, hemoglobinuria, and, in severe cases, acute kidney injury, particularly when effective tissue coupling is not achieved and energy is preferentially delivered to blood rather than myocardium.4 In addition, suboptimal tissue contact may promote microbubble generation or cause arcing (dielectric breakdown) at the electrode–tissue interface, both of which are believed to be potential causes of cerebral thromboembolism.5 These observations further underscore the importance of optimizing catheter–tissue contact using real-time biophysical markers such as impedance rise to confirm effective myocardial engagement.

A lower impedance-rise threshold reported by Watanabe et al6 (10%) has been proposed for atrial tissue contact. This likely reflects important differences in study design and endpoint definition.6 Watanabe et al6 evaluated catheter–tissue contact sufficient to prevent acute PV gaps, whereas our study assessed atrial tissue capture, a more stringent functional electrophysiologic marker of effective tissue engagement. Additionally, impedance measurements were obtained from different anatomical locations and catheter configurations. Consequently, their threshold prioritized sensitivity for detecting contact, whereas our ≥17% threshold prioritized specificity for confirming atrial tissue capture.

Our analysis combines LA posterior wall and PV data. We acknowledge that pacing capture and impedance behavior may differ between these regions based on exclusive use of flower and basket catheter configurations in these 2 areas, respectively. This is a limitation of our study. Additionally, the >17% impedance increase should be considered specific to the combination when using the EnSite contact index™ module with a Farawave™ catheter due to the unique design of the catheter.

In conclusion, although both bipolar EGMs (mV) and percentage increase in impedance from baseline (Ω) are excellent predictors of atrial tissue capture, a ≥17% impedance rise from baseline blood pool impedance serves as a very reliable indicator of atrial tissue capture (used as a surrogate for atrial tissue contact) when using EnSite contact index™ module with a Farawave™ catheter with a positive predictive value of 100%. A future prospective study is needed to validate the clinical significance of this cutoff value in PV isolation.
