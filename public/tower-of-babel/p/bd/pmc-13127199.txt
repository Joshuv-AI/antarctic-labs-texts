Neuroprotective role of NRF2 activation against ER Stress–Mediated apoptosis in obstructive sleep apnea
Authors: PeiPei Zhong, Shurui Zhuge, Yunjia Tu, Jiaqi Li, Youliang Xu, Tsague Mbiada Meffo, Yang Ke, Jie Liu, Liujia Xu, Fei Gao, Xuanhao Peng, Tong Wu, Yuesha Pan, Qiuyan Xue, Weikun Zheng, Xiucui Li, Chenyi Yu, Xiaohong Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) induces childhood cognitive impairment via chronic intermittent hypoxia (CIH), a process to which endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS)-mediated apoptosis critically contributes. Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) is widely recognized for its significant neuroprotective effects in various neurological diseases, yet its role in ERS-related apoptosis in prefrontal neurons under CIH remains unclear. This study investigated NRF2's impact both in vitro and in vivo. In CIH-exposed Pheochromocytoma 12 (PC12) cells, mimicking OSA-related neuronal injury, CIH significantly triggered ERS and subsequent apoptosis, evidenced by the upregulated protein levels of ERS markers (p-PERK, ATF4, CHOP) and apoptotic markers (Cleaved-Caspase-3). Additionally, the antioxidant system was activated, as shown by elevated mRNA levels of Nrf2, Hmox1, and Gclc and protein levels of NRF2, HO-1, and GCLC. Treatment with the NRF2 activator sulforaphane (SFN) reduced CIH-induced apoptosis by suppressing ERS signaling and enhancing Hmox1 mRNA and HO‑1 protein expression. Conversely, ML385 (a NRF2 inhibitor) resulted in opposite outcomes. In vivo, after 4 weeks of CIH exposure, mice demonstrated spatial memory and learning deficits in the behavior test. In the prefrontal cortex, histological examination revealed increased neuronal apoptosis, characterized by elevated Cleaved-Caspase-3 protein levels, alongside activation of the NRF2 pathway. Treatment with SFN alleviated cognitive impairment and neuronal apoptosis by inhibiting ERS signaling, while enhancing Hmox1 mRNA and HO‑1 protein expression. Conversely, treatment with a NRF2 inhibitor had the opposite effects. In summary, we demonstrate that NRF2 mitigates CIH-induced neuronal apoptosis by suppressing the PERK-ATF4-CHOP signaling cascade and augmenting HO-1 expression, thereby improving cognitive impairment.

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is defined by recurring episodes of apnea and hypopnea at night, often linked to decreased blood oxygen levels. In children, the prevalence is estimated to be between 1% and 5%, with no significant gender disparity (1). In recent years, with the increase of studies on OSA,there is a growing awareness that OSA is potentially harmful to children's health, growth and development, including cardiovascular function (2) and pulmonary health (3). Besides, studies have shown OSA patients are found to have deficits in cognitive skills, attention, and memory (4, 5). However, the mechanisms still remain elusive.

A characteristic of OSA, chronic intermittent hypoxia (CIH) is related to several pathophysiological processes. Specifically, CIH triggers a cascade of events, including peroxidation, endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS), and hypercapnia, which collectively compromise various physiological systems. Research on the nervous system has demonstrated that CIH can induce short-term vascular leakage within the cerebrovascular region (6), compromise the blood-brain barrier, alter neuronal structures, and subsequently impact synaptic plasticity. At the same time, changes in the brain microenvironment will eventually cause damage to the nervous system and affect cognitive function (7, 8).

As an ATP-rich compartment, the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) provides the requisite environment for the folding and post-translational modifications of most secreted and membrane proteins. Disruptions to ER homeostasis may result in the buildup of misfolded proteins inside the ER, a state termed ERS (9, 10). In response, cells initiate a coordinated set of recovery mechanisms collectively known as the unfolded protein response (UPR). At the cellular level, the nucleus receives signals from these accumulated unfolded proteins through three transmembrane proteins: PKR-like ER kinase (PERK), inositol-requiring enzyme 1 (IRE1) and activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6) (11). The three pathways help mitigate ERS by curbing protein synthesis, encouraging protein folding, and increasing protein degradation. Nevertheless, if the stress exceeds the regulatory ability of UPR, ERS may ultimately result in cell death (12). Earlier research indicated that CIH exposure triggers ERS, leading to cell apoptosis in the hippocampus via the PERK-ATF4 (activating transcription factor 4)-CHOP (C/EBP homologous protein) pathway (13). However, it remains unclear whether a parallel mechanism exists in the prefrontal cortex.

Our research team revealed that oxidative stress (OS) is crucial for CIH-induced cognitive dysfunction (14). When OS occurs, it simultaneously activates the antioxidant defense mechanisms. The Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2)/antioxidant response element (ARE) pathway is a critical antioxidant defense mechanism in cells. This pathway is known to play a neuroprotective role in a range of neurological conditions, including neurodegenerative diseases, traumatic brain injury, and cerebral ischemia (15, 16). Normally, NRF2 is confined in the cytoplasm through its interaction with Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1) and is quickly broken down by the 26 s proteasome, thus maintaining basal NRF2 at low levels. When OS occurs, KEAP1 undergoes structural changes that prevent NRF2 from degrading. This enables NRF2 to separate and attach to antioxidant response elements (ARE) within the nucleus that protect cells from OS (15).

As a downstream target of the NRF2/ARE pathway (17), glutamate-cysteine ligase catalytic (Gclc) is the rate-limiting enzyme in glutathione synthesis (18). Furthermore, its catalytic activity is subject to feedback inhibition by reduced glutathione (GSH) (18). Heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), as another downsteam component of NRF2/ARE pathway, is of great significance in scavenging active oxygen species and anti-cell stress response (19). However, roles of GCLC and HO-1 in the CIH model remain unclear.

Noteworthy, NRF2 is sensitive to ERS-associated apoptosis (20, 21). The exact role of NRF2 activation during ERS-associated apoptosis is unclear. Studies have found NRF2 knockout mice display more severe ERS through PERK-eIF2α-CHOP pathway (22), thus induce apoptosis. As it has been already shown, NRF2 is a substrate of PERK and can be phosphorylated by PERK (23). Phosphorylated NRF2 improves OS and restores the folding state of the protein, thus maintains homeostasis, similar to the process of activation of NRF2/ARE pathway (24). ATF4, which belongs to the ATF/CREB protein family (25), is crucial for metabolism, redox reactions, and apoptosis, and is vital in the integrated stress response (26). ATF4 can be activated by PERK and lead to apoptosis through CHOP induction under hypoxic conditions (27). Therefore, it is speculated that there may be some relationship between PERK mediated NRF2 and ATF4 signaling pathways. Studies have shown that there is a cross pathway between ATF4 and NRF2 to jointly regulate the expression of antioxidant elements to offer protection (28). Reports indicate that NRF2 reduces CHOP gene expression in the way of blocking ATF4's attachment to amino acid response elements within cells (29).

Although NRF2 is known to protect against CIH-induced injury, its precise modulation of the ERS-mediated PERK-ATF4-CHOP apoptotic pathway in the prefrontal cortex remains undefined. Moreover, the critical downstream effector (GCLC vs. HO-1) in NRF2-mediated neuroprotection is unidentified. This study therefore aimed to elucidate this mechanism and pinpoint the critical mediator, thereby providing a novel mechanistic insight into NRF2-mediated neuroprotection.

Results

CIH exposure decreased cell viability and cause morphological changes in pheochromocytoma 12 (PC12) cells

To determine whether CIH exposure exerts cytotoxic effects, we compared the cell viability and morphology between control (C) and CIH groups. The CCK-8 assay revealed that the CIH group showed a notably lower survival rate of about 73% relative to the C group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1A). The inverted optical microscope revealed morphological differences: cells maintained in normoxic condition were spindle-shaped with plump bodies, abundant axons, no vacuolization, and clear edges. In contrast, CIH-exposed cells exhibited atrophy, blurred edges, irregular shapes, vacuolization, reduced axons, and numerous black granules in the cytoplasm (Fig. 1B). NRF2 and ERS were activated after CIH exposure

To establish the baseline effects of CIH, we profiled the expression of NRF2 antioxidant pathway and ERS response. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed low baseline expression of NRF2, ATF4, and CHOP under normal conditions (Fig. 1, E–H). Specifically, in control cells, NRF2 exhibited low cytoplasmic expression, while ATF4 was detectable primarily in the nucleus at low levels, with minimal cytoplasmic presence (Fig. 1, G, I–K). Following CIH exposure, significant upregulation of both the protective factor NRF2 and the pro-apoptotic proteins CHOP and ATF4 was observed (Fig. 1, E–H). Notably, CIH treatment induced a pronounced increase and nuclear accumulation of both NRF2 and ATF4 (Fig. 1, G, J and K). Co-localization analysis revealed that NRF2 (Channel 1) and ATF4 (Channel 2) exhibit significant co-localization following CIH exposure, quantified by a Pearson's coefficient (R) of 0.62, Manders' overlap coefficients (M1 = 0.617, M2 = 1.000), and a Costes p-value of 1.00, confirming the specificity of the overlap. The spatial co-localization of NRF2 and ATF4 was demonstrated in cells with DAPI-stained nuclei (Fig. 1L). These results indicate that intermittent hypoxia activates NRF2, leading to its nuclear translocation, while also concurrently inducing the expression of ERS-related proteins CHOP and ATF4.

NRF2 can alleviate ERS caused apoptosis due to CIH

To probe the functional role of NRF2 activation in CIH-induced injury, we employed sulforaphane (SFN), a well-characterized natural compound that potently activates NRF2 by modifying specific cysteine residues on its negative regulator KEAP1 (30, 31). While SFN is known to act on multiple targets (32), we primarily focused on its role as a direct NRF2 activator to investigate ERS-associated apoptosis. We first investigated the impact of various SFN concentrations (ranging from 0 – 16 μM) (33, 34, 35) on cell viability and found that 0.5 μM SFN yielded the highest viability with statistical significance. Therefore, this concentration was utilized in the later experiments (Fig. 2A). As indicated in Figure 2, B, C, H, and L, both Nrf2 mRNA and Nrf2 protein levels significantly increased after SFN treatment in both C and CIH groups, indicating successful activation of NRF2 at 0.5 μM SFN. We measured the concentrations of ERS marker CHOP, ATF4, phosphorylated PERK (p-PERK) and apoptotic-related protein Cleaved-Caspase-3 in all groups. Gene expression was analyzed by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR), and protein levels were determined by Western blotting (WB). Atf4 and Chop mRNA levels were notably elevated in the CIH group relative to the C group (p < 0.05) but decreased in the CIH + SFN group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2, I and J). The C and C + DMSO groups demonstrated no remarkable difference. Analysis showed that the levels of Cleaved-Caspase-3, p-PERK, ATF4 and CHOP proteins were elevated in the CIH group compared to the C group (p < 0.05). However, treatment with SFN led to a significant decrease in these protein levels after CIH exposure (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2, B, D–G). The C + DMSO group exhibited no significant differences in these protein levels in comparison to the C group. Immunofluorescence further validated the CHOP results (Fig. 2K). Accordingly, SFN treatment effectively reversed the elevated intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels induced by CIH (Fig. 2, K and L). To definitively validate the functional role of NRF2, we employed pharmacological inhibition using ML385. This specific inhibitor binds to the Neh1 domain of NRF2 to block its transcriptional activity (36), allowing us to examine its effects on CIH-induced ERS and apoptosis in PC12 cells. Cell toxicity of ML385 was assessed using the CCK8 assay. As illustrated in Figure 3A, cell viability did not show a significant decline with ML385 concentrations ranging from 0.5 to 5 μM compared to the control group. However, cell viability decreased at concentrations above 10 μM (p < 0.05). Therefore, we selected 5 μM ML385 for subsequent experiments, as it did not affect cell viability. Compared with the CIH group, the CIH + ML385 (CIH + M) group showed a marked reduction in both Nrf2 mRNA and Nrf2 protein levels (Fig. 3, B, C, H, and L), which indicated that 5 μM ML385 effectively suppressed NRF2 expression. However, adding SFN to the CIH + M group did not reverse NRF2 suppression. We subsequently assessed the impact of ML385 on apoptosis and ERS markers. The CIH + M group had higher mRNA levels of Atf4 and Chop than the CIH group (p < 0.05), while the CIH + M + SFN group demonstrated a trend of decreased transcript levels compared to the CIH + M group (Fig. 3, I and J). Correspondingly, the CIH + M group had notably higher protein levels of Cleaved-Caspase-3, ATF4, CHOP, and p-PERK in comparison to the CIH group, and this increase was not fully reversed in the CIH + M + S group (Fig. 3, B–J). Immunofluorescence confirmed similar results for CHOP (Fig. 3K). Besides, CIH exposure increased intracellular ROS levels, and blocking the NRF2 pathway further elevated ROS accumulation (Fig. 3, K and L). NRF2/HO-1 pathway activates in PC12 cells after CIH exposure

Additionally, ML385 was utilized to observe the impact of inhibiting NRF2 on its downstream proteins in the CIH model of PC12 cells. The CIH + M group demonstrated a notable decline in Hmox1 mRNA and HO-1 protein levels in comparison with the CIH group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4, C, H, and I). NRF2 serves a protective function against CIH-induced cognitive dysfunction

BRU is a compound with broad cellular effects (37); however, in the context of NRF2 inhibition, it selectively reduces NRF2 protein levels by enhancing its ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation (38). The Y-maze test indicated that the CIH group displayed decreased alternation behavior versus the C group (p < 0.05). However, mice treated with SFN showed increased alternation behavior in comparison to the CIH group, while those receiving BRU exhibited even lower alternation behavior (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5C). Additionally, the novel arm was explored for a longer duration by the control group mice versus the CIH group. These data indicate that spatial and recognition memory impairments resulting from CIH can be improved with the NRF2 agonist SFN and worsened with the NRF2 inhibitor BRU.

NRF2 signaling stimulation diminishes the apoptosis caused by CIH in the prefrontal cortex

Our previous study demonstrated that NRF2 provides protection against apoptosis caused by CIH in the hippocampus (39). It is well-known that, in addition to the hippocampus, prefrontal cortex is also critical for spatial memory (40). In this study, we focused on the prefrontal cortex.

First, HE staining indicated that, the CIH group exhibited a significant cell number decrease, with cells appearing enlarged and some neurons showing vacuoles and transparent nuclei. These effects were more pronounced in the CIH + BRU group (indicated by arrows). Conversely, the CIH + SFN group showed more cells and fewer abnormal cell morphologies (Fig. 6A). Nissl staining showed that, compared to the C group, the CIH group experienced a notable quantity drop of surviving neurons. Moreover, these neurons were irregularly arranged and had abnormal morphologies, such as shrinkage, irregular shapes, and pyknotic nuclei. Notable changes in ER ultrastructure were detected following CIH exposure. In the control and SFN groups, the ER was organized into parallel sheets around nucleus. Conversely, the ER in the CIH group was misoriented, swollen, and distorted, with these effects being worse in the CIH + BRU group (Fig. 6C). These findings collectively suggest that SFN treatment can ameliorate ER pathology, whereas BRU treatment can aggravate these effects.

Additionally, western blotting indicated that NRF2 activation occurred in the CIH group and was further enhanced by SFN treatment, while it was decreased by BRU treatment at the doses used (Fig. 6, E and G). Immunofluorescence confirmed these results, indicating the process occurring in the neurons of the prefrontal cortex (Fig. 6D). Western blotting also indicated that the level of the apoptosis marker Cleaved-Caspase-3 increased with CIH exposure. This increase was reversed by SFN treatment and further elevated by BRU treatment (Fig. 6, E and H).

These findings collectively suggest that SFN enhances NRF2 activation, providing neuroprotection against CIH-induced apoptosis, while BRU inhibits NRF2, exacerbating neuronal damage.

These findings indicate that NRF2 restores antioxidant capacity and alleviates CIH-induced apoptosis through both the PERK-ATF4-CHOP pathway and the NRF2/HO-1 pathway. Specifically, NRF2 activation, enhanced by SFN, reduces pro-apoptotic markers (p-PERK, ATF4, CHOP) and increases the levels of the antioxidative marker HO-1, thereby mitigating neuronal apoptosis. Conversely, inhibition of NRF2 by BRU exacerbates these apoptotic processes, underscoring NRF2's protective function against neuronal injury caused by CIH.

Discussion

To sum up, our findings revealed that CIH-induced neuronal apoptosis in prefrontal cortex is related to ERS and can be alleviated by the activation of NRF2. Cellular and animal models consistently revealed that NRF2 mitigates neuronal apoptosis through inhibiting the PERK-ATF4-CHOP pathway and enhancing the antioxidant enzyme HO-1. These findings clarify the possible pathways behind neuronal apoptosis caused by CIH and highlight the pivotal roles of NRF2 and ERS in cognitive dysfunction. This suggests a promising strategy for preventing and treating CIH - associated cognitive impairment.

PC12 cells, which originate from a rat adrenal pheochromocytoma, are capable of differentiating into cells resembling sympathetic neurons and often exhibit characteristics of mature dopaminergic neurons. These cells are widely used in physiological and pathological studies of the nervous system due to their unique sensitivity to changes in oxygen supply. They are frequently employed as a cell model to study neuronal susceptibility to hypoxia (41). For establishing an in vitro OSA model, PC12 cells were treated with intermittent hypoxia in this study as previously described (42). We observed significant vacuolization and decreased cellular viability in the CIH group, along with a notable elevation in the expression of proteins associated with apoptosis, demonstrating that CIH induces cell apoptosis. These results align with earlier findings from animal experiments (43, 44).

ERS is activated by a range of internal and external environmental stimuli, such as nutrient deprivation, oxygen level fluctuations, and exposure to carcinogens. ERS is intricately linked to processes like autophagy, hypoxia signaling, and reactive oxygen species generation (45). During hypoxia, cells initiate adaptive pathways, including the UPR, to manage the stress. UPR signaling can influence cellular metabolism, protein synthesis, and decisions regarding cell viability or apoptosis. Under mild or transient stress, the PERK-ATF4 pathway predominantly supports survival by activating protective genes to restore homeostasis (46, 47). However, when stress is excessive or prolonged, it can initiate cell death programs and induce apoptosis. A study on ERS and autophagy in hydrogen peroxide-induced OS damage found that using an ERS inducer increased cell apoptosis and exacerbated OS damage, while using an ERS inhibitor reduced the rate of cell apoptosis (48). Under conditions of sustained ERS, PERK is activated and phosphorylates eIF2α, subsequently activating ATF4 and its downstream target CHOP. Overexpression of CHOP induces cell cycle arrest and apoptosis by downregulating anti-apoptotic proteins and upregulating pro-apoptotic factors, such as TRB3 and BBC3/PUMA (49, 50, 51). Additionally, CHOP can upregulate the activation of DR5 and Caspase-8, participating in the extrinsic apoptotic pathway (52, 53, 54).

In our study, CIH exposure induced significant upregulation of p-PERK and ATF4, along with endoplasmic reticulum morphological changes, confirming the occurrence of ERS both in vitro and in vivo. Since sustained ERS is known to promote apoptosis—as supported by Saveljeva et al. (55)—and given the marked increases we observed in Cleaved-Caspase-3, p-PERK, ATF4 and CHOP, we propose that CIH-triggered apoptosis is likely mediated through the PERK-ATF4-CHOP signaling pathway.

NRF2 functions as a crucial transcription factor for the body's defense against OS. Under normal conditions, it exists in the cytoplasm at low concentrations, regulating key components of antioxidant defense, for example, glutathione and thioredoxin. These factors play essential roles in cellular functions, including metabolism, maintenance of homeostasis, NADPH regeneration, detoxification of reactive oxygen species and xenobiotics, and heme metabolism, thereby maintaining cellular redox balance. During stress conditions, phosphorylation and activation of PERK lead to reduced degradation and increased levels of NRF2, which then migrates to the nucleus, combines with small musculoaponeurotic fibrosarcoma (sMaf) proteins, and regulates downstream gene expression to combat intracellular stress. Our experimental data show a substantial rise in p-PERK and NRF2 levels in the CIH group, and immunofluorescence results demonstrate evident nuclear translocation of NRF2. However, despite the activation of the protective NRF2 pathway, CIH still triggers apoptosis, with the sustained induction of CHOP likely being the key tipping factor.

As a potent electrophilic activator, SFN directly modifies KEAP1 to activate NRF2 through the major, PERK-independent KEAP1-NRF2 pathway (30, 31). The CIH + SFN group, which received treatment with SFN, displayed a significant reduction in p-PERK, ATF4, CHOP, and Cleaved-Caspase-3 expression in comparison to the CIH group. This was accompanied by improvements in cell viability, neuronal count, and cognitive behavior in CIH mice. Conversely, applying the NRF2 inhibitor produced opposite effects. These findings suggest that NRF2 mediated suppression of ERS and apoptosis-related genes and proteins, alleviate cell damage, and ultimately improve cognitive defects caused by CIH. This highlights its protective role in this process, consistent with previous studies (39, 56).

However, the precise mechanism by which NRF2 modulates the PERK-ATF4-CHOP pathway remains unclear. It is known that PERK phosphorylation during ERS activates NRF2, which in turn upregulates antioxidant genes to combat OS and maintain cellular homeostasis. Interestingly, our study found that NRF2 activation downregulated p-PERK and ROS levels, while NRF2 inhibition exerted the opposite effect, aligning with observations by Hyo-Jin Park et al. on PERK downregulation following NRF2 activation (57). This suggests a feedback loop where NRF2, by reducing ROS generation via HO-1 activation (21, 58), influences p-PERK levels, given PERK's sensitivity to intracellular ROS. To further establish causality and directionality within this proposed feedback loop, future studies could directly manipulate ROS levels (e.g., using H2O2 or antioxidants) in our CIH model, with or without concurrent NRF2 modulation, while simultaneously monitoring changes in PERK pathway activity and NRF2 signaling.

Currently, there is no consensus on the interaction between NRF2 and ATF4. According to He et al., ATF4 and NRF2 combine to create a heterodimer that modulates HO-1 gene expression by targeting stress response elements (59). Research has also explored the upstream-downstream relationship between NRF2 and ATF4. For example, Zong (60) et al. revealed that NRF2 can inhibit Chop gene expression by preventing ATF4 from interacting with the amino acid response element located on the Chop gene promoter in thyroid cancer cells. Sarcinelli (61) et al. found that during ERS, the PERK pathway stimulates NRF2 expression by binding ATF4 to a specific region of the Nrf2 promoter, which offers protection against ROS generation. Mimura (29) et al. showed that high concentrations of the NRF2 activator carnosic acid (CA) activate both NRF2 and the ATF4 pathway. Knockout of ATF4 reduced Nrf2 mRNA induction by CA but did not affect NRF2 protein levels. Conversely, NRF2 deletion did not affect Atf4 mRNA induction by CA. Our study revealed that treatment with an NRF2 activator decreased ATF4 expression, whereas NRF2 inhibition led to an increase in ATF4 expression. These complex and sometimes contradictory findings suggest a feedback loop or multiple pathways working together to regulate NRF2 and ATF4 levels. Further studies are needed to elucidate these interactions.

NRF2, once activated, relocates to the nucleus and combines with AREs in the promoters of its target genes. This binding initiates the upregulation of various antioxidant and cytoprotective proteins, which include GCLC and HO-1. HO-1 functions as an enzyme that breaks down heme into free iron, biliverdin and carbon monoxide, exhibiting anti-inflammatory, antioxidant and cytoprotective properties. The upregulation of HO-1 signifies a critical adaptive response to OS, mitigating oxidative damage and enhancing cell survival (62). Research has demonstrated that SFN, via NRF2 activation, further enhances HO-1 expression, thereby reducing doxorubicin-induced reactive oxygen species levels and safeguarding cells against apoptosis (63). Consistent with these findings, our study observed significant increases in HO-1 levels within the CIH group, which were augmented upon SFN treatment and attenuated by NRF2 inhibition. The combined expression patterns of apoptosis and ERS-related genes suggests that NRF2 contributes to cell survival under CIH by modulating the NRF2/HO-1 signaling pathway.

GCLC acts as a pivotal enzyme in glutathione synthesis, the primary intracellular antioxidant. By promoting glutathione production, GCLC helps to maintain cellular redox balance and defends against OS and toxic insults (64). In our in vitro experiments, exposure to CIH resulted in elevated Gclc mRNA and GCLC protein levels, further augmented by SFN treatment. Interestingly, despite Nrf2 inhibition, the protein levels of GCLC did not decrease, potentially implicating post-transcriptional epigenetic modifications (65) or alternative regulatory pathways influencing GCLC levels.

Several limitations should be noted. The reliance on PC12 cells may not fully replicate primary neuronal physiology under CIH. Furthermore, definitive causal attribution is limited by the lack of genetic perturbation (knockdown/rescue) experiments. Mechanistically, the precise means by which NRF2 regulates the PERK–ATF4–CHOP axis and its upstream activators, and the interplay between NRF2/HO-1 and PERK-ATF4-CHOP remain to be fully defined. Future work prioritizing genetic models, multi-pathway profiling, and direct biochemical assays (e.g., ChIP, Co-IP) will be essential to address these questions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the current research identified the activation of NRF2 and apoptosis induced by ERS following CIH exposure in both in vitro and in vivo experiments. Cognitive dysfunction in CIH mouse models is linked to ERS-induced apoptosis in the prefrontal cortex. NRF2 is crucial to the process. NRF2 activation can provide protection against apoptosis by modulating the PERK-ATF4-CHOP pathway and regulating the expression of downstream antioxidant element HO-1 (Fig. 9). Experimental procedures

Cell culture, experimental groups and in vitro CIH model

The PC12 cells were acquired from The Cell Bank of the Shaghai Institute of Cell Biology. The cell line was certified by the bank to be free of mycoplasma, bacterial, and fungal contamination and as of authentic rat origin without interspecies cross-contamination. The original vial was stored in liquid nitrogen for less than 1 year before resuscitation. The cells used in all experiments were low-passage (within 10 passages) from the original stocks. To prevent contamination, all the cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Solarbio) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Additionally, daily microscopic examination was performed throughout all experiments.

Cell cultures under normoxic and intermittent hypoxic (CIH) conditions were maintained in a Thermo Scientific Forma Series II and a Galaxy 170R CO2 incubator, respectively. Cells in the CIH group were subjected to six cycles of deoxygenation-reoxygenation (1% O2 for 60 min followed by 20% O2 for 30 min, 5% CO2, 37 °C) as described previously (42), whereas the control group was incubated under standard condition (95% air, 5% CO, 37 °C) for the same duration.

The in vitro experiment was conducted in two stages. In the first stage, the following groups were included: control group (C group), solvent control group (C + DMSO group), control + NRF2 activator sulforaphane (SFN, Sigma) group (C + SFN group), CIH group, and CIH + NRF2 activator group (CIH + SFN group). The second stage included the following groups: C group, control + NRF2 inhibitor (ML385, Glpbio) group (C + M group), CIH group, CIH + NRF2 inhibitor group (CIH + M group), and CIH + NRF2 inhibitor + NRF2 activator group (CIH + M + S group). Dimethyl Sulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma-Aldrich) was used as a vehicle at a final concentration of 0.05% in all treatments. As described in the Results section, the concentrations used were determined experimentally, with final working concentrations of 0.5 μM for SFN and 5 μM for ML385.

CCK-8 assay

The CCK-8 assay from New Cell & Molecular Biotech was used to evaluate cell viability. PC12 cells in the exponential growth phase were seeded at a density of 2 × 10ˆ4 cells per well and allowed to adhere. The CIH groups followed the CIH protocol, while the control groups remained in the standard condition for the same time, either with or without drug treatment. Afterward, the cells were washed using phosphate buffer solution (PBS, Gibco), and then 100 μl of culture media with 10% CCK-8 stock solution was added to each well. After a 1-h incubation under normoxic conditions, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific Varioskan LUX multimode microplate reader). The cell viability was calculated as follows: Viability (%) = [(As - Ab)/(Ac - Ab)] × 100%, where As, Ac, and Ab represent the absorbance of the test wells (cells + medium + CCK-8 + drug), control wells (cells + medium + CCK-8), and blank wells (medium + CCK-8 only), respectively.

qRT-PCR

PC12 cells underwent total RNA extraction using Trizol and chloroform. The RNA-containing aqueous phase was combined with isopropanol, and after centrifugation, the pellets were washed using 75% ethanol. Following the removal of the supernatant, the RNA pellets were resuspended in RNase-free water. The RNA concentration was assessed with a spectrophotometer (Thermo NanoDrop One), and 1 μg of RNA was reverse-transcribed with a Toroivd kit (TOROBlue All-in-One qRT Mix with dsDNase (RTQ-204)). An Accurate96- × 6 system from Bio-Rad was used to conduct real-time PCR with primers and the Toroivd qPCR Master Mix (TOROGreenqPCR Master Mix) Kit. The comparative CT method determined fold differences relative to the endogenous reference gene Tubulin. Furthermore, no-reverse transcription (no-RT) controls were included for each sample to confirm the absence of genomic DNA contamination. Cytoplasmic and nuclear protein extraction

Cytoplasmic and nuclear proteins were extracted using a commercial kit (Beyotime, P0027) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, cells were lysed in cytoplasmic extraction reagent A containing 1 mM phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (PMSF, Beyotime). After vortexing and ice incubation, cytoplasmic extraction reagent B was added. The cytoplasmic supernatant was collected following centrifugation at 16,000g for 5 min at 4 °C. The pellet was then resuspended in nuclear extraction reagent supplemented with 1 mM PMSF, vortexed intermittently for 30 min, and centrifuged at 16,000g for 10 min at 4 °C to collect the nuclear fraction. All extracts were stored at −80 °C until use.

Experimental groups in animals

This study was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Wenzhou Medical University (wydw2022–0612). A total of 40 healthy male C57BL/6 mice, each 4 weeks old and weighing 14 to 16g, were sourced from Experimental Animal Center at Wenzhou University. The experiment involved four groups of mice, each consisting of 10 mice: a control group (C), a CIH group (kept in an intermittent hypoxia chamber), a CIH + SFN group (administered with SFN), and a CIH + BRU group (administered with BRU, sigma). Thirty minutes prior to daily intermittent hypoxia (IH) treatment, mice in the CIH + SFN and CIH + BRU groups administered an intraperitoneal injection of either SFN (0.5 mg/kg in 1% DMSO) (56) or BRU (2 mg/kg in 1% DMSO) (66, 67), respectively.

CIH exposures in vivo

Mice belonging to the CIH, CIH + SFN, and CIH + BRU groups situated in a chamber designed for intermittent hypoxia, featuring a computer-operated system for delivering nitrogen and oxygen gases. This system generated hypoxia-reoxygenation cycles based on a previously established protocol (68).To simulate intermittent hypoxia, the chamber’s oxygen levels were alternated between 21.0 ± 0.5% and 8.0 ± 1.5% following a pattern of 30 s at 8.0% O2, followed by 18 s at 21.0% O5. Over a 4-weeks duration, the cycle of 90 s was repeated 40 times per hour, continuing for 7 h daily from from 8:00 to 15:00 during the light phase. The temperature within the chamber was stabilized at 22 °C, with a permissible variation of 2 °C.

Behavioral experiments

The Y-maze served as a tool to measure short-term memory in mice. This test was conducted following daily CIH sessions. The assessment of spontaneous alternation, which relies on the inherent curiosity of rodents to explore new environments as a measure of spatial working memory, took place on the 25th day. Mice had a 5-min period to explore the maze's three arms, and entries were noted only if both hind paws entered an arm. The behavior was considered an alternation when all three arms were entered consecutively and the percentage of this behavior was computed.

Mice underwent Y-maze training with one arm blocked for 5 min on the 28th day to assess their spatial reference memory. Following a 1-h rest, the mice were returned to the maze with all arms accessible for an additional 5 min. Mice with intact reference memory were expected to explore the previously unvisited arm more frequently. Spatial memory was assessed by recording duration and distance covered in the arms.

The NOR test was conducted in an open-field arena to assess non-spatial recognition memory. On the 27th day, two identical objects were placed in the arena, and mice were allowed to freely explore for 5 min. After a 24-h retention interval, one familiar object was replaced with a novel object of different shape but similar size. The mice were then returned to the arena for a 5 min test session. Exploration was defined as directing the nose toward the object at a distance ≤ 2 cm. The time spent exploring each object was recorded. Recognition memory was quantified using the Recognition Index (RI). RI = [(time spent exploring the novel object)/(total time spent exploring both objects)] × 100%. Shanghai Jiliang Animal Behavior Analysis System was used to analyze the video recordings of all the animal activities.

Sample collection and preparation

Post-Y-maze test, brain samples of six mice per group were extracted after anesthesia with sodium pentobarbital. Pieces of the left prefrontal cortex, each 1 mm square, were fixed in 4% glutaraldehyde for electron microscopy, and the leftover cortical tissue was preserved for Western blot analysis. The remaining mice were anesthetized for brain tissue collection after cardiac perfusion, followed by fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde for subsequent experiments.

Hematoxylin and eosin staining

Following the removal of wax from 4-micron-thick brain slices with dimethylbenzene, the sections were subjected to a series of alcohol concentrations (100%, 95%, 70%, 50%, and 30%), for 3 min per concentration. Sections underwent an 8-min hematoxylin stain, a 3-min differentiation in 1% hydrochloric acid alcohol, a 15-min counterstain under running water and a 3-min eosin stain. The sections were subjected to a series of alcohol concentrations (30%, 50%, 70%, 95%, and 100%) for 6 s each, followed by two 15-min xylene clearings, and then mounted using neutral resin. A Solarbio HE staining kit (G1120) was utilized to evaluate neuronal morphology.

Nissl staining

Sections underwent dewaxing, rehydration, and staining using a Nissl staining kit from Beyotime Biotechnology (C0117) at 37 °C for 25 min. They were then rinsed, dehydrated, permeabilized and sealed. Neuron density analysis was conducted using ImageJ software.

Detection through transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Fixed with 4% glutaraldehyde, tissue samples were then washed and soaked in 1% osmium tetroxide solution. After three comprehensive 10-min rinses using deionized water, the blocks were stained with uranyl acetate solution for 60 min at ambient temperature. Subsequetly, they were dehydrated with a gradient acetone series each for 10 min. Epoxy resin was used to embed the tissue blocks, which were later divided into ultrathin slices measuring 60 nm. Uranyl acetate and lead citrate were used to label the slices. The endoplasmic reticulum's structure was examined with a transmission electron microscope manufactured by Hitachi.

Immunofluorescence

PC12 cells, at a density of 200,000 cells per well, were cultivated in 12-well plates for 36 h. Then, they were subjected to either CIH exposure or standard incubation. Afterwards, they were stabilized with a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for half an hour. Fixed cell coverslips and brain sections were then permeabilized and blocked at ambient temperature for one hour using a mixture composed of 0.4% 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA), Triton X-100, and 5% goat serum. Subsequently, they were subjected to overnight incubation at 4 °C upon the introduction of primary antibodies: NRF2 (1:200, Cell Signaling Technology, #12721), ATF4 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, #97038), NRF2 (1:200, Affinity, #BF8017), CHOP (1:500, Proteintech, 66741-1-Ig), NeuN (1:500, Proteintech, 26975-1-AP). The following day, after three PBS washes, the sections were stained with secondary antibody (1:200, Affinity, #S0005, #S0007, #S0013, #S0018) accordingly in the dark at 37 °C for 60 min. DAPI with an anti-fading reagent was then used to stain the nuclei at ambient temperature in the dark for 10 min. As the final step, images of fluorescence were recorded employing a Nikon fluorescence microscope.

Western blotting

Prefrontal cortex tissues and cells were broken down in RIPA buffer to extract the whole proteins. Protein samples of equal quantity (40 μg) were subjected to SDS-PAGE separation and then transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. An overnight incubation was performed at 4 °C for the membranes with primary antibodies: ATF-4 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, #97038), p-PERK (1:1000, Affinity, #DF7576), CHOP (1:4000, Proteintech, 66741-1-Ig), NRF2 (1:1000, Affinity, #BF8017), Cleaved-Caspase-3 (1:1000, Proteintech, 25128-1-AP), GCLC (1:1000, Affinity, #DF8550), HO-1 (1:1000, Affinity, #AF5393), β-Actin (1:7500, Proteintech, 66009-1-Ig), Histone H3 (1:1000, Huabio, R1110–1)and β-Tubulin (1:1000, Affinity, #AF7011). The visualization of protein bands was achieved using ECL Western blotting detection reagent after treating them with peroxidase-linked secondary antibodies. OD values for particular proteins were standardized to the levels of tubulin or actin and presented as fold changes relative to the C group.

Measurements of ROS

Intracellular ROS levels were detected using the fluorescent probe dihydroethidium (DHE, Beyotime, S0063). For probe loading, 30 min prior to the completion of the modeling process, the original culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 4 μM DHE and incubated at 37 °C. Following the modeling treatment, cells were washed three times with PBS. Fluorescent images were then captured in a dark room using a Nikon inverted fluorescence microscope.

Glutathione kit

Brain tissue (25 mg) was homogenized in 0.2 ml of assay reagent and centrifuged at 3500g for 10 min. Following centrifugation, the resultant supernatant was collected for subsequent analysis. The levels of GSH and its oxidized form GSSG were measured according to the manufacturer's instructions (Glutathione Assay Kit, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, A061–1). Absorbance was measured at 405 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, USA).

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± SD. The assumptions of normality and equal variance were met for all parametric analyses. Therefore, two-group comparisons used Student's t test; multi-group comparisons used one-way ANOVA followed by the LSD post hoc test. Analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 10, with significance set at ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001.
