Murine vascular organoids are responsive and adaptable 3D systems with cellular heterogeneity and dynamic plasticity
Authors: Sophie Guelfi, Krish Skandha Gopalan, Pieter Maene, Dorothy John Robbert, Carla Pallarés-Moratalla, Gerlanda Vella, Max Nobis, Larissa Mourao, Pierre Bourdely, Bernard K. van der Veer, Kian Peng Koh, Massimiliano Mazzone, Colinda L. G. J. Scheele, Gabriele Bergers
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Blood vascular organoids (BVOs) have emerged as powerful tools for interrogating endothelial heterogeneity, function, and organ-specific cues during development, homeostasis, and disease. Here, we describe the stepwise generation and characterization of murine BVOs from different embryonic stem cells and introduce a fluorescent tracer system to follow their formation and maturation, visualizing functional and perfused vessels after transplantations into mice. Single-cell RNA sequencing, FACS, and whole-mount immunohistochemistry revealed a heterogeneous and dynamic trajectory of multiple endothelial, mesenchymal, and macrophage clusters in mBVOs at growth spike and full maturation. Endothelial cells inferred high plasticity toward arterial, venous, capillary, and tip/stalk cells and a neural-like identity at full maturation state. mBVOs responded to inflammatory cues and VEGF blockade in vitro and could be assembled with tumor spheroids in which tumor cells mimicked the same vascular growth pattern as observed in vivo. Our results highlight mBVOs as adaptable in vitro platforms, unlocking various applications and avenues for discovery in vascular biology.

INTRODUCTION

Recapitulating the cellular and structural complexity of the vasculature in vitro remains a major challenge in the field. Blood vessels are essential gatekeepers of homeostasis that deliver oxygen, nutrients, hormones, and immune cells to tissues (1). A mature vasculature exhibits a typical morphology in which endothelial cells (ECs), lined with mural cells and the basement membrane, assemble into characteristic three-dimensional (3D) tubular structures (2). These are maintained by intricate interactions with surrounding stromal and immune cell components, altogether ensuring barrier functions and homeostasis of the endothelium (3). The vasculature is also remarkably diverse to adapt within organ-specific vascular beds (4). 2D models have been instrumental in studying simple processes but are irrelevant to replicating in vivo conditions (5). More complex 3D systems are increasingly used to overcome these limitations, including spheroids, hydrogel-based systems, bioprinted vessels or “vessel-on-a-chip” models, each presenting advantages and disadvantages (6, 7).

For the past 15 years, organoids have revolutionized human disease modeling, as well as our understanding of organogenesis and cell differentiation programs (8, 9). These next-generation in vitro models are easily manipulated while retaining self-assembling complex structures. However, integration of a proper vasculature remains an obstacle to achieving more complexity in many types of organoids (10, 11).

Bona fide human vascular organoids derived from human pluripotent stem cells are now a well-established and characterized system that recapitulates the complexity of blood vessels in a dish. Since the initial protocol proposed in 2019 (12, 13), their generation has been refined and transposed to different contexts (14). They have been used in recent studies demonstrating their integration within microfluidics to achieve perfusion in vitro (15), their fusion with brain organoids to form assembloids that recapitulate neurovascular development and diseases (16–19) or with other organoid types to achieve vascularization (20), and lastly studies analyzing their response to antiangiogenic blockade (21) and extracellular or metabolic perturbations (22, 23).

Mouse models have long been foundational tools in vascular biology, yet they are not appropriate for complex mechanistic studies (24). Single-cell omic datasets have recently provided comprehensive atlases of the physiological and pathological vasculatures in mouse models (25, 26). However, this body of work included very high number of vascular cells purified from the starting material, an experimental requirement that is still a major limiting factor. Recently, there has also been a consensus in the research community to reduce and refine in vivo mouse model studies in the future for both ethical and economic reasons (27). Thus, there is a need for reliable 3D culture models to investigate these issues

In this study, we generated bona fide murine blood vessel organoids (mBVOs). Following a stepwise differentiation timeline starting from murine embryonic stem cells (mESCs), we validated the structure and functionality of mBVOs in vitro and in vivo and characterized their heterogeneity and plasticity by single-cell transcriptomics, flow cytometry, and whole-mount stainings. Last, we used mBVOs to interrogate the effects of proinflammatory signals and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) inhibition, and we generated tumor vessel organoids that resembled in vivo tumor-vascular interactions, providing evidence for multiple purposes of mBVOs.

RESULTS

Generation of blood vessel organoids from mouse ES cells

To establish mBVOs, we first adapted the differentiation protocol using human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) (Fig. 1A) (12). There are key differences in the in vitro maintenance of human versus mESCs (28). Where hESC culture requires the absence of serum and Matrigel coating for maintenance of pluripotency, mESCs have been widely cultured in serum and feeder-dependent conditions supplemented with leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF) (29, 30). We thus maintained C57BL/6 x 129S6 F1 hybrid (B6x129) mESCs on layers of mitotically inactivated mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs), in ESC medium supplemented with ES-qualified fetal bovine serum (FBS) and LIF (Fig. 1B). We confirmed their undifferentiated state as mESC colonies homogeneously expressed pluripotency markers such as SOX2, OCT4, NANOG, and SSEA1 (fig. S1A).

Similar to the human method, we initiated mESC differentiation with the formation of aggregates or embryoid bodies (EBs) in suspension, in the absence of MEFs and by removing LIF from the ESC medium at day 0. The EB stage is essential to prime mESCs for directed differentiation (31). Specifically, we optimized this step and seeded single cells in 1—very low densities, i.e., 2500 cells/ml for B6x129 mESCs—and 2—in nonadherent T25 cm2 flasks to avoid fusion of EBs and improve the efficiency of the protocol (Fig. 1B).

Next, we applied similar steps, timings, and growth factors as detailed in the human method (Fig. 1A) (12, 13). After 3 days of EB growth, we induced mesoderm lineage differentiation with murine bone morphogenetic protein 4 (BMP4; 30 ng/ml) until day 6, followed by vascular lineage differentiation with murine VEGF (100 ng/ml) and forskolin (2 μM) until day 8. However, we used N2B27 medium as a basis throughout the timeline until we obtained growing mBVOs. As previously described for human aggregates, embedding murine EBs in collagen I/Geltrex gels was a key step to initiate sprouting angiogenesis until day 13. Specifically, we seeded no more than 20 to 25 EBs per well in gels of 12-well plates, which was critical to optimize the sprouting phase (fig. S1B). At this stage, we observed the formation of vascular networks (VNs) with the presence of sprouting angiogenic tip cells (Fig. 1B). Next, we dissected out single murine vascular networks (mVNs) and grew them individually in 15% FBS, VEGF (100 ng/ml), and FGF (100 ng/ml) (fig. S1C). mVNs anastomosed into single spheroids to form growing mBVOs at day 21, which further matured until day 30 (Fig. 1B).

mBVOs recapitulated the structural characteristics of blood vessels in vitro. They self-assembled into networks comprising CD31+ ECs lined with NG2+ and PDGFRβ+ perivascular cells already detected at the mVN stage (day 13) until the growing mBVO stage (day 21) (Fig. 1C and fig. S1D). Using 3D imaging of whole mount and cleared mBVOs, we confirmed 3D networks of CD31+ ECs (Fig. 1D and movie S1) supported by the synthesis of a laminin- and collagen IV–rich basement membrane (Fig. 1E), altogether forming lumenized vascular tubes (Fig. 1F).

To assert the reproducibility of mBVO differentiation, we applied our protocol to two additional mESC lines derived from C57BL/6 wild-type (WT; B6) and from 129S6 WT (129) mouse strains (32, 33). Starting from pluripotent colonies (fig. S1A), we differentiated B6 and 129 mESCs following the same key steps during an identical timeline (fig. S1E) and successfully generated mBVOs with comparable characteristics (fig. S1, F and G). However, we optimized seeding densities required for EB formation for each cell line, i.e., 10,000 cells/ml and 5000 cells/ml for B6 and 129 mESCs, respectively. Although our method is transposable, we quantified the lowest sprouting efficiency with B6 mESCs, with 40% of single mVNs dissected from gels and further grown into single mBVOs. Moreover, 50% of 129-derived VNs were dissected and grown into mBVOs. This was comparable to B6x129 mESCs, for which 60% of mVNs were selected to grow into mBVOs (Fig. 1G). These observations are in line with reports showing varying differentiation potential across different strains of mESCs (34).

To measure differentiation efficiency in vitro, we generated mBVOs using mESCs derived from C57BL/6 Cdh5 iCre x mTmG mice (B6-Cdh5-iCre x mTmG) (Fig. 1H) (35, 36). The induction of cre recombinase upon an overnight treatment with 1 μM 4OH-tamoxifen at day 9 turned Cdh5 or VE-cadherin–expressing cells into green fluorescent protein (GFP)+ reporter cells, while nonexpressing cells or non-ECs remained tomato+. With this system, we were able to efficiently trace mBVO development from the sprouting mVN stage at day 10, during anastomosis, and until maturation at day 30 (Fig. 1I and fig. S2A), while cre-negative control mESCs differentiated into tomato+ mBVOs only (fig. S2B). We used this direct fluorescent readout to quantify the differentiation efficiency at the sprouting mVN stage (fig. S2C) and to exclude poorly differentiated mBVOs (fig. S2D). This screening method improved the efficiency compared to our initial quantifications, which were solely based on the morphology of dissected mVNs (Fig. 1J).

To validate mBVOs as bona fide blood vessels, we implanted them at day 21 into the renal capsule of WT mice, a well-established anatomical site that is permissive to grow and assess the vascularization of cells, tumors, and organoids (Fig. 2A) (37). Transplanted mBVOs were stable and visible when dissecting kidneys (Fig. 2B) and still contained networks of CD31+ cells (fig. S3A). We analyzed B6-VE-cad-GFP reporter mBVOs to track VE-cadherin+ cells transplanted into C57BL/6 (B6) mice. We assessed perfusion with intravenous injection of fluorescently labeled lectin before euthanasia. After 4 weeks, while transplants were easily detected because of the ubiquitous expression of tomato, we observed small and few VE-cadherin+ lectin+ vessels, mostly located at the periphery of transplants (Fig. 2C). At this stage, mBVOs only started to anastomose with the host vasculature. At 12 weeks, we observed bigger and more VE-cadherin+ lectin+ vessels located in the core of transplants (Fig. 2C). Thus, mBVOs require a long maintenance in vivo to achieve a full perfusion in WT mice. We performed complementary trials with GFP-labeled B6 mBVOs grafted into syngeneic B6 mice and GFP-labeled B6x129 mBVOs grafted into allogenic B6 mice. After 8 and 12 weeks, we observed lectin+ vessels in both syngeneic and allogenic conditions (Fig. 2D and fig. S3B and movies S2 and S3). We detected GFP+ cells adjacent to lectin signal, confirming that lectin-perfused vessels originated from the mBVO transplant which anastomosed with the host vasculature (Fig. 2E and movie S4). On the basis of the morphology of αSMA+ cells within transplants, we identified small capillaries, arterial-like and venous-like vessels, suggesting an in vivo maturation of mBVOs upon engraftment (fig. S3C). Together, we developed a reproducible and efficient method to differentiate mESCs into bona fide blood vessel organoids (mBVOs) that recapitulate the structural and functional characteristics of murine blood vessels in vitro.

mBVOs are heterogeneous and plastic

Given their highest differentiation potential in vitro (Fig. 1G), we extensively characterized B6x129 mBVOs by flow cytometry [fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)] and immunostainings at day 21, leading to the identification of three main lineages. Evidently, mBVOs entailed ECs expressing pan-endothelial markers such as CD31, VE-cadherin, and CD34 (Fig. 3A) and representing ≈15% of total live cells (Fig. 3, B and D). The mesenchymal subset was the main component of mBVOs, accounting for ≈80% of total live cells (Fig. 3B). Because of the known overlapping expression of markers, we broadly identified (i) NG2+, PDGFRβ+, and αSMA+ mural cells adjacent to CD31+ ECs, (ii) αSMA+, PDGFRβ+, and CD44+ stromal/mesenchymal cells homogenously distributed within mBVOs (Fig. 3A), and (iii) CD73+, CD90+, and CD73+CD90+ mesenchymal stem-like cells also expressing PDGFRβ (Fig. 3, B to D, and fig. S4A). We defined a third subset of CD45+ immune cells representing ≈3% of total live cells, which we classified as macrophages of myeloid origin, 95% of which expressed CD64 (Fig. 3, B and D). These cells also expressed CD68 in the vicinity of CD31+ ECs (Fig. 3A). Overall, the cellular composition of mBVOs was comparable to human BVOs (13), although myeloid differentiation was increased with our murine method.

To gain more insight into the cellular composition and transcriptional changes of cultured mBVOs over time, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing of growing (day 21) and mature (day 30) mBVOs using a 10x Genomics Flex approach. A minimum of 15 mBVOs were pooled for each time point resulting in a total of 44,202 cells sequenced. After quality control and correction of batch effect using cluster similarity spectrum (CSS), we visualized cell transcriptomes via uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) embeddings (Fig. 3E and fig. S4B). Hierarchical clustering of top differentially expressed genes (DEGs) revealed three main lineages reciprocal to those identified by immunophenotyping. Namely, an EC compartment (EC) expressing pan-endothelial genes such as Pecam1, Cdh5, Cd34, Cd93, and Cldn5, a mesenchymal compartment (MES) encompassing fibroblasts and mural cells that broadly expressed Col1a1, Col1a2, Col6a1, Col6a2, Pdgfrb, Rgs5, and Acta2, and a macrophage cell compartment (MAC) expressing not only high levels of Ptprc but also myeloid-specific markers Cd68, Csf1r, and complement components C1qa and C1qb (Fig. 3F and table S1). We noted a high degree of modularization of the lineage compartments, especially within endothelial and macrophage subsets (fig. S4C).

First, we appreciated the heterogeneity of ECs within nine clusters (EC1 to EC9) (Fig. 3G; fig. S5, A and B; and table S1). Examining differential gene expression, we easily identified EC7 as cycling ECs, expressing proliferation and cell cycle–related genes such as Mki67, Top2a, Birc5, and Cdk1 (Fig. 3H). EC8 specifically expressed organotypic brain-specific genes such as Slc1a2, Slc1a3, and Aldoc and was thus defined as a neural-like EC cluster (Fig. 3H). We defined EC9 as an embryonal cluster expressing keratin and Rhox family genes, as well as developmental vascular remodeling genes Tpbpa and Tpbpb (Fig. 3H). There was a marked overlap between clusters EC3, 4, 5, and 6, which shared modules (fig. S5A) and expressed high levels of collagen family genes and Pdgfra, reminiscent of the MES compartment. Moreover, EC3 and 4 were enriched for mural cell–related genes such as Pdgfrb, Pdgfa, Acta2, and Rgs5 (Fig. 3H). Clusters EC1 and 2 had a notable absence of specific gene modules, with top marker genes being broadly expressed by all EC subclusters (fig. S5A). We did not identify lymphatic gene expression in any of the EC clusters (Fig. 3H). Next, we used AUCell to analyze the expression of consensus gene signatures of arterial, venous, and capillary EC subtypes extracted from the dataset of Kalucka et al. (25) (table S2). While there were clear biases toward specific phenotypes, with EC1 having a more capillary identity, EC3 having a more arterial identity, and EC5 and 6 having a more venous identity, most clusters expressed signatures of multiple subtypes (Fig. 3I). We singled out CD31+ ECs expressing the postcapillary venule markers vWF and COUP-TFII, altogether confirming the potential for venous specification within mBVOs (fig. S5C). Further, we assessed tip and stalk phenotypes of ECs using marker gene signatures and by calculating enrichment scores as defined by Pan et al. (38) (table S2). While clusters EC1, 3, 5, and 7 were more enriched for tip cell markers, clusters EC3, 5, and 7 also had enrichment of stalk cell markers. In contrast, cluster EC6 had a clear stalk cell bias (Fig. 3J and fig. S5D). When comparing the day 21 and day 30 time points with each other, we found that the embryonal cluster EC9 and cluster EC2 disappeared at the mature stage, while the neural-like cluster EC8 appeared. The proportions of tip/stalk-like clusters EC3 and EC5 increased over time, while those of the cycling cluster EC7 decreased (Fig. 3K). To further explore these differences across a pseudotemporal axis, we applied CytroTRACE 2, a machine learning algorithm for prediction of differentiation potential (39, 40), and CellRank to visualize the directionality of state transitions (41, 42). As expected, EC9 had the most undifferentiated phenotype, followed by EC2, 4, and 6. In contrast, EC3, 5, 7, and 8 had distributions more characteristic of state transitions, while EC1 had a more differentiated phenotype (Fig. 3L). Moreover, the projection of the pseudotime-biased transition matrix onto the UMAP embedding confirmed that all ECs across clusters transitioned toward EC1 (Fig. 3M and fig. S5E). Together, the single-cell-transcriptomic analyses infer several plastic EC subclusters with differing venous, arterial, and tip/stalk expression characteristics that subsequently transition over time into more mature states.

Second, we examined the diversity of the mesenchymal lineage entailing 12 distinct subclusters (MES) based on differential gene expression (Fig. 4A; fig. S6, A and B; and table S1). We easily identified MES8, 9, and 11 as cycling clusters through the expression of proliferative and cell cycle–related genes (Fig. 4B). Using hierarchical clustering of the top DEGs, we identified that cluster pairs MES7/8 and MES9/10 were concordant, having shared modules (fig. S6A). We defined clusters MES7 and MES8 as neural-like based on the expression of brain-specific genes similar to those of cluster EC8 (Fig. 4B). Cluster MES12 specifically expressed lipid-related genes such as Apoe, Cd36, and Adipoq (Fig. 4B). To better discriminate mural and fibroblast-like clusters, we used AUCell enrichment to apply consensus signatures extracted from the dataset of Muhl et al. (43) (table S2). While clusters MES6 to 10 had low enrichment of both fibroblast and mural enrichment scores, MES1, 3, and 11 were biased toward a mural phenotype expressing Rgs4, Rgs5, and Kcnj8 (Fig. 4B and fig. S6C). As MES1 also exhibited the highest Acta2 levels, this cluster may lean more toward a smooth muscle cell state. MES2, 4, 5, and 12 exhibited fibroblast expression phenotypes (Fig. 4B and fig. S6C). When comparing the day 21 versus day 30 time points, we observed a marked reduction of cluster MES5, as well as a moderate reduction of cluster MES3 at day 30 (Fig. 4C). This was accompanied by an increase of cluster MES4 and the appearance of day 30–specific clusters MES7, 8, 9, 10, and 12 (Fig. 4C). Using CytoTRACE 2, we calculated the differentiation potential among MES clusters, and unexpectedly, we observed that day 30–specific clusters were more undifferentiated compared to clusters that were expressed at both time points (Fig. 4D). Using CellRank to project the transition matrix onto the UMAP, we observed that cells tended to differentiate in the direction of cluster MES1 (Fig. 4E and fig. S6D).

Last, we characterized the macrophage compartment (MAC), consisting of four clusters (Fig. 4F; fig. S7, A and B; and table S1). We defined MAC3 as a cycling cluster (Fig. 4G). Cluster MAC1 highly expressed the canonical markers Csf1r and Fcgr1, as well as complement components C1qa and C1qb, and the immune response gene Tyrobp (Fig. 4G). MAC2 was defined by collagen family genes and Pdgfrb, as well as genes involved in the remodeling of the extracellular matrix (ECM) and angiogenesis, such as Mmp2, Mmp9, Pdgfa, and Vegfa (Fig. 4G). MAC4 appeared to have a mixed phenotype, expressing classical endothelial markers such as Pecam1, Cdh5, Cd34, Cd93, and Cldn5, as well as genes related to ECM-remodeling and angiogenesis (Fig. 4G). Hierarchical clustering of the top DEGs confirmed a high level of modularization between subclusters (fig. S7A). Next, we used AUCell to assess macrophage function using canonical markers of inflammation (table S2) (44). There were no clear differences in pro- or anti-inflammatory enrichment across cluster identity, save for a slight proinflammatory-like tendency in the MAC3 cluster (fig. S7C). Exploring changes in composition across the two time points, we noted that although there were no time point–specific clusters, day 21 had a greater abundance of MAC1, while day 30 had a greater abundance of MAC2 (Fig. 4H). Next, we examined relative differentiation potential across subclusters, finding that MAC1 had a more differentiated distribution, while clusters MAC2, 3, and 4 had a distribution more characteristic of state transitions (Fig. 4I). Increased differentiation potential appeared correlated with increased enrichment scores of both M1-like and M2-like markers, with the most differentiated cluster (MAC1) having the highest enrichment of both scores (fig. S7C). Projecting a transition matrix onto the UMAP embedding, we found that cells tended to differentiate in the direction of cluster MAC1 (Fig. 4J and fig. S7D).

Together, this extensive characterization highlights the remarkable heterogeneity of mBVOs analyzed from the growing stage (day 21) until maturation (day 30). We demonstrated the plasticity of the EC, mesenchymal, and macrophage compartments.

mBVOs respond and adapt to inflammatory and antiangiogenic stimuli

The inclusive composition of not only mesenchymal cells but also hematopoietic cells in mBVOs is of advantage as blood ECs and hematopoietic cells are intimately linked. For example, ECs up-regulate leukocyte adhesion molecules in response to inflammatory cues and down-regulate those during wound healing (45). We therefore interrogated how mBVOs would respond to proinflammatory stimuli. First, we activated the tumor necrosis factor receptor (TNFR) and the lymphotoxin β receptor (LTβR) axes, two major nodes of inflammation that are important regulators of vessel remodeling (Fig. 5A) (46). Following single treatments with either tumor necrosis factor–α (TNFα; 100 ng/ml) or a potent agonist of LTβR (LTβRAg) (47) (1 nM) for 72 hours, mBVOs responded with a strong up-regulation of intercellular adhesion molecule–1 (ICAM-1) from 20 to 60% of CD31+ ECs. ICAM-1 was also up-regulated overall in the CD31− compartment, suggesting an additional stromal response within mBVOs following these treatments (Fig. 5, B and C). Both TNFR and LTβR axes are known to activate downstream effectors mainly via the nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) pathway, however using different signaling routes, i.e., canonical for TNFR versus noncanonical for LTβR (48). When we examined the expression of RelB, a direct transcriptional readout of the noncanonical NF-κB pathway, we confirmed its specific nuclear induction in 30% of CD31+ ECs after stimulation with LTβRag (Fig. 5, B and D). Upon TNFα treatment, RelB remained in the cytoplasm of ECs, suggesting preferential signaling via the canonical axis. Similar to ICAM-1, RelB was also induced in the CD31− compartment, hinting again at a systemic stromal response within mBVOs (Fig. 5, B and D, and fig. S8A). FACS analyses of treated VOs revealed that TNFα doubled the population of CD45+ CD64+ macrophages (Fig. 5E). Neither of these treatments significantly modified the proportion of CD31+ ECs and CD31−CD45−PDGFRβ+ mesenchymal cells nor changed the identity of CD64+ macrophages (fig. S8, B to E). The ubiquitous increase of ICAM-1 following both treatments led us to examine more specific inflammation-related readouts. Mucosal vascular addressin cell adhesion molecule 1 (MAdCAM-1), the ligand for α4β7 integrin, is specifically expressed in ECs to direct leucocyte migration into mucosal (fig. S8F) and inflamed tissues (49). We measured a 10-fold increase of MAdCAM-1 expression in the CD31−CD45−PDGFRβ+ mesenchymal cells of TNFα-treated mBVOs (Fig. 5F and fig. S8G), but not in CD31+ ECs (Fig. 5G). Next, we tested the response of mBVO ECs to anti-VEGF therapy as growing ECs, not only during embryonic development but also in tumors, greatly depend on VEGF signaling (50). Congruently, mBVOs rely on continuous supplements such as VEGF-A to maintain their identity, from early stages of vascular induction until later stages of maturation between day 21 and day 30. Blocking the VEGF/VEGFR2 signaling in day 21 mBVOs with B20, a VEGF-neutralizing antibody, or DC101, a VEGFR2 antagonist (51), until day 30 (Fig. 5H) inferred a significant decrease of the mBVO size with both treatment modalities (Fig. 5I). This was accompanied by a significant reduction in vessel density in a dose-dependent fashion, with a more marked effect following B20 treatments (Fig. 5, J and K). In addition to vessel pruning, the vascular architecture of mBVOs changed as the remaining vessels were less covered by PDGFRβ+ perivascular cells (Fig. 5, J and L). Together, these in vitro perturbations highlight the adaptability of mBVOs as complex organoid systems. Following proinflammatory stimulations, ECs adopted an inflamed phenotype together with a systemic response from other important components, namely, the macrophages and the mesenchymal stromal cells. Sustained antiangiogenic blockade also induced systemic consequences markedly affecting vessel integrity.

mBVO remodeling upon integration of glioblastoma cells into assembloids

It is well-recognized that blood vessels undergo morphological and functional abnormalities in tumors. Glioblastoma (GBM) multiforme is an aggressive, fast-growing, and invasive brain tumor with pathological hallmarks of hypoxia with palisading necrosis and angiogenesis. GBMs and lower grade glioma infiltrate the brain by percolating into the brain parenchyma or migrating along basement membranes as such of blood vessels, neurons, and meninges (52, 53). To date, in vitro modeling of specific GBM–blood vessel interactions has been technically challenging, mainly because brain or GBM organoid models lack a functional vasculature.

Thus, we took advantage of the mBVO platform to integrate two genetically engineered murine GBMs with distinct modes of cell invasion. Both NFpp10-GFP and NSCG-mCherry cells were generated from neural stem cells and represent common genomic alterations reminiscent of the human disease. NFpp10-GFP cells are deficient in NF1, p53, and PTEN, while NSCG-mCherry GBM cells lack Arf/Ink4a and express EGFRvIII, a common mutation of EGFR. Upon orthotopic injections into syngeneic mice, both models recapitulate the invasive nature of human GBM in vivo. NFpp10 tumors grow with single cells at the tumor rim migrating along blood vessels and into the brain parenchyma, while NSCG tumors are more invasive and display tumor cell clusters coopting blood vessels in the brain parenchyma (51, 54, 55).

To effectively incorporate tumor cells into mBVOs, we generated mBVO-GBM assembloids. This method has been used in several organoid systems (56) and, more recently, in brain-specific organoids in combination with human BVOs (16, 17). At day 21, we reembedded two mBVOs together with either one NFpp10-GFP or one NSCG-mCherry tumor spheroid into single collagen I/Geltrex droplets which we cultured until day 30 (Fig. 6A). To ensure homogeneity between assembloids, both NFpp10 and NSCG spheroids averaged a diameter of 400 μm (fig. S9A). After carefully assembling droplets in which mBVOs surrounded GBM spheroids (fig. S9B), we observed their fusion over time with resprouting of mBVOs to fully engulf GBM spheroids. We tracked NFpp10-GFP and NSCG-mCherry fluorescent cells until integration and formation of single mBVO-GBM assembloids at day 30 (Fig. 6B). Using whole mount 3D confocal imaging, we confirmed a rather uniform incorporation of GBM cells which penetrated VNs composing mBVOs (Fig. 6C, fig. S9C, and movies S5 to S7). We, however, also noticed differences between the two mBVO-GBM assembloids. The presence of NFpp10 cells did not alter the vessel density of mBVOs and resembled a network similar to that of control assembloids. In contrast, NSCG assembloids displayed a substantially reduced vessel density (Fig. 6D). The growth pattern of GBM cells within mBVOs was notably different. While a subset of NFpp10-GFP cells remained as a spheroid cluster in between and around mBVOs, predominantly single GFP+ cells invaded mBVOs along the vasculature. This strongly resembled their growth pattern in the brain, with cells adopting an invasive phenotype along blood vessels at the tumor rim (Fig. 6E and fig. S9D). A subset of NSCG-mCherry cells also remained as clusters in between and around mBVOs; however, some cells moved and expanded as small and larger clusters in mBVOs, thereby disrupting the mBVO vasculature. This growth pattern is also observed when NSCG-mCherry cells are injected in the brain where they proliferate and colonize the brain as vessel-coopting tumor clusters (Fig. 6F and fig. S9E). In addition, the brain-specific enzyme aldolase C (ALDOC, encoded by the Aldoc gene) and the glutamate transporter EAAT2 (encoded by the gene Slc1a2) were significantly up-regulated in ECs and, to a lesser extent, in mesenchymal cells when GBM cells were integrated (fig. S10, A to D). This is in line with our single-cell transcriptomic data which identified neural-like signatures including these proteins in subclusters EC8 and MES7/8 at day 30 (Figs. 3H and 4B and fig. S10, E and F). This suggests that the presence of GBM cells within assembloids increases this phenotype in the vascular compartment of mBVOs. We observed elevated levels of the glucose transporter GLUT1 in mBVO-NSCG, but not in mBVO-NFpp10 assembloids, triggering an endothelial-specific up-regulation of GLUT1 in 50% of CD31+ vessels but also in the CD31− compartment (Fig. 6, G and H). This may be because of the disrupting growth pattern of NSCGs causing an altered and dynamically remodeled vasculature that needs to metabolically adapt to the presence of NSCG cells. In summary, we successfully integrated distinct GBM cell types within mBVOs using an assembloid fusion method and recapitulated some of the key features of GBM–blood vessel interactions commonly observed in vivo.

DISCUSSION

Blood vessel organoids provide an excellent platform for studying the molecular regulators of vessel formation, mimicking vasculogenesis at early time points and angiogenic remodeling at later stages (57). ES cells undergo multilineage differentiation into diverse endothelial, mesenchymal, and hematopoietic cells, which is a clear advantage over 2D culture systems. While there have been several recent studies that successfully used human pluripotent stem cells to form BVOs, we have generated here bona fide mBVOs by carefully adapting the human protocol (12, 13). We developed a fluorescent tracer system to follow proper mBVO differentiation and maturation, which allowed us to quantify and improve the differentiation efficiency by screening and selecting the “good” VE-cadherin-GFP+ networks. Another advantage of our mBVOs is that they can be derived from distinct mESC strains and from genetically engineered mESCs of transgenic or knockout mice, easing studies of vascular regulators and pathways.

Using a combination of immunostainings, FACS, and sc-transcriptomics of mBVOs at their growth spike (day 21) and full maturation stage (day 30), we identified three main cell lineages resembling 25 subclusters, comprising ECs, mesenchymal cells, and macrophages. A range of ECs with a high differentiation potential expressed endothelial, mesenchymal, and mural markers, suggestive of a common progenitor and reminiscent of the process of embryonic EndoMT. More differentiated subclusters at the trajectory endpoint resembled generic ECs with a canonical signature. Transition subclusters expressed cycling genes and up-regulated proangiogenic signatures. These results highlight that ECs retain plasticity and proliferative potential in mBVOs. Congruently, we observed overlapping identities of tip and stalk cells or arteries and veins in mBVOs, regardless of their differentiation stage, reflecting the plastic nature and differentiation potential of these EC populations. All of these observations were comparable to those of human BVOs (58).

We also provide a detailed characterization of hematopoietic cells within mBVOs, resembling different macrophage subclusters that were similar in their identity and proportions over time. Trajectory endpoint was toward the most differentiated cluster expressing functional macrophage markers. Transition subcluster MAC4 also expressed endothelial genes, hinting at the potential of hematopoietic to endothelial differentiation (59). These findings highlight differences with the human BVO data in which an immune cell component had been identified but not characterized in depth (58, 60). The mesenchymal cells displayed the prevalent and most diverse compartment with an overlapping expression of key marker genes across the different subclusters. Nonetheless, we identified more differentiated mural cells with pericyte and smooth muscle cell features, while less differentiated subclusters expressed fibroblast-associated genes. At day 30, new subclusters were identified, with distinct differentiation scores and cycling features. Thus, over time, mBVOs acquire cellular complexity mainly in the stromal subset, yet they retain plasticity in the EC compartment.

Treatments with the proinflammatory molecules led to endothelial activation and ICAM-1 up-regulation. These treatments also induced up-regulation of ICAM-1 in CD31− cells of mBVOs, similar to the stromal adaptive response to inflammation in tissues. TNFα not only doubled the number of macrophages but also significantly up-regulated the expression of MAdCAM-1 in PDGFRβ+ mesenchymal cells. While mostly expressed in ECs of secondary and mucosal lymphoid organs or ectopically following inflammation (49), MAdCAM-1 is also expressed in marginal reticular cells (MRCs) of the spleen (61). Whether TNFα treatment induces an MRC phenotype in mBVOs requires further investigation. We also treated mBVOs with blockers of the VEGF/VEGFR2 axis and showed a dose-dependent effect on vessel density and morphology. B20 exerted more marked effects, probably due to a more systemic and quenching effect of VEGF, which is secreted not only by ECs but also by macrophages and mural cells. In future applications, mBVOs will be useful 3D platforms to screen new antiangiogenic agents or other innovative vascular-targeting therapies.

In the context of cancer, the heterogeneous nature of blood vessels is recapitulated, as evidenced by distinct vascular phenotypes depending on tumor types, ultimately contributing to differences in disease prognoses and therapy responses (62). Neovascularization in the form of angiogenic sprouting is commonly initiated during tumor progression (63). Tumors and metastases can also get access to the vasculature via vessel co-option, by migrating and growing along blood vessels (64). We, therefore, used two distinct glioma cell lines that exhibit distinct growth patterns around co-opted blood vessels and generated assembloids with mBVOs in which the tumor cells displayed the same distinctive cluster or single-cell growth pattern as in the brain. In turn, mBVOs responded to the presence of GBM cells by adopting a neural-like phenotype as evidenced by the increase in ALDOC and EAAT2 in the vascular and mesenchymal compartments. Furthermore, we observed similar metabolic adaptations as NSCG glioma cells, growing in clusters around blood vessels, but not NFpp10, migrating as single cells, caused stromal and vascular induction of GLUT1, a first step to angiogenic remodeling of the vasculature. Consistent with our findings, recent reports have shown glucose metabolic adaptations of the vasculature using the human BVO system (22, 23). Such generation of vascular tumor organoids opens up opportunities for drug testing on a high-throughput level. One can unravel and target angiocrine and pericrine communication cues independent of flow and hypoxia in these organoids (63).

Notwithstanding, genetically engineered mBVOs can be implanted into allogenic and syngeneic mice where they fully mature and anastomose with the existing host vasculature. This is specifically critical for studying the functional interregulation of immune cells and ECs. mBVOs, so far, enable the study of vascular-macrophage interactions, yet they lack a lymphoid compartment. For the past 20 years, various strategies targeting the tumor vasculature have been developed with overall disappointing results showing no significant improvement for patients (63). As it is now well-established that tumors are “wounds that never heal” because they unceasingly promote immunosuppression and neovascularization to sustain tumor growth (65), mBVOs integrating tumor cells and a broader range of immune cell types may offer a suitable platform to decipher these complex mechanisms. In conclusion, our findings established mBVOs as relevant and versatile tools, opening up avenues for research and innovation in vascular biology.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental design

The goal of our study was to design an in vitro model of the mouse vasculature and to validate its use to interrogate key aspects of vessel remodeling during inflammation, angiogenic blockade, and integration of tumor cells. In the first part, we generated bona fide mBVOs using a total of four mESC lines derived from four different mouse strains. We adapted the already available human method (12) to mESC specifics as detailed below. We validated mBVO functionality in vivo by performing syngeneic and allogenic transplantations for 4, 8, and 12 weeks in the renal capsule of mice. We characterized mBVOs in detail using immunostainings (whole mount and cryosectioned samples), FACS, and single-cell RNA sequencing of pooled mBVOs at day 21 or day 30 (10x Genomics Flex). In the second part, we perturbed mBVOs with (i) extracellular stimuli that included TNFα and an LTβRAg to induce inflammation, (ii) antiangiogenic agents that included the VEGF neutralizing antibody B20 and the VEGFR2 inhibitor DC101, both strongly reducing vessel density, and (iii) the formation of assembloids with two GBM cell lines that recapitulated two distinct vascularization pattern.

Murine embryonic stem cells

mESCs were isolated and maintained as previously described (66–68). Cells from the C57BL/6 x 129S6 F1 hybrid mouse strain (B6x129) were a gift from P. Carmeliet and used in all experiments to generate murine blood vascular organoids (mBVOs). Cells derived from C57BL/6 WT (B6), 129S6 WT (129) (32, 33, 69, 70), and C57BL/6 Cdh5-iCre x mTmG (B6-Cdh5-iCre x mTmG) mouse strains were differentiated for additional experiments specified in figure legends (35). All murine ESC lines were cultured on mitotically inactivated (mitomycin C, 10 μg/ml, Sigma-Aldrich) MEFs isolated from CD1xDR4 mice and cultured in standard ESC culture medium composed of knockout Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10829-018), 20% ESC-qualified FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10270106), 2 mM l-glutamine (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 25030-024), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 11360-039), 0.1 mM each of nonessential amino acids (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 11140-035), 0.1 mM 2-mercaptoethanol (Sigma-Aldrich, M6250), penicillin:streptomycin (100 U:100 μg/ml; Thermo Fisher Scientific, 15140-122), and supplemented with mouse leukemia inhibitory factor (mLIF; 20 U/ml or 10 ng/ml; PeproTech, 250-02). As feeder preparation, MEFs were cultured on 0.1% gelatin in MEF medium consisting of DMEM high glucose (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 41965-062), 10% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10270106), 2 mM l-glutamine (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 25030-024), and penicillin:streptomycin (100 U:100 μg/ml; Thermo Fisher Scientific, 15140-122). All lines were passaged using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 25200056), and media was changed every other day. Mycoplasma contamination was ruled out on a regular basis, and maintenance of pluripotency was assessed by immunostaining.

mBVO differentiation

At the onset of differentiation (day 0), mESCs were seeded as EBs (31) at low densities in ultralow attachment (ULA) T25 cm2 flasks coated with poly(2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate) (5 mg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich, P3932), and mLIF was removed from the ESC culture medium. Seeding densities were optimized for each mESC line, i.e., 2500 cells/ml for the hybrid B6x129 line, 5000 cells/ml for the 129 line, and 10,000 cells/ml for B6 and B6-Cdh5-iCre x mTmG lines. EBs were grown for 3 days and resuspended daily to avoid fusion. Mesoderm lineage differentiation was initiated by replacing mESC medium without mLIF with N2B27 medium consisting of DMEM/F-12 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 11320-033): Neurobasal medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 21103-049) (1:1), 0.5% N-2 supplement (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 17502-048), 1% B-27 supplement (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 17504-044), bovine albumin fraction V 7.5% solution (0.5 μg/ml; Thermo Fisher Scientific, 15260-037), 2 mM l-glutamine (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 25030-024), penicillin:streptomycin (100 U:100 μg/ml; Thermo Fisher Scientific, 15140-122), and 150 μM monothioglycerol (Sigma-Aldrich, D6145), supplemented with murine BMP4 (30 ng/ml; PeproTech, 315-27). EBs were grown for another 3 days and resuspended daily to avoid fusion. Vascular lineage differentiation was induced with N2B27 medium supplemented with recombinant murine VEGF165 (100 ng/ml; PeproTech, 450-32) and 2 μM forskolin (Bio-Techne, 1099). EBs were grown for another 2 days and resuspended daily to avoid fusion. Sprouting was initiated upon embedding of EBs within collagen I/Geltrex gels (8:1). This mix was prepared fresh for every differentiation and consisted of 313 μl of MEM (10X) no glutamine (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 21430020), 460 μl of Ham’s F-12 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 11765054), 63 μl of 1 M Hepes (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 15630080), 49 μl of 7.5% NaHCO3 (Sigma-Aldrich, S8761), 31 μl of 200 nM l-glutamine (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 25030-024), 25 μl of penicillin/streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 15140-122), 3.33 ml of bovine PureCol (3 mg/ml; Advanced Biomatrix, 5005), 500 μl of 1.0 N NaOH (Sigma-Aldrich, S2770) to adjust the pH at 7.4, and 750 μl of Geltrex LDEV-Free Reduced Growth Factor Basement Membrane Matrix (15 mg/ml; Gibco, A1413202). This solution was equally distributed over one 12-well plate (Corning, 3513) and allowed to polymerize for at least 2 hours at 37°C as a first layer. Next, EBs were resuspended in the same gel mix and equally distributed as a second layer at a final concentration of approximately 20 to 25 EBs in each well. After polymerization, N2B27 medium supplemented with 15% FBS, recombinant murine VEGF165 (100 ng/ml; PeproTech, 450-32), and recombinant basic fibroblast growth factor (100 ng/ml; PeproTech, 450-33), termed mBVO medium, was added on top of gels and refreshed every day. EBs were allowed to sprout for 5 days until they formed networks. These VNs were carefully selected on the basis of the morphology of sprouts, individually dissected under a stereomicroscope (Zeiss, Stemi 508) in sterile conditions and plated individually into 96-well round-bottom ULA plates (Corning, CLS7007) in 100 μl of mBVO medium. Networks developed into growing mBVOs for 8 days (day 21), which further matured for 9 days (day 30), and mBVO medium was refreshed every other day. mVNs (day 13), growing mBVOs (day 21) and mature mBVOs (day 30) were used for subsequent experiments.

Immunostaining and imaging

Pluripotent mESCs and mVNs in collagen I/Geltrex gels (day 13) were fixed for 20 min, and mBVOs (day 21 onward) were fixed for 1 hour with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at room temperature. For whole mount stainings, fixed mVNs and mBVOs were permeabilized and blocked for 2 hours in Dulbecco’s Phosphate Buffered Saline (DPBS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 14190144) with 3% FBS (Gibco, 10270106), 0.5 g of bovine serum albumin (Sigma-Aldrich, 10735094001), 0.5% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, T8787), 0.5% Tween 20 (Sigma-Aldrich, P9416), and 1% sodium deoxycholate solution (Sigma-Aldrich, D6750). For the preparation of frozen sections, fixed mBVOs (day 21 onward) were dehydrated in gradually concentrated (7.5, 15, and 30%) sucrose solutions (Sigma-Aldrich, S9378) and embedded in Tissue-Tek O.C.T. (Leica, 14020108926). Fourteen-micrometer-thick sections were cut using a Cryostar NX70 cryostat (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and collected on Superfrost Plus slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 22-037-246). Cultured mESCs and frozen sections were permeabilized and blocked for 45 min in DPBS with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, T8787) and 5% donkey serum (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 017-000-121). Primary antibodies listed in table S3 were diluted in respective blocking buffers and incubated overnight at 4°C. Alexa Fluor–conjugated secondary antibodies listed in table S4 were diluted 1:500 in respective blocking buffers and incubated for 1 hour at room temperature. Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole) (Sigma-Aldrich, D9564), and Dako fluorescence mounting medium (Agilent, S302380-2) was used for mounting of samples. A longer incubation time was used for whole mount stainings, i.e., up to 3 days at 4°C for primary antibodies and up to 3 days at 4°C for secondary antibodies under constant agitation. For 3D imaging, samples were cleared following the recommendations of the MACS clearing kit (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-126-719). Immunofluorescence images of cryosections were acquired on an inverted Observer Z1 microscope (Zeiss) linked to an AxioCam MRm monochrome digital camera (Zeiss) with objectives of ×10, ×20, and ×40 magnifications or on an inverted confocal Leica SP8 (Leica Microsystems) with objectives of ×40 and ×60 magnifications. Whole mount images were acquired on an inverted Leica SP8 Dive system (Leica Microsystems) with a 25× water immersion objective with a free working distance of 2.4 mm using the Leica Application Suite X (LasX) software. Overview merged tile images and individual Z-stacks were acquired at a step size of 1 μm. Movies were generated with LasX. Phase contrast and fluorescent images of live cells and growing mBVOs were acquired with a THUNDER Imager (Leica Microsystems) with objectives of ×10 and ×20 magnifications. All Images were analyzed and quantified with FIJI version 2.14 (71).

mBVO transplantation

Animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Research Advisory Committee of KU Leuven (ECD 058/2021) and were performed following the institutional and national guidelines and regulations. mBVOs (day 21) were transplanted under the kidney capsule of 6- to 8-week-old male C57BL/6 mice, with one mBVO implanted per mouse and the contralateral kidney used as a negative control (12, 37). To distinguish between the transplanted mBVO and the mouse endogenous vasculatures, GFP-labeled B6x129 hybrid and B6 mESCs were generated using lentiviral transduction as described previously (70, 72). GFP-labeled mBVOs were subsequently differentiated following our protocol. In addition, mBVOs from B6-Cdh5-iCre x mTmG mESCs were differentiated in vitro, and cre recombination was induced following an overnight treatment with 1 μM 4-hydroxytamoxifen (Sigma-Aldrich, SML1666) at day 9, when networks were sprouting. For analyses of perfusion, DyLight 649 Lycopersicon esculentum (tomato) lectin (Labconsult, DL-1178-1) was intravenously injected (50 μg/100 μl). Fifteen minutes after injection, mice were deeply anesthetized and perfused with cold phosphate-buffered saline followed with cold 2% PFA through the heart before tissue collection. Excised transplants were fixed with 4% PFA for 1 hour at 4°C and processed as whole mounts or frozen sections as described previously.

mBVO treatments

For proinflammatory treatments, B6x129 mBVOs were treated once at day 21 for 72 hours with murine TNFα (100 ng/ml; PeproTech, 315-01A) and 1 nM LTβR agonist nanobody (47) (P00200016, Oncurious). For antiangiogenic blockade, B6x129 mBVOs were treated every 2 days from day 21 until day 30 with anti-mouse VEGF (10 or 100 μg/ml; B20 biosimilar) (51) and with InVivoMAb anti-mouse VEGFR2 (DC101) (10 or 100 μg/ml; BioXCell, BE0060). Control conditions included B6x129 mBVO treated with DPBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific,14190250).

mBVO-GBM assembloids

NFpp10-GFP GBM cells were obtained from Inder Verma at the Salk Institute and generated from embryonic C57BL/6 neural stem cells infected with shP53, shNF1, and shPTEN containing lentiviral vectors and carrying the GFP reporter (51, 54, 55). NSCG-mCherry were isolated from high-grade murine gliomas generated by transplantation of adult Ink4/Arf−/− neural stem cells deficient for CDKN2A and expressing EGFRvIII, a constitutively active mutant of human EGFR, and carrying the mCherry reporter (73, 74). For intracranial tumor transplantation, FvB/N and C57BL/6 mice (Charles River Laboratories) at 6 to 8 weeks of age were anesthetized and placed into a stereotactic apparatus. A burr hole was made 2 mm right of the sagittal suture and 0.5 mm anterior to the bregma. Five thousand NSCG-mCherry or 20,000 NFpp10-GFP cells were slowly injected in a volume of 2 μl into the right striatum at a depth of 3.0 mm from the dura and grown for 14 days before euthanasia by perfusion and subsequent histology, as previously described. In vitro, both cell lines were grown in suspension in DMEM/F-12 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 11320-033), 2% B27 supplement without vitamin A (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 12587-010), 0.05 M Hepes (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 15630080), penicillin:streptomycin (100 U:100 μg/ml; Thermo Fisher Scientific, 15140-122), recombinant human epidermal growth factor (10 ng/ml; PeproTech, AF-100-15), and recombinant human fibroblast growth factor–basic (10 ng/ml; PeproTech, AF-100-18B). Before their incorporation, single spheroids for each cell line were grown individually for 5 days in 100 μl of medium in 96-well round-bottom ULA plates. Seeding densities were optimized, with 5000 cells/ml for Nfpp10-GFP and 2500 cells/ml for NSCG-mCherry, to generate GBM spheroids averaging a diameter of 400 μM. For mBVO-GBM assembloid formation, two mBVOs (day 21) and one GBM spheroid of Nfpp10-GFP or NSCG-mCherry were resuspended in collagen I/Geltrex droplets. Using a pipet tip, we gently rearranged the droplets to surround each GBM spheroid with two mBVOs. Control conditions included two mBVOs per droplet without a central GBM spheroid. Droplets were allowed to polymerize upside down at 37°C for 45 min, and mBVO medium was added for 1 hour. Using forceps, single droplets were delicately transferred to single wells of 96-well round-bottom ULA plates and grown for 10 days. mBVO medium was refreshed every other day. mBVO-GBM assembloids were fixed and processed as whole mounts or frozen sections as previously mentioned. The successful integration of NFpp10-GFP or NSCG-mCherry cells was checked by the presence of GFP+ or mCherry+ cells, respectively. For NSCG-mCherry cells, we additionally stained for EGFR to detect them within assembloids.

Flow cytometry analyses of mBVOs (FACS)

mBVOs were pooled (≥8 per condition) and mechanically dissociated for 30 min at 37°C while rotating with a specific enzymatic mix, consisting of dispase I (3 U/ml; STEMCELL Technologies, 07913), liberase (2 U/ml; Roche, 5401160001), and 100 U of deoxyribonuclease (Worthington, LS006333) in DPBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 14190250). Single-cell suspensions were blocked using anti-CD16/32 blocking antibody (BD Biosciences, 553142). Samples were stained for 30 min on ice with antibody cocktails and viability dyes listed in table S5. A BD FACS LSR Fortessa II was used for sample acquisitions. Results were analyzed with FlowJo version 10.8.0 (Becton Dickinson, Ashland).

Single-cell RNA sequencing

mBVOs at day 21 or day 30 were pooled (≥20 per condition) and dissociated for 30 min at 37°C with the enzymatic mix previously described for FACS preparations. Single-cell suspensions were fixed with 4% formaldehyde, quenched, and counted following the guidelines of the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell Fixed RNA Sample Preparation Kit (10x Genomics, 1000414). Samples were then processed for the Single Cell Gene Expression Flex Fixed RNA Profiling assay (10x Genomics), which uses probes that target protein-coding genes in the human or mouse transcriptome. Each barcoded probe consists of a pair of oligonucleotides that hybridize to the targeted transcript and are subsequently ligated. The Chromium Mouse Transcriptome Probe Set v1.0.1 was used to target 19,405 genes with 55,479 probes. Three hundred forty-six (1.8%) of these genes (targeted by 351 probes) are excluded by default due to predicted off-target activity to a different gene. A total of 19,059 genes targeted by 55,128 probes are present in the final filtered output. Libraries were generated from the barcoded probes, and sequencing was done on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 with 28/10/10/90 cycles and 1% PhiX spike-in.

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis

Reads were mapped to the mouse genome (build mm10/release M23) and demultiplexed as necessary using CellRanger software (10x Genomics, version 7.1.0). Gene expression count matrices of mBVOs were analyzed using Seurat (v 5.1.0) in R (75). Quality control was done on cells using the following criteria: number of mapped reads (nCount_RNA) from 200 to 10,000; number of genes (nFeature_RNA) from 200 to 6000; and percent of mitochondrial genes <5%. Data were log-normalized using the default NormalizeData function in Seurat. Highly variable features were identified using the FindVariableFeatures function, and data were centered and scaled using the ScaleData function. Principal components analysis was applied on the scaled data for the first 50 principal components. CSS was used for sample integration with a cluster resolution of 0.8 (76). The resulting 25 dimensions from the integrated representation space were projected onto UMAP embeddings using the RunUMAP function for data visualization. Distinct cell clusters were identified through the generation of k-nearest neighbor (KNN) graphs and subsequent application of the Louvain algorithm on the CSS space via the FindNeighbours and FindClusters (resolution = 0.8) functions. Broad annotations were made on the basis of expression of canonical marker genes of macrophages, ECs, and mesenchymal cells. These were sorted in silico and again subclustered using the FindNeighbours and FindClusters functions. For each subcluster, marker analysis was performed using the Wilcoxon rank sum test with the FindMarkers function using default parameters. Marker genes were only considered if their Bonferroni corrected P values were significant at the α = 0.01 level. Dotplot and cluster proportion visualizations were made using the package SeuratExtend (v 1.1.0) (77). Marker genes were selected/visualized on the basis of highest log fold changes present within subcluster identities in combination with well-established marker genes based on previously published literature (25, 38, 43, 78). Modular heatmaps were generated using the package dittoSeq (v 1.18.0). Cluster-level enrichment of gene signatures was calculated using the AUCell algorithm and visualized in heatmaps through the R package escape (v 2.2.4) or in Cleveland plots using ggplot2 (v 3.5.0) (79, 80). Differentiation potential was calculated using the R package CytoTRACE2 (v1.0.0) using default parameters (39, 40). Relative scores were reverse scaled so higher values would indicate greater differentiation potential. CellRank2 (v 1.5.1) was used to assess initial and terminal differentiation states and project transition matrices onto UMAP embeddings (41). Directionality of differentiation identified from transition matrices was confirmed in higher dimensionality through generalized principal component analysis (41).

Quantifications and statistical analyses

Data entry and all analyses were performed in a blinded fashion. Bar graphs show mean values ± SEM. Unpaired Student’s t test (two-tailed) (Mann-Withney U test) was used for the comparison of two groups. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparing >2 groups as indicated in the corresponding legends. Statistics were indicated only when significant. P < 0.05 was considered significant (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001). All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10).
