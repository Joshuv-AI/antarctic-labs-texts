Gene–Environment Interactions for Alzheimer's Disease Pathology in Cognitively Normal Adults: The CABLE Study
Authors: Ze‐Xin Guo, Xiao‐Yu He, Yi‐Jun Ge, Bing Zhao, Liang‐Yu Huang, Shi‐Dong Chen, Yan Fu, Pei‐Yang Gao, Ze‐Hu Sheng, Yi‐Ming Huang, Qiong‐Yao Li, Lan Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCharacterizing the gene–environment interactions with early pathological changes in Alzheimer's disease (AD) is critical to precision medicine.MethodsWe recruited 1007 cognitively normal participants from the Chinese Alzheimer's Biomarker and LifestylE (CABLE) study. Multiple linear regression models were applied to explore the associations between polygenic risk scores (PRSs) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers of AD, the interactions between PRSs and potentially modifiable risk factors, and the relationships between lifestyle categories and CSF AD biomarkers.ResultsA higher AD‐PRS was associated with more severe amyloidosis, as indicated by pTau/Aβ42 (β = 0.091, p = 0.005) and tTau/Aβ42 (β = 0.092, p = 0.004). There were significant interactions between AD‐PRS and three modifiable risk factors (anemia, gingivitis, and anxiety) in AD biomarker ratios. Stratified analyses by AD‐PRS indicated that anemia was associated with higher pTau/Aβ42 and tTau/Aβ42 in the first and second quartiles, while gingivitis and anxiety correlated with amyloidosis in the fourth quartile (all p < 0.05). Additionally, a favorable lifestyle was associated with milder amyloidosis in the high genetic risk group.ConclusionsAD‐PRS was associated with amyloidosis severity. The associations between modified risk factors (anemia, gingivitis, and anxiety) and biomarker ratios differed by genetic risk strata. Moreover, a healthy lifestyle was associated with less amyloid burden in individuals with high genetic risk. These findings can be used to generate hypotheses for future longitudinal studies to investigate whether targeted management of these factors influences AD pathological progression.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers, gene–environment, lifestyle, neurodegeneration, polygenic risk score

Introduction

As the predominant form of dementia, Alzheimer's disease (AD) is characterized by a decline in cognitive function and eventual loss of self‐care capabilities [1]. In the context of global aging, the societal economic burden of AD will continue to escalate [2]. Despite the previous efforts in clinical trials, effective interventions remain elusive [2]. Consequently, there is a growing awareness of the pivotal role of prevention strategies for this disease. Moreover, as our understanding of the disease deepened, preventive approaches transitioned from complex methodologies to more targeted and precise interventions [2].

AD is a complex, multifactorial disorder. Genetics accounts for a substantial portion of the AD risk, with an estimated heritability of 60%–80% [3]. And polygenic risk scores (PRSs) calculated based on previous genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) enable a direct assessment of an individual's inherited susceptibility to AD risk [4]. In addition, various lifestyle and co‐morbidities can modify the risk of dementia [5]. AD‐related genetic variables modulate the impacts of environmental factors upon the development of AD and vice versa [6, 7]. Recent large‐scale population‐based studies demonstrated that vascular and metabolic risk factors were less strongly associated with clinical AD among APOE ε4 carriers [8], indicating that genetic susceptibility may substantially modulate the impact of modifiable medical risk factors. Furthermore, adherence to a healthy lifestyle has exhibited substantial potential in reducing the risk of dementia in people at high genetic risk [9]. However, little is known about whether these effects are present in core AD pathology.

Exploring the role of gene–environment interactions in AD pathology could yield new insights into slowing pathological progression, particularly through interventions tailored to genetically stratified populations. Here, leveraging data from the Chinese Alzheimer's Biomarker and LifestylE (CABLE) study, we specifically explored the link of PRSs to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers of AD, the interactions between PRSs and potentially modifiable risk factors in AD biomarkers, and the the potential of a healthy lifestyle in decelerating the AD pathological progression.

Methods

CABLE Database

The CABLE study began in 2017 to identify hereditary and lifestyle risk factors of dementia. Participants were recruited from Qingdao Municipal Hospital, Shandong Province, China. The inclusion criteria were Chinese Han ethnicity and age between 40 and 90 years. The exclusion criteria contained central nervous system infections, neurological diseases such as epilepsy and Parkinson's disease, family history of hereditary diseases, and history of lumbar spine surgery. All the participants underwent clinical and neuropsychological assessments, as well as determination of biochemical indices in blood and CSF. Cognitive function was estimated through the Mini‐mental State Examination (MMSE) by well‐trained clinicians 1 day before lumbar puncture. Cognitive impairment was diagnosed according to the MMSE score: ≤ 24 for participants with educational years more than 6, ≤ 20 for participants with 1–6 years of education, and ≤ 17 for illiterates [10]. Participants who had higher MMSE scores and had no clinical diagnosis of mild cognitive impairment or dementia were classified as cognitively normal. Demographic information was collected through the hospital's electronic information system and questionnaires. The CABLE study has got approval from Qingdao Municipal Hospital Ethics Committee. The participants or their proxies provided written consent.

Participants

Since AD pathology might occur before cognitive impairment, interventions before cognitive impairment may be most effective [11, 12]. Thus, we limited the study to cognitively normal people. We recruited 1046 participants from the CABLE study, of which 35 participants failing in quality control (QC) of PRSs were excluded (23 participants had a mismatch between genetically inferred and self‐reported sex, 10 participants had extreme heterogeneity, 1 participant was an outlier in principal components analysis, and 1 participant had both gender mismatch and extreme heterogeneity). Four participants without CSF AD biomarkers data were also removed from our study. Finally, 1007 participants were included in this cross‐sectional study. To assess potential selection bias, we compared baseline characteristics between included and excluded participants (Table S1) and found no significant differences in age, sex, education, APOE ε4 status, or MMSE scores (all p > 0.05).

Polygenic Risk Scores

We constructed PRSs for AD based on a previous GWAS [13], the largest publicly available multi‐ancestry fixed and random effects meta‐analysis for AD to date (54,233 ad cases, 46,828 proxy AD and related dementia cases and 543,127 controls). With the inclusion of East Asian individuals, PRSs derived using this multi‐ancestry dataset would perform better than using those of single ancestry [13]. To ensure single‐nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) alignment between the summary statistics and the target dataset, a unique rsID was retrieved for each variant using Annovar (http://www.openbioinformatics.org/annovar/ [14]). SNPs with minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.01, call rate > 0.95, sample missingness > 0.05, and Hardy–Weinberg Equilibrium's (HWE) p‐value < 1 × 10−6 were excluded, leaving 7,664,824 SNPs for the PRS calculation.

The weights for PRSs were calculated using a Bayesian framework implemented in PRS–CS [15] with default options to model linkage disequilibrium (LD) from 1000 Genomes Asian ancestry as a reference set. A continuous shrinkage (CS) prior was performed to obtain the posterior effect sizes of the summary statistics. The ‘–score’ command and ‘sum’ modifier in PLINK v2 [16] were then used to combine the resulting weights for each variant from PRS‐CS into a genome‐wide PRS. To create a PRS without the APOE loci, i.e., non‐APOE AD‐PRS, we excluded variants within the APOE region (chromosome 19:44.4–46.5 Mb) before CS due to the high LD in this region. The genetic polymorphisms from the APOE ε2/ε3/ε4 alleles, rs7412 and rs429358, were subsequently added to the QC summary statistics, and AD‐PRS was calculated as a weighted sum of non‐APOE AD‐PRS and APOE [17].

On the basis of previous research [18, 19], we applied a quadratic approach and divided the subjects into four groups based on the PRSs (first quartile, second quartile, third quartile, and fourth quartile) for stratified analyses.

Potentially Modifiable Risk Factors

Potentially modifiable risk factors for AD were selected based on the following criteria: (i) availability in the cohort, (ii) biological/epidemiological plausibility (e.g., Lancet Commission/major reviews [5]), and (iii) prior CABLE publications if relevant [20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. A total of 13 risk factors were included in the current study, consisting of (1) lifestyles: smoking, alcohol, physical activities, and social isolation, (2) clinical comorbidities and symptoms: hypertension, diabetes, obesity, anemia, gingivitis, depression, and anxiety, and (3) environmental factors: hearing impairment and traumatic brain injury. The definitions of these risk factors were presented in Table S2. According to the number of healthy lifestyles an individual has, participants were divided into unfavorable lifestyle group (≤ 2 healthy lifestyles), intermediate lifestyle group (three healthy lifestyles), and favorable lifestyle group (four healthy lifestyles).

CSF Biomarkers of AD

CSF was collected via lumbar puncture and sent to the laboratory within 2 h. After centrifuging at 2000 g for 10 min, CSF was aliquoted into enzyme‐free EP tubes and then stored in freezers at −20°C until assay. ELISA kits (Innotest β‐AMYLOID (1‐42) [catalog number: 81583]; PHOSPHO‐TAU (181p) [catalog number: 81581]; hTAU‐Ag [catalog number: 81579]; Fujirebio, Ghent, Belgium) were applied to determine the concentrations of CSF biomarkers. Blank control was set each time the assay was conducted, and each sample was determined twice. The freeze–thaw cycle was not allowed to exceed twice. Participants' information was kept confidential from operators. Inter‐ and intra‐batch coefficients of variability were limited to less than 15% and 10%, respectively.

Statistical Analyses

In our analyses, we included two biomarker ratios (pTau/Aβ42 and tTau/Aβ42) as dependent variables. As better predictors for Aβ deposition [26] and cognitive decline [27, 28, 29, 30], these two ratios were regarded as primary outcomes, with pTau, tTau, and Aβ42 as secondary outcomes. Using the “car” package in the R software, we applied Box‐Cox transformations [27] to the data of PRSs and biomarkers to normalize their distribution since they exhibited skewness (p < 0.001 for the Shapiro–Wilk test).

Multiple linear regression models were conducted with PRSs (AD‐PRS and non‐APOE AD‐PRS) as independent variables, CSF AD biomarkers as dependent variables and age, gender, education, and the first genetic principal component as covariates. Given the non‐linear age effects on neurodegeneration and common age × sex interaction, we evaluated whether extending the baseline (null) model to include (i) a quadratic age term (age2) and (ii) an age × sex interaction would improve model fit for each CSF biomarker. Model fit was assessed using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). The results showed that adding age2 or age × sex did not evidently improve model performance (ΔAIC < 2 for most models, and the baseline model presented the minimum β value, Table S3). Therefore, the final null model used in all primary analyses was: Biomarker ~ Age + Sex + Education + PC1. We assessed the performance of PRSs derived from fixed and random effects meta‐analysis estimates by measuring the incremental variance explained by the PRSs (Nagelkerke's pseudo‐R 2). This was evaluated as the difference in R2 between a full model (biomarkers ~ PRS + age + sex + education + first genetic principal component) and a null model (biomarkers ~ age + sex + education + first genetic principal component) in linear regression. Multiplicative interactions were examined using multiple linear regression models that included PRSs × modifiable risk factors as interaction terms. Among these modifiable risk factors, social isolation, anemia, gingivitis, depression, anxiety, and hearing impairment were considered as primary risk factors in our study, since previous studies have rarely reported their interactions with genes in dementia. We used a primary interaction set, including interactions between AD‐PRS and primary factors. We also performed an exploratory study on the interactions between AD‐PRS and other modifiable risk factors, defined as exploratory interaction set in our study. Since the APOE locus is the most crucial genetic risk locus for AD [31], we also used APOE genotypes (ε2/ε2, ε2/ε3, ε3/ε3, ε2/ε4, ε3/ε4, and ε4/ε4) as indicators of AD genetic risk to test the above relationships. Due to the limited sample size, our study applied weighted regression approaches where each genotype receives weights based on established AD risk levels [32]. Multiple linear regression was employed to test the associations between different lifestyle categories and CSF AD biomarkers.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding participants with borderline cognitive assessment scores (MMSE ≤ 25) or using raw data before Box‐Cox transformation. Unless stated otherwise, multiple corrections were implemented following the Benjamini‐Hochberg method [33]. Data analyses and graphing were executed using R software, version 4.2.1, and GraphPad Prism software, version 8.0.

PRSs and CSF AD Biomarkers

AD‐PRS, but not non‐APOE AD‐PRS, exhibited association with two biomarker ratios (Table S4, Figure 1). AD‐PRS constructed using fixed effect estimates was positively correlated with pTau/Aβ42 (β = 0.091, p = 0.005) and tTau/Aβ42 (β = 0.092, p = 0.004). When the models were re‐run with PRSs constructed using random effects estimates, the above relationships remained unchanged. No significant associations were observed between AD‐PRS and individual biomarkers (pTau, tTau, Aβ42) (Table S5). AD‐PRS constructed using fixed effect estimates explained higher variance of CSF biomarkers than those constructed using random effect estimates (Figure S1). Therefore, we selected the AD‐PRS constructed using fixed effect estimates for the following analyses. Most environmental risk factors in our study were also validated to be associated with amyloidosis in cognitively normal participants of the CABLE cohort [20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. Thus, amyloid‐related biomarkers were selected as dependent variables in the following analyses.

Interactions Between AD‐PRS and Modifiable Risk Factors

Significant interactions between AD‐PRS and three modifiable risk factors (anemia, gingivitis, and anxiety) in biomarker ratios were found (Figure 2A–C and Table S6). There were negative interactions between AD‐PRS and anemia in tTau/Aβ42 (β = −0.234, p = 0.047), though the results did not survive multiple corrections. Positive interactions between AD‐PRS and gingivitis in pTau/Aβ42 (β = 0.441, p = 0.015) and tTau/Aβ42 (β = 0.436, p = 0.010) were observed. Furthermore, there was a positive interaction between AD‐PRS and anxiety in pTau/Aβ42 (β = 0.405, p = 0.008) and tTau/Aβ42 (β = 0.461, p = 0.002). No significant results were observed for primary interaction set in Aβ42 (Table S7) and exploratory interaction set in two biomarker ratios (Table S8). The results were consistent when the null model was frozen and used to generate a propensity score for each participant (Tables S9–S11).

To figure out the contributions of genetic and modified risk factors in biomarker variance, we further decomposed the explained variance across sequential models, including (i) covariates only, (ii) covariates + AD‐PRS, (iii) covariates + AD‐PRS + each risk factor, and (iv) the full interaction model. AD‐PRS alone explained approximately 0.7% of biomarker variance (ΔR 2≈0.007), while modifiable risk factors contributed 0%–3.4%, depending on the biomarker and risk factor. Interaction terms accounted for an additional 0.3%–1.6% of variance (Table S12).

Additional stratified analyses by AD‐PRS were conducted, with standardized β coefficients reported to enable direct comparison of effect magnitude across risk factors. We found that anemia, gingivitis, and anxiety correlated well with these two biomarker ratios in populations at specific risk, but not in all populations (Figure 2D–F). In the lowest PRS participants (first quartile), anemia correlated well with rising tTau/Aβ42 (β std = 0.188, p = 0.021); in the lower PRS participants (second quartile), anemia correlated well with rising pTau/Aβ42 (β std = 0.180, p = 0.031) and tTau/Aβ42 (β std = 0.212, p = 0.009). However, in the highest PRS participants (fourth quartile), gingivitis and anxiety correlated well with rising pTau/Aβ42 (β std for gingivitis = 0.271, p = 0.017; β std for anxiety = 0.140, p = 0.044) and tTau/Aβ42 (β std for gingivitis = 0.269, p = 0.017; β std for anxiety = 0.183, p = 0.007).

A data‐driven approach was also applied to inform grouping strategies, and participants were divided into three groups (low PRS, medium PRS, and high PRS groups; Figure S2). Consistent with the results from stratified analyses by quartiles of PRS, gingivitis and anxiety were associated with rising pTau/Aβ42 (β std for gingivitis = 0.415, p = 0.002) and tTau/Aβ42 (β std for gingivitis = 0.349, p = 0.010; β std for anxiety = 0.207, p = 0.015) in the high PRS group; anemia was associated with tTau/Aβ42 (β std = 0.113, p = 0.018) in the medium PRS group (Figure S3).

Validation Using APOE Genotype Weight Score

Similar to AD‐PRS, APOE genotype weight score showed associations with biomarker ratios (Table S13) and exhibited interactions with gingivitis and anxiety (Table S15). To be specific, APOE genotype weight score was linked to increasing pTau/Aβ42 (β = 0.227, p = 0.004) and tTau/Aβ42 (β = 0.224, p = 0.004). APOE genotype weight score showed interactions with gingivitis in pTau/Aβ42 (β = 1.218, p = 0.007) and tTau/Aβ42 (β = 1.205, p = 0.004), as well as interactions with anxiety in pTau/Aβ42 (β = 0.951, p = 0.011) and tTau/Aβ42 (β = 1.094, p = 0.003). No significant results were found when exploring associations between APOE genotype weight score and individual biomarkers and interactions between weight score and primary risk factors in CSF Aβ42. (Tables S14 and S16).

Since many results remained the same when substituting AD‐PRS with APOE genotype weight score, we further compared their performance in the models. According to the calculated R 2, AD‐PRS and APOE genotype explained a similar proportion of biomarker variance (Table S17).

Lifestyle Categories and Amyloid‐Related Biomarkers

A favorable lifestyle was associated with reduced amyloidosis in high genetic risk individuals. A favorable lifestyle was linked to decreasing pTau/Aβ42 (β = −0.287, p = 0.007) and tTau/Aβ42 (β = −0.270, p = 0.010), as well as increasing Aβ42 levels (β = 0.255, p = 0.015). The association did not change in participants with high genetic risk (Tables S18 and S19). There was no interaction between AD‐PRS and the number of healthy lifestyles for amyloid‐related biomarkers (p > 0.05). We also examined the associations of combined genetic and lifestyle categories with CSF AD biomarkers. Noteworthily, we only used two genetic risk categories, consisting of a low genetic risk group (the first and second quartiles) and a high genetic risk group (the third and fourth quartiles), due to our relatively small sample size. Then, we obtained six subgroups and the one with high genetic risk and an unfavorable lifestyle served as the reference. Reduced genetic risk and a more favorable healthy lifestyle were found to be associated with reduced amyloid deposition (Tables S20 and S21, Figure 3).

Sensitivity Analyses

To reduce the influence of borderline scores, we repeated our analyses in a subset with MMSE score > 25 (n = 838). The results did not change significantly (Tables S22 and S23). AD‐PRS was positively associated with pTau/Aβ42 (β = 0.092, p = 0.008) and tTau/Aβ42 (β = 0.088, p = 0.010). Interaction analyses indicated that AD‐PRS interacted with gingivitis and anxiety in the associations with biomarker ratios (pTau/Aβ42 and tTau/Aβ42, β: 0.473–0.573, p < 0.05).

To assess the impact of the Box‐Cox transformation on the results, we also conducted our analyses using data without transformation, and the significance of our results changed. AD‐PRS correlated well with pTau/Aβ42 (β = 0.008, p = 0.035), but not with tTau/Aβ42 (Table S24). Interactions between AD‐PRS and three risk factors, including anemia, gingivitis, and anxiety, were still significant and maintained the same direction (AD‐PRS × anemia: β, −0.043 in pTau/Aβ42 and −0.345 in tTau/Aβ42; P, 0.019 in pTau/Aβ42 and 0.001 in tTau/Aβ42, AD‐PRS × gingivitis and AD‐PRS × anxiety: β, 0.051–0.062 in pTau/Aβ42 and 0.226–0.324 in tTau/Aβ42; all p = 0.001; Table S25).

Discussion

In this study, based on the cognitively normal population of the CABLE cohort, we found an association between AD‐PRS and amyloidosis as well as interactions between AD‐PRS and three modifiable risk factors (anemia, gingivitis, and anxiety) in two biomarker ratios. The associations between these risk factors and amyloidosis varied across different genetic risks. These results did not change significantly when substituting AD‐PRS with the APOE genotype weight score. Furthermore, a favorable lifestyle was associated with reduced amyloidosis, irrespective of genetic risk.

APOE ε4 has been found to accelerate early seeding of amyloid pathology, stabilize soluble cytotoxic oligomeric Aβ fragments, and enhance fibril formation [34, 35]. In our study, we found that a higher AD‐PRS, but not non‐APOE AD‐PRS, was associated with more severe amyloidosis. When replacing AD‐PRS with APOE genotype, these associations persisted. Both AD‐PRS and APOE genotype weight score had associations with amyloidosis, while non‐APOE PRS did not, suggesting that APOE genotype was the key to the link of AD‐PRS to amyloidosis. These outcomes were consistent with some prior studies reporting that only one allele, APOE ε4, could predict amyloid deposition [36, 37]. Nevertheless, some studies yield different findings, which showed that PRSs, both with or without the APOE locus, were positively associated with Aβ burden, pTau, and tTau [38, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43, 44]. The inconsistency can be attributed to differences in population ethnicity, cognitive status, and the way CSF biomarkers are measured. Despite this, our results support the application of PRS in evaluating genetically determined amyloidosis in the early stage of AD. Previous studies have reported there were interactions between APOE ε4 status and various modifiable risk factors in dementia incidence, including air pollution, physical activities, diet, traumatic brain injury, smoking, and alcohol consumption [6, 7, 45, 46]. More recently, Elias et al. [8] reported that vascular and metabolic risk factors exerted a weaker influence on clinical AD risk among APOE ε4 carriers in the UK Biobank, suggesting that strong genetic susceptibility may attenuate the contribution of certain systemic risk factors. Our study revealed there were interactions between AD‐PRS and three modifiable risk factors (anemia, gingivitis, and anxiety) in amyloidosis among 1007 cognitively normal Chinese Han participants. The association between anemia and amyloidosis was more obvious in the low genetic risk group, whereas those of gingivitis and anxiety with amyloidosis were more pronounced in the high genetic risk group. These findings suggest that the observed associations between modifiable risk factors and amyloidosis may vary with genetic risk background, highlighting potentially distinct, but still hypothetical, intervention targets that warrant testing in prospective studies. Our variance decomposition analysis indicated AD‐PRS explained approximately 0.7% of biomarker variance, with modifiable risk factors contributing to 0%–3.4% of the variance and their interactions accounting for 0.3%–1.6% of the variance (Table S12). Although these effect sizes are modest, they are detectable during preclinical stages. Similar results were found when exploring the interactions between APOE genotype and modifiable risk factors, including gingivitis and anxiety, suggesting that these interactions were predominantly mediated by the APOE locus. However, anemia had interaction with AD‐PRS rather than APOE in amyloidosis, suggesting a potential role of other variants outside the APOE region in the anemia‐AD PRS interaction, which is worth further investigation in larger samples.

The potential mechanism linking anemia to AD pathology might involve increased amyloid production and impaired amyloid degradation or clearance. A persistently low hemoglobin concentration induces brain tissue hypoxia, activating hypoxia‐inducible factor 1 (HIF‐1), a key hypoxia‐responsive transcription factor. HIF‐1 binds to the promoters of the β‐secretase 1 (BACE 1) gene, leading to up‐regulation of BACE 1 gene expression [47, 48]. BACE, in turn, promotes the catabolism of amyloid β precursor protein, resulting in amyloid production [49]. Additionally, hypoxia diminishes the levels and activity of neprilysin, a protease responsible for degrading Aβ [50]. Hypoxia also stimulates the up‐regulation of myocardin and serum response factor in brain vascular smooth muscle cells, contributing to reduced cerebral blood flow and impaired amyloid clearance [51, 52, 53]. Nevertheless, the reasons for the more pronounced association between anemia and amyloid in low‐genetic‐risk populations remain unclear. Our findings concerning anemia suggested that a strong genetic predisposition might overshadow the influence of certain environmental factors, although the underlying mechanisms are still unclear. This interpretation was consistent with a prior study which demonstrated that vascular and metabolic risk factors exerted a weaker influence on clinical AD risk among APOE ε4 carriers [8].

Gingivitis, a localized form of oral inflammation, triggers the release of pro‐inflammatory factors from periodontal inflammatory mediators, potentially breaching the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and initiating neuroinflammatory lesions [54, 55]. Astrocytes and microglia play crucial roles in the neuroinflammatory process [56, 57]. A1 astrocytes cause microcirculatory disturbances and impair BBB function, promoting amyloid deposition [58, 59]. Microglia release cytokines that upregulate BACE production, ultimately leading to rising amyloid levels [60]. A previous study found that phagocytosis of amyloid was diminished in microglia carrying APOE ε4 compared to those carrying APOE ε3 [61]. Additionally, the specific lipid composition of APOE ε4 affects the structure of lipid rafts on microglial cell membranes, inducing a stronger disease‐associated microglial cell phenotype and exacerbating amyloidosis [62]. APOE ε4 also enhances pro‐inflammatory activation of microglia [63], which will disrupt the integrity of the BBB [64]. Collectively, APOE ε4 may amplify the negative effects of local inflammation on brain amyloidosis. Anxiety can enhance pro‐inflammatory activation of microglia [65] and inhibit amyloid clearance through modulation of immune genes expressed by microglia [66], which acts in concert with APOE ε4 in exacerbating amyloid deposition.

A healthy lifestyle may attenuate AD pathology via amyloid clearance [67], increased cerebral blood flow [68], and reduced cerebral oxidative stress [69]. Prior studies have demonstrated that a favorable lifestyle correlated well with lower dementia risk [9, 70]. Our study classified participants into unfavorable, intermediate, and favorable lifestyle groups on the basis of the count of healthy lifestyles and explored their associations with CSF AD biomarkers. The results revealed that a favorable lifestyle was linked to lower amyloidosis within high‐genetic‐risk groups, underscoring the potential of adhering to a healthy lifestyle for slowing the progression of amyloidosis during the early asymptomatic stage of AD.

Limitations

There were several limitations. Firstly, the low prevalence of some modifiable risk factors (e.g., depression 1.7%, anxiety 1.8%) may limit statistical power and influence the stability of interaction estimates. Secondly, reliance on questionnaires for information on risk factors introduces potential recall bias and misclassification, particularly for lifestyle factors such as physical activity or alcohol consumption. Thirdly, our study lacked imaging data, such as magnetic resonance imaging and positron emission tomography. The addition of imaging data in future studies would further enhance the robustness of our findings. Fourth, data transformation due to the skewed distribution of the data influenced the statistical significance of our results. However, our findings of gene–environment interactions remained highly consistent and significant across both transformed and original data. Fifth, the strong effect of the APOE locus in this modest sample size may mask independent contributions from other genetic variants within the PRS, potentially limiting the incremental value of PRS beyond APOE alone. While PRS offers enhanced granularity for risk stratification and reveals APOE‐independent interactions (e.g., anemia), larger cohorts are needed to fully validate its additive clinical utility over weighted APOE genotype. Finally, our study was conducted based on cross‐sectional data and it could not explain any causal relationship. These hypothesis‐generating results underscore the need for longitudinal studies with repeated CSF biomarker assessments to determine whether modifying these risk factors can slow amyloid accumulation over time.

Conclusion

These findings suggest that genetic susceptibility may shape how modifiable risk factors relate to early AD pathology in cognitively noraml adults. Interactions involving anemia, gingivitis, and anxiety, together with the protective association of a favorable lifestyle in those at higher genetic risk, provide hypothesis‐generating evidence for future longitudinal studies on precision prevention strategies for AD.
