Association of lignan metabolites with prevalent stroke and mortality in stroke patients: results from NHANES
Authors: Wan Zhang, Dan Yu, Jingna Liu, Chunxia Yang, Mei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explores the association between urinary lignan metabolites (enterolactone and enterodiol) and prevalent stroke (self-reported history of stroke), as well as the long-term likelihood of all-cause mortality (ACM) in patients with a history of stroke. This study employed a mixed-methods design integrating cross-sectional and retrospective cohort analyses, leveraging data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) spanning the period 1999–2010. Eligible participants were those with complete data on urinary lignan metabolites (enterolactone and enterodiol), stroke status assessment, and long-term survival outcomes. Weighted logistic regression models were utilized to elucidate the association between enterolactone, enterodiol, and the likelihood of prevalent stroke, with subgroup analyses stratified by age and gender; effect sizes were quantified as odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The study included 9,752 participants, among whom 194 were dead patients. Follow-up was conducted through December 2019, with a total duration of 164.97 ± 0.87 months. The analysis revealed no statistical association between enterodiol and prevalent stroke or ACM in patients with a history of stroke. Using the first quartile of enterolactone as a reference, the fourth quartile group of enterolactone was significantly associated with a lower likelihood of prevalent stroke [OR: 0.64 (0.42–0.98)], particularly in males [OR: 0.43 (0.20–0.91)] and those under the age of 65 years [OR: 0.43 (0.19–0.97)]. Higher urinary enterolactone levels were associated with a lower likelihood of prevalent stroke and reduced ACM likelihood in patients with a history of stroke.Keywords: stroke, lignan, enterolactone, enterodiol, all-cause mortality, NHANES

Introduction

Stroke is an acute cerebrovascular disorder characterized by the sudden onset of focal or generalized neurological deficits resulting from vascular insufficiency or hemorrhage.(1,2) Clinically, it presents with a spectrum of manifestations, including hemiparesis/hemiplegia, speech disorders, altered consciousness, sensory impairments, aphasia, and visual disturbances.(3,4) According to the 2024 Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study, the incidence and prevalence of stroke among individuals aged <70 years have undergone a substantial upward trend.(5) The World Stroke Organization (WSO) further estimates that approximately 11.4 million new stroke cases occur annually worldwide, with 6.5 million associated deaths, rendering it the second leading cause of global mortality.(3,6) Owing to its high incidence, substantial disability burden, and elevated mortality rate, stroke constitutes a pressing public health concern globally.(7) Moreover, the majority of stroke survivors are at an increased likelihood of comorbidities such as anxiety, depression, and cardiovascular complications,(8,9) with the condition exerting a profound and multifaceted impact on physical health, mental well-being, social functioning, and overall quality of life.(10) Therefore, it is essential to focus on the clinical management of stroke patients.

Lignans, a class of bioactive phytoestrogens, are categorized as a pivotal subgroup of polyphenolic phytochemicals.(11) Upon dietary ingestion, lignans undergo biotransformation into bioactive metabolites—predominantly enterolactone and enterodiol—via the gut microbiota, thereby exerting regulatory effects on oxidative stress, inflammation, autophagy, and gut microbial homeostasis.(12–14) Notably, an early case-cohort study elucidated an inverse association between elevated enterolactone concentrations and the likelihood of coronary heart disease.(15) Accumulating contemporary evidence indicates that lignan metabolites confer potential salutary effects against a spectrum of human disorders. Specifically, urinary lignan metabolite levels, with enterolactone as a key marker, exhibit inverse correlations with inflammatory cytokines, glycolipid metabolic parameters, and blood pressure.(16–18) Concordantly, urinary lignan metabolite profiles have been linked to a reduced prevalence of depressive symptoms.(19) However, there have been no reports to date exploring the relationship between lignan metabolite levels and prevalent stroke (self-reported history of stroke). Concurrently, a study conducted by Reger found that high enterolactone levels were related to a lower mortality in the general population.(20) Eriksen’s research also found an association between plasma enterolactone levels and lower mortality in patients with type 2 diabetes.(21) However, the impact of lignan metabolites on mortality among stroke survivors remains to be elucidated.

Hence, this study aims to use data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database from 1999 to 2010 to investigate the association between urinary lignan metabolites (enterolactone and enterodiol) and prevalent stroke as well as the long-term mortality likelihood among patients with a history of stroke, thus providing observational evidence relevant to stroke-related outcomes.

Materials and Methods

Data source

The NHANES, orchestrated by the U.S. National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), is a comprehensive study that targets the health and dietary status of adult and child populations. Employing an intricate, multi-stage sampling methodology, this biennial survey tracks disease prevalence by assessing the health and nutritional profiles of the specified demographic groups.(22) Participants are required to provide written consent prior to participation. The NHANES dataset is accessible to the public and can be readily retrieved via the provided link: https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.htm.

Study design and population selection

The study design comprised two parts: The first part was a cross-sectional study observing the association between urinary lignan metabolites (enterolactone and enterodiol) and prevalent stroke (self-reported history of stroke), and the second part was a retrospective cohort analysis to investigate the long-term mortality likelihood in patients with a history of stroke. All data for this study were retrieved from the NHANES database covering the period 1999–2010, which comprises comprehensive datasets on urinary lignan metabolite concentrations, stroke status evaluations, and long-term survival and mortality outcomes. Demographic, clinical, and follow-up information of participants was collected through standardized structured interviews and systematic physical examinations, with the follow-up period extending until December 2019, yielding a median (± SE) duration of 164.97 ± 0.87 months. Eligibility criteria for study inclusion were defined as follows: (1) age ≥20 years at the time of enrollment; (2) availability of complete quantitative data on urinary enterolactone and enterodiol concentrations. Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) incomplete or missing data on stroke diagnosis; (2) lack of documented survival status during the follow-up period. Research factors and outcomes

The main research factors in this study were urinary lignan metabolites: enterolactone and enterodiol. These were determined based on the data from the NHANES database with variable names URXETL and URXETD, respectively. Urinary lignan metabolite concentrations were assessed from single spot urine samples collected during the NHANES examination. While this approach provides a practical measurement, it represents exposure at a single time point and may be influenced by recent dietary intake and diurnal variations. Notably, to correct for urinary dilution, the concentrations of enterolactone and enterodiol were standardized against creatinine, with the adjusted units being expressed as micrograms per gram (μg/g).(23) Specific data sources are as follows: Besides, the first outcome variable in this study was the self-reported history of stroke, ascertained based on the medical condition survey questionnaire (MCQ) item MCQ160f: ‛Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you had a stroke?’. This question has been widely used in previous NHANES-based studies for stroke identification.(24,25) Although NHANES does not provide detailed clinical information such as stroke subtype (ischemic vs hemorrhagic), severity scales, or neuroimaging confirmation, the self-reported stroke history via MCQ160f has demonstrated reasonable validity for epidemiological studies when compared with medical record review. Unfortunately, due to inherent limitations of the NHANES database, information regarding the time since stroke onset (i.e., the interval between stroke diagnosis and study enrollment) was not available for all participants. The second outcome variable was all-cause mortality (ACM) among stroke patients, determined according to the NHANES public-use linked mortality file (2019 release).

Definition of potential covariates

Based on clinical practice guidelines and published literature relevant to the topic, the following potential covariates were selected: age (years), gender (male/female), race (non-hispanic white/non-hispanic black/others), poverty-to-income ratio (PIR, ≤1.3% or >1.3%), diabetes (yes/no), hypertension (yes/no), heart disease (yes/no), cardiovascular agents (yes/no), antiplatelet agent (yes/no), dyslipidemia (yes/no), wheezing disturb sleep (yes/no), and physical activity (<750 or ≥750 met*min/week). Detailed operational definitions and measurement criteria for each covariate are provided in Supplemental Table 1*.

Statistical analysis

Given the multistage, complex, and stratified sampling design of the NHANES database, this study conducted analyses using sampling weights according to the database’s recommendations. All analyses were weighted, with weighting factors including the primary sampling units (SDMVPSU) and strata (SDMVSTRA). The weighting of variables was conducted based on the data year. For data years 1999–2002, the weight was 1/3 * WTSPH4YR; for 2003–2008, the weight was 1/6 * WTSB2YR; and for 2009–2010, the weight was 1/6 * WTSA2YR. Additionally, due to the presence of missing data values in the original database, multiple imputations using a chained equation approach based on random forests were conducted for the missing variables (<20%). Missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) with random forests. We generated m = 20 imputed datasets, a number considered sufficient to achieve stable estimates and valid inference. The imputation model included all variables used in the primary analysis, as well as auxiliary variables potentially related to the missingness mechanism. Convergence of the chained equations was assessed by visual inspection of trace plots and the Gelman-Rubin diagnostic (R-hat <1.1), which indicated satisfactory convergence after 10 iterations. To account for the complex survey design of NHANES, the imputation procedure incorporated the primary sampling units (clusters), strata, and the full sample 2-year examination weights. The subsequent analyses were performed on each imputed dataset separately, and the results were pooled using Rubin’s rules, which appropriately combine the estimates and SE while accounting for the between-imputation variability. Sensitivity analysis was performed on the data before and after processing to observe any differences to ensure data reliability. The analysis results are shown in Supplemental Table 2*, where we found no statistically significant differences between the imputed and original data.

The description of the continuous data was carried out employing the mean (SE), while categorical data were presented as the number of cases (composition ratio,%). Comparisons for continuous data were made using independent samples t tests, categorical data comparisons were made using chi-square tests, and comparisons for ordinal data were made using rank sum tests. The determination of covariates was based on the adjustment of three common demographic variables: age, gender, and race. Initially, positive variables were selected through a weighted logistic regression model, followed by a stepwise regression to derive the covariates included in subsequent analyses: age, gender, race, PIR, diabetes, hypertension, heart disease, cardiovascular agents, and antiplatelet agents. Prior to formal analyses, mean centering was performed for continuous variables (enterolactone, enterodiol, age, and BMI) to minimize multicollinearity. Collinearity assessment via variance inflation factor (VIF) showed all values ranged from 1.03 to 2.78 (all <3), confirming no significant multicollinearity. Model fit evaluation using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) indicated that Model 2 (adjusted for age, gender, race, PIR, diabetes, hypertension, heart disease, cardiovascular agents, and antiplatelet agents) had better fit than Model 1 (AIC: 1,246.3 vs 1,289.7; BIC: 1,298.5 vs 1,332.1). Subsequently, weighted logistic regression was applied to assess the association between enterolactone and enterodiol, as both continuous variables and categorical variables (quartiles), with the likelihood of stroke. The model of weighted Cox regression model was employed for assessing the association of the aforementioned independent variables with the likelihood of ACM in the stroke population. Based on established literature on stroke influencing factors,(5,6) we additionally considered and adjusted for the following potential confounders: smoking status (current, former, never), alcohol consumption (grams per day), body mass index (kg/m2), renal function (estimated glomerular filtration rate), and C-reactive protein levels as a marker of systemic inflammation. These variables were included in the fully adjusted models where data availability permitted. Model 1 was adjusted for age, gender, race, and PIR (demographic factors identified as positive variables in weighted univariate logistic regression). Model 2 was adjusted for age, gender, race, PIR, diabetes, hypertension, heart disease, cardiovascular agents, and antiplatelet agents. Subgroup analyses were stratified by age and sex. Statistical analyses were two-sided, setting the significance level at α = 0.05. Python 3.9 (https://www.python.org/) was utilized for data preprocessing and imputation of missing data, whereas SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) was employed for the statistical modeling of the analyses.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

The study included 9,752 participants with an average age of 46.32 years (SE = 0.27), of which 48.02% were male and 70.55% identified as non-Hispanic white race/ethnicity. The prevalence of self-reported stroke (prevalent stroke) was 3.17% (309/9,752) (Table 1). Among these 309 patients with a history of stroke, 194 (62.78%) experienced ACM during follow-up. Compared to participants without stroke, stroke patients had a higher average age (45.85 years vs 64.74 years, p<0.05) and were more likely to be non-Hispanic white (70.43% vs 75.41%, p<0.05). Stroke patients were less likely to have received a university education (42.39% vs 55.87%, p<0.05), to be married (51.41% vs 58.38%, p<0.05), and to have a PIR greater than 1.3 (69.38% vs 79.41%, p<0.05), and they had a higher average BMI (29.82 ‍kg/m2 vs 28.39 ‍kg/m2, p<0.05). Stroke patients were also more likely to suffer from hypertension (89.75% vs 49.10%, p<0.05), dyslipidemia (87.45% vs 69.17%, p<0.05), heart disease (40.92% vs 5.94%, p<0.05), wheezing that disturbs sleep (5.27% vs 3.24%, p<0.05), and to be on cardiovascular medications (24.78% vs 4.50%, p<0.05). The differences in the average levels of enterolactone and enterodiol between stroke patients and those without stroke were found not to be statistically significant. Compared to stroke survivors, deceased stroke patients had a significantly higher average age (71.30 years vs 56.21 years, p<0.05), were less likely to have received a university education (34.63% vs 52.41%, p<0.05), less likely to be married (45.73% vs 58.80%, p<0.05), and less likely to engage in physical exercise of ≥750 met*min/week (14.48% vs 33.60%, p<0.05). Patients who died from stroke were also more likely to be on cardiovascular medications (30.93% vs 16.78%, p<0.05). Moreover, no significant differences in the average levels of enterolactone and enterodiol were observed between stroke survivors and those who died from stroke.

Association between urinary enterolactone and enterodiol with prevalent stroke

As shown in Table 2, when enterolactone and enterodiol were analyzed as continuous variables, no significant association with prevalent stroke was observed (all p>0.05). When enterodiol was analyzed as a categorical variable, no statistical association with prevalent stroke was found (all p>0.05). When enterolactone was analyzed as a categorical variable, using the lowest quartile as the reference, the highest quartile group was significantly associated with a lower likelihood of prevalent stroke [OR: 0.64 (0.42–0.98), p<0.05].

Association between urinary enterolactone and enterodiol with ACM in stroke patients

When enterolactone was treated as a continuous variable, each unit increase in enterolactone concentration was associated with a 8% lower likelihood of ACM among patients with a history of stroke [HR: 0.92 (0.86–0.98)] (Table 3). However, no statistically significant association was observed when enterolactone was treated as a quartile variable. No statistically significant associations were observed for enterodiol, regardless of whether it was treated as a categorical or continuous variable (all p>0.05).

Discussion

Stroke ranks among the top three causes of disability and death worldwide, imposing a staggering burden at both the individual and societal levels.(26) Recent research has suggested that lignan metabolites, particularly enterolactone, may have potential beneficial effects on human health.(12,27) Consequently, our study investigated the association between urinary enterolactone and enterodiol and prevalent stroke (self-reported history of stroke) as well as mortality likelihood in patients with a history of stroke. The results indicated an inverse association between higher urinary enterolactone levels and prevalent stroke as well as ACM in this population. It is important to note that this observed association does not necessarily imply causality, as urinary enterolactone concentration is influenced by multiple factors including dietary lignan intake, individual variations in gut microbiota composition and metabolic capacity, and other unmeasured confounders. Therefore, urinary enterolactone may serve as a biomarker reflecting a favorable metabolic and gut microbial profile rather than directly conferring protection against stroke.

Upon dietary intake, lignans undergo microbial metabolism within the gastrointestinal tract, with enterolactone being a principal bioactive metabolite. Elevated urinary enterolactone concentrations serve as a potential surrogate marker for a functionally favorable gut microbial ecosystem.(16) Accumulating evidence indicates that increased enterolactone levels are inversely associated with the prevalence of cardiometabolic disorders and cardiovascular diseases.(28,29) Furthermore, enterolactone has been shown to modulate the overall relationship between dietary quality and cardiometabolic health, underscoring its role as a key mediator in nutritional-metabolic crosstalk.(16) Our study also found an inverse association between higher concentrations of urinary enterolactone and prevalent stroke. The aforementioned association may be underpinned by multiple interconnected biological mechanisms. Accumulating experimental evidence has corroborated that enterolactone exhibits potent antioxidant activity, characterized by its capacity to directly scavenge hydroxyl radicals and attenuate oxidative stress cascades.(30,31) Beyond its direct antioxidant effects, findings from an animal toxicological study have revealed that enterolactone can activate nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) signaling in endothelial cells, thereby inducing the transcription and expression of heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) and exerting an indirect antioxidant effect.(32,33) Furthermore, the antioxidant properties of enterolactone contribute to the neutralization of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which in turn preserves endothelial integrity and function, ultimately reducing the susceptibility to atherosclerosis—a well-recognized key precursor of stroke.(34–36) In addition to oxidative stress modulation, inflammation plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of incident stroke and recurrence.(37) Mendelian randomization studies have demonstrated that downregulation of interleukin-6 (IL-6) signaling is associated with an 11% reduction in the likelihood of ischemic stroke,(38) while randomized controlled trials have confirmed that anti-inflammatory therapeutic strategies can mitigate stroke likelihood in patients with coronary artery disease.(39) Given enterolactone’s established potent anti-inflammatory properties, it is plausible that it may also alleviate stroke susceptibility through the modulation of pro-inflammatory pathways.(13) Moreover, epidemiological investigations by Struja and Weiner have substantiated that enterolactone exerts regulatory effects on lipid metabolism and blood pressure homeostasis—two critical modifiable factors that are central to the management of stroke likelihood.(18,23) The association between enterolactone and the above cardiometabolic factors provides a potential biological basis for the observed inverse relationship with prevalent stroke. While further research is warranted to clarify the underlying mechanisms, current evidence supports the need for future studies to explore the link between enterolactone and stroke-related outcomes, particularly in prospective designs.

In the subgroup analysis of this study, the inverse association between enterolactone and prevalent stroke was more pronounced in males, suggesting that the relationship may vary by gender. Emerging evidence suggests that biological and sociocultural gender-related disparities exert a multifaceted influence on the influencing factors, clinical assessment, therapeutic strategies, and prognostic outcomes of stroke.(40) This divergence is plausibly rooted in inherent physiological and metabolic differences between males and females. Epidemiological investigations have consistently documented a higher incidence of ischemic stroke in males relative to females,(41) implying that the association between enterolactone and stroke susceptibility may be more pronounced in the male population. Sex hormones are hypothesized to play a pivotal mediating role in this gender-specific effect. Testosterone, the primary androgen, has been ascribed salutary physiological functions.(42) Specifically, a murine model-based toxicological study demonstrated that testosterone administration significantly downregulated the expression of aortic F4/80 antigen, IL-6, and interleukin-1β (IL-1β)—key markers of vascular inflammation—thereby conferring vascular effects via anti-inflammatory mechanisms.(43) Furthermore, the present study identified a more robust association between enterolactone levels and stroke susceptibility in individuals aged <65 years. This age-related discrepancy may be attributed to the age-associated decline in metabolic capacity to biotransform dietary lignans into enterolactone, resulting in reduced circulating and excretory enterolactone concentrations and a consequent attenuation of its biological effects.(44,45) This conjecture is supported by converging evidence from population-based epidemiological studies and preclinical animal models, which collectively demonstrate that aging is accompanied by a significant reduction in the diversity and stability of the gut microbiota(46–48)—a critical determinant of lignan metabolism. These observations underscore the necessity of heightened attention to age-related alterations in the gut microbiome as a potential target for mitigating stroke likelihood in middle-aged and elderly populations.

A Danish case-cohort study revealed an inverse association between plasma enterolactone concentrations and mortality among individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus.(21) Concordantly, Liu et al.(49) reported that elevated serum enterolactone levels were correlated with a reduced likelihood of ACM in patients with breast cancer. Furthermore, a population-based epidemiological investigation documented a negative correlation between urinary enterolactone concentrations and ACM.(20) Consistent with these prior findings, the present study—focusing on the high-risk cohort of patients with a history of stroke—also identified that higher urinary enterolactone levels were associated with a decreased likelihood of ACM. As a bioactive phenolic metabolite, enterolactone exhibits potent antioxidant properties: it enhances the expression of superoxide dismutase and catalase, attenuates oxidative stress in macrophages, and scavenges free radicals.(13,44,50) These antioxidant effects may mitigate the oxidative stress-induced brain damage subsequent to stroke, thereby reducing the likelihood of post-stroke mortality. Additionally, experimental evidence has demonstrated that enterolactone can cross the blood-brain barrier, exerting a effect by shielding microglia from lipopolysaccharide-induced inflammatory injury.(51) Moreover, post-stroke recurrence is a major contributor to increased mortality likelihood, and inflammation is a key pathogenic driver of recurrent stroke;(52) enterolactone may alleviate this likelihood via its anti-inflammatory activity, characterized by the downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α).(51,52) Alternatively, elevated urinary enterolactone concentrations indirectly reflect enhanced gut microbiota activity and metabolic capacity in patients.(12) Accumulating research has established that perturbations in the gut microbiota composition can modulate post-stroke brain repair processes and exert a significant impact on stroke prognosis,(53) providing another plausible mechanism underlying the observed association between enterolactone and reduced post-stroke mortality. Therefore, the mechanisms underlying the observed association between enterolactone and reduced mortality likelihood in stroke patients are likely diverse and multifactorial. However, the specific mechanisms or targets through which enterolactone exerts its effects post-stroke are not yet clear and require further definitive research to explore, providing more scientific evidence for the clinical application of enterolactone to improve the prognosis of stroke patients in the future.

This survey, based on a large sample population from NHANES, observed that higher urinary enterolactone levels were associated with a lower likelihood of stroke and ACM in stroke patients. However, given the observational nature of this study and the complex determinants of urinary enterolactone levels, this association should be interpreted as reflecting a correlation rather than establishing causality. Urinary enterolactone may represent an integrated biomarker of favorable gut microbial metabolism and dietary patterns that collectively contribute to better cardiovascular health outcomes. Several important considerations should be noted when interpreting the association between urinary enterolactone and stroke outcomes. First, urinary enterolactone concentration is not solely determined by dietary lignan intake but is substantially influenced by the composition and metabolic activity of the gut microbiota. Interindividual variations in gut bacterial communities that convert plant lignans to enterolactone could therefore affect the measured levels independent of dietary intake. Second, enterolactone production may be influenced by factors such as antibiotic use, gastrointestinal health, and genetic polymorphisms affecting metabolism, which were not accounted for in our analysis. Third, urinary enterolactone may serve as a marker of overall healthy dietary patterns and lifestyle factors that themselves reduce stroke likelihood. Thus, the observed associations could partially reflect residual confounding by unmeasured lifestyle and socioeconomic factors.

The study possessed certain limitations. Firstly, our study was an observational design, so the study could not infer causality from the conclusions. Future prospective studies, such as cohort studies or animal toxicology studies, were needed to further explore and validate the aforementioned associations. Secondly, limited by the database, the sample size of stroke patients was restricted, and the relation between enterolactone and the likelihood of ACM in stroke patients still requires further exploration through large-scale prospective cohort studies. Third, the definition of stroke history relied solely on self-reported data from the MCQ160f questionnaire without validation by medical records, neuroimaging findings, or clinical stroke severity scales. This approach may introduce misclassification bias, as participants might inaccurately recall or report their stroke history. Additionally, the lack of information on stroke subtypes (ischemic vs hemorrhagic) and severity precluded stratified analyses based on these clinically important characteristics. Fourth, the assessment of lignan metabolites relied on single spot urine samples, which may not fully capture long-term habitual exposure due to intra-individual variability. While the use of creatinine-adjusted values helps account for urinary dilution, day-to-day variations in dietary intake and metabolic factors could introduce measurement error. Fifth, and importantly, the absence of data on ‘time since stroke’ precluded adjustment for disease chronicity and baseline severity. As stroke severity is a major determinant of both short-term and long-term mortality, the inability to account for this potential confounder limits the ability to infer a direct predictive value of enterolactone, independent of the initial stroke insult.

Conclusion

This study found that higher urinary enterolactone levels were associated with a lower likelihood of prevalent stroke (self-reported history of stroke) and reduced ACM likelihood in patients with a history of stroke. However, given the cross-sectional nature of the prevalent stroke analysis and the observational design of the mortality analysis, these associations cannot be interpreted as causal. The findings reflect potential relationships between enterolactone levels, metabolic health, and stroke-related outcomes, providing new insights for understanding the role of lignan metabolites in stroke epidemiology and supporting their potential as health biomarkers.
