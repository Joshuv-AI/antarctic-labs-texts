Use of parallel fluorescent confocal microscopy for detection of prostatic adenocarcinoma
Authors: Kevin Clare, Kenneth Haines, Jeremy Littlefield, Asher Mandel, Hannah Sur, Neeraja Tillu, Henry Jodka, Daniel Ajabshir, Fatima Ahmad, Chahat Arora, Cristina Pasat‐Karasik, Monali Fatterpekar, Ash Tewari, Ming Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo provide a histological catalogue and defined criteria for the diagnosis of prostatic adenocarcinoma and its variants using the fluorescent confocal microscope (FCM) platform.MethodsA total of 22 radical prostatectomy specimens were serially sectioned and scanned with the FCM. The prostatic tissue then underwent routine histological preparation. The FCM images were assessed against traditional haematoxylin and eosin images to document FCM prostatic histology.ResultsWe first comprehensively catalogued FCM images of a wide range of benign and malignant prostate tissue. We then analysed and identified FCM features characteristic of cancer glands. They include fused or connected solid glands, lack of lumens, heterogeneous nuclear signals and lack of three‐dimensional depth on greyscale imaging. Gleason patterns 3 and 4 cancer glands were morphologically different on FCM. The smallest focus of cancer detected on FCM was 0.7 × 1.2 mm Gleason score 3 + 3 = 6 cancer.ConclusionThe image atlas and the diagnostic criteria presented in this study will help train pathologists to proficiently use FCM for rapid intraoperative evaluation of surgical margins during radical prostatectomy.

Introduction

The incidence of prostate cancer has risen by as much as 4.8% from 2017 to 2021 [1]. The most common non‐cutaneous cancer in men, prostatic adenocarcinoma is managed by several modalities, including radical prostatectomy (RP). During RP, surgeons may elect to conduct intraoperative margin assessment with positive margins being associated with poor oncological and functional outcomes [2, 3, 4, 5]. To preserve critical neurovascular structures essential to sexual function, urological surgeons utilise the neurovascular structure‐adjacent frozen‐section examination (NeuroSAFE) protocol, which includes posterolateral intraoperative margin analysis.

Recently, surgeons and pathologists have explored alternative modalities for intraoperative consultation and margin assessment, as NeuroSAFE assessment can prolong operative time and requires an in‐house pathology team. The fluorescent confocal microscope (FCM) has emerged as a promising option as it provides high resolution quasi‐histological images up to 250 μm in depth while preserving tissue integrity for histological evaluation [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. The Histolog® scanner (SamanTree Medical, Awans, Belgium) is such a device. It excites tissue fluorescence with a laser at 488 nm and fluorescence emission with a wavelength of >500 nm is collected, producing a toluidine‐blue mono stain‐like image. The penetration depth is fixed at 30 μm.

Recognition of cancer and benign cancer mimics captured by this new modality by pathologists is critical to successful deployment of this technology. Prior studies assessing the use of FCM for prostatic tissue have attempted to describe FCM morphology of the prostate tissue by using prostate biopsy specimens [6], different device with two laser sources (VivaScope) [7, 10], or laser endomicrosope [8]. No comprehensive image atlas of the FCM prostate tissue generated on Histolog exists in the literature.

In this study, we have comprehensive analysed the FCM images of benign and malignant prostate tissue generated on Histolog. We focused on benign prostate structures and lesions that are often mistaken for cancer. We have identified FCM image characteristics that can be used to distinguish between benign and cancer glands. These features and the image atlas can be used to train pathologists, standardise interpretation, and improve their diagnostic accuracy when evaluating surgical margins using FCM.

Methods

Material Acquisition

All research was reviewed and approved by Mount Sinai Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai Institution Review Board (IRB number – STUDY‐24‐01611). At time of receipt of the RP specimen from the operating room, surgical margins (apical, left and right posterolateral) are assessed on frozen section as part of the NeuroSAFE protocol. The remaining prostate was sectioned by serially slicing in 6 mm thickness along the axis of the urethra per standard grossing protocol.

The FCM Acquisition

The FCM images were acquired using the Histolog platform. Prostate slices were first stained with acridine orange as described in the manufacturer's protocol. Cross‐sections were imaged en face to visualise the prostatic parenchyma as well as the circumferential margin. If too big to fit on the scanner, the slice was bisected or quadrisected. In total, 118 scans were performed with a mean (range) of 5.4 (3–12) scans/prostate.

Permanent Section Correlation and Image Analysis

Once the FCM images were collected, the tissue was then washed with saline and submitted for routine formalin fixation, paraffin embedding, and haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Images from the same prostatic cross section were reviewed and compared by pathologists with experience in prostate pathology (K.C. and M.Z.) between the Histolog and H&E sections.

Results

Patient and Disease Characteristics

The RP specimens from 22 patients were included in this study. The patients’ mean (range) age was 61.8 (48–74) years. The mean (range) weight of the prostate was 49.1 (24–81.4) g.

In RP specimens, the Gleason score was 3 + 4 = 7 (Grade Group 2), 4 + 3 = 7 (Grade Group 3) and 4 + 5 = 9 (Grade Group 5) in 17, four and one, respectively. The tumour stage was pathological T stage (pT)2, pT3a and pT3b in 14, seven and one, respectively. The N stage was N0, N1 and Nx in 17, two and three, respectively.

Normal Histology

Normal prostatic architecture was readily appreciable on the FCM and closely paralleled the appearance observed on routine H&E sections.

Benign prostate glands usually had a lobular arrangement. The glands were separate from each other and were only occasionally connected or fused together. Individual glands had simple architecture with smooth or slightly undulating luminal contour. The glandular wall appeared uniform in thickness. The nuclei within the glandular wall showed uniform, but ‘tapering’, distribution with strong signal at the periphery and dimmer signal towards the lumen. The lumen was visualised as an optically empty space—white on pseudo‐colour or a black void on greyscale imaging (Fig. 1a–f). This difference in signal intensity represents the cells being on a different optical plane. In larger glands, nuclei were occasionally present within the lumen, and these were best appreciated on greyscale imaging (Fig. 1d–f). On the greyscale image, one could visualise the three‐dimensional (3D) depth of the benign glands, which appeared to have flat or slightly undulated wall and empty lumens. There was no complex papillae or solid component within lumens. Crowded benign glands are still discrete and separate from each other (Fig. 1d–f).

Normal glandular histology: representative images of H&E, greyscale, and pseudo‐coloured for normal histology of the prostate including (a–c) benign, (d–f) crowded large benign glands, and (g–i) central zone, as well as (j–l) seminal vesicle.

Glands of the central zone maintained their expected lobulated and arborising architecture. The FCM imaging successfully captured the characteristic complex frond‐like projections extending into the glandular lumen. These intraluminal pillars exhibited sharply defined borders, and their constituent nuclei displayed uniform fluorescence intensity (Fig. 1g–i). Seminal vesicle epithelium was architecturally more complex than the central zone glands and had more exuberant epithelium, which showed slight variability in fluorescence intensity, producing heterogeneity within the epithelial layer. However, the signal gradient, i.e., stronger at the periphery and dimmer towards the centre, was preserved (Fig. 1j–l).

Recognition of periprostatic tissues, including stroma and neurovascular structures, was also feasible and is essential for evaluating extraprostatic extension and possible perineural or lymphovascular invasion. Nerves appeared as elongated, wavy structures analogous to their appearance on H&E. On pseudo‐colour images, nerves exhibited a darker, more intense signal relative to surrounding stroma, whereas on greyscale imaging they appeared brighter and more sharply outlined. Individual nuclei within nerve bundles were elongated and curved and aligned parallel to the nerve axis (Fig. 2d–f). Ganglion cells, typically encountered at the periphery of the gland, showed abundant cytoplasm that produced a distinct dark‐purple or light‐grey circular profile depending on the image reviewed. Within the cytoplasm, a single round nucleus was consistently visible, facilitating reliable identification of these structures (Fig. 2a–c).

Normal histology: traditional H&E with corresponding confocal images or (a–c) ganglion, (d–f) nerve, (g–i) smooth muscle, (j–l) periprostatic tissue, and (m–o) blood vessel.

Smooth muscle bands were evident within the prostatic parenchyma and appeared as fascicles of spindle‐shaped cells with nuclei aligned in a single direction, closely resembling their appearance on routine H&E sections (Fig. 2g–i). The transition to periprostatic tissue was marked by a noticeable decrease in cellularity and the absence of glandular structures. Periprostatic fat was readily identified, with mature adipocytes outlining the periphery of optically clear, well‐defined spaces (Fig. 2j–l). Small foci of inflammation were occasionally seen within the surrounding adipose tissue. Large luminal structures corresponding to periprostatic vasculature were recognised by their peripheral location and by the presence of multilayered, concentrically arranged nuclei that were approximately evenly spaced within the vascular adventitia (Fig. 2m–o).

Benign and Premalignant Prostatic Lesions

Beyond normal histology, FCM allowed reliable identification of several benign proliferative processes. Nodules of BPH were well circumscribed and demonstrated an increased density of glands within the nodule (Fig. 3a–c). The glandular morphology and cytological features within these nodules were similar to those described above for benign glands. In some BPH nodules, the stromal component was more prominent, with increased cellularity reflected by densely arranged nuclei within the intervening fibromuscular tissue (Fig. 3d–f). This produced a correspondingly more intense white or purple signal on the pseudo‐coloured images.

Representative images of non‐carcinoma prostatic pathologies including: (a–c) BPH, (d–f) BPH with stroma, (g–i) atrophic glands, (j–l) PAH, and (m–o) PIN.

Dilated atrophic glands showed enlarged, optically clear lumens, while the epithelial nuclei lining the lumen exhibited reduced fluorescence intensity and at times appeared to incompletely circumscribe the gland. Unlike the large luminal spaces of central zone glands or seminal vesicles, these atrophic lumens lacked intraluminal nuclei, aiding distinction (Fig. 3g–i).

Post‐atrophic hyperplasia (PAH), another benign mimicker of malignancy, consisted of small, crowded glands with lobular organisation. The glands are discrete and do not fuse together. They are small and comprise several layers, and sometimes solid nests, of nuclei, which show uniformly strong signal (Fig. 3j–l).

High‐grade prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PIN) demonstrated glandular architecture similar to benign glands, including the presence of relatively large luminal spaces. However, PIN was distinguished by characteristic epithelial expansion, with much thicker and multiple layers of cells forming tufted or micropapillary protrusions into the lumen. The nuclear signals are not uniformly distributed, with strong and dim signals admixed (Fig. 3m–o), similar to cancer (see below).

Malignant Pathologies

Prostatic adenocarcinoma consisted of crowded glands that were often fused together producing the impression of cross‐linked tubular structures, even though the cancer glands were well separated on H&E (Fig. 4a–c). All cancer glands were solid and lacked a well‐defined lumen, even when they had well‐formed lumens on H&E (Fig. 4a–c,g–i). The nuclear signals were heterogeneous with admixed strong and dim signals. On greyscale images, cancer glands appeared solid without appreciable 3D depth (Fig. 4).

Prostatic adenocarcinoma including: (a–c) Gleason Grade 3, and variants including (d–f) atrophic and (g–i) pseudohyperplastic adenocarcinoma. (j–l) Gleason Grade 4 with (m–o) cribriform cancer glands, and (p–r) IDC.

In addition to conventional patterns, atrophic and pseudohyperplastic variants of prostatic adenocarcinoma were also identifiable. Atrophic carcinoma, at low magnification, lacked a lobular arrangement and comprised densely packed, small glands with fewer epithelial layers. The glands vary significantly in sizes. Although a lumen was sometimes retained, the luminal cells are almost always solid and exhibited heterogeneous nuclear fluorescence (Fig. 4d–f). Pseudohyperplastic adenocarcinoma presented a particular diagnostic challenge due to its lobular glandular arrangement, which mimicked benign hyperplastic processes. However, glands were solid and lacked a clearly defined lumen and exhibited heterogeneous nuclear fluorescence (Fig. 4g–i).

In Gleason pattern 3 cancer (Fig. 4a–i), individual glandular contour was evident although many of them were fused or linked together. In Gleason pattern 4 cancer, glandular structures became less distinct (Fig. 4j–l). They formed large nests and sheets with irregular border with holes. The nuclear signals were heterogeneous. On pseudo‐colour images, nuclei varied significantly in sizes (Fig. 4l). Cribriform cancer had similar images as the Gleason pattern 4 cancer (Fig. 4m–o). On greyscale images, both did not show 3D glandular architecture. Intraductal carcinoma (IDC) (Fig. 4p–r) also demonstrated large irregular nests and sheets punctuated with holes. Different from Gleason pattern 4 and cribriform cancer, IDC still preserved peripheral strong nuclear signals and tapering towards the centre, at least focally, reminiscent of benign glands.

Detection Limit of Cancer on FCM

The smallest focus of cancer detected by FCM was 0.7 × 1.2 mm, Gleason score 3 + 3 = 6 (Grade Group 1) cancer (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Fluorescent microscopy offers a unique opportunity to visualise the surface architecture of fresh prostate tissue without disrupting specimen integrity. Images are first acquired in monochromatic greyscale and may be pseudo‐coloured to a purple hue that mimics H&E staining, a familiar visual paradigm for pathologists. Unlike traditional H&E histology, FCM images represent a tissue volume up to 30 μm in depth, projecting a 3D structure onto a 2D plane. This increased thickness—approximately six times that of a standard 5‐μm H&E section, potentially creating a 3D image of the tissue under examination, is best visualised on greyscale imaging. Several studies have utilised advanced imaging and optical clearing techniques to compare the 3D structure of benign prostate tissue and cancerous glands, revealing distinct architectural differences. Verhoef et al. [11] found benign prostate glands had organised acinar structure, while cancer glands showed architecturally disorganised structures. The study identified two major architectural subgroups in cancer: a tubular interconnecting network (Gleason pattern 3, poorly formed/fused pattern 4) and serpentine contiguous epithelial proliferations (cribriform pattern 4, solid pattern 5). When this 3D complexity of cancer glands is displayed as a 2D image, as is the case in Histolog imaging, it loses the 3D depth, which is characteristically seen in benign glands. The cancer glands are instead visualised as solid nests or sheets of nuclei. This provides pathologists with an opportunity to confirm or compliment their diagnosis with a different approach.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to document prostatic morphology on FCM using the entire cross‐section of the gland to capture the wide array of normal, benign, and malignant prostate lesions. Prior atlases have relied on small prostate tissue samples [6] or needle biopsy cores [7], both of which inherently limit the diversity and continuity of morphological patterns available for evaluation. Understanding prostatic architecture across broad tissue types at both low and high magnification is essential, as different scales provide distinct diagnostic information. Atlases restricted to small areas may fail to fully capture architectural relationships or accurately represent variants of benign and malignant pathology. This limitation mirrors challenges observed in clinical practice, where under sampling from short biopsy cores can contribute to discrepancies between biopsy and RP Gleason grading [12]. The only other detailed FCM prostate image atlas was reported by Bertoni et al. [10] using a different device examining ex vivo needle biopsy of RP specimens. The acquired images are similar to, but had better quality and higher resolution than, our Histolog images due to technical specifications of the device (two laser beams) and tissue source (fresh needle biopsy tissue). Therefore, the image atlas created by Bertoni et al. [10] is not suitable for training Histolog users to learn prostate images.

The objective of intraoperative evaluation of the prostate tissue for margin assessment is to identify cancer, including those that may be mistaken for benign such as atrophic and pseudohyperplastic cancer [13, 14, 15, 16], and to identify benign lesions that may be mistaken for cancer, such as PAH and BPH [17, 18, 19]. For prostate cancer, Gleason grading is one of the most important prognostic factors and distinction between a low‐grade cancer (Gleason pattern 3) and high‐grade one (Gleason pattern 4 and 5) is critical [20, 21].

In this study, we demonstrate that FCM reliably captures the spectrum of normal prostatic anatomy, benign proliferative changes, and malignant patterns. Comparing a wide array of normal, benign and cancer glands, we identified the FCM imaging features that can distinguish cancer from benign. These features include:

In our experience, benign and cancer glands can be distinguished based on these features.

Gleason grade is one of the most important pathological factors for prostate cancer. In this study, we found Gleason pattern 3 cancer forms well‐defined but often linked or connected tubules and nests, while Gleason pattern 4 cancer glands are ill‐defined large nests and sheets. However, we did not examine other Gleason pattern 4 morphologies, such as poorly formed, fused and glomeruloid cancer glands. Future studies will need to delineate the FCM features of these Gleason pattern 4 morphologies.

The FCM also represents a promising opportunity to shorten operative times and improve patient outcomes through rapid intraoperative margin assessment [13]. However, sensitivity and specificity for positive margin detection depend on margin size. In the IP8‐FLUORESCE multicentre study (International Standard Randomised Controlled Trial Number [ISRCTN] 21536411), margins ≥3 mm were detected with high sensitivity (0.79) and specificity (0.94), whereas margins <3 mm showed reduced sensitivity (0.48) with preserved specificity. These findings suggest that while FCM expedites margin evaluation, small foci of tumour at the margin may escape detection [11]. In this study, we identified foci of cancer as small as 0.7 × 1.2 mm, although our study was not designed to investigate the detection limit of FCM, as the pairing H&E and FCM images are not precisely matching each other after the tissue went through processing and sectioning.

While interpretation of FCM images involves a learning curve, Bertoni et al. [10] demonstrated strong intraobserver improvement, with kappa values increasing from 0.68 to 0.87 after repeated exposure. It is of note the diagnosis of small focus of prostate cancer in prostate biopsy went through the similar process. Refinement of the diagnostic criteria and education have significantly improved the diagnostic accuracy [22].

One caveat is that the cross‐sectional prostate slices used in this study contain many benign prostate glands, non‐cancer lesions, and cancer. Their identification could be challenging using FCM when cancer glands, especially of small focus, are inundated with benign glands. In contrast, en face analysis of the prostate surface during the intraoperative margin evaluation may potentially be straightforward on FCM, as the surface of the RP specimens is largely fibromuscular tissue devoid of prostate glands except in uncommon cases of so‐called capsular incision where the prostate tissue is incised and exposed [23].

In summary, by assessing morphology across the entirety of the prostatic gland, our study provides the most comprehensive atlas to date of FCM imaging and its correlation with histological entities. We also identified diagnostic features for cancer based on the FCM imaging characteristics. Continued work is warranted to further characterise FCM imaging features, refine interpretive criteria, and evaluate its performance in routine and intraoperative settings. As both the technology and user expertise evolve, FCM has the potential to serve as a valuable adjunct to traditional histopathology in prostate cancer diagnosis and surgical management.
