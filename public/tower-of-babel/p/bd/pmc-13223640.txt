Complete response to immune checkpoint blockade in sarcomatoid renal cell carcinoma with bone marrow metastasis: case report with tumor immune microenvironment profiling
Authors: Yuji Miura, Ryosuke Jikuya, Asaki Saijo, Hiromu Yano, Keiichi Kinowaki, Kohji Takemura, Keita Uchino, Hisashi Hasumi, Yoshihiro Komohara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBone marrow represents a unique microenvironment enriched with both immunosuppressive cells and immune-activating functions. However, its role in metastasis and response to immunotherapy remains poorly understood. We report a case of sarcomatoid clear cell renal cell carcinoma with systemic bone marrow metastasis that achieved a complete response to ipilimumab plus nivolumab. Using matched pretreatment and post-treatment bone marrow samples, we performed integrative immune profiling. Pretreatment analyses showed abundant CD14+ myeloid cells, phenotypically consistent with myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC)-like populations, together with CD8+ T cells. RNA-seq-based deconvolution revealed a predominance of monocytic-MDSC-like cells, which were selectively reduced after treatment with a shift toward monocyte and neutrophil-associated cells. Paired single-sample gene set enrichment analysis demonstrated that immunosuppressive (MDSC-like populations and TGF-β), and immune-activating (CD8+ T cell, interferon-gamma) pathways coexisted at baseline and were broadly attenuated following treatment. These findings suggest that the bone marrow microenvironment harbored a hybrid immune state characterized by concurrent inflammatory and immunosuppressive programs. The subsequent global contraction of both programs suggests that immune remodeling primarily reflects the resolution of tumor-driven immune activation following tumor eradication, rather than the effect from immune checkpoint inhibitors. This case highlights that even in an immune-restrictive niche, latent but functional antitumor immunity can be unleashed by immune checkpoint blockade.

Introduction

Bone marrow represents a unique hematopoietic niche with specialized microenvironmental features that regulate hematopoiesis and immune cell development and maturation.1 It is a recognized, although infrequent, site of metastasis in solid cancer.2 In renal cell carcinoma, bone marrow involvement is rare and remains poorly characterized, particularly regarding its tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) and response to immunotherapy.

Immunologically, the bone marrow is a complex and dual-function site. It is enriched with immunosuppressive cell populations such as myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), regulatory T cells, and M2 macrophages, which contribute to an immunosuppressive environment.3 At the same time, the bone marrow plays a central role in antigen presentation and immune cell priming, suggesting its potential as a site of immune activation under certain conditions. Despite such a unique immunological property of bone marrow, research on TIME in bone marrow metastasis in patients with solid tumors, particularly in response to immunotherapy, remains poorly characterized.

ICIs have revolutionized cancer treatment by enhancing antitumor immune responses.4 5 However, their impact on the bone marrow microenvironment is not well characterized.

Here, we report a rare case of sarcomatoid clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) with the recurrence as systemic bone marrow metastasis that achieved a complete response following combination therapy with ipilimumab and nivolumab. Using matched pretreatment and post-treatment bone marrow samples, we comprehensively analyzed the immunological landscape of the bone marrow to understand the mechanistic basis of treatment response in this highly immunologically complex and understudied metastatic niche.

Methods

Immunohistochemical analysis

The bone marrow biopsy specimens were cut into 3 µm sections, and stained with H&E and immunostained for cytokeratin, CD3, CD8, CD14, CD68 and HLA-DR. The primary antibodies used for immunohistochemistry (IHC) were listed in online supplemental table 1. Samples were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-labeled goat anti-mouse or rabbit secondary antibodies. Immunoreactions were visualized using a diaminobenzidine substrate kit (#425011, Nichirei Biosciences). Multiplex imaging was executed using a microfluidics instrument (Akoya Biosciences, Menlo Park, California, USA), a fluorescence microscope (BZ-X700; Keyence, Osaka, Japan), and CODEX Instrument Manager software (Akoya Biosciences). An automated microfluidics system performed iterative annealing and stripping of fluorophore-labeled oligonucleotide barcodes complementary to the barcodes attached to antibodies. The raw image files were processed using CODEX processor V.1.8 (Akoya Biosciences).

Flow cytometry analysis

Briefly, flow cytometry was performed on freshly collected bone marrow aspirates before and after treatment, using NAVIOS cytometer (Beckman Coulter). The antibodies used for flow cytometry were listed in online supplemental table 2. MDSC-like myeloid cells were operationally defined based on CD33+CD14+HLA-DR−/low phenotypes, as previously described,6 recognizing that this definition represents a phenotypic proxy and may encompass heterogeneous myeloid populations with distinct functional status. All samples were processed within 4 hours of collection to preserve antigen integrity.

RNA-seq analysis

Total RNA was extracted from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples of both primary and metastatic lesions. Tumor regions were identified based on HE-stained slides, and the regions were microdissected from 10 µm FFPE sections. RNA extraction was performed using the RNeasy FFPE kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA quality was assessed with the Bioanalyzer (Agilent), and samples with a DV200 greater than 30% were selected for downstream applications. RNA libraries were prepared using the Twist RNA library prep kit (Twist Bioscience). Sequencing was conducted on the NovaSeq X Plus platform (Illumina) with 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw fastq files were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using STAR (V.2.7.9a), with gene annotation based on the GENCODE release 38 comprehensive gene set. Aligned SAM files were sorted, and duplicate reads were marked using Picard MarkDuplicates. Read counts were obtained using featureCounts, and the resulting count matrix was used for subsequent gene expression analyses.

Deconvolution analysis

Immune cell composition was estimated using CIBERSORTx (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/). A custom signature matrix was generated from publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing data from human bone marrow immune cells (GSE120221, Gene Expression Omnibus). Bulk RNA-seq data from pre- and post-treatment bone marrow samples were normalized to transcripts per million (TPM) and used as input for deconvolution to estimate relative immune cell fractions. Batch correction was applied using S-mode, and quantile normalization was disabled. Deconvolution was performed in relative mode with 1,000 permutations.

To mitigate potential variability due to difficulties in resolving closely related cell populations and mismatches between the reference signature matrix and the input bulk RNA-seq data, deconvolution was repeated 10 times to ensure robustness.

ssGSEA analysis

Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was performed using the GSVA R package with Hallmark gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database and curated immune-related gene signatures. ssGSEA scores were used to estimate the relative enrichment of each gene set. Detailed information on the curated immune-related gene signatures is provided in the online supplemental table 4.

Case presentation

A patient in their 50s was initially diagnosed with ccRCC approximately 5 years before the current presentation. At that time, the patient’s International Metastatic RCC Database Consortium risk category was favorable, with bilateral multiple metastases confined to the lungs and no evidence of bone or bone marrow involvement. The patient underwent left-sided nephrectomy. Histopathological examination of the primary tumor revealed typical ccRCC without sarcomatoid or rhabdoid features. The tumor was classified as Fuhrman grade 3 and was positive for AE1/3 and PAX8. The patient subsequently received pembrolizumab (200 mg every 3 weeks) plus axitinib (5 mg two times per day) for 2 years, then continued axitinib monotherapy for three additional years. The patient’s lung metastases achieved complete response following axitinib plus pembrolizumab treatment. The overall clinical course is summarized in online supplemental figure 1. Approximately 1 year later, a solitary brain metastasis subsequently emerged in the right parietal lobe and was successfully controlled with stereotactic radiosurgery. Five years after starting systemic therapy, the patient developed diffuse bone pain while continuing axitinib monotherapy at a reduced dose of 3 mg two times per day. 18-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography-CT (PET-CT) revealed widespread bone marrow involvement with focal bone metastases (figure 1A, left), and bone marrow biopsy confirmed sarcomatoid ccRCC recurrence. The patient was treated with ipilimumab (1 mg/kg) plus nivolumab (3 mg/kg) every 3 weeks. Following three cycles, FDG PET-CT (figure 1A, right) demonstrated complete resolution of FDG uptake, and repeat bone marrow biopsy (figure 1B) confirmed the absence of tumor cells, indicating complete response. Bone pain resolved rapidly after treatment initiation. At baseline, laboratory findings showed grade 2 of anemia (hemoglobin 9.8 g/dL) without other cytopenias, but with elevated inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein 33.4 mg/dL, white blood cells 10.4×109/L with 64% neutrophil) and presence of immature myeloid cells in peripheral blood, suggestive of bone marrow involvement. These abnormalities gradually improved and normalized over approximately 3 months following treatment. Treatment was modified due to grade 2 immune-related hepatitis, which developed after the third cycle (peak aspartate aminotransferase and alanine aminotransferase levels of 88 and 122 U/L, respectively without bilirubin elevation). Ipilimumab and nivolumab were discontinued, and the patient was managed with observation without systemic corticosteroid use. Liver enzyme levels gradually improved to grade 1 over approximately 5 months, after which nivolumab monotherapy (240 mg/body every 2 weeks) was resumed. The patient has maintained complete bone marrow response for approximately 3 years. During follow-up, brain metastases recurred approximately 1 year after initiation of ipilimumab plus nivolumab therapy, presenting as a solitary lesion in the right parietal lobe and multiple cerebellar metastases. These were treated with whole-brain radiotherapy and subsequently controlled. Paired pretreatment and post-treatment bone marrow samples were subsequently analyzed pathologically and genomically.

Immunohistochemical analysis

To further investigate the immune microenvironment of the bone marrow, we performed IHC and multiplex-IHC (figure 1B and C). Pretreatment biopsy showed dense sarcomatoid tumor infiltration, and normal hematopoiesis recovery was revealed in post-treatment biopsy. CD3+ and CD8+ T cells were abundant before treatment and remained after therapy, whereas CD14+ myeloid cells that were prominently present before treatment were markedly reduced after therapy. Multiplex-IHC analysis confirmed spatial proximity between CD14+ or CD68+ myeloid cells and cytokeratin+ tumor cells pretreatment, with disappearance of tumor cells and CD14+ myeloid cells after therapy; CD68+ macrophages showed less change.

Flow cytometry analysis

To further characterize the CD14+ myeloid population observed in the bone marrow, we performed flow cytometry analysis using aspirated bone marrow samples (figure 2A). MDSC-like myeloid cells were operationally defined based on a CD33+CD14+HLA-DR−/low phenotype, as previously defined.6 These MDSC-like populations comprised 15.8% of myeloid cells pre-treatment and decreased to 0.1% post-treatment. This finding suggests that a substantial proportion of CD14+ myeloid cells that were predominantly present before treatment exhibited an immunosuppressive myeloid phenotype, which was markedly reduced following treatment.

RNA-seq analysis

Deconvolution analysis

To further resolve the complexity of bone marrow immune microenvironment, particularly the heterogeneity of myeloid cell populations, we performed deconvolution analysis using bulk RNA-seq data, which was repeated (n=10) to assess robustness; the distribution of inferred cell population fractions is shown in figure 2B, with the corresponding numerical values provided in online supplemental table 3.

Focusing on the myeloid compartment, a substantial decrease in monocytic-MDSC-like cells was observed after treatment, accompanied by an increase in monocyte-related populations and a marked increase of neutrophil-associated cells. In contrast, polymorphonuclear-MDSC-like cells remained relatively low and showed modest changes. Among lymphoid populations, T cells increased, whereas B cells were markedly reduced, with natural killer cells showing a moderate increase.

The reference uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) (online supplemental figure 2) supported a continuous transition between monocytes and MDSC-like states.

ssGSEA analysis

To further investigate immune-related signaling pathways and functional programs, we performed ssGSEA on matched pretreatment and post-treatment samples.

As an initial unbiased approach, paired ssGSEA (post–pre) using Hallmark pathway demonstrated that multiple immune-related pathways, including IFN signaling and inflammatory pathways such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha/NF-κB and IL6-JAK-STAT3 signaling, were enriched in the pre-treatment sample compared with the post-treatment sample and were broadly attenuated following treatment (online supplemental figure 4). To complement this paired analysis, we further examined raw ssGSEA score to assess the relative distribution of pathway activity at each point (online supplemental figure 5).

We next focused on curated immune-related gene signatures to clarify these changes in detail. The pretreatment sample demonstrated concurrent enrichment of immunosuppressive signatures, including MDSC-related and TGF-β signaling programs, together with immune activation signatures such as IFN-γ response and CD8 T-cell-associated programs (figure 2C and online supplemental figure 3). Following treatment, these signatures were broadly attenuated, indicating a simultaneous reduction of both immunosuppressive and immune activation programs.

Discussion

We examined immunological changes within the bone marrow microenvironment of a patient with sarcomatoid ccRCC and widespread bone marrow metastasis who showed marked response to ipilimumab and nivolumab.

To our knowledge, data on the treatment of bone marrow metastasis from ccRCC, particularly with ICIs, remain limited, making this case clinically informative. The enhanced responsiveness of sarcomatoid RCC to ipilimumab plus nivolumab has been well established in previously untreated patients, with an objective response rate (ORR) of 60.8%.7 Thus, the response observed in our case is broadly consistent with this recognized sensitivity.

However, our case differs in several important aspects. First, most available data are derived from treatment-naïve populations, whereas the efficacy of ipilimumab plus nivolumab after prior programmed death-1 (PD-1) inhibitor exposure appears limited. In a pooled analysis of prospective phase II trials including the TITAN-RCC trial, the OMNIVORE trial, and the HCRN GU16-260 study, the ORR to salvage nivolumab plus ipilimumab after prior nivolumab was only 12.6%.8 Second, the recurrent tumor exhibited sarcomatoid differentiation distinct from the initial histology. Given the inflamed and immune-sensitive biology of sarcomatoid RCC,9 10 such histologic evolution may have resensitized the tumor to immunotherapy, with the addition of ipilimumab potentially overcoming resistance to prior PD-1 blockade. On the other hand, Hallmark raw ssGSEA score (online supplemental figure 5) showed that the pretreatment samples in our case were enriched for cell cycle-related pathways, whereas IFN-related pathways were not among the top-ranked signatures. This contrasts with a previous report describing MYC-driven and immune-inflamed sarcomatoid RCC.9

Taken together, this case suggests that the bone marrow niche may shape a distinct transcriptional state compared with conventional sarcomatoid RCC, while ipilimumab plus nivolumab may still retain clinical activity in selected post-PD-1 settings.

Immunosuppressive microenvironments dominated by MDSCs and M2 macrophages are generally associated with resistance to ICIs.51113 However, a complete response was achieved in this patient. This apparent paradox may be explained by the dual nature of bone marrow, which consists of both an immunoregulatory niche enriched with suppressive immune populations1 and immune-activating capacity with antigen presentation by resident macrophages and dendritic cells,14 together with the intrinsically high immunogenicity of sarcomatoid ccRCC.15

Multiomics analyses suggest that the pretreatment bone marrow microenvironment was characterized by a hybrid immune state, in which inflammatory and immunosuppressive programs coexisted. Such a configuration is increasingly recognized across TIME, where tumor antigenicity drives immune activation while simultaneously inducing compensatory suppressive mechanisms, reflecting a dynamic interplay between stimulatory and inhibitory signals.16

In contrast, the post-treatment state was characterized by a global contraction of the immune landscape, involving not only suppressive myeloid populations but also inflammatory signaling and T cell-associated activity. This coordinated reduction suggests that the observed changes reflect the resolution of tumor-driven immune activation rather than simple immune quiescence or selective depletion of suppressive cell populations. Importantly, such remodeling is unlikely to represent a direct pharmacodynamic effect of ICIs. Rather, it is more plausibly explained by tumor eradication leading to loss of antigenic stimulation, followed by contraction of both inflammatory and compensatory immunosuppressive programs within the bone marrow microenvironment. This pattern is analog to immune resolution following pathogen clearance, in which removal of antigenic stimuli leads to coordinated dampening of immune responses.17

In conclusion, even within an immune-restrictive bone marrow niche, ICIs can elicit effective antitumor immunity when tumor antigenicity and latent immune competence coexist. Assessing the presence of suppressed but functional immune components within the tumor microenvironment may help identify patients who benefit from ICIs, even in immunologically unfavorable sites such as bone marrow metastasis.
