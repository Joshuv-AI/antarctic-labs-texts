The Hidden Constriction: Middle Aortic Syndrome Presenting as Acute Systolic Heart Failure in a 7-Month-Old Infant
Authors: Shannon R Zhang, Ryan Greene, Clint D Cochran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Middle aortic syndrome, Infant heart failure, Malignant hypertension

Introduction

Middle aortic syndrome (MAS) is a vascular disorder characterized by segmental or diffuse narrowing of the abdominal aorta, which often involves renal and visceral arteries as well. Here we describe a case of a previously healthy 7-month-old female infant who presented with acute decompensated heart failure (HF) and malignant hypertension. Primary myocardial dysfunction was suspected after initial echocardiogram, but subsequent cross-sectional imaging revealed significant narrowing of the abdominal aorta diagnostic of MAS. Treatment of MAS in infants is a tiered process that starts with medical management and progresses to invasive endovascular or surgical repair. Acute decompensated HF as the initial clinical manifestation of MAS is uncommon in pediatric patients and exceedingly rare in infants. Our case underscores the importance of evaluating for MAS in new infantile HF, as timely recognition and intervention are critical to optimizing outcomes.

Case Presentation

A 7-month-old infant presented to a local emergency department with a 9-day history of decreased oral intake, increased fussiness, fatigue, and a 2-day history of increased work of breathing. The baby was born full term and previously healthy. Weight was in the third percentile for age with an approximate 600 g weight loss over the past month. Vitals were notable for a blood pressure of 159/128 mm Hg, heart rate up to 185 beats per minute, respiratory rate up to 69 breaths per minute, and normal oxygen saturation of 97%. Examination revealed a toxic appearing infant with increased work of breathing, delayed capillary refill, and palpable hepatomegaly. Laboratory evaluation showed an elevated brain natriuretic peptide >5,000 pg/mL (reference range, 0-100 pg/mL), high sensitivity troponin I 338 ng/L (reference range 0-18 ng/L), blood urea nitrogen 21 mg/dL (normal range, 5-15 mg/dL), and ammonia 51 μmol/L (normal range, <40 μmol/L). Notably, electrolytes, transaminases, complete blood count, and thyroid studies were within normal range. Infectious workup, including respiratory viral panel, blood cultures, and urine testing, was negative. Chest x-ray demonstrated cardiomegaly, bilateral hazy lung opacities, and bilateral pleural effusions. Electrocardiogram demonstrated sinus tachycardia with heart rate of 182 beats per minute and high QRS voltage in all leads.

Due to concern for an underlying cardiac etiology, an urgent transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) was performed. This demonstrated a severely dilated left ventricle (LV) with increased left ventricular mass of 49.5 g (Z score of +6.15) and severely reduced global left ventricular systolic function. The ejection fraction was calculated to be 10% to 15% using the 5/6 area length method (Figures 1 and 2, Videos 1 and 2). The right ventricle was normal in size with no dilation or hypertrophy but with qualitatively reduced global right ventricular systolic function. Additionally, there was moderate mitral regurgitation (Figure 3, Video 3). Importantly, the bilateral coronary arteries appeared to have normal origin and course. The ascending, transverse, and proximal descending thoracic aorta had normal flow at the level of the diaphragm (Figure 4, Video 4).

The differential diagnosis for acute decompensated HF in a 7-month-old with a structurally normal heart and hypertension included inherited or acquired cardiomyopathy, infective myocarditis, a high-output HF state from an arteriovenous malformation, anemia, inborn error of metabolism, and hypertensive emergency. Renal and endocrine etiologies for significant hypertension were also possible etiologies, including vascular abnormalities such as aortic coarctation, renal artery stenosis, intrinsic renal disease, monogenic hypertension, pheochromocytoma, and thyroid dysfunction.

Computed tomography angiogram (CTA) of the aorta revealed the diagnosis of MAS at the level of the renal arteries (Figure 5, Video 5). Specifically, the abdominal aorta appeared lobulated with areas of relative stenosis and dilation. There was diffuse narrowing in the distal 2.1 cm of the abdominal aorta. In the narrowest portion, the aorta measured 0.35 × 0.38 cm. The bilateral renal arteries appeared markedly stenotic or occluded with abnormal patchy perfusion. A renal ultrasound demonstrated normal appearance of the kidneys. However, Doppler vascular ultrasound of the renal vessels revealed high-velocity flow through the left renal artery, indicating a greater than 60% diameter stenosis. There was normal spectral Doppler profile through the right renal artery and bilateral renal veins.

Computed tomography angiogram of the aorta, coronal (A) and sagittal (B) displays, demonstrates a lobulated appearance of the abdominal aorta with areas of relative stenosis, and diffuse narrowing in the distal 2.1 cm (narrowest portion 0.35 × 0.38 cm).

Angiography of the abdominal aorta demonstrated smooth narrowing beginning proximal to the celiac axis and extending to the infrarenal abdominal aorta, with poststenotic dilation of the juxtarenal aorta (Figure 6). There was no discernable left renal artery and no appreciable left renal parenchymal enhancement. The 2 right renal arteries had proximal stenosis, and the right kidney had normal parenchymal enhancement. Sampling of the bilateral renal veins revealed significantly elevated left renal vein renin levels of 8,280 to 9,196 pg/mL and right renal vein renin levels of 4,016 to 4,604 pg/mL (reference range, 3-30 pg/mL). These results suggest that left renal artery stenosis primarily drove the patient's hypertension.

Invasive angiography fluoroscopic image demonstrates a smooth narrowing of the abdominal aorta starting proximal to the celiac axis, with poststenotic dilation of the juxtarenal aorta, serial distal stenosis of the infrarenal abdominal aorta, and proximal stenosis of 2 right renal arteries and no discernable left renal artery.

Given these findings, the case was discussed at a multidisciplinary pediatric renovascular hypertension group consisting of vascular surgery and pediatric nephrology. They recommended conservative medical management of the hypertension and HF due to the patient's young age as well as the substantial risks associated with surgical intervention in the context of significant cardiac dysfunction. They also recommended monthly aortoiliac duplex monitoring to evaluate for aneurysmal degeneration.

To understand the etiology of the MAS, the patient underwent evaluation with the genetics and rheumatology teams. Whole genome sequencing did not identify a particular genetic etiology. Rather, the patient was found to have a variant of uncertain significance in 1 copy of their MYPN gene; pathogenic variants of this gene are associated with autosomal dominant dilated cardiomyopathy and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy; however, the variant has not previously been identified in patients with this phenotype. The rheumatology workup included a CTA aortogram and CTA head, which did not demonstrate evidence of vessel thickening or enhancement to suggest active inflammation within the vessel walls. Also, the patient did not present with clinical indicators of active inflammation that would suggest a rheumatologic etiology, such as fevers, elevated inflammatory markers, hemoptysis, hematochezia, or nephritis.

Two weeks into admission, the patient decompensated from pyelonephritis. They experienced cardiac arrest during intubation, requiring cardiopulmonary resuscitation, reflective of their low cardiac reserve. The patient's pyelonephritis was treated successfully, and they were discharged 1 month after admission with an outpatient regimen to manage HF.

One year from diagnosis, the patient remains on cardiac remodeling medications for HF at the direction of the outpatient pediatric HF cardiology, pediatric nephrology, and vascular surgery teams. They have no cardiac symptoms of infant HF and are now in the 63rd percentile of weight for age. Their systolic blood pressures range from the mid-120s to low 130s. The patient has had no interval hospitalizations or recurrent pyelonephritis. The latest TTE demonstrates low normal function of the LV with an ejection fraction of 53% (Figure 7, Video 6). The left atrium is mildly dilated, and the LV is moderately dilated and mildly hypertrophied, with a left ventricular mass of 50.2 g (Z score of +3.3; Figure 8, Video 7). The mitral regurgitation has now improved to trace, and right ventricular function has normalized (Figure 9, Video 8). Monthly aortoiliac duplex ultrasounds have not shown any evidence of expansion of the juxtarenal aorta. Despite the eventual need for surgical correction of the anatomical narrowing, the patient's improvements in ventricular function and blood pressure with oral therapy have enabled the vascular surgery team to defer surgical intervention, allowing for ongoing somatic growth.

Discussion

We describe a case of a 7-month-old previously healthy infant with MAS who presented in acute decompensated HF and significant hypertension. Middle aortic syndrome is a rare vascular disorder defined by narrowing of the abdominal aorta and is referred to as coarctation of the abdominal aorta, middle aortic dysplastic syndrome, and middle aortic coarctation. The syndrome was first described in 1963 by Sen et al.1 The estimated prevalence is 1 in 1,000,000, and the disease constitutes 0.5% to 2% of all cases of aortic stenosis.2 While diagnosis in pediatric patients is uncommon, diagnosis in infancy is extremely rare, with only sporadic cases having been reported in the literature.3,4 The majority of pediatric MAS cases are diagnosed later in childhood and adolescence, but infant presentation is possible and should be considered in the workup for acute HF.

A recent systematic review of 630 cases between 1953 and 2014 found that 64% of cases were idiopathic, 17% were linked to an acquired inflammatory disease such as infectious or autoimmune vasculitis (the most common being Takayasu arteritis), and 15% were associated with a specific genetic syndrome (neurofibromatosis type I, William's syndrome, and Alagille's syndrome).4,5 Vasculitis seemed less likely as an etiology based on the patient's imaging findings and clinical presentation. Our patient's younger age and severity of presentation suggest a higher likelihood of a genetic etiology; however, none has been identified thus far.

A hallmark of MAS is hypertension refractory to medical management. If left untreated, the hypertension from MAS can progress to hypertensive encephalopathy or cerebrovascular accidents in patients presenting in their third or fourth decades.3 Children and adolescents most commonly present with hypertension as the first clue, as was true for our patient. One retrospective analysis of 53 children with MAS showed the average age of presentation was 6.7 years, with 90% presenting with hypertension and only 10% with congestive HF.4 The 11 patients who presented in infancy were noted to have more severe disease, characterized by higher left ventricular mass Z scores, renal dysfunction, and hypertensive emergency. Key physical examination findings include diminished or absent femoral pulses, blood pressure gradient between upper and lower extremities, and an abdominal bruit.

Definitive diagnosis of MAS relies on cross-sectional imaging such as CTA or invasive angiography via cardiac catheterization. However, echocardiography can provide quick, real-time diagnosis, particularly if careful attention is paid to specific clues, which can help distinguish primary, or intrinsic, myopathic left ventricular dysfunction from secondary left ventricular dysfunction from chronic obstruction. In particular, patients with a primary myopathy show early dilation of the left ventricular chamber size, with normal or thin wall thickness, whereas patients with obstruction-related left ventricular dysfunction more often show concentric left ventricular hypertrophy, with increased left ventricular mass. The left ventricular ejection fraction in obstructive physiology might be preserved early on and then decline later due to chronic afterload. In this case, the patient's initial TTE showed findings consistent with both chronic volume overload (left ventricular dilation) and chronic pressure overload (left ventricular hypertrophy). Without the benefit of a baseline echocardiogram, however, it was difficult to discern the chronicity of the echocardiographic findings.

Additional helpful clues on echocardiography include abnormal flow patterns in the abdominal aorta interrogated by spectral and color-flow Doppler, which can indicate obstruction. For example, increased flow velocity at the point of obstruction, diminished pulsatility distal to an obstruction, and diastolic continuation of flow could suggest an abdominal narrowing. Such findings would inform an early investigation with supplemental cross-sectional imaging. Importantly, this was not demonstrated in our case, likely because routine echocardiographic views focus on investigation of the proximal descending aorta only to the level of the diaphragm. This illustrates a very real diagnostic trap from relying on 1 imaging modality alone.

The optimal management of MAS requires a multidisciplinary and stepwise approach, given its complexity and rarity as a disease. Initially, medical management is the preferable choice, particularly in younger-age children whose vasculature is undergoing continuous growth and thus not suitable for a definitive graft procedure. The value of medical management in managing cardiac dysfunction from MAS may be explained by the observation that hypertension occurs not solely from mechanical obstruction but also from abnormal activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system and increased sympathetic tone. The kidneys, sensing the reduced perfusion distal to the obstruction, trigger renin release and subsequent angiotensin II–mediated vasoconstriction in peripheral vascular beds. Sampling of our patient's renal vein renin levels revealed striking lateralization, with disproportionately elevated renin concentrations in the left renal vein relative to the right, reflecting severe left renal hypoperfusion in the setting of near absence of the left renal artery. This pattern strongly implicated renin-angiotensin system activation as the dominant mechanism underlying the patient's malignant hypertension and cardiac failure. Accordingly, our antihypertensive strategy centered on blockade of the renin–angiotensin cascade with the use of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers, effectively reducing systemic arteriolar resistance and ventricular afterload. Medical management, as seen in this case, can thus play a significant clinical role, potentially delaying surgical intervention until children have grown closer to full adult size.

Ultimately, invasive intervention through a percutaneous endovascular or surgical approach is often required to definitively address the area of narrowing to stop ongoing end-organ damage, particularly when aortic narrowing exceeds 60%. Percutaneous interventions such as balloon angioplasty and stent placement are effective in acutely relieving obstruction and have a lower risk profile compared to surgery. However, there is a high incidence of restenosis and reintervention for complications such as vascular tears or the development of aneurysms, particularly when interventions are performed earlier in childhood.3 Surgical approaches vary widely but have been successful in relieving obstruction, with longer freedom from reintervention, of approximately 83% at 1 year and 72% at 10 years.3

Middle aortic syndrome is rare in infancy and can masquerade as primary cardiomyopathy on initial presentation. This case offers several important diagnostic insights that underscore both the importance of echocardiography as an initial screening tool and the value of a multimodal diagnostic approach. The initial TTE demonstrated a markedly dilated LV with superimposed symmetric hypertrophy, a pattern suggestive of chronic pressure overload that should prompt further evaluation for an obstructive etiology. However, standard echocardiographic assessment of the thoracic aorta appeared reassuring, despite the presence of critical juxtarenal and infrarenal aortic disease on cross-sectional imaging. This highlights a key diagnostic pitfall, which is that normal thoracic aortic anatomy on routine echocardiographic views, as in our patient, does not definitively exclude clinically significant distal aortic abnormalities.

In addition, this case highlights the diagnostic value of renal vein renin sampling in infants with complex renovascular pathology to identify the underlying derangement in renin levels and guide antihypertensive strategy. With targeted medication choices, our patient demonstrated substantial myocardial recovery and somatic catch-up growth with intensive medical therapy alone, allowing for safe deferral of definitive vascular repair during early childhood.

Conclusion

Our case of a 7-month-old infant presenting with decompensated HF and malignant hypertension highlights the need for persistent diagnostic investigation to find rare causes when common etiologies do not explain clinical findings. Timely diagnosis and multidisciplinary management of HF and hypertension were central to achieving an excellent clinical outcome in this patient with MAS.
