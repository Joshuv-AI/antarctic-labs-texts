Clinical Impact of Reperfusion Techniques and Occlusion Sites in Thrombectomy: Insights from the ASSIST Stroke Registry
Authors: Ulf Neuberger, Lori Lyn Price, Salvador Miralbés, Bharath Naravetla, Alejandro Spiotta, Christian Loehr, Mario Martínez‐Galdámez, Ryan A McTaggart, Luc Defreyne, Pedro Vega, Osama O Zaidat, David S Liebeskind, Rishi Gupta, Markus Alfred Möhlenbruch, the ASSIST Investigators
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMechanical thrombectomy has changed the landscape of acute ischemic stroke treatment, offering improved outcomes for patients with large vessel occlusions. The interplay between reperfusion techniques and occlusion sites remains a critical consideration in treatment decisions. This study investigates the impact of primary reperfusion techniques—stent retriever classic, stent retriever combination with aspiration, and direct aspiration—on procedural and clinical outcomes at different occlusion sites.MethodsUsing data from the ASSIST registry, a prospective multinational initiative, patients with anterior circulation acute ischemic stroke with large vessel occlusions (n = 1477) were included. Three primary occlusion sites—M1 segment of the middle cerebral artery, M2 segment of the middle cerebral artery, and the internal carotid artery—were studied. Univariate tests and multivariable logistic regression models were used to assess associations between reperfusion techniques and procedural and clinical outcomes, including the expanded thrombolysis in cerebral infarction score and the modified Rankin Scale, stratified by occlusion site.ResultsAchieving expanded thrombolysis in cerebral infarction ≥2c after first pass was lower in internal carotid artery occlusions (18.2%, P<0.0001) with direct aspiration alone compared with stent retriever classic (38.2%) or stent retriever combination with aspiration (43.3%), while no differences were seen for M1 segment of the middle cerebral artery (P = 0.77) and M2 segment of the middle cerebral artery (P = 0.29) occlusions. Bailout maneuvers, which were more frequent in the direct aspiration group for all occlusion sites, did not result in longer procedure times or different final recanalization outcomes in direct aspiration compared with stent retriever classic or stent retriever combination with aspiration. Overall, there were no differences in the rate of patients with modified Rankin Scale scores of 0–2 at 90 days. No differences in safety outcomes were observed.ConclusionsThis study provides insight into the complex relationship between reperfusion techniques and occlusion sites in patients with acute ischemic stroke and shows that certain techniques have advantages in certain occlusion sites. As the field evolves, further research is warranted to refine our understanding of these dynamics and inform clinical practice.Keywords: acute ischemic stroke, clinical outcomes, mechanical thrombectomy, occlusion locations, reperfusion techniques

The management of acute ischemic stroke has been transformed by the introduction of mechanical thrombectomy, particularly in cases of large vessel occlusion. This innovative intervention has significantly improved patient outcomes, as demonstrated by several clinical trials demonstrating its efficacy. 1 Successful reperfusion, an important determinant of neurological recovery, is essential to achieve favorable clinical outcomes. Three major reperfusion techniques have emerged in this quest: stent retriever classic (SR classic), which uses only a stent retriever; stent retriever in combination with aspiration (SR combination), which combines the use of a stent retriever with aspiration 2 , 3 , 4 ; and direct aspiration (DA) without SR, which uses aspiration catheters to remove the clot directly. 5 , 6

Although previous studies have provided valuable insights into the comparative effectiveness of these techniques, an area of particular interest that requires careful investigation is the interplay between reperfusion methods and occlusion site. 7 The correlation between successful reperfusion and occlusion site has significant implications for treatment outcome, which could affect neurological recovery and functional independence. Although previous studies have provided some understanding of the wider field of mechanical thrombectomy, it is crucial to conduct a thorough analysis based on real‐world practice to clarify the differential efficacy of reperfusion technique in different occlusion sites.

The expanded thrombolysis in cerebral infarction (eTICI) score is a valuable tool for quantifying the extent of reperfusion achieved and provides a standardized measure of treatment success. 8 Higher eTICI scores, particularly eTICI 2c or 3, preferably after the first attempt, are associated with improved clinical outcomes and reduced disability. 9 , 10 Given the critical nature of initial reperfusion, this analysis investigates which technique—SR classic, SR combination, or DA—demonstrates superior efficacy in promptly achieving optimal eTICI 2c or 3 scores, particularly in the context of different occlusion sites.

Drawing from data of the comprehensive ASSIST registry, 11 a prospective, multicenter initiative, this secondary analysis aims to explore the complex relationship between reperfusion techniques and occlusion site in patients with anterior circulation acute ischemic stroke and large vessel occlusion. By examining procedural outcomes, clinical end points, and safety measures, we aim to provide a detailed understanding of how the choice of reperfusion method influences the frequency of achieving first‐pass eTICI 2c or 3 at different occlusion sites. This investigation aims to improve the decision‐making process for mechanical thrombectomy, providing valuable insights for tailoring treatment strategies to occlusion‐specific considerations and ultimately optimizing patient outcomes.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

The data supporting the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request. The analysis is based on the framework of the ASSIST registry, a multinational, multicenter initiative for patients with acute ischemic stroke and large vessel occlusion in the anterior circulation. The registry is a comprehensive platform to evaluate the efficacy of interventional strategies for revascularization. The primary focus of this paper is on the first pass treatment of target vessel occlusions. Our analysis examines three different interventional techniques: SR classic, SR combination, and DA, all using Stryker neurovascular devices on the first pass to treat target occlusion. The analysis was limited to 1477 patients with clots in the M1 Segment of the middle cerebral artery (M1), M2 segment of the middle cerebral artery (M2), or internal carotid artery (ICA).

Seventy‐one clinical sites in 11 countries actively participated in the registry, enrolling a total of 1492 patients. Prior to patient recruitment, each site received written approval from the ethics committee/institutional review board. To ensure standardized practice and operator expertise, sites completed a qualification survey outlining their primary technique. This trial is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (registration number NCT03845491). The ASSIST registry was an observational cohort study and thus adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.

Inclusion criteria required the presence of acute ischemic stroke with occlusion in an intracranial anterior circulation vessel. Treatment was performed in selected patients using 3 predefined techniques with approved Stryker Neurovascular products. Patients or their authorized representatives provided informed consent prior to or within 48 hours of the procedure. The registry data collection included multiple devices such as the AXS Infinity LS Long Sheath, AXS Infinity LS Plus Long Sheath, FlowGate2, and Merci Balloon Guided Catheter (BGC), AXS Catalyst Distal Access Catheter, AXS Vecta Aspiration Catheter, Trevo Retriever, and Medela Dominant Flex Pump.

Procedures

After initial clinical and radiological assessment, patients deemed suitable for mechanical thrombectomy underwent diagnostic angiography to identify and confirm the site of occlusion. The primary approach was chosen as the intention to treat for the initial intervention. The procedure was terminated when successful reperfusion was achieved or when the occluded cerebral vessel could not be recanalized, according to the established protocols of each institution.

Data collection included various parameters such as demographics, pretreatment neurological assessments, radiographic characteristics, and for each pass, technique, clot location, angiographic images, and eTICI after each pass and final eTICI. Participating sites were responsible for submitting deidentified angiographic data for enrolled patients. Health care professionals operated the devices in accordance with the stated intended use and local regulatory approvals.

The study evaluated recanalization techniques including SR classic, SR combination, and DA without SR as procedural techniques. SR classic represents the sole use of a stent retriever, whereas SR combination involves the use of DA, possibly with a pump, together with proximal flow control and a stent retriever. DA involves aspiration through an aspiration catheter, such as a BGC or long sheath, and may include a pump.

A dedicated, independent core laboratory evaluated baseline noncontrast computed tomography (CT), CT angiography and/or magnetic resonance imaging, and CT or magnetic resonance imaging scans performed at baseline, during the procedure, and 24 hours after treatment to identify any complications. The initial diagnostic angiography of the target vessel was also reviewed in detail to identify the initial site of occlusion and the degree of reperfusion after each pass and the final pass. Data management, surgery, and site monitoring for patients enrolled in the registry were overseen by the sponsor, Stryker Neurovascular.

Outcomes

Primary Procedural End Point

The primary procedural end point was defined as eTICI 2c or 3 recanalization on the first pass in relation to the primary site of occlusion.

Primary Clinical End Point

The primary clinical end point revolved around the 90‐day modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score, with a target range of 0–2 (defined as “good clinical outcome”). Clinical assessment was performed by certified personnel at the procedural centers.

Secondary End Points

Additional procedural outcomes included analysis of eTICI scores at the end of the endovascular procedure. Time intervals from inguinal puncture to achievement of eTICI 2c or 3 on the first attempt and final reperfusion were examined. Subsequent clinical considerations focused on the achievement of excellent functional outcomes, as signified by a 90‐day mRS score of 0–1. In addition, an early response was assessed either at discharge or on days 5–7 after the procedure, whichever came first. Early response was defined as a reduction of ≥10 National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) points from baseline or an NIHSS score of 0 or 1.

Safety End Points

Safety parameters included all‐cause and stroke‐related mortality, as well as any device‐ and/or procedure‐related serious adverse events during the study period. Supplementary assessments included the occurrence of neurological deterioration within 48 hours, defined as an increase of ≥4 points from baseline on the NIHSS scale. The occurrence of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage, adapted from the European Cooperative Acute Stroke Study III, 12 was also documented. In addition, cases of embolization to new territory were assessed by the core laboratory. Adverse events, including serious events, were monitored from the start of the procedure to 90 days.

Statistical Analysis

Clinical, demographic, and procedural characteristics specific to the SR vlassic, SR combination, and DA groups were presented separately for M1, M2, and ICA occlusion locations. Data were presented as means or frequencies, as appropriate. P values were calculated using generalized linear mixed models with a random effect for continuous variables and chi‐square tests adjusted for clustering for categorical variables to account for clustering within the center. In rare cases where the algorithm would not converge due to zero cell count, an unadjusted Fisher exact test was performed for categorical variables.

Multivariable Logistic Regression for “First‐Pass Efficacy”

Multivariable logistic regression models were fitted to assess first‐pass efficacy, defined as eTICI 2c or 3. Only variables with univariate P values <0.05 were selected as candidates for multivariable analysis. Backward selection was then used to include only variables with P values <0.05, except for the technique variable, which was mandatory. Only patients with complete data for all variables were included (n = 676 for M1 occlusions, n = 308 for M2 occlusions, n = 276 for ICA occlusions). Analyses were adjusted for clustering within centers.

Multivariable Logistic Regression for “Good Clinical Outcome”

Similar to the approach described earlier, multivariable logistic regression models were constructed to assess the primary outcome of good clinical outcome (90‐day mRS score 0–2). Backward selection was again used for parameter selection. Variables with univariate P ‐values <0.05 were first included in the model and then removed if their P values exceeded 0.05, with the exception of technique, which we forced into our analysis. Regression models were fitted separately for each occlusion site (M1, M2, and ICA). Only patients with complete data for all variables and a baseline mRS score of 0–2 were included (n = 502 for M1 occlusions, n = 240 for M2 occlusions, n = 193 for ICA occlusions). Analyses adjusted for clustering within centers.

Analysis to Assess Effects of BGC Usage

Additional univariate analyses were performed to assess first‐pass efficacy and 90‐day good clinical outcome rates with the use of a BGC in patients in the DA or SR combination arm. Patients treated with SR classic could not be included in these analyses as the use of a BGC was mandatory in this group.

SAS 9.4 (Cary, NC) was used for all analyses. P values <0.05 were considered as significant.

Ethics Statement

The study was performed in compliance with the World Medical Association's Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the local ethics committee or institutional review board.

Results

A total of 1477 patients were included in the analysis, divided into the SR classic arm (n = 246, 16.7%), the SR combination arm (n = 685, 46.4%), and the DA arm (n = 546, 37.0%). The overall mean age of the study population was 70.4±14.2 years with a median NIHSS score of 14 (interquartile range 9–19). Of all patients, 786 (53.2%) had a primary M1 occlusion, 368 (24.9%) had a primary M2 occlusion, and a further 323 (21.9%) had a primary ICA occlusion. For further details on baseline and demographic characteristics of the full study cohort, please refer to the primary publication of the ASSIST study. 11

Univariate Results for Technique by Clot Location

Clot Location M1 (n = 786)

Patients treated with DA were significantly younger (68.3±15.9 years) than those treated with SR classic (72.5±14.7 years) or combined technique (72.8±12.3 years; P<0.001). They also had a higher proportion of patients with a good premorbid mRS score of 0–2 at baseline (93.9% versus 88.1% for SR classic and 85.8% for SR combination; P = 0.002). However, patients in the DA and SR combination arms had lower baseline NIHSS scores of 14.7±6.0 and 14.1±6.6, respectively, compared with 16.2±6.2 for SR classic (P = 0.04). Furthermore, patients in the DA arm had a lower Alberta Stroke Programme Early CT Score (ASPECTS) at baseline (7.2±1.5) compared with 7.7±1.3 for SR classic and 7.5±1.4 for SR combination (P = 0.01).

Patients treated with DA on the first pass were significantly more likely to require a bailout technique, which refers to a modification of the primary approach, with 27.3% compared with 12.4% for SR classic and 8.2% for SR combination (P<0.001). Although there was no significant difference in the rate of achieving eTICI≥2c after the first treatment pass between the groups, patients treated with SR classic or SR combination as the initial technique had a higher proportion of complete recanalization (eTICI≥2c) at the end of the initial technique with 68.1% and 66.9% respectively, compared with 55.9% for those treated with the DA technique (P = 0.002). There was no significant difference in the outcome of recanalization at the end of the procedure, including when bailout maneuvers were performed (P = 0.75). Overall, the time from groin puncture to end of procedure was shorter in patients treated with DA with a mean of 28.6±22.6 min compared with 36.8±28.3 min for SR classic and 38.6±28.8 min for SR combination (P = 0.02).

There were no significant differences in safety outcomes.

M2 Clot Location (n = 368)

There were no significant differences in the clinical characteristics or primary clinical end point of patients with M2 clots when stratified by technique. However, we saw a higher rate of bailouts in patients initially treated with DA (34.8% in the DA group versus 9.5% and 11.7% in the SR classic and SR combination groups, respectively, P<0.001). Patients treated with SR classic had a higher rate of patients with an excellent outcome of mRS score 0–1 at 90 days with 62.9% compared with patients treated with SR combination with 45.8% (P = 0.001), whereas no significant difference was seen compared with patients treated with DA (55.0%, P = 0.26).

There were no significant differences in procedural or safety outcomes between the techniques.

Clot Location ICA (n = 323)

There were age differences between patients, with those treated with the SR combination having a mean age of 71.0±12.3 years, compared with 66.6±16.0 years in the DA arm and 69.6±14.6 years in the SR classic arm (P = 0.04). Patients in the SR classic arm were less likely to receive concomitant intravenous lysis therapy, with only 21.1% receiving treatment compared with 31.4% in the DA arm and 41.1% in the SR combination arm (P = 0.02). Patients in the DA group were significantly more likely to require bailout, with 56.9% requiring bailout compared with the SR classic (10.5%) and SR combination (16.4%) groups (P<0.001).

Patients treated with DA were less likely to achieve an eTICI≥2c on the first attempt, with only 18.2% achieving this compared with 38.2% in the SR classic and 43.3% in the SR combination groups (P<0.001). At the end of the procedure, including bailouts, there were no significant differences in achieving eTICI≥2c or in total procedure time.

No significant differences were observed in the achievement of the primary and secondary clinical end points or in the safety outcomes.

First‐Pass Efficacy Regression Models

In patients with M1 occlusions, a first‐pass effect was achieved in 320 of 676 patients (47.3%). According to the univariate analysis, higher age (odds ratio [OR] = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.01–1.03, P = 0.007), higher baseline ASPECTS (OR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.13–1.41, P<0.001), and shorter time from last known well to groin puncture (global P = 0.02; OR for 6–24 h = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.58–1.02, P = 0.06 and for >24 h = 1.83, 95% CI: 0.70–4.79, P = 0.22) all showed a significant association with achieving a first‐pass effect. Only higher age and higher total ASPECTS at baseline remained as independent predictors in the multivariable analysis. Technique group was not significantly associated with first‐pass efficacy in either univariate or multivariable models.

In the M2 occlusion group, 127 of 308 patients (41.2%) achieved first‐pass efficacy. Lower baseline NIHSS score (OR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.93–0.99, P = 0.02) and higher baseline ASPECTS (OR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.08–1.50, P = 0.005) were associated with first‐pass efficacy. In multivariable analysis, baseline NIHSS score and ASPECTS remained significant (OR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.93–1.00, P = 0.04 and OR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.02–1.46, P = 0.03, respectively). There was no significant association between technique groups and first‐pass efficacy in either univariate or multivariable models.

First‐pass efficacy was achieved in 84 of 276 patients (30.4%) with ICA occlusion. The DA technique group was associated with significantly lower odds of success (OR = 0.29, 95% CI: 0.12–0.70, P = 0.007) compared with SR classic in univariate regression. SR combination was not statistically associated with outcome in univariate analysis. No other variables showed a significant association with first‐pass efficacy.

For a complete overview of the first‐pass efficacy regression models, see online‐only Tables S2–S4.

Good Functional Outcome at 90 Days Regression Models

A total of 285 of 502 patients (56.82%) with M1 occlusions achieved a 90‐day mRS score of 0–2. On univariate analysis, younger age (OR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.96–0.99, P = 0.009), lower baseline NIHSS score (OR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.88–0.94, P<0.0001), and higher baseline total ASPECTS (OR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.09–1.43, P = 0.002) were significantly associated with better outcomes. Longer time intervals from last known well to end of procedure, <6–24 hours (OR = 0.44, 95% CI: 0.32–0.62, P<0.0001) and >24 hours (OR = 0.10, 95% CI: 0.02–0.42, P = 0.002) were significantly associated with unfavorable outcomes compared with <6 hours. Intravenous tissue plasminogen activator administration was also significantly associated with better outcomes (OR = 1.80, 95% CI: 1.26–2.55, P = 0.002). Achieving eTICI 2c or 3 at the end of the first run or eTICI 2b or higher at the end of the procedure was also significantly associated with a better outcome, whereas blood glucose ≥150 was associated with an unfavorable outcome. In univariate analysis, SR combination (OR = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.30–0.86, P = 0.01) and DA (OR = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.33–0.96, P = 0.03) were both associated with reduced odds of favorable outcomes compared with the reference technique of SR classic. In multivariable analysis, technique group, age, baseline NIHSS score, baseline total ASPECTS, eTICI 2b or higher at end of procedure, and time from last known well to end of procedure retained their significant associations.

In the cohort of patients with M2 occlusions, 164 out of 240 patients (68.3%) achieved functional independence at 90 days. Lower age (OR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.95–0.99, P = 0.001), lower baseline NIHSS score (OR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.81–0.89, P<0.0001), higher baseline total ASPECTS (OR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.09–1.58, P = 0.005), and achieving eTICI≥2c on the first pass (OR = 3.47, 95% CI: 1.76–6.85, P = 0.0006) and eTICI≥2b at the end of the procedure (OR = 3.99, 95% CI: 1.36–11.69, P = 0.01) were significantly associated with a favorable outcome in univariate analysis. Age, baseline NIHSS score, and higher eTICI scores remained significant variables in the multivariable analysis. The technique group showed no significant associations in either univariate or multivariable analysis.

Ninety‐five of 193 patients (49.2%) with ICA occlusion achieved functional independence. In univariate analysis, lower age (OR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.94–0.99, P = 0.002), lower baseline NIHSS score (OR = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.82–0.92, P<0.0001), higher baseline total ASPECTS (OR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.07–1.55, P = 0.009), and lower blood glucose levels (OR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.20–0.81, P = 0.01) were significantly associated with a favorable 90‐day mRS outcome. Age, baseline NIHSS score, baseline total ASPECTS, and blood glucose levels remained significant in multivariable analysis. The technique groups did not show significant associations in either univariate or multivariable analysis.

A complete overview of the univariate and multivariable regression analyses can be found in online‐only Tables S5–S7.

Analysis to Assess Effects of BGC Usage

In patients with M1 occlusions, no significant difference was observed in achieving eTICI≥2c on first pass (P = 0.29) or good clinical outcome (P = 0.65) when comparing patients with (n = 211) and without (n = 205) BGC in the DA and SR combination groups combined. When looking at patients with M2 occlusions, similar results were found for first‐pass efficacy (P = 0.73) or achieving good clinical outcome (P = 0.72) in patients with (n = 110) or without (n = 140) BGC use. In patients with ICA occlusions, the use of BGC (n = 115) was associated with good clinical outcome (P = 0.05, OR = 2.52, 95% CI: 1.01–6.25) but not with first‐pass efficacy (P = 0.60) compared with patients without BGC (n = 50).

Discussion

The present study, a secondary analysis of the multinational prospective stroke registry ASSIST, investigates the important interplay between reperfusion techniques and site of occlusion in patients with anterior circulation acute ischemic stroke and large vessel occlusion. Our investigation examined three primary reperfusion techniques—SR vlassic, SR combination, and DA—and their varying efficacy at different occlusion sites, namely M1, M2, and the ICA.

Most important, our investigation uncovered differences in achieving optimal reperfusion outcomes, with SR methods demonstrating an advantage over DA. The success rate of achieving eTICI≥2c after the first attempt or at end of initial technique showed a convincing trend, although it did not always reach statistical significance, consistently favoring the use of SRs for all occlusion sites, especially for ICA and M2 occlusions. This reliable pattern highlights the effectiveness of SR‐based methods in achieving the desired level of reperfusion, particularly in consistently achieving a high first‐pass effect. The consistency of this trend across different occlusion sites suggests that SR‐based methods are widely applicable to optimize reperfusion outcomes, regardless of the location of the occlusion. In a post hoc analysis of the multicenter randomized clinical trial of endovascular treatment for acute ischemic stroke in the Netherlands (MR CLEAN) registry, Bernsen et al compared the clinical and technical outcomes of first‐line DA versus SR thrombectomy in different anterior circulation occlusion sites. In contrast to our results, they observed a higher rate of successful first‐pass reperfusion with aspiration (54%) compared with their SR group (48%), especially in M1 segment occlusions. 7

The notable advantage demonstrated by SRs in our analysis may be due to their inherent ability to engage and capture thrombus material with precision and efficiency. The design and mechanism of SRs facilitate secure clot engagement, ensuring a higher likelihood of successful clot retrieval with fewer instances of fragmentation or distal embolization. 13 This feature may be particularly suited to the intricate and diverse occlusion sites in the anterior circulation, providing a reliable advantage across different anatomical regions. Although DA represents a different method of clot removal, its effectiveness may be affected by the composition and location of the thrombus, as well as the relationship between the size of the occluded vessel and the aspiration catheter, particularly in the case of the ICA. This interplay of factors may contribute significantly to the observed variability in achieving ideal reperfusion outcomes.

Furthermore, the observed benefit of using SR techniques to achieve eTICI≥2c highlights the importance of successful revascularization in reducing the extent of ischemic damage and optimizing clinical outcomes. Rapid and efficient reperfusion has been associated with better neurological recovery and reduced disability, emphasizing the critical role of the initial reperfusion method in determining patient outcomes. The consistent advantage of SR methods in this context highlights their potential to provide a prompt intervention, creating a more favorable environment for neuronal recovery and reducing the downstream effects of the ischemic insult.

A significant impact of this discrepancy in initial efficacy is the observed increase in the number of bailout maneuvers in the DA group. Patients in this group required more adjustments to the primary approach to achieve successful recanalization, with bailout rates for DA ranging from 27.3% to 56%. Interestingly, however, this did not significantly increase overall procedure time of DA compared with SR combination and SR classic. In fact, for M1 and ICA occlusions, when revascularization was possible with DA alone, the procedure time was shorter in DA, in line with the results of the ASTER (Contact Aspiration Versus Stent Retriever for Recanalisation of Acute Stroke Patients With Basilar Artery Occlusion) and direct aspiration first pass technique (COMPASS) trials. 5 , 6 This suggests that although DA required more frequent method changes, its innate effectiveness and ease of use allowed equal or even shorter procedure times in certain situations. When switching from DA, the guiding catheter is already in place, allowing for rapid subsequent maneuvers using other techniques. In the MR CLEAN registry analysis by Behrens et al, they reported high bailout rates of 76% in the DA group and 53% in the SR group; however, these figures represent only changes after an unsuccessful first recanalization attempt with the respective technique. 7

The initial difference in complete recanalization rates between SR‐based techniques and DA was attenuated by the end of the procedure after bailout maneuvers. The advantage in patients achieving complete recanalization in the SR groups after the first pass and before bailout disappeared by the end of the procedure, which has also been reported previously. 13 This suggests that although the SR methods initially showed a greater ability to achieve complete recanalization, the subsequent interventions required in the DA group managed to close the gap by the end of the procedure. Strikingly, the increased frequency of bailout maneuvers did not result in a significantly longer overall procedure time in the DA cohort compared with the other 2 cohorts. This suggests that the DA technique, although requiring additional modifications to the technical approach, remained effective in terms of time, possibly due to its simple and streamlined approach and the shorter duration of the maneuver compared with SRs, which often require a recommended waiting period after deployment.

Looking at the primary clinical end point, the proportion of patients achieving a favorable mRS score of 0–2 at 90 days, we found no significant differences between technique for any of the occlusion sites. This suggests that although the choice of reperfusion technique had an impact on early reperfusion success, it did not influence the ultimate clinical outcome as measured by mRS scores at 90 days.

For M2 occlusions, a significant difference in the achievement of an excellent clinical outcome (mRS score 0–1) was observed between the SR classic and SR combination groups. However, it is important to consider the potential bias introduced by the different cohort sizes, as only a limited number of patients were treated with SR classic for M2 occlusions. This discrepancy may affect the generalizability of this finding and warrants further investigation. In this regard, we also saw a significant difference in achieving a good clinical outcome in patients with ICA occlusions treated with a BGC. However, the results regarding the use of BGCs in our cohort are limited by the fact that the use of BGCs was mandatory in the SR classic group and only subgroup comparisons could be made in the other 2 groups, with correspondingly smaller case numbers.

In particular, the percentage of patients achieving first‐pass efficacy with DA treatment for ICA occlusion showed a significantly low success rate. This may be due to the challenging nature of ICA occlusions, which are often characterized by high thrombus burden and susceptibility to fragmentation and distal embolization. The lower use of BGCs in the DA group in the ASSIST registry may also have contributed to this observation. 14

Importantly, there were no statistical differences in the safety profile of any of the methods. Although some small differences in specific proportions were observed, there were no statistically significant differences in all‐cause mortality at 90 days, stroke‐related mortality at 90 days, or symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage up to 48 hours post procedure between the method groups for each occlusion site.

It is important to acknowledge several limitations that must be considered when interpreting our results. First, although efforts were made to standardize practice across the participating centers, the inherent variability in operator expertise and preference may introduce bias into the choice of reperfusion technique, potentially influencing the observed outcomes. In addition, the nature of data collection and reliance on the ASSIST registry may introduce inherent selection bias, as treatment decisions were not randomized and may have been influenced by patient‐specific characteristics or operator preference. Furthermore, the relatively small sample size within certain subgroups may limit the generalizability of our findings and potentially contribute to imbalances that could affect the observed results. Different sample sizes of groups lead to different groups having power differences, making comparisons across occlusion locations more difficult. Also, variations in procedural approaches, patient‐specific clinical considerations, and evolving interventional device technology may introduce confounding factors that were not captured in our analysis. We did not collect detailed information on the device sizes relative to occlusion locations, which may have influenced our results, especially where specific device sizes might be more or less optimal for successful recanalization. Importantly, our analysis of the ASSIST registry was limited to devices from a single manufacturer, Stryker. This may limit the generalizability of our findings, as different manufacturers offer a variety of device designs and possible inherent strengths. Finally, the potential influence of unmeasured or residual confounding variables, such as the specifics of clot composition or underlying vascular anatomy, cannot be completely excluded.

In conclusion, our analysis within the ASSIST registry provides valuable insights into the impact of reperfusion techniques on clinical outcomes in patients with acute ischemic stroke with different occlusion sites. Although certain patterns emerge, such as the potential superiority of SR classic and SR combination in achieving a first‐pass effect, the decision‐making process remains multifaceted and dependent on a variety of patient‐specific factors. Ultimately, the choice of reperfusion technique should be guided by a comprehensive understanding of the patient's clinical profile and site of occlusion, with the aim of achieving optimal patient outcomes and functional independence. As the field evolves, further research is warranted to refine our understanding of these dynamics and inform clinical practice.
