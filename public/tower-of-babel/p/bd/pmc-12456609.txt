Difficult airway and anesthesia management in a patient with sturge-weber syndrome related to excessive lip swollen and giant facial hemangioma: A case report
Authors: Figen Leblebici, Zeynep Kayhan, Nedim Çekmen, Zoukou Marie France Dominique Seri, Ezgi Güneş
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sturge-Weber Syndrome (SWS) is a non-familial neurocutaneous disease related to capillary-venous malformations affecting many parts, such as the brain, skin, eyes, face, and respiratory tract and characterized by facial capillary malformation (port wine stain), which is a highly complex and significant challenge for anaesthesiologists. Difficult ventilation and intubation are expected in patients with airway, mouth, lip, and facial involvement of angiomas. Soft induction intubation and extubation are required to prevent increased intracranial (ICP) and intraocular pressure (IOP). We aimed to present the case of a 36-year-old female patient with a hemangioma covering three-quarters of the oral cavity, pharynx, larynx, floor of the mouth, face, neck, and both arms, and her lower lip was significantly swollen and protruding due to the angioma, and she had macroglossia. Therefore, a comprehensive preoperative multidisciplinary approach to examining, evaluating, and closely monitoring these patients is crucial for successful anesthesia management.

Introduction

Sturge–Weber syndrome (SWS) is a neurocutaneous disease characterized by facial capillary-venous malformations, neurological disability, leptomeningeal angioma, glaucoma, and seizures. SWS is a neurocutaneous disease with many critical features for a significant challenge for anesthesia management. A somatic mosaic mutation in the GANQ gene causes SWS.[12] Intraoral and facial angiomas can involve the lips, buccal mucosa, floor of the mouth, gums, palate, pharynx, larynx, trachea, and bronchi, and many changes can be seen in the mouth due to asymmetrical jaw growth, and these can often cause difficulties in mask ventilation and intubation.[3]

Anesthesiologists should try to avoid trauma as much as possible due to the rise in increased intracranial (ICP) and intraocular pressure (IOP) caused by hemangioma. Techniques such as tracheal intubation can cause angiomas to rupture, resulting in uncontrolled bleeding. Safe and successful anesthesia management includes smooth anesthesia induction, decreased hemodynamic responses during airway maneuver and surgery, cautious and proper intubation/extubation, preventing trauma to angiomatous tissues, and sufficient pain management.[24567]

Herein, we report the uneventful, difficult airway and anesthesia management in a patient with SWS related to excessive lip swelling and giant facial hemangioma

Case Report

A 36-year-old female patient (weight: 59 kg, height: 158 cm) diagnosed with SWS was admitted to the ophthalmology clinic and planned for a left vitrectomy under general anesthesia due to glaucoma. The patient had a history of epilepsy, but it was learned that she had not had a seizure for 11 years under the control of antiepileptic treatment (sodium valproate and carbamazepine). There was no problem with verbal communication, intellectual ability, or neurological deficit. Her preoperative laboratory tests, electrocardiogram, and chest X-ray were within normal limits. The Mallampati score was evaluated as IV (only the hard palate was visible due to multiple hemangiomas), upper lip bite test (ULBT) III, and physical condition as American Society of Anesthesiologists II. Local ethics committee approval and the patient’s written consent were obtained to publish this case report.

A preoperative physical examination showed the patient had a hemangioma covering her face and her lower lip was significantly swollen and protruding due to the angioma and macroglossia [Figure 1]. Our routine airway management equipment included the ProSeal laryngeal mask airway, supraglottic airway, the laryngeal tube, the laryngeal tube with integrated suctioning tube and the esophageal tracheal combi-tube, tube stylet, gum bougie, direct laryngoscope, video laryngoscope, and fiberoptic bronchoscope, and we prepared an emergency tracheostomy and cricothyrotomy set in case of unsuccessful intubation.

A physical examination showed the patient had a hemangioma covering three-quarters of the oral cavity, face, neck, and arms

The patient was admitted to the operating room and standard monitoring, bispectral index, and axillary thermometer were performed. We placed an 18-gauge cannula in her left hand for venous access. Anesthesia smooth induction was continued with lidocaine 50 mg, fentanyl 100 μg, propofol 150 mg, and rocuronium bromide 40 mg. Although we placed an oral airway after 3 min of preoxygenation, we had difficulty with mask ventilation. One anesthetist held the mask with both hands while the other anesthesiologist performed manual ventilation. The larynx was anesthetized with 2% lignocaine spray to minimize intubation stress. We intubated the patient in a non-traumatic, well-lubricated, and careful manner with a size 7.0 endotracheal tube on the first attempt uneventfully. Video laryngoscopy revealed Cormack–Lehane Grade IV. The surgery was completed without any complications. For postoperative analgesia, 1000 mg of paracetamol was administered intravenously. Neuromuscular blockade was reversed by administering 150 mg sugammadex to the patient, whose spontaneous breathing returned 4 min after sevoflurane was discontinued. The patient complied with verbal commands and was extubated and transferred to the postanesthesia care unit (PACU). The patient with an Aldrete score of 9 was discharged to her room and from the hospital 1 day later.

Discussion

A comprehensive multidisciplinary (neurology, ophthalmology, anaesthesiology) evaluation and approach of patients with SWS is critical to prevent complications. Since SWS affects many regions such as the brain, eyes, face, skin, and respiratory tract due to facial capillary-venous malformations, difficult airway and intubation management poses a significant challenge for anesthesiologists and should be precisely planned for each patient.[123]

A difficult airway and intubation can quickly result in life-threatening and fatal complications if adequate preparation is not made in a situation where the possibility is correctly identified. Therefore, anesthesiologists should be familiar with difficult airway and intubation algorithms and have sufficient experience. The anesthesiologist’s primary goals are adequate ventilation, oxygenation, and a clear airway critical to patient survival.[1234]

İn our case, we performed intubation on our patient on the first attempt without any complications, using lignocaine spray, following gentle induction in a non-traumatic, smooth, well-lubricated, and careful manner, using a video laryngoscope. Close control of blood pressure is critical to prevent angiomas from rupturing. Our patient remained hemodynamically stable throughout the surgery, and we did not observe any bleeding complications. Moreover, in these patients, careful tracheobronchial suction is also mandatory.

Patients with SWS may have seizures, hemiparesis, stroke-like attacks, developmental delay, learning disability, and mental retardation due to leptomeningeal angiomas. Our patient’s seizures were under control with antiepileptic treatment since the age of 11, and the patient had no problems with her verbal communication and intellectual ability and did not have any neurological deficits. In patients with SWS, factors that provoke seizures, such as hypoxemia, hypoperfusion, hypotension, hypercarbia, acidosis, hypoglycemia, and hypothermia, should be minimized as much as possible during the intraoperative period.[245] We tried to prevent these as much as possible in our patient during the perioperative period.

Mathur R et al.[8] reported that a 12-year-old boy with SWS was intubated without complications, after applying lignocaine spray and following a mild induction, and no significant problems were observed, such as in our case.

Conclusion

SWS is a non-familial neurocutaneous disease that poses a significant challenge to anesthesiologists. In managing SWS, a multidisciplinary approach, comprehensive preoperative examination, and precision preparation are essential to reduce complications. The main goal should be preventing the expected difficulty of airway and intubation, smooth induction, non-traumatic intubation and extubation, preservation of hemodynamic stability, reducing factors that will increase the convulsion threshold, and preventing ICP and IOP increases.
