Peptic ulcer disease: Insights and risk factors
Authors: Haidar Houmani, Islam Saidi, Meline M'rini, Julie Nguyen, Assaad Salame, Patrick Bontems, Kallirroi Kotilea
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPeptic ulcer disease (PUD), affects adults and children, with Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection being the most frequent cause. Diagnosis requires an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy (UGE) and invasive tests. However, besides H. pylori, risk factors for PUD in children remain unclear. The aim of this study is to investigate pediatric PUD's epidemiology, etiology, and associated risk factors.MethodsA retrospective case‐control study included children aged 1 month to 17 years presenting PUD, defined as presence of ulceration/erosion of the gastric/duodenal mucosa, and two controls per case. UGE's were performed between 2016 and 2022 in a pediatric center. Diagnosis of H. pylori was based on culture and histology of gastric biopsies.ResultsAmong 2355 eligible children, 335 with PUD (14.2%) and 670 controls were included. The median age was 9.9 years (IQR: 6.1–13.1). The main indications for endoscopy were dyspepsia (38.7%) and chronic abdominal pain (15.5%). H. pylori infection was more frequent in cases than controls (35.7% vs. 17.2%, p < 0.001). Independent risk factors for PUD included age ≥ 11 years (OR 1.83, 95% CI: 1.21–2.75; p = 0.004), gastrointestinal bleeding (OR 3.18, 95% CI: 1.15–8.82; p = 0.026), and concomitant medication use (OR 6.93, 95% CI: 3.51–13.7; p < 0.001). Epigastric tenderness, systemic diseases, and a family history of PUD or H. pylori infection were associated with PUD only in univariable analysis.ConclusionOlder age, concomitant medications, and H. pylori infection are risk factors for pediatric PUD. In a tertiary center, PUD remains a frequent and clinically relevant diagnosis among children undergoing UGE.

INTRODUCTION

Peptic ulcer disease (PUD) defined as gastric and duodenal ulcers and erosions is a condition that affects both adults and children, with a reported prevalence between 6.8% and 12.1% in the latter 1 , 2 However, there are differences in PUD's clinical presentation and etiology between age groups. 3 Diagnosing PUD in children requires upper gastrointestinal endoscopy (UGE) and invasive tests for Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) detection, such as gastric biopsy for histology, culture, or polymerase chain reaction. 4 Indications for endoscopy in children have been described in a clinical guideline a few years ago. 5

The main cause of PUD is H. pylori infection, which is more strongly associated with duodenal ulcers than gastric ulcers in children. 2 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Other causes of PUD include nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), stress, Crohn's disease, and external agents such as tobacco, alcohol, and caustic substances. 9 However, a large proportion of PUD remains unexplained: no known risk factors are identified in a high proportion of children with PUD. 7 , 9 In children, the main indications for UGE include persistent upper gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms such as epigastric pain, dysphagia, unexplained vomiting, GI bleeding (overt or occult), iron‐deficiency anemia of unknown origin, and suspicion of chronic GI conditions such as celiac disease or eosinophilic esophagitis. The study aimed to investigate further the epidemiology and etiology of pediatric PUD and its associated risk factors in patients undergoing UGE.

METHODS

Ethics statement

The study protocol was approved by the HUDERF Ethics Committee (CEH n°82/22) on December 6, 2022.

Consent form

No informed consent was sought as this is an observational study for which the competent institutional ethics committee does not request written consent. The data were anonymized.

Study design

Retrospective study, based on children's medical records admitted to the pediatric gastroenterology department of a tertiary pediatric hospital in Brussels, Belgium. Patients aged 1 month to 17 years old who underwent an UGE between January 2016 and December 2022 were included in the study, and a database was constructed with the child's information, date of endoscopy and diagnosis. Infants younger than 1 month or patients aged 18 years or older were excluded, as were control endoscopies for the same patient or when the endoscopy protocols were incomplete.

Additional data comprising demographic, endoscopic, and histological data (date of birth, endoscopy date, sex, endoscopy indication, whether gastric biopsies were performed, endoscopic and histological diagnoses, and H. pylori culture) were collected from the patient's electronic medical record, anonymized, and stored using the secure REDCap platform. 12 Then, children presenting with PUD were identified while the others were considered as controls. For the analysis, the case group was composed of children with PUD, and the control group with no gastric or duodenal ulcer or erosion was visualized and selected while applying a 1:2 “case‐control” ratio including consecutive endoscopies.

Patients were then categorized according to age groups: < 1 year, 1–5 years, 6–10 years, 11–14 years, and > 15 years. Whenever not contraindicated, forceps biopsies (two antral and two corpus samples) are obtained as standard of care for histological examination and microbiological culture. Hematoxylin‐Eosin stain was used to assess the inflammation and immunohistochemistry to detect H. pylori. The updated Sydney classification was used to determine the degree of gastritis. 13

The current European Society for Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology, and Nutrition (ESPGHAN) recommendations were applied to determine the status of H. pylori infection. 10 Patients were considered “Positive” if the culture was positive and “Negative” if the culture and histology were negative. “Unknown” status refers to patients who didn't have a gastric biopsy and/or those who have taken protein pump inhibitors (PPIs) during the last 2 weeks before the endoscopy and/or who have had a questionable result (positive histology and negative or unavailable culture). These patients were excluded from the study of the incidence of H. pylori and the pathogenesis of PUD.

PUD was defined as the presence of ulceration and/or erosion of the gastric and/or duodenal mucosa. The differentiation between ulcers and erosions was based on the macroscopic appearance, depth, and size of the lesion, in which ulceration was defined as a deep lesion of the mucosa (> 3 mm) with an inflamed edge, while an erosion as a small superficial lesion of the mucosa ( < 3 mm) with a white or yellowish color. 1 , 9

Data analysis

After data transfer from the REDCap platform, statistical analysis was performed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences version 29 (SPSS, IBM) software. For descriptive statistics, median values ± standard deviation were used for quantitative variables, while frequencies and percentages were used for nominal/binary variables. Our population follows a non‐Gaussian distribution.

For inferential statistics, we used two types of analysis:

Univariable analysis was used to determine the risk factors of PUD using appropriate statistical tests. For quantitative and ordinal variables, the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney test was used. For nominal/binary qualitative variables, the Chi‐square test was used. Nonparametric tests were used to compare qualitative and quantitative variables. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To assess the risk of PUD, we calculated the odds ratio (OR) using a confidence interval (CI) of 95%.

Multivariable analysis was conducted using logistic regression to determine the independent risk factors. The following parameters were included in the model after testing for collinearity and variability and a threshold of p < 0.25: age group ≥ 11 years, H. pylori infection, short duration of pain (< 1 month), treatment (NSAIDs, corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, chemotherapy, antiepileptics), physical exam (pathological vs. normal), family history of PUD or H. pylori, presence of GI bleeding on admission, or concomitant chronic medical disease. OR and adjusted p‐values were described.

RESULTS

Population eligible for analysis

During the study period, 3496 upper GE were performed on 2420 children. The inclusion criteria were met for 2355 children (protocol missing in 57, 8 children outside age range—see flowchart as Figure S1). Gastric and/or duodenal ulcers/erosions were found in 335/2355 (14.2%—detailed endoscopic lesions and histology in Figure S2). Gastric erosions were found in 223 (9.5%), gastric ulcers in 45 (1.9%), duodenal erosions in 39 (1.7%) duodenal ulcers in 76 (3.3%) patients (more than one lesion possible).

Study group

Controls without PUD were selected as described above, resulting in 1005 children enrolled in the analysis, comprising 335 cases and 670 controls. The median age was 9.9 years with an interquartile range of 6.1–13.1 years, with a predominance of patients aged 6–10 years (34.2%) (Table S3). The main indications for the endoscopy were dyspepsia accompanied with alarm features in 389 patients (38.7%) and chronic abdominal pain in 156 patients (15.5%) (Figure S4). Dyspepsia was defined in this study as a symptom complex including upper abdominal pain or discomfort, bloating, early satiety, or nausea.

Univariable analysis of principal risk factors for PUD

Gender was not a significant risk factor for the diagnosis of PUD (p = 0.503), in contrast to age. Older children (median age of 11.10 years) had a higher incidence of PUD than younger controls (median age of 9.2 years), p < 0.001.

H. pylori status was determined in 262 cases and 494 controls. The infection was detected more frequently in cases (35.5%) than in controls (17.2%). In the overall analysis, including all age groups, H. pylori infection was a significant risk factor for PUD (OR: 2.65, 95% CI: 1.88–3.47, p < 0.0001). However, H. pylori infection was not identified as a risk factor in young subjects since infected patients were older (Figure S5).

The main symptoms in patients with PUD were epigastric pain (43.6%), abdominal pain (31%), and vomiting (23.6%). However, the difference was significant between cases and controls with GI bleeding, fatigue, irritability, and vomiting. On the physical exam, abdominal or epigastric tenderness and pallor were more pronounced in cases than controls (p < 0.01). A personal history of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) or systemic disease was significantly associated with PUD (p < 0.001 and p = 0.025, respectively). Also, a family history of PUD or H. pylori were significant risk factors (Table 1).

As for the impact of medical treatment, PPIs were more frequently consumed by cases than controls. Otherwise, NSAIDs, corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, antiepileptics, and chemotherapy were significant for development of PUD, with immunosuppressants being the most important. (Table S6).

When comparing the identified risk factors according to different lesion types, NSAIDs were a significant risk factor for all the types of lesions. In contrast, H. pylori infection was only identified as a significant risk factor in gastric erosions and DUs (p < 0.001). The study also shows a more pronounced association between duodenal ulcer and H. pylori infection (45%) than in GUs (33%). Furthermore, the age difference between cases and controls was significant in patients having gastric or duodenal erosions, whereas the results were insignificant in gastric and duodenal ulcers (Table 2).

Multivariable analysis of risk factors

Using binary logistic regression, the association of several independent variables was studied. Results showed that age older than 11 years, medical treatment irrespective of the mechanism of action, and the presence of H. pylori infection were significant risk factors (p < 0.005) for PUD presence. In terms of symptomatology and clinical presentation, only GI bleeding was significantly associated independently with PUD. Neither a normal physical exam nor the absence of concomitant chronic illnesses was significant for the absence of PUD (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

The study demonstrated that age, H. pylori infection, and medication consumption were risk factors for PUD. On the other hand, sex was not identified as a risk factor, and a normal physical exam or medical history can't exclude PUD. The presence of epigastric/abdominal tenderness, GI bleeding, or a personal history of systemic illnesses or IBD is associated to PUD. When considering the lesion types, NSAIDs were a significant risk factor for all the types, but H. pylori infection was only significant for duodenal ulcers and gastric erosions.

The incidence of PUD (16.4%), as shown in our study, has increased compared to the study conducted by Burgard et al. in our center between 1990 and 2013 (12.15%). 1 Since age was established as a significant risk factor for PUD by the latter study, and confirmed in ours, this could partly explain the higher incidence. PUD was more common in children ≥ 11 years of age, and this may be partially explained by prolonged H. pylori infection. Other risk factors, such as smoking, alcohol consumption, and ulcerogenic medications, may further promote the development of the disease in adolescents. 6 , 14 , 15

In our population, the incidence of H. pylori (22.1%) and the proportion of ulcers related to H. pylori (35.5%) remained stable compared to the study by Burgard. et al as shown in Table 4. 1 These results are in accordance with the overall incidence of H. pylori in European children recently published by Borka et al. in 2022. 19 This incidence has decreased from previous studies (32.7% in Belgium in 2017) published by Hooi et al. 17 The results obtained could suggest that, despite that H. pylori is the primary case of PUD in children, other important risk factors exist and need to be further explored.

The results of the multivariable analysis have shown that age, H. pylori infection, GI bleeding, and medications were significant risk factors for development of PUD. Unlike some older studies conducted between 1990 and 2012, sex was no longer identified as a risk factor for development of PUD in our study. 1 , 2 , 6 , 9 , 19 While the exact role of sex in development of PUD is not clearly understood, the lifestyle modifications and environmental factors which are gradually becoming indifferent regardless of gender could help better interpret this result.

As for the analysis of the different forms of lesions, H. pylori was an independent risk factor for DUs, gastric erosions, and duodenal erosions but not for GUs. These findings are consistent with Burgard's study. 1 Furthermore, despite being globally associated with a higher risk for development of PUD, older age was a risk factor for gastroduodenal erosions and not gastroduodenal ulcers, in contrast to what was already published, where older age was identified as a risk factor in all types of lesions. 1

While consumption of PPIs can be responsible for false negatives regarding the endoscopic diagnosis of PUD, its effect is mostly noticed in the diagnosis of H. pylori. Nevertheless, depending on the clinical presentation and indication, some patients were already being treated at the time of endoscopy. These patients were excluded from the analysis of H. pylori infection but not from the univariable study of other risk factors on PUD. Excluding them completely from the study would have led to selection bias, as information on PPI consumption was not available at the case/control selection stage. Nonetheless, receiving PPIs was not a protective factor for PUD (12.8% PPI consumption in cases vs. 7.3% in controls). Unfortunately, the duration of PPIs intake before the endoscopy was not available to help better interpret these results.

As for other medical treatment consumption, antiepileptics and chemotherapy showed an important impact on development of PUD, with immunosuppressants being the most important in our population, even more than NSAIDs, which is considered one of the most established risk factors. 9

According to our study, the most common symptoms suggesting endoscopy are dyspepsia (43.6%), abdominal pain (31%), vomiting (23.6%), and GI bleeding (19.1%). Symptoms such as nocturnal or postprandial pain and altered general condition have been suggested to differentiate between children with and without ulcers. 9 , 11 , 20 However, our study found that epigastric/abdominal pain and failure to thrive are not specific for detecting PUD. Nonetheless, a significant association between other symptoms such as fatigue, vomiting, GI bleeding, and irritability was demonstrated. In terms of physical exam, epigastric/abdominal tenderness and pallor are significantly associated with ulcers/erosions in univariable analysis. Their association with other risk factors may warrant endoscopy.

The presence of a family history of PUD is important to identify a hereditary factor. Our study identified 16 cases (5%) with a familial history of PUD, significant in the univariable analysis but not in the multivariable analysis. Regarding the personal history, systemic illnesses and IBD are associated with a risk of PUD, as shown previously in other studies. 9 In a prospective multicenter study conducted by Bontems et al., they also demonstrated the significant impact of neurological, chronic renal, and juvenile arthritis. 7

The limitations of our work: Since our study is retrospective, some data are missing or incomplete. This may limit the quality and reliability of our results. The presence of confounding factors even after adjustment by multivariable analysis to minimize their impact on the results. There are other risk factors cited in the literature that are not elucidated in our study. Notably, stress, tobacco, alcohol, and lifestyle.

The advantages of our work: A large study group, which reinforces the reliability and representativeness of our study. In depth and critical analysis of the data collected, using appropriate statistical methods and evaluation of the results. In depth literature review, which allowed us to well situate our study in an existing scientific context and reinforce the relevance of our work.

CONCLUSION

PUD is a rare but not negligible condition in pediatrics, in which the prevalence varies according to the geographical and demographic characteristics of the population studied. According to our study, belonging to an age group ≥ 11 years, the use of medications, and the presence of concomitant H. pylori infection are risk factors related to PUD. Since the diagnosis of PUD is difficult due to the variability of symptoms, it is essential to take a thorough history and a complete clinical examination to accurately assess the presence of the disease and justify the performance of UGE.
