Effect of perioperative lidocaine application on inflammatory factors, immune function, and quality of early postoperative recovery in patients undergoing video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Li Zhang, Pihong Wei, Haihong Wang, Yuehua Zhang, Huadong Wang, Yan Xu, Zaiqi Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess the effect of low-dose perioperatively continuous infusion of lidocaine on postoperative inflammation, immune function and quality of recovery in patients undergoing video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS).MethodsPatients with lung cancer aged 18–65 years, undergoing elective VATS were randomized into lidocaine intervention (L) and standard care (C) groups. For patients in Group L, 1 mg/kg lidocaine was intravenously injected within approximately 10 min during the induction of anesthesia, followed by a continuous infusion of lidocaine at a rate of 1.5 mg/kg/h until the patient left the operating room. The postoperative analgesia plan included 2% lidocaine at 5 mg/kg. Group C was given an equal volume of normal saline as a control.The primary outcomes were plasma concentrations of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1(IL-1), and interleukin-6 (IL-6), along with T lymphocyte counts of CD3+, CD4+, CD8+, and the CD4+/CD8+ ratio before anesthesia induction (T1), and 24 h (T2) and 48 h (T3) postoperatively. Secondary outcomes included the visual analog scale (VAS) for pain at rest and during movement, Time to first post-operative rescue analgesia, Cumulative OME(the oral morphine equivalents) at 24 h, along with the frequency and severity of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) within the initial 48-h after surgery.ResultsIn Group C and Group L, compared with preoperative levels, the levels of CD3 + , CD4 + and the ratio of CD4 + /CD8 + were significantly decreased at 24 and 48 h postoperatively, while the levels of TNF-α, IL-1 and IL-6 were significantly increased (P < 0.05). Compared with Group C, in Group L, the levels of CD3 + , CD4 + and the ratio of CD4 + /CD8 + were significantly increased at 24 and 48 h postoperatively, while the levels of TNF-α, IL-1 and IL-6 were significantly decreased (P < 0.05).Additionally, the L group experienced less pain on the movement VAS, the decreased OME dosage and a lower rate of PONV within 48 h postoperatively than the C group (P < 0.05).ConclusionIntravenous infusion of lidocaine during the perioperative period was effective in reducing postoperative inflammatory response and the postoperative suppression of cellular immune function in the body, as well as significantly reducing the level of postoperative pain and the incidence of PONV in patients undergoing VATS.Keywords: Lidocaine, Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery, Inflammatory factors, T lymphocytes, Lung cancer

Introduction

Lung cancer, originating from the bronchial mucosa or pulmonary adenomas, is categorized among the most prevalent and deadly malignancies in clinical settings [1, 2]. Data from the National Cancer Center of China in 2016 indicate that lung cancer tops the list of both occurrence and fatality rates among all cancers in the country. Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) is an essential treatment option for lung cancer [3]. VATS is widely recognized for its safety, feasibility, and efficacy and has gradually replaced traditional open-chest surgery, becoming the mainstream practice in modern thoracic surgery. One of the key features of thoracoscopic surgery is its minimally invasive small incision, which avoids the trauma associated with rib expansion in traditional open chest surgery. However, inflammatory responses and postoperative pain remain two common issues in the anesthetic management of thoracoscopic surgery and significantly affect postoperative recovery and quality of life.

Surgical stress response encompasses multiple aspects, including pain, nausea, vomiting, activation of the sympathetic nervous system, inflammatory responses, and immunosuppression [4]. During thoracoscopy, tissue injury prompts neutrophils and macrophages to migrate to the affected area, where they act as the first responders and initiate a local proinflammatory response. This process results in the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1 (IL-1), and interleukin-6 (IL-6). These pain and inflammatory responses, combined with the use of opioids, may suppress the immune function, which is compromised in patients with lung cancer. Therefore, protecting the immune function in patients undergoing thoracoscopic surgery is crucial for anesthesiologists. Further optimization of VATS anesthesia management is essential to minimize surgical stress, reduce complications, and promote rapid recovery.

Lidocaine is a commonly used local anaesthetic with rapid onset and long-lasting effects [5, 6]. The therapeutic mechanisms of lidocaine may involve multiple aspects, including analgesia, reduction of hyperalgesia, anti-inflammation, improvement in gastrointestinal function, and anti-thrombotic, antibacterial, and antitumor actions [7–9]. When administered intravenously, lidocaine can reduce stress responses during surgery, decrease the number of opioids used, and provide effective postoperative anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects [10, 11]. In patients with cancer, intravenous lidocaine administration can enhance the antitumor immune response and help reduce tumor recurrence after surgery. Recent studies have suggested that lidocaine may inhibit lung cancer progression [12]. International consensus statements indicated [13] that when lidocaine is administered intravenously at a dosage of 1.5 mg/kg/h, it exerts favorable effects. Furthermore, studies have shown that an initial bolus of 1.5–2.0 mg/kg of intravenous lidocaine, succeeded by a maintenance infusion at a rate of 1.5–2.0 mg/kg per hour, with plasma lidocaine concentrations stabilized at 0.58–5.0 μg/mL, are all within a safe range (< 5.0 μg/mL) [14, 15]. Although previous research on lidocaine has predominantly examined its role in enhancing recovery outcomes following thoracoscopic lung cancer surgery [4, 11, 16, 17], its effects on inflammation, immune function, and pain management after VATS, especially the dynamic changes in immune cell subsets, have been less reported. The objective of this study was to assess the impact of lidocaine administration throughout the perioperative period on postoperative inflammatory factors, immune function, and early recovery quality in patients undergoing VATS to provide a more comprehensive perspective for clinical practice.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This investigation was a randomized, double-blind clinical trial, and ethical approval was granted by the Medical Ethics Committee of Tai'an Central Hospital (no. 2022-06-29). The trial protocol was registered with the China Clinical Trial Registry, accessible at http://www.chictr.org.cn, under registration number ChiCTR2300070409. All attempts and practices were conducted within the framework of ethical regulations in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and followed the CONSORT reporting guidelines. Informed consent was obtained from all the participants and their families.

This study recruited patients who underwent thoracoscopic segmentectomy or lobectomy combined with lymph node dissection in our hospital's Thoracic Surgery Department from January to August, 2023. The eligibility criteria included: (1) adults aged 18–65 years undergoing elective thoracoscopic segmentectomy or lobectomy combined with lymph node dissection under general anesthesia, (2) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification of I or II, and (3) voluntary acceptance of postoperative patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA). The exclusion criteria were (1) prior thoracic surgery, (2) opioid allergies, (3) mental disorders, and (4) significant impairment of major organs, such as the heart, lungs, liver, or kidneys.

Randomization and blinding

Patients who met the criteria were assigned to either the lidocaine treatment (L) group or the conventional care (C) group in a 1:1 ratio using a random number table for randomization. The group assignment information was stored in sealed envelopes by a researcher who did not participate in the experiment, and the envelopes were numbered. The sealed envelopes were placed in the operating room with the patients and opened by an anesthesiologist blinded to the group assignments. Based on the allocated patient group, the anesthesiologist prepared either the lidocaine or saline solution according to the study protocol.

Study interventions

Participants in Group L were administered 1 mg/kg lidocaine (…… Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., ……; batch number: H20044187) concurrently with routine induction of anesthesia via intravenous bolus injection, which was administered slowly over approximately 10 min. This was supplemented with continuous lidocaine infusion at a rate of 1.5 mg/kg/h throughout the surgery until the patient left the operating room. The postoperative analgesic formula consisted of 2% lidocaine 5 mg/kg, 6 mg hydromorphone (……Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., ……; batch number: H20120100), 8 mg ondansetron, and 0.9% saline prepared to a total volume of 100 mL. The infusion was delivered at a rate of 2 mL/h, with a 2 mL bolus available and a 15-min lockout period. In contrast, Group C received a saline placebo with the same infusion parameters, and their postoperative analgesic regimen included a solution of 6 mg hydromorphone, 8 mg ondansetron, and 0.9% saline, prepared to 100 mL, with the same infusion settings.

Anesthesia protocol

Patients were instructed to observe an 8-h fast and 4-h preoperative fluid restriction with no preoperative medications. Upon arrival in the operating room, standard monitoring was established, including electrocardiography, blood pressure, and SpO2. A peripheral intravenous line was established and Lactated Ringer’s solution was administered at 6–8 mL/kg/h to maintain circulatory stability. After confirming normal results of the Allen test, radial artery cannulation was performed under local anesthesia to monitor the invasive arterial pressure. Anesthesia induction was performed with sequential administration of remimazolam tosylate (0.05 mg/kg), propofol (2 mg/kg), and alfentanil (30 μg/kg). After the patient became unconscious, an intravenous dose of cisatracurium (0.15–0.2 mg/kg) was administered to aid double-lumen endotracheal intubation. Once muscle relaxation was adequate, a double-lumen bronchial tube was inserted orally (37F for males, 35 F for females) and positioned under fiberoptic bronchoscopic guidance, and mechanical ventilation was initiated. The patients were then placed in the lateral decubitus position. Ultrasound-guided paravertebral nerve block was performed on the surgical side at the T4 and T6 levels, with 10 mL of 0.33% ropivacaine administered at each site. After thoracoscopic entry, the respiratory suspension technique was applied, followed by the PCV-VG mode for single-lung ventilation after 1.5 min. The ventilation parameters were set as follows: VT 5–6 mL/kg, airway peak pressure less than 35 cm H2O, RR maintained at 12–16 breaths/min, PEEP set at 5–6 cm H2O, FiO2 at 50%, and PETCO2 maintained between 35 and 45 mmHg.

Intraoperatively, sevoflurane was continuously inhaled, maintaining an end-tidal concentration of 1–2% until the final 10 min of the procedure. Alfentanil was infused continuously at 0.5–1 μg/kg/min using a syringe pump, ceasing 15 min before surgery ended. Cisatracurium (0.05 mg/kg) was administered intravenously every 30–40 min to maintain sedation. Intraoperative monitoring was stringent for all vital signs. A sudden increase in blood pressure more than 20% above preoperative levels or an increase in heart rate over 20% above preoperative levels indicated potential inadequate analgesia, and in such cases, an additional 10 μg/kg of alfentanil was administered. In cases where the mean arterial pressure rose by over 30% from the baseline, interventions included the administration of phenylephrine (20–50 μg) or urapidil (10 mg). For instances of bradycardia (heart rate < 50 beats/min) or tachycardia (heart rate > 120 beats/min), atropine (0.5 mg) or esmolol (20–40 mg) were administered intravenously to stabilize the heart rate.

At the conclusion of surgery, all general anesthetic drugs were discontinued for all patients, who were then mechanically ventilated with both lungs and moved to the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU). Once the individuals regained spontaneous ventilation, became conscious (eyes opened when called out), responded to commands, resumed the swallowing reflex, and met the respiratory parameters of VT > 8 mL/kg, RR between 16 and 20 breaths/min, SpO2 > 95% after breathing air for 5 min, and PETCO2 less than 45 mmHg, the endotracheal tube was removed after suctioning. Patients were observed in the PACU for half an hour, and upon confirmation of no abnormalities, they were returned to the ward. Within 24 h postoperatively, if patients had a visual analog scale (VAS) pain score of ≥ 4, they were administered 50 mg of intravenous tramadol as a rescue analgesic measure. All anesthetic procedures were performed by the same senior anesthesiologist who was proficient in nerve blocks and general anesthesia and blinded to the group assignments.

Study outcomes

Peripheral venous blood samples of 5 mL were collected by a senior nurse who was blinded to the group allocations at three specific intervals: pre-anesthetic (T1), postoperative 24 h (T2), and 48 h (T3). The study's primary outcomes were the changes in plasma concentrations of TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6, along with shifts in T lymphocyte counts for CD3+, CD4+, CD8+, and the CD4+/CD8+ ratio. Secondary outcomes included pain assessments using the VAS scale at rest and during activity at 6, 12, 24, and 48 h post-surgery, in addition to evaluating the intensity and frequency of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) within the first 48 h postoperatively.

The VAS pain scoring chart is a 10 cm long straight line, with the left anchor indicating no pain (0 points) and the right anchor indicating the worst possible pain (10 points). Patients marked the line according to the level of pain they felt, and the score was determined by the distance of the mark from the left end. The resting VAS pain score reflects the patient's pain sensation at rest, whereas the movement VAS pain score reflects the pain sensation during activities such as coughing. PONV severity was evaluated based on grading criteria established by the World Health Organization (WHO), as follows: grade 1, no nausea or vomiting; grade 2, mild nausea and abdominal discomfort without vomiting; grade 3, significant nausea and vomiting but without actual emesis of gastric contents; and grade 4, severe vomiting with emesis of gastric contents and difficult to control with medication.

ELISA

Peripheral venous blood samples were centrifuged at a speed–2,000–3,000 rpm for 20 min and the supernatant was extracted. The ELISA kits (Shanghai Fusheng Industrial Co., Ltd., China) for TNF-α (batch number: A096840-96T), IL-1 (batch number: A0099472-96T), and IL-6 (batch number: A0099472-96T) were utilized to measure serum concentrations of these cytokines at various time points, following the protocol provided by the manufacturer.

Flow cytometry

The collected peripheral venous blood was mixed and diluted with PBS buffer solution, followed by the addition of sterile lymphocyte separation medium, centrifugation, and washing. Immunofluorescent antibodies (Hangzhou Agilent Bio Co., Ltd., China; batch number: K000218) were added and incubated before washing. Samples were analyzed for T lymphocyte subsets using a flow cytometer (Hangzhou Agilent Bio Co., Ltd., China, approval number: 20143402205), which enabled the quantification of CD3+, CD4+, and CD8+ cells and the CD4+/CD8+ ratio.

Sample size calculation

The effect of intravenous saline or lidocaine on postoperative VAS pain scores in patients undergoing VATS is not well established. This study conducted a pre-trial that included 24 patients, with 12 receiving an intravenous infusion of saline and 12 receiving an intravenous infusion of lidocaine during VATS. The pre-trial results indicated that the incidence of PONV within 48 h was approximately 30% in the saline infusion group and 20% in the lidocaine infusion group. Therefore, this study used 80% test efficacy with a two-tailed α = 0.05 to detect differences between groups, and the required sample size was 40 cases per group, as calculated by G*power 3.1 (Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany). Considering a 10% loss to follow-up rate, approximately 45 cases were ultimately included in each group to ensure the statistical power of the study and reliability of the results.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed and processed using GraphPad Prism 7.00 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). The normality of all continuous variables was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Quantitative data that followed a normal distribution were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). For between-group comparisons, an independent sample t-test was used, whereas repeated one-way ANOVA was used for within-group analyses. Categorical data were analyzed by converting them to proportions and subjected to the χ2 test. Statistical significance was set at a P-value less than 0.05.

Results

Ninety patients were enrolled in the study, none of whom had significant organ dysfunction. After enrollment, these patients were randomly assigned to Group L or C. Postoperatively, Group C experienced two patients lost to follow-up, whereas Group L experienced no such losses. Therefore, 43 patients from Group C and 45 from Group L were included in the final statistical analysis (Fig. 1). The demographic and surgical characteristics of the patients, such as gender, age, body mass index (BMI), ASA status, surgery type (segmentectomy/lobectomy), and operation time, were not significantly different (Table 1).

Subsequently, this study assessed the impact of lidocaine on the levels of postoperative inflammatory factors in patients undergoing VATS, focusing on the levels of TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6. Initial assessments at T1 revealed no significant differences in these inflammatory markers between groups L and C (Fig. 2 and Table 2). However, significant differences in changes in inflammatory factors were observed at T2 and T3. For patients in Group C, compared with T1, the concentration of TNF-α significantly increased at T2 (P < 0.05), with IL-1 and IL-6 levels also showing significant increases at both T2 and T3 (P < 0.05). Group L patients exhibited a similar pattern, with elevated levels of TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6 at T2 compared to T1 (P < 0.05). Nonetheless, at T2 and T3, the concentrations of TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6 in Group L patients were markedly reduced compared to Group C (P < 0.05).

Discussion

Postoperative inflammatory response, immune function recovery, and pain management are critical aspects of postoperative rehabilitation, significantly influencing patient recovery speed, complication rates, and long-term outcomes. Excessive postoperative inflammation can lead to complications such as infections and organ dysfunction, while immune function recovery is essential for preventing infections and promoting wound healing. Effective pain management also enhances patient comfort and facilitates early mobilization and recovery. The present study shows that lidocaine, commonly used as a local anesthetic, has beneficial effects on inflammation, immune function, and pain.

The findings of this study indicate that, following surgery, patients in Group L who received continuous lidocaine infusion exhibited significantly decreased serum levels of TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6 at both 24 and 48 h postoperatively, with statistically significant differences compared to Group C (P < 0.05). This suggests that continuous lidocaine infusion can significantly reduce the levels of postoperative inflammatory factors and shorten the duration of the inflammatory response. Additionally, compared to Group C, patients in Group L showed a significant increase in the number of CD3 + and CD4 + T cells and the CD4 +/CD8 + ratio at 24 and 48 h after surgery. This indicates that perioperative lidocaine infusion may enhance the proliferative capacity of lymphocytes, thereby facilitating the recovery of immune function. Moreover, patients in the lidocaine treatment group had a reduced incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) within 48 h postoperatively and lower scores on the movement visual analog scale (VAS) at 24- and 48-h post-surgery. There was no significant difference in the time to first rescue analgesia between the two groups. We also statistically analyzed the oral morphine equivalents (OME) of the patients in the first 24 h postoperatively, and the results showed a statistically significant difference in the OME dosage in the Group L compared to the Group C. This suggests that the use of lidocaine is associated with improved postoperative pain management. These results are consistent with previous studies [18, 19], confirming that perioperative intravenous lidocaine infusion can effectively alleviate excessive postoperative inflammatory responses, accelerate the recovery of immune function, and significantly reduce postoperative pain intensity and the incidence of PONV in patients undergoing video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS).

Lidocaine exerts anti-inflammatory effects by directly targeting inflammatory cells, inhibiting neutrophil activation, reducing the production and release of inflammatory mediators, and blocking the amplification of the inflammatory cascade [20–22]. This effect may be related to its blocking effect on VGSCs [8]. Surgical stress can trigger the release of large amounts of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1, IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α. These cytokines activate nociceptors, sensitize the peripheral and central nervous systems, lead to hyperalgesia, and contribute to the development of acute and chronic postoperative pain [6, 23]. In this study, the levels of TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6 in both groups of patients were elevated after surgery compared to before anesthesia induction, which is consistent with this phenomenon. Additionally, this study observed that intravenous lidocaine significantly reduced the expression levels of TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6 in patients with lung cancer undergoing VATS surgery at 24 and 48 h postoperatively. This suggests that continuous intravenous lidocaine reduces the levels of inflammatory factors, significantly suppresses excessive postoperative inflammatory responses, and promotes postoperative recovery. The mechanism may involve lidocaine activating protein kinase C, inhibiting leukocyte activity through G protein-coupled pathways, reducing granulocyte function, and alleviating reactive oxygen species damage released by polymorphonuclear leukocytes [24].

T-lymphocyte subsets are crucial in the cellular immune response and play a significant role in anti-tumor immunity and postoperative recovery in cancer patients. In lung cancer immunotherapy, these subsets regulate immunity by releasing specific cytokines [25]. Surgical stress, which elevates stress hormone levels, can suppress the proliferation and induce apoptosis of T-lymphocyte subsets [26]. CD3 + T cells represent all mature peripheral T cells, divided into CD4 + and CD8 + subsets, with the CD4 +/CD8 + ratio being vital for immune balance. In this study, both patient groups showed decreased CD3 +, CD4 +, and CD4 +/CD8 + levels at 24 and 48 h postoperatively compared to pre-anesthesia levels, indicating significant immunosuppression due to surgical stress. Local anesthetics like lidocaine, administered intraoperatively, may enhance immune responses by inhibiting the release of inflammatory factors and stress hormones via the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and sympathetic nervous system (SNS) [27]. This regulation can boost lymphocyte proliferation and maintain Th1/Th2 cytokine balance, enhancing cellular immunity. The present study found that intravenous lidocaine significantly increased CD3 +, CD4 +, and CD4 +/CD8 + levels in patients undergoing VATS at 24 and 48 h postoperatively, suggesting that perioperative lidocaine attenuates the immunosuppressive effects of surgical stress and promotes recovery of immune function.

Lidocaine, composed of an aromatic ring, an intermediate chain, and a terminal amine group, exerts its local anesthetic effect by interacting with neuronal sodium channels, which is vital for reducing postoperative pain and promoting gastrointestinal function recovery [9]. Its mechanisms include: (1) blocking voltage-gated sodium channels (VGSCs) to inhibit ectopic firing and reduce pain stimulation [6]; (2) suppressing polysynaptic reflexes in the spinal dorsal horn and increasing acetylcholine release to enhance inhibitory pain pathways [7]; (3) inhibiting ion channels (sodium, potassium, calcium) and targeting TRP channels to block nociceptive impulses [6, 9]; and (4) exciting gastrointestinal smooth muscle and inhibiting inhibitory reflexes to restore gastrointestinal function and reduce nausea and vomiting. Additionally, lidocaine decreases opioid requirements and their side effects [28]. The significant reduction in movement VAS scores and PONV incidence observed in this study further confirms lidocaine’s role in alleviating postoperative pain.

Our study has the following limitations. Firstly, only patients between the ages of 18–65 years who were in good general health and without major complications were included in this study. This limits the generalizability of the findings to elderly patients or other specific patient groups. Second, our study suggests that the use of lidocaine for postoperative opioid-preserved analgesia in patients undergoing VATS segmental lung resection or lobectomy is objective, safe, and effective. However, the applicability of this approach to a broader patient population requires further validation. In addition, our study did not measure a broader range of metrics to assess the potential additional benefits of lidocaine. Future studies should consider incorporating more comprehensive assessments to fully explore the potential benefits of lidocaine in clinical practice.

Conclusion

In summary, intravenous infusion of lidocaine during the perioperative period was effective in reducing excessive postoperative inflammatory response and accelerating the recovery of immune function, as well as significantly reducing the level of postoperative pain and the incidence of PONV in patients undergoing VATS.
