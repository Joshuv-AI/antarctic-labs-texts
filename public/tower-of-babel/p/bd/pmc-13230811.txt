Multiscale electric fields direct charges to single-atom cobalt sites for photocatalytic H2 production
Authors: Ailing Pan, Bin Liu, Hong Du, Chuanyi Wang, Yuanyuan Che, Hewen Liu, Gang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The photocatalytic efficiency of covalent organic frameworks is often limited by poor charge separation and inaccessible catalytic sites. Here, we overcome these challenges by constructing a biomimetic “alveoli-on-lamina” heterostructure through coordination-directed assembly. This strategy concurrently exfoliates a bulk covalent organic framework into ordered lamellae and creates atomic-scale Co-N/O bridges, anchoring high-density, atomically dispersed cobalt sites. Crucially, this architecture spontaneously generates a multi-scale electric field, integrating a strong interfacial field for charge separation with an intra-structure potential gradient for directional electron transport. This field-driven vectorial charge flow delivers electrons to the catalytic sites, enabling a competitive photocatalytic performance: a hydrogen evolution rate of 534.6 mmol g-1 h-1 under standard irradiation without noble metals, an apparent quantum yield of 90.2% at 500 nm, and retained stability. This work demonstrates that engineering built-in electric fields across multiple scales is a valuable paradigm for advanced solar-to-fuel conversion.

Introduction

The conversion of solar energy into chemical fuels through artificial photosynthesis represents a pivotal pathway toward a sustainable energy future. Among emerging photocatalytic materials, covalent organic frameworks (COFs) have garnered significant attention due to their crystalline porosity, tunable optoelectronic properties, and molecularly precise architectures1–4. The extended π-conjugated networks in COFs facilitate directional charge transport, while their high surface areas and engineered pore environments promote reactant diffusion and active-site accessibility5–7. These attributes position COFs as ideal scaffolds for constructing advanced photocatalytic systems8–10. Despite these inherent advantages, the photocatalytic efficiency of most COFs remains constrained by two interrelated fundamental limitations: inefficient charge separation due to strong exciton binding, and inadequate utilization of catalytic sites buried within bulk structures11–14. The former leads to rapid non-radiative recombination, while the latter restricts mass transport and surface reaction kinetics.

To mitigate these issues, significant efforts have focused on exfoliating bulk COFs into ultrathin two-dimensional (2D) lamellae15,16. This structural refinement reduces charge migration distances and exposes more internal active sites17,18. However, conventional exfoliation techniques (e.g., sonication, mechanical peeling) often suffer from irreproducible yields, structural damage, and poor scalability19,20. More critically, these methods typically create physical mixtures rather than chemically integrated heterostructures, failing to establish atomic-level interfacial connections essential for efficient charge transfer21. Concurrently, the incorporation of atomically dispersed metal centers has proven highly effective for maximizing atom-utilization efficiency and providing well-defined catalytic environments22,23. The key challenge lies in seamlessly integrating these two concepts—achieving atomic dispersion within a precisely exfoliated, intimately coupled architecture—to create synergistic charge-transfer highways from the photoabsorber to the catalytic site.

In this work, we address a biomimetic design strategy that addresses these challenges through atomic-scale interfacial synergy and multi-scale structural ordering. We report a biomimetic “alveoli-on-lamina” heterostructure, constructed by the precise integration of hollow “CS alveoli” (derived from sulfidized ZIF-67) into an in-situ exfoliated COF matrix via interfacial coordination chemistry. This one-pot process concurrently achieves three critical functions: (i) controlled exfoliation of the bulk COF into ordered, ultrathin lamellae, (ii) formation of dense, atomic-scale Co-N/O coordination bridges that serve as directed electron-transfer pathways, and (iii) anchoring of high-density, atomically dispersed cobalt sites (1.81 × 1021 atoms g−1) within the conductive network. The resulting architecture (denoted as CT-x, x = mass of CS in mg) mimics pulmonary alveoli, maximizing interfacial area (akin to alveolar sacs), minimizing mass-transfer barriers (resembling alveolar membranes), and enabling confined nanoreactor effects. Crucially, we demonstrate that this design establishes a multi-scale electric field—comprising a strong interfacial field and an ordered intra-structure potential gradient—which ensures vectorial charge separation and rapid transport. This multi-scale integration yields exceptional photocatalytic performance, with the optimized CT-35 catalyst achieving a hydrogen evolution rate of 534.6 mmol g−1 h−1 under AM 1.5 G irradiation, an apparent quantum yield of 90.2% at 500 nm, and outstanding stability. This work demonstrates a rational design paradigm that integrates atomic-level site engineering with biomimetic hierarchical structuring and multi-scale charge management, offering a generalized platform for high-efficiency solar-to-chemical energy conversion.

Results

The fabrication of the heterostructure follows a two-step, coordination-directed assembly process. Figure 1 illustrates the construction of the dibenzothiophene-S,S-dioxide-based COF and the subsequent formation of the integrated CT-x architecture. First, hollow “CS alveoli” were synthesized through the solvothermal sulfidation of ZIF-67 precursors, yielding a porous sulfide framework with abundant under-coordinated cobalt sites. The key synthetic innovation lies in the functionalization of these “CS alveoli” with amine groups via in-situ Co-N coordination using the TDS (3, 7 diaminodibenzo [b,d] thiophene-5, 5-dioxide) linkers. These tethered amine groups then undergo interfacial Schiff-base condensation with the aldehyde functionalities of the TP (2, 4, 6- triformylphloroglucinol) monomers in a subsequent integrated step. This dual interfacial chemistry accomplishes two critical outcomes in one pot: (i) Co-N coordination anchors the “CS alveoli” at the atomic scale; (ii) concurrent Schiff-base reactions between tethered amines and aldehyde functionalities drive the dynamic exfoliation of the COF into ultrathin lamellae. Critically, this one-pot process simultaneously establishes dense, covalent Co-N/O coordination bridges at the interface. The resulting CT-x architecture is a purpose-built biomimetic system that translates the structural-functional principles of pulmonary alveoli into an efficient photocatalytic platform. This design is achieved through four integrated mechanisms: (i) Maximized interfacial area. Analogous to alveolar sacs, the “CS alveoli” form a high-density array of atomically dispersed Co sites (1.81 × 1021 atoms per gram; page 6 in the SI), ensuring maximum accessibility to catalytic centers. (ii) Ultrathin mass-transfer barriers. The structure combines ultrathin COF lamellae with thin-walled alveoli (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Fig. 2), mimicking the alveolar-capillary membrane to minimize diffusion resistance for H2O and H2. (iii) Efficient electron-transfer network. Continuous Co-N/O bonds create atomic-scale “highways,” similar to capillary networks, enabling rapid electron delivery to catalytic Co sites. (iv) Confined photothermal nanoreactor. Each hollow alveolus acts as a confined cavity where localized photothermal heating reduces the activation barrier for water dissociation, analogous to optimized concentration gradients in alveolar spaces. The morphological evolution from precursors to the final biomimetic architecture was characterized by electron microscopy. The ZIF-67 precursor exhibits a uniform rhombic dodecahedral morphology (Fig. 2a). After sulfidation, the resulting “CS alveoli” largely retain this framework but with a roughened surface and partially collapsed structure, forming hollow cavities that function as efficient light-trapping nanoreactors (Fig. 2b). In contrast, the pure TP-TDS COF self-assembles into an interconnected, three-dimensional coral-like network (Fig. 2c). Strikingly, the integration of “CS alveoli” during COF synthesis induces a dramatic morphological transformation. The CT-35 hybrid displays a well-defined marigold-like superstructure composed of radially arranged petal-like lamellae (Fig. 2d, e). This indicates that the presence of cobalt centers drives the exfoliation and oriented reassembly of the COF into a two-dimensional lamellar architecture. We reason that such a highly ordered and anisotropic superstructure could potentially establish a directional internal electric field, which would be pivotal for long-range vectorial charge transport. The validity of this reasoning is directly confirmed by subsequent spatially resolved potential measurements (discussed later). To confirm that this morphological evolution is governed by Co-N/O coordination rather than a template effect, we performed control experiments. When CoCl2 was used instead of “CS alveoli” under identical conditions, the resulting CoCl2@TP-TDS exhibited an essentially identical marigold-like morphology (Fig. 2f), demonstrating that cobalt coordination is the decisive factor. Conversely, physically milled mixtures of pre-formed components (milled CoCl2/TP-TDS or milled CT-35) yielded only featureless aggregates without any defined superstructure (Supplementary Fig. 3), confirming that in-situ coordination bonding is essential. The structural consequence of this coordination-directed assembly was quantified by atomic force microscopy (AFM). To directly evaluate the exfoliation effect, we first measured the pristine TP-TDS COF, which exhibits a typical thickness of ~71.5 nm (Fig. 2g). Upon coordination-directed integration, the resulting CT-35 forms highly ordered, ultrathin lamellae with a uniform thickness of ~6.4 nm (Fig. 2h)—representing an order-of-magnitude thinning (~11-fold reduction). In contrast, the milled CT-35 control, which lacks defined interfacial bonds, consists of irregular aggregates with a typical height of ~132.7 nm (Supplementary Fig. 6). Together, this order-of-magnitude thinning and the formation of ordered lamellae provide direct, quantitative evidence that the Co-N/O coordination bonds mediate the precise exfoliation and ordered stacking of the COF layers. The dispersion of catalytic sites within this architecture was examined at multiple scales. TEM imaging confirms the uniform distribution of “CS alveoli” throughout the COF laminae (Fig. 2i). To directly visualize the atomic dispersion of Co species, HAADF-STEM images reveal high-density, isolated bright dots corresponding to single cobalt atoms in both the “CS alveoli” and the CT-35 composite (Fig. 2j–l). This visual evidence is further corroborated by large-area STEM-EDS elemental mapping, which demonstrate the homogeneous spatial distribution of all constituent elements (Co, C, N, O, S) throughout the hybrid without aggregation (Fig. 2m, Supplementary Figs. S6, S7). These microscopic and elemental analyses provide compelling visual evidence for the high-density atomic dispersion of Co species (1.81 × 1021 atoms Co/g), which is a key structural feature underlying its enhanced photocatalytic performance.

The crystal structures and phase purity of the materials were analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD). The ZIF-67 precursor shows sharp peaks matching the simulated pattern, confirming high crystallinity (Supplementary Fig. 9a)24. After sulfidation, the resulting “CS alveoli” exhibit characteristic peaks at 31.36° and 50.22°, corresponding to the (311) and (511) planes of Co3S4 (Supplementary Fig. 9b)25, with its stoichiometry further verified by ICP-OES (Supplementary Table. 3). The structural integrity of the COF component upon hybridization is clearly retained. Both pristine TP-TDS and CT-35 show the characteristic (100) peak at ~3.47° and a broad peak at ~26° attributed to π-π stacking (Supplementary Fig. 9c), confirming the preservation of the TP-TDS COF framework26. Notably, in CT-35 the (100) peak intensity is reduced, indicating a preferential orientation of the exfoliated lamellae parallel to the substrate27. Concurrently, the enhanced intensity of the ~26° peak suggests improved layer-to-layer stacking order, which we attribute to the aligning effect of the interfacial Co-N/O coordination bonds. This structural ordering is conducive to extended π-conjugation and efficient inter-layer charge transport28. The absence of distinct diffraction signals from “CS alveoli” in CT-35 strongly indicates their atomic-scale dispersion within the COF matrix, consistent with the electron microscopy observations. Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy provides complementary evidence at the molecular level (Supplementary Fig. 10). Both TP-TDS and CT-35 show signature bands for the C=N stretch (~1350 cm−1), O=S=O symmetric stretch (~1301 cm−1), and C=O stretch (~1627–1658 cm−1), confirming the integrity of the ketenimine-linked framework29,30. Crucially, a new vibrational band emerges at ~460 cm−1 in CT-35, which is characteristic of Co-N stretching modes31. This spectroscopic evidence directly confirms the formation of interfacial coordination bonds. These bonds constitute the key chemical interaction responsible for directing the exfoliation and structural ordering observed by XRD.

The formation and nature of these interfacial Co-N/O bonds were investigated at complementary levels: X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) probed the surface chemical states and charge redistribution, while synchrotron-based X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) provided quantitative insights into the local coordination geometry and atomic dispersion. In the pristine “CS alveoli”, the Co 2p spectrum displays characteristic peaks indicative of mixed oxidation states32 (Fig. 3a). In striking contrast, the Co 2p spectrum of CT-35 exhibits two distinct features at 782.1 eV and 784.5 eV, which are assigned to Co-O and Co-N coordination environments, respectively33,34. Correspondingly, the N 1s spectrum of CT-35 shows a new component consistent with Co-coordinated nitrogen, which is absent in both pristine components (Fig. 3b). These shifts provide direct evidence for charge redistribution and the formation of Co-N/O bonds at the interface. The critical dependence of these bonds on the synthetic pathway was confirmed by control experiments. XPS analysis of milled CT-35 shows no characteristic Co-N/O signatures (Supplementary Fig. 13), confirming that simple physical proximity cannot induce the coordination chemistry observed in CT-35. To further decouple the interface formation process, we examined an intermediate: amine-functionalized “CS alveoli” (CS-NH2), prepared by stirring “CS alveoli” with TDS followed by rigorous washing. The Co 2p spectrum of CS-NH2 exhibits a distinct satellite peak at 784.5 eV (Supplementary Fig. 14), characteristic of Co-N coordination and identical to the feature observed in CT-35. This control experiment confirms that TDS acts as a nitrogen donor, directly coordinating with Co sites via covalent Co-N bonds during the initial functionalization step, prior to COF formation35,36. Together, the XPS analyses establish a clear sequence for the interfacial bond formation: (i) Co-N bonds form between “CS alveoli” and TDS linkers, creating the CS-NH2 intermediate; (ii) these pre-anchored amines then participate in the in-situ formation of the COF framework via Schiff-base condensation with TP; (iii) the final coordination environment in CT-35, encompassing both Co-N and Co-O characteristics, is achieved through this one-pot, coordination-directed synthesis.Fig. 3Local coordination and electronic structure analysis.a High-resolution X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra of the Co 2p region and b N 1s region for CS alveoli, CT-35, and TP-TDS covalent organic framework (COF). To quantify the precise local structure resulting from this synthesis—specifically, the bond lengths and coordination numbers of the proposed Co-N/O bridges—we performed synchrotron-based XAFS spectroscopy. The XANES spectrum shows the Co K-edge of CT-35 positioned between CoO (Co2+) and Co foil, indicating an intermediate valence state resulting from interfacial charge redistribution (Fig. 3c). It shows no detectable Co-Co coordination path (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Table. 4). This absence provides definitive evidence that Co species exist in an atomically dispersed state throughout the entire sample, with no Co-based clusters or nanoparticles present within the detection limit of the technique. This bulk-sensitive finding is fully consistent with the local STEM observations and confirms the global atomic dispersion of Co. With the atomic dispersion established, we then performed quantitative fitting to resolve the local coordination environment. The fitting reveals that the Co center in CT-35 maintains a primary coordination shell of S atoms (Co-S coordination number, CN = 3.9 ± 0.4; bond distance, R = 2.25 ± 0.02 Å), analogous to pristine “CS alveoli”. Concurrently, a distinct secondary coordination shell is identified, corresponding to the interfacial Co-N/O bonds (CN = 0.7 ± 0.4; R = 1.95 ± 0.06 Å). This non-integer CN reflects the heterogeneous yet definitive presence of these interfacial linkages. Wavelet-transform analysis further validates the atomic dispersion of Co and the specific coordination geometry37 (Fig. 3e, f). Thus, the XAFS analysis both verifies atomic dispersion through bulk-sensitive spectroscopy and quantitatively establishes the newly formed Co-N/O bonds (R ≈ 1.95 Å) as the atomic-scale bridges connecting the exfoliated COF lamellae to the catalytic “CS alveoli”38–42.

To evaluate the functional impact of the atomic-scale connectivity, we performed photoelectrochemical measurements. We first optimized the loading of “CS alveoli” and found that CT-35 delivers the highest photocurrent density—8-fold and 10-fold higher than that of the pristine TP-TDS COF and pure “CS alveoli,” respectively (Fig. 4a). To unequivocally attribute this enhancement to the Co-N/O coordination network, we conducted a controlled experiment by comparing CT-35 with a structurally disrupted counterpart (milled CT-35), in which the defined interfacial bonds are absent. This comparison reveals a decisive contrast: CT-35 exhibits a substantially lower charge-transfer resistance (EIS) and a significantly higher, stable photocurrent, whereas the milled sample shows a severely suppressed response (Supplementary Figs. 19, 20). This stark difference directly confirms that the atomic-scale coordination bonds are indispensable for establishing efficient charge-transfer pathways. Building upon this established charge-transfer superiority, the CT-35 catalyst delivers exceptional photocatalytic hydrogen evolution performance without precious-metal co-catalysts (Fig. 4b). Under AM 1.5 G irradiation, pristine “CS alveoli” and TP-TDS show negligible activity (0.12 and 0.26 mmol g−1 h−1, respectively). In stark contrast, the optimized CT-35 achieves a competitive rate of 534.6 mmol g−1 h−1, representing enhancements of ~4555-fold and ~2056-fold over the individual components, as visually demonstrated in Supplementary Movies 1 and 2. This performance is accompanied by a record apparent quantum yield of 90.2% at 500 nm (Fig. 4c). Under visible-light irradiation (λ ≥ 420 nm), CT-35 retains over 51% of its full-spectrum rate (Supplementary Fig. 22), highlighting efficient visible-light utilization. The activity exhibits a clear optimum with “CS alveoli” loading (CT-35 > CT-45, Fig. 4b). The performance decline at higher loadings is attributed to two factors: (i) light-shielding by excessive “CS alveoli”, which attenuates photon absorption by the COF, and (ii) the potential formation of Co-based clusters that act as charge-recombination centers43,44. CT-35 exhibits competitive operational stability, maintaining high activity over 10 consecutive cycles (Fig. 4d). Post-reaction characterization confirms its robust integrity: ICP-OES detects negligible leaching of Co or S (Supplementary Table. 6); SEM and XRD show preserved morphology and crystallinity (Supplementary Figs. 23, 24); and XPS/EXAFS analyses confirm that the atomic-scale coordination environment (both Co-S and Co-N/O bonds) remains intact after prolonged operation (Supplementary Fig. 25, Supplementary Table 7). Moreover, CT-35 achieves the a high reported photocatalytic hydrogen evolution (HER) rate among state-of-the-art COF-based photocatalysts (Supplementary Table 8). The competitive photocatalytic performance of CT-35 originates from its well-defined “alveoli-on-lamina” architecture. This architecture is built upon a foundation of high-density, atomically dispersed Co sites within the “CS alveoli,” which provide the essential catalytic centers. The full potential of these sites is unlocked through two integrated design strategies: (i) connecting them to exfoliated COF lamellae via covalent Co-N/O coordination bridges for directed charge transfer, and (ii) employing the electron-withdrawing TDS linker to optimize interfacial charge separation. To rigorously validate that both strategies are essential—and not merely correlative—for achieving the observed performance, we conducted control experiments that systematically decouple their contributions (Fig. 4f). First, the necessity of covalent Co-N/O bridges over physical mixing was tested using a mechanically mixed sample (milled CT-35). Its drastically lower HER rate (2.26 mmol g−1 h−1) and photocurrent confirm that intimate electronic coupling via chemical bonds is indispensable. Second, the critical role of the specific coordination pathway was examined using a sample synthesized without the amine-functionalization step (traditional CT-35). Its significantly reduced activity (~29-fold lower HER rate) demonstrates that the in-situ ormation of Co-N bonds during the initial functionalization is a prerequisite for creating an efficient interface. Finally, the indispensable electronic function of the TDS linker was isolated by synthesizing a control COF where TDS was replaced by 2,7-diaminofluorene (TD, Supplementary Fig. 27), yielding CS@TP-TD. Despite structural similarity, CS@TP-TD exhibits a HER rate over 10 times lower than CT-35 and a higher charge-transfer resistance (Supplementary Fig. 28). This result provides direct experimental proof that the electron-withdrawing sulfone group in TDS is crucial for facilitating hole extraction and optimizing the interfacial energy alignment, thereby enabling efficient electron injection into the catalytic sites. In conclusion, this triad of control experiments establishes a clear causal chain. The record-breaking performance of CT-35 is not accidental but is directly and exclusively enabled by the synergistic integration of (i) covalent Co-N/O interfacial bridges, (ii) their specific in-situ formation pathway, and (iii) the tailored electronic function of the TDS linker within the biomimetic “alveoli-on-lamina” architecture.

The charge carrier dynamics were investigated using time-resolved photoluminescence (TR-PL). CT-35 exhibits an average carrier lifetime (τ) of 4.43 ns, which is 22.2-fold and 9.0-fold longer than those of the isolated “CS alveoli” (0.20 ns) and the pristine TP-TDS COF (0.49 ns), respectively (Fig. 5a). This pronounced extension indicates significantly suppressed charge recombination, primarily due to the efficient interfacial charge extraction enabled by the Co-N/O coordination bridges. The role of the exfoliated lamellar architecture in promoting the initial separation of photogenerated charges was quantified by temperature-dependent PL spectroscopy. The extracted exciton binding energy (Eb) decreases substantially from 72.26 meV for pristine TP-TDS to 34.9 meV for CT-35 (Fig. 5b, c). This reduction confirms that exfoliation and the extended π-conjugation within the hybrid framework effectively weaken Coulombic interactions, facilitating the dissociation of excitons into free carriers45. Direct evidence for the consequent accumulation of these separated charges at reactive sites comes from electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy. Under illumination, CT-35 generates the most intense signal for superoxide radicals (•O2−), which are produced by the reaction of surface-accumulated electrons with adsorbed oxygen (Fig. 5d). This result demonstrates a higher yield of long-lived, mobile electrons that can migrate to the surface and participate in reduction reactions, consistent with the prolonged carrier lifetime and reduced Eb. In summary, this triad of spectroscopic techniques—TR-PL, temperature-dependent PL, and EPR—provides a coherent, multistep picture of optimized charge management. The interfacial Co-N/O bridges and the ordered lamellar superstructure work in concert to first facilitate charge separation (reduced Eb), then suppress their recombination (extended τ), and ultimately promote the accumulation of free electrons at catalytic surfaces for reaction (enhanced EPR signal). Complementary to the optimized charge dynamics, the hierarchical structure of CT-35 enhances mass transport and utilizes the full solar spectrum, contributing synergistically to its overall efficiency. Nitrogen physisorption analysis reveals how the integration of “CS alveoli” modifies the pore architecture (Fig. 5e, f). While the BET surface area of CT-35 (321.70 m2 g−1) is reduced compared to pristine TP-TDS (986.51 m2 g−1)—due to partial pore occupation — the pore size distribution shifts significantly to a larger average diameter (4.9 nm for CT-35 vs. 3.5 nm for TP-TDS). This expansion is attributed to a pillaring effect, wherein the coordinated “CS alveoli” spacers increase the interlamellar distance of the COF. Direct HRTEM measurement confirms an interlayer spacing of 4.94 ± 0.2 nm (Supplementary Fig. 29), quantitatively aligning with the BET data and visually confirming the structural expansion, which is beneficial for reactant and product mass transport. In addition to efficient photon capture, CT-35 exhibits a pronounced photothermal conversion capability. Under illumination in air, its surface temperature rapidly rises to 144.1 °C, substantially higher than that of TP-TDS (107.7 °C) under identical conditions (Fig. 5g, h). More critically, during actual photocatalytic HER operation in aqueous solution, the bulk reaction temperature reaches a steady-state 74.7 °C (Fig. 5i), confirming a substantial thermal effect within the operating environment. To rigorously deconvolute the role of this heating from the photochemical process, we performed decisive control experiments. Operating the system in darkness while maintaining the bulk solution at ~75 °C or 144 °C yielded no detectable hydrogen evolution (Supplementary Fig. S31). This unequivocally establishes that the reaction remains strictly photo-driven; thermal energy alone cannot initiate the HER. Therefore, we refine the mechanistic interpretation: the photothermal effect does not serve as an independent driving force but functions as a kinetic enhancer within the photo-driven process. The elevated local thermal environment accelerates subsequent surface-reaction steps (e.g., H* adsorption/desorption) via Arrhenius-type kinetics, thereby synergistically boosting the overall HER rate without altering the fundamental photochemical initiation step.

The electronic structure at the heterojunction interface dictates the thermodynamic driving force and direction for charge transfer. Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) determines the work functions (Φ) of “CS alveoli” and TP-TDS to be 2.95 eV and 3.08 eV, respectively (Fig. 6a, b). Accordingly, the Fermi level of “CS alveoli” is higher than that of TP-TDS. Upon intimate contact via Co-N/O coordination, electrons spontaneously transfer from “CS alveoli” to TP-TDS to equilibrate the Fermi levels, establishing a robust built-in electric field at the interface. The direction of this field points from TP-TDS (electron accumulation side) toward “CS alveoli” (electron depletion side)46,47. This ground-state charge redistribution is directly visualized by the calculated charge density difference (Fig. 6c), which shows clear electron accumulation on TP-TDS and depletion on “CS alveoli”, corroborating the UPS-deduced electron transfer direction. The strength of this interfacial field was quantified by Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM). A line-scan across the CT-35 interface reveals a sharp contact potential difference (CPD) of ~215.2 mV over a width of ~475 nm (Fig. 6d, Supplementary Fig. 33). This corresponds to a substantial built-in electric field strength of |E| ≈ 4.53 × 105 V m−1, providing a powerful, pre-existing driving force for charge separation. This built-in electric field governs charge separation across the heterojunction. Beyond the primary interfacial field, the unique marigold-like superstructure itself introduces an additional level of order that actively promotes directional charge transport. KPFM mapping across individual lamellar units reveals a well-defined internal potential gradient (ΔCPD ≈ 14 mV) along the radially arranged petals (Supplementary Fig. 34). This secondary, intra-structure electric field functions as a built-in directional guide, efficiently channeling the initially separated electrons along the ultrathin lamellae directly toward the catalytic sites, thereby substantially minimizing random diffusion and bulk recombination losses. The synergistic integration of this multi-scale electric field architecture—which combines the strong interfacial field (primary driver) with the ordered intra-structure gradient (secondary director)—collectively ensures vectorial charge separation and rapid transport across the entire hierarchical system. The functional role of this field under operational conditions was captured by in-situ characterizations. In-situ KPFM under illumination shows a significant decrease in the interfacial CPD from 215.2 mV (dark) to 149.8 mV (light) (Fig. 6e, Supplementary Fig. 35). This reduction signifies a relative increase in electron density on the “CS alveoli” side, directly visualizing the light-induced, field-driven electron transfer. Most definitively, in-situ XPS under illumination provides atomically resolved chemical-state evidence (Fig. 6f–h). Upon irradiation, the Co 2p binding energy decreases, confirming electron accumulation on the Co sites within “CS alveoli”. Concurrently, the binding energies of N and O species increase, indicating electron depletion from the TP-TDS framework. These complementary shifts offer unambiguous spectroscopic proof that photogenerated electrons are driven from TP-TDS to the “CS alveoli” by the built-in electric field. The integration of static interfacial analysis (UPS, charge density difference, dark KPFM) with dynamic operando probes (in-situ KPFM, in-situ XPS) establishes a coherent and quantitative charge-transfer mechanism. The charge dynamics are governed by a multi-scale electric field architecture: first, a strong, pre-formed interfacial electric field, originating from Fermi-level equilibration, provides the primary driving force for charge separation; second, the ordered intra-structure potential gradient within the marigold-like superstructure acts as a directional guide, channeling the separated electrons efficiently toward the catalytic Co-S sites. This synergistic design ensures that photogenerated electrons are not only effectively separated but also rapidly and directionally transported from the light-harvesting TP-TDS to the catalytic centers in the “CS alveoli”, minimizing bulk recombination losses. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations provide fundamental insights into the electronic structure and reaction energetics of the system (the atomic coordinates of the optimized computational models are provided in Supplementary Data 1). The bare “CS alveoli” exhibits metallic character, while the TP-TDS COF has a computed band gap of 1.51 eV (Fig. 7a, b) – smaller than the experimental value of 2.08 eV – which is typical for the PBE functional due to its known tendency to underestimate band gaps.48 The projected density of states (PDOS) reveals that the electronic states near the Fermi level are overwhelmingly dominated by Co 3d and S 3p orbitals (Fig. 7c). This pinpoints the Co-S unit as the electronic origin of the catalytic activity. This functional role is structurally anchored by the preserved Co-S coordination motif, as independently confirmed by EXAFS. The reaction energetics provide independent and quantitative validation. The calculated hydrogen adsorption free energy (ΔGH*) for the pristine TP-TDS COF is 0.76 eV, indicating excessively strong intermediate adsorbed hydrogen (H*) binding. In striking contrast, the “CS alveoli” unit exhibits a near-optimal ΔGH* of 0.53 eV (Fig. 7d), which thermodynamically favors efficient H2 release. These results suggest the Co-S moiety within the “CS alveoli” as the primary and superior active site for the hydrogen evolution reaction. Integrating all spectroscopic, electrochemical, and theoretical evidence, we propose a refined photocatalytic mechanism governed by atomically defined interfacial synergy within a multi-scale electric field architecture (Fig. 7e). The process is initiated by photoexcitation of the TP-TDS semiconductor. The favorable interfacial energy alignment (UPS) provides the thermodynamic driving force, while the strong, pre-formed interfacial electric field (primary driver, KPFM) directs the initial charge separation. The photogenerated electrons are then rapidly channeled through a dual pathway: (i) along the ordered intra-structure potential gradient (secondary director, KPFM) within the marigold-like lamellae, and (ii) across atomic-scale Co-N/O coordination bridges—whose formation is directly evidenced by XPS and EXAFS—toward the “CS alveoli”. DFT calculations are consistent with the interpretation that these bridges function not as new active sites but as essential electronic conduits and modulators, delivering and tuning the electron density at the Co-S catalytic center to lower the kinetic barrier for proton reduction (optimal ΔGH*). Collectively, this mechanism elucidates a cascaded synergy across scales: the Co-N/O bridges ensure directional charge delivery at the atomic level, while the Co-S sites provide optimized catalytic function at the molecular level, all housed within a biomimetic superstructure that maximizes light-harvesting and mass transport. Therefore, the exceptional performance of the “alveoli-on-lamina” architecture demonstrates a powerful design paradigm: the conscious integration of high-density atomic dispersion, atomic-scale charge-transfer highways, and biomimetic hierarchical structuring through precise interfacial engineering is a decisive strategy for constructing next-generation photocatalytic systems.

Discussion

In this work, we show a rational design paradigm that integrates high-density atomic cobalt sites with biomimetic hierarchical structuring. Our coordination-directed assembly simultaneously creates covalent Co-N/O bridges and a radially ordered “alveoli-on-lamina” superstructure. This unique architecture intrinsically establishes multi-scale electric fields, which drives efficient vectorial charge separation and transport to the atomically dispersed catalytic sites. The competitive photocatalytic performance—a high hydrogen evolution rate among COF-based systems—stems from this synergistic integration, where the electric fields ensure directed charge flow within the biomimetic architecture that also facilitates mass transport and light harvesting. This work establishes multi-scale electric fields engineering as a decisive strategy for designing high-efficiency, stable photocatalysts, offering a general prospect for the development of next-generation solar-to-chemical energy conversion systems. It is important to note the limitations of the computational models employed. The DFT calculations utilized simplified periodic or cluster models to approximate the complex “alveoli-on-lamina” heterostructure under static, idealized conditions. While providing valuable insights into electronic structure and thermodynamic trends, these models do not fully capture dynamic effects, explicit solvation, or the exact interfacial atomic configurations present under operational photocatalytic conditions. Therefore, the computational results should be interpreted as supportive evidence that aligns with the experimental observations, rather than as definitive proof of the mechanistic details.

Methods

Reagents

Cobalt (II) nitrate hexahydrate (Co(NO3)2·6H2O, 99.9%), 2-methylimidazole (2-MIM, 98%), methanol (CH3OH, ≥99.9%), thiacetamide (TAA, 99%), absolute ethanol (C2H5OH, ≥99.8%), glacial acetic acid (CH3COOH, ≥99.7%), 1, 4 dioxane (C4H8O2, ≥99.5%), mesitylene (C9H12, 98%), tetrahydrofuran (C4H8O, ≥99.9%), acetone (C3H6O, ≥99.5%), ascorbic acid (C6H8O6, ≥99%) and anhydrous sodium sulfate (Na2SO4, ≥99%) were purchased from Tianjin Zhiyuan Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. 2, 4, 6- triformylphloroglucinol (TP, 97%), and 3, 7-diaminodibenzo [b,d] thiophene-5, 5-dioxide (TDS, 95%) were supplied by Aladdin and Shanghai Hao Hong biological medicine Technology Co., Ltd., respectively. All nitrates were lyophilized for 12 h prior to use.

Synthesis of photocatalysts

Synthesis of ZIF-67 precursor

ZIF-67 was synthesized according to a modified procedure. Typically, cobalt(II) nitrate hexahydrate (4 mmol, 0.73 g) was dissolved in 50 mL of methanol (denoted as Solution A), and 2-methylimidazole (16.9 mmol, 1.622 g) was separately dissolved in another 50 mL of methanol (denoted as Solution B). Both solutions were stirred individually for 10 min at room temperature. Subsequently, Solution A was rapidly poured into Solution B under vigorous stirring. The resulting mixture was stirred for an additional 30 min and then allowed to age statically at room temperature for 24 h. The purple precipitate formed was collected by centrifugation, washed thoroughly with methanol (6 × 4 mL), and dried at 60 °C for 16 h in air49.

Preparation of “CS Alveoli” derived from ZIF-67

The cobalt sulfide “CS alveoli” were synthesized via a solvothermal sulfurization of ZIF-67. In a typical procedure, ZIF-67 (0.34 g) and thioacetamide (TAA, 0.38 g) were dispersed in absolute ethanol (60 mL) under vigorous stirring for 30 min to form a homogeneous suspension. This suspension was then charged into a 100 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave, which was sealed and maintained at 120 °C for 6 h. After the reaction, the autoclave was allowed to cool naturally to room temperature. The resulting black product was collected by filtration, sequentially washed with deionized water and absolute ethanol (six times with 4 mL each), and finally dried at 60 °C overnight50.

Synthesis of TP-TDS COF

TP (0.08 mmol, 16.8 mg) and TDS (0.12 mmol, 29.6 mg) were added to a 10 mL Pyrex tube containing a mixed solvent of mesitylene (1.5 mL) and 1,4-dioxane (1.5 mL). Glacial acetic acid (6 M aqueous solution, 0.3 mL) was then added dropwise as a catalyst. After sonication for 10 min to achieve a homogeneous dispersion, the tube was purged with N2 for 15 min and sealed. The reaction mixture was heated at 120 °C in an oil bath for 3 days. After cooling to room temperature, the precipitate was collected by centrifugation and washed sequentially with tetrahydrofuran and acetone (three times each). The product was subsequently soaked in acetone for 24 h with solvent renewal every 5 h. Finally, the resulting red solid was dried at 60 °C for 12 h to obtain the TP-TDS COF.

Synthesis of CS@TP-TDS-x (CT-x) heterojunctions

The CT-x heterojunctions were synthesized following a modified procedure based on the TP-TDS COF synthesis. In a typical process for CT-35, TDS (0.12 mmol, 29.6 mg) and pre-synthesized “CS alveoli” (35 mg) were first dispersed in 1,4-dioxane (1.5 mL) and stirred for 1 h. Then, TP (0.08 mmol, 16.8 mg) and mesitylene (1.5 mL) were added to the mixture. After stirring for an additional 20 min, glacial acetic acid (6 M aqueous solution, 0.3 mL) was introduced, followed by sonication for 10 min. Subsequent steps—N2 purging, sealing, solvothermal reaction at 120 °C for 3 days, cooling, product collection, washing with tetrahydrofuran/acetone, solvent soaking, and drying at 60 °C—were performed analogously to the TP-TDS COF synthesis. Products with different “CS alveoli” loadings (15, 25, 35, or 45 mg) were similarly prepared and are denoted as CT-x, where x represents the mass (in mg) of “CS alveoli” used.

Synthesis of control samples

To elucidate the specific roles of the interfacial coordination bonds and synthesis pathway, the following control samples were prepared:

CS-NH2

The synthesis was halted after stirring TDS with “CS alveoli” in 1,4-dioxane for 1 h. The black intermediate was collected by centrifugation, washed six times with ethanol, and dried overnight at 60 °C.

CoCl2@TP-TDS

This sample was prepared following the CT-35 procedure but with “CS alveoli” replaced by an an equal mass of CoCl2.

Milled CT-35

Pre-synthesized “CS alveoli” (35 mg) and TP-TDS COF were manually ground together in a mortar for 20 min to achieve physical mixing without chemical bonding.

Milled CoCl2/TP-TDS

Pre-synthesized TP-TDS COF and CoCl powder (35 mg) were manually ground together in a mortar for 20 min.

These two milled samples were synthesized exclusively via physical mixing, physical proximity while deliberately avoiding the solvothermal conditions required for coordination chemistry to occur.

Traditional CT-35

“CS alveoli” (35 mg), TP (0.08 mmol, 16.8 mg), and TDS (0.12 mmol, 29.6 mg) were directly mixed in a solvent mixture of mesitylene (1.5 mL) and 1,4-dioxane (1.5 mL). After sonication to dissolve the organic precursors and disperse the “CS alveoli”, glacial acetic acid (6 M aqueous solution, 0.3 mL) was added. The subsequent steps (sonication, N2 purging, sealing, solvothermal reaction, washing, and drying) were identical to those described in the section for the synthesis of TP-TDS COF.

Measurement of photocatalytic hydrogen evolution

Photocatalytic hydrogen evolution reactions were performed in a 100 mL three-neck quartz reactor containing 50 mL of an aqueous ascorbic acid solution (0.1 M, pH = 2.5) as a sacrificial agent. Catalyst (2 mg) was dispersed in the solution via ultrasonication for 20 min. The system was then purged with N2 for 40 min to completely remove air. The reaction was carried out under visible light irradiation (300 W Xe lamp with a 420 nm cutoff filter) and simulated sunlight (AM 1.5 G) with continuous stirring. The intensity was calibrated to 100 mW/cm2 at the sample position using a certified silicon photodiode power meter (CEL-FZ-A, Beijing Zhongjiaojinyuan Technology Co., Ltd.). The evolved hydrogen gas was quantified using an on-line gas chromatograph (GC-7900, TCD detector, N2 carrier gas). (Photo)electrochemical measurements

(Photo)electrochemical analyses, including electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and photocurrent response (i-t) measurements, were conducted on an electrochemical workstation using a standard three-electrode system. A Pt plate and an Ag/AgCl electrode (0.197 V vs. NHE) served as the counter and reference electrodes, respectively. The i-t measurements were performed at a constant applied potential of 0.60 V vs. Ag/AgCl, and the applied potential was iR-corrected automatically by the workstation. All potentials in this study have been converted to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) scale for consistency with the literature. The conversion was performed using the equation: ERHE = EAg/AgCl + 0.059 × pH + EAg/AgCl, where EAg/AgCl = 0.197 V vs. NHE. With the electrolyte pH of 6.8 ± 0.1, the conversion factor is approximately + 0.60 V. The working electrode was prepared by dispersing 5 mg of the photocatalyst in a mixture of 0.25 mL ethanol, 0.25 mL ethylene glycol, and 0.04 mL Nafion solution, followed by sonication for 1 h. The resulting suspension was drop-cast onto conductive glass and dried at 60 °C for 12 h. Measurements were performed in 0.5 M Na2SO4 (pH = 6.8 ± 0.1) electrolyte under simulated sunlight (AM 1.5 G, 100 mW cm−2) where applicable.
