Guanine storage vacuoles sequester guanine crystals and serve as nitrogen reserves in Chlamydomonas reinhardtii
Authors: Ursula Goodenough, Robyn Roth, Jae-Hyeok Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceA new organelle, the guanine storage vacuole (GSV), is described using quick-freeze deep-etch electron microscopy, a technique that preserves its membrane ultrastructure, its endowment of crystalline guanine, and its morphological changes during exogenous nitrogen starvation and repletion in Chlamydomonas reinhardtii. Also reported is an in-depth inventory of the C. reinhardtii genes that are predicted to participate in guanine metabolism, catabolism, and transport, many of which show enhanced expression with nitrogen starvation. We offer a model of the guanine cycle, testable using gene knockdowns and tagged proteins, that summarizes these discoveries.

From 2009 to 2017, Goodenough and Roth undertook an extensive analysis of Chlamydomonas reinhardtii cells using quick-freeze deep-etch electron microscopy (QFDEEM), where we monitored the formation of triacylglyceride-containing lipid bodies—potential sources of biodiesel fuel—under various culture conditions. We soon encountered images of vacuoles containing crystalline material (Fig. 1) and went on to find similar organelles in several other microalgae. A subset of these micrographs was published in a book chapter in 2023 (1). The QFDE images gave no information as to what the crystals were made of.

Guanine crystals, first reported in fish scales in 1861, are refractile and widely used by animals to manipulate light in a variety of organs (reviewed in refs. 2 and 3). Recently, confocal Raman microscopy (4) was applied to eukaryotic microalgae, and punctate crystalline guanine signals were detected in the cytoplasm of a wide range of species, including C. reinhardtii (5–8). The authors suggest that the crystals might serve the organisms as important nitrogen reserves: Guanine breakdown, which entails deamidation to xanthine and xanthine catabolism, releases five NH4+ that can freely enter biosynthetic pathways. The Raman images gave no information as to whether the crystals were cytoplasmic or encapsulated by membranes.

Here, we combine these two discoveries and propose that the vacuoles imaged by QFDEEM contain the guanine crystals detected by Raman microscopy, designating them as guanine storage vacuoles (GSVs). We show that GSVs have the properties expected of a nitrogen storage reserve: They are abundant in cells growing in NH4+-replete medium; they disappear within 30 h when such cells are acutely starved of exogenous nitrogen; and they reappear within 8 h when exogenous nitrogen is resupplied. Given the deep phylogenetic distances separating the microalgae evaluated in ref. 8, a guanine-based nitrogen reserve system apparently originated in an early ancestor of the algal radiation, after which it was subject to retention or loss (8).

We also identify guanine-related enzymes and transporters encoded in the C. reinhardtii genome and document their expression in nitrogen-depleted cells. We compare the GSV with the acidocalcisome (9), a second storage organelle with deep eukaryotic ancestry.

Results

Ultrastructure of the GSV in C. reinhardtii.

QFDE electron micrographs of C. reinhardtii document that the crystals detected in C. reinhardtii by Raman microscopy (SI Appendix, figure S12 of ref. 6) reside within membrane-delimited organelles that we call GSV (Fig. 1). GSVs range in size from 250 nm to 1.5 µm in diameter. In images of cross-fractured cytoplasm, some have no GSVs, some have one, and some have more than one. Counting all GSVs in a sample and dividing by the number of cross fractures examined yields what we term a GSV value, which is in the 50% range in log-phase cells. Raman images indicate the presence of ~15 GSVs per log-phase cell (6). The GSVs display no consistent proximity to other organelles in electron micrographs.

The crystals are shown at higher magnification in SI Appendix, Fig. S1, where several patterning systems are evident. Crystal patterns are best identified using electron diffraction technologies, and Pilátová et al. are currently undertaking such an analysis of C. reinhardtii material at the National Center of Electron Microscopy (Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory); they will report their findings independently.

Each GSV is surrounded by a membrane (Fig. 1 arrows) that encloses an aqueous domain, filled with a suspension of unidentified materials, that we call the matrix (Fig. 1, m). Within the matrix is a finely granular circular differentiation that we call a disc (Fig. 1 and SI Appendix, Fig. S1, d). Crystals arise at the periphery and sometimes within the interior of the discs. Since the disc material appears to merge into the crystallized material (SI Appendix, Fig. S1), we propose that it represents an amorphous form of solidified guanine (10, 11) that is poised to crystallize, perhaps conjugated with additional components that influence its crystallization potential. In some cases, the disc material is perforated with internal holes (Fig. 1 B and C).

In samples with no etching (Fig. 2G), or light etching (Fig. 1B), the matrix (m) is seen to be a uniform suspension of coarse granular material, readily distinguishable from the fine granules of the disc. With deep etching, the suspending water sublimes and the matrix material aggregates into meshworks (Figs. 1D and 2 A–E).

Additional information about disc/crystal associations has come from preparations of C. reinhardtii cells that were lysed in a warm mannitol-digitonin buffer (Materials and Methods). The GSV membrane is often solubilized, and the released disc/crystal dyads prove to be stable under these conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S2), documenting that their relationship self-maintains in the absence of a membrane or matrix.

GSV Membrane Ultrastructure in C. reinhardtii.

When internal cell membranes are frozen and fractured through their interior, the half-leaflets contiguous to the cytoplasm are called P and the half-leaflets contiguous to organelle interiors are called E. The P faces of membranes dedicated to ion trafficking display a very high density of intramembranous particles (IMPs), contributed by the transmembrane segments of ion pumps and transporters, as documented for the proton- and calcium-pumping acidocalcisome (9), and as shown for a cytoplasmic vacuole of C. reinhardtii in Fig. 3A and in figure 11 of ref. 9. Fig. 3 B–E show that the GSV P-face carries a similarly high density of IMPs, indicating ion-transporting capabilities. The E-faces of the GSVs are stable to etching, in contrast to the instability of the acidocalcisome E-face (9).

GSV Distribution in Microalgae.

Raman microscopy detects guanine-crystal signals in numerous microalgae (8). We have visualized GSVs in three additional members of the Order Chlamydomonadales: Chlamydomonas monoica, Haematococcus sp, and Dunaliella salina (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 and figure 17B of ref. 12); Haematococcus and Dunaliella were also scored as guanine-positive in the Raman survey (8). Each is organized like C. reinhardtii: A membrane encloses a matrix and a disc, and in most cases, guanine crystals emerge from the disc. No GSV candidates were found in examined microalgae from other green lineages (e.g., Trebouxiophytes, Prasinophytes) nor in Glaucophytes or red algae (e.g., Cyanidiophytes), albeit they may be present under other culture conditions.

Crystal-containing vacuoles are also present in several complex microalgae whose plastids derive from endosymbioses with red algae (13). Shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S4 are GSVs from the Eustigmatophyte Nannochloropsis oceanica (A), the Cryptophyte Chroomonas mesostigmata (B), and the Dinophyte Symbiodinum sp (C). All three groups yield guanine-crystal signals (8). None of these vacuoles contain identifiable discs. Symbiodinium also displays islands of free crystals in regions of its cytoplasm (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D), lifting up the importance of coupling Raman microscopy with electron microscopy images to verify GSV identification. In the Chrysophyte Ochromonas danica (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 E and F), which was not included in the Raman survey (8), crystals are scattered in large vacuoles that also harbor numerous disc-like inclusions, vacuoles that likely store the soluble polysaccharide chrysolaminarin. No GSV candidates were observed in other complex microalgae examined, albeit they may be present under other culture conditions.

GSV Loss with Nitrogen (N)-Starvation in C. reinhardtii.

When N-replete cells, fully endowed with GSVs, are harvested and resuspended in N-free medium, GSVs are gradually lost. In one experiment, starting with log-phase cells with a GSV value of 53%, this value was maintained after 2 h of N-deprivation but dropped to 32% and 31% in the 4- and 8-h samples and to 2% at 15 h.

In the cells N-starved for 2 h, we encountered several examples of GSVs with ruptured membranes, their crystals and discs in direct contact with the cytoplasm (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A–E), and islands of free crystals were very occasionally present in the cytoplasm (SI Appendix, Fig. S6F), suggesting that this is an early avenue taken to supply the cells with nitrogen.

A second and more common avenue became evident in the 4- and 8-h N-starved samples, where we found examples of small GSVs containing membrane stacks and whorls (Fig. 4 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S7). These small GSVs are not found after 15 h of N-starvation, however, discouraging the idea that they persist as “seeds” for future GSV biogenesis.

GSV Biogenesis in C. reinhardtii.

Acidocalcisomes and contractile-vacuole cisterns have been shown to mature at the trans face of the Golgi in C. reinhardtii (1, 9), but recognizable GSVs have not been observed to associate with the Golgi. Instead, our images suggest two ways that new GSVs are created.

In SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and B, GSVs appear to be dividing in two, and in SI Appendix, Fig. S5C, a GSV appears to be generating a bud (asterisk), suggesting that in some cases an existing organelle can self-replicate.

The second avenue is visualized when N-starved cells, lacking any recognizable GSVs, are harvested and resuspended in N-replete medium. In one experiment, after 8 h of N provision, the GSV value was 36%; after 12 h it was 85%, and rose to 100% by 24 h, perhaps in anticipation of mitotic distributed to daughter cells. These GSVs show no consistent physical proximity to other organelles.

The most distinctive feature of the new GSVs is their endowment of flat membrane stacks or whorls in their interiors (Fig. 4 C–G, arrows). This endowment decreases with time—62% of the new GSVs visualized include membranes at 8 h, 41% at 12 h, 36% at 17 h, and 11% at 24 h—and such configurations are rarely seen in mid-log (Fig. 2A). The smallest GSV encountered (~250 nm in diameter) displays crystals (Fig. 4C), indicating that crystal formation is not size/time-dependent, but most of the small organelles contain only discs and membranes.

Interestingly, these new GSVs closely resemble those encountered in the early stages of N-depletion (Fig. 4 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S7), raising the possibility that GSV induction goes through the same stages as GSV elimination.

Guanine-Crystal Persistence in Stationary Phase.

When C. reinhardtii cultures reach ~107 cells/mL, they enter the stationary phase. We examined two versions of the stationary phase.

When the culture medium is supplemented with CO2, it maintains a pH of 7, and after 3 d in the stationary phase under these conditions, cells had a GSV value of 35%, with the same proportion of disc-only and disc-plus-crystals found in log-phase cells.

Nonsupplemented cells enter stationary phase at pH > 9 due to CO2 depletion. SI Appendix, Fig. S8A shows such a cell that has dried down and burst onto a glass slide, releasing its phase-refractile guanine crystals, which are comparable in number to the crystals released by a lysed log-phase cell (SI Appendix, Fig. S8B).

These crystals prove to reside within large cytoplasmic vacuoles (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 C–E) that are induced by the alkaline conditions. The crystal-containing vacuoles are distinguished from GSVs by their size, their distinctive matrix morphology, and their frequent content of membrane vesicles in addition to crystals, and they persist for at least 2 wk in stationary cells. They also persisted when stationary cells were transferred to N-free medium for 30 h (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). Therefore, in contrast to the rapid depletion of GSVs with acute N-withdrawal, stationary-phase programs entail retention of crystals in GSVs (pH7) or in vacuoles (pH9).

The GSV Cycle Is the Reciprocal of the Acidocalcisome Cycle in C. reinhardtii.

In a prior report (9), we note that the signature polyphosphate granules contained within acidocalcisomes are present in cells that are acutely N-starved or in stationary phase (Fig. 5A, pp granule) but are not found in log-phase cells. We proposed that the polyphosphate might instead adopt a more accessible gel-like configuration in log phase and presented candidate images in SI Appendix, figure S4 of ref. 9. In the present study, we realized that we were often confusing gel-containing acidocalcisomes with GSVs containing crystal-free discs and that we could verify acidocalcisome identification by their unique membrane topology (9), with etchable E fracture faces and flat rims at their borders. Fig. 5 B–E show N-replete acidocalcisomes with gel-like (“syrupy”) contents, as contrasted with the granular discs within the GSVs (Fig. 5 A and B). The acidocalcisomes also lack the matrix phase present in the GSVs (Fig. 5B, m). Hence, the guanine solidifies during growth and the polyphosphate solidifies during stress.

Guanine Crystals and Polyphosphate Granules in Thin-Sectioned Preparations.

Why have GSVs not been previously described in the numerous publications showing classical thin sections of C. reinhardtii? Our answer is illustrated in SI Appendix, Fig. S9, which shows cells that were first taken to stationary phase and then N-starved for 30 h before freezing. QFDE replicas of these cells showed numerous vacuoles containing crystals, and in freeze-substituted preparations, thick sections display vacuoles with irregular osmiophilic inclusions, presumably the crystals (SI Appendix, Fig. S9A, arrows). The thick sections also display round osmiophilic polyphosphate granules. Neither the crystals nor the granules are adequately infiltrated by epoxy-based resins, however, perhaps because they resist acetone dehydration, and when the sections are thin, both inclusions fall out, either during sectioning or when exposed to an electron beam, leaving irregular or round holes (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 B and C). Images with ragged holes are unlikely to be chosen as figures for research publications.

Discussion

Acute depletion of exogenous nitrogen triggers numerous responses in C. reinhardtii: ribosome and protein degradation (16, 17); triacylglyeride biosynthesis and storage in lipid bodies (17); polyphosphate granule formation in the acidocalcisome (9); and the induction of sexual differentiation (16, 18–20). Extensive research has elucidated many details of these responses, and candidate regulatory proteins have been identified (21). Absent from these studies has been consideration of a recently reported endowment of guanine crystals in the C. reinhardtii cytoplasm (5–8) which, we report here, reside within cytoplasmic vacuoles. We document that crystal endowment is high when cells are N-replete and low when N-starved, consonant with the suggestion (6) that they serve as a nitrogen reserve. We therefore propose that they be called GSVs.

Unique to the GSVs of C. reinhardtii and three other members of the Chlamydonamodales is that the crystals appear to arise from a finely granular amorphous differentiation in the vacuole interior that we call a disc. Discs have not been observed in complex algae that assemble guanine crystals within vacuoles, suggesting that other modes of crystal formation have evolved in these radiations. Disc/crystal associations persist when vacuoles are lysed in a detergent solution. Amorphous forms of guanine have been described (2, 3), but nothing is known about how disc/crystal ratios are regulated nor whether the disc contains components in addition to the posited guanine.

The GSV membrane displays a high density of IMPs, a feature of membranes endowed with numerous multipass pumps and transporters (9) but not, for example, membranes of the microalgal endoplasmic reticulum or chloroplast envelope (22). Some of these may derive from the transporters that we suggest import guanosine as a guanine precursor or export guanine or xanthine for degradation (Fig. 6), but many presumably derive from pumps and channels that control intravacuolar pH, as is the case for other members of the vacuole family (23). Guanine is insoluble at neutral pH and soluble at acidic and alkaline pH (3); hence, crystal formation/solubilization would likely be influenced by pH modulation.

Acute N-starvation results in a total loss of identifiable GSVs within 15 h, presumably accompanied by the degradation of guanine into xanthine and then ammonium ions (Fig. 6). Transcriptomes of N-starved cells document a >fourfold, and in some cases many-hundred-fold, increase in the expression of genes encoding guanine-related enzymes and transporters (Table 1). Particularly notable is the upregulation of two families of nucleobase cation symporters, candidates for mediating guanine or xanthine efflux from the GSV, as drawn in Fig. 6. These transporters may remove protons from the GSV interior, moreover, which would facilitate the release of guanine from its crystalline state.

As N-starvation progresses, GSVs transform into small vacuoles containing disc material and stacks or whorls of membrane. Similar small vacuoles also mark the onset of GSV biogenesis when induced in GSV-free cells. The membrane disappears as the GSVs enlarge and return as the GSVs shrink. These images raise the interesting possibility that GSV elimination goes through the same stages as GSV induction, but in reverse order.

We also analyzed C. reinhardtii cells maintained in stationary phase under two pH conditions where, in both cases, crystals were retained, either within GSVs or within cytoplasmic vacuoles. Crystals have also been reported in stationary cultures of Desmodesmus quadricauda, in the Order Sphaeropleales (5). In general, the program governing acute N-starvation appears geared toward short-term survival and formation of dormant zygotes, while the stationary program is geared toward long-term survival in a quasivegetative state.

While regulation of the guanine crystal cycle is still largely not understood, it is becoming apparent that many versions have evolved, presumably geared to the needs of a given lineage. For example, Shaked et al. (24) report that Phacotus lenticularis, in the Order Chlamydomonadales, has no guanine-crystal Raman signal in fully supplemented medium, in contrast to the four other Chlamydomonadales described in this report, but exhibits a strong signal with phosphate starvation.

Finally, our studies have led us to revisit acidocalcisome ultrastructure, concluding that their polyphosphate content is “syrupy” in log-phase cells and converts to a solid granule under stress conditions, as we tentatively suggested in our earlier study (9). Whether the syrup has the properties of a liquid crystal (25) or some other colloidal state is not known, but it is presumably more metabolically accessible than the granule. Granule formation/dissolution is likely influenced by fluxes of protons and/or Ca++ in the acidocalcisome lumen.

These observations lead us to conclude that C. reinhardtii cells maintain polyphosphate in an accessible fluid form when they are growing and convert it into an inaccessible granular storage form when N-stressed and not growing, while GSVs do the reverse: They maintain guanine in an inaccessible crystalline storage form when nitrogen is abundant and convert it into ammonium when N-stressed. While the two cycles may operate independently, the possibility that they function synergistically to maintain sustainable N:P ratios is an idea worth exploring.

We conclude with a quote from the pioneering paper by Mojzeš et al. (6):

By locking excessive N in insoluble crystals and mobilizing them when required, symbiotic microalgae may effectively mitigate the negative effect of N excesses or deprivations and maintain stable nutrient stoichiometry (C:N and N:P ratios).

Analogous to polyphosphate, which was regarded as a molecular fossil and was, subsequently, revealed to have a multitude of functions, crystalline guanine can also be considered as an evolutionary old, overlooked, and forgotten multifunctional tool of nature popping up from oblivion.

Materials and Methods

Growth.

Liquid cultures (500 mL in 1-L Erlenmeyer flasks) of sta-6 (CC-4348; Chlamydomonas Center) were grown on rotary shakers (125 rpm) in continuous light at 25 µE m−2 s−1 light intensity in phosphate-buffered high-salt medium (HSM) (26) containing 9.3 mM NH4Cl as a nitrogen source and supplemented with 20 mM potassium acetate. For -N, log-phase (4 × 106 cells/mL, as determined by hemacytometer count) cells were pelleted (1,200 × g for 3 min) and resuspended in HSM + acetate lacking NH4Cl. GSVs were also observed in a wild-type strain (21gr) and in strain cw-15 (CC-4349), but the images in this report are of sta-6.

Stationary Phase Cultures.

Cells went into the stationary phase at 107 cells/mL under two culture conditions. pH7: Wild-type 21gr cells in HSM bubbled with 0.5% CO2 at 150 µE m−2 s−1 light intensity; pH9: sta6 cells in HSM + 20 mM acetate at 30 µE m−2 s−1 light intensity.

Quick-Freeze Deep-Etch Electron Microscopy.

Pelleted live cells were placed on a cushioning material and dropped onto a liquid-helium-cooled copper block; the frozen material was transferred to liquid nitrogen and fractured under vacuum, etched at −80 °C for 2 min (or 1 min for “lightly etched”) and Pt/C rotary-replicated under vacuum as described (27). Replicas were examined with a JEOL electron microscope, model JEM 1400, equipped with an AMTV601 digital camera. The images are photographic negatives; protuberant elements of the fractured/etched surface are most heavily coated with platinum, which blocks exposure of the electron beam so they appear black in the original images and white in the reversals.

Quick-Freeze Freeze-Substituted Electron Microscopy.

Samples were prepared as described in ref. 28.

Digitonin/Mannitol Cell Breakage.

Following an earlier protocol developed to isolate intact chloroplasts (29), stationary sta6 cells were harvested, resuspended in 40 mL of digitonin solution (5 mM potassium-phosphate buffer pH 6.5, 6% PEG 6000, 0.004% digitonin,1 mM PMSF), transferred to a cold metal beaker and heated to 27 °C in a 48 °C water bath with constant swirling. Once 27 °C was reached, the suspension was transferred to a 30 °C shaking water bath, shaken for 10 min, centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 5 min, resuspended in 10 mL of mannitol buffer [20 mM HEPES-NaOH pH 7.7, 0.15 M mannitol, 1 mM MgCl2, 2 mM EDTA, 1protease inhibitor tablet (Roche Complete Ultra Mini)], and then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 min prior to freezing the pellet.
