Insights into vascular physiology from sleep photoplethysmography
Authors: Gizem Yilmaz, Ju Lynn Ong, Lieng-Hsi Ling, Michael W L Chee
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy ObjectivesPhotoplethysmography (PPG) in consumer sleep trackers is now widely available and used to assess heart rate variability (HRV) for sleep staging. However, PPG waveform changes during sleep can also inform about vascular elasticity in healthy persons who constitute a majority of users. To assess its potential value, we traced the evolution of PPG pulse waveform during sleep alongside measurements of HRV and blood pressure (BP).MethodsSeventy-eight healthy adults (50% male, median [IQR range] age: 29.5 [23.0, 43.8]) underwent overnight polysomnography (PSG) with fingertip PPG, ambulatory blood pressure monitoring, and electrocardiography (ECG). Selected PPG features that reflect arterial stiffness: systolic to diastolic distance (∆T_norm), normalized rising slope (Rslope) and normalized reflection index (RI) were derived using a custom-built algorithm. Pulse arrival time (PAT) was calculated using ECG and PPG signals. The effect of sleep stage on these measures of arterial elasticity and how this pattern of sleep stage evolution differed with participant age were investigated.ResultsBP, heart rate (HR) and PAT were reduced with deeper non-REM sleep but these changes were unaffected by the age range tested. After adjusting for lowered HR, ∆T_norm, Rslope, and RI showed significant effects of sleep stage, whereby deeper sleep was associated with lower arterial stiffness. Age was significantly correlated with the amount of sleep-related change in ∆T_norm, Rslope, and RI, and remained a significant predictor of RI after adjustment for sex, body mass index, office BP, and sleep efficiency.ConclusionsThe current findings indicate that the magnitude of sleep-related change in PPG waveform can provide useful information about vascular elasticity and age effects on this in healthy adults.

Statement of Significance

Photoplethysmography (PPG) can inform about elasticity of the vascular system, but the PPG signal has hitherto not been used to evaluate this in relatively healthy persons. We found that the morphology of PPG signals recorded from the fingertip evolves with deepening NREM sleep in tandem with heart rate, heart rate variability, and blood pressure, reflecting increased arterial elasticity. The magnitude of sleep-related change in PPG arterial indices diminished with age even in this healthy sample. Nocturnal PPG may therefore have utility in assessing vascular elasticity in relatively healthy persons, the most common purchasers of consumer sleep trackers.

Introduction

Photoplethysmography (PPG) is an established, noninvasive technology that interrogates tissue using specific wavelengths of light, with photodetectors capturing light either reflected from or transmitted through a tissue bed. The measured signal is amplified, filtered, and processed to produce the characteristic PPG waveform synchronized to each heartbeat [1]. The shape of the PPG pulse wave is influenced by chronological age [2–4], health conditions such as hypertension [5], diabetes [6], obstructive sleep apnea [7], insomnia [8], and autonomic nervous system (ANS) activity [9–11]. Changes in the morphology of the PPG waveform reflect elasticity and adherence of peripheral arteries, making it a candidate measure of arterial stiffness [4, 12–14] and blood pressure (BP) [15–17]. PPG while less accurate than tonometry or ultrasound [18] is highly portable. As such, PPG-based arterial stiffness assessment has the potential to aid the collection of high-temporal resolution longitudinal data relevant to assessing cardiovascular risk objectively and at scale. Nonetheless, the susceptibility of clinical-grade PPG tools to external sources of noise [19], such as motion, limits their use outside controlled laboratory conditions during daytime activity.

Sleep provides a unique opportunity for assessing arterial stiffness on account of reduced motion and the orderly progression of physiological changes like lowering heart rate (HR) and BP [20], body temperature, and respiration [21] with deepening sleep. These autonomic changes which include an increase in HR variability (HRV) are largely a consequence of increased parasympathetic nervous system (PNS) activity during NREM sleep [22]. Conversely, REM sleep is characterized by PNS withdrawal [22–24]. PNS dominance is reduced with age [25–27], in sleep disorders [28, 29], and in cardiovascular disease [30–33].

The arterial pulse waveform exhibits well-characterized changes with chronological age due to decreased adherence of peripheral arteries [34, 35]. For example, the time difference between systolic and diastolic peaks of the PPG pulse gets shorter [4, 36] and the duration from the foot of a pulse to its peak (also called rise time) lengthens, leading to a reduced up-slope [37, 38]. Hence, several PPG-derived parameters have been proposed to assess vascular health [39, 40], but their application has largely been limited to stationary conditions in the awake state. Recently changes in PPG pulse waveform in sleep were evaluated together with markers of ANS function [8, 11], but a more detailed characterization of such changes across sleep stages is absent.

We characterized changes in PPG pulse waveform in healthy individuals during different stages of sleep and examined how these changes are temporally related to changes in BP and HRV. Secondly, we investigated the effects of age on PPG features, HRV, and BP during different stages of nocturnal sleep in these healthy participants. We hypothesized that distinct PPG features would reflect ANS modulation during sleep, with the greatest difference occurring between N3 and the pre-sleep wake condition.

Methods

Participants

The sample of participants used in the analysis was a combination of two separate samples (sample-1 and sample-2) recruited one year apart. To qualify, participants had to be free from preexisting sleep, neurological or psychiatric disorders, excessive daytime sleepiness (Epworth Sleepiness Scale 43 scores > 10), not be on wake-promoting medications, have a body mass index (BMI) < 35 kg/m2, and habitually sleep more than 5 hours/night. Participants visited the lab for a daytime briefing session where height, weight, waist circumference, and office BP measurements were taken.

Sample-1 was part of a larger study that recruited nominally healthy adults aged between 21 and 70 years for a 2-night protocol in a sleep laboratory. Each participant then underwent two overnight polysomnography (PSG) sessions in the lab; some participants also had concurrent ambulatory BP monitoring (ABPM) during one of the recording nights. The order of nights with and without ABPM was randomized. Participants with < 3 BP measurements during the night or pre-sleep baseline period, with a history of hypertension or antihypertensive medication use, and with less than 5 minutes of artifact-free PPG signal in the pre-sleep baseline were excluded. Sample-1 consisted of 62 adults (32 males; median [IQR] age: 26.0 [23.0, 37.8]). Similarly, sample-2 underwent an overnight PSG session in the lab and slept according to their habitual bed and waketimes. They did not have concurrent ABPM. Participants with a history of hypertension or antihypertensive medication use, and with less than 5 minutes of artifact-free PPG signal in the pre-sleep baseline were excluded. Sample-2 consisted of 16 adults (7 males; median [IQR] age: 37.5 [32.8, 50.0]).

Thus, after combining sample-1 and sample-2, the final sample consisted of 78 healthy adults (39 males; median [IQR] age: 29.5 [23.0, 43.8], see Supplementary Figure S1 and S2 for details).

Laboratory protocol

Participants arrived at the sleep laboratory approximately 2 hours before their self-reported habitual bedtime. Trained assistants set up PSG and ABPM equipment (for sample-1 only), following which participants were required to sit still for the 15-minute pre-sleep baseline recording. Lights off and lights on times were assigned according to the participant’s habitual bed and wake times.

The Institutional Review Board of the National University of Singapore approved the study, and the protocol was in accordance with the principles in the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed written consent was obtained from all participants during the briefing session.

Data acquisition

PSG

Electroencephalography was recorded from two channels (C3 and C4) according to the international 10–20 system via SOMNOtouch devices (SOMNOmedics GmbH, Randersacker, Germany) during overnight sleep. Channels were referenced to the contralateral mastoids. The common ground and reference electrodes were placed at Fpz and Cz, respectively. Electrooculography (EOG; right and left outer canthi), submental electromyography (EMG), thoracic breathing effort, electrocardiography (ECG), and finger plethysmography (PPG) signals were recorded concurrently. The sampling rate of EEG, ECG, and PPG was 256 Hz.

PSG scores were obtained as follows: we derived a consensus score from 3 independent scoring systems to minimize the impact of individual scorer bias: (1) Neurobit PSG automated scoring (Neurobit Inc., New York, USA) [41] checked by trained in-house staff, (2) Somnolyzer 24 × 7 automated scoring (The Siesta Group Schlafanalyse GmbH, Vienna, Austria) [42] reviewed by experts from The Siesta Group, and (3) U-Sleep webservice automated scoring (sleep.ai.ku.dk) [43] which was trained and evaluated on PSG recordings from 15 660 participants. For each 30 seconds epoch, a consensus score was given based on the majority score of the three scorers. If all 3 scorers differed, the scoring system of (1) was used. Wake after sleep onset, REM sleep, N1, N2, and N3 were calculated based on 30-second epochs according to the AASM manual criteria [44]. Stages used for comparison are referred to as AWAKE (sit) referring to the pre-sleep, awake, and sitting condition, AWAKE (ib) referring to awake, but lying supine in bed, N1, N2, N3, and REM.

Blood pressure

Ambulatory BP was measured using the OnTrak 90227 monitor (Spacelabs Healthcare, Snoqualmie, WA) [45] on the nondominant arm, and the BP measurement interval was 30 minutes. Nights with < 3 valid sleep BP measurements were excluded from the dataset.

Data analysis

All PPG and ECG waveform analyses were conducted in MATLAB version R2021b (The Math Works, Inc., Natick, MA).

PPG pulse onset detection

After band-pass filtering of the PPG signal (0.05–20 Hz via fourth-order Chebyshev filter), the onset of each PPG pulse was detected using the PPG onset detector from The PhysioNet Cardiovascular Signal Toolbox [46]. Artefactual detections were automatically removed based on physiological plausibility criteria: (1) Lower and upper limits of onset-to-onset duration were set to 0.4 and 2 seconds, (2) Maximum acceptable change in peak-to-peak duration between consecutive pulses was set to 40%. (See Supplementary Materials).

PPG pulse waveform analysis and feature extraction

The pulse onset time series was windowed in temporal alignment with the sleep stage assigned to each time window. Within these 30 seconds windows, fiducial points on the pulse wave were located using a custom algorithm that detected onset, offset, systolic peak, diastolic peak, and the dicrotic notch of each pulse waveform (Supplementary Figure S3). Our algorithm combined a derivatives approach [47] with Gaussian-fitting [48] to detect fiducial points (algorithm details are provided in Supplementary Materials). We first calculated primary features such as pulse duration from onset to offset (PD), pulse rise time from onset to systolic peak (Rt), time difference between systolic and diastolic peaks (∆T), and amplitudes of systolic peak and diastolic peak (Sys Amp and Dias Amp, respectively) to represent duration and amplitude-based changes in PPG pulse contour (Figure 1 A). Second, we applied duration normalization to PPG features to correct for the influence of small changes in HR. Individual pulses were normalized in width into 100 equally spaced intervals by cubic spline interpolation [36], hence the unit of normalized pulse duration was sample (Supplementary Figure S4). We then calculated normalized primary features such as ∆T_norm (sample), Rt_norm (sample), Sys Amp_norm (z-score), and Dias Amp_norm (z-score). Third, using the normalized primary features, we calculated “stiffness indices” that have been proposed as surrogates for arterial age and elasticity: rising slope (Rslope) [49], a measure of pulse sharpness, was calculated as systolic amplitude (Sys Amp_norm) divided by diastolic amplitude (Rt_norm) and had a unit of z-score/sample. Reflection index (RI) [6] is a measure evaluating the degree of wave reflection from peripheral arteries. RI was defined as the ratio of diastolic pulse amplitude to systolic amplitude (Dias Amp_norm/Sys Amp_norm, with no unit). Additionally, we derived the Stiffness Index (SI) [4], originally defined as the ratio of participant’s height to ∆T, but recalculated as height/∆T_norm (with a unit of m/sample) to normalize for HR. Both RI and SI have been proposed as predictors of CV risk and mortality [50–52]. (As ∆T_norm and SI are highly dependent, SI was not included in the main results, but shown in Supplementary).

Median values of PPG features within each 30-second window were defined as epoch-level PPG features. HR was used as a reference feature due to its well-established dynamics during sleep. PPG analysis was applied to all participants in the combined sample. We then investigated the bivariate correlations between age and PPG features in (1) AWAKE (sit) and (2) magnitude of change (percentage difference in features between AWAKE (sit) and N3, denoted as Change (%)).

ECG - R-peak detection and HRV calculation

The ECG signal was band-pass filtered (0.05–40Hz) and R-peaks were identified with the QRS detector from the PhysioNet Cardiovascular Signal Toolbox. To exclude incorrectly detected peaks, (1) Lower and upper limits of peak-to-peak duration were set to 0.4 and 2 seconds, and (2) The maximum acceptable change in peak-to-peak duration between consecutive peaks was 30%. Following automated data cleaning, R-peaks, and R-R intervals were visually checked. If necessary, artefactual peaks were deleted manually in a custom-made GUI. No R-peak additions were performed. HRV analysis was performed on consecutive epochs of 5 minutes duration, and the respective HRV for each sleep stage was calculated as the average across 5 minutes segments. Normalized frequency-based HRV metrics, high-frequency (HF)nu, and low-frequency: HF ratio [53] were computed in the analysis. HRV analysis was computed only for sample-1. ECG data were available only for sample-1 (N = 48).

BP measurements were grouped and averaged for each stage within participants, resulting in BP values for AWAKE (sit), AWAKE (ib), N1, N2, N3, and REM. As with the PPG features, the change in BP was calculated as the percentage change between AWAKE (sit) and N3 periods. ABPM data were only available for sample-1.

Pulse arrival time

Pulse arrival time (PAT) [54], a surrogate measure of pulse propagation delay between two points in the arterial tree, was calculated from ECG and PPG signals. PAT was defined as the time delay from the R-peak of the QRS wave to the onset of the PPG pulse. PAT was included as a supplementary index due to its established relationship with arterial stiffness and BP. PAT data were only available for Sample-1.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted in RStudio [55] (version 2022.7.0.548) with packages tidyverse [56], ggpubr [57], R.matlab [58], gtsummary [59], ggridges [60], and cowplot [61]. The alpha level of all statistical tests was 0.05.

For the sleep stage-based analysis, results were first averaged within participants for each stage, followed by repeated measures ANOVA with stage as the within-participant factor. Data were included only if they contained average values from all stages (AWAKE [sit], AWAKE [ib], N2, N3, REM) per participant. The p-values were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction.

The bivariate relationships between age and PPG features in sleep were tested with Pearson’s Correlation. The significance of the age effect on Change (%) was tested in linear regression models adjusted for sex, BMI, office SBP, office DBP, and sleep efficiency.

Changes in PPG pulse waveform during sleep

The contour of the waking pulse waveform changes with increasing age, manifesting as decreased ∆T, and disappearance of the diastolic peak [4]. In our healthy adults, we found changes in the amplitude and location of fiducial points of the PPG pulse across different sleep stages. Typical PPG waveforms from a young female participant (Figure 1B) show greater pulse amplitude (Sys Amp and Dias Amp) and PD during NREM sleep compared to the pre-sleep baseline, and a gradual deepening of the valley between systolic and diastolic peaks (i.e. dicrotic notch amplitude became relatively lower) from pre-sleep baseline to N3.

Repeated measures testing confirmed that these duration and amplitude-based features (PD, Sys Amp, Dias Amp, Rt, and ∆T) differed by sleep stage. There was a significant effect of sleep stage on PD [F (4, 308) = 177, p < 0.0001], Sys Amp [F (4, 264) = 62, p < 0.0001], Dias Amp [F (4, 300) = 54, p < 0.0001], Rt [F (4, 268) = 8, p < 0.0001], and ∆T [F (4, 300) = 92, p < 0.0001]. Post hoc analyses with Bonferroni adjustment revealed that all these PPG features were significantly different in N2 and N3 sleep compared to the awake sitting baseline (p < 0.05) except N3 stage of Rt (Figure 1 C). After duration normalization, stage effect remained significant for ∆T_norm [F (4, 300) = 26, p < 0.0001], Sys Amp_norm [F (4, 264) = 62, p < 0.0001], and Dias Amp_norm [F (4, 296) = 58, p < 0.0001]. Although Rt_norm differed by sleep stage after normalization [F (4, 268) = 5, p < 0.001], there was no difference in NREM compared to awake sitting baseline.

Vascular health indices such as HR, Rslope, RI, and SI also differed by sleep stage. There was a significant effect of sleep stage on HR [F (4, 308) = 171, p < 0.0001], Rslope [F (4, 292) = 60, p < 0.0001], RI [F (4, 300) = 55, p < 0.0001], and SI [F (4, 296) = 24, p < 0.0001]. Post hoc analyses with a Bonferroni adjustment revealed that all these PPG features were significantly different in N2 and N3 sleep compared to the awake sitting baseline (p < 0.05) (Figure 2 A and Supplementary Figure S5). The largest change in HR compared to the pre-sleep baseline was observed in N2. For ∆T_norm, Rslope, RI, and SI the largest difference was in N3.

Since all PPG features showed maximal shifts during NREM sleep, we next examined the relationship between age and magnitude of change from pre-sleep baseline to N3 [Change (%)] in PPG health metrics. Except for HR (r = −0.19, p = 0.093), there were significant correlations between age and Change (%) for ∆T_norm (r = 0.26, p < 0.05), Rslope (r = 0.27, p < 0.05), and RI (r = 0.47, p < 0.0001) (Figure 2 B) and for SI (r = −0.26, p < 0.05) (Supplementary Figure S5). The significant relationships with RI remained after correcting for sex, BMI, office BP, and sleep efficiency (Supplementary Table S1–S4).

Changes in HRV, BP, and PAT during sleep

As HRV reflects ANS activity, we examined ECG-derived HRV across sleep and used it as a reference to interpret the PPG-based changes across the night. There was a significant effect of sleep stage on HF HRV (HFnu) [F (4, 176) = 46, p < 0.001]. HFnu was highest in N3 compared to the pre-sleep baseline condition (Figure 3 A). Similarly, there was a significant effect of sleep stage on SBP [F (4, 156) = 16, p < 0.0001], and DBP [F (4, 156) = 19, p < 0.0001] (Figure 3 B). BP values were lowest during N3. However, age did not alter the N3 sleep-related lowering of HRV, SBP, and DBP. Age was not significantly correlated with change in HRV (r = 0.0064, p = 0.68), SBP (r = −0.021, p = 0.9), or DBP (r = 0.07, p = 0.67).

(A) HRV (HFnu) across stages. (B) Systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP and DBP) across sleep stages.

Finally, as a surrogate measure of arterial stiffness, PAT values differed significantly by sleep stage [F (4, 224) = 52, p < 0.0001], with highest PAT observed in N2 (Supplementary Figure S6). Age was significantly correlated with change in PAT (r = 0.27, p < 0.05). The amount of change from awake baseline to N3 became smaller with age.

Discussion

We observed alterations in PPG waveform during nocturnal sleep, in concert with changes in HR, HRV, BP, and PAT. PD, Rt, and ∆T were prolonged, and Sys Amp was augmented with deepening NREM sleep. HF HRV activity followed a similar pattern, with the highest values observed during N3, highlighting the modulation of both cardiac rhythm, blood volume, and peripheral conductance by PNS activity. In addition, changes in ∆T_norm, RSlope, and RI with deepening sleep were significantly attenuated with age. In contrast, the magnitude of HR, HRV, and BP change remained stable across age, indicating higher sensitivity of RI to modulation by the combined effect of age and PNS activity.

Pulse waveform modulation during sleep reflects autonomic modulation

Pulse shape depends on the ventricular contraction pattern and the elasticity of blood vessels. Waveform changes have been observed in relation to aging [37] as well as metabolic syndrome [62], diabetes [6, 63] and hypertension [12, 64]. Typical PPG pulse waveform changes (with increasing age) include (1) shortening of the interval between systolic and diastolic peaks, (2) disappearance of the dicrotic notch, and (3) decreased amplitude ratio between diastolic and systolic peaks. Yet, the modulation of arterial elasticity during sleep in healthy persons has not previously been characterized. In our relatively healthy participants, we observed that PPG features changed with deeper NREM sleep stage reflecting autonomic modulation of cardiovascular function. Indeed, HRV was highest during N3 sleep and PPG-based arterial stiffness measures reached a nadir at stage N3.

These findings might be explained by sleep-modulated alterations in ANS activity. The ANS constantly regulates blood flow and BP by controlling vascular tone, such that increased tone leads to reduced blood flow which in turn lowers PPG pulse amplitude [65–67]. During N2 and N3 stages of sleep, BP declines as a result of reduction in HR and sympathetic vasomotor tone [20]. In line with this, we found that both Sys Amp and Sys Amp_norm increased with NREM sleep, reaching a peak during N3. Previous studies have associated pulse wave amplitude drops with obstructive respiratory events and cortical arousals [9, 67] as a marker of an increased sympathetic drive. While NREM sleep is also characterized by increased parasympathetic activity, this is unlikely to be directly responsible for increased PPG amplitude as digital arteries and indeed most blood vessels in the body do not have cholinergic innervation [68]. Finally, BP and HR are linked to the regulation of vasomotor tone and are modulated by the ANS [20]. If PNS activity is impaired due to disease [69] or sleep deprivation [70], HR and BP lowering can be blunted. As expected, both HR and BP were significantly lower during NREM sleep compared to baseline.

The magnitude of change in PPG features between wakefulness and N3 as an indicator of vascular function

To the best of our knowledge, only a few studies have used overnight PPG features to assess vascular function. Older adults with hypertension displayed higher non-normalized ∆T during sleep compared to age-matched normotensives [12], as well as smaller relative amplitude between systolic and diastolic peaks during sleep compared to normotensive controls [71]. Uniquely, our study focused on younger, healthy adults, assessed a broader swathe of PPG arterial health indices, and normalized pulse width across sleep stages to facilitate comparability. Even in this low-risk demographic, arterial elasticity during deep sleep correlated with age, suggesting a potential role for PPG in assessing subclinical vascular disease. Compared to conventional daytime measurements of arterial stiffness with reference clinical methods (such as tonometry) [18], continuous PPG recording provides a unique opportunity to conveniently monitor vascular function longitudinally from a young age. Furthermore, the decrease in arterial stiffness in N3 (as measured by Rslope, RI, and SI) was modulated by age, whereas BP and HRV were not. The magnitude of change in PPG features may therefore be an early indicator of occult vascular disease.

As a surrogate for the speed of pulse propagation due to arterial stiffening, decreased PAT has been associated with higher arterial stiffness and increased future CVD events [72]. Our PAT values increased during NREM sleep compared to pre-sleep baseline, consistent with our PPG features and previous findings in PAT [73]. However, it is important to note that PAT is a combination of pulse transit time and a short but variable pre-ejection period [74]. Thus, straightforward interpretation of PAT results is difficult without further information on pre-ejection period and reference stiffness measurements.

PPG features and vascular health assessment

Nocturnal BP and BP-dipping are strong predictors of CV disease and mortality [69, 75]. However, measuring nighttime BP using cuff-based systems can be uncomfortable, leading to a surge in interest in using PPG-based methods [39, 76]. Various PPG features have been proposed as potential predictors of vascular age and cardiovascular risk, but studies estimating nocturnal BP are limited [17]. The relationship between arterial stiffness and BP is bidirectional, dynamic, and complex. In line with the growing interest in noninvasive and continuous assessment of vascular health, there has been a proliferation of studies employing different PPG features [40, 77] but it is not yet clear which measures of pulse waveform provide the best assessment of vascular age and the behavior of these PPG waveform indices during sleep remains largely unexplored. As our aim was to characterize changes in the PPG pulse waveform during sleep, we selected (1) previously studied, and (2) easy-to-interpret waveform features. While primary features (PD, Rt, ∆T, Sys Amp, and Dias Amp) directly reflect the change in pulse contour, stiffness indices (Rslope, RI, and ∆T_norm and SI) were included as established predictors of CV health. Our results highlight the modulating effect of sleep on current set of PPG features, that need to be considered when designing ML algorithms.

Finally, modern consumer sleep trackers with advanced PPG sensors have great potential to monitor parameters of cardiovascular health longitudinally. Wearable-derived HR and HRV show good agreement with reference HRV at rest [78] and during sleep [79], but the implementation of more complex pulse waveform analysis to predict arterial health in sleep remains limited. Although PPG waveform may differ in transmission mode, light wavelength, and measurement site between the conventional fingertip PPG and wearables, several recent studies have demonstrated the feasibility of CV health assessment using wearable-based PPG [80, 81] in awake daytime conditions.

Strengths and limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study examining PPG-based arterial stiffness in healthy and predominantly young adults during sleep, together with simultaneous HR, BP, and HRV measurements. As most PPG feature extraction algorithms are developed using data from intensive care patients (such as the MIMIC dataset [82]), they are often difficult to generalize to healthy populations. We have developed our own pulse waveform analysis algorithm, with clear methodological guidelines for reproducibility. Because the PPG waveform is shaped by the combined effect of HR and vascular properties, and since differences in HR might also influence the timing of the reflected wave in the arterial pulse [35], we controlled PPG-based vascular health indices for HR. The observed changes in these duration-normalized PPG features during sleep therefore provide robust evidence for the dynamic modulation of vascular properties, independent of HR.

An important limitation is the absence of a gold standard metric of vascular stiffness, for example, carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity assessed using applanation tonometry [18]. Measures of arterial stiffness are typically obtained in the awake or ambulatory state, and the clinical relevance of nocturnal indices as markers of future CV risk is unknown. Because PPG-based assessment of arterial stiffness has previously shown good agreement with central pulse wave velocity [83], we posit a relationship between the observed parallel changes in PPG waveform and arterial elasticity, even if it is not possible to determine the exact pattern of modulation.

Our particpants were in good general health and dominated by young to middle-aged adults. Therefore, it is unclear how our findings on arterial stiffness and health can be generalized to other age groups or populations with different health conditions. Because the sample was limited to healthy people, it may not be appropriate to extrapolate the results to people with existing cardiovascular or other disease.

Conclusion

PPG signal morphology changes systematically across nocturnal sleep. PPG-measured vascular stiffness was lowest in N3 compared to wakefulness in this healthy adult population. Nocturnal variation of the PPG waveform paralleled changes in HRV and BP. Taken together, our results suggest that arterial elasticity is sensitive to the modulating effect of sleep, and continuous PPG recording provides a unique opportunity to monitor vascular function non-invasively.
