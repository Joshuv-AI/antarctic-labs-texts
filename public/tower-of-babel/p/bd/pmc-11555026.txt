Relationship between growth and ambulation loss in Duchenne muscular dystrophy boys on steroids
Authors: Georgia Stimpson, Deborah Ridout, Anna Sarkozy, Adnan Manzur, Francesco Muntoni, Giovanni Baranello, the NorthStar Network
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and purposeTreatment with glucocorticoids (GCs) is part of the standard of care in Duchenne muscular dystrophy, but excess weight gain and height stunting are common side‐effects. It is still unclear how these growth‐related side‐effects affect motor function.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study utilized 2228 observations from 648 participants in the UK NorthStar database who had growth and ambulation data recorded between 2006 and 2020. Joint modelling was used to analyse the effect of longitudinal growth centiles on loss of ambulation with respect to GC type and regimen.ResultsLoss of ambulation was observed in 113 patients. National estimates of loss of ambulation age were updated by GC group and showed no significant association between loss of ambulation risk and absolute growth centile. However, yearly drift in weight and/or height centile had an associated risk effect on loss of ambulation. Over a 2‐year period, a yearly drift in weight from the 50th to the 75th, 75th to the 90th and 90th to the 95th centile was associated with 138%, 118% and 64% increased risk of loss of ambulation, respectively. Conversely, a 2‐year drift in height from the 50th to the 25th, 25th to the 10th and 10th to the 5th centile was associated with 53%, 49% and 35% decreased risk of loss of ambulation, respectively.ConclusionsOur results suggest a complex relationship between growth and loss of ambulation in Duchenne muscular dystrophy boys on chronic GCs, the first step in understanding the effects of drugs which also affect growth patterns.

INTRODUCTION

Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is an X‐linked recessive disease affecting 1 in 5000 to 1 in 6250 live male births [1], caused by pathogenic variants affecting the expression of the dystrophin gene. Boys with DMD typically achieve peak motor function at the age of 7 years, followed by a decline in motor abilities and loss of ambulation at the age of approximately 12 years.

Glucocorticoids (GCs), namely prednisone (or its active metabolite prednisolone) and deflazacort, remain the primary standard of care treatment for DMD [2], despite significant efforts to develop novel therapies that restore dystrophin expression. In the UK, GCs are prescribed primarily on a daily or intermittent (10 days on, 10 days off) regimen. GC treatment delays loss of ambulation compared to GC naïve boys by between 1 and 3 years [3, 4, 5, 6], with daily GCs preserving long‐term motor function more effectively than intermittent GCs [7, 8]. The double‐blind randomized FOR‐DMD clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT01603407) showed that both daily deflazacort and prednisone improved motor function more than intermittent prednisone over a 3‐year period [9]. Although loss of ambulation was not captured in the FOR‐DMD study, observational studies have shown that deflazacort is more effective in delaying loss of ambulation compared to prednisone/prednisolone [6, 7].

Posited reasons for the differential effect of deflazacort and prednisone/prednisolone on motor function include differential anti‐inflammatory, immunosuppressive and anabolic effects, and rates of adverse effects relating to bone health and growth [7, 10, 11, 12]. Natural history studies have shown that boys with DMD exhibit unusual growth patterns relative to their peers [13]. They typically have normal height at birth, high prevalence of short stature before the age of 5 years [14] and shorter stature compared to non‐DMD reference populations at older ages, which has been shown to be more pronounced in those lacking expression of the dystrophin isoform Dp71 [15].

The differential impact of different GC types and regimens on growth has already been shown in observational studies. Patients treated with deflazacort were observed to have a lower percentage of body fat, weight gain and a more severe height stunting than those on prednisone [10, 15, 16, 17]. Additionally, in the FOR‐DMD trial, both the prednisone‐daily‐treated and prednisone‐intermittent‐treated groups showed excess weight gain at 3 years compared to the deflazacort‐daily‐treated group [9]. The prednisone intermittent regimen showed milder height stunting compared to the daily regimens, with the daily deflazacort group having the most severe height stunting [9]. It has been previously suggested that the resulting shorter stature in DMD boys on deflazacort could play a role in delaying loss of ambulation; however, this has not been analysed in‐depth [15].

Over the past decade, the proportion of individuals with DMD treated with GC has increased and continuation after loss of ambulation is associated with preserving upper limbs, cardiac and respiratory functions [18, 19]. As such, excessive weight gain or height stunting are factors that need to be considered when deciding to reduce dose or to switch GC type or regimen. With the implementation of the updated standard of care recommendations [2, 20, 21] and the increasing body of evidence on the effects of long‐term GC treatment, this study aims to describe and update the loss of ambulation pattern by GC group (daily/intermittent deflazacort/prednisolone and GC naïve) in the UK NorthStar database since the initial report in 2013 [7], and to analyse the impact of growth trajectory (namely weight, height and body mass index [BMI] trajectories) on loss of ambulation.

METHODS

This is a retrospective cohort analysis of observational data on boys in the UK with DMD. Established in October 2006, the NorthStar database is the UK's national DMD longitudinal registry, where data from 23 paediatric neuromuscular centres are stored. Data are collected and recorded by clinicians and physiotherapists, with participants scheduled for clinics approximately every 6 months. The NorthStar has Caldicott Guardian approval, and data are collected only after informed consent has been obtained. Clinical assessments are conducted in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2000) and the Principles of Good Clinical Practice. Patients are treated in line with the 2018 standards of care recommendations [2].

Participants

From the full NorthStar database cut, only those with diagnosed DMD (as opposed to Becker muscular dystrophy, intermediate DMD [an intermediate clinical presentation between DMD and Becker muscular dystrophy] and symptomatic female carriers) were included. All data from visits to clinic before the first non‐ambulant visit and after the age of 5 years were included. The age of 5 years was chosen as the lower quartile age by which patients with DMD initiate GC in the UK. Participants who were left censored at clinical trial enrolment and those who were never observed as ambulant were removed. GC treatment was defined as one of five subgroups: GC naïve, deflazacort daily, deflazacort intermittent (10 days on, 10 days off), prednisolone daily and prednisolone intermittent. Patients were assigned to the treatment group they spent the majority (>50%) of time on between GC initiation and loss of ambulation. Those who never initiated GC were assigned to the GC naïve group and participants with unspecified GC type or regimen at all time points were removed. Only participants who had weight and height measurements recorded in the database were included, and weight and height measurements were sense‐checked and corrected where the incorrect unit had been used (e.g., 1.06 cm → 106 cm). This occurred in <1% of assessments. Height was measured using standing height as in the Royal College of Paediatrics and Child Health guidance [22], as the cohort included only ambulant participants. Outcomes—loss of ambulation

Children typically attend clinics at an interval of every 6 months. The details of how this was adjusted for in the model are included in the supplementary information and Figure S1. Loss of ambulation was calculated as the time at which a 0 was scored on the NorthStar Ambulatory Assessment (NSAA) item 2—walk; the participant was recorded as non‐ambulant by the clinician if the NSAA was missing, or the non‐ambulant administrative form was used if neither NSAA nor clinician record was available. There was a discrepancy between the NSAA item and clinician record in 0.2% of visits. Some patients may regain ambulation after loss (e.g. if the ability was lost due to injury), but in this analysis the first loss was included. Visits after the last known ambulant visit (i.e., right‐censored visits) and participants who were non‐ambulant at all visits were removed from the study (i.e. left‐truncation).

Statistical methods

Due to the complexity of growth in childhood, each of the three measures of growth (weight, height and BMI) were converted to standard deviation scores (SD scores) using the British 1990 growth reference (UK90) [23]. Estimates of the median age at loss of ambulation for the GC groups and overall were generated via Kaplan–Meier. To understand the relationship between the longitudinal growth measures and time‐to‐event loss of ambulation, a shared parameter joint model was used [24]. As the growth SD and the rate of change of growth SD were of interest here, the models were fitted to include both as longitudinal outcomes. These were implemented in R 2022.07.2 using the ‘joint modelling’ package [25]. GC group was included in the model three times, as the effect of GC on growth SD intercept, the effect on growth SD slope and the risk of loss of ambulation. To check the validity of the model the residuals and fitted values for the marginal residuals, subject‐specific residuals and multiply imputed residuals were considered [26].

The majority of participants were treated with prednisone (66%), 22% with deflazacort and 11% were treatment naïve. The cumulative hazard of loss of ambulation was significantly increased by yearly weight SD gain, but not weight SD value. That is, a child with a yearly weight gain 0.25SD greater than an average child has a 90% (95% CI 33%, 171%) greater risk of loss of ambulation, whilst a child who is on the 90th centile and gains weight along the same centile would have no increased risk. Independent of the effect of weight gain on loss of ambulation, the prednisolone daily, prednisolone intermittent and deflazacort daily subgroups all had a significantly lower risk of loss of ambulation compared to the GC naïve group (represented by the red line in Figure 3a).

In the longitudinal height model, the deflazacort daily subgroup was significantly taller than the GC naïve group at age 5. The yearly height stunting of the deflazacort and prednisolone daily subgroups was significantly worse (respectively 0.24SD, 95% CI 0.12, 0.35, and 0.13SD, 95% CI 0.03, 0.24, shorter) than the GC naïve subgroup. The deflazacort and prednisolone intermittent subgroups were not significantly different in height trajectory compared to the GC naïve subgroup, having a height gain profile similar to the reference population. This was particularly true for the prednisolone intermittent group, whose average trajectory was between the 25th and 75th percentiles of the non‐DMD population (Figure 4b).

Reduced height gain, but not reduced height, significantly decreased the risk of loss of ambulation. Notably, a yearly height stunting of 0.25SD relative to the average height‐for‐age increase in the non‐DMD population was associated with a 74% (95% CI 14%, 167%) reduction in risk of loss of ambulation. Additionally, there was a significantly reduced risk of loss of ambulation, independent of the steroid‐related height gain reduction, in the prednisolone daily and prednisolone intermittent groups compared to the GC naïve group. That is, the prednisolone daily and prednisolone intermittent subgroups have a 60% (95% CI 22%, 79%) and 69% (95% CI 33%, 85%) lower risk of loss of ambulation respectively than the GC naïve subgroups. The prednisolone daily, prednisolone intermittent and deflazacort daily subgroups also display a yearly BMI SD gain significantly greater than the GC naïve population, with an average BMI SD yearly gain of 0.27SD (95% CI 0.17, 0.37), 0.26SD (95% CI 0.15, 0.36) and 0.21 (96% CI 0.10, 0.32) greater than the GC naïve group, respectively. The cumulative hazard of loss of ambulation is driven partially by yearly BMI SD gain, but not by BMI SD. In particular, a yearly BMI change of 0.25SD compared to an average non‐DMD individual is associated with a 66% (95% CI 5%, 163%) increase in the risk of loss of ambulation. DISCUSSION

With this study, an update is provided to the estimates of the median loss of ambulation in the UK NorthStar database, last published in 2013 [7]. To our knowledge, our study is also the first that focuses on the relationship between longitudinal growth and loss of ambulation in boys with DMD treated with GC.

The median age of loss of ambulation in the UK was described in 2013 as 12 years in the prednisolone intermittent group and 14.5 years in the prednisolone daily group [7]. Data on deflazacort were not available at that time due to the limited number of patients on this medication. In the present study, the figures on loss of ambulation were updated to 12.7 and 14.9 years for the prednisolone intermittent and daily groups, respectively. Also for the first time, the median age of loss of ambulation was presented as 12.9 and 15.8 years for the deflazacort intermittent and daily groups, respectively. No significant difference was observed between the two daily regimens by GC type. The FOR‐DMD trial of GC types and regimens in DMD observed no short‐term (3‐year) difference in motor function outcomes between daily deflazacort and prednisolone [9]. However, in other trial placebo arms, deflazacort was associated with a significantly lower rate of decline over 1 year than prednisolone in several motor function assessments [27]. In observational studies, a difference in the average age at loss of ambulation between the two GC types has been found [4], with a lower rate of loss of ambulation in the deflazacort‐treated individuals [6] and an estimated 53% reduction in the hazard of loss of ambulation in the deflazacort group compared to prednisone [16].

In this paper, time to loss of ambulation was modelled using joint modelling, which controls for time‐varying growth and GC treatment. Consequently, it was identified that growth rates were correlated with increased risk of loss of ambulation, and that adjusting for growth accounted for some of the differential effects of the GC regimens on motor function. Unsurprisingly, however, the increase in risk of loss of ambulation due to growth‐related GC side‐effects was considerably lower than the risk associated with not taking GC. One limitation of this joint modelling approach is that causal relationships cannot be identified, but instead the correlations between these variables can be discussed. In the absence of a long‐term study on the impact of GC on loss of ambulation, which would ideally include data on longitudinal muscle imaging, this observational study represents the best approach to understanding the complexity of the causal relationship between GC regimen, growth and motor function in DMD.

A higher rate of height stunting was observed in deflazacort‐treated boys in our study, which is consistent with previous observational studies [10, 15, 16, 17]. Similarly, it was also observed that participants on daily regimens were significantly shorter than those on intermittent regimens [7, 8], and those on deflazacort daily were the shortest group [28]. With this analysis, it was found that reduced height gain relative to age‐specific average height gain (but not height SD) was associated with a decreased risk of loss of ambulation. A yearly height stunting of 0.25SD (moving, for example, from the 60th to the 50th percentile, or from the 31st to the 23rd percentile in 1 year) was associated with a significant decrease in risk of loss of ambulation of 74%. For context, a trajectory of greater than 0.25SD decline per year was observed in 13% of participants, but any excess yearly height stunting was protective against loss of ambulation. In the literature, data on the impact of longitudinal growth trajectories on motor function and loss of ambulation are limited. A case report of an individual with co‐occurring growth hormone deficiency and DMD who exhibited a significantly milder phenotype than expected (ambulant beyond age 18) led to the investigation of the human growth inhibitor mazindol in boys with DMD [29], but results from clinical trials were inconclusive [30, 31]. Our results confirm that a reduced height gain compared to that expected in the general paediatric population is a protective factor from loss of ambulation.

In GC‐treated individuals, DMD‐associated higher weight is compounded by GC‐treatment‐associated weight gain [6], which has been attributed to increased appetite and sodium and fluid retention [2]. Some observational studies have identified a significantly lower percentage of body fat or lower weight gain in deflazacort‐treated individuals compared to prednisone/prednisolone [5, 9, 10, 15, 16, 32], although this finding is not universal [5, 7]. Here, a participant with a yearly weight gain of 0.25SD had a significant 90% increase in risk of loss of ambulation. To contextualize this risk, 27% of our participants were observed to have a weight gain trajectory of more than 0.25SD per year. Notably, increased weight where a participant followed their centile was not associated with an increased risk of loss of ambulation, but any weight gain trajectory was associated with an increased risk of loss of ambulation. Finally, a BMI trend consistent with a yearly gain of 0.25SD was associated with a significant increase in risk of loss of ambulation of 66%. However, absolute growth SD values did not have a significant effect on loss of ambulation risk.

It will be important to understand if there is an underlying reason why excess growth could lead to increased risk of loss of ambulation, or if a differential biological mechanism of the GC is responsible. Here, controlling for the differential effect of GC type and regimen on growth outcomes resulted in little difference in the risk of loss of ambulation between the GC groups. Although our results should be interpreted with caution, as they are not accompanied by investigations of the long‐term molecular, cellular and tissue effects of the two GC types on muscles, they may partly explain the differential effect of deflazacort over prednisolone on loss of ambulation. This is particularly pertinent as there are ongoing investigations into alternative steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs like vamorolone, which has been shown to have significantly lower rates of height stunting in DMD [33].

The observed effect of growth on loss of ambulation could potentially be due to the mechanical properties of shorter and lighter DMD boys to be more mobile and able to continue to stand up and move around. Another effect could be attributed to altered muscle physiology as the boys grow and this may contribute to their reduced motor function. Additionally, weight gain (or centile drift) in boys with DMD is probably due to increased adipose tissue rather than to increased muscle mass secondary to the anabolic effect of steroids. However, individuals who are constantly on a high weight or BMI centile might well have a higher muscle mass proportion. This could explain why only excess weight gain increased loss of ambulation risk, while increased weight centile was not significantly associated with loss of ambulation risk. Further work will therefore be needed to understand body composition and its impact on motor function in DMD.

This study has some limitations. NorthStar participants have been treated according to standard of care guidelines, with more participants being on prednisolone over the 15 years of this study, due to deflazacort only being approved for DMD in the UK in more recent years. Overall, those on prednisolone daily started GC slightly later than the other GC groups, and this is probably due to the decreasing age of GC initiation with time. Consequently, it can be argued that the difference in loss of ambulation age between deflazacort and prednisolone could be at least in part due to changes in the standard of care over time for DMD. Additionally, GC switching may be due to growth‐related side‐effects and, as such, common patterns of regimen switching could lead to participants with ‘unfavourable’ growth characteristics being systematically moved to a GC regimen that is deemed to have lower growth‐related side‐effects. In this cohort, 34 participants switched from a daily to an intermittent regimen, and 31 from intermittent to daily regimen, whilst 31 switched from prednisolone to deflazacort and none from deflazacort to prednisolone. Finally, with age it may become more difficult to accurately capture height in DMD boys due to increasing contractures and lordosis. However, the majority of these symptoms are associated with the non‐ambulant disease stage, and as such the impact on this study is probably minimal. Further work to understand the impact of dystrophin isoform expression on growth and loss of ambulation concurrently is necessary, given its published univariate impact; however, this was not possible in this analysis due to small sample sizes.

In the analyses presented, the cumulative hazard of loss of ambulation in GC naïve participants was much larger than any increased risk due to increased height, weight and BMI. These results confirm the long‐term beneficial effects of GCs, particularly when side‐effects are limited or manageable. This study can help clinicians in counselling families and adjusting therapeutic protocols with GCs. Within a wider context, the implications of short stature and excess weight gain on quality of life should not be ignored. Both excess weight gain and height stunting have been flagged as factors that are concerning for boys with DMD [34] and which could lead to low self‐esteem and difficulties socializing [35].
