Unraveling the mechanism of tetracycline-induced renal injury: an evaluation of drug safety based on network toxicology and molecular dynamics simulations
Authors: Yimao Wu, Yalun Liang, Jintao Liang, Zifeng Chen, Bo Tang, Junlong Zhu, Yifeng Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTetracyline, a widely used antibiotic, is associated with nephrotoxicity, though its underlying mechanisms require further investigation.MethodsWe investigated the toxicological process of tetracycline-induced renal injury using network toxicology, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulations. The potential targets of tetracycline were obtained by integrating target information from multiple databases, and a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was established. The key targets were then screened based on Degree and MCODE scores. Meanwhile, enrichment analysis was conducted on the potential targets. The molecular docking of the core targets and the five target proteins with the lowest binding energies was then subjected to molecular dynamics simulations.ResultsBased on in silico analyses, tetracycline is predicted to potentially induce renal injury by interacting with key targets (TP53, ESR1, BCL2, TNF, HSP90AA1). Our results also suggest it might exacerbate renal injury via AGE-RAGE, Hepatitis C, MAPK, IL-17, cancer-related pathways, and PI3K-Akt, which are involved in regulating inflammation, apoptosis, and metabolism.ConclusionCollectively, our in silico analyses propose a potential molecular mechanism of tetracycline nephrotoxicity, providing a preliminary theoretical framework for understanding its renal hazards, which requires further experimental validation.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40360-025-00991-3.

Introduction

Renal injury represents a prevalent and severe clinical condition associated with high mortality [1]. Various factorscan cause renal injury, including drug nephrotoxicity [2], hemodynamic alterations following burns, systemic inflammation, apoptosis, hemolysis, and other factors [3]. Tetracycline, a broad-spectrum antibiotic, is frequently employed in the treatment of various clinical conditions such as typical pneumonia [4], trachoma [5], and may possess anti-cancer properties [6, 7]. The application of tetracycline has expanded from the medical field to agriculture. However, its extensive use has led to environmental residues, which have become a contributing factor to pollution [8]. These residues in agricultural soils can contaminate animals and vegetables, posing a risk to our health [9]. Despite its antimicrobial efficacy, tetracycline use carries toxicity risks. Tetracycline analogs (e.g., minocycline, doxycycline) share structural similarity with tetracycline and exert nephrotoxic effects by accumulating in proximal tubule cells. This accumulation disrupts ribosomal protein synthesis, leading to renal cell damage or triggering delayed T-cell-mediated (type IV) hypersensitivity reactions, which cause interstitial inflammation [10]. Similarly, tetracycline is listed among the FDAs cautionary drugs due to its potential to cause acute renal injury [11]. Additionally, our previous studies have shown that tetracycline carries a risk of inducing acute pancreatitis [12].

The precise mechanism of tetracycline-induced renal injury remains ambiguous. To address this gap, network toxicology—an interdisciplinary field integrating systems biology, toxicology, and network analysis—provides a powerful approach. This methodology analyzes extensive biological data to investigate the toxicological processes of chemicals, predict their detrimental effects, and may enhance drug safety evaluation [13]. Molecular docking techniques employs computer simulations to assess the binding affinities and interactions between drugs and proteins, yielding critical insights into drug toxicity [14]. Furthermore, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations have been demonstrated to offer critical stability insights into protein-ligand complexes, thus overcoming the sampling limits of molecular docking and facilitating a more thorough understanding of drug-induced diseases [15].

To address challenges associated with the use of this drug in disease treatment, our study utilized network toxicology to investigate the mechanisms underlying tetracycline-induced renal injury, combined with molecular docking techniques and MD simulations to evaluate compound interactions. This integrated approach aims to provide insights for improving the clinical management of tetracycline-induced renal injury.

Methods

Preliminary analysis of tetracycline toxicity

The structural data of tetracycline was retrieved from PubChem [16] and subsequently imported into ProTox-3.0 [17] and ADMETlab 3.0 [18] for an initial prediction of tetracycline’s toxicity. See Supplementary Material 1 for specific URLs of the websites used in the study.

Selection of tetracycline targets

The standard structural formula and SMILES of the drug were obtained by querying “tetracycline” in the PubChem database [19]. Potential tetracycline targets were found using this search and tools including SwissTargetPrediction [20], the SEA Search Server, and Super-PRED [21]. Only targets with probability values greater than 0.1 and Homo sapiens are considered. After that, a thorough tetracycline target library was produced by integrating and filtering the data.

Selection of renal injury targets

Using “renal injury” as the keyword, Drugbank [22], GeneCards [23], and OMIM [24] databases were used to identify relevant targets. To establish a strong correlation between the selected genes and renal injury, we conducted further analysis of the data from the GeneCards database, employing a “score” criterion at the median value. Genes with “score” values beyond the median were later chosen to construct the renal injury target network. Due to the absence of “score” values in the DrugBank and OMIM databases and the restricted number of eligible targets, these datasets were not utilized. Additionally, Venn diagrams were used to identify potential common targets between tetracycline and renal injury. The intersections were recognized as potential targets for tetracycline-induced renal injury, and these results were visualized using the SRPLOT platform.

Building PPI networks and screening key targets

The STRING database was utilized to construct the PPI network. The network was then optimized using three conditions: the species was Homo sapiens, the “minimum required interaction score” was set to “high confidence > 0.7,” and the “FDR strictness” was adjusted to 0.7.

Subsequently, we employed Cytoscape 3.10.2 to analyze the structural properties of the network nodes and to visualize the interactions between the molecules [25]. The first PPI network consisted of 166 nodes and 2,962 edges. Core targets were identified by intersecting those with the highest 24 Degree scores and the highest 30 MCODE scores, which were subsequently visualized using the SRPLOT platform. The degree denotes the quantity of interactions a protein engages in with others and is frequently utilized to assess its importance within the network. Proteins with elevated Degree scores are typically regarded as critical nodes and may be crucial to biological processes [26]. MCODE (Molecular Complex Detection) is employed to find significant sub-networks or modules inside the PPI network, with each module signifying a cluster of functionally connected genes. We depicted the protein network according to Degree scores and subsequently employed the MCODE plugin of Cytoscape 3.10.2 to allocate scores to each target within the PPI network. The clusters with the highest MCODE scores were chosen for further visualization. The highest 30 MCODE scores were intersected with the top 24 Degree scores to identify 16 core targets, which were visualized using the SRPLOT platform [27].

Enrichment analysis of 166 intersecting targets

Two enrichment analyses were performed using the DAVID database, Gene Ontology (GO), and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG). The GO analysis has 3 parts, which are: 1. Biological Process (BP), 2. Cellular Composition (CC), 3. Molecular Function (MF) [28]. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed significant pathways associated with tetracycline and renal injury, and a P-value of less than 0.05 could be screened to obtain the major toxicity pathways of tetracycline-induced renal injury [29]. DAVID performed a GO enrichment analysis of 166 possible targets for BP (668), CC (75), MF (134). The results were shown using the SRPLOT platform. KEGG enrichment analysis was performed and 156 signaling pathways were obtained based on a P-value of less than 0.05. The twenty paths with the lowest P-values and the ten pathways with the greatest count values were chosen for display on the SRPLOT platform.

Molecular docking analysis of 16 key targets

We began a search in the PubChem database for tetracycline and acquired its ‘sdf’ structure. The structures of the 16 core proteins were obtained from the RCSB or UniProt databases. Dehydration and charge modification were executed using AutoDock software (1.5.6) [30], and the altered target proteins and associated compounds were exported as pdbqt files. Docking of 16 key proteins and tetracyclines was performed using the software AutoDock Vina, and the PyMOL software (3.1.0) displays the docking results.

MD simulation

We identified the five target proteins (TP53, ESR1, BCL2, TNF, and HSP90AA1) exhibiting the most stable binding in the molecular docking data for MD modeling. Gromacs2022 software was used to perform MD simulations using force fields for different substances: the GAFF force field (tetracycline), the AMBER14SB force field (5 core proteins), and also in combination with the TIP3P water model. The data for the 5 core proteins and tetracycline were merged together to form a complex simulation system. The simulations were carried out at stable temperature, stable pressure and conventional boundary conditions. Throughout the MD simulations, hydrogen bonding was confined by the LINCS method with an integrated time step of 2 femtoseconds. The Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method calculates the electrostatic interactions of molecules with a threshold distance of 1.2 nm. Non-bonding interactions were thresholded at 10 Å and revised every 10 iterations. The V-rescale was set to a temperature of 298 K, and Berendsen kept the pressure at 1 bar. The NVT and NPT equilibria were simulated for 100 ps at 298 K. Subsequently, MD simulations of the complex system were carried out for 100 ns, with conformations being recorded every 10 ps. At the end of the simulations, the obtained trajectories were checked using VMD and PyMOL, while the g_mmpbsa plug-in was used to evaluate the free energy of MMPBSA binding between the protein and the small molecule ligand.

RMSD analysis

Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD) is a commonly employed statistic for structural comparison, quantifies conformational differences by analyzing the spatial coordinates of corresponding atoms. During simulations, RMSD tracks structural alterations relative to the initial configuration to assess convergence stability. A lower RMSD indicates a greater structural similarity. Figure 9 A illustrates that the RMSD of the complex progressively stabilized during the simulation, indicating that the complex structure achieved gradual stabilization. Despite changes in the modeling process of the other four targets, the binding stability of small molecules and proteins was ultimately preserved. Collectively, these observations tentatively suggest that tetracycline compounds might exert prolonged effects on these targets, underpinned by a sustained interaction at the molecular level.

Rg analysis

The Radius of Gyration (Rg) provides a measure of how tightly packed the overall structure protein-small molecule complex is. A smaller Rg value indicates the molecule is more compact and densely folded, while a larger Rg suggests the structure is more spread out. As shown in Fig. 9B, the Rg of the complex remained relatively consistent throughout the simulation, indicating that the complex maintained a well-defined, compact structure, essential ofr its potential biological function, without undergoing significant unraveling or destabilization. This stability also suggests that tetracycline binds tightly across multiple potential conformations of the complex.

Buried SASA analysis

The Buried Solvent Accessible Surface Area (SASA) is a method used to assess which parts of a molecule or molecular complex are buried internally and cannot be directly reached by a solvent. A higher value for Buried SASA indicates stronger interactions between molecules and a larger area of contact.

As shown in Fig. 9E, the Buried SASA reaches and maintains a stable level over time. This indicates that the contact surface between small molecule and protein became well-defined and stable, reflecting a persistent and firm association between them.

Binding energy analysis

The conformational stability of the complex was assessed through rigorous solvation energy, RMSD, Rg, key interaction distance, buried SASA, and interaction energy. Binding energy componements were computed using the Molecular Mechanics-Poisson Boltzmann Surface Area (MM-PBSA) method as detailed in the accompanying table. The energy terms are defined as follows: ΔEele represents the electrostatic interactions between the small molecule and protein, ΔEvdw indicates the van der Waals forces, ΔEpol reflects polar solvation energy (associated with electrostatic desolvation), ΔEnonpol denotes non-polar solvation energy (primarily linked to hydrophobic effects via SASA changes). The sum of these componements yields ΔEMMPBSA, while the Gibbs binding energy (ΔGbind) combines ΔEMMPBSA, with the entropic term -TΔS.

Given that the significant computational uncertainty associated with -TΔS, this term is conventionally omitted in comparative binding energy analyses, permitting ΔEMMPBSA to serve as a practical estimate of binding energy. Additionally, the polar solvation component (ΔEpol) frequently exhibits substantial errors, prompting greater reliance on ΔEele, ΔEvdw, and ΔEnonpol for mechanistic interpretation.

Analysis of MM-PBSA results reveals consistent trends: both ΔEvdw and ΔEele contribute favorably (negative values) to binding, with |ΔEvdw| exceeding |ΔEele| in magnitude. These dominant contributions surpass the favorable but comparatively smaller |ΔEnonpol|. This suggests van der Waals interactions constitute the primary energetic driver, while electrostatic effects provide secondary stabilization, supplemented by hydrophobic contributions – though these interpretations remain constrained by MM-PBSA methodology limitations. Calculated ΔEMMPBSA values for all complexes (Supplementary Material 6) and derived affinity metrics collectively indicate variations in binding strength across the studied systems.

Conclusion of MD simulations

Small chemicals attach stably to proteins, exhibiting considerable binding energy and affinity, with the exception of TNF. The van der Waals force interaction is predominant, the electrostatic interaction is secondary, and the hydrophobic interaction is supplemental.

Discussion

This research examines the mechanisms of tetracycline-induced renal injury via network toxicology, molecular docking, and MD simulations.

TP53, a key oncogene, is sensitive to cellular stress [31]. Tetracycline disrupts protein synthesis through mechanisms including inhibition of amino acid-tRNA binding to ribosomes [32] and interference with ribosome function [33]. These disruptions might indirectly generate DNA damage, possibly resulting in the activation of TP53, which triggers cell cycle arrest and allows for DNA repair. In cases of irreversible damage, TP53 may facilitate the release of cytochrome C from mitochondria via several mechanisms. For instance, via TP53-related retrogene protein [34] and the inhibitor of inducible glycolysis and apoptosis [35], therefore activating the apoptosis-inducing CASP3 to eliminate damaged cells and mitigate the detrimental consequences of renal injury.

ESR1 encodes the estrogen receptor α (ERα), an important transcriptional regulator, mediates estrogen signaling [36]. This receptor regulates gene expression to influence cell proliferation and differentiation [37]. ESR1 plays a crucial role in the carcinogenesis of estrogen receptor-positive malignancies. Furthermore, abnormal ESR1 signaling in renal tissues may be linked to the onset or advancement of renal cell carcinoma. ESR1 may also regulate cell survival and growth, especially in conjunction with the PI3K-AKT pathway.

BCL2, an anti-apoptotic protein, inhibits formation in the mitochondrial membrane by binding to pro-apoptotic effectors BAX and BAK BCL2 [38, 39]. By preventing this pore formation, BCL2 thereby blocks cytochrome C release and subsequent activation of executioner caspases, including CASP3 [38, 39]. If tetracycline administration is found to inhibit BCL2 function, it might potentially fail to prevent BAX/BAK-mediated pore formation. This could consequently allow cytochrome C release, triggering apoptosis. In renal tissues, this apoptotic cascade may contribute to cellular damage and potentially exacerbate renal injury [40].

TNF is a key inflammatory molecule that binds to TNF receptors (TNFR), activating the NF-κB signaling pathway through protein kinase cascades. This pathway regulates expression of numerous inflammatory genes (e.g., IL-1β, IL-6), thereby amplifying the inflammatory response and exacerbating renal tissue injury. In addition, TNF can directly induce apoptosis or necrosis in renal tubular epithelial cells [41], suggesting that tetracycline may contribute to renal injury by promoting TNF expression.

Heat shock protein 90α (HSP90AA1) is a conserved chaperone protein that participates in protein signaling, folding, and degradation. It prevents the aggregation of misfolded proteins, thereby preserving cellular function under stress condition [42]. HSP90AA1 also involved in the stabilization of key signaling proteins (e.g., AKT1) [43, 44], which indirectly influences cell survival, proliferation, and stress response. If tetracycline promotes protein misfolding or denaturation intracellular, this could trigger an upregulation of HSP90AA1, which helps refold and restore activity to denatured proteins in renal tubular cells under oxidative stress.

KEGG enrichment study revealed many pathways via which tetracycline may cause renal injury. The pathways include the AGE-RAGE signaling route, IL-17 signaling pathway, Hepatitis C, MAPK signaling pathway, cancer pathways, and the PI3K-Akt signaling network.

The AGE-RAGE signaling system is involved in apoptosis and cell cycle control, and is associated with renal injury. Studies have shown that advanced glycosylation end products (AGEs) accumulate in diabetic nephropathy, contributing to renal fibrosis. Tetracycline may exacerbate renal injury by affecting this pathway [45, 46]. If tetracycline administration impairs the regulation of the AGE-RAGE pathway within renal tissues, it could exacerbate processes like inflammation or fibrosis.

IL-17, a cytokine produced by certain T cells, initiates the IL-17 signaling pathway, facilitating the recruitment and activation of inflammatory cells. This activation facilitates the recruitment and activation of neutrophils and other inflammatory cells, amplifying innate immune responses. In immunoglobulin A nephropathy (IgAN), gene expression analyses of renal biopsies have revealed involvement of inflammatory pathways, highlighting dysregulated immune responses [47]. Chronic inflammation results in renal injury, and tetracycline may exacerbate this by hyperactivating the IL-17 signaling pathway, leading to an exaggerated inflammatory response [48].

Individuals with chronic kidney disease (CKD) exhibit a greater incidence of hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection compared to the general population [49, 50]. HCV infection has been associated with the onset of renal disorders, including acute glomerulonephritis, especially in recipients of renal transplants [51]. This epidemiological association suggests a potential link between chronic HCV infection and renal pathology. Research in patients with HCV-associated glomerulonephritis indicates elevated toll-like receptor 3 (TLR3) expression within affected renal tissue This upregulation correlates with impaired renal function and increased IL-1β levels [52]. The expression of TLR3 messenger RNA was elevated in the affected cells of HCV-related renal disease, and this increase was associated with heightened production of pro-inflammatory cytokines [53]. These results indicate that TLR3 is implicated in HCV-associated glomerulonephritis (GN), possibly linking viral infection to the onset of GN [54]. Whereas interleukin-1β levels correlate with the IL-17 signaling pathway [48], tetracycline may upregulate interleukin-1β levels via the IL-17 signaling pathway, which in turn contributes to GN, i.e., renal injury.

The MAPK signaling pathway - comprising the p38MAPK, JNK (c-Jun amino-terminal kinase), and ERK (extracellular signal-regulated kinase) subfamilies - critically regulates fundamental cellular processes including growth, differentiation, and apoptosis. Echinacea polysaccharide (EP) has been demonstrated to alleviate the effects of lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced acute renal failure by significantly reducing the expression levels of phosphorylated extracellular signal-regulated protein kinase (P-Erk), phosphorylated c-Jun amino-terminal kinase (P-JNK), and P-p38, as well as inhibiting the nuclear translocation of P-ERK, P-JNK, and P-P38. Acute renal injury produced by LPS [55]. Ranunculus erythropoietin protected the kidneys of diabetic rats by modulating the MAPK/NF-κB signaling pathway via the down-regulation of p38MAPK and NF-κB p65 expression [56]. Prophylactic norelin alkaloids safeguarded the kidneys of diabetic nephropathic rats via modulating the p38MAPK pathway. Prophylactic norethindrone treatment decreased the expression of collagen IV and CD31 in the kidneys, reduced serum markers of inflammation, lessened the expression of p38MAPK, TNF-α, COX-2, and MMP-9, and mitigated biochemical changes in the blood and urine of diabetic nephropathic rats [57]. These imply that renal injury and the MAPK signaling pathway are closely related. Based on our KEGG enrichment analysis, tetracycline is predicted to potentially induce renal injury through the MAPK signaling pathway, though specific mechanistic details remain to be elucidated experimentally.

Tetracycline may cause renal injury via pathways associated with malignancy. Epidemiological studies have shown a significant correlation between renal injury and renal malignancies. It has been proposed that DNA damage initiates and the proliferation of (pre)malignant cell clones is facilitated by renal injury and repair mechanisms during acute renal injury and prolonged injury in chronic renal disease [58]. Instances of acute renal injury have been documented in individuals receiving cancer immunotherapy, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors and CAR T cell treatment [59]. These suggest a potential relationship between the pathogenesis of cancer and renal injury.

The PI3K-AKT signaling pathway is intricately linked to the pathological progression of chronic renal disease [60]. The function of renal tubular epithelial cells is a vital indicator for studying renal injury. Research suggests that hydroxychloroquine may reduce inflammatory factors and apoptotic functions in renal tubular epithelial cells by inhibiting the PI3K/AKT and NF-κB signaling pathways [61]. This protective effect provides functional evidence for the involvement of hyperactivated PI3K-AKT signaling in renal injury pathogenesis. Furthermore, HIF1-α and mTOR within the PI3K-AKT pathway are crucial elements in the regulation of apoptosis and autophagy [62].

This study relies solely on computational simulations and bioinformatics, lacking direct experimental validation. Additionally, findings are limited by the completeness of databases (e.g., PubChem, STRING), the 100 ns time scale of molecular dynamics simulations (insufficient for long-term biological interactions), and potential discrepancies between in silico predictions and in vivo physiological complexity (e.g., cellular microenvironment, biomolecular crosstalk). Future work should include in vitro cytotoxicity assays and in vivo nephrotoxicity modeling to verify TP53/BCL2-mediated apoptosis and TNF-driven inflammation, combined with pathway-specific inhibitor interventions (e.g., PI3K/MAPK inhibitors) and genetic manipulations (siRNA silencing/overexpression) to functionally validate core targets and pathways. We should also explore the differences in the effects of different tetracycline analogs, dosages, and exposure times on renal injury, using metabolomics to uncover metabolic reprogramming linked to AGE-RAGE/PI3K-Akt pathways, as well as the effects of individual differences, co-administration, and other factors on nephrotoxicity, in order to assess the wide range of effects of tetracycline on human renal health in different clinical scenarios, and to provide a more complete and reliable basis for the safety of clinical drug use.

Conclusion

This study used network toxicology, molecular docking tools, and MD modeling to systematically investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying tetracycline-induced renal injury. We identified five potential targets—TP53, ESR1, BCL2, TNF, and HSP90AA1. These targets might be critically involved in renal injury—by combining data from several databases and building a PPI network. These targets modulate essential biological processes including cell cycle regulation, inflammatory responses, apoptosis, and cell survival. Notably, while existing literature lacks conclusive evidence regarding substantial intrinsic nephrotoxicity of tetracycline itself, GO and KEGG analyses revealed that tetracycline-induced renal injury involves synergistic activation of AGE-RAGE, IL-17, MAPK, PI3K-Akt, Hepatitis C, and cancer-related pathways. This suggests that tetracycline might potentially synergize with other signaling pathways, such as inflammation, apoptosis and metabolic disorders, to induce renal injury. Particular caution is warranted given the documented nephrotoxic potential of structural analogs (e.g., minocycline) and pharmacologically related compounds (e.g., polymyxins) under specific clinical conditions [10, 40, 63].

Our work provides a preliminary methodological framework for evaluating the nephrotoxicity of tetracycline, delivering three critical contributions: novel mechanistic insights into tetracycline-induced kidney injury, a predictive model for drug safety assessment, and structural validation of tetracycline-target interactions through molecular docking to inform rational drug design. Collectively, these findings might contribute to advancing the understanding of tetracycline nephrotoxicity while providing a theoretical foundation for developing targeted prevention and therapeutic strategies.
