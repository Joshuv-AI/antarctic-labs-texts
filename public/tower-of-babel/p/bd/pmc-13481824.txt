Prior Authorization for Sacral Nerve Stimulation in the Era of WISeR: Evaluation of U.S. Payer Coverage Policies
Authors: Thriaksh Rajan, Andre Revnew, David Sheyn, Adonis Hijaz, Ramy Abou Ghayda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionSacral nerve stimulation (SNS) is an established therapy for refractory urinary incontinence, yet the 2025 Wasteful and Inappropriate Service Reduction Model (WISeR) designated it as requiring mandatory prior authorization. No analysis has examined how U.S. payers translate SNS evidence into coverage policies or whether restrictions are evidence‐based. We sought to: (1) compare covered and excluded indications, (2) evaluate restriction criteria and conservative therapy requirements, and (3) assess quality of evidence cited.MethodsThis was a systematic, retrospective analysis of publicly available SNS coverage policies. Independent reviewers extracted data from 11 major U.S. payers, including Medicare. Data included indications, contraindications, implantation criteria, and cited references. References were classified using Oxford Centre for Evidence‐Based Medicine hierarchy into Levels I–V or “Other” (guidelines, technology assessments, regulatory documents).ResultsAll payers covered core urinary indications, while 63.6% excluded neurologic disease. All required conservative therapy failure for trial, but specificity varied from specific drug combinations to voiding diaries. All required symptomatic improvement for permanent implantation; 90.9% specified > 50% improvement. References ranged from N = 15–112 per policy. While 90.9% cited Level I evidence, proportions ranged from 0% to 31.6%. Five payers had > 50% of references classified as “Other;” only Medicare had majority primary evidence (89.5%).DiscussionWhile payers agree on core urinary indications, coverage for neurologic conditions is inconsistently defined. Because FDA labeling does not endorse SNS for neurologic etiologies such as spinal cord injury, variable exclusions are understandable yet create confusion for clinicians navigating prior authorization. Wide variation in therapy and documentation requirements highlights lack of transparency as WISeR expands.Level of EvidenceLevel III.Level of EvidenceLevel IV.

Introduction

In March 2025, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services under the Trump administration launched the Wasteful and Inappropriate Service Reduction Model (WISeR), a Medicare pilot program using artificial intelligence to screen prior authorization requests for over a dozen high‐cost elective procedures across six states [1, 2]. Sacral nerve stimulation (SNS) for urinary incontinence was prominently featured at the top of the procedures requiring mandatory prior authorization [1, 3]. The 2021 OIG report identified more than 40% billing non‐compliance for neurostimulator implantations, prompting CMS inclusion of SNS in WISeR's AI‐based prior authorization pilot. This policy targets high‐cost, variable procedures representing around 25% of national health expenditures deemed potentially wasteful [4]. While the program excludes emergency services and treatments posing substantial patient risk if delayed, this policy shift places SNS, a well‐established therapy for refractory urinary incontinence, under unprecedented administrative scrutiny [5]. The clinical significance is substantial: urinary incontinence affects approximately 25%–45% of women and 5%–15% of men in the United States, with SNS representing a critical therapeutic option for the estimated 30%–40% of patients who fail conservative management and are unsuitable for or decline more invasive surgical interventions [6, 7]. The new prior authorization requirements may create access barriers for millions of Americans seeking definitive treatment for a condition that profoundly impacts quality of life, employment, social participation, and mental health [8].

The evidence supporting SNS for refractory urinary incontinence has evolved considerably since FDA approval in 1997, with multiple randomized controlled trials and systematic reviews demonstrating efficacy for urge incontinence, urgency‐frequency syndrome, and non‐obstructive urinary retention [9, 10]. Previous ICS guidelines recommended a stepwise progression through behavioral and pharmacologic therapy before procedural interventions such as SNS were considered; however, the systematic evidence review underlying the 2024 AUA/SUFU guideline did not support this mandatory “step therapy” model [11, 12]. In accordance with the updated 2024 AUA/SUFU guidelines, clinicians no longer follow strict step therapy for SNS (such as requiring prior failure of behavioral and pharmacologic interventions) and instead utilize shared decision‐making to select the therapy that best fits the patient's lifestyle, symptom severity, and tolerance, with treatment options now grouped by approach rather than sequence [12]. Despite two decades of Level I and II evidence supporting SNS efficacy, it remains unclear how major payers operationalize this evidence into coverage policies, what restrictions they impose on access, and whether their policy decisions are grounded in high‐quality scientific evidence. The implementation of WISeR and expanded prior authorization requirements intensifies the urgency of understanding existing payer policy landscapes. Physicians, patients, and healthcare systems navigate increasingly complex administrative pathways to access established therapies, raising concerns about whether coverage barriers are evidence‐based or primarily cost‐driven.

A comprehensive review of payer coverage policies for SNS would provide critical insights into variations in coverage criteria, restriction requirements, and the quality of evidence supporting policy decisions across the diverse U.S. insurance landscape, including national commercial payers, regional insurers, integrated delivery networks, and federal programs. Therefore, this study aimed to: (1) identify and compare covered and excluded indications for SNS across major U.S. health insurance payers, (2) evaluate the restriction criteria and conservative therapy requirements that patients must satisfy before trial and permanent implantation, and (3) assess the level and quality of scientific evidence cited by payers to support their coverage policies using established evidence hierarchy frameworks. Systematically mapping these policy variations can help clinicians anticipate payer‐specific authorization requirements, aid patients in appealing denials, and inform policymakers about whether existing restrictions align with evidence‐based care or primarily reflect administrative cost containment.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This study was a retrospective review of publicly available health insurance payer coverage policies for SNS in the United States. The methodology was adapted from previous literature reviews of payer coverage policies in orthopedic surgery, plastic surgery, and rheumatology [13, 14, 15]. Two independent reviewers conducted the policy identification, data extraction, and evidence classification between June 1, 2025 and September 1, 2025. Any discrepancies in data extraction or evidence classification were resolved through consensus discussion, with adjudication by a senior investigator when necessary.

Payer Selection and Policy Identification

Major U.S. health insurance payers were identified from the AMA, including national commercial payers, regional insurers, integrated delivery networks, and the federal Medicare program [16]. Specifically, the candidate payer pool was drawn from the 10 largest national insurers by commercial market share identified in the AMA's 2023 Competition in Health Insurance report, supplemented by additional payers with substantial Medicare Advantage, Medicaid managed care, or integrated delivery network market share (Humana, Centene, UPMC, and Geisinger) and the federal Medicare program, yielding a candidate pool of 15 payers [16]. Eligible payers were required to have publicly accessible medical coverage policies or clinical guidelines specific to SNS or neuromodulation for urinary incontinence available as of September 1, 2025. Payers were excluded if their coverage policies were not publicly available, required subscriber login credentials for access, or did not provide sufficient detail regarding coverage criteria and evidence supporting policy decisions. Policies were identified via payer websites using standardized search strings (“sacral neuromodulation,” “urinary incontinence,” “coverage policy”). Policies requiring subscriber login were excluded. State Medicaid programs were not included in this analysis, as Medicaid coverage criteria for SNS are determined independently by each state Medicaid agency and, in many states, by individual contracted Medicaid managed care organizations, resulting in up to 50 distinct and frequently non‐public sets of coverage criteria that were beyond the scope of this study. Notably, several of the national payers included here (e.g., UHG, Centene, CVS/Aetna, Anthem) also administer Medicaid managed care plans, though typically under separate, state‐specific policy documents not captured by this review.

Data Extraction

For each payer, the following data elements were systematically extracted from publicly available coverage policy documents: payer name and type (national, regional, integrated delivery network, or federal), date of last policy update, covered indications for SNS, explicitly excluded indications, absolute contraindications to coverage, relative contraindications to coverage, requirements for trial implantation eligibility, requirements for permanent implantation eligibility, and all bibliographic references cited in support of the coverage policy. Policies were found by web scraping for official policies from the insurer's website. Only policies that were on sacral nerve stimulation and urinary incontinence were included. Policies that mentioned sacral nerve stimulation but were focused solely on other indications (e.g., fecal incontinence) were not included. Insurers whose policies were behind a paywall, required provider authorization, or who used external care guidelines (e.g., MCG clinical guidelines) as their official policy were excluded due to the inability to access these policies. Data were extracted independently by two reviewers using a standardized data extraction form developed a priori.

Evidence Quality Assessment

All references cited in each payer's coverage policy document were classified according to the Oxford Centre for Evidence‐Based Medicine (OCEBM) 2011 Levels of Evidence hierarchy [17]. References were categorized as: Level I (systematic reviews and meta‐analyses of randomized controlled trials), Level II (randomized controlled trials or observational studies with dramatic effect), Level III (non‐randomized controlled cohort or follow‐up studies), Level IV (case‐series, case‐control studies, or historically controlled studies), Level V (mechanism‐based reasoning), or “Other” (clinical practice guidelines, health technology assessments, regulatory documents, textbooks, internal assessments, non‐clinical documents, point‐of‐care reference tools, manufacturer documentation, and consensus statements). Each reference was independently classified by two reviewers based on study design and methodology as described in the citation or, when necessary, by reviewing the original source document. Discrepancies in evidence level classification were resolved through consensus discussion. The proportion of references in each evidence level category was calculated for each payer to assess the overall quality of evidence supporting coverage policies. Inter‐rater reliability for evidence‐level classification was quantified using Cohen's κ coefficient.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize payer characteristics, covered and excluded indications, restriction criteria, and evidence quality metrics. Continuous variables (number of references cited, number of covered indications, number of excluded indications) were reported as counts and ranges. No comparative statistical testing was performed because this was a descriptive cross‐sectional analysis of payer policies, not a hypothesis‐testing study. All data management and analysis were performed using Microsoft Excel version 16.0 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA).

Ethical Considerations

This study was granted exemption from institutional review board approval as the analysis examined publicly available policy documents and did not involve human subjects, human‐derived data, or protected health information. No patient consent was required. This study adhered to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for transparent reporting of observational studies [18].

Results

Payers and Indications for Sacral Nerve Stimulation Devices

The final review included 11 health insurance payers: United Health Group (UHG), Anthem, Centene, Humana, CVS/Aetna, Health Care Service Corporation (HCSC), Cigna, Highmark, University of Pittsburgh Medical Center (UPMC), Geisinger, and Medicare. These payers represented national (N = 5), regional (N = 3), integrated delivery network (N = 2), and federal (N = 1) insurance models (Table 1). Among 11 payers, coverage was universal for urge incontinence, urgency/frequency, and non‐obstructive retention, but inconsistent for neurogenic etiologies (2 of 11 payers, 18%). Such heterogeneity may lead to differential access for up to 15%–20% of potential SNS candidates with neurogenic bladder, based on epidemiologic estimates. Ten payers (90.9%) covered fecal incontinence, an FDA‐approved indication as of 2020; however, Medicare was the exception due to lacking a specific National Coverage Determination. Two payers (18.2%), Anthem and Geisinger, explicitly covered neurogenic bladder secondary to spinal cord injury.

Seven payers (63.6%) excluded dysfunction secondary to neurologic disease or injury. Four payers (36.4%) excluded stress incontinence. Three payers (27.3%) explicitly excluded chronic constipation within their urinary incontinence policies; however, several others addressed constipation separately in dedicated fecal incontinence policies, suggesting broader exclusion in practice. Similarly, two payers (18.2%) explicitly excluded chronic pelvic pain. UPMC had the most extensive exclusion list (N = 6), while Anthem had no excluded indications.

Restriction Criteria for Sacral Nerve Stimulation Coverage in Urinary Incontinence

Across payers, conservative‐therapy requirements ranged from broad, like failure of conservative management, to prescriptive. Table 2 summarizes heterogeneity in behavioral and pharmacologic prerequisites. Such variability creates non‐clinical delays in access, emphasizing the importance of payer‐specific documentation templates. Five payers (45.5%) required patients to be ≥ 18 years old (Table 2). Four payers (36.4%) listed pregnancy as an absolute contraindication. Three payers (27.3%) explicitly listed mechanical urinary obstruction as an exclusion; however, nearly all effectively limited coverage to non‐obstructive urinary retention, thereby excluding obstructive etiologies de facto. Three payers (27.3%) excluded patients who were unable to operate the device. Two payers (18.2%) explicitly excluded pathologies from neurologic disease origins. Five payers (45.5%) listed no absolute contraindications.

Discussion

SNS has emerged as an important therapeutic option for refractory urinary incontinence, yet recent WISeR policy changes mandating prior authorization have intensified scrutiny of payer coverage policies [19]. In fact, on January 1, 2026 WISeR launched as planned across its six pilot states, and CMS has subsequently updated its WISeR Provider and Supplier Operational Guide to clarify coding and documentation requirements specifically for permanent SNS implantation, underscoring the continued evolution of administrative requirements for this therapy. This study qualitatively and quantitively evaluated coverage policies of 11 major U.S. health insurance payers to identify variations in covered indications, restriction criteria, and quality of scientific evidence supporting these policies. Our analysis reveals that while payers demonstrate broad consensus on core urinary indications for SNS, substantial heterogeneity exists in exclusion criteria (particularly for patients with neurologic pathology), conservative therapy requirements, and reliance on lower‐quality evidence sources. These findings suggest patients may face inconsistent access to SNS depending on their insurance carrier despite similar clinical presentations. Clinicians can use these insights to better navigate prior authorization by understanding specific payer requirements for conservative therapy documentation and trial stimulation thresholds. Policymakers should consider standardizing evidence‐based coverage criteria to ensure equitable access while maintaining appropriate utilization controls.

Payers and Indications for Sacral Nerve Stimulation Devices

Universal coverage (100%) for core urinary indications—urge incontinence, urgency/frequency, and non‐obstructive retention—reflects robust evidence supporting SNS efficacy and FDA approval pathways establishing these as standard indications [20]. Policies were relatively current (last updated August 2023–May 2025), suggesting active maintenance and responsiveness to evolving evidence. Notably, no payer specified preferred devices, maintaining technology neutrality for clinician selection based on patient‐specific factors.

However, 63.6% of payers excluded neurogenic bladder secondary to neurologic disease, despite two payers (Anthem and Geisinger) explicitly covering neurogenic bladder from spinal cord injury—a critical inconsistency with profound coding and coverage implications. This discordance may reflect lingering uncertainty regarding efficacy in neurologically compromised patients or efforts to constrain utilization in higher‐cost populations, despite landmark SNS studies including patients with neurogenic voiding dysfunction and emerging evidence showing benefit in selected neurologic populations [21, 22]. The 36.4% exclusion of stress incontinence aligns with clinical guidelines and limited evidence for SNS efficacy in pure stress incontinence. However, it does not address patients with mixed incontinence, a combination of urge and stress incontinence symptoms, which accounts for 33% of all incontinence in women [23].

Fecal incontinence coverage by 90.9% of payers, but lacking a Medicare National Coverage Determination, creates a two‐tiered access system disproportionately affecting older adults. This gap is concerning, given the significant overlap between urinary and fecal incontinence in many patients, suggesting Medicare policy lags behind private payer standards [24]. Furthermore, UPMC's extensive exclusion list (six exclusions) contrasts with Anthem's absence of exclusions, highlighting how integrated delivery networks may adopt more restrictive policies reflecting local practice patterns rather than evidence‐based determinations.

Critically, clinicians should be aware that coding decisions can directly influence authorization outcomes; for instance, inclusion of neurologic comorbidities may inadvertently trigger denial despite equivalent therapeutic benefit. This facilitates incentives for under‐coding comorbidities important for surgical planning and risk stratification. Surgeons should clearly document specific covered indications, understand which diagnostic codes trigger exclusions, and when appropriate, emphasize primary urologic pathology over secondary neurologic contributors. Payers should harmonize neurologic disease exclusions with emerging evidence and FDA‐approved indications, and clarify whether exclusions apply to all neurologic comorbidities or only cases where neurologic disease is the primary etiology.

Restriction Criteria for Sacral Nerve Stimulation Coverage in Urinary Incontinence

Absolute contraindications demonstrate reasonable clinical judgment in most cases, age restrictions (45.5% requiring ≥ 18 years), pregnancy exclusions (36.4%), and mechanical obstruction exclusions (27.3%) align with established surgical principles. However, excluding patients unable to operate devices (27.3%) or maintain voiding diaries (9.1%) raises equity concerns, potentially excluding patients with cognitive impairment, limited health literacy, or disabilities who might otherwise benefit [25]. The variation in absolute contraindications—45.5% listing none versus others having extensive lists—suggests that some payers apply broader clinical discretion. In contrast, others use more detailed eligibility criteria that may reflect utilization management priorities as much as evidence‐based decision‐making. Notably, plans that do not list absolute contraindications may still impose them by stating that only the indications explicitly listed in their policies are covered. Stricter criteria serve dual purposes: they legitimize patient selection based on the likelihood of benefit and also manage utilization by reducing procedural volume and costs for high‐cost implantable devices.

Hundred percent of payers require failure of conservative management before trial implantation. Still, specificity varies dramatically, from Cigna's general “failure of conservative medical management” to CVS/Aetna's particular pharmacologic requirements (at least two different anticholinergics or an anticholinergic plus mirabegron) and UPMC's requirement for failure of BOTH behavioral and pharmacologic interventions. This variability increases administrative burden but, when applied consistently, may also support shared decision‐making across the spectrum of non‐invasive to minimally invasive therapies prior to SNS [26]. Centene's 6‐month symptom duration and Anthem's 2–3 month conservative therapy trial represent evidence‐based approaches balancing adequate therapeutic trials with timely access. Understanding specific payer requirements enables prospective documentation to meet criteria before authorization submission, thereby expediting approvals.

Permanent implantation criteria show remarkable consistency, with 90.9% requiring > 50% symptom improvement, a threshold supported by seminal SNS trials and FDA pivotal study endpoints [27, 28]. Yet this relies on patient‐reported outcomes, which are inherently subjective. Payers increasingly require objective voiding diary documentation (18.2% for permanent implantation) instead of relying on patient or clinician attestation, which introduces logistical challenges for healthcare systems implementing standardized diary collection.

Geisinger's failure to specify quantitative thresholds introduces subjectivity risking inconsistent decisions. The 18.2% requiring adequate voiding diary documentation is clinically sound but disadvantages vulnerable populations and creates operational burdens. Clinicians should maintain detailed documentation of conservative therapy attempts, including specific dates, medications, dosages, and reasons for discontinuation, and implement standardized voiding diary protocols during trial stimulation [29]. Level of Scientific Evidence in Sacral Nerve Stimulation Policies

Wide variation in total references cited, from 15 (Highmark) to 112 (Humana), raises questions about the depth of evidence review underlying coverage determinations. While 90.9% cited some Level I evidence, proportions ranged from 0% to only 31.6%, suggesting payers rarely rely primarily on high‐quality evidence despite its availability.

A fundamental flaw across nearly all policies is poor citation practice: references are listed in end bibliographies without specific in‐text citations linked to particular coverage criteria, making it difficult to determine which evidence supports which policy elements. This practice obscures how evidence informs specific coverage elements, undermining transparency on which claims are evidence‐supported versus based on cost considerations or administrative convenience. While acceptable in internal administrative documents, this lack of direct citation linkage is not seen in peer‐reviewed literature and undermines transparency in policies that substantially affect patient access [30]. Adoption of standardized citation mapping analogous to PRISMA for systematic reviews could have improved reproducibility of payer policy development.

Medicare's 31.6% Level I evidence and 0% “Other” references demonstrate that evidence‐based policy development is achievable, suggesting that federal payers maintain more rigorous standards. Universal Level II evidence citation (100%) reflects robust RCT literature including pivotal InterStim trials, yet relatively low proportions (2.7%–46.7%) indicate even RCT data doesn't dominate most policies.

45.5% of payers deriving over half their references from “other categories,” including guidelines, health technology assessments, and regulatory documents rather than primary clinical evidence. Consistent reliance on clinical guidelines (AUA/SUFU, ACOG, NICE, ASCRS) provides standardized recommendations payers adopt with minimal internal review [31]. Technology assessments from Hayes, AHRQ, and NICE are widely incorporated as intermediaries between primary literature and policy decisions. Regulatory documents (FDA approvals, PMAs, SSEDs) reflect the importance of device‐specific evidence. Some payers (UHG, Humana, UPMC) supplement policies with point‐of‐care tools (UpToDate, StatPearls) designed for clinician education rather than policy development, while others (Geisinger) use internal reports, newsletters, and patient FAQs, highlighting heterogeneity in evidence rigor. Cochrane reviews appear unevenly, more often in Anthem, Centene, and CVS/Aetna policies.

While guidelines efficiently synthesize evidence, over‐reliance may perpetuate outdated recommendations or introduce guideline author biases [32]. Payers may make coverage decisions based on evidence interpretations rather than evidence itself, potentially delaying adoption when primary literature supports use but guidelines lag [33]. This is particularly relevant where SNS requires prior authorization within WISeR. If criteria are based on non‐primary evidence sources, patients face barriers rooted in administrative interpretation rather than clinical reality.

Collectively, payers rely on core guidelines and technology assessments but diverge substantially in their reliance on narrative reviews, educational tools, or internal documents, which are important for understanding variability and transparency in evidence appraisal. Professional societies should engage directly with payers ensuring policies reflect current high‐quality evidence. Payers should commit to periodic evidence reviews, prioritize Level I and II evidence, implement transparent in‐text citation practices linking specific evidence to specific policy elements, and publicly disclose evidence bases for external validation.

Limitations

This analysis examined publicly available coverage policies, which may not reflect actual implementation involving medical director review, peer‐to‐peer discussions, or case‐by‐case exceptions. Additionally, only 11 payers were included. However, these 11 payers represent many of the major insurers in the U.S. Consequently, these findings may not generalize to self‐insured employers or to state Medicaid programs, which set SNS coverage criteria independently at the state level (often through contracted managed care organizations) and were beyond the scope of this analysis; future studies could systematically evaluate state Medicaid SNS coverage policies and their alignment with the commercial and federal policies described here. Policies requiring subscriber login were excluded, introducing possible selection bias favoring transparent payers. Actual authorization decisions may also incorporate case‐specific clinical review by medical directors, which may not be reflected in the publicly available written policies analyzed. Additionally, our analysis focused on conditions explicitly listed within payer policies. However, unlisted indications may be implicitly excluded in practice, which could underestimate the true breadth of restrictions. However, publicly available policies represent the stated standard providers should navigate, and differences in written policies likely translate to broader access differences. Evidence level classification relied on OCEBM criteria applied to payer‐presented citations without independent verification of citation accuracy. This is partially addressed by focusing on which evidence levels payers chose to cite rather than evaluating citation validity, and observed patterns remain valid regardless of individual citation accuracy. The analysis is limited to policies last updated between August 2023 and May 2025, potentially missing subsequent changes. Despite this, the analysis captures policies concurrent with recent legislative attention to WISeR prior authorization, making it highly relevant to current debates. The systematic methodology provides a framework for longitudinal policy tracking.

Conclusion

This review demonstrates that most major U.S. payer coverage policies for SNS are broadly aligned on core urinary indications and general eligibility criteria. However, notable variability persists in specific domains—including coverage of neurologic conditions, details of conservative therapy requirements, and the quality and transparency of evidence cited to support policy decisions. Professional medical societies should engage directly with payers to promote clearer, evidence‐based standardization of criteria. At the same time, healthcare systems should develop structured prior authorization toolkits to document conservative therapy attempts and voiding diary data prospectively. As the WISeR program expands prior authorization for SNS across multiple states, policymakers should ensure that coverage criteria remain clinically justified and do not inadvertently create administrative barriers to appropriate care. Future research should track how payer policies evolve following WISeR implementation and assess whether differences in coverage criteria meaningfully impact patient access or outcomes.
