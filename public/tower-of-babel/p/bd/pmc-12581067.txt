High-risk neuroblastoma as a model for immunotherapy of non-immunogenic cancers: where might we be in 20 years?
Authors: Paul M Sondel, Alexander L Rakhmilevich, Israrul H Ansari, Amy K Erbe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract Children and adults diagnosed with malignancies that are not curable in 2025 with surgery alone require multimodal therapy. For decades, this has included radiation therapy and/or chemotherapy. This approach can be curative for many patients with certain cancers, such as acute lymphoblastic leukemia, Wilms tumor, Hodgkin’s disease, and testicular cancer. For those who are not being cured, additional therapies are needed. The last 15 years have shown that those previously incurable patients are now having a chance for a cure using immunotherapy in the form of immune checkpoint inhibition (ICI). However, with the exception of rare patients with mutations in their DNA repair pathways associated with very high neoantigen loads, most patients receiving ICI therapy are not cured by it. This pertains to virtually all patients with some tumors, such as prostate or gastrointestinal (GI) cancers and even for a majority of those patients with advanced cancers classified as “immunogenic”, such as melanoma, renal cell cancer, and lung cancer. One feature of these many patients not responding to ICI is that their tumors are immunologically cold. Namely, they are characterized by low actionable neoantigen load, low or absent major histocompatibility complex expression, and/or an immune-hostile tumor microenvironment. These features are typical of high-risk neuroblastoma (HR-NBL). Despite its cold features, substantial progress has been made in advancing the treatment of HR-NBL using different forms of immunotherapy. This manuscript describes a clinical goal for HR-NBL for 20 years from now. In addition, it extrapolates from this experience and anticipated progress for HR-NBL to how these concepts might apply to improving treatments for patients with more common, yet cold, cancers of adults.Keywords: Antibody, Combination therapy, Chimeric antigen receptor - CAR, Immunotherapy, Chemotherapy

Introduction: features of HR-NBL are similar to ICI-non-responsive cancers in children and adults

Neuroblastoma (NBL) is the most common extracranial solid tumor diagnosed in children. Nearly half of these patients have high-risk NBL (HR-NBL), which in 2010 had a 5-year event-free survival (EFS) of~35%.1,7 Incorporation of immunotherapy increased the 5-year EFS to over 50%, with an encouraging event-free survival of 75% in smaller studies that involved additional immunotherapy.8 9 Like patients with adult cancers that are not responding to combination therapy regimens and/or immune checkpoint inhibition (ICI), most patients with HR-NBL have low tumor mutation burden, low actionable neoantigen levels, low or no expression of major histocompatibility complex (MHC), and an immune-hostile tumor microenvironment. They do not respond to ICI.5 Even though antigens expressed on HR-NBL are somewhat distinct from those of other pediatric or adult cancers, the immunologic features of these cold tumors have some similarities. Most of these distinct tumor types are susceptible to antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC) in vitro.10

Current NBL therapy

The current standard of care for HR-NBL has been determined by a rigorous series of randomized clinical trials, conducted in parallel by the Children’s Oncology Group (COG) and by the collaborative European Neuroblastoma Group (SIOPEN) consortium. These groups have learned from one another as their therapeutic strategies and regimens are similar. Newly diagnosed COG patients receive a highly aggressive 5 months of induction combination chemotherapy and surgical resection of residual disease. Children who have shown at least a partial or better response proceed to “consolidation” treatment of tandem autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation following myeloablative combination chemotherapy, using different ablative drugs in the two transplants.11 Following recovery, children receive local radiotherapy to the prior tumor bed and also undergo 6 months of post-consolidation treatment with the differentiating agent isotretinoin in combination with five courses of dinutuximab, an anti-GD2 monoclonal antibody (mAb) and granulocyte-macrophage colony stimulating factor (GM-CSF).12 For eligible patients in this Phase-III COG study (namely the~70% of patients with newly diagnosed HR-NBL that achieved complete response (CR) or very-good partial response following induction), the inclusion of the post-consolidation anti-GD2 antibody substantially increased overall survival (OS) and EFS (to 56.6% EFS after 5 years), with few patients relapsing after 5 years.13 14 For those who remain disease-free, many experience long-term side effects from the genotoxic high-dose chemotherapy and radiation therapy received, associated with life-threatening and life-altering toxicities.15

Furthermore, ~50% of patients initially treated with this full regimen do not have EFS because of cancer that is either refractory to initial therapy or that initially responds but ultimately relapses. In the past, virtually all patients with relapsed disease died from the disease, and only a few showed substantial responses to salvage therapy for relapsed-refractory disease.16 In 2017, the COG published a small randomized trial for patients with relapsed-refractory disease using two chemotherapy drugs, temozolomide and irinotecan, given either with a third chemo agent (temsirolimus) or with dinutuximab. The results were striking, with the dinutuximab group having a 53% overall response rate as opposed to a 6% response rate for the chemotherapy group.17 Confirmatory studies have shown that this salvage regimen has an overall response rate of 42%; ~1/4 of the responders (~10% of all treated patients) exhibit a complete and durable response with no recurrence for five or more years.18 19 This has become the standard of care for relapsed-refractory disease. Combining these findings from trials that include anti-GD2 in both up-front therapy and in salvage therapy reflects roughly a 60% OS for patients with HR-NBL. The futuristic perspective below summarizes where we may hope to be heading in the next 20 years to improve these results.

How might we get there?

Front-line therapy

Given the success of adding anti-GD2 mAb+GM-CSF to chemotherapy as a salvage approach, St. Jude researchers showed that the addition of anti-GD2 mAb to the initial induction chemotherapy enabled a doubling of the overall response rate after the first two courses of chemotherapy when compared with historic data for that same chemotherapy given without anti-GD2 mAb.20 Furthermore, when induction anti-GD2 mAb is added to the proven use of anti-GD2 mAb in the post-consolidation phase (following bone marrow transplant), the overall cancer-free 3-year survival increases to 74%.8 The COG is now testing this addition of anti-GD2 mAb during the induction phase in a large, randomized trial that is expected to confirm the striking results obtained from St. Jude.21 Analyses of patients with relapsed or refractory disease following treatment with anti-GD2 mAb therapy have identified patients with a downregulation of GD2, or at times loss of GD2 expression on their NBL.22 While the GD2 antigen may be lost, other targets for anti-NBL antibodies are still well expressed on these tumors. Therefore, including additional anti-NBL antibodies such as anti-GM2, anti-B7H3, or anti-GFRA2 mAb as part of the induction immunotherapy with anti-GD2 mAb might well provide potent coverage of heterogeneous tumor cells downregulating GD2 antigen.23,25 This should enable a much greater fraction of patients to finish induction therapy with a CR, with no residual detectable tumor cells. Any subsequent relapse would be from residual microscopic amounts of cancer cells that are resistant to the initial therapy.

The role of GM-CSF in this regimen remains controversial. Preclinical data indicate that the use of GM-CSF not only increases ADCC in vitro, but augments in vivo antitumor effects of anti-GD2 mAb in vivo, likely via ADCC.26 Clinical data looking at results from sequential clinical trials are consistent with a beneficial role for adding GM-CSF to treatment with the 3F8 anti-GD2 mAb;27 however, this was not a randomized controlled comparison. As GM-CSF is available in the USA and was part of the Phase-III testing with dinutuximab, GM-CSF is part of the US Food and Drug Administration approved regimen for the post-consolidation use of dinutuximab.9,14 As GM-CSF is not readily available in Europe, the SIOPEN group has been giving dinutuximab-beta, an mAb with the same amino acid sequence as dinutuximab, but produced in chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells rather than SP2 cells, without concomitant GM-CSF and obtaining similar antitumor efficacy as that seen by COG using dinutuximab with GM-CSF.13 No randomized trial has been done comparing antitumor efficacy for anti-GD2 mAb treatment given with versus without GM-CSF.

Once CR is achieved (during or after induction) may be the best time for the use of chimeric antigen receptor T cells (CAR T cells) as consolidation. Preclinical work is demonstrating improved efficacy via modulating the immune-hostile tumor microenvironment prior to administration of CAR T cells.28 Recent work with “3rd generation” anti-GD2 CAR T cells has shown a substantial response rate of 63% for patients with relapsed or refractory disease.29 In this study, the patients that did not respond (or that relapsed) did not show a loss or decrease of GD2 expression (in contrast to the frequent loss of the CD19 epitope seen in patients with acute lymphoblastic leukemia that recur following anti-CD-19 CAR-T cells.30 Importantly, in this NBL study, patients receiving CAR T cells in the face of low, rather than bulky, tumor burdens had a greater chance for response, with a substantial fraction of these patients remaining cancer-free for years.29 This suggests that several possible mechanisms may be interfering with CAR-T efficacy in the face of larger tumors: these may include immunosuppressive lower pH, immunosuppressive immune cells (T regulatory cells and myeloid-derived suppressive cells), immunosuppressive cytokines (interleukin-10), collagen and vascular barriers, as well as the sheer number of tumor cells potentially being too large for control by the number of non-exhausted CAR-T cells that can amass in the growing tumor.31 As such, the best time for the use of CAR T cells may be for newly diagnosed patients who have achieved CR from the initial induction chemoimmunotherapy and surgery at a time when the tumor burden is microscopic. This concept is being tested for childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia soon after achieving CR.32 A single dose of CAR T cells may be most effective as soon as patients with NBL achieve CR, and it may prove curative in eliminating any residual persistent NBL cells. If so, this might prevent the need for consolidation autologous stem-cell transplant or post-consolidation phases. To avoid immunotherapy escape due to antigen loss, the CAR T cells might be directed at multiple antigens, including some not targeted by mAb in induction, such as B7-H3 or GPC2.33 34 This could be done by infusion of a combination of two separate CAR-T preparations, each reacting against separate tumor antigens (such as B7-H3 and GPC2). Alternatively, a single preparation of CAR-T cells that have dual or tandem CARs can enable the CAR-T cell to be activated if it sees either the first or the second antigen, enabling killing of tumor cells that may have lost one or the other target.35 36 Such approaches would minimize the potential for tumor escape from immunotherapy seen when tumors downregulate the single target being recognized by a monospecific immunotherapy.22 Additional potency for CAR-T cells involves armoring them by having them secrete cytokines or mAb-based therapeutics to augment antitumor CAR-T efficacy.33,37

If successful, at least for the children that receive such an approach, the length of induction chemotherapy may be shortened from 5 months down to 2–4 months in order to achieve clinical CR, followed by a single potentially curative dose of CAR T cells. If this abbreviated chemo-immunotherapy induction followed by CAR-T cells regimen is successful, the dual transplant and post-consolidation phases (with their associated toxicities) might potentially be completely avoided! To test these concepts, large, randomized trials will be needed. To initiate this strategy, it will be essential to have a potent armamentarium of effective salvage regimens to apply for those (hopefully few) patients with relapsed or refractory disease.

Salvage therapy for relapsed/refractory disease

Sensitive methods for detecting signs of early relapse should enable salvage therapy to be initiated in patients with small amounts of early detected relapse or refractory disease.38 For these patients, detailed assessments of cell-free tumor DNA, detailed tumor genotyping/phenotyping to identify molecular targets unique to individual patient’s tumors, coupled with genotyping/phenotyping of important host metabolic and immune-regulating genes, should enable personalizing individual salvage regimens for each patient requiring salvage therapy. These individualized regimens should be targeted to the antigens, pathways, mechanisms and effector cells most likely to eradicate the individual patient’s refractory-relapsed disease.39 Multiple preclinical strategies suggest that pathways that may involve other effector cells may be useful here. A well-augmented activation of innate immune cells, such as macrophages, may provide clinical benefit.40 41 Preclinical data and clinical correlative analyses suggest that the benefit of using antitumor mAbs largely involves ADCC pathways by natural killer cells and neutrophils, with little or no involvement of T cells.42 43 Preclinical data suggests that the use of epigenetic modifier inhibitors may re-express sufficient MHC to enable T-cell recognition, provided that additional immune activations are given in a combination immunotherapy regimen.44 Small molecule inhibitors of oncogenic pathways can act on those patients whose NBL is being driven by the targets of such agents, such as inhibitors of ALK; lorlatinib has been particularly useful in this setting.45 46 Treatments directed towards the immune-hostile tumor microenvironment can enable augmented function of antitumor immune cells. This can include appropriately timed radiation to beneficially modify the tumor microenvironment and induce phenotypic changes in tumor cells, thereby augmenting immune recognition and destruction.47 These approaches are in preclinical and/or clinical testing. Preclinical data suggest that these strategies may prove to be even more effective if used early at a time of molecularly detected relapse, rather than at a time of overt, bulky clinical relapse.

Cost considerations for 2045

Provision of multimodality complex care for HR-NBL in 2025 requires substantial hospitalization time as well as major costs for the therapeutic agents involved, particularly the mAbs and the CAR T cells. If the more effective and less toxic regimens envisioned above are realized, the toxicity and hospitalization time may be dramatically decreased. The cost of generically produced biosimilar mAbs should be substantially lower in the future than the current costs. Importantly, the extreme cost for the production of CAR T cells for current clinical trials may well be made~100-fold lower if current research proposing in vivo production of CAR T cells within patients, using lipid nanoparticle delivery of CAR plasmid messenger RNA, proves to be successful as suggested.48 Other yet unimagined basic science breakthroughs in molecular genetics, and in biochemical drug design and production are expected to provide even more effective agents at lower cost for wider applicability once proof of principle efficacy is demonstrated.

Extrapolation to other pediatric cancers and to high-risk common cancers in adults, not currently being cured with surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, targeted small molecules, and ICI immunotherapy

Unlike ICI therapy, efficacy for the above proposed strategy for HR-NBL does not require pre-existing MHC presentation of tumor peptides to antigen-specific T cells, nor does it require a pre-existing endogenous tumor-specific T-cell response to be present. Rather, it is based on tumor-reactive mAbs. These are used to: (1) selectively recognize the tumor and help innate immune cells to recognize and destroy it; and (2) create tumor-reactive chimeric antigen receptors, enabling genetically altered CAR-T cells to selectively destroy the cancer. The other components of the therapy (surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, small molecules, and supportive care) can be integrated with immunotherapy to augment antitumor efficacy, while hopefully decreasing the exposure to genotoxic chemo-radiotherapy doses associated with life-altering long-term side effects.

These concepts are moving forward both preclinically and clinically for other high-risk pediatric cancers not being cured with current standard therapy regimens.49 50

In those settings where an endogenous T-cell response may be desired, treatments to induce and maintain MHC expression by the tumor and to prime/activate and expand an endogenous tumor-specific T-cell response may be needed; pathways for this are being developed, including epigenetic modifier inhibitor treatment as mentioned above.44

A major requirement for this concept is the existence/availability of tumor-reactive mAbs that can either attract innate immune cells to function through their Fc receptors, or that can be linked to an activating agent that might activate T cells or Fc receptor-bearing cells in the form of a bispecific T-cell engager that bridges tumor cells to immune cells enabling immune-mediated destruction, or may carry a drug package to deliver it to the tumor in the form of an antibody-drug conjugate.51,53 Multiple monospecific mAbs are approved clinically, or in clinical testing, against a variety of antigens overexpressed, or selectively expressed on a variety of human cancers.54 55 Collectively, these mAbs can react with some selectivity to virtually any type of tumor. Their major hurdle is that the antigenic targets of these tumor-reactive mAbs are often expressed on some normal cells. Even if the expression on normal cells is at a lower level, such monospecific mAbs can cause profound toxicities to tissues that cannot afford such toxicity. Thus, monospecific mAbs to lung cancer can also bind weakly to normal lung tissue and cause substantial life-threatening tissue damage, thereby preventing clinical use. Similarly, antibodies to epithelial cancer antigens on epithelial cancer cells also recognize (at lower levels) the same determinants on normal epithelial cells in the GI system, genito-urinary (GU) system, or other tissues and are too toxic for standard intravenous use.56 One approach to circumvent this toxicity involves the use of a bispecific antibody that requires simultaneous co-recognition of two separate antigens expressed on the same tumor. For example, GD2 is strongly expressed by NBL (and certain other tumors) but is also expressed on the nerve fibers, particularly pain fibers. B7H3 is also well expressed on NBL; it is not on nerve fibers but is expressed at lower levels on cells within the liver. Anti-GD2 antibody can recognize NBL but is associated with neuropathic pain and some nerve damage.57 Anti-B7H3 antibody can bind to NBL but is associated with some liver toxicity.24 Recent ongoing work with a bispecific antibody (bsAb) that requires co-recognition of both GD2 and B7H3 (GD2×B7-H3 bsAb) shows in vitro and in vivo exquisite tumor specificity for NBL without substantial binding to nerves or the liver57 (figure 1). This form of tumor-specific recognition requiring simultaneous recognition of both target antigens being co-expressed on the same tumor cell to achieve activation is considered an “AND” gate and has also been explored with CAR-T cells that have an “AND” gate requiring simultaneous co-recognition of GD2 and B7-H3.58

This bsAb concept might potentially extend to virtually any tumor type expressing two unique and independent antigens not co-expressed on critical normal tissues. One could imagine a bsAb directed against ovarian cancer that needs to see both the epithelial-cell-adhesion molecule (EpCAM) and mesothelin in order to get adequate binding avidity. Both are highly expressed on ovarian cancer cells. EpCAM is expressed on many normal epithelial cells but not on the serosal surfaces of pleura, peritoneum or pericardium.59 Mesothelin is on those serosal surfaces but not highly expressed on normal epithelial cells.60 As such, an anti-EpCAM×mesothelin bsAb might provide anti-ovarian cancer specificity without normal tissue toxicity. For any tumor type where three separate pairs of well-selected antigens (eg, A-B, C-D and E-F) are all highly expressed on the tumor cells, but no normal cells express both antigens of any one pair, one could potentially create a panel of three separate bsAb (anti-A×B, anti-C×D, and anti-E×F). Even if a normal tissue expressed one of the antigens from each of these separate bsAb (ie, A+C+E), that normal tissue would still not be recognized by a cocktail of these three bsAbs. As such, using any one of these three bsAb should provide far greater in vivo antitumor specificity than is available with current monospecific antitumor mAbs. Furthermore, simultaneous use of a cocktail of all three of these bsAb should retain exquisite antitumor specificity but would also address antigen heterogeneity and antigen loss on the tumor, which remain a major hurdle when using single monospecific mAb-based immunotherapy.61

Summary

While the wide application of ICI has provided potent efficacy for many patients with cancer with immunogenic tumors and pre-existing tumor-reactive T cells, most patients with cancer do not meet these criteria. Additional strategies like laboratory-generated antitumor mAbs might be better engineered and combined with other therapies to provide an increased antitumor efficacy for the majority of patients with cancer not yet benefiting from ICI therapy.
