Effect of mechanical and chemical surface treatments on the repairing of milled and 3D-printed denture bases
Authors: Hein Linn Htat, Wisarut Prawatvatchara, Siraphob Techapiroontong, Jae-Hyun Lee, Nareudee Limpuangthip
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnsuring a strong bond between chairside autopolymerized acrylic resin to denture base is essential for denture repair and reline procedures. However, there is no established protocol to enhance bond strength between autopolymerizing resin and computer-aided design and computer-aided manufacturing (CAD-CAM) denture base materials. The purpose of this study was to determine shear bond strength of CAD-CAM denture bases and autopolymerizing acrylic resin after mechanical and chemical surface treatments compared with heat-polymerized acrylic resin. Heat-polymerized, milled, and 3-dimensional (3D) printed denture bases were divided into 4 surface treatment protocols: none (control), airborne-particle abrasion (APA), tetrahydrofuran, and Vitacoll application. Autopolymerizing acrylic resin cylinders were bonded to denture surface. Shear bond strength and failure modes were determined after thermocycling. Denture base surfaces were assessed for surface roughness, surface morphology, and microhardness before and after surface treatment. Data was analyzed using two-way ANOVA and multiple comparison tests. The results showed that APA significantly increased shear bond strength and surface roughness of all denture base materials. Tetrahydrofuran and Vitacoll application improved shear bond strength of heat-polymerized acrylic resin, but did not reach the level achieved by APA. Conversely, tetrahydrofuran application improved bond strength of 3D-printed denture to the level of APA. Tetrahydrofuran and Vitacoll application significantly reduced denture base hardness, compared with control and APA. In conclusion, mechanical surface treatment using APA enhances the adhesion of autopolymerizing acrylic resin to heat-polymerized and CAD-CAM denture bases. Tetrahydrofuran and Vitacoll chemical surface treatment improved adhesion to heat-polymerized acrylic resin, with only tetrahydrofuran enhancing bond strength of 3D-printed denture to the level of APA. Introduction

In recent decades, the use of computer-aided design and computer-aided manufacturing (CAD-CAM) technology has become more popular in fabricating removable dental prostheses1. Two principal CAD-CAM techniques utilized in denture manufacturing are subtractive (milling) and additive (3D-printing) techniques2. Compared with conventional heat-polymerized denture processing, CAD-CAM dentures offer advantages, including efficient data storage, simplified teeth arrangement, the capability for precise duplication and reproduction, and the elimination of complications associated with conventional heat-polymerization techniques3–5.

In addition to possessing optimal mechanical properties, denture base materials should be repairable because denture breakage is one of the most common mechanical complications following denture wear6, and be suitable for relining, such as for the direct pick-up of implant housing in implant-retained removable dental prostheses7. Autopolymerizing acrylic resin is commonly used in these procedures for denture repair and relining7–14. Various surface treatment protocols have been recommended to improve the bond strength between denture base and autopolymerizing acrylic resin, comprising mechanical methods10,14,15 and chemical surface treatments8,9,16. Mechanical protocols include bur grinding14,15, or airborne-particle abrasion with alumina oxide particles of various sizes8–10,14,15, and laser treatment15. Chemical surface treatment include the application of methyl methacrylate (MMA) monomer8–10, or other chemical substances, such as methyl formate and methyl acetate16, acetone17, and tetrahydrofuran18–20. These chemical substances assist in polymer swelling and allow for the penetration of MMA monomer and the adhesion of autopolymerizing resin12,13,16.

Previous in vitro studies have demonstrated the positive effect of surface treatment on enhancing the bond strength between conventional heat-polymerized denture base materials and autopolymerizing resin12,13. Recently, the bond strength between CAD-CAM denture base materials and autopolymerizing resin has been evaluated8–13,15,17. However, conclusive results showing which is the optimal surface treatment protocol for enhancing the adhesion between autopolymerizing resin and various CAD-CAM denture base materials are limited8,9. The objective of this in vitro study was to determine the shear bond strength of different denture base materials and autopolymerizing acrylic resin after undergoing various surface treatments. The null hypothesis was that the surface treatment protocol would not affect the bond strength between denture base materials and autopolymerizing acrylic resin.

Methods

Specimen preparation and surface treatment

The materials used in this study are shown in Table 1 and the study flow diagram is presented in Fig. 1. The denture base materials were divided into 3 types based on their manufacturing technique: heat-polymerized, milled, and 3D-printed. The sample size was calculated based on a previous study that determined the shear bond strength of 3 types of relined CAD-CAM dentures compared with heat-polymerized acrylic resin, and an effect size of 0.74 was calculated from the study11. To achieve 90% power and 5% type I error, with 10% estimated sample damage, a sample size of 30 was calculated for each surface treatment. Comprising 3 types of denture bases, the present study required 10 samples for each material and each surface treatment protocol.

Flow diagram of the study.

Specimens of heat-polymerized acrylic resin (VERTEX Rapid-Simplified; 3D Systems, Soesterberg, Netherlands) were fabricated using polytetrafluoroethylene molds. The powder and liquid were mixed at a ratio of 10 g powder to 4 ml liquid monomer, placed into the molds, and subsequently heat polymerized under 100 oC temperature for 30 min. The CAD-CAM specimens were designed with the CAD software (3Shape Dental System DS; 3Shape, Copenhagen, Denmark) and the designed object was exported as a standard tessellation language (STL) file. By using the STL file, the pre-polymerized discs (Smile Cam; Pressing Dental Srl, San Marino, Italy) were milled to fabricate the milled denture base specimens with a 5-axis dental milling machine (DWX-52D; DGShape, Roland, Shizuoka, Japan). Based on the manufacturer’s recommendation, the 3D-printed denture base specimens (NextDent Denture 3D+; 3D Systems, Soesterberg, Netherlands) were printed vertically with a 50-µm thick layer using a digital light procession 3D printing machine (NextDent 5100 3D Printer; 3D Systems, Soesterberg, Netherlands). For the post-curing protocol of the 3D-printed material, the specimens were immersed in 95% isopropanol within an ultrasonic bath (VGT-1990QTD; GT Sonic, Shenzhen, China) for 3 min to remove excess resin, following the manufacturer’s recommendation21. This was followed by an additional 2-min isopropanol immersion. After a 10-minute resting, the specimens underwent 45-min post-curing in an ultraviolet-light curing box (NextDent LC-3DPrint Box; 3D Systems, Soesterberg, Netherlands) for final polymerization.

The denture base specimens were prepared into 8 × 8 × 8 (± 0.05) mm rectangular-shaped specimens. The specimens were measured using a digital vernier caliper (Digimatic; Mitutoyo, Kawasaki, Japan) to ensure standardization. The denture base dimensions were adapted from an in vitro study by Gad et al. (2022), with the length and width chosen as the smallest size compatible with 4-mm bonding diameter, allowing for at least 2-mm space around the bonded area. Each denture base specimen was attached to a nylon pipe using a clear autopolymerizing acrylic resin, with the specimen surface positioned 3 mm above the surface of the clear resin. The remaining 5-mm thickness was designated for retention to prevent specimen dislodgement during bond strength testing (Fig. 2). Each specimen’s surface was polished with 600-grit silicon carbide paper (Buehler CarbiMet™; Lake Bluff, IL, USA) to mimic the surface roughness generated by a dental bur10. They were then cleaned with distilled water in an ultrasonic bath for 5 min, and dried with absorbent paper prior to surface treatment procedure.

Schematic diagram illustrating shear bond strength test. Load was applied perpendicularly to material interface.

The specimens were covered by masking tape with a 4-mm diameter hole, and a custom 4-mm diameter and 3-mm high metal split mold. A total of 120 specimens were randomly divided into 4 groups (n = 10 per group) by the type of surface treatment: (1) no surface treatment (control); (2) airborne-particle abrasion with 50 μm aluminum oxide particles applied at a 10-mm distance for 10 sec under 0.4-MPa pressure22; (3) tetrahydrofuran application with a microbrush for 1-min, followed by a 5-min resting period; and (4) Vitacoll (Vitacoll bonding agent; VITA Zahnfabrik, Bad Säckingen, Germany) application with a microbrush, followed by a 5-min resting period18. Prior to bonding, the denture base surface was coated with liquid monomer of autopolymerizing acrylic resin for 2 min10,11, poured with the resin into the metal mold, and after polymerization, the metal mold and tape were removed. To mimic material aging during their use in the oral cavity, the specimens underwent thermocycling by immersing them in distilled water from 5 °C to 55 °C with a 30-sec dwelling time for 5,000 cycles. It has been hypothesized that 5,000 cycles represent use in oral cavity for 5 years10. After thermocycling, the specimens were stored at 37 °C prior to testing.

Additional denture base resin specimens from each group were fabricated and surface-treated to evaluate surface roughness, surface morphology, and microhardness following mechanical and chemical surface treatments.

Shear bond strength and failure mode

Shear bond strength was tested using a universal testing machine (EZ-S; Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) where the specimen was mounted in a metal holder. A curved metal blade was positioned parallel to the contact point at the interface between the denture base and autopolymerizing resin, and a progressive compressive load was applied at a crosshead speed of 1.0 mm/min until debonding (Fig. 2)9,11. The shear bond strength was calculated using the formula = T/A, where ‘T’ was the maximum tension applied to debond the specimen and ‘A’ was the surface area of adhesion.

The debonded surfaces were observed using a stereomicroscope (SZ61; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) at ×﻿7 magnification to identify the mode of failure. The failure modes were classified into 4 types: (1) cohesive failure in the relined zone, where failure occurred exclusively within the autopolymerizing acrylic resin; (2) cohesive failure in the non-relined zone, where failure occurs within the denture base resin; (3) adhesive failure, where failure occurred at the interface (no trace of reline material on the specimen); and (4) mixed failure, which was a combination of all of the above11.

Surface roughness and surface morphology

The surface roughness from each denture base material in control group and after the surface treatment were assessed (n = 5 per subgroup) using a non-contact profilometer (InfinitefocusSL; Alicona, Graz, Austria) with ×﻿50 magnification. Three measurements were recorded for each specimen, and the mean values were calculated in Ra (µm). One sample of each group was sputtered-coated with gold in a vacuum and the surface morphology was observed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) (FEI; Quanta250, Oregon, USA) at an acceleration voltage of 20 kV (n = 1 per subgroup).

Microhardness

The surface hardness was measured (n = 5 per subgroup) using a Vickers hardness tester (FM-810; Future-Tech, Kawaski, Japan). Each specimen was indented at 3 different areas using a pyramid-shaped diamond indenter with a 50-g load and a 30-sec dwelling time23. The average Vickers hardness value (VHN) was calculated for each sample.

Data analyses

The data were analyzed using a statistical software program (IBM SPSS Statistics, version 28.0; IBM) at a 5% significance level. The data distribution normality was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and the homogeneity of variances was assessed by the Levene test. The differences in shear bond strength, surface roughness, and hardness between the denture base material types and surface treatment protocols were analyzed using a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by the Bonferroni post-hoc multiple comparison test.

Results

Shear bond strength and failure mode

Two-way ANOVA revealed a significant interaction effect between the denture base materials and surface treatment protocols on shear bond strength (p < 0.001). In the control group, the highest bond strength was observed for the 3D-printed denture base material (Fig. 3). Airborne-particle abrasion significantly increased the shear bond strength of all denture base materials. Chemical surface treatment with tetraphydrofuran and Vitacoll improved the shear bond strength of heat-polymerized acrylic resin, but not achieve the level of airborne-particle abrasion. In contrast, tetrahydrofuran application improved the shear bond strength of the 3D-printed denture base to the level of airborne-particle abrasion, while the bond strength after Vitacoll application was comparable to that of the non-treated surface.

Surface roughness and surface morphology

In accordance with shear bond strength, there was a significant interaction effect between the denture base materials and surface treatment protocols on surface roughness (p < 0.001). Airborne-particle abrasion significantly increased the surface roughness of all denture base materials, with the highest roughness in the 3D-printed group (Table 2). The surface roughness of the denture base material did not significantly increase after chemical surface treatment.

Microhardness

Based on two-way ANOVA, the denture base materials (p < 0.001) and surface treatment protocols (p < 0.001) were significantly associated with surface hardness (Table 2), however, there was no interaction effect between the two variables (p = 0.958). The 3D-printed dentures had the significantly lowest hardness among the denture base material types. The tetrahydrofuran and Vitacoll application significantly lowered hardness, compared with control and airborne-particle abrasion.

Discussion

From the present study findings, the effect of mechanical and chemical surface treatments on improving the bond strength between denture base materials to autopolymerizing acrylic resin varied across the denture base materials and surface treatment protocols. Mechanical surface treatment using airborne-particle abrasion led to the highest shear bond strength for the conventional heat-polymerized and CAD-CAM denture base materials. Compared with the non-surface-treated group, chemical surface treatment with tetrahydrofuran and Vitacoll enhanced bond strength only in heat-polymerized denture base, whereas only tetrahydrofuran improved the bond strength of 3D-printed denture base. Therefore, the null hypothesis was rejected.

Mechanical surface treatment by airborne-particle abrasion using alumina oxide articles generally yields the highest bond strength and surface roughness for denture base materials compared with non-treated and chemical surface treatments. This finding is consistent with previous in vitro studies on milled8,9 and 3D-printed dentures8,15. The enhanced bond strength can be attributed to increased surface microroughness, as evidenced by the irregular surface morphology observed in the SEM images. The microroughness increases the surface area for bonding and facilitates better mechanical interlocking between materials, resulting in an improved shear bond strength between the two materials8,10,22. A study reported no improvement in shear bond strength after airborne-particle abrasion on milled denture resin9, which was inconsistent with the results of our study. This could be explained by differences in the specimen preparation process. In our experiment, the denture base surfaces were initially ground with P600 silicon carbide abrasive paper before undergoing airborne-particle abrasion. As a result, airborne-particle abrasion had a more pronounced effect on smoother surfaces by inducing surface morphology changes, thereby enhancing retention through increased surface area bonding. It was noted that there was a slight difference in failure modes between denture base materials after airborne-particle abrasion. Conventional heat-polymerized and milled denture bases predominantly fractured within the autopolymerizing resin, indicating a higher bond strength between material interfaces. Conversely, breakage in 3D-printed dentures suggests a stronger bond between the material interface than the toughness of the 3D-printed denture.

The impact of chemical surface treatment varied among denture base materials. Chemical surface treatments using tetrahydrofuran and Vitacoll enhanced the bond strength of heat-polymerized denture base material, while only tetrahydrofuran had a positive effect on 3D-printed denture base. This difference could be attributed to a compatibility between the denture base materials and the chemical substances used. Tetrahydrofuran, a heterocyclic organic substance with both hydrophobic and hydrophilic structures, has been reported to swell, soften, and dissolve the polymer matrix of composite resin24 and polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA)-based polymer12,13, enabling better interpenetration and interlocking of additional components or adhesives18–20. In contrast, Vitacoll is a combination of MMA monomer and butanone (methyl ethyl ketone). Butanone has been used as a chemical solvent for dissolving composite resin25 and PMMA-based denture for silicone bonding26. For heat-polymerized denture base material, the butanone in Vitacoll dissolves the polymer matrix of the denture base, and MMA monomer can swell and create a chemical bond with residual monomer in the PMMA polymer of heat-polymerized denture base12,13. Conversely, the concentration of butanone in Vitacoll might not be sufficient to effectively dissolve the matrix of the 3D-printed denture, and the MMA monomer cannot create a chemical bond with the bisphenol A dimethacrylate structure of the 3D-printed denture base material. Although decreased microhardness of the 3D-printed denture base after chemical surface treatment with THF and Vitacoll may suggest material swelling, this value alone does not comprehensively reflect the extent of bonding27.

In contrast, chemical surface treatment with either tetrahydrofuran or Vitacoll failed to improve the bond strength between milled denture base and autopolymerizing acrylic resin. The failure mode supported this finding in that non-treated and chemical surface treatment led to most of the adhesive failure. This could be explained by the fact that milled denture bases, composed of highly polymerized polymethyl methacrylate9,14,28, exhibit less swelling due to minimal residual monomer and reduced likelihood of crosslinking at the material interface29.

Without surface treatment, the bond strength of 3D-printed denture base was the highest compared with conventional heat-polymerized and milled denture base. This phenomenon may be attributed to the mechanical properties of the material and polymerization method29,30. The 3D-printed denture base had lowest surface hardness, which may enhance penetration of autopolymerizing acrylic resin monomer to swell the material and create micromechanical interlocking. This is supported by the present findings that 3D-printed denture base predominantly demonstrated cohesive failure within denture base material rather than adhesive failure between the material interface, indicating a stronger bond than the material strength. The layer-by-layer construction of 3D-printed material can lead to improve overall material strength and enhanced interlayer bonding with other substances10,15. However, this finding contradicts a previous study reporting a significantly higher shear bond strength between relined conventional heat-polymerized and milled denture base than relined 3D-printed denture base9,11. This discrepancy might be due to variations in 3D-printed materials and their processing techniques9,31.

Based on the present findings, treating the denture base surface before adhering with autopolymerizing acrylic resin is recommended. This is particularly relevant when repairing a broken denture or performing a direct pick-up of an implant housing for bonding with an implant-retained removable dental prosthesis. Airborne-particle abrasion is suggested for heat-polymerized and CAD-CAM denture bases to increase surface roughness and facilitate micromechanical interlocking. Tetrahydrofuran and Vitacoll application efficiently improve the bond strength of heat-polymerized denture base, although not to the extent achieved by airborne-particle abrasion. Conversely, tetrahydrofuran enhances the bond strength for 3D-printed denture base, comparable to that achieved by airborne-particle abrasion. It is important to note that airborne-particle abrasion may potentially increase plaque accumulation on the denture surface due to increased surface roughness32, leading to oral mucosal infection. Therefore, carefully controlling the airborne-particle abrasion area during denture repair or relining procedures is essential.

The present study has some limitations. The findings from the in vitro experiments limited the generalizability of the findings to clinical practice due to the variations in the oral environment and individual patient function33. Only one type of each denture base and repair material was included, and it should be noted that different materials may exhibit varying properties. Therefore, future investigations using a broader range of material compositions and manufacturing procedures for denture base materials, as well as other types of denture repairing materials, should be performed to confirm the application of tetrahydrofuran results and to provide more comprehensive and clinically relevant results.

Conclusions

Based on the findings of this in vitro study, the following conclusions were drawn:

Mechanical surface treatment using airborne-particle abrasion with alumina oxide particles enhanced the bond strength of the conventional heat-polymerized, milled, and 3D-printed CAD-CAM denture base materials to autopolymerized acrylic resin.

Mechanical surface treatment using airborne-particle abrasion with alumina oxide particles enhanced the bond strength of the conventional heat-polymerized, milled, and 3D-printed CAD-CAM denture base materials to autopolymerized acrylic resin.

Tetrahydrofuran and Vitacoll chemical surface treatments both improved the bond strength of conventional heat-polymerized acrylic resin. However, these chemical surface treatments did not enhance the bond strength when relining milled denture base. For 3D-printed denture base, only tetrahydrofuran was effective in enhancing bond strength.

Tetrahydrofuran and Vitacoll chemical surface treatments both improved the bond strength of conventional heat-polymerized acrylic resin. However, these chemical surface treatments did not enhance the bond strength when relining milled denture base. For 3D-printed denture base, only tetrahydrofuran was effective in enhancing bond strength.

Without surface treatment, the 3D-printed denture base material exhibited the highest bond strength, while conventional heat-polymerized and milled materials demonstrated comparable bond strength.
