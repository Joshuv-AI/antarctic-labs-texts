Preclinical in vitro and in vivo activity of trastuzumab deruxtecan against ERBB2-mutated cervical carcinomas with low HER2 expression
Authors: Namrata Sethi, Sarah Ottum, Stefania Bellone, Cem Demirkiran, Natalia Buza, Pei Hui, Orazio De Tommasi, Luca Palmieri, Victoria M. Ettorre, Michelle Greenman, Tobias M. Hartwich, Elena Ratner, Alessandro D. Santin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveSomatic HER2 mutations occur in up to 6% of cervical cancer patients and are associated with poor prognosis. We explored the activity of trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd) as a novel targeted therapy against HER2-mutated primary cervical cancer cell lines and xenografts with low HER2 expression.MethodsHER2 expression was assessed by flow cytometry, while ERBB2 mutations were identified by whole-exome sequencing. Cytotoxicity was measured by flow cytometry-based viability assays, and double-stranded DNA breaks were evaluated using anti-phosphorylated-histone H2AX antibody. Bystander effect was assessed by co-culturing HER2-S310F-mutated (CVX4) and non-mutated (CVX8) tumor cells. In vivo, efficacy was tested over 47 days in severe combined immunodeficient mice bearing CVX4-xenografts (n = 4 in each group).ResultsAll eight primary cell lines available demonstrated low HER2 expression. CVX4 (HER2-S310F activating mutation) was significantly more sensitive to T-DXd than control antibody-drug conjugate (CTL-ADC) as demonstrated in the cytotoxicity experiments (p = 0.0036) and double-stranded DNA break assays (p = 0.0057). CVX3, a tumor with a HER2 mutation of unknown significance (HER2-E405D) and CVX8 (HER2-non-mutated) showed no difference in sensitivity to the ADC. T-DXd induced substantial bystander killing in the co-culture (p = 0.0223) while in vivo, a single retroorbital injection of T-DXd (4 mg/kg) was well tolerated and achieved remarkable growth inhibition compared with CTL-ADC in CVX4-xenografts (p < 0.0001).ConclusionsT-DXd was shown to be highly active against HER2-S310F-mutated cervical cancer cell lines and xenografts. T-DXd may represent a novel, effective therapeutic option for recurrent cervical cancer patients harboring mutations in the ERBB2 gene, regardless of low HER2 expression.

Introduction

Cervical cancer is the third most common cancer in women worldwide. According to a recent annual global analysis (Globocan 2022), there were an estimated 662,301 new cases of cervical cancer worldwide, with 348,874 deaths (Filho et al., 2025). While cervical cancer is most detected at an early stage in regions with established screening programs, according to the U.S. National Cancer Institute's Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results Program (SEER), around 15% of cases are diagnosed with distant metastases (Cancer of the Cervix Uteri, 2026). In cases of progression on chemo-immunotherapy in advanced/recurrent cervical cancer, molecular testing has become increasingly critical to identify new biomarkers that guide treatment strategies, including targeted therapies based on Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) testing, antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), and others. ADCs combine monoclonal antibodies directed against tumor-associated surface antigens with highly cytotoxic payloads via chemical linkers, optimizing targeted cytotoxicity while limiting systemic adverse effects (Peters and Brown, 2015).

Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor-2 (HER2) is encoded by the ERBB2 gene located on chromosome 17q12. It is a growth-promoting protein, found overexpressed on the surface of a variety of human cancers. Over the last few years, ERBB2-activating mutations have been identified through genetic sequencing and recognized as actionable alterations in solid tumors such as lung and breast cancers, regardless of the lack of ERBB2 amplification and/or HER2 overexpression (Yoon and Oh, 2024).

The frequency of ERBB2 mutations varies by tumor types: 7–18% in bladder cancer, ∼ 4% in breast cancer, 1–8% in lung cancer, 3–4% in gastric adenocarcinoma, 1–3.8% in colorectal cancer, and 3–6% in cervical cancer (Cen et al., 2021; Cocco et al., 2019; Ojesina et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2022; Xiang et al., 2018; Zheng et al., 2023). Notably, in a prospective analysis of 177 cervical cancer cases using the MSK-IMPACT NGS platform, 13% of patients harbored ERBB2 alterations; 4% amplifications, and 9% oncogenic mutations (Friedman et al., 2023). Consistent with these results, a comprehensive study previously published using whole-exome sequencing (WES) to analyze the genetic landscape of 69 fresh-frozen cervical tumors, including 15 primary cervical carcinomas, identified activating ERBB2 gene mutations in 6% of the samples (Zammataro et al., 2019). These genetic studies suggest that cervical cancer may have one of the higher frequencies of ERBB2 mutations among human solid tumors. The extracellular domain of HER2 is organized into four regions: leucine-rich domains I and III for ligand binding and cysteine-rich domains II and IV that mediate dimerization. Despite being a receptor without a known ligand (Cho et al., 2003), HER2 constitutively adopts a ligand-bound-like conformation in which the dimerization arms remain extended and available for homo- or heterodimerization (Lemmon, 2009; Tsé et al., 2012). Unlike ERBB2 amplification or HER2 overexpression, which promote oncogenic signaling by facilitating homodimerization and heterodimerization with other c-ERBB receptors (Gutierrez and Schiff, 2011), activating mutations in the ERBB2 gene drive tumorigenesis through distinct structural mechanisms.

Trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd; DS-8201a) is a novel ADC targeting HER2 composed of trastuzumab, a humanized anti-HER2 monoclonal antibody, and deruxtecan, the toxic payload, linked by an enzymatically cleavable peptide-based linker. Deruxtecan is an exatecan derivative and causes double-stranded DNA damage and apoptosis when it binds to topoisomerase I and DNA complexes (Nakada et al., 2019; Ogitani et al., 2016). It is currently approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the agnostic treatment of HER2 3+ overexpressing human cancers, HER2-low breast cancers, as well as non-small cell lung cancer with activating HER2 mutations (ENHERTU, 2026). Accordingly, the objective of this study was to explore for the first time the potential role of T-DXd as a novel targeted therapy against ERBB2-mutated cervical cancer. To this end, we evaluated its preclinical activity against ERBB2-mutated and non-mutated primary cervical cancer cell lines and xenografts with low HER2 expression.

Determination of HER2 expression/mutation in the cell lines

Flow cytometry analysis for HER2 expression was performed as previously described (Palmieri et al., 2026). Cell lines expressing a mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of 20 or less were considered low HER2 expressors since such an MFI value has previously been demonstrated to correlate with an Immunohistochemistry (IHC) score of 1+ or less. Somatic ERBB2 mutation analysis of the primary cervical cancer cell lines was performed using whole-exome sequencing (WES) to identify coding somatic variants. The analysis was previously published (Zammataro et al., 2019). Two of the lines (CVX3 and CVX4) were found to have missense mutations.

Drugs

The non-targeting control antibody-drug conjugate (CTL-ADC), the ADC T-DXd (DS-8201), as well as the monoclonal antibody trastuzumab were obtained from Daiichi Sankyo Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan) through a material transfer agreement. T-DXd and the CTL-ADC were stored at −80 °C until use. At the time of use, T-DXd and the CTL-ADC were diluted to prepare serial concentrations from 0.005 μg/mL to 0.2 μg/mL. Phosphate-buffered saline was used to dilute the ADC, CTL-ADC, and trastuzumab for injections for in vivo experiments.

Flow cytometry-based cytotoxicity assay

Cervical carcinoma cell lines were plated in six-well tissue culture plates at a density of 60,000–70,000 cells/well in Keratinocyte serum-free media containing l-Glutamine, supplemented with bovine pituitary extract and human recombinant epidermal growth factor, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 1% amphotericin. The plates were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Subsequently, they were treated with the vehicle (as an untreated control) in one well and either T-DXd or CTL-ADC at concentrations of 0.005, 0.01, 0.025, 0.05, and 0.2 μg/mL in the other five wells. After 72 h, the cells were collected and stained with Propidium Iodide (2 μL of 500 μg/mL stock solution in phosphate-buffered saline). Flow cytometry was used to analyze viable cell percentage relative to untreated cells (considered 100% viable controls). At least 3 such experiments were performed per cell line, and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration was determined for T-DXd and CTL-ADC for each line.

Bystander effect assay

CVX8 cells (no ERBB2 mutation) tagged with carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester dye were mixed with CVX4 cells (HER2-S310F mutation) in a 1:1 ratio (30,000 cells per well per cell line). Cells were seeded in six-well plates, incubated overnight, and treated with either T-DXd or CTL-ADC at 0.05 μg/mL. After 48 h, the cells were harvested and stained with Propidium Iodide. Cell viability was evaluated using flow cytometry, which enabled discrimination between carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester-labeled viable CVX8 cells and unlabeled CVX4 cells. Viability was expressed as the mean ± SEM relative to untreated controls, which were defined as 100% viable. All experiments were repeated at least thrice.

Double-strand DNA-breakage assay

Phycoerythrin-conjugated mouse anti-H2AX (pS139) antibody (BD Biosciences, catalog #562377) was used to assess γ-H2AX as a marker of double-stranded DNA breaks in the cell lines. Cells were plated, incubated overnight, and treated with 0.05 μg/mL T-DXd or CTL-ADC. After 48 h, cells were fixed in 200 μL of fixative buffer (4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline 1×). After 15 min, the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline, and permeabilized using saponin solution (0.5% saponin, 1% bovine serum albumin in phosphate-buffered saline). Samples were incubated with 5 μL anti-H2AX antibody (or isotype control) for 30 min at 4 °C, washed with 0.5% bovine serum albumin in phosphate-buffered saline, and analyzed for phosphorylated H2AX using the FACSCalibur.

In vivo studies with patient-derived xenograft

Specimen collections and animal studies were all approved by the Yale University Human Investigations Committee and the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (2025–11205). CVX4 was selected for the in vivo experiments due to the HER2-S310F mutation and the consistent engraftment of the primary tumor cells in severe combined immunodeficient mice (Zammataro et al., 2019). To test the antitumor activity of T-DXd and CTL-ADC against xenograft models established from CVX4, 6 to 8-week-old mice were given single subcutaneous injections of 8 million cells each, composed of approximately 300 μL of a 1:1 solution of Matrigel (BD Biosciences) and carcinoma cells in sterile phosphate-buffered saline. Vernier calipers were used to check tumor size twice weekly. The volume of each tumor mass was calculated as: length x (width)2 /2. At approximately 0.25 cm3 of tumor size, drug doses were selected based on prior publications (Mutlu et al., 2024; Ogitani et al., 2016), and the mice were randomized into four groups of n = 4, receiving a single retro-orbital injection (4 mg/kg) of T-DXd, CTL-ADC, trastuzumab, or control (vehicle). Tumor size was checked twice weekly, and body weight was checked weekly. The primary endpoint was overall survival (from enrollment to either death or tumor volume of 1.0 cm3). At the end of the 47-day study period, any surviving animals were censored.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 11. For the flow cytometry-based cytotoxicity assays, viability data were analyzed separately for each cell line using two-way analysis of variance, with treatment and drug concentration as fixed factors. Half-maximal inhibitory concentration values were estimated by nonlinear regression using a four-parameter logistic model (log[inhibitor] versus normalized response, variable slope) fitted to dose-response curves generated from viability values normalized to untreated controls. When the fitted curve did not cross 50% inhibition within the tested concentration range, the half-maximal inhibitory concentration was considered not reached.

For the DNA double-stranded break analyses, comparisons between CTL-ADC and T-DXd-treated samples were performed using a two-tailed paired Student's t-test. For the bystander effect assay, planned comparisons between the two independent groups were performed using two-tailed unpaired t-tests, and Welch's correction was applied when appropriate.

For the in vivo studies, longitudinal tumor volume and body weight data for all four groups were analyzed using two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance. Overall survival data were analyzed and plotted using Kaplan-Meier survival curves and compared using the log-rank test. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

HER2 amplification and overexpression have long been recognized as therapeutic targets in a variety of human cancers. However, activating mutations in ERBB2, the gene encoding for HER2, are increasingly understood to also be actionable alterations (Bose et al., 2013; Greulich et al., 2012; Zammataro et al., 2019). Among the various ERBB2 mutations identified, S310F and S310Y, both located in the extracellular domain (ECD II) of the HER2 receptor, are the most frequently reported and better characterized. S310F/Y mutations in the ECD alter receptor conformation to favor noncovalent dimerization independently of overexpression or gene amplification (Bose et al., 2013; Greulich et al., 2012), thereby activating downstream signaling pathways linked to proliferation and survival via transphosphorylation of tyrosine residues within the kinase domains (Marchini et al., 2013; Wagner et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2006). Accordingly, these mutations have been functionally validated as oncogenic drivers and therapeutic targets in various cancers such as breast, lung, and colorectal cancers (Bose et al., 2013; Cocco et al., 2019; Greulich et al., 2012; Kavuri et al., 2015). In our study, among the eight primary cervical cancer cell lines available, two demonstrated mutations in the ERBB2 gene. Specifically, CVX4 harbored the canonical HER2-S310F hotspot mutation while CVX3 harbored an E405D mutation, a variant of unknown significance. Interrogation of the COSMIC database with respect to the E405D variant found only a single report in breast cancer, describing a similar amino acid substitution, while the public archive ClinVar documented additional alterations at residue 405, involving different substitutions. Importantly, as demonstrated in our preclinical experiments, we found remarkable T-DXd preclinical activity only against the CVX4 primary cell line harboring the HER2-S310F mutation, with negligible T-DXd activity detected against the CVX3 cell line harboring the HER2-E405D mutation or v/s the cell line with no ERBB2 mutation (i.e., CVX8). Since all primary cervical cancer cell lines studied in our experiments were found to be low HER2 expressors and, accordingly, seemed to only differ in HER2 mutational status, we inferred that the S310F activating mutation, similar to previous reports (Li et al., 2024), was rendering tumor cells differentially susceptible to T-DXd activity.

Recent studies have postulated that HER2 ubiquitination and internalization in low HER2-expressing tumors may underlie the efficacy of ADCs such as T-DXd (Li et al., 2020). Other reports in breast cancer and solid tumors have shown that cathepsin enzymes can efficiently cleave ADC linkers, allowing release of T-DXd's payload in the tumor microenvironment even before internalization (Tsao et al., 2025). The drug-to-antibody ratio of T-DXd is approximately 8, higher when compared to other ADCs such as trastuzumab emtansine, which has a ratio of about 3.5, or disitamab vedotin, which has a ratio of 4 (Zhang et al., 2022). This high ratio may enable more toxic payload to be delivered into low HER2-expressing target cells, which may explain the high activity of T-DXd reported in clinical trials against HER2-low ERBB2 non-amplified tumors (Modi et al., 2022). Furthermore, the payload is membrane-permeable, allowing for HER2-independent cytotoxicity towards neighboring tumor cells irrespective of receptor status (bystander effect) (Tsurutani et al., 2020). T-DXd also exhibits good stability within the plasma due to the tetrapeptide linker, and a short systemic half-life (Nakada et al., 2019; Ogitani et al., 2016).

Importantly, T-DXd was approved by the FDA in 2022 for the treatment of HER2-mutant NSCLC after the publication of the DESTINY-Lung02 study, which demonstrated high objective response rates (57.7%) and confirmed clinical benefit in lung cancer patients (Goto et al., 2023). The clinical activity of T-DXd was later investigated in the DESTINY-PanTumor01 study (Li et al., 2024). Across the broader cohort of HER2-mutant tumors, the objective response rate (ORR) by independent central review was 29.4%, with particularly high responses observed in tumors harboring ECD S310F/Y or TKD mutations (29.4–36.5%), compared with a lower response in transmembrane domain mutations such as R678Q (5.9%). These findings highlight the functional relevance of specific ERBB2 mutations and suggest differential sensitivity to HER2-targeted therapies based on mutation type and location, underscoring the rationale for preclinical and clinical evaluation of T-DXd in HER2-mutant cervical carcinoma patients.

Of interest, the IC50 for T-DXd calculated in our cytotoxicity assay for the HER2-S310F mutated CVX4 cell line was 0.22 μg/mL, a value along the lines of what was previously reported by other investigators (Naveed et al., 2025; Ogitani et al., 2016). This concentration is also well within the therapeutic range achievable in vivo in T-DXd-treated patients (i.e., T-DXd mean maximum plasma concentrations (Cmax) = 122 μg/mL) (Martín et al., 2024). In this regard, while plasma measurements provide reference exposure levels, actual extracellular concentrations within the tumor microenvironment are known to be shaped more by HER2 binding, internalization, and payload diffusion than by systemic levels alone.

Importantly, the high membrane permeability of DXd and its ability to redistribute into the tumor microenvironment may represent a critical factor enabling the killing of neighboring tumor cells (i.e., bystander effect), with heterogeneous HER2 expression. Consistent with this view, we found T-DXd to induce significant bystander killing against non-HER2 mutated tumor cells when such tumor cells were co-cultured with S310F-HER2 mutated tumor cells. These findings support the potential efficacy of T-DXd in treating ERBB2-mutated cancers with intratumor heterogeneity in HER2 expression. Furthermore, through our double-stranded DNA-breakage assays that used phosphorylation of histone H2AX as a marker, it was evident that the release of the ADC toxic payload within the cell may cause both DNA fragmentation and induction of apoptosis in the cells carrying the HER2 mutation.

In vivo experiments with CVX4 demonstrated that a single injection of T-DXd effectively induced the regression of all cell-derived xenografts when compared to the CTL-ADC. Of interest, the unconjugated monoclonal antibody trastuzumab, used in our experiments as an additional experimental control, was also able to decrease tumor growth when compared to animals treated with CTL-ADC and Vehicle Control buffer. Trastuzumab in vivo activity, although significant, was not as potent as T-DXd and likely attributable to the binding of the unconjugated antibody to the HER2-mutated receptor, causing a limited reduction of downstream PI3K/AKT or MAPK pathways activation.

Conclusions

While T-DXd is a known therapeutic option for multiple gynecologic malignancies including cervical cancer patients harboring HER2-overexpression with or without ERBB2 gene amplification, a significant unmet medical need remains for cervical cancer patients developing tumors with HER2 mutations but low HER2 expression. This population has limited effective options after failing standard therapies. Our study provides the first evidence of the potent preclinical in vitro and in vivo activity of T-DXd against HER2 S310F-mutated cervical carcinomas in the absence of HER2 overexpression or amplification. Given that about 6% of cervical cancer patients harbor HER2 mutations and about two-thirds of them have S310F/Y mutations, T-DXd has the potential to benefit a significant population of cervical cancer patients worldwide. The cost of Next Generation Sequencing and ADCs may pose significant barriers to implementation, particularly in low-income countries with resource-limited settings. Regardless, the encouraging activity of T-DXd demonstrated in our preclinical study provides a biological rationale for further investigation of T-DXd in HER2-mutant, non-amplified cervical cancer patients with recurrent disease no longer responsive to standard treatments. Further studies, including clinical trials, are warranted.
