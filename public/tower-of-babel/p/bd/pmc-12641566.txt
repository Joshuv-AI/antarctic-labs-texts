Safety evaluation of Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus KF7 based on complete genome, phenotypic assays and alternative models: Caenorhabditis elegans
Authors: Ying Cheng, Yilin Zhang, Xuechun Pang, Chunping You
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated the safety of Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus (L. rhamnosus) KF7, a probiotic strain originating from kefir, for potential use in infant and children's food products in China. The safety evaluation was conducted through a comprehensive approach involving whole-genome sequencing, in vitro and in vivo studies using alternative models: Caenorhabditis elegans (nematodes). Genomic analysis confirmed the absence of virulence factors and antibiotic resistance genes in KF7. Phenotypic characterization demonstrated that KF7 exhibits no antibiotic resistance, does not produce indole, is non-hemolytic, and lacks amino acid decarboxylase activity. Furthermore, KF7 was found to not produce histamine and generate only a minimal amount of tyramine when cultured in MRS medium supplemented with amino acids. The strain was also shown to produce both L- and D-lactic acid, with the concentration of D-lactic acid falling within a safe range. To further evaluate the safety of KF7 in vivo, nematode model was employed, with Pseudomonas aeruginosa serving as a pathogenic control and L. rhamnosus GG, known to be safe for infants and children, as a positive control. The results demonstrated that KF7 does not adversely affect nematode egg-laying, egg development, growth, locomotion, or lifespan. Instead, it appeared to have beneficial effects on gut microbiota and extending the nematodes' lifespan. In summary, this study established the method of using nematode as an in vivo evaluation to the safety of probiotics, and the safety data of the KF7 strain was supplemented by in vitro research, which proved that KF7 has a certain degree of safety.Keywords: Probiotics, Safety, Assessment, Nematodes, Toxicity, Lacticaseibacillus

Introduction

Probiotics are active substances essential for the healthy development of life (Ozen and Dinleyici, 2015). In 2001, the Joint Expert Committee of the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) and the World Health Organization (WHO) defined probiotics as live microorganisms that, when consumed in adequate amounts, confer health benefits to the host (Ozen and Dinleyici, 2015). Among the various probiotic microorganisms, Lactobacillus species are the most extensively studied and widely used commercially (Lee et al., 2022). Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus (L. rhamnosus), previously classified under Lactobacillus, is a key bacterium in fermented foods with a long history of use (Yan and Polk, 2012). This species can produce a range of metabolites and interact with other probiotics, thereby imparting unique functional properties to fermented foods. In China, L. rhamnosus is approved as a permissible strain for use in food products (National Health Commission of the People’s Republic of China, 2022a). Specific strains such as L. rhamnosus GG, HN001, and MP108 are also authorized for use in infant food (National Health Commission of the People’s Republic of China, 2022b).

The safety assessment of a strain is a prerequisite for its application in humans. Currently, international standards for the safety evaluation of probiotic are primarily established jointly by the FAO and WHO (Food and Agriculture Organization of the United NationsWorld Health Organization, 2002) These standards encompass strain identification, origin and usage history, absence of pathogenic traits, drug resistance assessment, metabolite analysis, and safety tests. In the national standard of Safety Assessment Procedure of Food-grade Strains, jointly issued by the National Health Commission and the Administration of Market Supervision and Administration of China on March 16, 2025, methods for the safety evaluation of food-grade strains are specified. These include the review of domestic and foreign evaluation data, whole genome sequencing, animal pathogenicity tests, drug resistance tests, toxin production assays, toxicological tests, and other active substance determinations. For instance, D-lactic acid, must be assessed when a strain is directly added to foods intended for infants under one year of age. Traditionally, pathogenicity tests have been conducted using mouse models (National Health Commission of the People's Republic of China, 2025).

Although mammalian models such as mice, pigs, and non-human primates offer numerous advantages, their high breeding and maintenance costs, the complexity of analyzing biological processes, and ethical and safety concerns have driven the search for alternative models. Alternative models such as Drosophila melanogaster, Caenorhabditis elegans (nematodes), and zebrafish are widely favored due to their ease of laboratory maintenance, short generation times, and high fecundity (Khan et al., 2018). Nematodes share highly homologous genes and similar metabolic pathways with humans (Yun et al., 2022). To data, most nematode studies have focused on lifespan extension and its underlying mechanisms (Yun et al., 2022). An increasing number of studies using this model and have shown that potential probiotic bacteria can extend the lifespan of nematodes and modulate host defense mechanisms (Guantario et al., 2018; Kim et al., 2024; Menberu et al., 2022; Sim et al., 2018). It is noteworthy that different microorganisms exert distinct effects on the nematode gut. Specific strains such as Lactiplantibacillus plantarum have been demonstrated to significantly extend the lifespan of nematodes, whereas opportunistic bacteria like Pseudomonas aeruginosa can accelerate aging and even lead to lethal infections (Clark and Hodgkin, 2013; Mclntyre et al., 2021).

Research on toxicity and safety using nematode as a model organism remains relatively limited, with relevant studies only beginning to emerge in the past two years. For example, Feng et al. (2023) investigated the stereoselective toxicity effects of beta-cypermethrin on nematodes. Zhang and Wang, (2008) established an assessment system for environmental toxin toxicity using this alternative model. The safety and efficacy of Corynebacterium accolens were evaluated in vivo using nematode by Menberu et al. (2022). However, most current studies remain at the level of phenomenological observations and have yet to delve into the underlying mechanisms. An ideal safety assessment framework should be able to establish causal relationships between specific microbial changes and host health phenotypes (such as lifespan and stress response), similar to the approach taken by Liu et al. (2023) in broiler models. Therefore, there is a critical need to develop a nematode-based safety assessment framework for probiotics that can systematically elucidate the "microbiota-phenotype" interactions.

L. rhamnosus KF7 is a strain originated from Kefir, a traditional fermented milk in Yunnan province of China, and has demonstrated probiotic efficacy in enhancing intestinal barrier integrity (Zhang et al., 2024; Dong et al., 2024). While is included in China's list of approved strains for general food use, and the KF7 strain is commercially applied in Bright Dairy's yogurt and probiotic solid beverage products, it has not yet been approved for infant foods. To expand its applications, more comprehensive safety data are required. Furthermore, there is a need to establish a new safety evaluation system based on alternative models such as nematodes. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the safety of L. rhamnosus KF7 through a combination of in vitro and in vivo methods using nematodes as an alternative model, with the goal of establishing a reliable safety assessment framework.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and cultivation

L. rhamnosus KF7 (CGMCC No. 6430), provided by Bright Dairy & Food Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), L. rhamnosus GG (LGG, ATCC53103), purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), were stored at −80 °C in De Man-Rogosa-Sharp (MRS, Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK) broth containing 50 % (v/v) glycerol. KF7 and LGG were grown on MRS agar at 37 °C in an anaerobic workstation (Whitley A85 Workstation, Don Whitley Scientific, Bingley, UK) with an atmosphere of 10 % CO2, 10 % H2 in N2. Bacteria on agar plates were stored up to 3 weeks at 4 °C as work-plates. Liquid cultures of KF7 and LGG were prepared by inoculating 10 mL of MRS broth with a single colony from an agar plate and incubating overnight at 37 °C in an anaerobic workstation. Escherichia coli OP50 (OP50) was purchased as food of nematodes from Sunny Biotech Co., Ltd. (Fujian, China). Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC27853 (PA), Bacillus cereus ATCC14579 (BC) and Escherichia coli ATCC25922 (EC) were purchased as positive control strains from ATCC. OP50, BC and EC were stored at −80 °C in Lennox Broth (LB, Sangon, Shanghai, China) broth containing 50 % (v/v) glycerol, and grown on LB agar and LB broth at 37 °C. Working plates were stored at 4 °C up to 3 weeks. Liquid cultures of OP50 and positive control strains were prepared by inoculating 10 mL of LB broth overnight at 37 °C.

Analysis of whole genome sequencing

The whole genome information of KF7 (Accession number: NZ_CP067365) and LGG (Accession number: FM179322) were downloaded from National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The genome sequences of these two strains were compared to the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB, http://www.mgc.ac.cn/VFs/) and PathogenFinder (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/PathogenFinder/), and the annotation results with similarity greater than 50 % and E value less than 1 × 10−10 were selected. The predicted gene sequences were compared to the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD, https://card.mcmaster.ca/) for annotation using Resistance Gene Identifier (RGI). Default values of the CARD database were used for parameters/comparison thresholds.

In vitro toxicity study

Antibiotic resistance tests

The drug sensitivity was tested by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) panel (Rychen et al., 2018). This guideline specifically includes the drugs and microbial critical values considered for the antimicrobial susceptibility tests of L. rhamnosus. These susceptibility tests used the gram-positive bacteria susceptibility plate (Thermo Fisher, USA), and are conducted in accordance with the instructions of the Gram-positive bacteria susceptibility plate.

Indole tests

Single colonies on working plates of KF7, LGG and positive control strain EC were picked respectively and inoculated into peptone water (tryptophan broth) from Hopebio, Qingdao, China. The blank control group did not carry out any operation. After culturing at 37 °C for 24 h ± 2 h, 0.2 mL–0.3 mL (2–3 drops) of Kovacs indole reagent (Hopebio, Qingdao, China) was added. For the positive reactors, a red ring appeared on the upper layer, and for the negative reactors, a yellow ring appeared on the upper layer.

Hemolysis tests

Bacterial suspensions of KF7, LGG and positive control strain BC were streaked and inoculated on the Columbia Blood Agar Base plates (Hopebio, Qingdao, China) with 20 % (v/v) off fiber sheep blood (Hopebio, Qingdao, China). Then, these blood plates were inverted and placed in a 37 °C incubator for 24–48 h to observe whether hemolytic phenomenon occurred. Three sets of parallel repeats were set up for the tests at least.

Amino acid decarboxylase activity tests

Single colonies of KF7, LGG and positive control strain SA were picked respectively and inoculated into lysine decarboxylase broth (Hopebio, Qingdao, China) and ornithine decarboxylase broth (Hopebio, Qingdao, China). The blank control group did not carry out any operation. Then, sterilized liquid paraffin (Sangon, Shanghai, China) was dropped to cover the surface of the medium. Subsequently, they were cultured at 37 °C overnight to observe the color of lysine or ornithine decarboxylase broth. Yellow was considered as a negative result, and purple or fuchsia was considered as a positive result.

Biogenic amine production tests

Single colonies on working plates of KF7, LGG and positive control strain BC were picked respectively and inoculated into MRS and LB broth added with 0.1 % (m/v) Histidine or Tyrosine (Sangon, Shanghai, China) at 37 °C to stationary phase. The bacterial concentrations were counted by the drop plate method.

Refer to the method of Liu et al. (Liu & Liu, 2021), the qualitative detection of biogenic amines adopted the method of double-layer medium (Bottom medium: MRS agar medium or Tryptic Soy Agar (TSA, Becton, Dickinson and Company, USA). Upper medium: 1.5 % (m/v) agar, 0.006 % (m/v) bromocresol purple). The bacterial suspensions in the stationary phase were spread on the bottom layer plates at 37 °C for 48 h. Then, covered them with upper layer medium. Because biogenic amines are alkaline substances, the result is judged as negative in yellow and positive in red or purple.

The quantitative determination of biogenic amines produced by bacteria adopts high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Prepared the standard stock solutions of 1 g/L histamine and tyramine with 0.4 mol/L perchloric acid as solvent. Then, prepared the mixed standard solutions with final mass concentrations of 1.0, 2.0, 5.0, 10, 20 and 30 mg/L, and the histamine or tyramine standard solution with a concentration of 20 mg/L. They were prepared immediately before using.

Sample pretreatment: 2 mL 0.4 mol/L perchloric acid was added into 1 mL bacterial suspensions in the stationary phase. Ultrasonically disrupted (300 w, 2 s of ultrasound, with an interval of 7 s, for 5 min) and centrifuged 2500 r/min for 10 min twice to get supernatant fluid. Diluted the supernatant with 0.4 mol/L perchloric acid to 10 mL. Derivative of standard solutions and samples: Added successively 400 μL 2 mol/L sodium hydroxide sequence, 600 μL saturated sodium bicarbonate solution for buffering, 4 mL of dansyl chloride solution (10 g/L in acetone) to 2 mL of standard solution or sample. Then, the reaction was carried out in a 40 °C water bath in the dark for 45 min. At the end of the reaction, added 200 μL of 25 % (m/m) ammonia solution to terminate the reaction. After mixing, removed residual dansyl chloride solution by Nitrogen blowing instrument (Eyela, Japan) and diluted to 5 mL with acetonitrile for chromatography (Merck, Germany). After derivative treatment, filtered through 0.22 μm organic filter membrane for analysis and detection by HPLC (Agilent Technologies 1200 Series, USA). Chromatographic conditions: T3 column (3 mm × 150 mm,3.5 μm, Waters Atlantis, USA), ultraviolet detector, wavelength 254 nm, column temperature 35 °C, injection volume 10 μL, flow rate 1 mL/min, mobile phase A: acetonitrile, and mobile phase B: water. Gradient elution was performed using the method: 0 min: A (60 %), B (40 %); 22 min: A (85 %), B (15 %); 25 min: A (100 %), B (0 %); 32 min: A (100 %), B (0 %); 32.01 min: A (60 %), B (40 %), 37 min: A (60 %), B (40 %).

D-lactic acid and L-lactic acid production tests

Single colonies on working plates of KF7 and LGG were picked respectively and inoculated into MRS broth and sterile milk at 37 °C to 24 h. According to the methods of L-Lactic acid assay kit (Sangon, Shanghai) and L-Lactic acid assay kit (AmyJet Scientific, UK) to measure the content of D-lactic acid and L-lactic acid produced by these two strains.

Nematodes growth conditions

Wild nematodes N2 was purchased from SunnyBiotech Co., Ltd. (Fujian, China) and was stored at −80 °C in cryopreservation solution (0.59 % (m/v) NaCl, 0.68 % (m/v) KH2PO4, 30 % (v/v) glycerol, 0.3 % (v/v) 0.1 M MgSO4 liquor in deionized water). Nematodes were carried out as previously described with minor modifications (Zhou et al., 2018). Eggs of nematodes were first isolated from mature adults using a hypochlorite solution (9 % Sodium hypochlorite and 8 M NaOH) and allowed to hatch on fresh Nematode Growth Medium (NGM) agar plates seeded with OP50 as a food source at 20 °C in order to obtain a synchronized nematodes population. The synchronized nematodes at fourth larval (L4) stage were then collected in M9 buffer (0.5 % (m/v) NaCl, 0.6 % (m/v) Na2HPO4, 0.3 % (m/v) KH2PO4, and 1 M MgSO4 liquor in deionized water).

In vivo toxicity study

Preference tests

The bacterial suspensions of KF7, LGG and the pathogenic control strain PA in the stationary phase were respectively mixed with OP50 in a 1:1 proportion. Mixed suspension and OP50 were used as nematode food in NGM plate as preference test plates. Preference tests were conducted in two modes. The L4-stage nematodes fed with OP50 (Mode 1) or OP50 mixed probiotics (Mode 2) were washed with M9 buffer, centrifuged twice at 135 rcf for 1 min, and transferred to an empty NGM plate for 1 h to clear the adherent bacteria. About 30 to 40 nematodes were randomly selected and placed on the preference test plates at a distance of 5 mm from the edge of the two ends and equidistant from the central colony. The number of nematodes entering the central bacterial circle was counted at 1 h and 2 h, respectively. Three parallel experiments were performed in each group.

Group setting

Normal diet group: Feed OP50. Safety control group: Feed LGG with OP50 as 1:1 proportion. Test group: Feed KF7 with OP50 as 1:1 proportion. Pathogen control group: Feed PA with OP50 as 1:1 proportion.

Spawning capacity

Ten L4 stage nematodes were randomly selected and transferred to a new probiotic plate (one/plate) for culture at 20 °C to calculate the number of eggs laid for six days until the end of the breeding period (Roh et al., 2009). Three parallel experiments were performed in each group.

Developmental ability of eggs

The eggs were washed and transferred to a probiotic culture plate and cultured at 20 °C. The developmental ability of the eggs at L1-L4 stages was observed and recorded with nematode microscope and Motic Images Plus 3.0 (Motic, China). The length and width of each group in L4 stage were measured and compared. Three parallel experiments were performed in each group.

Movement parameters of adult nematodes

Movement parameters of adult nematodes was shown by head swinging, body bending and pharyngeal pumping at 6, 8, 10 and 12 days at different probiotics plates. Each nematode was video recorded by nematode microscope and Motic Images Plus 3.0 (Motic, China). And the number of head swings and body bending within 30 s was calculated. The number of pharyngeal pumping within 10 s was calculated.

Longevity of nematodes

Thirty OP50-fed stage L4 nematodes were randomly selected and transferred to different probiotics, OP50 and PA plates (0–6 days: NGM plates with 5 % (m/v) 5-fluorodeoxyuridine (5-FUdR) at 20 °C until the last nematode died (Yun et al., 2022). During the period, the number and status of nematodes, including survival, loss and death, are counted daily. The dead nematodes were considered dead if they did not respond to the light touch of the pick needle.

Gut microbiota of nematodes

OP50-fed stage L4 nematodes were randomly selected and transferred to different probiotics, OP50 and PA plates (0–6 days: NGM plates with 5 % (m/v) 5-FUdR) respectively at 20 °C for seven days. The adding of 5-FUdR is to prevent progeny and keep all nematodes are adults. Collected 300 nematodes from each group and washed them with M9 buffer for three times to clean the bacteria adhered to the surface of the nematodes. Prepared 3 samples for each group of nematodes and sent the samples to Sinotech genomics (Shanghai, China) Co., Ltd. by dry ice for 16S sequencing.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were performed with Microsoft Excel 2016 (USA). All data are presented as mean ± standard error mean (SEM) of three experiments with duplicate or triplicate measurements. The results from in vitro experiments were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The results from in vivo experiments in nematodes were evaluated using one-way ANOVA with Tukey's multiple comparisons test. In each assay, results were compared with an appropriate control group. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05, and high statistical significance as P < 0.01. The microbiota sequencing data were analyzed using QIIME (Quantitative Insights Into Microbial Ecology) and R version 4.4.2. To investigate associations between gut microbiota and host physiological metrics, we implemented an integrated data processing strategy. Microbial abundance data underwent centered log-ratio (CLR) transformation to address compositional constraints, while physiological indicators were standardized via Z-score normalization to eliminate scale effects. Pseudo-counts (10−6) were added to zero values to enable logarithmic operations. Spearman's rank correlation coefficients were then computed to assess monotonic relationships between genus abundances and physiological traits, with statistical significance evaluated through 1000-permutation tests. To mitigate false positives from multiple comparisons, Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied. Results were visualized in hierarchically clustered (average linkage) heatmaps where correlation strengths were represented by blue-red color gradients, with all bacterial genus names consistently formatted in italic Arial font. Analyses were conducted in Python 3.9 using SciPy (v1.11.1), scikit-bio (v0.5.8), and seaborn (v0.12.2) libraries.

Results

Genome-based safety analysis

The whole genome sequences of KF7 (accession number: NZ_CP067365) and LGG (accession number FM179322), submitted by Liu J. and Paulin L.G., respectively, were retrieved from NCBI database. The KF7 genome consists of one chromosome with a length of 3, 172, 945 bp and three plasmids, whereas the LGG genome contains only of one chromosome with a length of 3, 010, 111 bp. Genome-based safety analysis was performed in three areas: virulence factor genes, pathogenic protein genes and antibiotic resistance genes, as shown in Table 1. Virulence factor genes of pathogenic bacteria have the capacity to endure, establish infection, and cause disease in the host. A total of 50 potential virulence factor genes were identified in both KF7 and LGG using VFDB (Supplementary File 1). However, all of these genes exhibited low coverage (less than 20 %) and high overall identity when aligned against known virulence factors in the VFDB. Moreover, both KF7 and LGG were predicted to be non-pathogenic to humans, with low probabilities of being human pathogens, about 0.195 and 0.198, respectively. In addition to virulence factors, antibiotic resistance is another critical aspect of safety evaluation. The extensive use of antibiotics in human healthcare and agriculture has led to the emergence of drug-resistant strains, posing a serious threat to public health. When the KF7 and LGG genomic data were compared against the CARD, no “perfect” or “strict” hits of antibiotic resistance genes were detected. Overall, the genomic analysis indicates that KF7 and LGG are unlikely to present significant risks in terms of virulence or antibiotic resistance, supporting their potential as safe probiotic candidates.

Detection of harmful compounds in vitro

Indole tests

Certain bacteria possessing tryptophanase can break down tryptophan in peptone to produce indole, a metabolite that may exert adverse effects on human health (Sinha et al., 2024). Indole can react with p-aminobenzaldehyde to form a red color complex. Qualitative analysis is usually performed using a colorimetric method based on this principle. In the present assay, the positive control strain EC exhibited a distinct red ring at the contact surface (Fig. 1A), indicating a positive result. In contrast, neither KF7 nor LGG produced any red coloration, confirming negative results and demonstrating that KF7, like LGG, lacks tryptophanase activity.

Indole tests (A), hemolysis tests (B) and amino acid decarboxylase activity (C).

Hemolysis tests

Some pathogen bacteria can produce hemolysin during their growth phase, eading to the rupture and lysis of red blood cells (Kavianpour et al., 2024). When hemolysin is produced, a transparent or translucent hemolytic zone can be observed around the bacterial colonies on the blood agar plate. Therefore, the hemolysis assay is commonly used for bacterial identification. The positive control strain BC formed a clear hemolytic ring around its colonies (Fig. 1B). In contrast, no obvious hemolytic zones were observed around the colonies of KF7 and LGG, indicating that KF7 lacks hemolytic activity, consistent with the safety strain of LGG.

Amino acid decarboxylase activity tests

Bacteria that harbor amino acid decarboxylase enzymes can break down amino acids through decarboxylation, resulting in the production of biogenic amines (Espinosa-Pesqueira et al., 2018). Specifically, the decarboxylation of lysine yields cadaverine, while ornithine is converted into putrescine. This reaction also releases carbon dioxide, increasing the pH of the culture broth and causing the pH indicator bromocresol purple to turn purple. As shown in Fig. 1C, both KF7 and LGG remained yellow in lysine and ornithine decarboxylase broth, whereas the positive control strain BC exhibited a purple color, indicating that neither KF7 nor LGG possesses amino acid decarboxylase activity.

Biogenic amine production tests

The decarboxylation of amino acids by amino decarboxylase can lead to the formation of biogenic amines. Excessive accumulation of these biogenic amines can cause respiratory distress, headache, and nausea (Dala-Paula et al., 2023). Qualitative detection of biogenic amines of bacteria (Fig. 2A) revealed that neither KF7 nor LGG produced detectable levels of biogenic amines, with only trace amounts observed in limited cases. In contrast, the positive control strain BC produces biogenic amines. To quantify the production of histamine and tyramine, HPLC method was employed. Fig. 2B(a) and Fig. 2B(b) showed the chromatographic peak of histamine and tyramine at retention time 9.21 min and 14.34 min, respectively. In Fig. 2B(c), histamine and tyramine were effectively separated and detected by HPLC. The production levels of histamine and tyramine by KF7 and LGG strains were presented in Fig. 2B(d and e). The standard curves of histamine and tyramine were showed in Fig. 2C(a and b), both exhibiting R2 values no less than 0.99, indicating high reliability of the analytical method. As summarized in Table 3, when KF7 and safety control strain LGG were cultured in milk for 24 h, no histamine or tyramine was detected. Similarly, neither strain produced histamine in MRS broth. However, in MRS medium, KF7 and safety control strain LGG produced tyramine at concentrations of 2.67 ± 0.01 mg/L and 22.43 ± 0.03 mg/L, respectively.

L-lactate and D-lactate production tests

Some lactate bacteria produce lactate with two isomers, L-lactate and D-lactate, depending on environmental conditions. Since D-lactate can't be metabolized in the human body (Remund et al., 2023), its accumulation may lead acidosis. As shown in Fig. 3, both strains examined in this study produced L- and D-lactate isomers in varying ratios. In Fig. 3(A and B), the contents of L- and D-lactate produced by KF7 in MRS broth, about 0.58 ± 0.07 mM and 0.03 ± 0.00 mM, were both higher than LGG (0.91 ± 0.16 and 0.06 ± 0.01 mM) with extremely significant difference (P < 0.01). In milk, however, the lactate levels were similar between the two strains. In Fig. 3C, both strains produced over fifteen times more L-lactate than D-lactate, regardless of the culture medium. KF7 exhibited higher L-lactate to D-lactate ratios (20.15 and 20.83) compared to LGG (16.15 and 16.89), indicating a reduced risk of D-lactic acidosis in human consumption.

D-lactate and L-lactate acid production of two strains in MRS broth and milk. (A) L-lactate; (B) D-lactate; (C) ratio of L-lactate to D-lactate. ∗∗ means extremely significant differences (P < 0.01).

In vivo safety analysis by nematodes

Preference tests

Dietary restriction has been demonstrated to extend lifespan across multiple species. Given that nematodes consume E. coli OP50 as its standard diet, it is essential to conduct food choice assays prior to lifespan experiments (Sutphin and Kaeberlein, 2008). As shown in Fig. 4, no significant feeding preference for any bacterial strain was observed in this study (P ≥ 0.05), under either Mode 1 or Mode 2. Therefore, the subsequent nematodes related results were not affected by dietary preferences for different strains.

Preference tests of KF7, LGG and PA with OP50. (A) mode 1; (B) mode 2; (C) results of mode 1; (D) results of mode 2. ‘ns’ means no significant differences (P ≥ 0.05).

Spawning capacity

Counting the number of eggs laid by nematodes is a commonly employed method to assess the effects of external environmental alterations on their reproductive capacity (Aleuy and Kutz, 2020). As shown in Fig. 5A, nematodes fed with KF7 laid 238 ± 22 eggs per individual, showing no significant difference compared to those fed the standard diet OP50 or the safe strain LGG (P ≥ 0.05). However, egg production in the group fed with the pathogenic strain PA was significantly lower (131 ± 8 eggs per nematode, P < 0.05), indicating that KF7 has no bad effect on eggs laid by nematodes.

Developmental ability of eggs

Body length and width of nematodes are widely used as indirect indicators of their developmental rate and growth condition (Roh et al., 2009). These parameters are commonly employed to assess the effects of various foods on the growth and development of nematodes (Liu et al., 2015). To investigate the effect of KF7 on developmental ability of eggs, the body length and width of nematodes at L4 stage were measured across all experimental groups. In Fig. 5C and D, no significant differences in body width were observed among the groups (P ≥ 0.05). However, the body length of nematodes at L4 stage fed with KF7 (950.38 ± 6.29 μm) was significantly greater than that of those fed with OP50 (885.47 ± 23.08 μm) (P < 0.01).

Movement parameters of adult nematodes

The locomotive capacity of nematodes reflects their health and survival status. Commonly used metrics for assessing the well-being of nematodes consuming various diets include the frequency of body bends, head thrashes, and pharyngeal pumps (Yu et al., 2013).

As shown in Fig. 5E, F and 5G, from day 6–12, the body bending, head swing and pharyngeal pump frequency of nematodes fed with KF7, safety control strain LGG and normal food OP50 were extremely significantly higher than those of nematodes fed with pathogen PA (P < 0.01).

Body bending frequency of nematodes fed with LGG, KF7 and OP50 was the highest, more than 9 times/30 s on the day 8 and rapidly decreased thereafter, shown as Fig. 5E. On the day 10 and 12, body bending of nematodes fed with LGG and KF7 was higher than that of nematodes fed with OP50 significantly (P < 0.05).

As shown in Fig. 5F, no significant differences were observed in heading swing among nematodes fed KF7, LGG and OP50 from day 6 to day 12. Notably, the peak head swing frequency occurred on day 10, which differed from the peak of body bending (day 8).

In Fig. 5G, the pharyngeal pump frequency of nematodes fed KF7 and LGG was higher than that of nematodes fed OP50, and there was a significant difference on day 10.

Lifespan of nematodes

The lifespan assessment of nematodes can intuitively reflect the safety of probiotics (Kim et al., 2024). As shown in Fig. 5H, no significant difference in the lifespan was observed between nematodes fed KF7, the safety strain LGG, or normal food OP50, whereas all three groups exhibited significantly longer lifespans than those fed the pathogenic strain PA (P < 0.05). In Table 4, the maximum lifespan of nematodes fed KF7 reached 33 days, matching that of the LGG group and exceeding those fed OP50 (29 days) or PA (21 days). The mean lifespan of nematodes fed KF7 and LGG were similar, 17.2 ± 0.6 days and 17.5 ± 0.8 days respectively, and both were significantly longer than those fed OP50 and PA (P < 0.05). KF7 extended lifespan by 9.6 % and LGG by 11.5 % compared to OP50. The average maximum lifespan of nematodes fed KF7 and LGG were about 30.0 ± 2.6 days and 29.3 ± 3.2 days, respectively, indicating that both LF7 and LGG had a tendency to prolong lifespan of nematodes compared to OP50 (P > 0.05). Therefore, KF7 has no bad effect on nematodes lifespan.

Gut microbiota of nematodes

The β-diversity comparative analysis of the intestinal microbiota composition of nematodes fed different microorganisms was shown in Fig. 6A. Principal component analysis (PCA) is a method that applies variance decomposition to reduce the dimensionality of multi-dimensional data, thereby extracting the most significant elements and from the data. Applying PCA analysis, the two coordinate axes that reflect the greatest difference between samples can be extracted, thus reflecting the difference of multi-dimensional data on a-dimensional coordinate graph, and then revealing the simple rules behind the complex data background. The more similar the community composition of the samples, the closer they are in the PCA plot. In Fig. 6A, the points of KF7 and LGG clustered together and were far away from OP50 and PA in the PCA plot, indicating that the gut microbiota of nematodes fed with KF7 and LGG were highly similar. The comparative results of the relative abundance of the gut microbiota in each group of nematodes are shown in Fig. 6B and C. At the phylum level, Firmicutes is the dominant phylum in the gut microbiota of nematodes fed with KF7 and LGG (Fig. 6B). In contrast, the dominant phylum in the gut microbiota of nematode fed with OP50 and PA is Proteobacteria. At the genus level, Lactobacillus is the dominant genus in the gut microbiota of nematodes fed with KF7 and LGG (Fig. 6C). Escherichia-Shigella and Pseudomonas are the dominant genus in the gut microbiota of nematodes fed with OP50 and PA, respectively. Moreover, the correlation between the major bacterial genera (the top four taxa at the genus level across all samples) in the intestinal flora and the physiological indexes of nematodes (including lifespan, eggs yield, body length and body width) is shown in the in Fig. 6D. It can be seen that Pseudomonas is negatively correlated with the lifespan (Spearman ρ = −0.86, P < 0.0001) and yield of eggs (Spearman ρ = −0.63, P < 0.01), and Escherichia-Shigella is also negatively correlated with the lifespan (Spearman ρ = −0.68, P < 0.001). Lactobacillus is positively correlated with the egg yield (Spearman ρ = 0.68, P < 0.01) and lifespan of nematodes (Spearman ρ = 0.68, P < 0.0001). Therefore, KF7 and LGG have similar effects on regulating the gut microbiota.

Discussion

Lactic acid bacteria are widely recognized as safe and play a crucial role in food and feed fermentation (Food and Agriculture Organization of the United NationsWorld Health Organization, 2002). To confirm their safety, comprehensive in vitro and in vivo studies in animal and human hosts are required (Food and Agriculture Organization of the United NationsWorld Health Organization, 2002). However, considering animal welfare and experimental costs, an increasing number of scholars advocate for the adoption of next-generation safety evaluation technologies, such as organ, zebrafish, and nematode, to replace animals in in vivo safety evaluations (Aguirre Garcia et al., 2022; He et al., 2014; Hua et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2023). In this study, safety evaluation of L. rhamnosus KF7 was investigated through complete genome analysis and phenotype assessment, both in vitro and in vivo based on nematodes.

Virulence factor assessment

Virulence factor genes are known to encode toxic proteins, cell surface structures, and membrane proteins, which can contribute to pathogenicity (Peng et al., 2023). The evaluation of virulence factors is a critical first step in probiotic safety assessment. If a strain carries virulence genes, it is deemed unsafe and requires no further safety assessment. At present, VFDB and PathogenFinder are commonly used virulence gene databases, which contain many reported virulence gene sequences and are used in many studies and guidelines (Barbour et al., 2013; Chen, 2004; Liu et al., 2022). In this study, a total 50 potential virulence genes with low coverage and no pathogenic protein were both identified in KF7 and LGG searching through these two databases (Table 1). These genes were annotated to the participate in the metabolism and transport of amino acids, energy production and conversion, and defense mechanisms. Therefore, it is not sure whether these factors completely belong to virulence genes. Similarly, Chen et al. (2024) reported that 97 potential virulence factor genes with similar results were annotated in the genome of L. rhamnosus LR-ZB1107-01. In another study, Lactobacillus delbrueckii DMLD-H1 had 87 potential virulence genes (Tang et al., 2023). Therefore, KF7 is considered safe in terms of virulence factors and requires further in vitro and in vivo safety evaluations.

Antibiotic resistance assessment

Some Lactobacillus species may harbor antibiotic resistance genes that could be transferred to pathogenic bacteria through the food chain, leading to the spread of antibiotic resistance. In this study, no genes associated with antibiotic resistance were identified in either KF7 or the positive control strain LGG, as summarized in Table 1, and both strains were confirmed to be susceptible to several antibiotics by antibiotic resistance tests (Table 2).

It is worth noting that some lactobacilli, including L. fermentum, L. gasseri, and L. salivarius, have been reported to exhibit phenotypic resistance to antibiotics like gentamicin, kanamycin, and streptomycin (Lee et al., 2022). However, such resistance is often phenotypic and not associated with horizontally transferable genetic determinants. The primary concern for public health lies in the potential mobility of resistance genes, rather than in intrinsic or non-transferable resistance mechanisms.

In summary, the KF7 strain lacks acquired antibiotic resistance genes and demonstrates susceptibility to multiple antibiotics. With regard to antibiotic resistance profiles, KF7 is considered as safe as the well-characterized probiotic strain LGG.

In vitro safety evaluation

The in vitro safety assessment of probiotics involves screening for potentially harmful metabolites, typically through indole production, hemolytic activity, amino acid decarboxylase activity, biogenic amine formation, and D-/L-lactate ratio analysis.

The indole tests can detect whether the strain can break down tryptophan (Yan et al., 2024). Tryptophan is an essential amino acid involved in protein synthesis, regulating immune function and promoting digestion. Disorders in tryptophan metabolism can lead to liver function decline and malignant tumors (Xue et al., 2023). Both KF7 and LGG showed negative results in the indole test, consistent with findings for L. rhamnosus LR-ZB1107-01 (Li et al., 2024).

Hemolysis test is a crucial indicator for assessing the safety of probiotics (Roe et al., 2022). Red blood cells, the most abundant cells in the bloodstream, are primarily responsible for transporting oxygen to tissues and removing carbon dioxide (Chen et al., 2023). Hemolysis, often triggered by hemolysins produced during bacterial growth, is a significant pathogenic factor contributing to bacterial invasion (Jeong and Lee, 2015). It leads to the destruction and complete dissolution of red blood cells, resulting in severe hemolysis. Neither KF7 nor LGG showed hemolytic activity, indicating the absence of hemolysin production.

Some bacteria contain amino acid decarboxylase, which can decarboxylize amino acids to produce amines. In this study, both KF7 and LGG showed negative in amino acid decarboxylase activity test, which is same with L. rhamnosus LR-ZB1107-01 evaluated by Li et al. (2024).

In fermented dairy products, some Lactobacillus species can produce toxic biogenic amines such as histamine and tyramine, which can accumulate in food and cause food poisoning. Histamine and tyramine are considered the most important biogenic amines from a toxicological point of view by the EFSA because their consumption at high concentrations can cause hypertension, headaches, palpitations and vomiting in certain individual (Jaguey-Hernández et al., 2021). KF7, like LGG, did not produce biogenic amines in the biogenic amine qualitative tests, a result consistent with Lactobacillus species IDCC 3401 (Lee et al., 2023). Although trace tyramine was detected in amino acid-supplemented MRS broth—22.43 ± 0.03 mg/L for LGG and 2.67 ± 0.01 mg/L for KF7, showing a statistically significant difference—these values remain well below the tolerable threshold for histamine set by the FDA and EFSA at 50 mg/kg (EFSA, 2011; FDA, 2011). Moreover, cytotoxic levels of histamine and tyramine have been reported at levels of 440 mg/kg and 301 mg/kg, respectively (Linares et al., 2016). Under the conditions of this study, the amount of tyramine produced by KF7 would require ingestion of an unrealistic volume of fermented broth—113 L—to reach the lower toxicity threshold, far exceeding typical dietary intake. Furthermore, neither KF7 nor LGG produced histamine or tyramine when cultured in milk for 24 h. Therefore, the biogenic amines produced by the experimental strain KF7 were completely within the safe range.

Lactic acid, a primary metabolite of Lactobacillus species, xists in two stereoisomeric forms: D-lactate and L-lactate. Based on the proportion of L-lactate, strains can be classified as L-type (>75 % L-lactate), DL-type (25–75 %), or D-type (<25 %) (Manome et al., 1998). In human, Lactic acid can be metabolized to pyruvate by D/L-lactate dehydrogenase (Li et al., 2022). Humans don't possess D-lactate dehydrogenase, instead, D-lactate is metabolized to pyruvate by D-2-hydroxy acid dehydrogenase (Li et al., 2022). When D-2-hydroxy acid dehydrogenase activity is diminished or inhibited, D-lactate metabolism is impaired, leading to an accumulation of D-lactate in the intestine and potentially causing D-lactate poisoning. The symptoms of D-lactate poisoning include dizziness, headache, fatigue, nausea, and vomiting, and it is particularly common in individuals with compromised immunity, such as those with short bowel syndrome (Pohanka, 2020). In this study, both KF7 and LGG produced D/L-lactate in MRS broth and milk, with L-lactate proportions exceeding 75 %, classifying them as L-type strains. This aligns with L. rhamnosus ATCC 7469, which was reported to produce 94.2 % L-lactate (Manome et al., 1998). Although no official limits for D-lactate in probiotic products have been established, the L-/D-lactate ratio of KF7 was comparable to those reported for other Lactobacillus species in studies by Lee et al. (2023) and Chai et al. (2020), confirming that KF7 produces lactate within a safe range.

In vivo safety evaluation using nematode model

The nematode is a well-established model organism in aging research. Feeding nematodes different bacterial strains allows for the assessment of probiotic effects on host survival and gut microbiota composition (Backes et al., 2021). While nematodes have been widely used for functional evaluation of probiotics, their application in probiotic safety assessment remains limited. However, nematodes can be used as model animals for toxicity studies. Bacteria such as P. aeruginosa, Bacillus megaterium, and Staphylococcus aureus can produce secondary metabolites, such as toxin, and kill the nematode quickly, or colonize in the intestine of nematodes and persistent infection resulting in slow death (Dunbar et al., 2012; Guttman et al., 2010; Tan et al., 1999). With the need for next-generation safety assessment, the nematode, as a research model, has advantages such as a similar intestine to the human, high cost performance, and a short life cycle for experimental manipulation, can be used as an alternative toxicology for in vivo safety evaluation (Hua et al., 2025).

In this study, a new probiotics safety evaluation method was established by using the reproductive, growth and development, motility, lifespan and gut microbiota of the alternative model nematodes. The in vivo study was conducted with feeding OP50 as the normal control group, LGG as the safety control group, and PA as pathogen control group. Egg-laying capacity serves as an indirect measure of nematode reproductive health and is a commonly used fertility indicator (Muschiol et al., 2009). Similarly, body length and width can indirectly reflect the growth and development condition of baby nematodes, and are commonly used indicators for evaluating the growth and of nematodes (Muschiol et al., 2009). The total eggs yield of nematodes fed KF7 is not significantly different from those of the LGG and OP50 group (Fig. 5A). KF7 strain has a longer egg-laying period (4 days) than LGG group (3 days) in Fig. 5B. The egg yield of nematode fed LGG on fourth day is less than 10 eggs/nematode. These results indicate that strain KF7 does not negatively affect the egg-laying capacity of nematodes and the growth of baby nematodes to adults.

The movement parameters of nematodes is indicative of their survival status, with the frequency of head swinging and body bending being the primary indicators used to assess their viability (Muschiol et al., 2009). As shown in Fig. 5E and F, body bending and head thrashing rates peaked on day 8 and day 10, respectively, before gradually declining. This suggests an age-related decline in physiological function beyond day 10, particularly in the OP50 group, where body bends decreased sharply and were significantly lower than in the LGG group (P < 0.05; Supplementary File 2) shown in Supplementary File2. Frequency of the pharyngeal pump can reflect the feeding ability and metabolic level of nematodes. The pharyngeal pumping rate of the nematodes fed with LGG and those fed with KF7 were higher than those fed with OP50, and much higher than those fed with PA with significant differences (P < 0.05). Lifespan of nematodes, about 2–3 weeks with strong operability, can reflect the toxicity of the substance to be evaluated. Nematodes fed with KF7 or LGG lived longer than those fed with OP50 or PA. KF7 and LGG can extend the lifespan of nematodes, with maximum lifespan extended by 13.8 days (Table 4).

In studies utilizing nematodes as model organisms, the chemotherapeutic agent 5-FUdR is commonly administered to prevent egg laying and reproduction, thereby enabling the synchronization of nematode populations by age. However, 5-FUdR has been reported to potentially alter host physiology and modify the intestinal environment (Mclntyre et al., 2021). In the present study, the same concentration of 5-FUdR was applied across all experimental groups; therefore, its potential effects were considered consistent between groups and not a source of bias in comparative analyses. Nevertheless, this assumption should be validated in future investigations.

The effects of different bacteria on β-diversity analysis showed that LGG and KF7 are close to each other and clustered together, and are far from OP50 and PA groups (Fig. 6A), indicating that the metabolic profiles of probiotic strains (KF7/LGG) differ significantly from pathogenic (PA) and basal diets (OP50), which may contribute to differential impacts on nematode intestinal health. KF7 strain had a positive effect on the regulation of gut microbiota, like LGG. Both KF7 and LGG groups significantly increased the number of Lactobacillus in the intestinal microbiota in Fig. 6B and C. LGG strain possesses a unique pilus biosynthesis gene cluster (spaCBA and spaFED) in its genome (Yun et al., 2022). The encoded SpaC protein specifically binds to mucins within the intestinal mucus layer, enhancing the strain's colonization capacity in the gut (Kankainena et al., 2009). We also found spaFED gene (LGG_02370) is located on the KF7 chromosome (NZ_CP067365.1) with query cover 100 % and percent of identity 97.30 % by blasting in NCBI database. Therefore, KF7 may had a mucosal adhesion potential that would enable its colonization within the nematode intestine. This corresponds to the high abundance of Lactobacillus in the intestinal microbiota of nematodes fed with KF7.

Correlation analysis between nematode intestinal microbiota composition and physiological indicators (Fig. 6D) revealed that Lactobacillus abundance was positively correlated with nematode lifespan and egg production, whereas Pseudomonas showed a negative correlation with both parameters. These findings are further supported by independent studies from Dong et al. (2024) and Zhang et al. (2024), which demonstrated the beneficial effects of KF7 on the host. Both research groups showed that KF7 metabolites did not compromise Caco-2 cell viability but instead protected the integrity of the Caco-2 cell monolayer barrier. Specifically, KF7 metabolites attenuated lipopolysaccharide-induced inflammatory responses and oxidative stress, up-regulated the expression of intercellular tight junction protein-related genes, enhanced transepithelial electrical resistance, and reduced monolayer permeability, thereby restoring barrier integrity.

Thus, the presence of genes associated with colonization capacity in the gut, such as spaFED, suggests a potential mechanism for its colonization and observed beneficial effects.

Conclusion

This study systematically evaluated the safety of L. rhamnosus KF7 through comprehensive genomic, in vitro, and in vivo analyses, confirming its favorable safety profile. Genome-based safety analysis and in vitro safety assessments demonstrated that the KF7 strain carries no virulence factors or antibiotic resistance genes, and exhibits no resistance phenotypes and does not produce indole. It also showed negative results in hemolysis and amino acid decarboxylase activity tests. While KF7 produced a small amount of tyramine in MRS broth supplemented with amino acids, the level was within a safe range and poses no harm to humans. Additionally, KF7 primarily produced L-lactic acid, with D-lactic acid production falling within safe limits. To further assess safety, this study established an in vivo safety evaluation method using nematodes as a model. The experimental design utilized PA as the pathogenic control group and LGG (a strain of the same species approved for infant and young children's food) as the safety control. The results indicated that KF7 has no negative impact on the egg-laying of nematodes, the development of eggs, growth, athletic, and lifespan but rather has a promoting effect on extending their lifespan. These findings collectively demonstrate that L. rhamnosus KF7 is safe for potential use in food and feed applications.
