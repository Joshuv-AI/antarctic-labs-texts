How a healthy lifestyle mediates socioeconomic inequities in healthy ageing and life expectancies: A 20-year cohort of older adults
Authors: Jinhui Zhou, Haibin Li, Changfa Xia, Wenhui Shi, Jun Wang, Chen Chen, Virginia Byers Kraus, John S Ji, Yuebin Lv, Wanqing Chen, Xiaoming Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLower socioeconomic status (SES) is associated with various adverse health outcomes, but its association with healthy ageing is less known, and whether adherence to a healthy lifestyle at late-life years can reduce socioeconomic disparities in health requires further investigation. In a 20-year cohort of 33,541 older adults aged ≥65 years across 23 provinces in China, we investigated socioeconomic disparities in mortality and unhealthy ageing considering physical, cognitive, mental, visual, and hearing functions, and evaluated whether adherence to a healthy lifestyle at late-life years can reduce these health disparities. We categorized SES into low, medium, or high using latent class analysis based on education level, financial status, and occupation, and established the healthy lifestyle score by never smoking, no heavy alcohol consumption, regular physical and leisure activity, and a healthy diet. Compared with older adults of high SES, those of low SES had increased risks of all-cause mortality and unhealthy ageing, with adjusted hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) of 1.25 (1.17, 1.33) and 1.46 (1.33, 1.60), respectively. Overall, a healthy lifestyle mediated 20.5% (16.4, 24.7) and 16.4% (12.0, 20.9) of socioeconomic inequity in all-cause mortality and unhealthy ageing, respectively. In low, medium, and high SES subpopulations, the healthiest lifestyle group had 3.18 (2.69, 3.67), 3.76 (2.85, 4.67), and 5.65 (3.75, 7.55) more years of total life expectancy, and 1.40 (1.06, 1.74), 2.73 (2.09, 3.37), and 3.90 (2.64, 5.16) more years of healthy life expectancy at age 65, respectively, compared to the least healthy group. The findings underscored the urgent need for tailored, accessible interventions in low-SES populations to mitigate health inequities and reinforced the importance of healthy lifestyle promotion, even at late-life years, for healthy ageing and longevity.Keywords: Socioeconomic disparities, Healthy lifestyle, Healthy ageing, Healthy life expectancy, Functional health

Introduction

Over the past two decades, unhealthy life expectancy has increased globally, posing a significant challenge to promoting healthy aging [1]. Lower socioeconomic status (SES) is associated with higher risks of mortality and disabilities, yet effective strategies for reducing socioeconomic health disparities remain unclear [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. Changing SES at the population level requires long-term, system-wide efforts—often spanning generations—to improve education, health care, and employment opportunities [6]. Given these considerations, targeting lifestyle modification offers a practical, individualized approach to reducing SES-related health disparities.

Current evidences on socioeconomic disparities primarily focus on mortality and morbidity risks [3,[7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15]]. However, SES-related disparities in risk of late-life adverse functional events (integrating physical, cognitive, mental, and sensory abilities–key measures of healthy ageing) and healthy expectancies have not been systematically clarified [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15]]. Maintaining a healthy lifestyle, even in later life, is associated with lower mortality risk and longer life expectancy [[16], [17], [18]]. Lifestyle seems to be an important mediator between SES and health outcomes, though studies showed varied mediation effects [7,11,13,19,20]. Compared to high-income countries, people in low- and middle-income countries often have lower educational attainment and are more likely to work in low-wage jobs, potentially altering the impacts of SES and lifestyle on outcomes [8,11]. Hence, it is essential to add new evidence from low- and middle-income countries to better understand SES-related health disparities in late-life health and evaluate the role of lifestyle in mitigating the disparities.

In this study, we analyzed data from the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS), conducted from 1998 to 2018 in a low- and middle-income country setting, to investigate the independent and joint associations of SES and healthy lifestyle with late-life health outcomes and healthy life expectancies, considering five functional dimensions and mortality. We also assessed the extent to which a healthy lifestyle reduces socioeconomic health disparities.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

Participants were recruited from the CLHLS, a nationally representative ageing cohort covering 801 counties in 23 Chinese provinces [21,22]. The study was initiated in 1998 with follow-up evaluations at 2- to 3-year intervals. It used a questionnaire adapted from the Danish longevity survey and applied a targeted random-sample strategy to enroll willing centenarians with age-matched younger controls (65–99 years), repeatedly assessing functional status at each wave [22,23].

For this analysis, we included cohort participants enrolled between 1998 and 2014 with follow-up assessments to 2018. Among 44,620 participants recruited in the CLHLS study, the final analytical sample included 33,541 participants for mortality analysis, of whom two or more health status measurements were included for life expectancy estimation, and a subsample of 10,236 participants healthy at baseline was analyzed for incident unhealthy ageing, disability, or poor functional status (Fig. 1).

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-13074) and the National Institute of Environmental Health, Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (No. 201922). All the procedures performed in this study were conducted in accordance with good clinical practice and the Declaration of Helsinki, Finland. Written informed consent was collected from all older adults.

Assessment of SES

An SES variable was generated via latent class analysis based on participants’ education level, financial status, and occupation [24]. Given the population's low educational attainment, we classified education into no schooling (0 years), primary school (1–6 years), and middle school or above (7 years or above) [25]. Financial status was classified as “financially independent” if participants had retirement wages or any paid job; otherwise, they were classified as “financially dependent” [26]. Occupation was assessed by occupational status before age 60 and classified into farmers, white-collar workers, and others [25]. Three mutually exclusive latent classes were identified considering three education levels, two financial statuses, and three occupation types, and were then grouped into high, medium, and low SES groups (Supplementary Methods) [7].

Assessment of lifestyle

An integrated healthy lifestyle score was constructed by four factors, including never smoking, no heavy alcohol consumption, ideal physical and leisure activity, and a healthy diet [7,11]. Specifically, participants who reported never smoking were considered “healthy” and coded “1,” while past smokers and current smokers were coded “0.” Daily alcohol consumption was calculated for drinkers; participants who were never-drinkers, or with daily consumption of 1 drink or less (14 g/d) for women and 2 drinks (28 g/d) or less for men, were considered to lack heavy alcohol consumption (coded as 1) [27]. Physical and leisure activities were assessed through nine domains according to a previous study [17]; they are regular exercise, personal outdoor activities, playing cards or mahjong, watching TV or listening to the radio, reading, gardening, doing housework, raising domestic animals or pets, and attending social activities. A healthy diet was assessed by nine commonly consumed Chinese foods, namely, fruits, vegetables, meat, eggs, fish and seafood, legumes and their products, pickle, garlic, and tea [17]. Summed scores for both physical and leisure activities and diet were calculated, and then standardized to a 0–1 scale. In line with previous evidence, participants in the top 40% of score distribution for each metric were considered as having ideal status (coded as 1) [17,[28], [29], [30]].

Scores of these four lifestyles (smoking, alcohol, physical and leisure activity, diet) were summed to calculate the integrated healthy lifestyle score, which ranged from 0 to 4. Participants were then classified into three groups based on the number of healthy lifestyles: least healthy (0–1 healthy factor), moderately healthy (2 healthy factors), and healthiest (3–4 healthy factors) [7,31].

Covariates

Covariates include age, sex, race, marital status, living arrangement, body mass index (BMI), and prevalence of hypertension, heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, respiratory disease, diabetes, and cancer. Due to the “obesity paradox”—where older adults with higher BMI show reduced risks of adverse health outcomes, we included BMI as a covariate rather than a lifestyle factor [7,32].

Study outcomes and follow-up

The outcomes were all-cause mortality and unhealthy aging, a composite measure covering physical, cognitive, mental, visual, and hearing functions, and defined as “yes” for participants with at least one functional problem [1,22,[33], [34], [35], [36], [37]]. Functional status was assessed repeatedly in baseline and follow-up surveys from 1998 to 2018. Date of death was obtained from family members of deceased participants and verified by qualified physicians. Follow-up time was calculated as the time from baseline investigation to the date of follow-up measurements, death, end of study, or loss-to-follow-up.

Specifically, physical disability was assessed by physical performance on three objective tests (standing up from a chair, picking up a book from the floor, and turning around 360° without help) and six activities of daily living (bathing, transferring, dressing, feeding, toileting, and incontinence). Participants who could not complete any activities independently were considered physically disabled [22,33]. Cognitive impairment was assessed by the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) adapted to the Chinese cultural context [22,34]. This 24-item instrument (score range: 0–30) assesses: orientation, registration, calculation, copying figure, word recall, comprehension, repeating, and three-step tasks. Respondents with MMSE scores < 18 or a previous diagnosis of dementia were considered to have cognitive impairment. Mental health was evaluated by psychological resources for well-being, a seven-item scale with a total score of 28 (0–4 points were assigned to each item). It measures optimism, conscientiousness, sense of personal control, positive feelings about aging, neuroticism, loneliness, and perceived loss of self-worth in older adults. Given the absence of established classification criteria for this scale and its skewed score distribution, participants scoring below the median were defined as having poor mental health [35,38,39]. Visual function was examined by a vision test, defined as “vision loss” if the participant could not see the circle on the cardboard sheet under a flashlight or if the participant was blind [36]. Hearing loss was assigned to a participant if he/she could only hear a part of what the interviewer said with or without a hearing aid, or could not hear anything [37].

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics are presented as median with interquartile range (IQR) or mean with standard deviation for continuous variables, and as number of participants (percentage) for categorical variables. The baseline characteristics among different SES groups were compared using variance, Kruskal-Wallis and χ2 tests.

We used Cox proportional hazard regression models to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for associations of SES and healthy lifestyle with all-cause mortality and unhealthy ageing. According to previous studies [7,[11], [40]], we included SES, age, sex, race, marital status, living arrangement, BMI, and prevalence of hypertension, heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, respiratory disease, diabetes, and cancer in the first model, with further addition of the healthy lifestyle score in the second model. The proportional hazards assumption was examined using Schoenfeld residuals, and we found no violation of the assumption. To estimate the mediation proportions of a healthy lifestyle in the associations between SES and adverse outcomes, we performed mediation analysis using the “mediation” package in R, generating 95% CIs via 1000 bootstrap resamples. Absolute risk reductions (ARRs) between the healthiest and least healthy lifestyle groups at different SES levels were calculated using standardized cumulative incidence estimates, adjusted to the mean of covariates.

To estimate the multiplicative interaction, we included a product term of “SES × healthy lifestyle” in Cox models and estimated the corresponding HRs of this term to measure multiplicative interaction [41]. For observed multiplicative interactions, adequate sample sizes were confirmed for SES-stratified analyses by calculating statistical power using the R package “survivalpwr.” Power estimates were ≥ 0.885 based on HRs comparing participants with 0–1 vs 3–4 healthy factors. To assess additive interaction, we estimated the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) of healthy lifestyle score (0–1 vs 3–4) and SES (low vs high) using the “epiR” package in R [7]. A RERI < 0 indicates a joint effect that is lower than the sum of individual effects. To evaluate the joint associations of SES and healthy lifestyle, we grouped participants into nine groups by SES (low, medium, high) and healthy lifestyle (0–1, 2, or 3–4 healthy factors), with the high SES and 3–4 healthy factors group as reference.

Total life expectancies (TLEs), disability-free life expectancies (DFLEs), and disabled life expectancies (DLEs) for ages 65 to 105 years were estimated using continuous-time multi-state models [42]. Transition from an unhealthy status to a healthy status was permitted (e.g., from cognitive impairment to cognitive health). All directions for changing health status and death are shown with arrows (Fig. 2). The life expectancies were estimated under the assumption that humans are unlikely to survive beyond 120 years [43]. The 95% CIs of life expectancies were generated with 1000 repetitions. Differences in life expectancies by TLEs, DFLEs, and DLEs were compared across different SES and healthy lifestyle groups [44].

Permitted transitions of health status for participants. Functions were evaluated for physical disability, cognitive impairment, poor mental health, vision loss, hearing loss, and unhealthy ageing, which combined all these five dimensions, respectively.

A total of 9.7% of older adults had missing data for SES and lifestyle measurements and were excluded from analyses. The missing rates for covariates were below 8.1% and were addressed using multiple imputation to minimize the potential bias [45]. Sensitivity analyses were performed to test the robustness of results by utilizing the complete dataset, examining each of five specific function domains, excluding participants with a follow-up time of ≤ 6 months, additionally adjusted for indicators of family support and healthcare access, using SES components separately (education level, financial status, and occupation), using SES grouped by summed SES scores (range 0–6; calculated by assigning 0, 1, and 2 points to each low-level, medium-level, and high-level SES component), and estimating life expectancies at different ages. The potential influence of unmeasured confounding was evaluated by E-value, representing the minimum strength of association for the associations of an unmeasured confounder with both independent variable (SES) and dependent variables (all-cause mortality and unhealthy ageing), conditional on the measured covariates, to fully explain away the observed exposure–outcome association [46].

All analyses were conducted using R 4.3.1 software for Windows (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided P-value threshold of < 0.05 was set for statistical significance.

Results

Participant characteristics

Overall, 33,541 older adults (median age: 90.0 [IQR: 81.0–99.0] years) were included in this study, of whom 60.4% (20,266/33,541) were female. The median follow-up time was 3.79 years, with 25,299 deaths recorded during the follow-up. Participants with high SES, medium SES and low SES were 1989 (5.9%), 4476 (13.3%), and 27,076 (80.7%), respectively (Table 1). Compared to the analyzed participants, excluded individuals tended to be younger, male, of Han nationality, married, and have higher SES, poorer lifestyle behaviors, and worse health status (Table S1).

Associations of SES with adverse outcomes and the mediating effect of a healthy lifestyle

Compared to older adults with high SES, those with medium SES showed similar all-cause mortality risk but higher unhealthy ageing risk, with HRs of 1.01 (0.94, 1.08) and 1.21 (1.10, 1.34), respectively. The risk magnitude significantly increased comparing low vs high SES older adults for both all-cause mortality and unhealthy aging, with HRs of 1.25 (1.17, 1.33) and 1.46 (1.33, 1.60), respectively. The HRs attenuated after additional adjustment for the healthy lifestyle score. A healthy lifestyle mediated 20.5% (16.4, 24.7) of the association between SES and all-cause mortality, and 16.4% (12.0, 20.9) of the SES-unhealthy aging association (Table 2).

Interaction and joint effects of SES and healthy lifestyle

We observed significant multiplicative and additive interactions of SES and healthy lifestyle in relation to all-cause mortality and unhealthy ageing (P-interaction values for multiplicative interactions ≤ 0.031; RERI and 95% CIs < 0). Compared with participants with only zero or one healthy factor, the adjusted HRs of mortality for participants with three or four healthy factors were 0.62 (0.52, 0.74), 0.73 (0.66, 0.82), and 0.76 (0.73, 0.79) for the high, medium, and low SES populations, respectively. For unhealthy ageing, corresponding HRs were 0.48 (0.37, 0.64), 0.71 (0.61, 0.84), and 0.76 (0.69, 0.84), respectively (Table S2 and Fig. 3).

ARR by lifestyle groups across different SES

Standardized cumulative incidence at the median follow-up showed ARRs for mortality were 18.70%, 11.31%, and 14.11% in high, medium, and low SES groups, respectively. This represents a 1.33-fold higher ARR in high versus low SES groups. Similarly, ARRs for unhealthy ageing were 24.88%, 8.54%, and 8.36% in high, medium, and low SES groups, respectively, corresponding to a 2.98-fold higher ARR in high vs low SES groups (Fig. 5).

Life expectancies by SES and lifestyle groups

The DFLE, DLE, and TLE measured by unhealthy ageing at age 65 were 7.65 (7.50, 7.80), 8.25 (8.09, 8.40), and 15.91 (15.69, 16.12) years for the overall population. Compared to older adults with low SES, those with high SES experienced longer DFLE but shorter DLE; differences were 3.85 (3.44, 4.26) and −1.16 (−1.53, −0.79) years, respectively. Compared with the least healthy participants, those with the healthiest lifestyle experienced 3.18 (2.69, 3.67), 3.76 (2.85, 4.67), and 5.65 (3.75, 7.55) more years of TLEs at age 65 in low, medium, and high SES groups, respectively. Specifically, the corresponding extended DFLEs were 1.40 (1.06, 1.74), 2.73 (2.09, 3.37), and 3.90 (2.64, 5.16) years, respectively; extended DLEs were 1.79 (1.41, 2.17), 1.02 (0.34, 1.70), and 1.75 (0.57, 2.93) years, respectively (Fig. 6).

Sensitivity analyses

The associations of SES, healthy lifestyle with all-cause mortality and unhealthy ageing remain robust in analyses using the complete dataset, excluding participants with follow-up time ≤ 6 months, additionally adjusted for family support and healthcare access, and using original SES components separately considering participants’ education level, financial status, and occupation, and using SES grouped by summed SES scores (Tables S3 to S16 and Figs. S1 to S14). Similar patterns of DFLE, DLE, and TLE were observed for analyses using original SES components (Figs. S15 to S17), and using SES grouped by summed SES scores (Fig. S18). The E-value was 1.61 for all-cause mortality and 1.92 for unhealthy ageing, suggesting that residual confounding could explain the observed associations only if there exists an unmeasured confounder having a relative risk of at least 1.61 with both SES and all-cause mortality, or having a relative risk of at least 1.92 with both SES and unhealthy ageing, conditional on measured covariates (Table S17).

The findings remain robustness in analyses of each specific function domain regarding: 1) excess risk of low SES and the mediation role of healthy lifestyle (Table 2); 2) interaction and joint associations (Figs. 3 and S19); 3) ARRs at median follow-up time, and 3 or 5 years (Figs. 5, S20, and S21); 4) health expectancy estimations by different functions (Figs. S22 to S26) and at ages of 80 or 90 years (Figs. S27 to S38); 5) unhealthy life expectancy proportions from ages 65 to 105 years by different functions (Figs. S39 to S43).

Discussion

In this national cohort of Chinese older adults, low SES was associated with higher risks of all-cause mortality and unhealthy ageing. A healthy lifestyle mediated nearly one-fifth of observed socioeconomic inequity in health. We found significant interactions between SES and healthy lifestyle, indicating greater benefits from a healthy lifestyle for participants with high SES compared to those with low SES. The group with high SES and the least healthy lifestyle exhibited the highest risk of unhealthy ageing and the second-highest risk of all-cause mortality. Similar patterns were observed for assessing healthy lifestyle-related ARRs and extension of health expectancy, showing larger differences in the high SES group.

While socioeconomic disparities in mortality have been documented in several studies, evidence regarding unhealthy ageing and late-life functions remains limited. Compared to high SES, participants with low SES had a higher risk of all-cause mortality; the risk magnitudes are 113% increase in a US NHANES study, 96% in a UK Biobank study, and 26% in a multicohort study covering the US, Europe, and Australia [2,7]. Aligning closely with the multicohort study [2], low SES participants in our study experienced a 25% higher risk of all-cause mortality, and had comparable years of life lost (2.1 vs 2.7 years). Crucially, we further evaluated health expectancies to unveil the composition of life years lost, demonstrating that this loss comprises a 3.85-year reduction in healthy life expectancy and a 1.16-year increase in unhealthy life expectancy. Our study emphasized the importance of SES in all-cause mortality and extended this understanding to unhealthy ageing.

Low SES may elevate the risk of mortality and adverse functional events among older adults by exposing them to increased stressors, poisonous or harmful substances, and limited access to health care services and age-friendly environments. This can lead to unhealthier lifestyles and higher morbidity of diseases [7,14]. In this study, we conducted mediation analyses to explore the role of a healthy lifestyle as a potential solution to address health disparities. Consistent with previous studies, we observed that a healthy lifestyle serves as an important mediator in the relationship between SES and adverse outcomes [7,11], with mediating proportions of 20.5% for all-cause mortality, 16.4% for unhealthy ageing, and ranging from 11.1% to 20.7% across five function domains examined. The proportions are consistent with previous reports of 4.0% to 26.0% for all-cause mortality [2,7,19], and 11.7% for dementia [11].

Across all socioeconomic groups, older adults maintaining healthier lifestyles consistently showed lower risks of all-cause mortality and unhealthy ageing, but with diminished protective effects of a healthy lifestyle among low SES individuals compared to their high SES counterparts (significant multiplicative and negative additive interactions). This aligns with two US studies [7,47], but contrasts with UK Biobank findings that reported a stronger protective effect in disadvantaged groups [7,40]. Limited evidence exists regarding SES-lifestyle interactions in unhealthy aging or adverse functional events. Two dementia studies examined it using UK Biobank data, but yielded conflicting results [11,12]; one supported our finding of enhanced lifestyle benefits in high-SES populations [12]. Interestingly, our findings revealed that the high SES and least healthy lifestyle group had the highest risk of unhealthy ageing, and the second-highest risk of all-cause mortality [48], following the low SES and least healthy lifestyle group [7,11]. The observed discrepancies across populations regarding SES-lifestyle-outcome relationships may stem from methodological variations in exposure assessment, contextual socioeconomic differences, and cohort characteristics. It requires further research into the complex interplay between SES and health behaviors.

Adopting healthy lifestyles was associated with life expectancy extension and healthcare expenditure reduction [10,49], which partly explained the SES-related health disparities. Promoting healthy lifestyles among older adults would be vital to reduce the risk of death, prevent unhealthy ageing, and prolong health span. However, findings on HR and healthy life expectancy both indicated a reduced protective effect of a healthy lifestyle in low-SES individuals. It may stem from disparities in healthcare access and heightened exposure to environmental stressors. High-SES individuals typically benefit from better healthcare access, facilitating earlier detection and better management of health conditions [[50], [51], [52]]. This preserves the advantages gained from healthy lifestyles. Conversely, limited healthcare access in low-SES individuals often results in poorly managed health problems, which can negate lifestyle benefits. Furthermore, low-SES individuals experience greater exposure to chronic stressors such as financial insecurity, anxiety, depression, job strain, noise pollution, environmental toxins, and poorer air/water quality [[53], [54], [55]]. These factors likely counteract the protective effects of healthy behaviors. This evidence underscores that achieving health equity requires addressing fundamental social determinants and barriers, extending beyond individual behavior change. Future research should focus on developing lifestyle strategies tailored to different socioeconomic settings. It might require a lifelong effort spanning from early childhood to late life stages [56]. Beyond lifestyle factors, addressing socioeconomic health inequalities might require complementary strategies targeting other individual characteristics (e.g., marital status, living arrangement, environmental exposures, disease morbidity, access to health care and long-term care) and local area characteristics (e.g., government expenditures, the proportion of immigrants, and the educational attainment of residents, e.g. college graduates or not) [8,[57], [58], [59], [60], [61], [62]].

The strengths of this study are noteworthy. First, we used a nationally representative sample (n = 33,541) from China’s longest-running ageing cohort and provided robust epidemiological evidence on SES-lifestyle-health outcome relationships. Second, longitudinal repeated measurements of multidimensional health domains allowed us to comprehensively evaluate the role of SES and lifestyle in healthy ageing and health expectancies. Third, we examined the complex interactions and joint associations regarding all-cause mortality, unhealthy ageing, and five specific function domains, to enhance our understanding of the interplay between SES and healthy lifestyle in adverse outcomes. Finally, we extended our findings to include health expectancies under different definitions, offering a clearer perspective on the impacts of SES and healthy lifestyle on healthy ageing.

Nevertheless, several limitations warrant attention when interpreting results. First, since participant health status was evaluated only at 2- to 3-year intervals, the lack of functional measurements immediately prior to death could bias life expectancy estimates and underestimate unhealthy years. Future research should therefore incorporate more frequent assessments. Second, although CLHLS aimed to interview all voluntarily participating centenarians in the sampled areas, differences between participants and non-participants could bias the results. Third, participants excluded from the analyses were predominantly high SES and less healthy populations, potentially skewing the risk estimates. Fourth, information on variables like financial status remains quite crude in the study; the findings may only reflect the approximate reality for given individuals. Fifth, mental health was dichotomized based on the median due to the lack of established classification criteria. Future research employing scales with validated clinical thresholds would be valuable to confirm our findings. Sixth, although adjusted for multiple personal characteristics and health covariates, residual confounding may exist due to unmeasured factors (e.g., healthcare access, social support, environmental factors, and biomarkers). Seventh, despite adequate statistical power being confirmed for all-cause mortality, unhealthy aging, and poor mental health analyses across SES strata, the limited high-SES participants might reduce our capacity to detect SES-stratified associations of healthy lifestyle with other domains. Lastly, while this cohort provides unique insights into healthy longevity, findings may not be generalized to older populations at younger ages or in higher-SES contexts due to its advanced age profile, overrepresentation of low-SES population, and female-predominant sex distribution.

Conclusion

In conclusion, socioeconomic health disparities still warrant attention even in late-life years. While maintaining a healthy lifestyle was demonstrated to be effective in reducing late-life adverse outcome risk and extending health expectancies, the benefits are disproportionately greater in high SES populations. These findings revealed that adherence to a healthy lifestyle remains essential in older adults to mitigate socioeconomic health disparities and promote healthy longevity. Crucially, interventions must extend beyond healthy lifestyle promotion to address social determinants of health, especially via tailored, accessible interventions in low-SES communities to combat existing health inequities.
