Patient-specific guides and osteosynthesis versus CAD/CAM occlusal splints in orthognathic surgery: a systematic review and meta-analysis of accuracy, operative time, and complications
Authors: Xiaoshuang Guo, Hao Gong, Fangning Zhao, Guodong Song, Xiaolei Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOrthognathic surgery increasingly relies on digital transfer workflows, most commonly CAD/CAM occlusal splints or patient-specific implants (PSI). However, prior meta-analyses were limited by methodological heterogeneity, sparse randomized evidence, and insufficient axis-specific analyses, leaving the magnitude and clinical relevance of any accuracy advantage unclear. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to compare PSI and CAD/CAM splint workflows in orthognathic surgery.MethodsThis study followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines and was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251039754). PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane were searched from inception to August 23, 2025. Comparative clinical studies evaluating PSI versus CAD/CAM splints in orthognathic surgery were included. Outcomes included linear accuracy, angular accuracy, operative time, and postoperative complications. The primary linear accuracy outcomes were axis-specific translational deviations across the mediolateral, anteroposterior, and superoinferior directions. Angular accuracy was assessed across pitch, roll, and yaw. Randomized trials were assessed using RoB 2.0, and non-randomized studies using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale.ResultsThirty studies (12 RCTs and 18 cohort studies; 1317 patients) were included; 21 provided sufficient quantitative data for meta-analysis and 9 were synthesized narratively. Compared with CAD/CAM splints, PSI was associated with smaller axis-specific linear deviations across all three translational axes (all P < 0.01); a supportive combined linear summary showed the same direction but was not treated as a primary outcome. A possible advantage in angular accuracy was suggested (MD − 0.54°, 95% CI − 1.07 to − 0.01), but this was based on only six maxillary comparisons and should be interpreted cautiously. No statistically significant difference in postoperative complications was detected (RR = 1.01, 95% CI 0.64–1.60, P = 0.959). For operative time, current evidence did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference between workflows; however, few studies and substantial heterogeneity precluded firm conclusions.ConclusionCompared with CAD/CAM splints, PSI workflows were associated with smaller axis-specific translational deviations, while the available angular evidence suggests possible but limited rotational advantages. Current evidence remains inconclusive regarding operative time and does not demonstrate a significant difference in complication rates. PSI may be considered selectively in cases requiring high transfer precision; further studies should assess long-term stability and patient-reported outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09056-1.

Introduction

Orthognathic surgery aims to correct dentofacial deformities while restoring stable occlusion and harmonious facial esthetics [1, 2]. Achieving precise surgical outcomes remains a fundamental objective. With the development of three-dimensional virtual surgical planning and computer-aided design/manufacturing (CAD/CAM) technologies, CAD/CAM occlusal splints have provided a reproducible and efficient means of transferring preoperative surgical plans into the operating theatre with a clinically acceptable level of accuracy, and have become a conventional tool in routine practice [3, 4]. However, splint-based transfer has inherent limitations, as it relies on precise interocclusal registration and stable condylar seating [5, 6]. These prerequisites are difficult to satisfy in complex clinical scenarios, such as facial asymmetry, multi-piece osteotomies, or large sagittal movements [4, 7]. In these scenarios, minor errors in condylar positioning or vertical dimension might lead to significant translational and rotational deviations [8].

Patient-specific implant (PSI) workflows, also described as patient-specific osteosynthesis, represent a “splintless/waferless”-oriented alternative [9]. PSI workflows typically integrate bone-borne cutting guides with customized titanium plates designed to match the patient’s unique skeletal anatomy [10]. In principle, PSI workflows reduce dependence on interocclusal registration, minimize intraoperative plate bending, and may improve reproducibility of both segment positioning and fixation [11]. However, whether these theoretical advantages translate into clinically meaningful benefits remains debated.

Beyond accuracy, safety is also a clinically relevant consideration when comparing transfer workflows. Splint-based techniques may be affected by condylar seating errors, unstable interocclusal registration, and occlusal interference, which can contribute to postoperative occlusal discrepancy and, in some cases, revision. PSI workflows require guide adaptation and customized hardware placement, raising potential concerns regarding plate exposure, dental root injury, infection, and reoperation [12–14]. Although several comparative studies have reported complication outcomes, these data have not been systematically synthesized, and the relative safety profile of PSI versus CAD/CAM splints remains insufficiently defined.

Two recent reviews have addressed related topics but differ from the present study in scope and methodology. Kobravi et al. conducted a broad systematic review of digital innovations in orthognathic surgery, encompassing virtual surgical planning, multiple digital transfer methods, and conventional model surgery; however, their review was not designed as a focused quantitative comparison of PSI versus CAD/CAM splint workflows, nor did it provide axis-specific accuracy analyses [1]. Nguyen et al. performed a meta-analysis comparing patient-specific implants with stock plates across craniomaxillofacial surgery more broadly, rather than evaluating orthognathic surgery as a distinct setting with procedure-specific accuracy outcomes [15]. In addition, these reviews did not incorporate several recently published comparative studies. For example, Yan et al. and Kuehle et al. separately reported that PSI workflows may improve maxillary repositioning accuracy compared with CAD/CAM splints in skeletal Class II patients and in segmental Le Fort I maxillary surgery, respectively [7, 16], whereas Morsi et al. observed no significant difference between the two groups in mandibular repositioning accuracy [17]. Consequently, important questions regarding the magnitude, consistency, and clinical relevance of any PSI-related accuracy advantage remain unresolved, including axis-specific translational and rotational outcomes, operative efficiency, and complication profiles.

Given the rapid growth of new comparative data, an updated systematic review and meta-analysis is warranted. Overall, the current literature suggests a possible accuracy advantage of PSI in selected orthognathic transfer workflows, but remains limited by methodological heterogeneity, variable outcome definitions, inconsistent complication reporting, and insufficient high-quality randomized evidence. Therefore, the present study was designed to quantitatively compare PSI and CAD/CAM splint-based techniques across orthognathic procedures and to explore heterogeneity through prespecified subgroup and sensitivity analyses. Our review question was structured according to the PICO framework: in patients undergoing orthognathic surgery for dentofacial deformities (P), do PSI workflows (I), compared with CAD/CAM occlusal splint workflows (C), improve plan-to-postoperative transfer accuracy and differ in operative time or postoperative complications (O)?

Materials and methods

Protocol and standards

This study was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251039754) and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines. The primary accuracy outcomes remained aligned with the registered outcomes, which specified translational and rotational accuracy. Protocol deviations and post-registration methodological updates included not analyzing long-term skeletal stability as a separate pooled outcome because eligible comparative data were insufficient, inconsistently reported, or reported in formats not suitable for quantitative synthesis, adding operative time and postoperative complications as secondary clinical outcomes, and adding GRADE assessment and exploratory subgroup/sensitivity analyses to improve transparency and interpretability.

Search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted from inception to August 23, 2025, online in PubMed, Embase (via Ovid), and the Cochrane Library, in accordance with Cochrane Handbook recommendations [18]. Records were exported to EndNote (Clarivate Analytics) for deduplication and reference management, and subsequent screening records were organized manually in Excel.

The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text keywords linked by Boolean operators (OR within domains; AND between domains) to maximize sensitivity while maintaining specificity. The three search domains corresponded to the clinical setting and the two competing workflows of interest: (1) orthognathic procedures (e.g., orthognathic surgery, Le Fort I, bilateral sagittal split osteotomy [BSSO]); (2) patient-specific fixation workflows (e.g., patient-specific implants, patient-specific osteosynthesis, cutting/repositioning guides, and customized plates); and (3) splint-based transfer methods (e.g., CAD/CAM interocclusal splints and surgical wafers). The full database-specific search syntax is provided in Supplementary Appendix S1.

In addition to the electronic database search, the reference lists of all included studies were manually screened to identify additional eligible records.

Eligibility criteria

Using the PICOS framework, the review question was defined as follows: in patients undergoing orthognathic surgery for dentofacial deformities (Population), do PSI workflows, including patient-specific cutting/repositioning guides, drilling guides, and/or customized osteosynthesis plates (Intervention), compared with CAD/CAM occlusal splint or wafer-based workflows (Comparator), improve plan-to-postoperative transfer accuracy, including axis-specific translational deviations and rotational deviations, with operative time and postoperative complications as secondary outcomes? PSI workflows were defined as patient-specific guide-, positioning-, or osteosynthesis-based transfer systems, with or without adjunctive intermediate, final, or safety splints. Eligible study designs included comparative clinical studies, encompassing randomized controlled trials and observational comparative studies. Inclusion criteria: (1) comparative clinical studies comparing PSI workflows versus CAD/CAM splints in orthognathic surgery; (2) studies with at least two arms and five patients per arm; (3) reporting quantitative data on accuracy, operative time or complications; (4) full-text articles in English. Exclusion criteria: (1) single-arm studies or those without external comparators; (2) case reports, reviews, letters, and animal/in vitro studies; (3) studies with insufficient or duplicated data.

Selection and extraction

Records were managed in EndNote (Clarivate Analytics), and screening decisions were recorded in a structured Excel file based on the predefined eligibility criteria. Two reviewers independently screened titles/abstracts and full texts. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer consulted when necessary. No formal inter-reviewer agreement statistic was calculated. Data were extracted using a standardized form and checked by a second reviewer. Extracted data included study characteristics, patient demographics, procedure details, PSI components, three-dimensional assessment methods, and outcome data on linear and angular accuracy, postoperative complications, and operative time. Data not reported in the original publications were treated as unavailable for quantitative synthesis and were synthesized narratively where appropriate. Accuracy measurement methods varied across studies. Most studies compared the virtual surgical plan with postoperative CT or CBCT images using surface-based registration, voxel-based matching, iterative closest point algorithms, landmark-based registration, or cranial-base/zygomatic-arch superimposition. Linear accuracy was reported as axis-specific translational deviations in the mediolateral, anteroposterior, and superoinferior directions or as combined three-dimensional linear deviation. Rotational accuracy was reported as pitch, roll, and yaw deviations or as combined angular deviation. Study-level measurement details are summarized in Table 2 and Supplementary Tables 3 and 4.

Quality assessment

Methodological quality was assessed by two reviewers, following the same disagreement-resolution procedure described in section "Selection and extraction". Randomized trials were evaluated using the Cochrane RoB 2.0 tool across its five domains, with overall judgments derived per the RoB 2.0 algorithm [19]. Non-randomized comparative studies were evaluated using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale across Selection, Comparability, and Outcome, with overall grades (Good/Fair/Poor) assigned per AHRQ-based conversion criteria [20]. Study-level assessments are reported in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. The certainty of evidence for the principal outcomes was additionally assessed using the GRADE approach, as described in the Cochrane Handbook [18], and is summarized in Supplementary Table 7.

Outcome assessment

Accuracy outcomes were categorized into linear accuracy, defined as translational deviations between planned and postoperative positions along three orthogonal spatial directions (mediolateral, anteroposterior, and superoinferior), and angular accuracy, defined as rotational deviations comprising pitch, roll, and yaw. The primary linear accuracy outcomes were the axis-specific translational deviations, because these were the units in which the original studies most consistently reported plan-to-postoperative deviations and did not require author-side recombination across axes. A combined three-dimensional linear deviation was also computed as a supportive summary outcome: for studies that did not directly report a combined value, the per-study combined deviation was calculated as the mean of the absolute axis-specific deviations, with the pooled standard deviation derived using the root-mean-square method. This is a non-standard aggregation that does not account for the within-study correlation between axes, and the combined estimate is therefore presented only as a supportive summary, not as the primary outcome. Additional outcomes included operative time and postoperative complications, both extracted as originally reported.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in Python (v3.11.7). Continuous outcomes were pooled as mean differences (MDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and dichotomous outcomes as risk ratios (RRs) with 95% CIs. Given the anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies, random-effects models were used for all pooled meta-analyses, with between-study variance (τ²) estimated using the DerSimonian–Laird method [21]. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic and Cochran’s Q test; these measures were used only to characterize between-study variability and were not used to determine the choice of statistical model. Sensitivity analyses included leave-one-out analysis, design-stratified sensitivity analyses for the supportive combined linear accuracy outcome and for the three primary axis-specific translational outcomes, and an exploratory quality-based sensitivity analysis excluding poor-quality cohort studies. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test when at least 10 comparisons were available. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P < 0.05.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

The systematic search identified 950 records from databases and one additional record from manual searching (total n = 951). After deduplication and screening, 47 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 30 studies were included in this meta-analysis (Fig. 1) [4, 7, 8, 12–14, 16, 17, 22–43]. Of these, 21 studies provided sufficient quantitative data for meta-analysis [4, 7, 12–14, 22−24, 26, 28–32, 34−36, 39–41, 43], while 9 studies were synthesized narratively due to incomplete or incompatible outcome reporting [8, 16, 17, 25, 27, 33, 37, 38, 42]. The eligibility-stage exclusions and corresponding reasons are reported in Fig. 1.

Quality assessment

Methodological quality was assessed using the RoB 2.0 for RCTs and the NOS for observational studies (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2; Supplementary Fig. 1).

Among the 12 RCTs, one trial was rated as high risk of bias due to substantial attrition at one-year follow-up [42], and the remaining 11 trials were rated as having some concerns [8, 17, 22, 24, 26, 28, 33–35, 37, 40]. The most common sources of concern were related to the randomization process and measurement of outcomes. Eight trials lacked adequate reporting of sequence generation or allocation concealment, and seven did not report blinding of outcome assessors for imaging-based measurements. Missing outcome data and deviations from intended interventions were generally well controlled.

Among the 18 observational studies, six were rated as good quality [12, 16, 25, 32, 38, 41], whereas 12 were rated as poor quality [4, 7, 13, 14, 23, 27, 29–31, 36, 39, 43] according to the NOS-to-AHRQ conversion. The primary limitation was inadequate control for confounding, with these 12 studies receiving zero points for comparability due to the absence of matching, stratification, or multivariable adjustment [4, 7, 13, 14, 23, 27, 29–31, 36, 39, 43]. Selection and outcome ascertainment were generally adequate, with most studies using objective imaging-based measurements and standardized follow-up protocols.

Given that most observational studies were rated as poor quality and one RCT was rated as high risk of bias, the pooled findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution. To improve transparency, a combined traffic-light plot summarizing both the NOS and RoB 2.0 assessments has been added as Supplementary Fig. 1. The GRADE certainty of evidence ranged from low to very low across the principal outcomes and is summarized in Supplementary Table 7.

Linear accuracy

Accuracy measurement methods varied across the included studies, as summarized in Table 2 and Supplementary Tables 3 and 4. Axis-specific translational accuracy, the primary linear accuracy outcome, is presented in Fig. 2 (Table 2). PSI was associated with smaller deviations than CAD/CAM splints in mediolateral translation (13 comparisons; MD = − 0.40 mm; 95% CI: −0.60 to − 0.20; p < 0.001; I² = 64.4%; Fig. 2a), anteroposterior translation (15 comparisons; MD = − 0.52 mm; 95% CI: −0.77 to − 0.28; p < 0.001; I² = 50.5%; Fig. 2b), and superoinferior translation (15 comparisons; MD = − 0.48 mm; 95% CI: −0.78 to − 0.17; p = 0.002; I² = 73.2%; Fig. 2c). Negative MD values indicate smaller deviation with PSI.

Angular accuracy

Combined three-dimensional angular deviation was analyzed for 6 comparisons from 6 studies, all involving maxillary procedures (Supplementary Table 4) [4, 7, 30, 34, 36, 39]. Random-effects meta-analysis indicated a smaller angular deviation with PSI than with CAD/CAM splints (MD = − 0.54°; 95% CI: −1.07 to − 0.01; p = 0.047), with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 42.0%; τ² = 0.170; Q = 8.62, p = 0.125) (Fig. 4). However, this estimate was based on only six maxillary comparisons and the upper confidence limit lay close to the null; the angular result should therefore be regarded as suggestive rather than definitive.

Spatial axes rotational accuracy is presented in Fig. 5 (Table 3). PSI demonstrated significant improvement in roll (6 comparisons; MD = − 0.72°; 95% CI: −1.15 to − 0.29; p = 0.001; I² = 60.2%; Fig. 5b) and yaw (6 comparisons; MD = − 0.63°; 95% CI: −1.11 to − 0.15; p = 0.010; I² = 68.4%; Fig. 5c). Given the limited number of available comparisons (k = 6 per axis), these axis-specific angular estimates should likewise be interpreted with caution. In leave-one-out analyses, the roll and yaw estimates remained directionally consistent and statistically significant after exclusion of each individual study, including Liu [36]. However, the combined angular estimate was borderline and became non-significant after omission of some studies, supporting an exploratory interpretation of the angular findings.

Postoperative complications

Nine studies reported postoperative complications, including 538 patients (PSI: 253; CAD/CAM splints: 285) (Table 4) [7, 12–14, 31, 32, 34, 35, 41]. Complications reported across studies included infection, wound complications, hardware removal, reoperation for malocclusion, neurosensory disturbance, plate exposure, and dental root injuries. Across these studies, 27 complication events occurred in the PSI group and 50 in the CAD/CAM splint group. Meta-analysis showed no significant difference in overall complications between PSI and CAD/CAM splints (RR = 1.01; 95% CI: 0.64–1.60; p = 0.959), with low between-study heterogeneity (I² = 7.6%; τ² = 0.046; Q = 8.65, p = 0.372) (Fig. 6). Complications were reported at the patient level rather than the segment level in the original studies; therefore, patient-level pooling was used for this outcome.

For mediolateral translation, significant subgroup differences were detected (Qbetween = 12.52, p = 0.002; Fig. 8a). PSI demonstrated significantly improved accuracy in patients with facial asymmetry (MD = − 0.64 mm; 95% CI: −1.06 to − 0.22; p = 0.003; I² = 0.0%) and mixed deformities (MD = − 0.36 mm; 95% CI: −0.61 to − 0.10; p = 0.007; I² = 74.8%), but not in Class III malocclusion (MD = − 0.13 mm; 95% CI: −0.62 to 0.37; p = 0.620; I² = 37.6%). For anteroposterior translation, no significant subgroup differences were observed (Qbetween = 3.69, p = 0.158; Fig. 8b). PSI showed a significant advantage only in facial asymmetry patients (MD = − 0.91 mm; 95% CI: −1.39 to − 0.42; p < 0.001; I² = 0.0%), with a trend toward significance in Class III malocclusion (MD = − 0.54 mm; 95% CI: −1.10 to 0.01; p = 0.055; I² = 43.0%) and no significant difference in mixed deformities (MD = − 0.19 mm; 95% CI: −0.64 to 0.25; p = 0.394; I² = 79.6%). For superoinferior translation, no significant subgroup differences were detected (Qbetween = 0.09, p = 0.955; Fig. 8c), and effects within individual diagnosis strata were not significant, despite the significant overall effect in the main analysis.

Accuracy among maxilla VS mandible

Subgroup analyses were conducted to investigate whether linear accuracy differed by jaw segment (maxilla vs. mandible) (Fig. 9). No significant subgroup differences were observed between maxilla and mandible for mediolateral (Qbetween = 0.05, p = 0.816; Fig. 9a), anteroposterior (Qbetween = 0.01, p = 0.943; Fig. 9b), or superoinferior (Qbetween = 2.74, p = 0.098; Fig. 9c) translation.

For mediolateral translation, PSI demonstrated significantly improved accuracy in the maxilla subgroup (MD = − 0.33 mm; 95% CI: −0.56 to − 0.10; p = 0.004; I² = 76.7%; Fig. 9a), whereas no significant difference was detected in the mandible subgroup (MD = − 0.47 mm; 95% CI: −1.31 to 0.37; p = 0.271; I² = 0.0%). For superoinferior translation, neither subgroup showed a statistically significant effect: the maxilla subgroup indicated a trend favoring PSI (MD = − 0.33 mm; 95% CI: −0.67 to 0.02; p = 0.067; I² = 82.7%; Fig. 9c), whereas the mandible subgroup remained non-significant with wider confidence intervals (MD = − 0.36 mm; 95% CI: −2.03 to 1.31; p = 0.671; I² = 34.6%; Fig. 9c).

Overall, jaw-segment subgroup analyses did not demonstrate statistically significant between-subgroup differences. The mandibular estimates were imprecise because of the limited number of comparisons.

Sensitivity analysis and heterogeneity assessment

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the influence of individual studies on the pooled estimates and heterogeneity (Table 6). This analysis was intended to evaluate individual-study influence rather than to serve as a quality-stratified sensitivity analysis. For linear accuracy outcomes, Hanafy [26] was the most influential study, with its removal reducing I² from 41.3% to 14.7% for supportive combined deviation, from 50.5% to 23.9% for anteroposterior axis, and from 73.2% to 53.3% for superoinferior axis. For angular accuracy outcomes, Liu [36] had the greatest impact, with its removal reducing I² from 42.0% to 0.0% for combined deviation, from 65.3% to 5.3% for pitch, and from 68.4% to 25.9% for yaw. For operative time, heterogeneity remained substantial after leave-one-out analysis; removal of Kim SH [31] reduced I² from 85.3% to 51.1% for total bimaxillary operation time, while removal of Chen [24] reduced I² from 95.7% to 69.2% for maxillary surgery time alone. For overall complications, heterogeneity was low (I² = 7.6%); removal of Pietzka [14] reduced I² from 7.6% to 0.0%. Across all outcomes, the direction and statistical significance of pooled effects remained unchanged after removal of any single study. In an additional design-stratified sensitivity analysis, the supportive combined linear accuracy outcome remained directionally consistent in randomized trials (k = 9; MD = − 0.56 mm; 95% CI: −0.92 to − 0.19; p = 0.003; I² = 57.4%) and observational cohort studies (k = 9; MD = − 0.46 mm; 95% CI: −0.66 to − 0.25; p < 0.001; I² = 19.0%). Design-stratified sensitivity analyses for the three primary axis-specific translational outcomes are presented in Supplementary Table 8. The direction of effect favored PSI in both randomized and cohort strata across all axes. For the mediolateral and anteroposterior axes, both strata reached statistical significance, whereas for the superoinferior axis the cohort stratum was significant but the randomized-trial stratum was not and showed substantial heterogeneity. Because all cohort comparisons contributing to these outcomes were rated poor quality and no contributing randomized trial was at high risk of bias, an exploratory quality-based analysis excluding poor-quality cohort studies coincided with the randomized-trial stratum. These analyses are interpreted as supportive and do not alter the main findings. These findings support the consistency of this supportive estimate across study designs, while the pooled results should still be interpreted cautiously because observational studies remain susceptible to residual confounding (Table 6).

Publication bias assessment

Assessment of publication bias was restricted to linear accuracy outcomes due to the sufficient number of included comparisons (k ≥ 10). Visual inspection of the funnel plots revealed a generally symmetric distribution across combined and spatial axes comparisons (Supplementary Fig. 2). This was confirmed by Egger’s linear regression tests, which indicated no significant asymmetry for combined linear accuracy (p = 0.204) or for the mediolateral, anteroposterior, and superoinferior axes (p = 0.551, 0.646, and 0.529, respectively) (Supplementary Table 6). Overall, these findings provide no evidence of substantial small-study effects in the linear accuracy analyses.

Discussion

The pursuit of precise surgical outcomes in orthognathic surgery is paramount. Although CAD/CAM occlusal splints remain the conventional standard in clinical practice, occlusal-referenced workflows can pose challenges for maxillofacial specialists due to potential variability in interocclusal registration and condylar seating during intraoperative positioning. Several studies have suggested improved accuracy with “waferless” methods using cutting guides and PSI. However, due to heterogeneous methodology and limited sample sizes, previous studies have yet to offer reliable recommendations regarding the use of PSI.

Principal findings

Synthesizing evidence from 30 studies (12 RCTs and 18 observational studies; 1317 patients), this meta-analysis showed that PSI workflows were associated with smaller axis-specific translational deviations than CAD/CAM splint workflows across the mediolateral, anteroposterior, and superoinferior axes, with a directionally consistent combined three-dimensional summary that was interpreted only as supportive and secondary to the axis-specific findings because of its non-standard aggregation; in addition, the limited available angular evidence suggested a possible advantage in rotational control, particularly in roll and yaw. These findings should be interpreted in light of the limited certainty of the underlying evidence: many contributing observational studies were rated poor quality, and the GRADE certainty was low to very low across the principal outcomes (section "Quality assessment"; Supplementary Table 7). Current evidence did not demonstrate a significant difference in complication rates and remains inconclusive regarding operative time. Several interrelated mechanisms may account for these accuracy gains. PSI workflows integrate osteotomy guidance, drilling templates, segment positioning, and customized osteosynthesis into a single bone-borne construct, transferring the virtual plan through fixed anatomical references rather than reconstructing it intraoperatively [44–46]. This guide-anchored approach reduces dependence on interocclusal registration and condylar seating, two recognized sources of error in splint-mediated transfer [34, 45], and the use of customized plates with predetermined screw vectors minimizes intraoperative plate bending and freehand adjustment [45, 47]. The precision of patient-specific plating has been described as depending on accurate matching between the plate, the bone surface, and the planned screw-hole positions, so that proper seating of the construct helps reproduce the planned position [48]. These mechanisms are most relevant to translational positioning and may also contribute to roll/yaw control, although the angular findings remain exploratory because they were based on only six maxillary comparisons; site- and axis-specific considerations are discussed in sections "Linear deviation and condylar stability" and "Rotational control". Exploratory subgroup analyses yielded numerically larger estimates in some strata, but many subgroup strata contained few comparisons and between-subgroup interaction tests were generally non-significant; these findings are therefore hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in larger studies rather than supporting specific clinical indications.

Linear deviation and condylar stability

The axis-specific linear accuracy findings support a potential advantage of bone-borne transfer, particularly in the anteroposterior and mediolateral dimensions. Conventional splint-based transfer relies heavily on the stability of the condyle-fossa relationship [34, 44]. Intraoperative “sag” or passive displacement of the condyle during intermaxillary fixation can introduce errors that propagate to the osteotomized segment once the splint is removed. PSI workflows shift the surgical plan transfer from an occlusion-referenced to a bone-anchored approach via guides and customized plates, thereby potentially mitigating errors associated with interocclusal registration and condylar seating [45, 46]. The superoinferior dimension exhibited the highest heterogeneity (I² = 73.2%). This variability likely reflects the complexity of vertical control, which is influenced not only by the fixation method but also by intraoperative muscle tone, soft tissue tension, and the degree of neuromuscular blockade. Measurement methodology may also contribute: registration methods and reference regions differed across studies (Table 2), and reliability and comparability may vary with the registration algorithm, reference region, and superimposition protocol [49, 50], with vertical measurements being especially sensitive to head orientation and reference plane definitions such as the Frankfort Horizontal Plane [51]. While PSI theoretically offers rigid vertical support, biological factors and measurement inconsistencies remain important contributors to heterogeneity, and the superoinferior estimate should therefore be interpreted with particular caution.

Rotational control

Among the rotational outcomes, the random-effects analysis indicated a possible advantage of PSI in roll and yaw control, which may be clinically relevant when precise three-dimensional repositioning is required. Because this finding rests on only six maxillary comparisons, it should be regarded as exploratory and confirmed in larger studies. Mechanistically, rotational errors in splint-based surgery can arise from the “rocking” effect of the wafer if occlusal contacts are uneven or if interferences exist in the posterior osteotomy gaps [47, 52], whereas PSI plates may constrain segment orientation through multi-vector screw fixation and pre-bent geometry. Pitch showed no significant difference between groups. This may be because pitch is the rotational component most directly coupled to the occlusal plane and may remain dependent on occlusal reference and segmental stability. Consistent with this interpretation, Wang and Choi reported that waferless PSI workflows can be challenging for controlling segmental and rotational movements in the multi-segment maxilla and proposed adding a tooth-supported wafer as an adjunct to improve segmental positioning [53]. This may partly explain why a clear PSI advantage was not observed for pitch control. Minor residual pitch errors may also be partly offset by mandibular autorotation. The study by Liu et al., which focused on hemifacial microsomia, was an influential source of heterogeneity for angular outcomes [36], and excluding it eliminated heterogeneity (I² reduced to 0%); this observation should be regarded as exploratory rather than as evidence of a confirmed diagnosis-specific advantage.

Operative efficiency and safety

Our meta-analysis did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference in operative time between PSI and CAD/CAM splint workflows. However, this finding should be regarded as insufficiently powered and inconclusive rather than as evidence of equivalence: the pooled analysis included only 198 patients across seven studies, and between-study heterogeneity was very high (I² = 85.3% for total bimaxillary operation time and 95.7% for maxillary surgery time alone). Differences in surgical scope, case complexity, surgeon experience, learning curve, and time definitions across studies may have contributed to this variability, and the available data do not support firm conclusions in either direction. Regarding safety, the comparison should not be interpreted as a contrast between a temporary splint and a permanently implanted device: in both workflows the osteotomized segments are ultimately stabilized with titanium plates and screws, the CAD/CAM occlusal splint serving only as a temporary intraoperative transfer device, whereas PSI workflows transfer the virtual plan and assist fixation through patient-specific guides and/or customized osteosynthesis components. The complication analysis therefore reflects workflow-level postoperative events rather than device-only complications. Although more crude events were reported in the splint group, the pooled estimate did not demonstrate a significant difference in overall complications, and the reported events were heterogeneous in type and attribution; the current evidence should therefore not be read as showing that splints cause more complications or that PSI is safer. Notably, Pietzka et al. [14] reported fewer dental root injuries in the PSI group, suggesting a possible safety advantage of integrated drill guides during screw placement [14], although this outcome-specific finding requires confirmation. Therefore, the current evidence on operative efficiency remains inconclusive.

Clinical implications

The axis-specific translational improvements associated with PSI were approximately 0.4–0.5 mm. A mean plan-to-postoperative discrepancy within 2 mm has been used as a success criterion for accuracy in orthognathic surgery [54]. As this defines an acceptable range rather than the smallest clinically relevant difference, a consistent directional improvement of this magnitude should not be considered clinically irrelevant solely because both workflows already lie within that range, although it is small in absolute terms. In complex cases involving large three-dimensional movements, where errors may accumulate, greater transfer precision may reduce intraoperative adjustments and facilitate orthodontic finishing.

These differences should be interpreted cautiously. The included studies used heterogeneous CT/CBCT registration and superimposition methods, reference structures, and postoperative imaging time points, which may introduce measurement uncertainty overlapping with the magnitude of the observed effects. The subgroup analyses were also limited and do not define diagnosis- or jaw-specific indications; any advantage in complex presentations such as marked facial asymmetry or large three-dimensional movements therefore remains hypothesis-generating. For more straightforward cases, CAD/CAM splints remain a reasonable standard, and the choice of PSI should weigh the expected accuracy gain against workflow, resource, and experience considerations as well as the still-limited evidence on long-term stability and patient-reported outcomes.

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the certainty of our pooled estimates is constrained by the methodological quality of the underlying evidence: as detailed in section "Quality assessment", 12 of the 18 observational studies were rated as poor quality and one RCT was rated as high risk of bias, while most remaining RCTs were rated as having “some concerns” largely due to the inherent difficulty of blinding in surgical trials. Leave-one-out analysis nevertheless showed that exclusion of any single study did not change the direction or statistical significance of the pooled effects (Table 6). The GRADE assessment also indicated low to very low certainty across the principal outcomes.

Second, measurement methods varied considerably across studies, with different registration approaches and reference regions (see sections "Selection and extraction" and "Linear deviation and condylar stability", and Table 2). The pooled estimates should therefore be interpreted as approximations of plan-to-postoperative transfer accuracy rather than as fully interchangeable measurements across studies. The combined three-dimensional linear deviation was retained only as a supportive summary because most values were author-derived from axis-specific data and may not fully capture axis-level uncertainty.

Furthermore, nine of the 30 included studies could not be incorporated into the quantitative pooled analyses because the original reports presented data in formats incompatible with standard meta-analytic computation, including median/IQR values, missing standard deviations, paired planned-versus-postoperative comparisons without within-subject variance, stability-only outcomes, condylar deviation-only outcomes, or non-standard comparators. These studies were therefore synthesized narratively rather than pooled quantitatively (Table 1, footnotes †a–†e). This limitation may reduce the completeness of the quantitative synthesis, but it avoided inappropriate pooling of non-comparable outcome formats.

Moreover, randomized and non-randomized comparative studies were pooled in the quantitative analyses. Although a design-stratified sensitivity analysis for the supportive combined linear accuracy outcome showed consistent results across study designs, the findings should still be interpreted cautiously because observational studies remain susceptible to residual confounding.

In addition, economic outcomes, including cost and cost-effectiveness, were not systematically assessed because they were not prespecified and were inconsistently reported across the included studies.

Finally, long-term skeletal stability and patient-reported outcomes (PROMs) were not consistently reported. Future research should prioritize standardized reporting protocols and focus on long-term functional and aesthetic stability.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this meta-analysis indicates that, in patients undergoing orthognathic surgery for dentofacial deformities, PSI workflows were associated with smaller axis-specific translational deviations than CAD/CAM splint workflows across the mediolateral, anteroposterior, and superoinferior axes, while the limited available angular evidence suggested possible but uncertain differences in angular control that should be regarded as exploratory and require confirmation. Current evidence does not demonstrate a significant difference in complication rates and remains inconclusive regarding operative time. PSI may be considered selectively in cases requiring high transfer precision, while current subgroup analyses do not establish diagnosis- or jaw-specific indications, and CAD/CAM splints remain a reasonable standard for more straightforward cases. Further high-quality prospective studies with standardized outcome reporting and longer follow-up are needed to confirm these findings and to evaluate long-term stability and patient-reported outcomes.
