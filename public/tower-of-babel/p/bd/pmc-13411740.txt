Experiences of older adults and healthcare providers regarding utilization of primary health care services: a qualitative study from Isfahan, Iran
Authors: Fatemeh Fatahian, Mostafa Maleki, Maedeh Kholafaei, Majid Rahimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe increasing number of older adults and the burden of diseases in this age group have increased the need for health services, especially preventive services. The level of utilization of primary health services is a health challenge for older adults. This study aimed to investigate the experiences of older adults and primary health care providers regarding the factors affecting the utilization of these services.MethodsThe present descriptive-explanatory qualitative study examined physicians and urban and rural healthcare providers in Isfahan province from October 2024 to March 2025. A total of 56 participants were selected through purposive sampling for the study. In-depth and unstructured interviews were conducted to collect data. Sampling continued until no new ideas emerged during interviews, indicating that data saturation had been reached. The content analysis presented by Graneheim and Lundman was used to analyze the data. The present study employed four strength criteria: credibility, confirmability, transferability, and dependability.ResultsFive themes emerged from 210 open codes. These codes were grouped into five interrelated themes: Personal determinants of healthcare utilization; structural and environmental determinants of access; policy and system-level determinants of service utilization; interpersonal communication and clinical conduct; and operational capacity and system performance. These themes together describe how older adults’ healthcare services are experienced, delivered, and utilized in comprehensive health centers.ConclusionAccording to the results, the provision of primary healthcare services for older adults has personal, interpersonal communication, structural, environmental, policy, and health system problems. In this regard, policymakers must review and improve primary health service programs for older adults.Keywords: Aged, Primary care, Healthcare provider, Policymaking

Introduction

Population aging is one of the most significant demographic shifts of the twenty-first century. As life expectancy increases and fertility rates decline, the proportion of older adults continues to grow worldwide.

The World Health Organization projects that the global population aged 60 years and older will nearly double between 2015 and 2050 [1]. In Iran, this transition is occurring at an even faster pace. Older adults accounted for approximately 10% of the population in 2015, and projections suggest that this proportion may approach one-third by 2050 [2]. Such a rapid demographic transformation places substantial pressure on healthcare systems, particularly on primary healthcare services that serve as the first point of contact for most individuals.

In Iran, primary healthcare services are delivered mainly through comprehensive health centers affiliated with universities of medical sciences. These centers are designed to provide preventive, promotive, and basic curative services across the life course, including for older adults. Despite this infrastructure, the utilization of healthcare services remains suboptimal. Evidence suggests that older adults, although experiencing a higher burden of chronic disease and multimorbidity, do not consistently use available primary care services [3, 4]. Limited engagement with primary healthcare may delay timely management of chronic conditions and increase reliance on hospital-based services, thereby escalating healthcare costs [5, 6].

International research has identified multiple barriers that hinder older adults’ access to healthcare, including transportation difficulties, financial constraints, long waiting times, inadequate facility design, and age-related discrimination [7, 8]. Studies conducted in Iran have similarly reported limited health literacy, insufficient awareness of available services, physical mobility limitations, and dissatisfaction with overcrowded facilities as factors associated with reduced service utilization [9–11]. However, most Iranian studies have employed quantitative, cross-sectional designs and have primarily focused on individual-level determinants. As a result, there is still a limited in-depth understanding of how older adults and healthcare providers themselves experience and interpret barriers within the organizational context of comprehensive health centers. In particular, qualitative evidence from central regions of the country remains scarce.

Isfahan, one of Iran’s major metropolitan areas located in the central part of the country, functions as an important administrative and healthcare hub. Its large and socioeconomically diverse population makes it a meaningful context for examining healthcare delivery challenges that may reflect broader national patterns.

Despite the strategic importance of comprehensive health centers in this region, the underlying reasons for low older adults’ service coverage have not been sufficiently explored from the perspectives of both service users and providers.

Because healthcare utilization is shaped not only by measurable factors but also by lived experiences, perceptions, interpersonal dynamics, and organizational processes, a qualitative approach is particularly appropriate for this inquiry. Qualitative research enables exploration of complex social phenomena and provides deeper insight into contextual and experiential dimensions that structured surveys may overlook [12].

Accordingly, this study was conducted from October 2024 to March 2025 to explore the experiences of older adults and healthcare providers regarding the challenges of delivering and utilizing the older adults’ healthcare services in comprehensive health centers affiliated with Isfahan University of Medical Sciences. By addressing the limited qualitative evidence in Iran and focusing on a key regional healthcare system, this study aims to generate context-sensitive findings to inform policies and strategies that enhance access to and utilization of primary healthcare services for the growing older population.

Method

Study design and setting

This exploratory descriptive qualitative study was performed with the use of a conventional content analysis. The study was carried out in health centers affiliated with Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (IUMS) across Isfahan Province.

Participants and sampling

A total of 56 participants took part in the study: 21 older adults aged 60 and above, and 35 healthcare providers, including 7 physicians and 28 urban and rural health workers. We used purposive sampling to include participants with varied backgrounds—different ages, genders, work histories, and places of residence—to capture a wide range of viewpoints. Sampling continued until no new ideas emerged during interviews, indicating that data saturation had been reached.

Data collection

Data were collected through unstructured, in-depth interviews conducted from October 2024 to March 2025. Eligible participants included healthcare providers with at least one year of experience in IUMS-affiliated centers and older adults who had used these services and were willing to share their experiences. Anyone who chose not to continue at any point was excluded.

All interviews were conducted by a gerontology specialist with experience in qualitative research. After explaining the study and obtaining written informed consent, interviews were held face-to-face with healthcare providers and either face-to-face or by telephone with older adults, depending on their preference.

The central interview question was: “What are your experiences in receiving healthcare in health care centers?” for the older adults, and “What are your experiences in providing healthcare services for older adults?” for health care providers.

To encourage deeper reflection, the interviewer used follow-up prompts such as: “Can you tell me more about that?” “What influenced this experience?” “How did this affect your view of care?”

Interviews lasted between 15 and 60 min and were conducted in quiet, private settings. With participants’ permission, all interviews were audio-recorded and later transcribed verbatim. Data collection and analysis took place simultaneously, and two additional interviews were conducted in each group after saturation to ensure completeness.

Data analysis

We analyzed the data using the qualitative content analysis approach described by Graneheim and Lundman [13]. The analysis was inductive, meaning that codes, subcategories, categories, and themes were developed directly from the data rather than being based on pre-existing frameworks.

The analysis unfolded in several steps: Transcripts were read multiple times to gain a sense of the whole. Meaning units—phrases or sentences that captured important ideas—were identified. These units were condensed while preserving their core meaning. Condensed units were labeled with initial codes. Codes with similar meanings were grouped into subcategories. Subcategories were then organized into broader categories. Finally, overarching themes were developed to capture the deeper, latent meanings in the data.

The principal investigator conducted the initial coding, and two experienced qualitative researchers reviewed the coding and category development to strengthen the analysis.

Trustworthiness

We applied Guba and Lincoln’s criteria to ensure the rigor of the study [14, 15]. Credibility was enhanced through prolonged engagement with the data, member checking, and discussions with qualitative research experts. Dependability was supported by involving multiple researchers in the analysis and maintaining detailed documentation of all methodological decisions. Confirmability was strengthened through bracketing, reflexive memo-writing, and transparent reporting of the research process. Transferability was facilitated by sampling participants from diverse backgrounds and providing contextual details about the study settings.

Reflexivity and researcher bias

Throughout the study, the research team engaged in ongoing reflexive practice. The interviewer kept a reflexive journal to record personal assumptions, emotional reactions, and potential biases during data collection and analysis. Regular team discussions helped ensure that interpretations were grounded in participants’ accounts rather than researchers’ expectations. These steps contributed to minimizing bias and enhancing the trustworthiness of the findings.

Results

A total of 210 initial codes were extracted from interviews with older adults, physicians, healthcare providers, rural health workers (Behvarz), and managers. These codes were grouped into five interrelated themes that together describe how the older adults’ healthcare services are experienced, delivered, and utilized in comprehensive health centers (Table 1). Rather than operating independently, the themes reflect interacting individual, structural, organizational, and relational factors that collectively shape service utilization.

Multilevel determinants shaping older adults’ utilization of primary healthcare services

Personal determinants of healthcare utilization

This theme captures how older adults’ personal circumstances, perceived needs and service expectations, and cultural beliefs shape their engagement with health services. Financial hardship, living alone, physical disability, and limited family support were frequently identified as barriers to healthcare utilization. As one community health worker explained: “The long distances in the satellite villages mean that older adults cannot come for care… They no longer have money to buy medications, and that is why they do not come.” An older adult similarly stated, “Medications are expensive. That’s why I do not go anymore.” At the same time, some older adults perceived less need to attend because they had access to home monitoring devices or medical advice within the family: “My son is a physician… and I have blood pressure and blood sugar devices at home.”

Expectations regarding service delivery played a significant role. Many older adults expected proactive contact for appointments, valued free services, and emphasized the importance of appropriate conduct and education tailored to their needs. These expectations influenced whether they viewed services as responsive and worth attending. Many participants emphasized that older adults expected medications, supplements, laboratory tests, or specialized services. When these were unavailable, trust decreased. As one community health worker explained: “Providing medication increases their trust, and then they come more often.” Older adults confirmed this perspective: “If I have to pay for the medication, then why should I go?” Following the COVID-19 pandemic, providers observed a shift toward passive utilization patterns: “COVID made them wait for an invitation. Now they do not come just because we tell them the schedule.”

Respectful communication was repeatedly described as decisive for return visits. One older adult reported: “They did not pay attention to me, so I stopped going.” Cultural beliefs further influenced attendance, particularly among men who perceived preventive services as unnecessary: “I do not need to come because I am healthy. If I get sick, I go to the hospital.”

Cultural beliefs and cognitive perceptions also shaped utilization patterns. Some older adults did not believe they needed preventive care, expressing a preference for “‘treatment’ over ‘care’” and perceiving that “services are only for women”. These beliefs reduced engagement with routine or preventive health services.

Together, these findings indicate that service utilization is strongly shaped by perceived need, perceived benefit, cultural framing of prevention, and experiences of respect.

Structural and environmental determinants of access

This theme reflects the infrastructural and logistical conditions that either facilitate or hinder access to primary healthcare. Geographical and transportation-related challenges were prominent. Participants highlighted long distances, shortages of public transportation, and the consolidation of health posts, which increased travel burdens. Additionally, waiting times at reception, physician visits, and provider stations were described as deterrents. A healthcare provider noted: “If they come once and there is no medication or physician, they do not come again.” Older adults described similar experiences: “It is crowded and there is a long wait. Everyone comes, and there is only one physician.” In rural areas, consolidation of health posts increased travel distance, further reducing access. Even when older adults made the effort to attend, negative experiences often led to discontinuation.

Conversely, certain organizational features facilitated access. The presence of specialists, designated days for older adults, proximity of centers, and quick service turnaround were identified as strong motivators for service use. A health worker stated, “I dedicate one day only to older adults, and all assessments are done on that day.” Older adults appreciated structured and efficient services: “The center is close to us… the tasks are done quickly. They guide you.”

Environmental design significantly influenced older adults’ comfort and ability to navigate facilities. The data referenced unattractive appearance; presence/absence of stairs; lack of elevator; lack of wheelchairs; insufficient infrastructure; lack of educational space, underscoring the importance of age-friendly physical environments: “There are stairs here, but there is no elevator for us.”

These findings suggest that when structural barriers combine with unmet expectations, even minor inconveniences can deter continued utilization.

Policy and system-level determinants of service utilization

This theme captures how policy prioritization and governance, parallel systems and fragmented service provision, and policy-driven incentives and engagement strategies initiatives shape older adults’ engagement with health services.

Participants described the older adults’ healthcare as insufficiently prioritized within the broader health system. Managers and providers emphasized limited intersectoral collaboration and fragmented service delivery: “The older adults’ programs are not important to authorities. Older adults have been excluded.” Older adults often navigated multiple, fragmented service systems, including Social Security services, Relief Committee programs, and company-based retiree services. While these systems provided valuable support, fragmentation created confusion and inconsistency in care. Many older adults reported preferring these clinics due to free medications and tests: “I go to Social Security every month. Blood sugar control and medications are free.” (Older adult). Healthcare providers noted that this parallel system reduced continuity of care and created comparisons that disadvantaged comprehensive health centers.

Several policy initiatives were viewed positively, including the Health Ambassador program, cultural center activities, specialized days for older adults, and the “Respect for Older Adults” initiative. Follow-up by care providers also enhanced engagement, suggesting that targeted policy-driven strategies can improve service utilization. However, providers expressed concern about managerial emphasis on quantitative indicators: “For managers, quantity is more important than quality.”

These findings indicate that systemic fragmentation and performance-driven management structures influence both perceived service value and continuity.

Interpersonal communication and clinical conduct

This theme highlights the relational and communicative aspects of care that shape older adults’ experiences.

Older adults emphasized the importance of respectful and patient communication. Negative experiences included inappropriate conduct, impatience, and feeling turned away, as reflected in the code “Inappropriate Conduct; Impatience; Communicating at Older Adults’ Level; Feeling of Being Turned Away”.

Communication emerged as a central determinant of utilization. Providers highlighted that visits often fulfilled psychosocial needs: “Measuring blood pressure is just an excuse. Most of the time, they come to talk and share their problems.” (Community health worker). Older adults confirmed the importance of emotional support: “You can tell everything, even personal issues.” Conversely, perceived impatience or lack of attention discouraged return visits: “They do not have enough patience or attention. That is why I do not go very often.” (Older adult). Physicians acknowledged that heavy workloads limited meaningful communication: “If I have time, I listen to their problems, but most of the time it is not possible.”

Local familiarity and culturally sensitive communication facilitated trust and continuity. Overall, communication functioned not only as part of clinical care but as a mechanism for building trust, dignity, and sustained engagement.

Operational capacity and system performance

This theme reflects the broader operational and infrastructural challenges within the health system.

Participants described several limitations in service scope and quality. Staff shortages and limited availability of specialists were frequently mentioned: “Six thousand people with only three physicians? We cannot respond to everyone.” (Physician). Providers expressed dissatisfaction with superficial assessments and administrative burden: “Care is low quality. It is just a few questions and data entry in the system.” The SIB electronic system was widely criticized for being restrictive and slow: “The system is very limiting… older adults lose patience and leave.” (Physician). Older adults described repetitive encounters that did not address evolving needs: “It is always the same repetitive work. The questions never change.”

Discussion

This study explored the experiences of older adults and healthcare providers regarding the delivery of the older adults’ health services utilization in comprehensive health centers affiliated with the IUMS. Older adults’ use of primary healthcare services was shaped by five interconnected determinants that operated not in isolation but through continuous interaction. Personal determinants of healthcare utilization, including personal and household circumstances, perceived needs, and cultural beliefs, influenced how older adults interpreted their health and the value of seeking care, often shaping their expectations before they ever encountered the system. Structural and environmental determinants such as logistical barriers, service availability, and the physical design of centers affected their ability to act on these personal motivations; even strong perceived need could be undermined by inaccessible or poorly organized services.

Policy and system‑level determinants, including governance priorities, fragmented service provision, and policy‑driven incentives, created the broader conditions that either amplified or mitigated these structural barriers, influencing both provider behavior and resource allocation at the local level. Interpersonal communication and clinical conduct reflected in provider–client interactions and physician behavior shaped trust and willingness to return, often mediating the effects of structural and policy constraints by either compensating for or exacerbating them. Finally, operational capacity and system performance, including human resources and service delivery processes, determined the consistency and quality of care, directly influencing whether older adults’ initial attempts to seek care translated into meaningful service utilization.

Together, these determinants demonstrate that older adults’ healthcare utilization emerges from the dynamic interplay of personal motivations, structural opportunities, relational experiences, and system‑level conditions. Utilization is therefore best understood not as a single decision but as the cumulative outcome of how these factors reinforce or counteract one another across the care‑seeking process. Similar multi-level determinants of the older adults’ healthcare utilization have been documented in middle-income settings [16, 17].

Within the theme of personal determinants of healthcare utilization, personal and household circumstances captured how disability, loneliness, poverty, and the presence or absence of supportive resources directly influenced the ability to recognize health needs and seek timely care. These findings mirror evidence from India, where high out-of-pocket payments increase poverty risk and reduce access [16], and from Nigeria, where social isolation and limited formal care contribute to unmet needs [18].

However, financial hardship alone did not fully explain reduced utilization. The subcategory perceived needs and service expectations showed that expectations for proactive appointment reminders, free or low-cost services, respectful conduct, and education tailored to individual needs shaped how older adults judged the value of attending primary care. Perceived needs emerged as a key mediator: when services offered tangible benefits (e.g., medications, laboratory tests), older adults tolerated costs; when perceived benefit was low, even minimal fees deterred attendance. This aligns with Andresen’s behavioral model, which emphasizes the role of perceived need and enabling resources in shaping service use [19].

Under the subcategory cultural and cognitive determinants, many older adults did not believe they required routine care, preferred treatment over ongoing care, or viewed primary healthcare centers as spaces primarily for women. These beliefs reduced uptake of preventive services and shaped expectations of what primary care should provide, echoing Morgan et al.’s findings on the influence of cultural beliefs on program enrollment and perceived care quality [17]. Importantly, these cultural interpretations interacted with economic and structural barriers: when preventive care was not perceived as necessary or appropriate, financial constraints became even more decisive in discouraging attendance.

Within the theme of Structural and Environmental Determinants of Access, older adults described barriers that shaped their engagement with primary healthcare. Access-related barriers—distance, transportation challenges, overcrowding, and waiting times—were consistently reported. Dableh et al. emphasize that physical accessibility, including transportation and facility design, significantly affects primary healthcare utilization among older adults [20]. Similarly, proximity to facilities and ease of transport were identified as primary determinants of care-seeking [18].

However, the findings suggest that access barriers function less as primary causes and more as amplifiers of dissatisfaction. When older adults perceive services as incomplete or unresponsive, logistical inconvenience becomes decisive. This reflects the WHO framework for age-friendly primary healthcare, which underscores both physical accessibility and responsiveness of care [21].

Within the theme of Policy and System‑Level Determinants of Service Utilization, participants emphasized that older adults’ health remains insufficiently prioritized in national and local governance. Fragmentation between comprehensive health centers and Social Security clinics created parallel systems that disrupted continuity of care. International literature highlighted that fragmented financing and service delivery models reduce efficiency and continuity in chronic disease management among older adults [16]. Morgan et al. demonstrated that financial protection mechanisms, proximity to care, and administrative efficiency significantly influence older adults’ engagement with insurance schemes. Our findings similarly suggest that policy misalignment and lack of coordination weaken trust and continuity [17]. Furthermore, managerial emphasis on quantitative indicators may unintentionally compromise care quality. Fee-for-service and performance-based pressures have been shown to reduce consultation depth and patient-centered communication [20]. This administrative-clinical tension may diminish both provider morale and patient satisfaction.

Under the category caregivers’ interaction with older adults, the subcategory provider-client interaction quality captured experiences of inappropriate conduct, impatience, communication that did not match older adults’ cognitive or emotional needs, and feelings of being dismissed or turned away. The subcategory physician communication and professional behavior highlighted contrasts between non-local and local physicians, noting that physician impatience undermined trust, whereas clear communication and timely referrals strengthened confidence in the system. These findings align with Nnadi et al., who report that empathetic, respectful provider relationships are critical to older adults’ satisfaction and continued service use [18].

Participants also linked communication challenges to systemic pressures. High workload and workforce shortages limited opportunities for meaningful interaction, reducing consultation depth and weakening trust. This pattern reflects WHO guidance that age-friendly systems require workforce training in geriatric communication and psychosocial sensitivity [21]. The causal chain observed—staff shortages leading to rushed encounters, diminished rapport, and lower utilization—underscores the need for sustained investment in the primary care workforce.

Under the theme of Operational Capacity and System Performance, participants reported major constraints in the services available to older adults. Participants reported limitations in service comprehensiveness, staff availability, and electronic system functionality. Insufficient public investment in health infrastructure in rapidly aging societies compromises service quality and affordability [16]. Similarly, Kundu et al. note that healthcare utilization among older adults is influenced not only by availability and cost but also by service comprehensiveness and system organization [22]. The SIB electronic system’s focus on quantitative reporting appeared to prioritize administrative documentation over holistic geriatric assessment. Such structural misalignment may undermine person-centered care, particularly for older adults with multimorbidity and polypharmacy concerns [23].

Inconsistencies in human resources, equipment, systems, and guidelines negatively impacted both service delivery to older adults and their willingness to seek care. Addressing these deficiencies requires substantial investment. Regarding weak health infrastructure in India, Sahoo et al. argue that, given the considerable diversity among older adults in socioeconomic, educational, and health status, coupled with rapid demographic shifts leading to increased disease burden and healthcare demand, current public investment in public health infrastructure is inadequate for an aging population [16]. These findings underscore the need for greater investment in public healthcare infrastructure, human resources, adequate drug supplies, and skill development within the public health system. Barriers such as shortages of health specialists, lack of integrated and person-centered care are also influential. Physician availability in the older adults’ primary care centers is often limited to a few hours per week. Drug shortages, understaffing in some centers, and the absence of general practitioners during all working hours—especially in the afternoons—are perceived as obstacles to timely care [17, 24]. Implementing the services and supports outlined in policies is only feasible with adequate financial resources. Furthermore, infrastructural challenges, including the need for additional facilities, personnel, or technical resources, can significantly hinder service delivery. Resistance from various sectors presents another major barrier, potentially stemming from a lack of understanding of older adults’ needs, conflicting priorities, or reluctance to deviate from established procedures [25].

This qualitative study was conducted in health centers affiliated with IUMS; therefore, findings may not be generalizable to other provinces or health systems with different organizational structures. As with all qualitative research, findings rely on subjective experiences and may be influenced by recall bias. Additionally, older adults who do not engage with formal healthcare services were not included, potentially limiting representation of the most marginalized groups. Nevertheless, triangulation of perspectives from both providers and older adults enhances credibility and transferability to similar middle-income contexts undergoing demographic aging.

Conclusion

The provision of primary health services to older adults in comprehensive health centers faces multiple interrelated challenges and determinants. Individual-level barriers, such as loneliness and difficulties accessing services, were frequently reported by participants. Infrastructure limitations, including inadequate physical facilities, further hinder service delivery. Human resource issues, such as staff shortages, lack of expertise, and reluctance to work with the older adult populations, were also highlighted, with participants describing long waiting times and insufficient attention from providers. Additionally, the availability of parallel organizations offering more comprehensive services reduces utilization of primary health centers.

To address these challenges, the study suggests targeted solutions directly informed by participant experiences. Enhancing access responds to reported mobility barriers. Upgrading facilities and ensuring specialized training for staff addresses infrastructure and human resource constraints observed in the field.

Expanding service coverage and updating programs based on the evolving needs of the older adults can mitigate gaps noted by participants. Finally, integrating the older adults more explicitly into health policy planning can strengthen their engagement and trust in primary care services. Collectively, these measures can improve utilization and the quality of care for the older adult population.
