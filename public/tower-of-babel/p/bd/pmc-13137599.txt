The association between obstetric comorbidity index and delivery hospitalization associated cost: a nationwide population study in Taiwan
Authors: Wen-Chu Huang, Shou-Hsia Cheng, Ching-Ching Claire Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe obstetric comorbidity index (OCI) is well recognized as a validated tool for maternal risk adjustment in healthcare service and population-based researches. The aim of the study was to further investigate the association between OCI scores and medical cost during delivery hospitalization.MethodsA nationwide retrospective study was conducted by using Taiwan National Health Insurance Research Database released from the Ministry of Health and Welfare of Taiwan. Delivery hospitalization records during the period from 2017 to 2021 were retrieved. The OCI scores were calculated by summing the weights with all maternal conditions and their age at delivery. All the delivery hospitalizations were further categorized into five groups by the OCI scores: OCI = 0 or 1, OCI = 2, 3, 4 and OCI ≥ 5. The medical cost and length of stay (LOS) derived from each delivery hospitalization were also obtained from the claim data. Generalize estimating equation model was estimated to investigate the association between OCI and medical cost as well as LOS. All data analysis was conducted using SAS 9.4.ResultsThere were 824,329 delivery hospitalizations identified for analysis. Among all, 292,612 childbirths were via cesarean delivery, yielding an average cesarean rate of 35.5% during the study period. The mean medical cost among vaginal and cesarean deliveries was 37,265 points and 51,015 points, respectively. The average LOS was 2.9 days for vaginal deliveries and 4.9 days for cesarean deliveries. After adjusting for all covariates, higher OCI score is significantly associated with higher predicted medical cost and LOS.ConclusionsStudy results demonstrated positive relationship between OCI scores, LOS, and medical cost. The application of OCI can be expanded into estimation of the medical cost during delivery hospitalization. OCI could be considered one of the important factors in risk adjustment for payment for delivery hospitalization.Keywords: Childbirth, Delivery hospitalization, Maternal morbidity, Medical cost, Obstetric comorbidity index

Introduction

For the past decades, the characteristics of women of reproductive age changed worldwide. Along with the trend of delayed childbearing age, more and more women carry comorbidities into their pregnancy, such as chronic diseases or obesity [1–4]. Prior research showed that approximately one out of ten deliveries in the US was complicated by chronic maternal comorbidities [5–7]. The associated maternal characteristics may also result in the increases in cesarean delivery [8].

The global decline in maternal health—driven by advancing maternal age and rising cesarean delivery rates—is a critical concern, as these factors significantly elevate the risk of severe maternal morbidity (SMM) and mortality [9–11]. Recent evidence from Taiwan also mirrored these global trends, identifying advanced maternal age and cesarean sections as primary drivers of the region’s steadily increasing SMM rates [12].

In health services research, accounting for patient comorbidities is essential to minimize confounding effects and ensure the robustness of statistical analyses. The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) and the Elixhauser Comorbidity Measure remain the most widely utilized measures globally for this purpose [13, 14]. However, these tools were not originally developed for obstetric populations. Both indices lack the sensitivity to capture pregnancy-specific comorbidities, which are critical determinants of maternal morbidity and mortality.

For better prediction of maternal end-organ injury and even death, Bateman et al. developed a simple measurement tool, the obstetric comorbidity index (OCI), by using Medicaid claim data enrolling more than 854,000 delivery hospitalizations [15]. This comorbidity index incorporates twenty distinct maternal conditions alongside maternal age. Each condition is weighted according to its statistical association with adverse outcomes; these weights are then summed to produce a single, composite OCI score. It provides a single numeric score that effectively measures the combined influence of chronic illnesses, behavioral factors, and pregnancy-induced conditions.

After the development of OCI by Bateman et al. in 2013, several studies had been conducted to validate this comorbidity index in Canada, Denmark and USA [16–18]. Notably, the OCI score was originally developed using International Classification of Disease 9th Revision by Bateman in 2013. In 2015, Metcalfe et al. conducted a validation study of ICD-10 transition with kappa static ranging from 0.92 to 1 depending on different comorbidities [16]. They all concluded this comorbidity index demonstrated moderate ability for discriminating and prediction of end-organ injury or death in obstetric patients. Therefore, OCI was well recognized as a useful tool for maternal risk adjustment in healthcare service and population-based researches [15, 19]. In recent studies, OCI was commonly applied to predict the adverse maternal outcomes, such as SMM and mortality [19–24]. These results demonstrated a significant positive correlation between OCI scores and adverse maternal outcomes; specifically, higher scores were associated with increased odds of both SMM and maternal mortality. Furthermore, Chaalan et al. extended the application of the OCI to predict fetal morbidity, confirming its utility as a helpful clinical tool [24].

Maternal morbidities and pregnancy-induced complications significantly escalate healthcare utilizations, primarily through the increased costs associated with clinical management during childbirth and extended postpartum care [25]. While the mode of delivery is a primary determinant of length of stay (typically three days for vaginal delivery and five days for cesarean section in Taiwan) and cost, maternal comorbidities likely play a critical, independent role in driving healthcare resource utilization within each group. Generic measures like the CCI, however, often fail to capture the specific risks women encounter during childbirth. Therefore, this study aims to fill this gap by investigating the association between the OCI and healthcare resource utilization, measured as medical cost and length of stay, hopefully evaluating the OCI’s potential for improving risk adjustment in payment design and reimbursement.

Materials and methods

Data

This study utilized Taiwan National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD) linked to birth certificate registry data to compare cost associated with different OCI levels. Operating on a fee-for-service basis, Taiwan National Health Insurance is compulsory social insurance that covers approximately 99% of 23 million Taiwanese residents [26]. The database included all claims from outpatient visits, hospitalizations, prescription orders, claimed payments, and copayment. In Taiwan, medical cost during delivery hospitalization was fully covered by the national health insurance (NHI). Nearly all the childbirths and postpartum care were carried out in either a hospital or an obstetrics and gynecology clinic [12]. For insurance reimbursement, the associated diagnoses and managements during delivery hospitalization were required to be submitted by using the coding system of International Classification of Disease 10th Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) after July 2016. These made the maternal comorbidities as well as their medical cost during childbirths available from hospitalization discharge records at national level.

All reimbursement claims for delivery hospitalizations during the period between Jan 1, 2017 and Oct 31, 2021 were retrieved. In Taiwan, the ICD-10 coding system was implemented in July 2016. To avoid potential bias associated with the transition between coding systems, this study included only delivery hospitalizations occurring between 2017 and 2021, utilizing ICD-10 codes exclusively. Because childbirths are rare among Taiwanese adolescents (defined as those under 18) and to ensure data accuracy by avoiding the inclusion of pregnancy terminations, we restricted our analysis to individuals aged 18 years and older. Furthermore, to minimize potential bias arising from administrative delays or incomplete reimbursement processing, we concluded the study period on October 31, 2021. This allowed for a two-month buffer to account for late reporting and data corrections. Delivery mode (vaginal or cesarean delivery) associated with a childbirth was identified with the current procedure terminology (CPT) codes (vaginal delivery: 81010 C, 81017 C, 81018 C, 81019 C, 81024 C, 81025 C, 81026 C, 81034 C; cesarean delivery: 81004 C, 81005 C, 81011 C, 81028 C, 81029 C).

For the purpose of reimbursement for inpatient care, each hospitalization claim contained one primary diagnosis and up to four additional diagnoses as well as five procedure codes at maximum. The delivery hospitalizations were reimbursed by Diagnosis Related Groups Payment System (DRG) in Taiwan since July, 2014. To minimize the risk of missing cases through diagnosis-based searching alone, we supplemented our data extraction with DRG codes to ensure that all delivery hospitalizations involving obstetric complications were identified and included.

Key variables: OCI, medical cost, and length of stay

The OCI score, developed by Bateman et al., was a weighted sum of 20 maternal comorbidities, whether the delivery was for multiple gestation, and maternal age [15]. Multiple gestation was identified through birth certificates. All the subjects were further categorized into five groups by the level of their OCI score: OCI = 0 or 1, OCI = 2, 3, 4, and OCI ≥ 5.

We defined medical costs as the sum of all maternal expenses incurred during the delivery hospitalization, including labor, delivery, and postpartum care. Neonate-associated costs (e.g., newborn center or NICU care) were not included. All costs were aggregated based on NHI points. Under global budget in Taiwan, the transferring ratio of NHI points to NTD was floating every year. To more precisely represent the medical resource use during delivery hospitalization and investigate the association with OCI scores, the medical cost was measured with NHI points in this study.

Besides medical cost, the length of stay is also an important indicator of healthcare utilization. The length of stay during delivery hospitalization was derived from each inpatient claim by calculating the difference between admission and discharge dates. We investigated the association between (1) OCI and LOS (2) OCI and medical cost for both delivery models.

Confounding variables

The NHIRD defined insurance eligibility into six categories. The fourth category which contains police and military personnel is sheltered and was not included in this study. The hospital locations were categorized as six regions including Taipei, north, central, south, Kao-Ping and east area. For institutional characteristics, hospital levels and annual delivery volume were controlled. In Taiwan, the institutions were categorized as medical center, regional hospital, district hospital and maternity clinics by accreditation levels which can be identified from the NHIRD. The hospitals were divided into quartile according to their annual delivery volume using the cut-off value of 600, 1200, 2400 by our previous study [12].

Statistical analysis

Generalize estimating equation (GEE) model with log link function and gamma distribution was used to exam the association of OCI scores with medical cost and LOS. The medical cost and LOS were adjusted for several potential confounding variables which included insurance eligibility, hospital level, annual delivery volume and residential area according to our previous study [12].

The results were shown as mean±standard error (SE) for GEE model and standard deviation (SD) for descriptive results. The results were presented as a regression β coefficient estimate and 95% confidence interval, with a Chi-square test for statistical significance. All the data were analyzed using SAS statistical software version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). The study was approved by the research ethics committee of National Taiwan University (202212HM033).

Results

From Jan 2017 to Oct 2021, there were 824,329 delivery hospitalizations identified and met the inclusive criteria for analysis. Among them, 292,612 childbirths were via cesarean delivery, yielding an average cesarean rate of 35.5% during this period. Among vaginal deliveries, the majority (93.76%) had the OCI score ≤ 1, followed by 5.15%, 0.9%, 0.15% and 0.03% with OCI score of 2, 3, 4 and ≥ 5, respectively. For cesarean deliveries, 66.74% of the subjects had the OCI score ≤ 1, followed by 21.71%, 8.2%, 2.45% and 0.9% with OCI score of 2, 3, 4 and ≥ 5, respectively (Table 1).

The average LOS in all vaginal deliveries was 2.93 days (95% CI: 2.926–2.932 days). The LOS increased from 2.9 days in group with OCI ≤ 1 to 3.9 days in group with OCI ≧ 5 (Table 1). In both types of delivery modes, the higher the OCI score, the longer LOS (Table 1).

Discussion

In this study, our results demonstrated significant differences in LOS and medical costs associated with the severity of maternal comorbidity, as measured by Bateman’s OCI [15]. For both delivery modes, higher index scores were associated with a significant increase in LOS/medical costs during childbirth hospitalization. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the association between OCI scores and healthcare utilization for delivery hospitalization.

Bateman’s obstetric comorbidity index was originally designed to predict maternal end-organ injury or death [15]. After the development and validation of this specific index, OCI was well recognized as a risk adjustment tool for obstetric patients. Thereafter, many studies utilized this index to identify the risk of severe maternal morbidity and mortality in pregnant women. According to Bateman’s previous study, each incremental point increase in the OCI score was associated with 37% increased risk of SMM [19]. They also declared that OCI had moderate ability in discrimination of SMM (c-statistic:0.66) and offering better predicative accuracy than Charlson comorbidity index [19]. Similarly, Chaalan et al. conducted a cohort study in Qatar demonstrating for every one unit increased in OCI score, the odds of SMM increased by 44% [24]. Somerville analyzed the OCI changes in deliveries between 1998 and 2013 and found the steadily rising trend of mean OCI score from 0.6 in 1998 to 0.82 in 2013 [20]. Furthermore, pregnant women with OCI score ≥ 10 had the highest SMM rates reaching 138.36 per thousand while those with OCI of 0 had lowest SMM rates with only 2.28 per thousand delivery hospitalizations [20]. Using OCI as a risk assessment tool, Easter et al. also demonstrated significantly increased prevalence of SMM reaching 18.75% in those with OCI score ≥ 9 compared with 0.41% in those with OCI score equal to 0 [27]. Their results showed that for every single point increase in OCI score, a women experienced 1.55 times odds of SMM. Additionally, OCI was indicated strong discrimination with c-statistic of 0.83 [27].

With regard of hospital stay during childbirth, the existing research aimed to compare the difference in length of stay between SMM and non-SMM. The results from Howland revealed over 40% of delivery hospitalizations with SMM last five days or longer, while only 6.4% of cases without SMM last five days or more [28]. The study in Canada by Dzakpasu et al. further demonstrated that the rate ratio of prolonged hospital stay in cases with any SMM was 14.5 in comparison with no SMM [29]. Our results showed that the average LOS among vaginal deliveries increased from 2.9 days in those with OCI score ≤ 1 to 3.9 days in those with OCI score ≥ 5. With each point increase in OCI, the LOS was significantly prolonged. Additionally, among cesarean deliveries, the average LOS increased from 4.9 days in those with OCI score ≤ 1 to 5.2 days in OCI score ≥ 5. Notably, prolonged hospitalization was significantly associated only with OCI scores of 4 or higher. This finding suggests that an OCI threshold of ≥ 4 could serve as a valuable clinical indicator for identifying high-risk patients with severe comorbidities who require extended medical management, a distinction not previously established in the literature.

Most studies examining healthcare utilization during delivery hospitalization have been limited to comparing SMM versus non-SMM cases. In a population-based study conducted in New York City, Howland evaluated the hospital delivery costs associated with SMM [28]. The findings revealed that SMM nearly doubled the cost of delivery hospitalizations; specifically, the average medical cost for SMM cases reached 14,442 USD, compared to 7,289 USD for non-SMM deliveries [28]. Chen et al. also investigated the association between SMM and its medical cost during delivery hospitalization. They found that while the unadjusted mean cost increased from 4,300 USD to 11,000 USD for deliveries with SMM, the adjusted analysis indicated that SMM costs were 2.1 times higher than those without SMM [30]. Chen further declared the ratio of cost among hospitalized deliveries increased from 1.7-fold in obstetric patients with only one indicator of SMM to 10.3-fold in whom with concomitant five or more indicators [30]. OCI scoring system was proven moderate to strong discrimination in evaluation the risk of SMM [16–19, 27]. The associated studies also showed significant differences in delivery hospitalization cost between SMM and non-SMM.

Built on the prior research, we further provided evidence on the association between medical cost and different OCI score group during delivery hospitalization. Our results showed that the average medical cost in vaginal and cesarean deliveries were 37,265 points and 51,015.4 points, respectively. The generalize estimating equation model demonstrated positive relationship between OCI scores and medical cost after controlling for potential confounding variables. Higher OCI score is significantly associated with increasing medical cost for both delivery modes. Our results implies that OCI can be expanded its application into estimate of medical cost during delivery hospitalization.

Childbirth costs are reimbursed via DRGs in many health systems, including Taiwan. However, classification systems vary in efficiency; Or et al. found that across ten European countries, DRGs and patient variables explained only 48% to 70% of cost variation, suggesting significant room for improvement [31]. In Taiwan, where seven obstetric DRGs exist (five for vaginal, two for cesarean). Huang et al. reported significantly higher 30-day readmission risks for pregnancy-related cases (MDC 14) following DRG implementation [32]. These findings imply that Taiwan’s current classification system requires refinement. Furthermore, our own analysis indicates that medical costs for cesarean deliveries with an OCI ≥ 4 exceed current reimbursement levels. Therefore, integrating the OCI into DRG schemes could improve risk adjustment and ensure more equitable resource allocation. OCI should be considered an important factor in resource allocation and DRG-based risk adjustment for consumption of delivery hospitalization.

Facility-level characteristics are significantly associated with the LOS for delivery hospitalizations in both unadjusted and adjusted models. While our unadjusted analyses showed that higher OCI scores were associated with longer LOS, this relationship reversed after adjusting for confounding factors such as hospital level and annual delivery volume. Specifically, adjusted estimates indicated that cesarean deliveries with OCI scores of 2 and 3 had shorter LOS than those with OCI ≤ 1 (Table 4). This may be driven by practice patterns in maternity clinics. Our previous work found that maternity clinics have a significantly higher cesarean delivery rate (42%) compared to hospitals (31–37%) [12], and account for over half of all cesarean deliveries in Taiwan. Because these lower-accredited facilities have fewer beds, they may prioritize earlier discharge to increase bed turnover and maximize DRG incentives, resulting in the observed negative regression coefficients for higher OCI scores.

There were several strengths of our study. First, this was a nationwide study enrolling more than 0.8 million of delivery hospitalizations for analysis. Results from this study represented all deliveries covered by Taiwan National Health Insurance. The comprehensive coverage for delivery hospitalization by NHI made our estimates of medical cost during childbirth reliable. Secondly, Taiwan’s NHI covers only medically necessary services during childbirth. Elective, out-of-pocket interventions, such as epidural analgesia for vaginal delivery, or patient-controlled analgesia and anti-adhesive agents for cesarean sections, are not reimbursed and therefore not recorded in the NHIRD. While this means total out-of-pocket expenditures are not captured, it ensures that our analysis is not confounded by elective costs. Consequently, our results provide a precise estimate of the resource utilization on the public insurance system, a distinction that is often difficult to make in other healthcare payment systems. Thirdly, we identified vaginal or cesarean delivery modes from CPT codes rather than ICD-10-CM. This ensured the retrieved hospitalization records were all admission for delivery rather than other medical or pregnancy-related treatments.

Several limitations should be noted. First, OCI scores were calculated based on weights derived from ICD-10-CM diagnostic codes within an administrative database. As with all secondary data analyses, the potential for coding inaccuracies in administrative claims cannot be entirely eliminated. However, since the twenty conditions comprising the OCI are considered major diagnoses of DRG, the DRG-based reimbursement system provides a strong incentive for accurate documentation. Coders are motivated to capture these primary diagnoses to ensure appropriate reimbursement from the NHI, thereby minimizing the risk of underreporting. Additionally, prevalence of health behavior factors of the OCI, such as drug or alcohol abuse, were extremely low in our study. Missing coding of the two factors potentially leads to underestimated OCI scores.

Conclusions

We found that OCI scores are positively correlated with both medical costs and length of hospital stay during delivery. Compared to a baseline score of OCI less or equal to 1, each incremental increase in the OCI score is significantly associated with extended lengths of stay and higher medical costs. Beyond its utility in assessing the risk of SMM or mortality, the OCI serves as a reliable tool for estimating healthcare utilization associated with different severities of maternal comorbidities. Consequently, the OCI could be integrated as a key factor in payment design for delivery hospitalization and maternal care.
