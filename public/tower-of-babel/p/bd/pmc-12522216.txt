Comparison of Worth Four Dots (W4D) and Bagolini Striated Lenses in adult suppression detection
Authors: Chieh-Yueh Lin, Guan-Cheng Lin, Kun-Shiang Chen, Li-Te Yin

Background

After visual stimuli from the external environment enter the two eyes, information is transmitted to the visual cortex for integration, resulting in binocular vision [1]. Binocular vision can be categorized into three levels: simultaneous perception, fusion, and stereopsis [2]. Visual abnormalities may disrupt the formation of normal binocular vision, as observed in conditions such as strabismus, amblyopia, and anisometropia [3]. For instance, strabismus causes the image of the fixated object to fall on non-corresponding retinal points in both eyes, leading to diplopia [4]. Anisometropia results in unequal image sizes (aniseikonia) between the two eyes, which hinders fusion and can also cause diplopia [5]. To mitigate such conflicts and disturbances, the visual system may develop suppression to maintain monocular vision. Suppression is an adaptive response in which the information received by one eye is inhibited or overridden by the other [6].

In fact, suppression may enhance visual quality. However, under certain circumstances, it can be advantageous to eliminate suppression. Sharma et al. (2024) investigated the recovery of binocular visual function in 18 children with small-angle esotropia following anti-suppression training. The results demonstrated significant improvements in stereopsis and esodeviation [7]. As noted in the textbook by Scheiman and Wick, visual training protocols also include procedures for monitoring suppression, with the goal of helping patients become aware of and overcome suppression, ultimately learning to use both eyes simultaneously [2].

The detection of suppression is crucial for both assessing visual status and conducting visual training. Common methods used in clinical practice for detecting suppression include the Worth Four Dots (W4D) and the Bagolini Striated Lenses (BAG) [8–13], both are not mechanically complex, and they are non-invasive visual assessments. Notably, the BAG is more difficult to understand than the W4D, and this limitation has also been reflected in previous studies. For example, one study evaluated 133 pediatric patients using the BAG and the Polarized Four Dots (P4D) tests. The results showed that the failure rate of the BAG (14%) was significantly higher than that of the P4D (5%, p = 0.0005) [12].

Although the W4D and BAG tests are widely utilized, empirical evidence comparing their clinical performance in large-scale screenings is still limited. To address this research gap, this study aims to compare the performance of the W4D and BAG using real-world data, providing a practical reference for the selection of clinical tests.

Methods

This study utilized a single-center, prospective, cross-sectional design. All participants were patients from the Yuan Dong Optometry Clinic in Chiayi, Taiwan. The inclusion criteria was age ≥ 18, and the period was from April 1 to November 15, 2024. Each participant underwent monocular subjective refraction using an autorefractor (TOPCON KR-800) and a phoropter (TOPCON VT-10). The subjective refraction was refined to achieve each participant’s best corrected visual acuity (BCVA), following the maximum plus for maximum visual acuity (MPMVA) principle. The refractive error results were subsequently transferred to a trial frame for suppression detection of the distance W4D and BAG. To minimize operational bias, all measurements were conducted by the same researcher, who was the first author and an experienced licensed optometrist, with the W4D consistently performed prior to the BAG.

For participants who demonstrated suppression on the W4D, Ishihara pseudoisochromatic plates were administered after completing both the W4D and BAG to check their color vision status and minimize the potential influence of color vision anomalies on the results. In this study, participants with red-green color blindness were intended to be excluded from the statistical analyses.

Worth Four Dots (W4D)

Participants were instructed to wear red-green filter glasses, with the red filter positioned over the right eye and the green filter over the left eye. Before the test commenced, each eye was alternately occluded to ensure that the corresponding target dots could be perceived by each eye individually. When the left eye was occluded, the right eye was expected to perceive two red dots; conversely, when the right eye was occluded, the left eye was expected to perceive three green dots. Participants were then asked to report the number and color of the dots they observed. Based on their responses, suppression could be assessed. If a participant reported seeing four dots with the bottom dot appearing white, this indicated no suppression. However, if the participant reported seeing four dots with the bottom dot appearing either green or red, or if they reported seeing only two red dots (indicating left eye suppression) or only three green dots (indicating right eye suppression), these responses were interpreted as evidence of suppression, as illustrated in Fig. 1 [14].

Bagolini Striated Lenses (BAG)

Participants were instructed to wear Bagolini Striated Lenses, with the striations oriented at 135 degrees for the right eye and 45 degrees for the left eye. They were asked to describe the appearance of the light source and the lines they observed. Suppression was assessed based on their responses. In the absence of suppression, participants would perceive two diagonal lines intersecting at the point of the light source. If suppression occurred in one eye, they would report a partial or complete absence of one of the lines. For instance, in individuals with central suppression, a participant might report that the central portion of one line was missing while the peripheral segments remained visible, as illustrated in Fig. 2 [15].

Statistical analysis

Post hoc analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0. To evaluate the agreement between the W4D and BAG in detecting suppression, cross-tabulation was employed, along with the calculation of Cohen’s kappa coefficient. To further assess whether there was a significant difference in detection rates between the two methods, McNemar’s test was applied for paired data analysis. In the presentation of results, a positive outcome was defined as the detection of suppression, while a negative outcome indicated that no suppression was detected. A significance level was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed).

Ethical approval

At Yuan Dong Optometry Clinic, large posters and written brochures were displayed to clearly inform all patients that a study on suppression detection was being conducted. Therefore, all participants who underwent the W4D and BAG were aware of their involvement in the study and understood that their anonymized data would be used for academic publication. In this study, a participant was classified as having suppression if it was detected in either the W4D or BAG test. Written informed consent was obtained from participants who exhibited suppression, while those without suppression were not required to sign an additional consent form. This protocol received approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB), and all procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was granted by the Human Research Ethics Committee of National Chung Cheng University in Chiayi, Taiwan (IRB Number: CCUREC113020101).

Results

A total of 2280 patients participated in this study, all of whom successfully completed suppression measurements using the W4D and BAG. As shown in Table 1, the W4D identified 35 positive participants (positive rate: 1.54%), while the BAG identified 26 positive participants (positive rate: 1.14%). Cross-tabulation analysis revealed that 22 participants tested positive on both tests; 13 were positive only on the W4D, and 4 were positive only on the BAG. The remaining 2241 participants tested negative on both tests. The calculated Cohen’s kappa coefficient was 0.72. According to statistical standards, values between 0.61 and 0.80 indicate substantial agreement, whereas values between 0.81 and 1.00 indicate almost perfect agreement. Therefore, in this study, the two tests demonstrated good overall agreement [16]. However, the results of McNemar’s test demonstrated a statistically significant difference in suppression detection between the two tests (p = 0.049), with the W4D identifying significantly more positive participants than the BAG (Table 2).

Discussion

According to Parks (1979), when the W4D is conducted at a distance, all four dots fall within the stimulation area of the fovea [17]. Consequently, if suppression is present, it is impossible to determine whether it is central suppression or complete suppression, the test only provides preliminary evidence regarding the presence or absence of suppression. In contrast, the BAG allows for the observation of the partial or complete disappearance of the lines when suppression occurs, thereby offering the advantage of distinguishing between different types of suppression. Therefore, the W4D and BAG are expected to demonstrate high consistency in suppression detection. However, in the present study, while the overall agreement between the two tests was good, this was largely attributed to the numerous participants who tested negative on both tests. When focusing specifically on the 39 participants who exhibited suppression in at least one test, a significant difference was observed.

Notably, the findings do not imply that the BAG is ineffective. Rather, they likely reflect fundamental differences in the design of the two measurements. As described in Clinical Procedures in Primary Eye Care, the W4D creates a relatively strong dissociative visual condition due to the use of red-green filters. This can induce suppression even in individuals with usable or normal binocular vision. While this characteristic may increase the risk of false positives, it may also enhance the ability to detect mild suppression. In contrast, the BAG introduces minimal dissociation and more closely resembles natural visual conditions, thereby reducing test interference and making it more suitable for further classification and confirmation of suppression type [18]. Nonetheless, this study suggests that such test conditions may fail to detect mild suppression, resulting in a lower detection rate.

Currently, there is no universally accepted gold standard for measuring suppression [19–21]. Given the importance of suppression detection in evaluating binocular vision, conducting visual training, and managing amblyopia, it is prudent to prioritize tools with higher detection capabilities during the initial screening phase. Based on the findings of this study, the authors recommend using the W4D test as a first-line screening tool for suppression. For individuals identified by the W4D test, further evaluation using methods such as the Von Graefe technique and the 4Δ base-out test is advisable to enable a more accurate assessment. For instance, in the Von Graefe technique, the presence of suppression can be inferred if the participant reports seeing only a single target after prism dissociation; if two targets are perceived, suppression is not indicated [22].

A notable limitation of this study is the restriction of optometric practice in Taiwan, where the scope of care is limited to refractive correction, contact lenses fitting, and binocular vision assessments. It does not include the diagnosis or management of ocular diseases. Consequently, further ophthalmological examinations could not be conducted to confirm the exact etiology of suppression, and only participants’ self-reported medical history could be considered. For this reason, the study was designed to focus on comparing the detection performance of the W4D and BAG rather than investigating the underlying causes of suppression.

Although the exact etiology of suppression could not be clinically confirmed, according to participants’ self-reports, the etiologies of suppression varied and included conditions such as trauma, macular degeneration, or strabismus, which introduced heterogeneity among participants. However, the authors considered that this variability may enhance ecological validity by more accurately reflecting real-world clinical conditions, thereby facilitating a more representative comparison between the W4D and BAG.

It should also be noted that the statistical significance observed in McNemar’s test was borderline, as the findings may differ with a greater number of participants. Future studies may consider collaborating with ophthalmologists to compare the performance of the W4D and BAG specifically in participants with confirmed suppression, and further stratifying them by underlying etiology to determine which conditions are associated with higher sensitivity in each test.

Conclusion

This study represents the first large-scale investigation comparing the W4D and BAG tests for suppression detection. The results indicate a significant difference in the performance of the two tests in identifying suppression. In light of the current lack of a gold standard for suppression testing, the authors recommend using the W4D test as the initial screening tool. Additional confirmatory assessments should be conducted as needed to ensure that individuals with suppression are not overlooked, thereby promoting more comprehensive visual care.
