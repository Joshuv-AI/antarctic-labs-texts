A rare case of peripheral ameloblastic fibroma manifesting as a maxillary gingival mass
Authors: Yu Du, Ping Yang, Nan Xie, Yang Ning
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAmeloblastic fibroma (AF) is a rare benign odontogenic tumor characterized by the coexistence of epithelial and mesenchymal components, typically arising intraosseously in the posterior mandible during the first two decades of life. Its peripheral variant, occurring extraosseously in gingival soft tissue without bone involvement, is exceedingly rare. Owing to its clinical resemblance to reactive gingival lesions, peripheral AF poses diagnostic challenges and is frequently misdiagnosed without histopathological confirmation.Case presentationWe report a case of peripheral AF in a 34-year-old Chinese woman who presented with a slow-growing, asymptomatic soft tissue mass on the palatal gingiva between teeth 23 and 24. Clinical examination revealed a well-defined, sessile lesion measuring 6 mm × 6 mm, with no signs of pain, bleeding, or tooth mobility. Radiographic evaluation, including panoramic imaging and cone beam computed tomography, confirmed the absence of bone involvement. The lesion was excised under local anesthesia, followed by root planing and soft tissue reconstruction using a laterally positioned flap to preserve esthetics. Histopathological examination demonstrated classic features of AF, including epithelial strands and cords within a cell-rich ectomesenchymal stroma, with peripheral palisading and central stellate reticulum-like cells. Immunohistochemistry revealed low Ki-67 proliferation index and negative BRAF expression, supporting the benign nature of the lesion. The final diagnosis was peripheral ameloblastic fibroma. Follow-up over 20 months showed no recurrence and excellent gingival healing with preserved architecture.ConclusionThis case underscores the importance of considering peripheral AF in the differential diagnosis of gingival masses, particularly those mimicking reactive lesions. Histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses are essential for accurate diagnosis and appropriate management. Conservative surgical excision with careful flap design can achieve excellent functional and esthetic outcomes. Given its rarity, every well-documented case of peripheral AF contributes meaningfully to the understanding of its clinical behavior and optimal treatment strategies.

Background

Ameloblastic fibroma (AF) is an uncommon benign mixed odontogenic neoplasm, characterized by the coexistence of epithelial and mesenchymal components. Histologically, it exhibits a highly cellular ectomesenchymal stroma reminiscent of the dental papilla, interspersed with epithelial strands and nests that recapitulate features of the dental lamina and enamel organ. Notably, there is a complete absence of hard tissue formation [1]. Originally described by Kruse in 1891 [2], AF most frequently arises during the first two decades of life, with a slight male predilection. It predominantly affects the posterior mandible and is often associated with unerupted teeth, presenting as a centrally located intraosseous lesion [3]. In contrast, peripheral AF represents an exceptionally rare variant, with only a limited number of cases reported to date—many of which lack comprehensive clinical characterization. Herein, we report an unusual case of peripheral AF occurring in a female patient, presenting clinically as an epulis-like lesion on the palatal gingiva of the maxillary arch. In addition, a brief review of previously reported cases is included to provide context and highlight the clinical features of this uncommon entity.

Case presentation

A 34-year-old Chinese female patient presented to the Department of Periodontology with the primary complaint of a soft tissue mass on the left palatal gingiva of the maxilla, which had persisted for over three years. Since its initial appearance, the lesion had remained stable in size and was asymptomatic, with no associated pain or bleeding. Her medical and family histories were unremarkable, and routine hematologic examinations yielded results within normal ranges. Extraoral examination showed a symmetrical facial profile without skin abnormalities or lymphadenopathy. Intraoral examination identified a sessile, well-defined gingival mass measuring approximately 6 mm × 6 mm, situated on the palatal interdental papilla between teeth 23 and 24. The lesion exhibited a smooth, pink-colored surface, a broad attachment base, and firm consistency (Fig. 1). Percussion tests of the involved teeth (23 and 24) were negative. Both teeth exhibited no mobility or noticeable displacement. Panoramic radiographic evaluation revealed no signs of root resorption involving teeth 23 and 24, nor was there radiographic evidence of alveolar bone resorption (Fig. 2). To further rule out potential involvement of adjacent osseous structures, cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) was performed, confirming intact cortical continuity without evidence of significant bone destruction or abnormalities (Fig. 3). Based on these findings, a provisional clinical diagnosis of fibrous epulis was established.

Local infiltration anesthesia was administered to the palatal gingiva spanning teeth 22 to 25 using articaine with epinephrine, followed by meticulous disinfection and sterile draping. The lesion was completely excised and promptly submitted for histopathological evaluation. An intrasulcular incision was carefully executed along the gingival margins from teeth 22 to 24, and a full-thickness flap was elevated to expose the root surfaces and the underlying alveolar bone. Examination of the alveolar bone adjacent to tooth 23 revealed a superficial, saucer-shaped area of concave resorption. Subsequently, meticulous recontouring of the affected alveolar bone was performed, followed by thorough scaling and root planing procedures on the exposed root surfaces of teeth 23 and 24. Following repositioning of the mucoperiosteal flap, a laterally positioned flap was harvested from the palatal region of tooth 24 and employed to effectively cover the interdental papilla between teeth 23 and 24. Interrupted 6 − 0 non-resorbable sutures were placed to stabilize the flap (Fig. 4). Postoperative care included administration of ibuprofen for analgesia and chlorhexidine mouth rinse for optimal healing and infection control.

Surgical excised tissue was stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histopathological evaluation. Under the low power field, the lesion was solid and well-demarcated underneath the superficial mucosa which was comprised with odontogenic epithelial and mesenchymal components (Fig. 5a). It indicated that the provisional diagnosis of fibrous epulis was excluded and suggested an odontogenic tumor, including peripheral ameloblastoma, peripheral AF, and peripheral odontogenic fibroma. On high power field, numerous epithelial strand-like structures were observed, showing a distinct peripheral palisading arrangement reminiscent of ameloblastic cells, with central cells displaying a relatively loose configuration. In addition, areas of immature ectomesenchymal stroma were present (Fig. 5b-f).

Immunohistochemical staining was conducted to further refine the diagnosis and assess proliferative activity of the lesion. Ki-67 staining, used as an indicator of cellular proliferation, revealed a low labeling index, suggesting minimal proliferative activity within the tumor [4]. Moreover, immunostaining for BRAF was negative, indicating the absence of the BRAF V600E mutation (Fig. 5g, h). The limited Ki-67 expression and lack of BRAF mutation collectively reinforce the benign nature of the lesion, with no histological indications of aggressive behavior or potential for malignant transformation [5]. Considering all clinical and histopathological evidence, a definitive diagnosis of peripheral ameloblastic fibroma was established.

At the 10-day postoperative evaluation, the surgical site demonstrated satisfactory healing without signs of inflammation, redness, swelling, or bleeding. The palatal gingival papilla exhibited excellent integrity, with no apparent recession (Fig. 6a, b). Following local disinfection, sutures were removed uneventfully. The patient was advised to continue regular follow-up visits and seek prompt consultation if discomfort or other symptoms emerged.

Clinical follow-ups at 3, 14, and 20 months postoperatively revealed no signs of recurrence or bone destruction (Fig. 7). The gingival architecture remained stable and aesthetically pleasing, characterized by full, well-defined interdental papillae. Additionally, no significant gingival recession was observed on either the buccal or palatal surfaces (Fig. 6c-h)

Discussion

Peripheral ameloblastic fibroma (AF) represents an exceedingly rare variant of the ameloblastic fibroma, a benign mixed odontogenic neoplasm that classically presents as an extraosseous lesion. Unlike its central counterpart, peripheral AF arises within the soft tissues overlying tooth-bearing regions, most commonly the gingiva, without any involvement of the underlying bone. A comparative analysis of the reported cases reveals notable epidemiologic differences between peripheral and central AF. While central AF predominantly affects adolescent males and favors the posterior mandible, peripheral AF demonstrates a female predilection, with a reported male-to-female ratio of 5:8. Furthermore, peripheral lesions tend to localize more frequently in the anterior maxilla, with a maxilla-to-mandible ratio of 9:4 and an anterior-to-posterior ratio of 8:5. The age at diagnosis spans a broad spectrum—from as early as 2 weeks of age to as late as 54 years—with a mean age of 19.66 years, indicating that peripheral AF can present in both pediatric and adult populations, including rare congenital cases. Lesion duration averaged approximately 15.2 months, while tumor size showed wide variability, ranging from as small as 0.8 mm to as large as 40 × 30 × 20 mm.

Clinically, peripheral AF often presents as a firm, painless, sessile gingival mass, closely resembling common reactive lesions such as fibrous epulis. Because the clinical presentation is nonspecific and radiographic findings are usually subtle, a definitive diagnosis cannot be established based on clinical features alone and requires histopathological confirmation. In the present case, the lesion was initially suspected to be fibrous epulis. However, this impression was revised after histological examination revealed distinct odontogenic epithelial strands and cords, which indicated an odontogenic tumor rather than a reactive fibrous lesion. The main diagnostic considerations were peripheral odontogenic fibroma, peripheral AF, and peripheral ameloblastoma.

Histopathological evaluation demonstrated odontogenic epithelial nests with an enamel organ–like appearance embedded within a relatively cellular mesenchymal stroma. The peripheral cells resembled inner enamel epithelium or pre-ameloblasts, exhibiting columnar morphology with mildly reversed nuclear polarity, while the central cells displayed a loosely arranged configuration reminiscent of an early-stage enamel organ. Although ameloblastoma may also present with enamel organ–like epithelial structures, its neoplastic epithelium is typically more mature, with taller columnar peripheral cells and a greater abundance of stellate reticulum-like cells. In addition, the stroma in our case appeared immature and hypercellular, contrasting with the mature fibrous stroma commonly seen in ameloblastoma [17].

Immunohistochemical analysis revealed no BRAF V600E mutation, providing supportive but not definitive evidence against ameloblastoma. Most ameloblastomas harbor this mutation, with reported frequencies ranging from 46% to 80%, irrespective of clinical or histopathological subtypes [18]. Interestingly, recent clinical research has shown that approximately 68% of ameloblastic fibromas also harbor BRAF p.V600E mutations, most often within the mesenchymal component [19]. Therefore, in this case, the final diagnosis was determined primarily on the basis of the histopathological characteristics.

In addition to ameloblastoma, peripheral odontogenic fibroma was also considered in the differential diagnosis. However, in the present case, the epithelial component was relatively abundant and exhibited enamel organ–like differentiation. By contrast, peripheral odontogenic fibroma typically contains sparse epithelial elements, which appear as small nests, islands, or strands of undifferentiated odontogenic epithelium, set within a bland fibrous stroma with moderate to dense collagen [20]. Taken together, these clinical, histopathological, and immunohistochemical findings supported the final diagnosis of peripheral ameloblastic fibroma.

Notably, none of the reported cases of peripheral AF have exhibited evidence of bone invasion, further distinguishing this extraosseous variant from its intraosseous counterpart. In all documented cases, surgical excision served as the definitive treatment modality. Recurrence was reported in only a single case involving a congenital lesion; however, this instance was later associated with possible intraosseous involvement. Given the incomplete clinical documentation and diagnostic uncertainty surrounding that case, its inclusion remains inconclusive [11]. By contrast, all other cases, including the present one, demonstrated no signs of recurrence during follow-up, reinforcing the favorable prognosis of peripheral AF when appropriately managed with complete surgical excision.

In the current case, the lesion’s position in the anterior maxillary region, adjacent to the gingival papilla between teeth 23 and 24, necessitated a precise and esthetically mindful surgical approach. A laterally positioned flap was carefully designed and mobilized from the palatal aspect of tooth 24 to cover the excised site, with the dual objective of reconstructing the interdental papilla and minimizing the risk of postoperative gingival recession. This technique facilitated tension-free primary closure while maintaining the integrity of the surrounding soft tissue architecture. Follow-up evaluations confirmed uneventful healing, with no evidence of significant gingival recession or distortion of the papillary contour—validating the effectiveness of this conservative approach in achieving both functional stability and esthetic preservation. Such surgical strategies are especially advantageous in the esthetically sensitive anterior maxilla.

In conclusion, peripheral AF represents a rare but distinct odontogenic entity, marked by a benign clinical course, absence of osseous involvement, and a low risk of recurrence. Its demographic and anatomical characteristics differ notably from those of intraosseous AF, and while it often mimics reactive gingival lesions clinically, accurate diagnosis hinges on histopathological confirmation. Conservative yet precise surgical management ensures both oncologic control and optimal esthetic outcomes, particularly in anatomically and cosmetically critical regions.
