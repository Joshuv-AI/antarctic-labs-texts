Predicting recurrent chest pain in patients with myocardial bridging using explainable machine learning: a multicenter study integrating CT-derived functional and inflammatory imaging biomarkers
Authors: Jingquan Hu, Kuigang He, Jing Luo, Rui Xia, Yufeng Zhang, Xiaolong Ma, Zhihong Shao, Xiaolong Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMyocardial bridging (MB) is a common congenital coronary anomaly that is increasingly recognized as a potential cause of myocardial ischemia and recurrent chest pain, even in the absence of obstructive coronary artery disease. However, reliable noninvasive risk stratification for recurrent chest pain in patients with MB remains challenging. Advanced coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA)–derived biomarkers, including CT-based fractional flow reserve (CT-FFR) and the pericoronary fat attenuation index (FAI), may capture complementary functional and inflammatory mechanisms underlying MB-related symptoms, but their combined prognostic value has not been fully clarified.MethodsPatients diagnosed with myocardial bridging on CCTA were consecutively enrolled. Clinical characteristics and CCTA-derived anatomical, functional, and inflammatory parameters were systematically collected. Recurrent chest pain during follow-up was defined based on standardized clinical criteria. After feature selection using Cox proportional hazards regression, a random survival forest (RSF) model was constructed to predict recurrent chest pain. Model performance was evaluated using concordance indices and time-dependent receiver operating characteristic analyses. Model interpretability was assessed using Shapley additive explanations (SHAP).ResultsA total of 174 patients with MB were included and 54 patients (31.03%) experienced recurrent chest pain during follow-up. Multivariable analysis identified advanced age, female sex, diastolic luminal stenosis of the bridged segment, systolic compression index, CT-FFR ≤ 0.80, elevated FAI and increased pericoronary adipose tissue (PCAT) volume as independent predictors of recurrent chest pain. SHAP-based interpretability analysis demonstrated that the predictive model achieved good discrimination, with area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) values of 0.895 (95% CI, 0.854–0.926) in the training cohort, 0.837 (95% CI, 0.781–0.889) in the validating cohort, and 0.804 (95% CI, 0.718–0.872) in the independent testing cohort.ConclusionsAn explainable machine learning model integrating CT-derived functional and inflammatory imaging biomarkers supports individualized prediction of recurrent chest pain in patients with myocardial bridging. Introduction

Myocardial bridging (MB) refers to a congenital anatomical variant in which a segment of an epicardial coronary artery, most commonly the mid‒left anterior descending (LAD) artery, courses intramyocardially beneath a “bridge” of myocardial fibers. Depending on the depth of myocardial embedding, MBs are classified into superficial and deep types [1, 2]. Although traditionally regarded as a benign finding, accumulating evidence suggests that MB may be associated with myocardial ischemia, angina, acute coronary syndromes, and, in rare cases, sudden cardiac death. Importantly, a subset of patients with MB experience recurrent chest pain despite the absence of significant atherosclerotic coronary artery disease, posing substantial diagnostic and therapeutic challenges [3, 4].

Fractional flow reserve (FFR) remains the reference standard for identifying ischemia-causing coronary lesions, with a threshold of ≤ 0.80 indicating hemodynamically significant impairment. Nevertheless, the invasive nature of FFR limits its routine use, particularly in patients without overt coronary stenosis. CT-based fractional flow reserve (CT-FFR), derived from coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA), has emerged as a reliable noninvasive alternative and demonstrates good concordance with invasive FFR measurements. In parallel, the pericoronary fat attenuation index (FAI), which quantifies the radiodensity of perivascular adipose tissue, serves as a surrogate marker of coronary inflammation and has shown prognostic relevance in coronary artery disease [5–7].

Conventional coronary angiography (CCA) has limited sensitivity for MB detection because of dynamic systolic compression, known as the “milking effect”. In contrast, CCTA offers superior anatomical visualization and allows simultaneous assessment of coronary stenosis, wall shear stress, and perivascular inflammation. Advanced imaging biomarkers such as CT-FFR and FAI, provides a unique opportunity to capture both the functional consequences of dynamic coronary compression and the inflammatory milieu surrounding bridged coronary segments [8–10]. However, the extent to which these complementary mechanisms jointly contribute to recurrent chest pain in MB patients remains insufficiently explored.

From a methodological perspective, conventional Cox proportional hazards models are widely used for time-to-event analyses but rely on assumptions of linearity and proportional hazards, which may not adequately capture the complex, nonlinear interactions among imaging biomarkers and clinical variables. Machine learning approaches, particularly ensemble methods such as random survival forests (RSFs), offer greater flexibility in modeling such interactions and have demonstrated improved predictive performance in cardiovascular risk stratification. Nonetheless, the limited interpretability of machine learning models has hindered their clinical adoption. Explainability techniques, including Shapley additive explanations (SHAP), provide a framework to quantify feature contributions at both global and individual levels, thereby enhancing clinical interpretability.

Against the background, the present multicenter retrospective study aimed to address the existing knowledge gap by integrating CT-derived functional and inflammatory imaging biomarkers within an explainable machine learning framework. Specifically, we sought to (1) identify CCTA-based predictors of recurrent chest pain in patients with myocardial bridging, (2) develop an RSF-based predictive model, and (3) apply SHAP analysis to elucidate the individual contributions of CT-FFR, FAI, and other relevant variables. We hypothesized that the combined assessment of functional impairment and perivascular inflammation would improve individualized risk prediction for recurrent chest pain in this patient population.

Materials and methods

Study population

This multicenter retrospective cohort study consecutively screened 857 patients who underwent at least two coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) examinations between June 2016 and June 2022 across three tertiary medical centers. Eligible patients met all the following inclusion criteria: (1) chest pain as the initial and primary clinical presentation; (2) a coronary artery disease–reporting and data system (CAD-RADS) score of 0, indicating no evidence of coronary atherosclerosis; and (3) the presence of a myocardial bridge (MB) located in a coronary artery main trunk. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) documented coronary plaque or fixed coronary stenosis at the time of chest pain onset; (2) concomitant moderate-to-severe valvular heart disease or clinically significant arrhythmias; (3) a history of malignancy or prior cardiovascular intervention or cardiac surgery; (4) suboptimal CCTA image quality insufficient for diagnostic analysis, defined as a signal-to-noise ratio < 15; and (5) missing key baseline clinical or imaging data. After applying these criteria, 174 patients were ultimately included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of patient selection. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 174 patients with MB and no evidence of coronary atherosclerosis (CAD-RADS 0) were included in the final analysis

The study protocol was approved by the institutional review boards of all participating centers, and the requirement for written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Patients enrolled at Center 1 between June 2016 and June 2019 (n = 96) were assigned to the training cohort. Patients from Center 2 enrolled between May 2019 and May 2022 (n = 43) constituted the validating cohort, while patients from Center 3 enrolled between June 2019 and June 2023 (n = 35) served as the independent testing cohort. The study endpoint was recurrent chest pain, defined as ≥ 2 episodes of chest discomfort occurring after the initial CCTA evaluation, recorded as events when at least two of the following criteria were fulfilled: (1) chest pain with compressive, constrictive, or heavy characteristics lasting longer than 5 min; (2) symptoms triggered or aggravated by physical exertion or emotional stress; (3) newly developed or ischemia-worsening electrocardiographic changes, including ST-segment depression ≥ 0.1 mV or T-wave inversion; and (4) determination by an attending cardiovascular specialist that the symptoms were of cardiac origin after systematic exclusion of non-cardiac causes (e.g., gastroesophageal reflux disease, pleural disorders, or musculoskeletal pain) based on medical history, physical examination, and necessary ancillary tests such as cardiac enzyme profiling. Patients meeting these criteria with recurrent episodes were classified as having recurrent chest pain [11]. Follow-up commenced on the date of the first CCTA examination performed for chest pain evaluation and ended at the occurrence of recurrent chest pain or the date of the last follow-up (outpatient visit or telephone interview), whichever occurred first.

CCTA scanning protocol

All patients underwent CCTA according to a standardized imaging protocol. Scans were performed using multidetector CT systems, including Aquilion One 320 (Toshiba, Otawara, Japan), SOMATOM Force dual-source CT (Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany), uCT 860 (United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China), and CT 6000 (Philips Healthcare, Surrey, UK). Whole-heart image acquisition was completed within a single cardiac cycle.

Scanning parameters were adjusted according to body mass index, with typical settings including a tube voltage of 120 kV, tube current of 500–650 mA, a field of view of 16 × 16 cm, slice thickness and increment of 3 mm, and a pitch of 0.5 mm. A nonionic iodinated contrast agent (Ultravist 370; Bayer Healthcare, Berlin, Germany) was administered via an antecubital vein using a dual-head power injector at a flow rate of 5 mL/s, with a total volume of 60–80 mL depending on body weight, followed by a 40 mL saline flush at the same rate.

An artificial-intelligence-based bolus-tracking system was used to determine the scan timing, with the region of interest positioned at the aortic root. Image acquisition was triggered 6 s after the attenuation reached 100–200 Hounsfield units. Images were reconstructed at the optimal diastolic or systolic phase, with a reconstruction slice thickness of 0.625–0.75 mm and an increment of 0.33–0.625 mm. To ensure adequate image quality, patients with a heart rate exceeding 75 beats per minute received oral β-blockers (25–75 mg) approximately 1 h before scanning. All imaging datasets were transferred to a dedicated workstation (ShuKun Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) for subsequent analysis.

To minimize inter-center variability, all CCTA datasets were transferred to a dedicated workstation and processed using a standardized post-processing pipeline, including coronary segmentation and feature extraction, with identical software versions and parameter settings across centers. Given that the analyzed imaging biomarkers were HU-based and physiologically defined, no additional intensity normalization was applied.

Image analysis

Image analysis was independently performed by two senior attending radiologists, each with more than five years of experience in coronary CTA interpretation, who were blinded to clinical outcomes. Assessments were conducted in accordance with the standardized CAD-RADS framework [12]. Interobserver agreement was assessed using the kappa statistic, with kappa values ≥ 0.80 indicating acceptable agreement. In cases of disagreement, the images were jointly reviewed; if consensus could not be reached, a third senior radiologist with more than 15 years of coronary CTA experience adjudicated the final decision.

All measurements were performed on images reconstructed at both optimal systolic and diastolic phases. The following MB-related parameters were assessed: MB location (distance from the coronary ostium to the MB entry), MB type (superficial or deep), MB depth (maximum thickness from the myocardial surface to the deepest point of the tunneled artery), MB length (distance from the proximal entry to the distal exit), MB muscle index (MB length × MB depth), MB systolic compression index [(minimum diastolic luminal area − minimum systolic luminal area) / minimum diastolic luminal area], luminal stenosis during diastole and systole, and the length of the involved coronary artery main trunk.

To further ensure the robustness of measurement reproducibility, interobserver reliability for continuous MB-related parameters was additionally evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Following a predefined washout period, the same two radiologists independently re-evaluated all MB measurements under blinded conditions. Interobserver ICCs were calculated using a two-way random-effects model with absolute agreement. The MB stenosis ratio was calculated as [(luminal area of the adjacent coronary segment − minimal luminal area within the MB segment) / luminal area of the adjacent segment] × 100%. The MB length ratio was defined as (MB length / length of the corresponding coronary artery main trunk) × 100%.

Coronary FAI and CT-FFR measurements

Pericoronary fat attenuation index (FAI) and pericoronary adipose tissue (PCAT) volume were quantified using semiautomated software (FAI Application of CoronaryDoc, ShuKun Technology, version 1.0.0). FAI was defined as the mean CT attenuation of adipose tissue located within a radial distance from the outer vessel wall equal to the vessel diameter. PCAT volume was calculated as the total volume of voxels with attenuation values ranging from − 190 to − 30 HU. During analysis, regions with severe calcification or motion-related artifacts were excluded. All measurements were performed using a uniform reconstruction kernel and consistent noise-reduction parameters to ensure interpatient comparability.

CT-derived fractional flow reserve (CT-FFR) was computed using a deep learning–based platform (Shukun-FFR, version 1.0.0). After automatic extraction of coronary anatomy and hemodynamic features, CT-FFR values were measured at locations 2–4 cm distal to the MB segment to avoid direct compression effects within the bridged segment. All analyses were conducted using identical software versions and default parameter settings.

Machine learning and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2). Continuous variables with normal distributions are presented as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed variables are expressed as median (Q1, Q3). Categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages. Between-group comparisons were conducted using the independent-sample t test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

In the training cohort, univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to identify risk factors associated with recurrent chest pain. Variables with P < 0.05 were subsequently incorporated into a random survival forest model constructed using the “randomForestSRC” package. Model hyperparameters included the number of trees (ntree), number of variables randomly sampled at each split (mtry), node size (nodesize), and the log-rank splitting rule.

Variable importance ranking was applied to eliminate features with minimal contribution, thereby reducing dimensionality and mitigating the risk of overfitting. Model performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, area under the curve (AUC), and calibration plots. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to compare the cumulative incidence of recurrent chest pain between groups.

Model interpretability was assessed using the Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) framework to elucidate the decision-making process of the machine learning model and to quantify the contribution of individual features. SHAP value computation and visualization were performed using the “iml” and “shapviz” R packages.

Independent risk factors for recurrent chest pain in MB patients

Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors of recurrent chest pain. Advanced age, female sex, diastolic luminal stenosis rate of the bridged segment, systolic compression index, CT-derived fractional flow reserve (CT-FFR) ≤ 0.8, FAI, and PCAT volume were all identified as independent risk factors for recurrent chest pain (Table 2; all P < 0.05). In contrast, several lesion-specific anatomical parameters, including MB depth, the presence of newly developed coronary plaques, and fixed coronary stenosis, did not differ significantly between patients with and without recurrent chest pain (all P > 0.05).

The RSF model was implemented using the randomForestSRC package with a log-rank splitting rule to accommodate the time-to-event nature of recurrent chest pain. A sufficiently large number of trees (ntree) was specified to ensure model stability, while the number of candidate variables at each split (mtry) and the minimum terminal node size (nodesize) were optimized within the recommended range based on out-of-bag (OOB) error estimation. Internal validating using OOB samples demonstrated gradual convergence and stabilization of prediction error with increasing tree numbers, suggesting no evident underfitting (Supplementary Table S2).

Model calibration was evaluated using bootstrap-based calibration curves with 1,000 resamples in all cohorts (Fig. 3b–d). In the training cohort, the bias-corrected calibration curve closely approximated the ideal 45-degree line, with a mean absolute error (MAE) of 0.028 (n = 96). Similar calibration was observed in the validating cohort (MAE = 0.031, n = 43). In the independent testing cohort, calibration was modestly reduced, particularly at higher predicted risk levels (MAE = 0.045, n = 35). Time-dependent Brier scores and calibration slopes at 12, 24, and 36 months further quantified calibration performance. In the training cohort, Brier scores were 0.118, 0.094, and 0.081, with an integrated Brier score (IBS) of 0.086 and a 36-month calibration slope of 0.97. In the validating cohort, Brier scores remained acceptable (0.124, 0.101, and 0.089), with a calibration slope of 0.93 at 36 months. In the testing cohort, prediction error increased modestly (Brier scores: 0.137, 0.112, and 0.098), and the 36-month calibration slope decreased to 0.88, indicating moderate miscalibration, particularly in higher-risk ranges. Detailed results are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Time-dependent performance, calibration, and risk stratification

Time-dependent ROC analyses were conducted to assess predictive performance at different follow-up durations. In the training cohort, the AUC values for predicting recurrent chest pain at 12, 24, and 36 months were 0.725, 0.781, and 0.837, respectively. Corresponding AUC values in the validating cohort were 0.746, 0.776, and 0.804 (Fig. 4a, c and e). Calibration curves demonstrated good agreement between predicted and observed recurrence probabilities, particularly beyond 24 months of follow-up (Fig. 4b and d, and 4f), indicating satisfactory long-term predictive accuracy.

Model explainability using SHAP analysis

SHAP analysis was performed to interpret the contribution and directionality of each feature in the model’s predictions. CT-FFR ≤ 0.8 was the most important feature, with negative SHAP values (ranging from − 0.31 to -0.14), suggesting an acceptable positive association between impaired coronary flow and cardiovascular risk. Other major contributors included PCAT volume, FAI, and the diastolic stenosis rate, whereas demographic variables (age and sex) had comparatively lower importance (Fig. 7a).

Discussion

This study focused on the prediction of recurrent chest pain in patients with MBs by incorporating CT-FFR, FAI, MB-specific morphological parameters, and clinical variables into a machine learning model. The results demonstrated that CT-FFR ≤ 0.8, elevated pericoronary FAI, increased PCAT volume, advanced age, female sex, increased diastolic stenosis rate and decreased systolic compression index were significantly associated with recurrent chest pain. The predictive model exhibited acceptable discrimination across the training, validating, and testing cohorts, with AUCs of 0.895, 0.837, and 0.804, respectively. Time-dependent ROC analysis further revealed that the model maintained high prognostic accuracy at 12, 24, and 36 months (AUCs of 0.752, 0.841, and 0.895, respectively).

Using the SHAP method, we visualized the contribution of each variable and helped explain their contribution to predictions. CT-FFR ≤ 0.8 emerged as the most influential predictor (SHAP contribution: +0.251), and its threshold effect led to a sharp increase in the predicted risk probability (from 0.75 to 0.996). This finding is consistent with prior studies identifying a CT-FFR ≤ 0.8 as a marker of hemodynamically significant stenosis, supporting the importance of impaired coronary flow reserve in adverse prognoses [13].

The secondary risk factors included the PCAT volume (SHAP: +0.193), pericoronary FAI (SHAP: +0.131), and diastolic stenosis rate (SHAP: +0.129). The SHAP summary plot revealed a concentration of high FAI values (orange points) in the positive range, suggesting that visceral fat accumulation may exacerbate endothelial dysfunction via inflammatory cytokine release [14]. The dose-dependent effect of inflammation should be carefully considered in prognostic modeling. Notably, the PCAT volume exhibited a bimodal SHAP distribution, indicating a potential threshold effect: when the PCAT volume exceeded 1973.16 mm³ (corresponding to a positive SHAP region), its prognostic impact substantially increased.

Female sex had a SHAP contribution of + 0.108, although the wide interquartile range (− 0.1–0.3) indicates marked individual variability. This heterogeneity may be related to menopausal status, the hormonal milieu, or anatomical differences [15], and future models may benefit from incorporating interaction terms. Although age contributed + 0.076, the SHAP values were clustered near zero, suggesting a marginal diminishing effect with increasing age.

Previous studies have shown that CT-FFR outperforms anatomical parameters in predicting major adverse cardiac events [16, 17], with CT-FFR ≤ 0.8 being associated with a higher incidence of revascularization, myocardial infarction, and death [18]. In our cohort, a CT-FFR ≤ 0.8 was an independent predictor of recurrent chest pain. This is likely due to the reduced flow velocity and volume caused by MB-induced systolic compression of the coronary wall, particularly in cases of deeper or longer bridges. Additionally, coexisting coronary vasospasm may further aggravate luminal narrowing and reduce CT-FFR values.

We also identified a significant association between recurrent chest pain and both FAI and PCAT volume. Consistent with prior research, an elevated FAI is considered a noninvasive biomarker of coronary inflammation [19], reflecting pathological PCAT states. Proinflammatory cytokines released by inflamed PCAT diffuse into the adjacent coronary wall, contributing to plaque vulnerability and adverse events [20, 21]. Dang et al. [22] reported a positive correlation between FAI values and CAD severity, suggesting that the FAI is a surrogate for the PCAT inflammatory burden. Yan et al. [23] reported that the FAI improved the diagnostic performance of CCTA in identifying ischemic coronary lesions. Qin et al. [24] further demonstrated that the FAI can predict in-stent restenosis, supporting its role as a noninvasive biomarker. Chronic perivascular inflammation has also been implicated in both plaque formation and vulnerability [25]. In our study, 14 patients developed coronary plaques during follow-up, although plaque formation was not significantly associated with recurrent chest pain, possibly because of the relatively short 36-month follow-up period and the limited number of ischemia-inducing plaques.

Myocardial perfusion primarily occurs during diastole [26]. MB segments experience persistent dynamic compression during diastole, leading to a reduced coronary luminal diameter and impaired subendocardial perfusion. This phenomenon is driven by both mechanical compression and endothelial dysfunction [27]. Several studies have linked vasomotor abnormalities in MB segments to luminal narrowing, coronary spasm, and endothelial damage [28], suggesting that diastolic flow limitation is a key pathophysiological driver of symptoms [29]. Moreover, a reduced systolic compression index has been shown to reflect increased diastolic restriction [30], which is consistent with our finding that lower compression indices are associated with recurrent chest pain.

Notably, chest pain can occur in MB patients even in the absence of overt atherosclerosis [31]. With aging, the physiological cardiac reserve decreases, and several factors may interact to worsen MB-related ischemia: proximal plaque formation reduces distal perfusion pressure; left ventricular diastolic dysfunction and hypertrophy aggravate oxygen supply–demand mismatch; and microvascular compression, vasospasm, and endothelial dysfunction further impair myocardial perfusion [32]. Our findings corroborate these mechanisms, showing significantly elevated chest pain recurrence in older MB patients.

Compared with men, women are more likely to experience stable angina symptoms, possibly due to stress-induced microvascular dysfunction and vasospasm [33]. Our data further support this sex difference, with higher recurrence rates in elderly and female MB patients, which is consistent with previous reports [34].

Although traditionally viewed as complex and opaque, recent advances in explainable machine learning have greatly improved model transparency, supporting broader clinical applicability [35]. By employing techniques such as SHAP, these models provide clear insights into how individual features contribute to predictions, thereby enhancing their interpretability and clinical utility [36]. In this study, we employed a Cox regression–based framework integrated with SHAP to enhance interpretability. The random survival forest model exhibited acceptable predictive performance, and SHAP-based interpretation further supported the clinical relevance of key imaging biomarkers, thereby supporting individualized risk stratification and supporting therapeutic decision-making.

MB presents diagnostic challenges in patients with recurrent chest pain without obstructive coronary artery disease. The 2024 ESC guidelines emphasize early consideration of the INOCA and the roles of coronary microvascular dysfunction and MB [37]. Evidence shows that MB may cause ischemia and infarction through mechanisms unrelated to plaque, such as endothelial dysfunction [27, 38]. Additionally, MB can exacerbate ischemia when it coexists with moderate stenosis [39]. These findings support the ESC’s recommendation for invasive coronary function testing [37], underscoring the need for a comprehensive, mechanism-based diagnostic approach beyond conventional assessment.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, although we integrated imaging-derived biomarkers with machine learning techniques to develop a predictive model for recurrent chest pain in patients with myocardial bridging, the retrospective design and the stringent inclusion criteria, specifically restricting enrollment to patients with CAD-RADS 0 and excluding those with concomitant fixed stenosis or coronary plaques, were intentionally applied to ensure population homogeneity and relative pathophysiological purity. While methodologically justified, this strategy substantially reduced the number of eligible cases and recurrent events, which may have limited statistical power and affected model stability. Consequently, the risk of overfitting cannot be entirely excluded, despite favorable discrimination and calibration observed in the validating and testing cohorts. Moreover, the generalizability of the proposed model to broader clinical populations remains to be confirmed through further external validation. Second, inter-center heterogeneity in imaging platforms and acquisition parameters may influence model robustness and applicability, potentially affecting the reproducibility of imaging-derived features across different clinical settings. Third, the associations identified between imaging biomarkers and outcomes are primarily statistical and do not establish causality. SHAP analysis elucidates feature contributions within the model but does not imply direct biological causation. For example, a high SHAP value for CT-FFR ≤ 0.8 reflects acceptable discriminatory ability within the dataset rather than definitive pathogenic influence. Some MB morphological parameters and age demonstrated lower SHAP contributions, which may indicate that their effects are mediated indirectly through functional impairment and inflammatory activity rather than reflecting a lack of biological relevance [40, 41].

Several MB morphological parameters and age demonstrated relatively low SHAP contributions in multivariable models. This finding does not necessarily imply limited biological relevance. Instead, it may reflect that impaired coronary flow reserve and active pericoronary inflammatory status represent more proximal and potentially modifiable pathophysiological drivers of symptom recurrence, whereas age and certain morphological characteristics may exert their effects indirectly by influencing these functional and inflammatory processes. As a result, their contributions may be partially mediated or attenuated in multivariable modeling. Rather than constituting a model limitation, this pattern may offer deeper insights into the underlying pathophysiology of MB-related chest pain, a hypothesis that warrants further investigation through larger, multicenter cohorts and prospective mechanistic studies.

In summary, this study provides preliminary imaging-based and explainable machine learning evidence to support risk stratification for recurrent chest pain in patients with myocardial bridging. The principal contribution of this work lies in highlighting the combined role of coronary hemodynamic impairment and pericoronary inflammatory activity in symptom recurrence, rather than in establishing definitive causal inference or immediate clinical decision rules. Future prospective, large-scale, and standardized multicenter studies, complemented by dedicated mechanistic research, are required to validate model performance and to elucidate the true causal roles of these imaging biomarkers in the development and progression of MB-related chest pain.
