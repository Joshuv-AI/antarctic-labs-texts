A unique case of unilateral optic pit and coloboma without macular pathology: an asymptomatic 42-year-old female
Authors: İsa Yuvacı, Muhammed Muaz Osmanoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo report a rare case of unilateral hybrid optic disc anomaly characterized by the coexistence of optic disc pit and optic disc coloboma in the absence of maculopathy, evaluated with multimodal imaging including optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA).Case presentationA 42-year-old asymptomatic woman was found to have coexisting optic disc pit and temporal optic disc coloboma in the right eye. Visual acuity was normal, and no structural or clinical evidence of maculopathy was detected. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) demonstrated preserved macular architecture. Fluorescein angiography (FFA) showed normal vascular filling without leakage. Optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) revealed a hypointense area at the base of the pit and localized peripapillary capillary rarefaction. The absence of corresponding FFA abnormalities suggests that the OCTA hypointensity may reflect signal attenuation rather than true ischemia.ConclusionsHybrid optic disc anomalies are exceedingly rare, and the absence of maculopathy in this setting is exceptional. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of a hybrid optic disc anomaly without associated maculopathy, as well as the first evaluated using OCTA. Multimodal imaging, including OCT, OCTA, and FFA, is essential for accurate characterization, and OCTA findings should be interpreted cautiously in deeply excavated lesions.

Introduction

Congenital optic disc anomalies, including optic disc pit (ODP) and optic disc coloboma (ODC), are rare cavitary malformations that may be associated with serous maculopathy and visual impairment [1–3]. They are generally encountered as isolated conditions. The coexistence of both anomalies in the same eye, referred to as a hybrid optic disc anomaly, is extremely uncommon and may present diagnostic challenges.

We report a 42-year-old asymptomatic woman with unilateral coexisting ODP and temporal ODC without maculopathy. To the best of our knowledge, this appears to be the first reported case of a hybrid optic disc anomaly presenting without macular involvement. Comprehensive multimodal assessment—including fundus examination, structural optical coherence tomography (OCT) [4], fluorescein angiography (FFA), ultrasonography, and optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA)—was undertaken to characterize the anatomical and microvascular features [5]. Imaging confirmed preserved macular integrity despite marked structural excavation.

Case report

A 42-year-old woman visited our clinic for a routine eye examination. The patient reported no visual issues. Her medical history was unremarkable, with no systemic diseases or previous eye surgeries. Best-corrected visual acuity was 20/20 in both eyes. Light reflexes were intact, and color vision was normal. Anterior segment examination showed no abnormalities.

A dilated examination of the right eye’s fundus revealed a localized depression on the temporal side of the optic disc, along with a large, sharply defined, white, bright-based, deep excavation extending temporally, indicative of an optic disc coloboma. There was no evidence of pigment deposition or hemorrhage. The neuroretinal rim was intact in the nasal superior and inferior quadrants, but absent in the temporal region due to the excavation (Fig. 1A, B). The macula and foveal reflex appeared normal. The left eye’s disc and macula were normal.

Color fundus photography, swept-source optical coherence tomography (SS-OCT), and optical coherence tomography angiography (OCT-A) were performed in this patient. SS-OCT images revealed no evidence of significant subretinal fluid accumulation or intraretinal cystic spaces between the optic pit and the retina. The macular architecture was interpreted as normal on OCT, indicating the absence of optic pit maculopathy. A small, well-defined hyporeflective space was noted at the base of the optic pit; however, it did not extend toward the macular region. Only a minimal intrapapillary fluid cavity adjacent to the pit was observed (Fig. 2a). In the fellow eye, OCT sections of the macula and optic disc were unremarkable.SS-OCT imaging did not reveal any thinning of the retinal nerve fiber layer. The optic disc in the right eye appeared enlarged, a finding consistent with colobomatous disc morphology (Fig. 2b).

In addition, B-scan ultrasonography was performed to exclude other excavated optic disc anomalies. B-scan ultrasonography showed no evidence of diffuse posterior scleral ectasia. Visual fields were obtained using the Humphrey Field Analyzer with the 24–2 SITA Standard strategy. Examination revealed enlargement of the blind spot in the right eye without decreased retinal sensitivity at the central visual field points, while the left eye showed normal visual field findings.

In the OCT angiography analysis (4 × 4 mm scan centered on the optic disc), a hypointense flow signal was observed at the base of the optic pit, with localized reductions in flow density in the region corresponding to the colobomatous excavation. The surrounding peripapillary vascular network and the macular region showed no definite areas of nonperfusion (Figure 3).

To confirm the absence of serous elevation or leakage, fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) was performed, revealing normal chorioretinal filling and a single cilioretinal artery at the pit margin in the early phase without abnormal hyperfluorescence. In the late phase, no leakage or pooling was detected in the macula or peripapillary area, and mild staining was strictly confined to the anomalous disc tissue (Fig. 4).

OCTA findings

To evaluate the microcirculatory architecture of the patient’s retinal tissue, OCTA scanning was performed using the REVO FC 130 device (Optopol Technology Ltd., Poland).

Accordingly, in the 6 × 6 mm retinal OCTA scans, no signs of neovascularization or intraretinal/subretinal flow abnormalities were detected. However, in the SCP and DCP images of the right eye, a marked reduction and irregularity in capillary density were observed in the peripapillary region, attributed to the optic disc coloboma and the overlying optic pit (Fig. 5).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first report describing a hybrid optic disc anomaly—coexistence of an optic pit and optic disc coloboma in the same eye—without associated macular involvement or any resulting complications.

Congenital optic pits may occur alone or in combination with optic disc colobomas [7]. A review of previously reported cases reveals that the coexistence of an optic pit and coloboma in the same eye has been described in only a few instances. [8–13]. Examination of these cases indicates that macular involvement has been present at some point during the patient’s lifetime.In contrast, the current patient exhibited no visual symptoms and no evidence of macular edema or retinoschisis on OCT, underscoring the uniqueness of this case (Table 2).

SRD: Serous Retinal Detachment

The pathophysiological mechanism underlying the development of maculopathy in association with optic pits and colobomas has been a subject of ongoing investigation and debate. Optic disc pit (ODP) is thought to result from incomplete closure of the superior segment of the embryonic fissure, whereas optic disc coloboma is attributed to a developmental defect involving the proximal portion of the embryonic fissure [10]. This observation may support the concept that optic disc pit and coloboma are not entirely distinct entities, but instead represent different phenotypic expressions within the spectrum of cavitary optic disc anomalies. Optic disc coloboma is a congenital anomaly that, like optic disc pit, is classified among excavated optic nerve head defects. It is classically located in the inferonasal region, corresponding to incomplete closure of the embryonic fissure. In contrast, the present case demonstrated an atypical temporal localization of the colobomatous excavation [14].

Doyle et al. suggested that a membranous structure covering the optic pit may act as a physical barrier, potentially limiting fluid entry into the retina and thereby reducing the risk of maculopathy [4]. In contrast, Jain and Johnson proposed that the primary mechanism involves abnormal anatomical communications between intraocular and subarachnoid spaces, through which fluid may migrate under the influence of translaminar pressure gradients [15]. In the present case, no membranous structure was observed over the optic disc, yet no subretinal or intraretinal fluid accumulation was detected in the macula. Although minimal peripapillary fluid was present on OCT imaging, the absence of an intraretinal schisis tract extending toward the fovea prevented macular involvement. These findings support the anatomical and pressure-gradient-based model described by Jain and Johnson, rather than a mechanism based solely on the presence or absence of a membranous barrier.

In the differential diagnosis, peripapillary staphyloma typically appears as a deep peripapillary excavation surrounding a relatively normal-appearing optic nerve head, rather than as a focal defect confined within the disc itself [16]. Morning glory disc anomaly is characterized by an enlarged optic disc located within a funnel-shaped peripapillary excavation and is frequently associated with central glial tissue and radially arranged retinal vessels emerging from the disc margins [17]. In contrast to these entities, our case was characterized by a sharply demarcated, bright white, bowl-shaped excavation located within the confines of an enlarged optic disc [18]. The absence of diffuse posterior scleral ectasia and atypical retinal vascular configuration further supported exclusion of these diagnoses.

Fundus examination enables the initial identification of optic disc anomalies; however, adjunctive imaging improves diagnostic assessment. In our case, OCT and fluorescein angiography primarily served to confirm the absence of maculopathy. Fluorescein angiography showed fluorescence in the region of the optic disc pit without hyperfluorescence or dye leakage, consistent with its non-vascular nature (Fig. 5). Structural OCT demonstrated preserved macular architecture.

OCTA was the principal modality for evaluating the peripapillary microvascular features. Despite regional vascular signal alterations on OCTA, no structural macular abnormality or visual impairment was detected. Although signal alterations were observed within the excavation, these findings should be interpreted cautiously due to the depth and structural irregularity of the lesion.

The most notable finding in this case was the discrepancy between OCTA and fluorescein angiography (FFA). OCTA demonstrated a hypointense area at the base of the optic disc pit that could suggest reduced flow. However, FFA showed normal vascular filling without leakage in the same region.This discordance highlights a key limitation of OCTA in deeply excavated optic disc anomalies. The depth of the pit and colobomatous excavation may cause signal attenuation, creating a false impression of flow deficit. Therefore, the hypointensity observed at the pit base is more likely related to attenuation artifact rather than true ischemia.

Localized peripapillary capillary rarefaction adjacent to the coloboma may reflect structural vascular hypoplasia. Cennamo et al. reported incomplete development of the radial peripapillary microvascular network in optic disc coloboma, which is consistent with our observation, although interpretation remains limited by imaging constraints [19].

In conclusion, to our knowledge, this is the first reported case of a hybrid optic disc anomaly without associated maculopathy, as well as the first evaluated using OCTA. It clearly demonstrates that hybrid anomalies can remain asymptomatic. Therefore, regular clinical monitoring with multimodal imaging is recommended for patient management.
