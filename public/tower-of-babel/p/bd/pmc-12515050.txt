Effects of Mineral Soil Supplementation on In Vitro Digestibility of Dry Matter and Organic Matter and In Sacco Degradability of Urea Treated Maize Stover
Authors: Sefa Salo, Mengistu Urge, Mohammed Y. Kurtu, Mesay Guyo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe quality of livestock feed resources enhances yield performance and plays a significant role in keeping production sustainable. However, today's climate forces the usage of crop residues as primary feeds due to soil degradation and the shortage of natural feed resources. Moreover, crop residues have low mineral content, requiring treatments to increase quality. This study evaluated the effect of the mineral soil (MS) supplement on in vitro digestibility of dry matter (IVDDM) and in vitro organic matter (IVOMD), and in sacco dry matter degradation (ISDMD) of urea‐treated maize stover (UTMS).MethodsThe in vitro experiment was done after the first stage of the Tilley–Terry method using three replicates of all treatments. The rumen cannula‐equipped Boran × Holstein Friesian steers were used to measure the ruminal degradation rate of dry matter (DM). The diet includes 70% urea‐treated maize stover (UTMS) and 30% concentrate mixtures (wheat bran and noug seed cake at a 2:1 ratio). Treatments were UTMS + concentrate alone (T1); UTMS + concentrate with 1% MS (T2); UTMS + concentrate with 1.5% MS (T3); UTMS + concentrate with 2% MS (T4); and UTMS + concentrate with 2.5% MS (T5).ResultsThe concentrate DM and crude protein (CP) content were 90.94% and 23.8%, respectively. Urea treatment increased the content of CP (15.49 [treated] vs. 4.97 [untreated]) and ash (7.14 vs. 6.37) and reduced the content of DM (86.40 vs. 92.53), NDF (70.12 vs. 84.63), ADF (33.93 vs. 41.58), and ADL (12.64 vs. 20.11) of maize stover. The highest (p < 0.05) DM and OM digestibility were recorded in supplements compared with the control group, while differences among the supplemented group followed the order of DM (T4 = T5 > T2 = T3) and OM (T3 > T2 = T4 = T5). The degradation of DM increased (p < 0.05) with increasing incubation time and level of mineral soil. Supplement levels of mineral soil improved (p < 0.05) the slowly soluble fraction ‘b’, rate of ‘b’ fraction degradability ‘c’, and effective degradability of urea‐treated maize stover.ConclusionsIt can be concluded that mineral supplementation enhanced the fermentability and degradability of the diet.

Introduction

In Ethiopia, limited access to quality and quantity of feed resources is a significant challenge to livestock productivity. Livestock primarily rely on natural pastures and crop residues (Getahun and Tegene 2019). The growing human population has increased pressure on land, reducing grazing areas and heightening reliance on crop residues for ruminant feeding (Tesfaye et al. 2005). However, crop residues are often high in fibre and deficient in essential nutrients, including nitrogen, energy, minerals and vitamins (Samson et al. 2017). The nutritional inadequacy leads to insufficient rumen degradation, failing to meet the needs of rumen microorganisms and the host animal (Undi et al. 2001). Consequently, livestock production that relies on low‐quality feeds remains suboptimal.

Improving crop residue's nutritional value and its incorporation into mixed diets is a promising approach to enhancing livestock production (Salem et al. 2004). For instance, the nutritional value of crop residues such as maize stover can be improved through urea treatment and supplementation (Tesfaye et al. 2005). Urea treatment promotes the action of ammonia on fibre, resulting in the release of soluble carbohydrates and energy that support the growth of cellulolytic bacteria, thereby improving the effective use of roughages (Tesfaye et al., 2018). This method does not only increase the nutritional value of crop residues by boosting protein content, nutrient digestibility and palatability (Woyengo et al. 2004). But, it also improves ruminal fermentation when complemented with vital nutrients such as minerals, nitrogen and energy (Tesfaye et al. 2005). This, results to better utilisation of low‐quality roughage (Khalil et al. 2015).

The growth of bacteria and the subsequent digestion rate of feed components in the rumen may be hindered by insufficient mineral supply (Adriani and Mushawwir 2009). Typically, the mineral content in crop residues is low and imbalanced (Garg et al. 2008). Hence, major feed sources in Ethiopia lack essential macro‐ and micro‐minerals such as sodium, phosphorus, zinc and copper, despite deficiencies varying across topographies and locations (Fekede et al. 2013). Thus, livestock keepers have traditionally supplemented deficits by licking mineral soils in mountainous and watercourse areas (Salo et al. 2024). Deficiencies in essential minerals such as phosphorus, calcium, potassium and sodium significantly affect rumen microbial biosynthesis and cellulolysis (Kumar et al. 2024). The availability of minerals can influence fermentation processes (Eberly et al. 2023), and an inadequate mineral supply may suppress microbial growth and fermentation (Imsya et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2020). In addition, the type and level of dietary supplementation can affect the microbial ecosystem and activity, impacting the availability of minerals, nitrogen and glucogenic precursors (Eberly et al. 2023).

Mineral supplementation has been shown to enhance the digestibility of treated crop residues (Imsya et al. 2019), leading to improved ammonia utilisation and increased digestibility of organic matter and fibre by rumen microbes (Lu et al. 2023). Adding minerals has been linked to greater microbial synthesis and enhanced cellulose digestibility (N. Nurhaita et al. 2008; Zain et al., 2010). Therefore, mineral supplementation is crucial in improving the digestibility of low‐quality roughages (Tjakradidjaja and Zain 2009). The mineral supplements can be obtained from locally available resources (Khalil et al. 2015), such as mineral soil licking. In Ethiopia, mineral soil is a widely accessible and cost‐effective resource favoured by livestock (Muluken et al. 2016) and has been shown to enhance nutrient digestibility (Tilahun et al. 2007). However, there is a lack of information regarding the effect of mineral soil levels on the digestibility of urea‐treated low‐quality feed. Thus, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of mineral soil supplementation on the in vitro digestibility of dry matter, organic matter and the in sacco dry matter degradability of urea‐treated maize stover.

Materials and Methods

Description of the Study Area

The study was conducted at Harmaya University. The area is at 526 km east of the Ethiopian capital, Addis Ababa. It is at 4203'E longitude, 9026'N latitude and 1980 m above sea level. The mean annual rainfall is 790 mm, and the average minimum and maximum temperatures are 8°C and 24°C, respectively (Sahle, 2008).

Experimental Feed Preparation

Maize stover was collected from the research and production fields of Haramaya University. The maize stover was cut manually to approximately 3 cm in size using a locally‐made axe. Ten kilogram dry matter (DM) of chopped stover was treated with a solution of 0.4 kg urea in 10 L of water (Sundstǿl and Coxworth 1984). Ten litres of the prepared urea solution was uniformly sprayed using garden watering cans mixed with 10 kg of DM chopped stover and rubbed with a hand to ensure proper penetration of the solution. The treated stover was placed in polyethylene bags and left for 21 days for incubation. After 21 days, the plastic was opened and aerated to remove excess ammonia (Sundstol et al. 1978). The mineral soil was collected from Adelle Lake. Noug seedcake and wheat bran were purchased from Addis Ababa oil and Dire Dawa Flour processing factory. Dicalcium phosphate was purchased from animal feed suppliers in Addis Ababa.

Treatment Diets and Analysis

Treatment diets were formulated by mixing 4% urea‐treated maize stover (UTMS), concentrate (wheat bran and noug seed cake at a 2:1 ratio), mineral soil and dicalcium phosphate after being ground separately. Mineral soil (MS) was added at 0, 1, 1.5, 2 and 2.5% per kg dry matter of the total mixed diet (Table 1). A 1% dicalcium phosphate was added to the supplemented treatment in the total mixed diet to meet the P requirements of sheep based on the recommendation of AFRC (2007). The proportion of concentrate was decided based on the recommendations of Merhun et al. (2016).

In Vitro Digestibility

In vitro dry matter and organic matter digestibility were determined following a two stage fermentation procedure of Tilley and Terry (1963) using three replicates of all treatments. The samples were incubated for 48 h with rumen fluid and buffer, followed by another 48‐h digestion with pepsin and HCl. Rumen liquor was collected from rumen‐fistulated Boran × Holstein Friesian steers in the morning before the animals were offered feed. The animals were fed at a maintenance level of feeding and had free access to water. The fistulated animals were fed with ad libitum native grass hay (6.5% CP) and 2 kg of concentrate mixture containing 74%, 25% and 1% wheat bran, noug seed cake and salt, respectively. The sample feed residue after incubation was ashed in a muffle furnace at 5500°C for 5 h to determine the ash and organic matter. The IVDMD was calculated according to Galyean (2010) as follows: IVDMD%=100×Initialdrysamplewt−Residue−blankinitialdrysamplewt.

In Sacco Dry Matter Degradability

Ruminal in sacco dry matter degradability characteristics were determined by incubating 3 g duplicate samples of feed in nylon bags having 41 µm pore size and 6.5 × 14 cm dimension, in three rumen‐fistulated Boran × Holstein Friesian steers for 0, 6, 12, 24, 48, 72 and 96 h (Ørskov and McDonald 1979). The nylon bags were inserted at different times and were removed simultaneously. Upon removing the nylon bags at the end of the incubation period, all bags were hand washed under running tap water and oven dried at 105°C for 24 h. Zero‐hour solubility was also estimated by hand washing the samples in nylon bags, as in the incubated samples. The degradability of dry matter (DMD) was determined for each incubation time as follows: DMDg/kgDM=100×DMinthefeedsample−DMinresidueDMinthefeed. Chemical Analysis

Samples of diet ingredients and treatment diets were dried at 60°C for 72 h in a forced dry oven. A portion of the dried samples was ground using a Willey mill and passed through a 1 mm sieve for chemical analysis and in vitro digestibility of dry matter. The remaining portion was ground and passed through a 2 mm sieve for the sacco degradability. Dietary ingredient samples were analysed for DM, ash and nitrogen (N) content according to the AOAC (2000). Crude protein content was calculated as N × 6.25. The NDF, ADF and ADL contents were determined using the detergent extraction technique outlined by Van Soest and Robertson (1985). Organic matter was calculated by subtracting the ash content from 100. The Ca, Mg, Mn, Zn, Cu and Fe contents of the mineral soil and feed were determined by using an atomic absorption spectrometer. The concentrations of Na and K were determined using a flame photometer, and P was determined using a spectrophotometer (AOAC 2000).

Statistical Analysis

The data collected for the study on in vitro dry matter and organic matter digestibility, as well as in sacco dry matter degradability were checked for normality and analysed using the general linear model of SAS software ver. 9.4 (SAS 2012). When the test revealed significant differences, the least significant difference (LSD) method was used to compare the means at p < 0.05. Results and Discussion

Chemical Composition of Feed

The CP content of untreated maize stover was 4.97% (Table 3), which is similar to the 4.31% reported by Sekhonyana and Fulpagare (2015) but greater than the 3.1%–3.8% noted by Tolera and Sundstøl (2000), Tesfaye et al. (2003) and Koralagama et al. (2008). Compared to the untreated, the CP content of the urea‐treated maize stover was 14.49%, a value which was comparable with the findings of Tesfaye et al. (2005). This value is higher than the CP levels of 5.09% and 9.7% reported by Wambui et al. (2006) and Hyelda et al. (2017), respectively, which may be due to factors such as the ensiling method, maize stover variety and nitrogen content. The increase in CP due to the treatment of maize stover in the current study could be attributed to nitrogen absorption by stover particles (Sekhonyana and Fulpagare 2015) and nitrogen fixation resulting from urea breakdown by bacterial urease (Dean et al. 2008). The CP level is above the recommended minimum of 7% for optimal rumen microbial action (Njidda et al. 2013). Crude protein content below 7.5% can restrict voluntary feed intake and diminish microbial activity, reducing feed digestibility (Van Soest 1994).

The urea treatment reduced the neutral detergent fibre (NDF) content, likely due to its ability to dissolve the hemicellulose fraction and facilitate the extraction of cell wall components (Rehrahie and Ledin 2001). Hirut et al. (2011) reported similar findings when maize stover was treated with urea. Puastuti et al. (2017) noted a decrease in the NDF content of ammoniated corn cob due to urea treatment. Both acid detergent fibre (ADF) and acid detergent lignin (ADL) levels were also reduced following urea treatment. This finding is comparable with Puastuti et al. (2017), who reported decreased ADF and lignin content due to urea treatment. Conversely, the ash content of maize stover increased due to the urea treatment. The increase in ash content may be attributed to the solubility of crude protein, nitrogen‐free extract and ether extract caused by urea treatment. The disintegration and elimination of insoluble carbohydrates from the solution result in increased fibre, associated cell wall components and ash content (Musimba 1981). Ma et al. (2020) noted increased ash content in urea‐treated rice straw. The ash content of urea‐treated maize stover was consistent with the findings of Wambui et al. (2006), who reported an ash content of 7.75%.

In Vitro Digestibility of Dry Matter and Organic Matter

The urea treatment significantly improved (p < 0.05) the dry matter (DMD) and organic matter digestibility (OMD) of maize stover (Table 4). This improvement is likely due to the enhanced solubility of hemicelluloses and cellulose, making the treated material more digestible (Ma et al. 2020). So, the quantity of cell wall material readily soluble in the rumen increases, which might have enhanced the availability of fermentable sugars in that environment (Rehrahie and Getu 2010). Urea treatment also supplies extra nitrogen, which boosts microbial activity (Van Soest 1994), further enhancing digestibility. Similar to the present result, sorghum stover's in vitro dry matter digestibility increased following urea treatment (Daniel et al. 2016). Furthermore, Rehrahie and Getu (2010) reported increased IVOMD of wheat straw treated with urea solution.

Addition of mineral soil enhanced (p < 0.05) the in vitro dry matter digestibility (IVDMD) of urea‐treated maize stover and concentrate mixture diet, likely due to enriched ruminal microbial population and enzyme activity (Supapong et al. 2019; Lu et al. 2023) resulting from mineral supplementation (Mahfuz et al. 2016). Literature showed that mineral salt licks are typically alkaline (Molina et al. 2013), which may help create a favourable environment for rumen microbes by stabilising the pH levels of the rumen. Alkaline minerals such as alkaline mineral complex, CaCO3 and NaHCO3 enhance the fermentation and decomposition of carbohydrates (Liu et al. 2024). Amongst the supplemented treatments, T4 and T5 exhibited the highest (p < 0.05) IVDMD, whereas non‐significant differences were found between T2 and T3. Therefore, adding phosphorus improved the digestibility of ammoniated rice straw (Zain et al. 2010). Likewise, the IVDMD of ammoniated palm oil leaves increased with sulfur and phosphorus supplementation (R. Nurhaita et al. 2014). Urea mineral licks also improved the digestibility of rice straw and hay dry matter (Wu et al. 2005).

In addition, supplementation of sulfur and nitrogen enhances cellulose digestion by rumen microbes, thereby facilitating fibre fermentation (Cherdthong et al. 2011). This implies that the mineral content in the diet is insufficient for rumen microbes and the supplementation of minerals supports microbial growth (Imsya et al. 2019), which in turn enhances fermentation processes (Febrina et al. 2016). An increase in the rumen microbial population leads to the production of more enzymes, thereby improving digestibility (Puastuti et al. 2017; Lu et al. 2023). Moreover, mineral supplementation can enhance the activity of cellulolytic bacteria (Wahyono et al. 2016), leading to improved fibre digestibility (Mahfuz et al. 2014). Essential mineral deficiencies can limit the growth of these bacteria and the rate at which feed components are broken down in the rumen (Adriani and Mushawwir 2009; Lu et al. 2023). In addition, mineral supplements can influence ruminal enzyme activity, overall microbial nitrogen production and nutrient digestion (Kumar et al. 2024). Increasing rumen microbial populations and cellulolytic activity would enhance nutrient digestibility. Furthermore, an increase in rumen microbial populations improves the animals' access to microbial protein (R. Nurhaita et al. 2014).

Mineral supplementation also enhanced (p < 0.05) the in vitro organic matter digestibility (IVOMD) of the diet (Table 4). The highest IVOMD was recorded in T3, with no significant differences observed amongst T2, T4 and T5. The improvement could be attributed to the stimulation of microbial growth by mineral addition (Zain et al. 2010; Lu et al. 2023). In agreement with the present result, previous literature (R. Nurhaita et al. 2014; Peripolli et al. 2017) also showed improved IVOMD of a variety of low‐quality diets. Low‐quality feeds often lack essential minerals vital for the growing of rumen microbes (Khalil et al. 2015), which can adversely affect digestibility (Nathaniel et al. 2021). A deficiency in minerals, particularly sulfur, phosphorus, magnesium and certain trace elements, can hinder the development of rumen microorganisms (Leng 1991). In addition, minerals such as copper, manganese and zinc play a critical role in promoting the growth of cellulolytic rumen microbes (Kumar et al. 2024), which may enhance the fermentation and digestion of organic matter, crude protein, cellulose and hemicelluloses (Tiwari et al. 2000).

In Sacco Dry Matter Disappearance

Ruminal degradability is a crucial factor to assess when evaluating the nutritional value of any feed (Kendall et al. 1991). Fermentative kinetics studied in situ and in vitro illustrates the degree of feed degradation caused by microbial action (Njidda et al. 2016). In line with the results of Selim et al. (2022), the disappearance of dry matter (DMD) in each of the diets in the current investigation increased with longer incubation durations. The washing loss was not significant (p > 0.05) (Figure 1). The similar DM disappearance at 0 h may be attributed to the comparable presence of fine and dusty particles in the treatment diets (Ogden et al. 2005; Gebrekidan et al. 2019). At this incubation time, DM disappearance is primarily due to mechanical action rather than microbial fermentation (Khan et al. 2009). This indicated that under the enhanced treatment, mineral soil and dicalcium phosphate were not very soluble in water.

Conversely, the washing loss of silages decreased with increasing limestone supplementation (Gaafar et al. 2010). These discrepancies may stem from differences in washing methods (hand washing vs. mechanical washing), volume of water used for rinsing (Evitayani et al. 2011), NDF content of the feed (Čerešňáková et al. 2007) and solubility of minerals in water (Abegaze and Chopra 2013). Mineral solubility can vary depending on the source (Genther and Hansen 2015) with organic minerals being more soluble than inorganic (Silva et al. 2020) in an alkaline environment (Lu et al. 2023). It was reported that dicalcium phosphate exhibited low solubility at a neutral pH of 7 (Abegaze and Chopra 2013), which account for the consistent washing losses observed across all treatments. Moreover, feeds with elevated NDF levels exhibited a smaller reduction in dry matter during washing (Čerešňáková et al. 2007). They also reported that mineral release occurred during a 72‐h incubation rather than washing. Thus, mineral solubility increases in acidic environments (Shaeffer et al. 2017).

Amongst the supplemented treatments, T5 exhibited the highest (p < 0.05) DMD at 24 h of incubation. At 12 and 48 h of incubation, T2 showed the greatest DMD (p < 0.05) while at 96‐h incubation periods, T3 showed the highest (p < 0.05) degradability (Figure 1). The digestibility enhancement can be attributed to the increasing microbes and the activity of microbial enzymes (Gresakova et al. 2018; Lu et al. 2023), which are influenced by the availability of minerals in the rumen (Faulkner and Weiss 2017). Also, the solubility of minerals can differ depending on their source, and only those minerals that dissolve in the rumen are likely to be available to microbes (Genther and Hansen 2015). In addition, mineral salt licks are typically alkaline (Molina et al. 2013), potentially creating a conducive environment for rumen microbes by stabilising the rumen pH. Maintaining a stable pH level promotes the production of microbial protein, which increases the overall rumen microorganisms and enhances digestion (Guo et al. 2023). Constantly, degradability of silage‐NDF increased with increasing limestone supplementation and extended incubation time (Gaafar et al. 2010).

In addition, the degradability of rice straw after 48 h was improved with high phosphorus supplementation (Mahfuz et al. 2014). Similarly, manganese supplementation enhanced the degradability of dry matter during both the 24‐ and 48‐h incubation periods (Biswas and Purnendu Biswas 2012). Moreover, it highlighted the beneficial impact of minerals on cellulase activity, which increased fibre degradation (Mishra et al. 2024). It was also noted that adding mineral block improved dry matter degradation (Insoongnern and Wachirapakorn 2017). The significance of degradability at the 48‐h mark is particularly noteworthy, as it closely corresponds to the average retention time of fibrous feed in ruminants (Kimambo and Muya 1991). High degradability after 48 h indicates better digestibility, as degradability values are often equated with digestibility (Ehargava and Ørskov 1987). Generally, high crude fibre content in the feed, which is associated with cell walls, is largely resistant to microbial enzymatic action and results in lower degradability (Tadesse et al. 2014).

In Sacco Dry Matter Degradability Characteristics

The soluble DM fraction ‘a’ did not significantly change (p > 0.05) with supplementation (Table 5). This was due to the presence of readily available soluble carbohydrates for the microbial population (Insoongnern and Wachirapakorn 2017). Similarly, adding zinc did not enhance the DM ‘a’ fraction of alfalfa hay (Arelovich et al. 2008). Moreover, supplementation with mineral selenium and zinc did not improve the rapid soluble fraction of feed (Mahgoup and Ibrahim 2015). Conversely, Gaafar et al. (2010) reported a notable reduction (p < 0.05) in the rapid degradability fraction as the levels of limestone supplementation increased. This variation may be associated with differences in mineral solubility within the rumen and NDF levels in the feed. Different sources of trace minerals exhibit varying solubility levels in rumen, which can influence rumen fermentation (Caldera et al. 2019). The soluble and potentially degradable dry matter fractions are considered as feed characteristics that are unaffected by rumen fermentation processes (Jelantik et al. 2021).

As per the study, mineral supplementation influenced the degradable fraction of dry matter (DM) ‘b’ and the degradation rate of DM ‘c’ for the slowly degradable portion (p < 0.05). The fraction ‘b’ is a gradually decomposed fraction of cell walls, mainly cellulose and hemicellulose (Ernawati and Abdullah 2022). The insoluble but degradable DM fraction ‘b’ value was higher (p < 0.05) in treatments T3 = T4 than in the other treatments. This indicates the presence of nutrients that can be utilised by rumen microbes (Ahmad Khan et al. 2015). The degradation rate ‘c’ is a key parameter influenced by the fermentative activities in the rumen (Jelantik et al. 2021). A higher value of ‘c’ reflects the rapid adaptability and digestive capacity of rumen microbes concerning the components of fraction ‘b’ (Ernawati and Abdullah 2022). Notably, the degradation rate ‘c’ for the slowly degradable portion was considerably greater (p < 0.05) in treatments T2 = T5, which is directly linked to the availability of fermentable substrates (Ernawati and Abdullah 2022). Adding minerals can promote the growth of rumen microorganisms, resulting in a higher solubility rate of feed (Anam et al. 2023).

Furthermore, adding minerals may alter the composition of rumen bacterial communities, and the activity of cellulolytic bacteria (Wang et al. 2020). Consistent with this finding, the slowly degradable fraction of silage increased with limestone supplementation (Gaafar et al. 2010). Hence, selenium administration increased the fractional degradation rate and slowly degradable portion of Leymus chinensis DM (Xun et al. 2012). Moreover, roughage's slowly degradable fraction and breakdown rate were enhanced by urea mineral lick supplementation (Wu et al. 2005). However, supplementation had no effect on the potentially degradable (PD) proportion (p > 0.05).

ED refers to the proportion of dry matter (DM) fermented in the rumen before its passage into the lower digestive tract (McDonald et al. 2002). In this study, mineral supplementation had a significant impact (p < 0.05) on the ED of dry matter. This effect is likely due to enhanced microbial activity and better feed degradability in the rumen as a result of mineral supplementation (Wahyono et al. 2016; Lu et al. 2023). In addition, mineral salt licks are typically alkaline (Molina et al. 2013), which may help create a conducive environment for rumen microbes by stabilizing rumen pH. Stable pH in the rumen allows for better microbial protein synthesis, which increases the total population and digestion (Pazla et al. 2018; Guo et al. 2023). Amongst the treatments with supplementation, the ED of DM was greater (p < 0.05) in T5. Consistent with this result, different levels of limestone supplementation led to improved silage ED (Gaafar et al. 2010). Furthermore, urea‐mineral lick supplementation increased the ED of roughage (Wu et al. 2005).

In addition, zinc supplementation increased the ED of urea‐treated straw (Biswas and Purnendu Biswas 2012). These results suggest that mineral supplementation can improve the degradability of low‐quality roughage in the rumen. The degradability of DM generally increases with higher levels of crude protein and ash content (Tesfayohannes et al. 2013), whereas it tends to decrease with elevated levels of NDF, ADF or ADL (Haj‐Ayed et al. 2000; Tesfayohannes et al. 2013). Mineral supplementation may reduce NDF and ADF content (Gaafar et al. 2010) in the diet through substitution, which could account for the higher ED observed in the supplemented treatments. Variations in the ED of DM are closely associated with the proportion of potentially degradable DM and NDF levels (Mupangwa et al. 2003).

Conclusions

The study demonstrated that urea treatment significantly improved the nutritional quality of maize stover by increasing crude protein and ash content while reducing dry matter, NDF, ADF and ADL. The digestibility of dry matter and organic matter was enhanced with urea treatment. Moreover, the addition of mineral soil supplementation further improved the digestibility and degradability of the urea‐treated maize stover and concentrate mixture diet. Therefore, mineral supplementation was found to be an effective strategy to improve the utilisation of low‐quality roughages in ruminant diets supporting better rumen function. Furthermore, supplementation with 2% mineral soil (T4) was recommended amongst the treatments for future on‐farm trials to enhance yield performance and plays a significant role in keeping production sustainable.
