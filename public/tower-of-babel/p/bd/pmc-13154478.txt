A nomogram based on dual-layer spectral detector CT-derived 40-KeV virtual monoenergetic images and iodine maps for preoperative prediction of regional lymph node metastasis in colorectal cancer
Authors: Yi Chen, Yuntao Xu, Wu Cai, Guangqiang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo develop and validate a dual-layer spectral detector CT (DLSCT)-based nomogram integrating DLSCT-derived quantitative parameters, radiomics features extracted from 40-KeV virtual monoenergetic images (VMI) and iodine maps, and clinical factors for preoperative prediction of regional lymph node metastasis (LNM) in colorectal cancer (CRC).MethodsA total of 225 patients with pathologically confirmed CRC were retrospectively enrolled and randomly assigned to a training cohort (n = 157) and a validation cohort (n = 68) at a 7:3 ratio. Clinical and pathological data, as well as DLSCT-derived quantitative parameters, were collected. Radiomics features were extracted from DLSCT-derived 40-KeV VMI and iodine maps in both the arterial phase (AP) and venous phase (VP). Radiomics features were further selected using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression. Multiple machine learning algorithms were then applied to develop radiomics models and compare predictive performance. Logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent clinical variables and DLSCT-derived quantitative parameters and to establish a clinical model. The radiomics score (Radscore), defined as the predicted probability from the optimal radiomics model, was integrated with clinical variables and DLSCT-derived quantitative parameters to construct a nomogram. Model performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsSerum carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 (CA19-9), enhancement pattern, normalized iodine concentration in the AP (NICAP), CT value at 100-KeV in the VP (CT100KeVVP), and normalized iodine concentration in the VP (NICVP) were identified as independent predictors of LNM in CRC. The nomogram integrating the Radscore with clinical variables and DLSCT-derived quantitative parameters achieved the best predictive performance, with areas under the ROC curve (AUCs) of 0.959 and 0.920 in the training and validation cohorts, respectively. DCA indicated that the nomogram provided a higher net clinical benefit for predicting LNM in CRC.ConclusionsA DLSCT-based nomogram incorporating radiomics features derived from 40-KeV VMI and iodine maps may provide a useful tool for the preoperative prediction of regional LNM in CRC and could potentially aid individualized treatment planning.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the third most commonly diagnosed malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1], with more than 1.9 million new cases and over 900,000 deaths reported annually [2]. Regional lymph node metastasis (LNM) is one of the major pathways of tumor dissemination in CRC and represents a key determinant of TNM staging [3]. The presence of LNM has important implications for treatment strategy and patient prognosis, influencing decisions regarding surgical management and adjuvant therapy [4, 5]. Therefore, accurate preoperative assessment of lymph node status is essential for appropriate staging and individualized management of patients with CRC.

Although pathological factors such as tumor differentiation and lymphovascular invasion are strongly associated with LNM, these parameters can only be determined after surgical resection, limiting their value for preoperative risk stratification [6]. Imaging examinations are thus the primary tools for preoperative nodal evaluation. However, conventional CT and MRI mainly rely on lymph node size and morphologic criteria, which show limited sensitivity for micrometastases and atypical metastatic nodes, leading to suboptimal diagnostic performance [7]. Accordingly, improved noninvasive approaches are needed to enhance preoperative prediction of LNM in CRC.

Dual-layer spectral detector CT (DLSCT) provides energy-resolved information and allows retrospective reconstruction of spectral images, including virtual monoenergetic images (VMI) and iodine maps. These images improve lesion conspicuity and provide functional information related to tumor perfusion [8]. Notably, low-energy VMI at 40-KeV maximizes iodine attenuation and improves visualization of subtle enhancement heterogeneity, whereas iodine maps directly reflect iodine uptake and intratumoral distribution [9]. These advantages suggest that radiomics features derived from DLSCT-based 40-KeV VMI and iodine maps may capture biologically relevant intratumoral heterogeneity associated with lymphatic dissemination. Previous studies have demonstrated the potential value of DLSCT in CRC staging, identification of metastatic lymph nodes, and prediction of molecular characteristics [10, 11]. Nevertheless, evidence regarding the application of DLSCT-derived 40-KeV VMI- and iodine map-based radiomics for preoperative prediction of regional LNM in CRC remains limited.

Radiomics has emerged as a promising approach for tumor staging and prognostic assessment by extracting high-throughput quantitative features from medical images, enabling characterization of tumor heterogeneity beyond visual interpretation [12]. Several CT-based radiomics models have been developed to predict LNM in CRC. Li et al. constructed a nomogram combining clinical risk factors and radiomics features from both primary tumors and regional lymph nodes, achieving moderate discriminatory performance [13]. Cheng et al. reported an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.830 using a multiphase CT-based radiomics model [14], indicating the potential for further optimization.

Accordingly, this study aimed to develop and validate a DLSCT-based nomogram integrating DLSCT-derived quantitative parameters and radiomics features extracted from 40-KeV VMI and iodine maps to improve the preoperative prediction of regional LNM in CRC.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University, and the requirement for informed consent was waived. A total of 380 consecutive patients with CRC who met the inclusion criteria between January 2021 and December 2024 were initially reviewed. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pathologically confirmed colorectal adenocarcinoma; and (2) DLSCT examination performed within 2 weeks prior to surgery. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) incomplete imaging or clinicopathological data; (2) unidentifiable tumors or poor image quality; (3) non-adenocarcinoma histology or coexistence of other malignancies; (4) receipt of any preoperative anticancer treatment (radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or biological therapy); and (5) complications such as intussusception or perforation that impeded accurate region-of-interest (ROI) delineation. Finally, 225 patients were included. Patients with one or more metastatic lymph nodes were classified as LNM-positive (n = 87), whereas those without metastatic lymph nodes were classified as LNM-negative (n = 138). The cohort was randomly divided into a training cohort (n = 157) and a validation cohort (n = 68) at a 7:3 ratio. Randomization was performed using a computer-generated random sequence. DLSCT acquisition and image reconstruction

All patients underwent contrast-enhanced abdominal and pelvic CT examinations using a single DLSCT scanner (IQon Spectral CT; Philips Healthcare, Best, the Netherlands). Patients were scanned in the supine position with the head-first orientation and both arms raised above the head. The scan range extended from the dome of the diaphragm to the lower margin of the anal canal. A nonionic iodinated contrast agent (iohexol, 350 mg I/mL) was injected through the antecubital vein using a dual-head power injector at a dose of 1.5 mL/kg and a flow rate of 3.0 mL/s. Bolus tracking was performed with a trigger threshold of 150 HU in the abdominal aorta. After the threshold was reached, patients were instructed to hold their breath during inspiration, and arterial-phase (AP) imaging was acquired. Venous-phase (VP) and delayed-phase (DP) images were obtained at 60 s and 180 s after contrast injection, respectively. The scanning parameters were as follows: tube voltage, 120 kVp; tube current, automatic tube current modulation; pitch, 1.234; gantry rotation time, 0.75 s/rotation; and collimation width, 64 × 0.625 mm. The acquired triphasic contrast-enhanced images were reconstructed using spectral-based imaging (SBI) to generate spectral datasets, including VMI, iodine maps, and effective atomic number (Eff-Z) maps. All images were reconstructed with a slice thickness and interval of 0.67 mm and transferred to a dedicated workstation (Philips IntelliSpace Portal, version 12.1) for further analysis.

DLSCT image analysis and quantitative measurements

Eff-Z maps, iodine maps, and VMI in both the AP and VP were analyzed on a dedicated DLSCT workstation. Clinical T stage was determined according to the eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system [15]. Tumor length was defined as the maximum longitudinal extent along the bowel axis, whereas maximum tumor thickness was measured as the largest perpendicular diameter on axial images. Enhancement pattern was assessed on VP images based on intratumoral CT attenuation differences. A difference of ≤ 10 Hounsfield units (HU) between the maximum and minimum CT values indicated homogeneous enhancement, whereas a difference of > 10 HU indicated heterogeneous enhancement [16]. The maximum tumor slice was identified using multiplanar reconstruction. ROIs were delineated on this slice as well as on the adjacent superior and inferior slices. ROIs encompassed the main solid components of the tumor while excluding intraluminal contents, peritumoral fat, adjacent vessels, and calcifications, with consistent ROI placement maintained across imaging phases. Additional ROIs were placed within the abdominal aorta or iliac artery on the same slice, avoiding areas of calcified plaque. The normalized iodine concentration (NIC) was calculated as the ratio of the iodine concentration of the lesion to that of the abdominal aorta or iliac artery on the same slice. All ROIs were independently delineated by a radiologist with 3 years of experience in abdominal imaging under blinded conditions and subsequently reviewed by a senior radiologist with 25 years of experience. Tumor segmentation on DLSCT images

Preoperative AP and VP 40-KeV VMI and iodine map images were exported for all patients. Primary tumors were manually segmented slice-by-slice using ITK-SNAP software (version 3.8.0) to generate three-dimensional volumes of interest (VOIs) encompassing the entire lesion. Tumor boundaries were delineated according to contrast-enhanced CT findings, including focal or asymmetric bowel wall thickening and abnormal enhancement relative to the adjacent normal bowel wall [17]. Care was taken to avoid intestinal contents, surrounding fat, vessels, and calcifications. The VOI masks were saved in NIfTI (.nii) format for subsequent radiomics analysis. All tumor VOIs were manually segmented by a radiologist and subsequently reviewed by a senior radiologist with extensive experience in abdominal imaging. During the review process, attention was paid to accurate tumor boundary delineation and exclusion of adjacent non-tumoral structures. In cases with indistinct tumor margins, the final segmentation was determined by consensus between the two radiologists. To assess reproducibility, 30 cases were randomly selected for intra-observer agreement analysis. The same radiologist repeated the VOI segmentation while blinded to the initial results. Radiomics features were re-extracted from the repeated segmentations, and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated. Features with ICC values ≥ 0.75 were considered reproducible and were retained for subsequent analysis. Representative examples of tumor segmentation are shown in Fig. 4.

Radiomics feature extraction and selection

Prior to feature extraction, image preprocessing was performed to reduce variability caused by heterogeneous voxel spacing. All images were resampled to an isotropic voxel size of 1 × 1 × 1 mm³ using a fixed-resolution resampling method. Radiomic features were then extracted from the original images using the open-source Python package PyRadiomics. The extracted feature set included first-order intensity statistics, shape descriptors, and multiple texture features. In addition, higher-order features were generated by applying image filters, including wavelet decompositions (LLL, LLH, LHL, LHH, HLL, HLH, HHL, HHH) and the Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG) filter (σ = 2, 3, 4, and 5). Detailed definitions and mathematical formulations of these features are available in the PyRadiomics documentation. To eliminate the influence of different feature scales, all radiomic features were standardized using Z-score normalization. All feature selection procedures were performed exclusively in the training cohort. Univariate analysis was first performed using the independent-samples t test or Mann-Whitney U test, depending on data distribution, to identify features significantly associated with LNM (P < 0.05). Pearson correlation analysis was then applied to reduce redundancy, and for feature pairs with correlation coefficients > 0.90, only one representative feature was retained. Next, the minimum redundancy-maximum relevance (mRMR) algorithm was used to select features with high relevance and low redundancy. Subsequently, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was conducted in the training cohort. The optimal regularization parameter (λ) was determined via ten-fold cross-validation, and features with non-zero coefficients were selected. All LASSO procedures were implemented using the scikit-learn package in Python.

Model construction and evaluation

Univariate and multivariate binary logistic regression analyses were performed to identify clinical and DLSCT factors associated with LNM, which were used to develop the clinical model. Radiomics-based classification models were developed using the selected features. To identify the classifier with the best discriminative performance, several commonly used machine learning algorithms were trained in the training cohort, including Naive Bayes, Extra Trees, AdaBoost, support vector machine (SVM), and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM). Model development and hyperparameter tuning were performed using a 5-fold stratified cross-validation combined with grid search. To address class imbalance, SMOTE oversampling was applied only to the training subset within each cross-validation fold, while the corresponding validation subset retained the original data distribution to prevent information leakage. All preprocessing procedures were incorporated into a unified pipeline and were fitted exclusively on the training subset within each fold before being applied to the corresponding validation subset.

Model performance was evaluated using AUC, sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and F1-score. The classifier achieving the highest cross-validated AUC with stable performance across the other evaluation metrics was selected as the final radiomics model. The selected model was then refitted using the entire training cohort and subsequently validated in the independent validation cohort. Calibration was assessed using calibration curves and the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, while discrimination was evaluated using AUCs in both the training and validation cohorts. The DeLong test was applied to compare the AUCs between models, and decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to estimate the clinical net benefit across different threshold probabilities. All analyses were implemented in Python (version 3.7.12). Additional packages such as lightgbm, imbalanced-learn, and shap were used for gradient boosting modeling, class imbalance handling, and model interpretability analysis.

Statistical analysis

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using independent-samples t tests, whereas non-normally distributed variables are presented as median (Q1-Q3) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages and compared using the chi-square test. Univariate and multivariate regression analyses were performed to identify independent risk factors for LNM. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 27.0), and a two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Radiomics models

A total of 1197 radiomics features were extracted, including shape features (n = 14), first-order features (n = 234), and texture features (n = 949), comprising Gray Level Dependence Matrix (GLDM, n = 182), Gray Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM, n = 286), Gray Level Size Zone Matrix (GLSZM, n = 208), Gray Level Run Length Matrix (GLRLM, n = 208), and Neighboring Gray Tone Difference Matrix (NGTDM, n = 65) features. Through the feature selection procedure, 10 AP and 11 VP radiomics features were retained (Fig. 5), and feature heatmaps were generated (Fig. 6). Based on the non-zero coefficients obtained from the LASSO regression model, LASSO-derived linear feature combination scores were calculated for each patient in the AP and VP, referred to as the AP-LASSO score and VP-LASSO score, respectively. These scores represent linear combinations of the selected radiomics features weighted by their corresponding LASSO coefficients.

The selected radiomics features were also used to train five commonly used machine learning algorithms to construct AP and VP radiomics models and compare predictive performance. Among these classifiers, LightGBM demonstrated the best overall performance in both phases. Therefore, LightGBM was selected as the final radiomics model, and its predicted probability for each patient was defined as the radiomics score (Radscore) for subsequent combined-model and nomogram construction. The AP and VP radiomics models achieved AUCs of 0.842 (95% CI: 0.777–0.908) and 0.885 (95% CI: 0.834–0.935) in the training cohort and 0.795 (95% CI: 0.687–0.902) and 0.859 (95% CI: 0.770–0.947) in the validation cohort, respectively. The performance of the five machine learning algorithms for constructing AP radiomics models is presented in Table 4, and the corresponding performance of VP radiomics models is shown in Table 5. To improve interpretability of the LightGBM models, SHAP analysis was performed to evaluate the contribution of individual radiomics features to the prediction of LNM, and the SHAP summary and feature-importance plots are presented in Figs. 7 and 8.

Nomogram model

The AP-Radscore and VP-Radscore were significantly higher in LNM-positive than in LNM-negative patients in both cohorts (Fig. 9). To evaluate the incremental value of radiomics beyond clinical factors, we constructed a combined model by integrating the Radscore with clinical variables and DLSCT-derived quantitative parameters, and presented it as a nomogram based on the training cohort (Fig. 10). The nomogram demonstrated superior predictive performance, with AUCs of 0.959 (95% CI: 0.932–0.986) and 0.920 (95% CI: 0.858–0.982) in the training and validation cohorts, respectively (Fig. 11). DeLong tests indicated that the nomogram significantly outperformed both the individual radiomics and clinical models (all P < 0.05; Table 6). The detailed performance metrics of the clinical model, AP radiomics model, VP radiomics model, and the combined nomogram in both cohorts are summarized in Table 7. Calibration curves showed good agreement between predicted and observed outcomes (Fig. 12), and the Hosmer-Lemeshow test revealed no significant lack of fit (P = 0.268 for training cohort, P = 0.235 for validation cohort). DCA demonstrated that the nomogram provided greater net clinical benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities (Fig. 13).

Discussion

In this study, clinical data and conventional CT features played a fundamental role in predicting LNM in CRC patients. The results showed that LNM-positive patients had higher levels of CEA and CA19-9 compared to LNM-negative patients, which is consistent with previous studies [6, 18]. CEA and CA19-9 are considered important serological markers reflecting the biological invasiveness of CRC and are commonly used for early diagnosis of CRC. Elevated levels are often closely associated with increased tumor burden, deeper local invasion, and enhanced metastatic potential [19, 20]. CEA lost statistical significance in multivariate analysis, possibly due to collinearity with other tumor burden-related variables.

In terms of imaging, patients in the LNM-positive group exhibited higher cT-staging and more prominent heterogeneous enhancement characteristics. The T-stage reflects the depth of tumor infiltration into the intestinal wall and surrounding tissues, and an increase in infiltration is often accompanied by a higher probability of lymphatic vessel invasion [21]. Additionally, heterogeneous enhancement usually suggests uneven distribution of neoangiogenesis within the tumor, with necrotic and highly perfused areas coexisting, representing one aspect of tumor heterogeneity. This may indicate an increased risk of local tumor extension and LNM [22–24]. These clinical and CT findings provide valuable references for assessing LNM.

DLSCT, through multi-energy data analysis, can distinguish different tissue compositions and quantitatively reflect tumor blood supply, metabolism, and tissue heterogeneity. These characteristics provide important biological insights for preoperative staging, differentiation assessment, and metastasis risk prediction in CRC [25, 26]. In the multivariate analysis of this study, CT100KeVVP, NICAP, and NICVP were identified as independent predictors of LNM in CRC tumors. CT100KeVVP is defined as the mean CT attenuation of the tumor volume on 100-KeV VMI in the venous phase, reflecting the spectral attenuation characteristics of the tumor under high-energy X-rays. This parameter can characterize tissue vascularization and metabolic activity. Higher CT100KeVVP values may indicate abundant tumor blood supply and increased metabolic activity. These conditions may increase the tumor’s demand for oxygen and nutrients, which can promote neoangiogenesis and increased microvascular density [27, 28]. Therefore, CT100KeVVP, as a key quantitative parameter in DLSCT, is of significant value in predicting LNM in hypervascularized CRC.

Both NICAP and NICVP are quantitative parameters derived from DLSCT iodine maps and reflect tumor blood perfusion, angiogenesis, and microcirculation characteristics at different phases [29]. NICAP mainly reflects the perfusion level of the tumor parenchyma, while NICVP better represents iodine retention and microvascular permeability in tumor tissue and surrounding stroma [30]. Previous studies have shown that high angiogenesis levels are closely related to invasive behavior and LNM in CRC [31–33]. Our results further support that higher NICAP and NICVP values may indicate abundant tumor blood supply and increased vascular permeability, thereby increasing the risk of LNM in CRC.

Radiomics enables quantitative characterization of tumor heterogeneity from medical images. It is regarded as an important bridge connecting imaging phenotypes with tumor biological behavior [34]. Compared to single imaging models, the nomogram model integrating clinical-DLSCT features and Radscore demonstrated better stability in discrimination, calibration, and clinical net benefit. This suggests that multimodal information integration helps to comprehensively characterize the tumor’s structure, blood supply, and microheterogeneity, thereby improving the model’s generalizability and clinical applicability [5]. From a clinical perspective, accurate preoperative prediction of lymph node metastasis is important for optimizing treatment strategies in patients with colorectal cancer. The proposed nomogram may facilitate preoperative risk stratification by identifying patients at higher risk of LNM who could benefit from more aggressive therapeutic approaches, such as extended lymphadenectomy or consideration of neoadjuvant therapy [35]. Conversely, patients with a low predicted risk may avoid unnecessary overtreatment and undergo more individualized surgical planning. Therefore, this nomogram provides a potential noninvasive tool to support clinical decision-making and personalized management in colorectal cancer.

Compared with previous CT-based radiomics studies, our work highlights the added value of low-energy monoenergetic images and iodine maps derived from DLSCT, which provide complementary information on tumor vascularity and heterogeneity. This integration may partly explain the superior performance of the combined nomogram.

However, this study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective single-center study with a relatively small sample size, which may introduce potential selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. Multicenter studies with larger prospective cohorts are needed to further validate the robustness of the proposed model. Second, tumor segmentation was performed manually, which may introduce inter-observer variability despite reproducibility assessment. Automated or semi-automated artificial intelligence-based segmentation methods may help improve consistency in future studies. Third, this study mainly focused on radiomics features derived from the primary tumor, whereas imaging features of regional lymph nodes were not included. Incorporating both primary tumor and lymph node features may provide complementary information and further improve predictive performance.

Conclusions

CA19-9, enhancement pattern, NICAP, CT100KeVVP, and NICVP are independent risk factors for LNM in CRC. A nomogram integrating clinical data, DLSCT quantitative parameters, and radiomics features from dual-phase 40-KeV VMI and iodine maps demonstrated good predictive performance, which may serve as a valuable tool for preoperative LNM assessment in CRC.
