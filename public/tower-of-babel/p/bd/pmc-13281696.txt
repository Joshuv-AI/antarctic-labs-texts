A Blended Course for Dental Faculty Development in Educational Design and Digital Teaching Practices
Authors: Meixun Sinky Zheng, Cassio Luiz Coutinho Almeida‐da‐Silva, Didem Ozdemir Ozenen
Published: 2026

Problem

Effective teaching requires pedagogical content knowledge, the integration of subject expertise and instructional methods [1, 2]. Yet many dental faculty enter academia without formal preparation in teaching and learning, making professional development essential [3]. They also need to keep pace with digital teaching modalities, including artificial intelligence [4]. Traditional face‐to‐face training programs are valuable for networking, but faculty's busy schedules and geolocation constraints make scheduling challenging. More flexible formats are needed to supplement in‐person programs. One approach that has gained popularity is blended learning, which integrates face‐to‐face and online instruction to enhance flexibility, engagement, and learning outcomes [5]. This study reports the design and outcomes of a blended dental faculty development program, focusing on faculty perception of the online delivery (IRB # 2024‐23).

Solution

During the 2023–2024 academic year, we developed the Teaching and Assessment in Dental Education blended course for faculty members. Hosted on Canvas, this course included four modules: (1) course planning; (2) item writing and analysis; (3) active learning and assessment; and (4) online and blended learning design. Generative AI (GenAI) content was integrated across the modules, including guidance on course policies and assessment redesign in the GenAI era (Table 1).

The modules addressed foundational knowledge acquisition and application. The module design was grounded in active learning and the use of formative feedback to support learning. Modules 1 and 2 were self‐paced. They incorporated multimedia, including short videos created with ScreenPal, and activities with feedback delivered through the cloud‐based tool SoftChalk (Figure 1). Module 3 was live on Zoom, and Module 4 was in person. Each module included post‐session activities to encourage reflection and application, and each required about 2 h to complete.

Warm‐up activity for the self‐paced module on writing MCQs. The activity was created with the cloud‐based learning tool SoftChalk. It prompted faculty to reflect on factors affecting exam performance beyond content mastery, including question quality, which then led to guidelines for writing meaningful MCQs.

Participation in the course was voluntary, and faculty could choose the modules relevant to their needs. For each completed module, faculty received continuing education (CE) credits and a certificate of completion. Faculty seeking CE credits completed a survey developed by the American Dental Association (ADA), administered through the CE office.

Results

The numbers of participants were 22 for Module 1, 17 for Module 2, 22 for Module 3, and 18 for Module 4, for a total of 79 enrollments. Both full‐time and part‐time faculty attended, with teaching responsibilities across didactic, preclinical, and clinical areas. Several part‐time faculty who had never attended in‐person programs, often because they were rarely on campus, were able to participate.

A total of 34 surveys were submitted across the four modules (43% response rate). For each module, the mean scores for survey questions ranged from 4.6 to 4.8 on a 5‐point scale. Table 2 presents the aggregated mean scores across all modules for questions on delivery format and effectiveness. All respondents indicated that the instructional methods were effective and that they were satisfied with the courses. Anecdotal verbal comments indicated that the certificate of completion served as an extra motivator for completing the modules. Several faculty members noted the plan to implement a reward system in their teaching, and some have adopted the technologies used in this course.

Overall, the blended format was well accepted. Beyond the relevance of the content, the active learning strategies also contributed to the course's success. The study used a non‐validated survey, and the findings should be interpreted with this limitation in mind. Future directions include gathering data from a larger sample, incorporating qualitative data, and assessing the impact on instructional practices.
