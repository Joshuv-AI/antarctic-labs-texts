Self-Efficacy–Based intervention for stress management in health centers employees
Authors: Hamideh saeidavi, Mahin Nazari, Leila Ghahremani, Abouzar Nazari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProfessional occupations for the healthcare workers are usually rife with occupational stress, especially when job demand exceeds the capabilities of the worker and workplace support. The effects of this condition include burnout, low well-being, and low-quality care. Accordingly, using self-efficacy theory of Bandura, the study appraises the impact of self-efficacy-based education intervention on stress management among health center employees of Ramhormoz County in Iran.MethodsThe quasi-experimental study was conducted on a sample of 103 employees drawn from urban and rural health centers, who were clustered for randomization into an intervention group (n = 49) and a control group (n = 54). The intervention constituted eight weekly 45-minute face-to-face self-efficacy sessions spread over two months. Before and two months after the intervention, data were collected with the Parker and DeCotiis Occupational Stress Questionnaire and the Schwarzer’s General Self-Efficacy Questionnaire. Data analyses were conducted using paired, independent t-tests, and Mann-Whitney U-test using SPSS 27; a level of significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsBefore the educational intervention, there were no significant differences in terms of job stress (p = 0.107), occupational anxiety (p = 0.280), occupational stress (p = 0.181), and self-efficacy (p = 0.176) between experimental and control groups. But after the intervention, there were significant differences between the groups in terms of job stress (p = 0.012), occupational stress (p = 0.034), and self-efficacy (p = 0.038). No significant difference found in occupational anxiety (p = 0.473).ConclusionThe effectiveness of the self-efficacy-based educational intervention was evident in its positive effect on stress management and coping techniques among health center employees in the Ramhormoz county. The study emphasized that training in self-efficacy could be beneficial for enhancing self-efficacy and reducing work stress in the interventional group-by linking individual competence to job adoption and stress management.Trial registrationThis study was registered with the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT), registration number IRCT20211222053487N1, on 2023-09-26.

Introduction

Occupational stress is considered the most significant pressure source in an individual’s life and can lead to both short- and long-term health consequences [1]. It is defined as the impact of work demands on an individual that results in internal changes and deviations from normal behavior [2]. Symptoms of this stress show across psychological, physical, and behavioral terms. Some may include depression, anxiety, emotional instability, and social withdrawal in the psychological sphere [3]; while physical symptoms may refer to heart increased rate; gastro-intestinal discomfort; sleeping disturbances; headache; musculoskeletal disorders, and immune dysfunction [4]; while behavior manifestations include absenteeism, diminished productivity, and substance abuse [5].

Moreover, healthcare personnel are very susceptible to occupational stress as a result of a high work demand with consistent patient contact, resulting in critical situations. This intensified pressure during the COVID-19 period included reports from different countries revealing high prevalence rates of health care workers who reported depression (25–43%), fatigue, sleep disturbances, or suicidal ideation [6]. For instance, Aberhe et. al.(2024) [7] revealed that health workers in Ethiopia exhibit a high level of occupational stress, and Zou et. al.(2022) found that about one-third of medical staff in China experience considerable occupational stress [8]. Moreover, people in Iran experience occupational stress. About 34% of health workers in health centers experience stress, while 42% are reported to have anxiety and 26% to suffer from depression (Kak Imam et al., 2024) [9]. Such findings indicate that occupational stress is a lifelong challenge for care systems worldwide.

Stress has direct effects on individuals and organizations. Research in diverse areas confirmed association of occupational stress with psychological disorders, musculoskeletal disorders, decreased quality of performance, and deterioration in quality of care [10]. There are different factors contributing to occupational stress. Some of these include organizational issues and work-related factors such as workload and lack of resources, while personal characteristics include age, gender, education, and pre-existing health conditions [11]. Among these, self-efficacy has emerged as a key person-centered determinant of how individuals cope with stress.

There are countless interventions in stress management such as results of cognitive-behavioral therapy, mindfulness-based treatment regimens, resilience training, relaxation techniques, and psycho-education [12]. And while evidence backs their use in reducing stress and improving coping strategies, perhaps effective are the ones that directly target self-efficacy, primarily because self-efficacy itself-the belief in one’s ability to perform a particular task and meet its challenges affects mental states, motivation, behavior, and persistence against obstacles [13]. People with high self-efficacy display better coping strategies, greater resilience, and more efficient stress management [14]. New studies confirm that among occupational settings training in self-efficacy produces a significant reduction of stress along with improvements in both job satisfaction and performance outcome [15, 16].

However most of this growing evidence is usually confined to studies on self-efficacy interventions on hospital nurses, as the stress experienced there is primarily acute and high-intensity, while primary health center employees are affected by varying forms of pressure, such as extensive administrative workload, available suboptimal resources, simultaneous responsibilities for preventive, promotive, and curative services, and high involvement with community members. These chronic and multifaceted demands make their experience distinct from hospital-based staffs, not much has been researched on stress management intervention suited for this category.

Hence the present study was designed to assess the effect of an educational intervention based on the self-efficacy model on stress management in health center employees of Ramhormoz County, Iran. This study, focusing on this under-researched population of health center staff, will contend with and build on the understanding of the role of self-efficacy training in reducing occupational stress in community-based healthcare settings.

Sampling method

In this study, we employed a randomly clustered sampling method. Ramhormoz has two urban health centers, four urban bases, four rural centers, and four rural-urban centers. Each of these centers was considered a cluster. We randomly selected four centers (comprising one Comprehensive Health Center, one Urban Base, one Rural Center, and one Rural Central Center). From these, we further randomly selected two centers (one Comprehensive Health Center and one Urban Base) as the test group, while the remaining two centers (one Rural Center and one Rural Urban Center) served as the control group. Finally, participants were randomly selected from each center based on the entry criteria.

Data collection tools

A. Demographic information questionnaire

This questionnaire collects demographic details, such as age, gender, marital status, employment and education status, number of children, type of employment, income level, spouse’s occupation, and place of residence.

B. Parker and dekotis occupational stress questionnaire

The Parker and DeCotiis Occupational Stress Questionnaire (developed in 1983) was used to assess job-related stress. It consists of 13 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = completely disagree to 5 = completely agree), with total scores ranging from 13 to 65, where higher scores indicate greater occupational stress. The questionnaire comprises two subscales: Time Pressure and Job-Related Anxiety.

To ensure content validity, the questionnaire was reviewed by a panel of 10 experts in health education and occupational health. Content Validity Ratio (CVR) and Content Validity Index (CVI) were calculated according to Lawshe’s method (1975). The CVR value was 0.78 and the CVI was 0.86, both above the acceptable thresholds, confirming good content validity.

Reliability was assessed in the current study using Cronbach’s alpha, which was 0.89 for the overall scale, 0.85 for the Time Pressure subscale, and 0.78 for the Job-Related Anxiety subscale, indicating satisfactory internal consistency. The Parker and DeCotiis questionnaire has previously demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties in Iranian populations, such as in the study by Amani et al. (2021) [18], where Cronbach’s alpha was reported as 0.90 and the reliability coefficient as 0.68.

C. Rogers general Self-Efficacy questionnaire

The Rogers General Self-Efficacy Questionnaire, developed by Rogers et al. (1982) [19], was used to measure self-efficacy. The original scale contained 36 items, but following factor analysis, items with a factor loading below 0.40 were removed, resulting in a 23-item version. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (completely disagree) to 5 (completely agree). The total score is calculated by summing the responses across all items, yielding a minimum possible score of 23 and a maximum possible score of 115, with higher scores indicating greater perceived self-efficacy.

Delavar et al. (2013) [20] assessed the psychometric properties of this scale among university employees in Iran. The reliability coefficient, determined using Cronbach’s alpha, was 0.87 for the total scale, 0.85 for men, and 0.88 for women, indicating satisfactory internal consistency.

Educational intervention

That intervention program was developed around the principles of self-efficacy and lasted eight sessions in an eight-week approach. Each session was 45 min long, taking place once a week in health centers for the experimental group. These were amalgams of lectures, questions and answers, and discussions, which stimulated the participants’ learning and encouraged active participation. The program included Bandura’s four proposed sources to build participants’ self-efficacy. Individual mastery experiences were gained through practical and problem-solving situations where stress management strategies were applied into simulated workplace situations leaving participants confident to try the same solutions for everyday problems. Second, vicarious experience was enhanced by encouraging participants to share their own tales and watch their fellow mates greatly involved in the task of coping with stress, thereby reinforcing their belief that that they too can acquire the coping strategies. Third, verbal persuasion was achieved through continuous encouragement and constructive feedback, as well as motivational statements from the trainers, thus elevating participants’ belief in their ability to cope with work-related stress. Finally, their basis was a physiological-emotional control by teaching relaxation techniques and breathing exercises along with stress management techniques that help participants in controlling symptoms of anxiety and achieving more emotional stability while dealing with challenging situations. Two months after the interventions, the questionnaires were completed again, and asking the response differences between the post- and pre-intervention scores in computing the measure of change. The intervention was thus directed at elevating participant belief in the successful management of stress along with better psychological resilience and occupational outcome performance. Table 1.

Session content and educational methodology

Method of collecting information

The information collection process began with obtaining the code of ethics (IR.SUMS.SCHEANUT.REC.1401055) from the ethics committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences approved on 04/12/2023 and the trial code (IRCT20211222053487N1). After securing the necessary permits from Jundishapur University of Medical Sciences, the researcher visited the health network centers (urban and rural) approved by the school and headquarters. Written informed consent was obtained from the participants, and pre-test questionnaires were distributed to the employees. They received instructions on how to complete the questionnaires, the purpose of the study, and the conditions for exiting the study. Employees were given a week to complete the questionnaires, after which the researcher revisited the centers to collect the completed forms. Two months after conducting the training sessions, post-test questionnaires were collected from both the experimental and control groups. This method ensured ethical compliance, informed consent, and accurate data collection before and after the educational intervention.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS version 27 statistical software. Initially, the normality of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Descriptive statistics, including frequency indices, mean, and standard deviation, were employed to summarize the data. To compare the two groups before and two months after the intervention, independent t-tests and chi-square tests were utilized. A significance level of 0.05 was applied to all tests.

Results

The intervention and control groups were comparable in terms of demographic characteristics. Participants were mostly aged 31–40 years (25% control, 27% intervention) and predominantly female (46% control, 43% intervention). Most were married (43% control, 38% intervention) and held a bachelor’s degree (35% control, 41% intervention). Spouse’s occupation, number of children, employment type, income, and place of residence were similarly distributed between groups, with no significant differences observed across any variable (all p > 0.05). These findings indicate that the two groups were demographically balanced at baseline(Table 2).

Discussion

This study evaluated the effectiveness of a self-efficacy-based educational intervention on stress management among healthcare workers in health centers in Ramhormoz County. The results showed that the intervention significantly reduced occupational stress and job pressure, while also enhancing self-efficacy. However, occupational anxiety did not significantly change. Effect sizes for the significant outcomes were moderate, indicating meaningful improvements in participants’ ability to cope with workplace demands in real-world settings.

The reduction in occupational stress and job pressure suggests that the intervention helped participants recognize stressors, improve coping and problem-solving skills, and strengthen their confidence in managing job-related challenges. The increase in self-efficacy further supports Bandura’s framework, which posits that belief in one’s capabilities fosters adaptive coping, motivation, and resilience under stress [21].

When compared with previous studies, our findings are consistent with research showing that self-efficacy-based and other stress management interventions can reduce occupational stress and enhance coping strategies among healthcare workers [22, 23]. The use of lectures, group discussions, and problem-solving exercises parallels the approaches employed in earlier programs that also reported improvements in job-related outcomes. On the other hand, the lack of significant reduction in occupational anxiety aligns with findings from other studies [24–26], where anxiety levels were less responsive to short-term interventions. This may be due to external organizational pressures, persistent job demands, or the influence of contextual factors such as the COVID-19 pandemic. Similar studies have suggested that longer interventions or more targeted approaches are needed to address entrenched anxiety symptoms [27, 28].

The relevance of this study lies in its focus on health center employees, a group often overlooked in occupational stress research, which tends to concentrate on hospital nurses. Unlike hospital-based personnel who primarily deal with acute patient care, health center employees must multitask across preventive care, community outreach, patient education, and administrative responsibilities. These diverse roles expose them to different stressors, underlining the need for tailored stress-management interventions. Our results confirm that self-efficacy-based training is effective in this setting, though a longer intervention period or more specialized focus may be necessary for reducing work-related anxiety [29, 30].

Taken together, these findings add to the literature by demonstrating both statistical significance and practical relevance. The observed effect sizes suggest that the intervention can lead to meaningful improvements in workplace functioning and employee well-being. By showing that self-efficacy can be enhanced through structured training, this study contributes to evidence supporting the application of Bandura’s framework in occupational health promotion [31].

The study has several limitations. First, although ethical approval was obtained and the trial was registered, the study was not preregistered, potentially affecting transparency. Second, all data were self-reported, which may introduce bias. Third, the short follow-up period limits conclusions about the sustainability of the intervention’s effects. Finally, cultural and organizational factors specific to Ramhormoz may limit generalizability to other regions. Future research should consider longer intervention durations, multicenter designs, and objective measures of occupational stress to validate and extend these findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this investigation proves that a self-efficacy-based educational intervention effectively lessens occupational stress while promoting coping skills among health center employees and that anxiety may need additional strategies. Addressing an almost entirely under-investigated population, these findings contribute to the mounting landscape investigating stress management in healthcare and propose practical suggestions for health organizations wishing to ameliorate employee well-being.
