Protocol for minimizing necrotic core formation in iPSC-derived cortical organoids
Authors: Md Mahfuz Al Mamun, Sundari Chetty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryCortical organoids (COs) derived from human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) provide a unique opportunity to model human neurodevelopment in vitro. Here, we present a protocol to prevent necrotic core formation without requiring organoid slicing. We describe steps for hiPSC culturing, preparation of single-cell suspension, seeding, and embryoid body (EB) and neuroectoderm formation. We then detail procedures for CO formation and maintenance without necrotic core formation. This protocol sustains long-term viability and preserves structural integrity by enhancing oxygen and nutrient diffusion.Subject areas: Neuroscience, Stem Cells, Organoids

Before you begin

Innovation

Substantial progress has been made in modeling human brain development using brain organoids derived from human-induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs). These three-dimensional in vitro models exhibit a highly organized architecture, encompassing progenitor cells, differentiated neuronal populations, and glial lineages, which collectively recapitulate key features of the human brain during early developmental stages.1,2,3,4 Despite these advances, several challenges still limit their full potential, particularly the persistent issue of necrotic core formation. Necrotic cores typically develop as organoids exceed 300–400 μm in size, exceeding the diffusion limits of oxygen and nutrients essential for maintaining cell viability. This hypoxic and nutrient-deprived environment compromises the structural integrity and reduces the functional utility of the organoids.5,6,7,8,9 This problem is compounded by the absence of vascularization, which further limits oxygen and nutrient supply.8,10 Current interventions, such as organoid slicing6 or air-liquid interface culture,11 help mitigate necrosis but have limitations. These techniques are labor-intensive, introduce variability, and do not directly address oxygen and nutrient diffusion constraints. Efforts to develop vascularized organoids, which mimic angiogenic processes and neurovascular interactions, offer promise but are still in the early stages of development.12 While these methods have successfully demonstrated the formation of neurovascular unit-like structures, their complexity and scalability challenges limit their broad application.12,13,14 Given the pressing need for reproducible and scalable methodologies, the development of an optimized protocol for generating long-term iPSC-derived cortical organoids is important. Here, we optimize and develop a simplified protocol using the Xiang et al.3 design to generate cortical organoids that can be grown beyond 3 mm without necrotic core formation. Key improvements include optimized culture density, enhanced medium exchange and washing, and dynamic agitation to facilitate efficient diffusion and waste clearance. This approach eliminates the need for vascularization, organoid slicing, or specialized bioreactors, allowing for the generation of viable, structurally intact organoids in conventional laboratory settings. This approach overcomes a major obstacle in cortical organoid generation and enhances the prospects of using these models in studies of human brain development and disease.

To ensure proper organoid isolation and maintenance, each well contains only one organoid to prevent unintended fusion that alters developmental trajectories, and the orbital shaker speed remains between 85–100 RPM, as excessive agitation disrupts neuroectoderm structure while insufficient speed impairs differentiation. Morphologically, neuroectoderms display a dense, well-compacted structure with small neuroepithelial folds forming along the periphery, and over time, organoids transition from a spherical to an asymmetrical morphology, which indicates proper neurodevelopment. Optimized pipetting techniques further protect the cultures: avoid direct pipette contact with organoids to prevent mechanical damage, gently aspirate spent medium during medium changes to minimize shear stress, and transfer neuroectoderms by holding the pipette vertically and dispensing with controlled pressure to ensure precise deposition. By following these steps and precautions, the protocol ensures robust and reproducible neuroectoderm formation, establishing a solid foundation for subsequent cortical organoid differentiation.

Expected outcomes

This study presents a streamlined and optimized protocol for generating cortical organoids (COs) from human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs), specifically designed to mitigate necrotic core formation and improve cell viability.

We validate the pluripotency of hiPSCs prior to differentiation through immunocytochemical analysis of core pluripotency markers, including NANOG, OCT4, TRA-1-60, and SSEA4 (Figures 1A–1D), and assess their morphology by identifying compact hiPSC colonies with well-defined, sharp borders (Figure 1E).15 After EB formation, early-passage hiPSCs (P14) generate spherical EBs with distinct, well-demarcated borders within 24–48 h of plating (Figure 2B). Over the next 3–4 days, these EBs progressively increase in diameter to approximately 400–500 μm, forming smooth, refractive edges indicative of successful EB formation (Figure 2C). By day 5, the EBs display well-defined outer borders, and optically distinct, radially organized neuroectodermal structures begin to emerge at the periphery. Between days 7–10, these neuroectodermal domains continue to develop, forming neuroepithelial bud outgrowths and small folds while gradually losing their original spherical symmetry—a morphological hallmark of early organoid formation (Figure 2D).1,16,17

Between days 18–25, the early-stage COs exhibit increased neuroepithelial folding, which can be readily observed using brightfield microscopy (Figure 2F). By day 40, the COs reach a diameter of approximately 3–4 mm (Figure 2G). After day 50, we observe a modest decline in their size (Figure 2H).

By day 25, the developing COs form rosette-like structures and ventricular zone (VZ)-like regions that characterize the radial organization of neural progenitor cells (NPCs). These NPCs express PAX6, a well-established marker of forebrain/cortical radial glial cells (RGCs), which play a key role in early cortical patterning and neural tube development (Figure 3A). SOX2-positive RGCs in the COs display radial organization, effectively recapitulating early neurogenesis (Figures 3A–3C). The NPCs also exhibit high proliferative capacity, as evidenced by Ki67 expression, mirroring patterns observed in the developing human brain (Figure 3B).

SOX2-positive progenitors extend beyond the VZ-like domain, and MAP2 expression appears in regions outside the rosettes and VZ-like structures (Figure 3C), indicating the formation of SVZ (subventricular zone) and cortical plate-like regions. We observe intense TUJ1 immunoreactivity beyond the progenitor zones, indicative of the emergence of early differentiated neurons (Figure 3D). Consistently, the early-born deep-layer neuronal marker CTIP2 displays prominent expression outside these regions (Figure 3E), signifying the onset of deep-layer neurogenesis. These findings demonstrate that deep-layer neuronal markers such as CTIP2 expresses during early stages of neurogenesis, thereby confirming the appropriate temporal and spatial progression of cortical neuron differentiation.

We further characterize neurogenesis by detecting NESTIN, a canonical NPC marker, within the rosette-like and VZ-like regions (Figure 3F). We detect Neurofilament Light (NF-L) primarily in intermediate zones of the COs but not in proliferative NPC populations within the rosette-like and VZ regions (Figure 3G), indicating early neuronal differentiation and migration. The expression pattern of NF-L also suggests the initiation of axonal development and organization within cortical plate-like regions.

By day 120, we detect extensive axonal outgrowth and maturation through immunocytochemical analysis of Neurofilament-Medium (NF-M) (Figure 4A) and Neurofilament-Heavy (NF-H) (Figure 4B), which suggests the presence of long-range projection neurons establishing synaptic connectivity (Figures 4A and 4B). We also identify both glutamatergic excitatory neurons (BRN2+, vGLUT1+) (Figures 4A and 4B) and GABAergic inhibitory interneurons (GAD1+) (Figure 4C), indicating the formation of functional excitatory-inhibitory circuits and advanced cortical maturation. Additionally, we confirm astrocyte differentiation by GFAP expression, which marks both radial glial progenitors and astrocytes (Figure 4C). We confirm cortical layer specification through the expression of BRN2) (Figure 4A), SATB2 (Figure 4D), and REELIN (Figure 4D). Notably, REELIN expression highlights Cajal-Retzius cells in Layer I, which play critical roles in regulating neuronal migration and cortical layering. SOX2 marks progenitor populations, whereas MAP2 marks maturing neurons within the COs (Figure 4E).

Importantly, we detect no significant necrotic core formation at either day 25 or day 120, as confirmed by histological and immunocytochemical analyses (Figures 3 and 4). This absence of necrosis suggests that our optimized culture conditions effectively maintain adequate nutrient and oxygen diffusion while facilitating efficient waste removal. These findings underscore the efficacy of our protocol in preventing necrotic core development and enhancing the viability and maturation of cortical organoids.

Overall, this study demonstrates that our optimized protocol reliably supports the generation of cortical organoids with preserved cellular architecture, sustained neurogenesis, and long-term viability. This model provides a robust and physiologically relevant platform for investigating human brain development in both healthy and disease contexts.

Limitations

While this optimized protocol aims at reducing necrotic core formation in COs derived from hiPSCs, primarily by enhancing nutrient and oxygen delivery and promoting effective metabolic waste removal—it has certain limitations that may affect reproducibility, scalability, and fidelity. First, the success of the protocol is highly dependent on the quality and undifferentiated state of the starting hiPSCs; the use of partially differentiated or unhealthy colonies can lead to poor organoid formation, morphological variability, and reduced viability. Second, proper organoid spacing is critical to maintaining healthy growth conditions. Transferring more than one embryoid per well in a 24-well plate often leads to organoid fusion, which impairs nutrient diffusion and promotes necrotic core development. Similarly, exceeding three COs per well in a 6-well plate or failing to perform consistent and thorough media changes can compromise organoid health and experimental consistency. Additionally, while this protocol has been validated using a standard CO2 orbital shaker as well as 6-well plate, its performance in other dynamic culture systems such as spinner flasks or bioreactors including plate having larger surface remains untested and may require separate optimization. To ensure reproducibility, it is essential to maintain the specific shaker speed and conditions as outlined in Box 1.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Absence of Embryoid Body (EB) Formation After 24–48 H.

Potential solution

Begin the protocol with robust, healthy hiPSC colonies characterized by compact morphology and well-defined, sharp borders. The dissociation process should be optimized to limit the handling time, ideally completing cell seeding into 96-well plates within 10–15 min for 24 wells. Utilize a cell viability analyzer to confirm cell health post-dissociation (>85%). Employ gentle pipetting techniques to preserve cellular integrity. Pluripotency of the hiPSC colonies must be validated using immunofluorescence staining for canonical markers such as NANOG and OCT4. Furthermore, use early-passage hiPSCs (ideally within passages 10–30) and verify genomic stability through karyotype analysis to ensure chromosomal integrity.

Problem 2

Lack of Neuroepithelial Bud Formation During Early Neuroectoderm Development (day 2–10).

Potential solution

Ensure the pluripotent state of hiPSCs prior to differentiation through immunostaining (e.g., NANOG, OCT4) and morphological evaluation—colonies should appear compact with distinct edges. Confirm the accuracy of medium composition and validate the activity of critical reagents. Frequent quality control of basal and supplement components is recommended.

Problem 3

Inconsistent Neuroepithelial Bud Formation Across Neuroectoderms (day 2–10).

Potential solution

Manually select and retain only those neuroectodermal structures exhibiting clear neuroepithelial bud outgrowths for continued corticogenesis. Discard non-responsive or morphologically abnormal structures to maintain downstream consistency.

Problem 4

Early Cortical Organoids Display Necrotic Cores (day 18–25, size > 2 mm).

Potential solution

Strictly adhere to the protocol by maintaining one organoid per well in a 24-well plate during early stages. Implement regular washing steps to remove cellular debris and prevent accumulation of waste. Medium should be replaced at the recommended frequency to ensure adequate nutrient supply. Orbital shaker speed should be maintained precisely as defined in BOX 1 (135 RPM, 19 mm orbit), promoting efficient nutrient and oxygen diffusion.

Problem 5

Cortical Organoids Exhibit Central Necrosis during Long-term Maintenance

Potential solution

Limit CO culture to a maximum of three organoids per well in a 6-well plate to prevent nutrient competition and facilitate efficient diffusion. Conduct regular washing and transferring procedures in alignment with the protocol. Maintain medium change frequency and shaker speed settings as detailed in BOX 1. These parameters are critical to prevent the hypoxic microenvironment that promotes necrosis in organoid cores.
