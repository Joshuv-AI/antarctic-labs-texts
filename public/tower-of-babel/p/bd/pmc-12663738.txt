Acylation modification-mediated metabolism reprogramming in cancer
Authors: Kaiwei Huang, Yunshan Wang, Bohao Li, Yuchen Xiu, Hanxiang Zhan, Guangwei Wei, Yangmiao Duan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryWith the development of high-resolution liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), various modifications resulting from the reactivity of acyl-CoA intermediates with protein residues have been identified. Acylation modifications, a diverse type of post-translational modifications (PTMs), play pivotal roles in regulating protein functions and are critically involved in tumor metabolic reprogramming. These modifications exhibit significant regulatory effects in various cancers, and their dysregulation is closely associated with malignant tumor progression. In this review, we summarize existing evidence and describe how different types of acylation modifications, including succinylation, crotonylation, lactylation, palmitoylation, and β-hydroxybutyrylation, regulate protein functions and signaling pathways, thereby influencing tumor metabolism reprogramming. Future research into the regulatory functions of acylation modifications and their roles in tumor metabolism will help elucidate the molecular mechanisms of metabolic reprogramming and provide potential targets for developing novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for cancer.Subject areas: Molecular physiology, Cancer

Introduction

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of death worldwide, with its incidence and mortality rates continuing to rise, posing a significant threat to human health.1 Despite notable advancements in surgical resection, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy, the heterogeneity and complexity of tumors continue to present major challenges in clinical treatment. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying tumorigenesis and progression, as well as identifying novel therapeutic targets, remains a central focus in current cancer research. However, the precise mechanisms driving tumor progression have not yet been fully elucidated and require further investigation.

PTMs refer to the covalent addition of chemical groups to proteins after translation and play pivotal roles in regulating protein structure, functional activation, and signal transduction across diverse biological processes.2 In cancer, PTMs modulate the activity, localization, and interactions of key proteins, thereby influencing critical pathological processes such as cell proliferation, apoptosis, and metabolic reprogramming.3 Increasing evidence shows that metabolic intermediates activated with coenzyme A (CoA), mediate a variety of acyl modifications (e.g., succinylation, crotonylation, lactylation).4,5,6 These modifications not only regulate the functions of proteins but also drive metabolic reprogramming, which is a hallmark of cancer.7,8

Metabolic reprogramming is a process by which cells adapt their metabolic pathways to meet the demands of growth, proliferation, and survival under specific physiological or pathological conditions. This process involves alterations in multiple metabolic pathways, including glycolysis, fatty acid metabolism, and glutamine metabolism.9 In cancer, metabolic reprogramming is characterized by enhanced glycolysis, increased fatty acid synthesis, and heightened glutamine metabolism, which provide energy and biosynthetic precursors for tumor cells while shaping an immunosuppressive microenvironment to facilitate tumor progression.10 As a critical link between metabolism and signaling regulation, acylation modifications play a significant role in tumorigenesis by mediating metabolic reprogramming.7,8

This review systematically explores the mechanistic roles of diverse acylation modifications, including succinylation, crotonylation, lactylation, palmitoylation, and β-hydroxybutyrylation, in tumor metabolic reprogramming. We examine how these modifications regulate protein functions and metabolic pathways in cancer cells and further discuss their implications in developing therapeutic strategies for tumor ablation.

Post-translational modifications and their biological roles

Protein post-translational modifications

PTMs are crucial mechanisms by which eukaryotic organisms regulate protein activity and function. They typically involve the covalent addition of one or more modifying groups to the protein backbone or side chains after synthesis.2 PTMs occur through two primary mechanisms: enzyme-catalyzed and non-enzymatic reactions. Enzyme-catalyzed modifications represent the predominant regulatory mode of PTMs. For example, kinases catalyze the phosphorylation of serine, threonine, or tyrosine residues to regulate signal transduction, while phosphatases reverse this process.11 Histone acetyltransferases (HATs) mediate lysine acetylation to modulate chromatin structure.12 Non-enzymatic modifications, in contrast, are often driven by the concentration of chemical substances or metabolic intermediates in the microenvironment, such as glycosylation under hyperglycemic conditions.13

To date, more than 400 distinct types of PTMs have been identified, yet it remains difficult to classify them into mutually exclusive categories. Worthwhile classification of PTMs requires the consideration of multiple dimensions, including the sites of modification (e.g., Lys, Ser, Cys), and the chemical composition of the modification (e.g., phosphorylation, acetylation, ubiquitination). This multi-dimensional perspective can more accurately reflect the complexity of PTMs compared to considering any one category of classifier alone.3

Post-translational modification-mediated regulation of protein function

PTMs can modify protein function by introducing or removing charged groups, thereby altering the surface charge distribution. For example, phosphorylation of histone H3 (H3S10ph) introduces a negative charge, promoting chromatin condensation,14 whereas histone acetylation reduces electrostatic attraction between nucleosomes, leading to chromatin relaxation and facilitating gene transcription.15 PTMs can serve as molecular barcodes, adding molecular recognition tags to proteins, serving as a binding site for other proteins to carry out specific functions. Ubiquitination, for instance, attaches a 76-amino acid ubiquitin protein (∼8.5 kDa) to a substrate, forming mono or polyubiquitin chains that mark proteins for proteasomal degradation.16 Additionally, PTMs regulate protein function by modulating hydrophilicity. For example, N-glycosylation rearranges the extracellular domain of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), reducing its hydrophobicity and stabilizing its membrane localization, thereby promoting the dimerization and activation of downstream pro-proliferative signaling.17

PTMs significantly influence various biological processes in cancer cells.3 For instance, N-glycosylation of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) generates a longer-lived glyco-PD-L1 (gPD-L1), which evades degradation by the 26S proteasome.18 This enhances the PD-1/PD-L1 signaling pathway, suppressing T cell proliferation and cytokine secretion, thereby promoting immune evasion.19 Similarly, HAT1-mediated acetylation of histone H4 regulates chromatin replication and assembly, sustaining rapid cancer cell proliferation.20 Additionally, phosphorylated extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) inhibits the activity of apoptotic proteins such as caspase-9, reducing the efficacy of apoptosis-inducing drugs against tumor cells,21 which highlights the relationship between PTMs and cancer biology, offering potential diagnostic and therapeutic targets.22

Metabolic reprogramming in cancer

Metabolic reprogramming refers to the process by which cells adjust and transform their metabolic patterns under specific physiological or pathological conditions to meet their growth, differentiation, and environmental adaptation needs (Figure 1).23 This reprogramming is primarily reflected in the altered uptake and utilization of energy and biosynthetic precursors, encompassing various metabolic pathways such as glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, fatty acid metabolism, and amino acid metabolism.24

(1) Aerobic glycolysis (Warburg effect): Even under conditions of abundant oxygen, tumor cells still preferentially metabolize glucose through glycolysis to produce lactic acid, providing energy and biosynthetic precursors for their rapid proliferation. (2) Lipid metabolism reprogramming: Tumor cells enhance lipid synthesis to meet the demands of membrane structure construction and, in times of energy shortage, provide alternative energy through lipid oxidation, promoting their survival and metastasis. (3) Abnormal glutamine metabolism: Tumor cells take up large amounts of glutamine and generate intermediate products such as α-ketoglutarate through glutaminolysis, which supports the replenishment of the tricarboxylic acid cycle, nucleotide synthesis, and antioxidant defense.

Metabolic reprogramming, as one of the hallmarks of malignant tumors, enables cancer cells to adapt to harsh microenvironments by altering energy metabolism pathways and nutrient utilization patterns. These alterations include aerobic glycolysis (the Warburg effect), fatty acid metabolism, and glutamine metabolic reprogramming.21,25,26,27 Additionally, metabolic reprogramming contributes to the creation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment, weakening antitumor immune responses and facilitating tumor immune evasion.28

Warburg effect

The Warburg effect refers to the phenomenon where cancer cells preferentially utilize aerobic glycolysis for energy production even under oxygen-sufficient conditions.29 Although this process generates less ATP compared to oxidative phosphorylation, it proceeds at a faster rate while producing substantial amounts of lactate, thereby meeting the demands of rapidly proliferating cancer cells and creating an acidic tumor microenvironment that suppresses immune cell activity.30 This metabolic reprogramming also influences epigenetic modifications and genetic alterations, generating diverse cellular phenotypes that enhance cancer cell growth and invasiveness.29

The regulation of the Warburg effect involves multiple signaling pathways. The PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway is a central regulator of cell growth and metabolism. Phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K), a lipid kinase, phosphorylates phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate to generate phosphatidylinositol 3,4,5-trisphosphate (pyruvate kinase M2 [PKM2]), a critical second messenger for protein kinase B (Akt) activation.31 Activated Akt phosphorylates and inhibits the tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC1/2), relieving its suppression on Ras homolog enriched in brain (Rheb) and thereby activating the mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1).32 Additionally, Akt can directly phosphorylate components of mTORC2, further amplifying signal transduction.33 Activated mTORC1 upregulates glycolysis-related genes (e.g., glucose transporter 1 [GLUT1], hexokinase II [HK2], lactate dehydrogenase A [LDHA]) through downstream effectors such as hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α) and c-Myc,34 while also phosphorylating and activating rate-limiting glycolytic enzymes (e.g., phosphofructokinase-1 [PFK1]) to coordinately enhance glucose uptake and lactate production.35,36 The HIF-1α pathway not only activates the transcription of glucose transporters (e.g., GLUT1, GLUT3) and glycolytic enzymes (e.g., hexokinase [HK]),37 but more critically, it induces the expression of pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1 (PDK1). PDK1 phosphorylates and inhibits pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH), preventing pyruvate entry into mitochondria for oxidative phosphorylation and redirecting it toward glycolysis.38

Fatty acid metabolism

Fatty acids serve as one of the primary energy sources for cells and are essential building blocks for biomembrane synthesis. Tissue cells typically prioritize the uptake of exogenous free fatty acids to support biosynthesis and energy production.39 In tumor cells, metabolic reprogramming of fatty acid metabolism is primarily characterized by increased fatty acid uptake and lipid droplet formation to promote tumor metastasis40; and upregulation of fatty acid synthesis-related enzymes to promote lipogenesis, converting nutrients into metabolic intermediates for membrane biosynthesis, energy storage, and signaling molecule production.41

The metabolic reprogramming of fatty acids in tumor cells is regulated by multiple signaling pathways, which are frequently aberrantly activated in cancer to further promote fatty acid synthesis. For instance, the PI3K/Akt/mTORC pathway plays a pivotal role in fatty acid metabolism regulation. Activated mTORC1 phosphorylates and activates downstream fatty acid synthesis-related enzymes (e.g., fatty acid synthase [FASN] and acetyl-CoA carboxylase [ACC]) to stimulate fatty acid production.42 Additionally, mTORC1 upregulates the expression of fatty acid transporters (e.g., cluster of differentiation 36 [CD36]), enhancing tumor cell uptake of free fatty acids.43 AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) functions as a cellular energy sensor. Under energy-deficient conditions, activated AMPK inhibits the activity of fatty acid synthesis-related enzymes (e.g., ACC), thereby reducing fatty acid synthesis to maintain cellular energy homeostasis.44 However, in tumor cells, AMPK activity is often suppressed, leading to the sustained activation of fatty acid synthesis that fuels rapid proliferation by providing energy and biosynthetic precursors.45

Glutamine metabolism

Glutamine serves as a crucial carbon and nitrogen source for cells. The intermediates derived from its metabolism not only provide carbon skeletons for the synthesis of macromolecules (e.g., nucleotides and amino acids) but also serve as substrates for glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation. Additionally, glutamine metabolism generates NADPH, which protects cells from oxidative damage. In many tumor cells, glutamine metabolism is dysregulated, manifested by increased glutamine uptake to support rapid proliferation46; enhanced conversion of glutamine to α-ketoglutarate via the regulation of glutaminase activity, thereby activating the mTORC1 signaling pathway and suppressing autophagy47; and elevated glutathione synthesis to strengthen antioxidant capacity, enabling tumor cell survival under oxidative stress.48

The regulation of glutamine metabolism involves multiple signaling pathways. The PI3K/Akt/mTORC pathway promotes glutamine metabolism by phosphorylating and activating downstream enzymes (e.g., glutaminase [GLS] and glutamate dehydrogenase [GDH]). Moreover, mTORC1 upregulates the expression of glutamine transporters (e.g., alanine-serine-cysteine transporter 2 [ASCT2]), increasing glutamine uptake in tumor cells.49,50,51 The HIF-1α pathway also plays a pivotal role in glutamine metabolism regulation. HIF-1α not only enhances the expression of glutamine metabolic enzymes (e.g., GLS and GDH) to stimulate glutamine catabolism but also upregulates glutathione synthetase expression to boost glutathione synthesis. This adaptive response strengthens cellular antioxidant defenses, promoting tumor cell survival under oxidative stress.52,53

Emerging acylation modifications and their roles in metabolic reprogramming

Emerging acylation modifications include crotonylation, succinylation, lactylation, palmitoylation, and β-hydroxybutyrylation, among others.54 Acylation generally refers to the process of covalently linking acyl groups to biomacromolecules, with an acyl-CoA as a substrate and CoA as a leaving group, which can occur via enzymatic or non-enzymatic mechanisms.

Current research suggests that the stoichiometry of acylation modifications, such as nuclear enzyme-driven acetylation, is significantly higher than that of mitochondria, which is mostly non-enzymatic. While some high-occupancy sites in the mitochondrion can reach over 1%, mitochondrial acylation exists mostly at very low stoichiometry, below 0.1%.55,56,57

This phenomenon is closely related to the chemical properties of the acyl donor. Negatively charged acyl-CoAs (e.g., succinyl-CoA, glutaryl-CoA) are intrinsically more reactive than neutral acyl-CoAs such as acetyl-CoA.58 In the mitochondrion, where mostly non-enzymatic acylation occurs, the enhanced reactivity of negatively charged acyl-CoAs can drive higher PTM stoichiometries than observed for non-enzymatic acetylation.59 By contrast, in the nucleus, lysine acetylation and other uncharged acylations are predominantly enzyme-catalyzed, enabling very high site occupancies (approaching ∼80% for certain histone acetylation sites), largely independent of the intrinsic chemical reactivity of the acyl donor.

Succinylation

The occurrence of succinylation

Protein succinylation modification typically involves the addition of a succinyl group to the ε-amino group of lysine residues and is a reversible modification.60 The succinyl group is a sterically bulky and negatively charged moiety derived from succinyl-CoA.4 Succinyl-CoA is an intermediate metabolite generated during glucose metabolism. In the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA cycle), it is produced from α-ketoglutarate via catalysis by the α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex.61 Additionally, the catabolic products of valine and isoleucine can also generate acetyl-CoA and succinyl-CoA through the action of the branched-chain α-keto acid dehydrogenase complex (BCKDH).62

Currently, succinylation is primarily considered to occur via non-enzymatic reactions, driven by locally high concentrations of succinyl-CoA.63 However, certain enzymes, such as lysine acetyltransferase 2A (KAT2A), have been reported to weakly catalyze succinylation.64 The primary erasers of succinylation are sirtuin 5 (SIRT5) and SIRT7. SIRT5 is the major desuccinylase, exhibiting significantly stronger desuccinylation activity than its deacetylation activity.65 On the other hand, SIRT7 has been identified as a histone desuccinylase, particularly within the nucleolus. It plays a pivotal role in chromatin compaction and genome stability by regulating the desuccinylation of histone H3K122.66

Protein succinylation is implicated in the progression of various cancers. For example, succinylation of GLS (glutaminase) affects glutamine metabolism in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cells and enhances their survival.67,68 Succinylation at the K222 site of LDHA promotes the migration and proliferation of gastric cancer cells.69 Additionally, KAT2A can succinylate histone H3 to regulate gene expression in cancer cells.64,70

Regulation of succinylation in metabolic reprogramming

Succinylation has been demonstrated to modulate the activity of key glycolytic enzymes, thereby promoting glycolysis as the primary energy metabolism pathway in cells.63 For instance, carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A (CPT1A), which is responsible for catalyzing the condensation of long-chain acyl-CoA with carnitine to form acylcarnitine, thereby transporting fatty acids into the mitochondrial matrix for β-oxidation,71 has also been found to be highly expressed in lymphoma and mediate the succinylation of lysine 318 (K318) of LDHA, enhancing its enzymatic activity and stability.72 LDHA catalyzes the conversion of pyruvate to lactate under anaerobic conditions, and its increased activity facilitates glycolytic flux.73 Researchers have also discovered that histone acetyltransferase 1 (HAT1), in addition to its known functions, can succinylate substrate proteins.74 Reduced HAT1 expression leads to decreased succinylation at K99 of phosphoglycerate mutase 1 (PGAM1), subsequently diminishing its enzymatic activity. PGAM1 catalyzes the conversion of 3-phosphoglycerate to 2-phosphoglycerate during aerobic glycolysis, a critical step for maintaining glycolytic flux and supporting tumorigenesis.75,76,77 Furthermore, Yang et al. found that elevated succinate levels increase the succinylation of phosphoglycerate kinase 1 (PGK1) at K191 and K192, reducing its affinity for ubiquitin binding while enhancing enzymatic activity. This modification promotes PGK1’s role in converting 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate to 3-phosphoglycerate with concomitant ATP production, thereby accelerating glycolysis.78,79 SIRT5, an NAD+ dependent protein desuccinylase, has been shown to regulate p53 activity. The level of p53 succinylation at K120 was elevated following SIRT5 knocked out.69,80 Succinylated p53 exhibits reduced activity, which (1) weakens its inhibitory effect on GLUT1 translocation and (2) diminishes its ability to activate AMPK through target genes. Consequently, the suppression of the PI3K-Akt pathway and mTOR is relieved, leading to upregulated hexokinase II (HKII) expression.81 GLUT1 facilitates rapid glucose uptake into cancer cells, while HKII sustains this uptake. Their combined effects significantly enhance glycolytic flux in tumor cells.69,82 In summary, protein succinylation generally promotes glycolytic metabolism in cells and may serve as a critical target for tumor detection (Figure 2).

In murine fatty acid metabolic pathways, Jeongsoon Park et al. discovered that approximately 60% of proteins are identified as being succinylated on at least one site.83 For example, when the K540 site of enoyl-CoA hydratase (ECHA) undergoes desuccinylation by SIRT5, its structure and function are restored to normal. This enables ECHA, a key enzyme in fatty acid β-oxidation, to participate more efficiently in the process of breaking down fatty acids into acetyl-CoA.84,85 The absence of SIRT5 also leads to the succinylation of carnitine palmitoyltransferase 2 (CPT2) at Lys424, an enzyme responsible for converting acylcarnitine back to acyl-CoA during fatty acid oxidation. This modification significantly reduces CPT2’s enzymatic activity, resulting in the accumulation of fatty acid intermediate metabolites.86,87 Uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1), a mitochondrial inner membrane protein, promotes thermogenesis in brown adipose tissue under cold conditions by dissipating the mitochondrial proton gradient, uncoupling ATP synthesis from oxidative phosphorylation.88 Succinylation of UCP1 at K56 and K151 decreases its activity and stability, impairing the dissipation of the proton motive force across the mitochondrial membrane. This reduces the driving force for fatty acid oxidation and diminishes the thermogenic capacity of brown adipose tissue.89 Desuccinylation of protein arginine methyltransferase 5 (PRMT5) at K387 reduces its binding to the carboxyl terminus of Hsc70-interacting protein (CHIP), thereby decreasing PRMT5 ubiquitination levels.90,91 Simultaneously, it enhances PRMT5’s ability to form an octamer with Mep50, a structural configuration that significantly increases PRMT5’s methyltransferase activity.92 Activated PRMT5 can symmetrically dimethylate R321 of sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1a (SREBP1a). The increase in the dimethylation modification of SREBP1a further promotes the expression of its target genes, including FASN and acyl-CoA carboxylase (ACACA), thereby promoting lipid metabolism reprogramming.91,93 Additionally, NADPH-generating enzymes such as isocitrate dehydrogenase 2 (IDH2) and glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) require desuccinylation for activation. Their succinylation may lead to decreased NADPH production, reducing the reducing equivalents necessary for fatty acid synthesis.94 In summary, protein desuccinylation may serve as a critical regulatory mechanism for cellular fatty acid metabolic reprogramming.

SIRT5-mediated desuccinylation can also influence cellular glutamine metabolism. For example, when SIRT5 removes succinyl groups from Lys320 and Lys245 near the active site of GLS, the enzymatic activity of GLS decreases. This reduction leads to diminished ammonia production during the conversion of glutamine to glutamate by GLS, thereby lowering intracellular ammonia levels and subsequently inhibiting ammonia-induced autophagy and mitophagy.95,96 Another key example is mitochondrial malic enzyme 2 (ME2), whose primary function is to catalyze the conversion of malate to pyruvate, thereby regulating the flux of the TCA cycle in response to cellular energy demands.97 During glutamine metabolism, glutamine is catabolized into α-ketoglutarate (α-KG), which then enters the TCA cycle. ME2 deficiency can obstruct TCA cycle flux, resulting in reduced glutamine metabolism.98 This observation aligns with the findings of Teng et al., who demonstrated that the SIRT5-mediated desuccinylation of ME2 at K346 activates its enzymatic activity, thereby enhancing glutamine metabolism.99

Crotonylation

The occurrence of crotonylation

Crotonylation is a special PTM that was first discovered in histones by Tan et al. in 2011. It is mainly characterized by the introduction of crotonyl groups onto lysine residues. It is highly conserved evolutionarily and typically occurs in transcriptionally active regions, playing a crucial regulatory role in chromatin remodeling and cellular metabolism.100,101

The intracellular crotonylation is also regulated by enzymatic and non-enzymatic reaction mechanisms.5,102 For instance, the reported crotonylation writer enzymes, E1A-binding protein p300/CREB-binding protein (p300/CBP), show that an increase in intracellular crotonyl-CoA levels enhances p300-mediated histone crotonylation and gene transcriptional activation.5 Acyl-CoA synthetase short-chain family member 2 (ACSS2) has been proven to activate crotonic acid to crotonyl-CoA, and the knockout of ACSS2 significantly reduces the intranuclear crotonyl-CoA levels and histone crotonylation modifications.103,104 The erasers of crotonylation, such as histone deacetylase1/2/3 (HDAC1/2/3), have been identified as the main histone decrotonylases.105 SIRT1/2/3 also possess de-crotonylase activity, and their overexpression significantly reduces the intracellular histone crotonylation levels.106 The production of crotonyl-CoA occurs through multiple pathways. One is that the extracellular short-chain fatty acid crotonic acid can be taken up by cells and then converted to crotonyl-CoA under the catalysis of ACSS2.5 The other is that during fatty acid β-oxidation, fatty acid molecules are gradually decomposed into acetyl-CoA, and during the oxidation of butyryl-CoA, crotonyl-CoA intermediate products are generated. For example, acyl-CoA dehydrogenase short chain (ACADS) and Acyl-CoA oxidase 3 (ACOX3) can catalyze the conversion of butyryl-CoA to crotonyl-CoA.107 Additionally, in the lysine metabolism process, glutaryl-CoA is oxidized by glutaryl-CoA dehydrogenase (GCDH) to produce crotonyl-CoA.108

Crotonylation also has a significant impact on various malignant tumors. For instance, Wan et al. found that increasing the overall crotonylation level of proteins inhibits the proliferation and invasion ability of liver cancer cells109; after interfering with the expression of P300 in HeLa cells, the crotonylation modification level of heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein A1 (HNRNPA1) significantly decreased, inhibiting its invasion and migration ability,110 and the effect of P300 on protein crotonylation is also manifested in prostate cancer cells,111 all of which reveal the possibility of crotonylation as a target for cancer treatment.

Regulation of crotonylation in metabolic reprogramming

The crotonylation level of proteins has a certain regulatory effect on the glycolytic pathway of cells. For example, after treatment with crotonic acid, the crotonylation level at the Ser46 site of the p53 protein in cells significantly increases. This enzyme catalyzed O-acyl modification leads to a decrease in the stability of the p53 protein, thereby significantly reducing the protein level of p53 and relieving its inhibition on the key glycolytic enzymes Phosphofructokinase Platelet Type (PFKP) and PKM2, resulting in an increase in the glycolytic activity of cells.112 Additionally, the K305 site of PKM2 can be directly modified by crotonylation. This modification promotes the transformation of PKM2 from a tetramer to a dimer and its migration to the nucleus. The dimerized PKM2 has significantly reduced pyruvate kinase activity, thereby affecting the conversion of phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) to pyruvate. This change in enzyme activity leads to the accumulation of metabolites in the glycolytic pathway, causing a reprogramming of the glycolytic pathway. As a result, cells become more dependent on the intermediate metabolites of glycolysis to support other biosynthetic pathways.113,114 Under hypoxic conditions, Guo et al. found that the crotonylation levels at K131, K156, and K220 sites of PGK1 protein were significantly reduced, but the activity of PGK1 as a key enzyme in glycolysis did not change significantly. Nevertheless, the reduction in crotonylation levels promoted the interaction between PGK1 and pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1 (PDHK1), thereby activating PDHK1. The activated PDHK1 can phosphorylate the E1α subunit of PDH, inhibiting the activity of PDH and preventing it from effectively converting pyruvate into acetyl-CoA, thus limiting the entry of acetyl-CoA into the TCA cycle. This leads to the accumulation of pyruvate in the cytoplasm, which is then converted into lactate by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). This process enhances glycolysis and makes cells more dependent on glycolysis for energy production (Figure 3).115,116,117

In fatty acid metabolism, SIRT3 is a deacetylase belonging to the Sirtuin family and is mainly located in mitochondria.118 It can de-crotonylate dihydrolipoamide dehydrogenase (DLD), a core metabolic enzyme in the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDHc) and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex.119 The de-crotonylation of DLD by SIRT3 reduces the enzyme’s activity, thereby weakening the metabolic activity of the TCA cycle. This process inhibits the browning of white adipocytes, reduces the expression of thermogenesis-related genes (such as UCP-1, PGC-1α, and so forth), and ultimately leads to a decline in the oxidative metabolic capacity of adipocytes.120 Huang et al. discovered that DgTIL1 is a temperature-induced lipid transport protein. Under cold conditions, DgTIL1 is induced to express itself and can interact with non-specific lipid transfer protein (DgnsLTP). It was found that crotonylation modification at the K72 site of the DgTIL1 protein significantly enhances its binding ability to DgnsLTP. This modification not only helps stabilize the DgnsLTP protein and prevent its degradation but also further promotes the efficiency of lipid transfer and transport.121 Additionally, the crotonylation of histone H3K9 was found to mainly accumulate near the transcription start and end sites of genes related to fatty acid oxidation and acyl-CoA metabolism. The YEATS domain of Taf14 can recognize the crotonylation modification of histone H3K9 and regulate the expression of genes related to lipid metabolism, thereby optimizing the energy utilization of cells.122,123

Lactylation

The occurrence of lactylation

Protein lactylation is an emerging PTM. In the past, lactic acid was regarded as a metabolic waste product. However, recent studies have revealed that lactic acid can also affect protein function through lactylation in cells. Specifically, either lactic acid or lactyl-CoA can serve as a lactyl donor to covalently modify lysine residues on proteins, thereby forming lactylation.6,124,125 Lactic acid is typically produced through the glycolytic pathway in cells under hypoxic conditions, during which pyruvate generated from glucose is reduced to lactic acid.126

Hypoxia is a hallmark of solid tumors. Under hypoxic conditions, HIF-1α is stabilized and translocates into the nucleus, where it activates a suite of glycolysis-related genes—including GLUT1, HK2, PKM2, and LDHA—thereby promoting the conversion of glucose to lactate.127 Recent studies further demonstrate that the high lactate levels generated during hypoxia can induce the lactylation of HIF-1α (at K644 in murine cells and K12 in human cells). Lactylated HIF-1α exhibits an increased affinity for the transcriptional co-activators p300/CBP, which in turn potentiates the transcription of its target genes.128 Therefore, the hypoxia-induced accumulation of lactate significantly increases global lactylation levels, which is associated with poor clinical outcomes in various types of cancer.129

Recent studies have found that lactylation (Kla) can competitively replace other acyl modifications on the same lysine residue.130 The relative abundance of different acyl-CoAs within the cell determines the “priority” of lysine modifications.131 When glycolysis is vigorous and lactic acid accumulates, the concentration of lactyl-CoA rises, which can directly compete with acetyl-CoA, succinyl-CoA, and β-hydroxybutyryl-CoA for the catalytic center of p300/CBP.130,131

The regulation of anaerobic glycolysis is similar to the Warburg effect, both relying on the PI3K/Akt/mTORC pathway to regulate the activity of key glycolytic rate-limiting enzymes such as LDHA and PFK1.34,35,36 The lactylation of proteins is regulated by various factors, including intracellular lactic acid concentration, the catalytic action of specific enzymes, and the overall metabolic state of the cell.132 Currently, multiple enzymes have been identified to mediate lactylation, including Tat-interactive protein 60 (TIP60), CBP, Alanyl-tRNA synthetase 1/2 (AARS1/2), p300, and KAT8.125,133,134,135,136 De-lactylation is mainly carried out by HDAC1-3. Notably, the discovery of HDAC1-3 has revealed that lactylation and acetylation compete for the same lysine site.136 Additionally, SIRT1/3 has also been shown to have de-lactylation activity.136,137 Lactylation cannot only influence the structure and function of histones, such as enhancing gene expression through the lactylation of histone H3K18,138 but also act on various non-histone proteins. For instance, among the 9,275 lactylation sites identified in liver cancer cells, 9,256 are on non-histone proteins.139

Moreover, elevated levels of lactylation are closely associated with poor prognosis in multiple cancers. For example, Yang et al. found that the lactylation level in gastric cancer tissues was significantly higher than that in adjacent tissues and was associated with poor prognosis of gastric cancer140; or by promoting the lactylation of Snail1, it can drive the migration, invasion, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition of pancreatic cancer.141

Regulation of lactylation in metabolic reprogramming

The concentration of lactic acid during glycolysis can affect the lactylation level of proteins, and the regulation of protein lactylation can, in turn, influence the glycolytic pathway. For instance, in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cells, enhanced glycolysis leads to lactate accumulation. Under conditions of lactate accumulation, P300 is recruited to chromatin and catalyzes the lactylation of lysine 18 on histone H3 (H3K18). H3K18la is enriched in the promoter regions of key mitotic checkpoint regulators (threonine and tyrosine kinase [TTK] and budding uninhibited by benzimidazoles 1 homolog beta [BUB1B]), weakening the electrostatic interaction between histone H3 and DNA, causing conformational changes in nucleosomes, exposing the transcription start site (TSS), and promoting the transcription of TTK and BUB1B.142,143 Among them, TTK can phosphorylate tyrosine 239 (Y239) of LDHA, thereby activating LDHA activity. The activated LDHA further increases lactate production; BUB1B prolongs the mitotic phase by inhibiting the APC/C-CDC20 complex (anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome bound to Cdc20), stabilizing Myc in the nucleus, and Myc directly binds to the E-box of the P300 promoter, up-regulating the transcription of P300. The increase in lactate and P300 levels further promotes histone lactylation, which in turn reactivates TTK and BUB1B, further enhancing glycolysis and forming a positive feedback loop.142,144 Jiang et al. found that in lung cancer cells, an increase in histone lactylation levels leads to a decrease in the levels of hexokinase 1 (HK1) and pyruvate kinase M (PKM) during glycolysis. This change inhibits the glycolytic process of tumor cells, thereby weakening their proliferation and migration abilities.145 The K207 site of PKM2 can also be specifically delactylation by SIRT1. K207 is located near the ATP binding site of PKM2. When this site is lactylated, it hinders the formation of PKM2 tetramers, making it more prone to form dimers. The dimer form of PKM2 has significantly reduced enzymatic activity, thereby inhibiting glycolysis.137 Serine hydroxymethyltransferase 2 (SHMT2) plays a key role in the serine-glycine metabolic process and is one of the key enzymes promoting cancer cell proliferation.146 Its lactylation level increases under hypoxic conditions, enhancing its stability and promoting the proliferation, migration, invasion, stemness, and glycolysis of esophageal cancer cells (Figure 4).147

Mitochondrial pyruvate carrier 1 (MPC1) is a key component of the mitochondrial pyruvate carrier, mainly responsible for transporting pyruvate produced during glycolysis into the mitochondrial matrix, thereby participating in further metabolic processes such as the tricarboxylic acid cycle.148 When the MPC1 gene is knocked out, the efficiency of pyruvate entering the mitochondria significantly decreases, leading to its accumulation in the cytoplasm.149,150 Since pyruvate can be further converted to lactate in the cytoplasm, the knockout of the MPC1 gene results in a significant increase in intracellular lactate levels. Gao et al. found that the knockout of MPC1 increases the lactylation level of the K673 site of FASN. This site is spatially close to the catalytically active site (His683) of the MAT subunit. This lactylation may interfere with the binding or catalytic process of FASN to its substrates, thereby inhibiting its activity. The reduction in FASN activity leads to a decrease in de novo fatty acid synthesis, reducing lipid accumulation in the liver.151,152 Additionally, Jia et al. discovered that under nutrient deprivation conditions, ULK1 (Unc-51 like autophagy activating kinase 1) is activated, which then phosphorylates the Ser196 site of LDHA, promoting lactate production. Lactate lactylates Vps34 through modifications at K356 and K781 on the catalytic subunit of PI3K. This modification enhances the binding of Vps34 (vacuolar protein sorting 34) to autophagy-related proteins such as Beclin1, Atg14L, and UVRAG (UV radiation resistance-associated gene), thereby increasing the lipid kinase activity of Vps34 in lipid autophagy.153,154

Glutamine metabolism plays a significant role in lactate production. Within cells, glutamine undergoes a series of catalytic reactions to convert into α-KG, which then enters the TCA cycle. In the TCA cycle, α-KG is further metabolized and converted into pyruvate through the action of malate dehydrogenase, ultimately generating lactate.155 However, glutamine also inhibits glycolysis by regulating the expression of glycolysis-related enzymes. For instance, in TNF-α (tumor necrosis factor-α) -induced human nucleus pulposus cells (NP cells), the expression of key glycolytic enzymes such as HK2, PFKP, and LDHA significantly increases, leading to an increase in lactate production. However, glutamine treatment can significantly reduce the expression levels of these enzymes, thereby inhibiting lactate production and reducing the level of protein lactylation.156 Additionally, Zhang et al. found that glutamine treatment could reverse the significant increase in protein lactylation induced by TNF-α.156 The accumulation of lactate may also exert a feedback regulatory effect on cellular metabolism. Studies have demonstrated that, in cervical carcinoma and cervical squamous cell carcinoma, lactate can induce the expression of the proto-oncogene c-Myc, which can bind to the promoter region of the glutamine transporter ASCT2, promoting glutamine uptake and thus promoting tumor progression.157,158,159 This process further illustrates the complex interaction between glutamine metabolism and lactate production and its importance in cellular physiology and pathology.

Palmitoylation

The occurrence of palmitoylation

Palmitoylation is an important lipid modification of proteins, which covalently attaches palmitic acid, a 16-carbon fatty acid, to cysteine residues of proteins via a thioester bond. The acyl group for protein palmitoylation comes from palmitoyl-CoA, an important intermediate in fatty acid metabolism. In the fatty acid synthesis pathway, malonyl-CoA, produced by ACC, is an important precursor for the synthesis of palmitic acid,160 which can be converted to palmitic acid through a series of reactions with acetyl-CoA under the action of the fatty acid synthase complex (FAS), and then palmitic acid combines with CoA to form palmitoyl-CoA.161

According to the specific way palmitic acid binds to proteins, palmitoylation can be classified into N-palmitoylation, O-palmitoylation, and S-palmitoylation.162 Among them, S-palmitoylation is the most common type and the only reversible modification, which is catalyzed by the zinc finger DHHC-type (ZDHHC) protein family, while the removal of palmitate esters is catalyzed by palmitoyl protein thioesterases (PPTs).163 Enzymes responsible for depalmitoylation also include acyl protein thioesterases 1 and 2 (APT1 and APT2), which remove palmitoyl groups from proteins through catalytic hydrolysis, thereby affecting the intracellular transport and membrane localization of proteins.164 Palmitoylation can occur on many soluble and membrane-anchored proteins, enhancing the hydrophobicity of the modified protein subunits and influencing protein expression, membrane localization, activity, and protein-protein interactions.165

Palmitoylation also has multiple effects on cancer; for example, palmitoylation of phosphatidylinositol 4-kinase IIα (PI4KIIα) promotes tumor growth166,167; palmitoylation of integrin β4 (ITGβ4) promotes the invasive ability of breast cancer cells168; palmitoylation of PD-L1 inhibits anti-tumor immunity.169

Regulation of palmitoylation in metabolic reprogramming

Zinc finger DHHC-type palmitoyltransferase (ZDHHC9) is a membrane-embedded protein containing a zinc finger domain and having palmitoyltransferase activity. The zinc finger domain is a common structure in proteins, which can participate in important functions such as DNA binding and protein-protein interactions. The DHHC domain is closely related to the palmitoylation modification of proteins.170 GLUT1 is a key transmembrane glucose transporter in red blood cells and the brain, mainly responsible for transporting glucose from the blood into cells to meet their energy demands.171 ZDHHC9 maintains the localization of GLUT1 on the cell membrane by palmitoylating the Cys207 site of GLUT1, thereby effectively promoting glucose uptake and subsequent glycolysis.172 Chen et al. found that ZDHHC9 can also catalyze the palmitoylation of LDHA at the Cys163 site. Palmitoylation significantly enhances the enzymatic activity of LDHA, promoting the conversion of pyruvate to lactate, thereby increasing lactate production.173 This process is crucial for maintaining the glycolytic rate of cancer cells because LDHA replenishes NAD+ in the final step of glycolysis to maintain its high activity.174 KRAS4A is one of the two proteins encoded by the oncogene KRAS, produced by the alternative splicing of the KRAS gene transcript.175 The co-localization of KRAS4A and hexokinase 1 (HK1) on the outer mitochondrial membrane is regulated by the level of palmitoylation. When KRAS4A is palmitoylated, its interaction with HK1 weakens, thereby inhibiting the consumption of glucose by cancer cells and indirectly affecting the glycolytic process of cancer cells (Figure 5).175

Palmitic acid (PA) is an important saturated fatty acid, and its synthesis and utilization are key steps in cellular lipid metabolism. AKT protein, also known as protein kinase B, is a serine/threonine kinase that plays a crucial role in cell signaling.176 ZDHHC17 and ZDHHC24 are palmitoyltransferases of AKT. Among them, ZDHHC17 mainly acts on the C77 site of AKT, while ZDHHC24 mainly acts on the C224 site. Palmitoylation modification can activate AKT, anchor it to the cell membrane, prevent it from assembling into inactive polymers, and maintain its active state. Activated AKT can promote lipid synthesis and inhibit lipid breakdown, ultimately leading to lipid accumulation in the liver.177 CD36 is a membrane-localized fatty acid translocase and a major fatty acid transporter, playing a key role in lipid homeostasis.178 Wang et al. found that the deletion of the Takeda G protein-coupled receptor (TGR5) gene can upregulate the palmitoylation of CD36 mediated by palmitoyltransferase DHHC4. Palmitoylated CD36 is more localized on the cell membrane, enabling it to more effectively uptake fatty acids and thereby exacerbating the accumulation of fatty acids in cardiomyocytes.179 Studies have found that in liver cancer cells, ZDHHC23 can mediate the palmitoylation modification of plant homeodomain finger protein 2 (PHF2), thereby increasing the ubiquitin-dependent degradation of PHF2. The reduction of PHF2 interferes with the binding of sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1c (SREBP1c) to E3 ubiquitin. SREBP1c is a major lipogenic transcription factor, and its increased stability leads to elevated levels of free fatty acids in liver cancer cells.180

Mitochondrial malate dehydrogenase (MDH2) is a key enzyme in the TCA cycle, capable of catalyzing the interconversion of malate and oxaloacetate.181 Pei et al. found that ZDHHC18 can directly interact with MDH2 and palmitoylate it at the Cys31 site. This modification significantly enhances the enzymatic activity of MDH2, promoting the oxidation of malate and ultimately increasing the flux of the TCA cycle. This enables glutamine-derived α-ketoglutarate to enter the TCA cycle more effectively, generating more oxaloacetic acid and NADH, leading to a shift in glutamine metabolism from simple glutamate production to more complex TCA cycle metabolism.182 Studies have also found that acyl-protein thioesterase 1 (APT1) is an enzyme capable of reversing S-palmitoylation and is mainly responsible for the depalmitoylation of mitochondrial proteins. For instance, APT1 may influence glutamine transport and metabolism by regulating the palmitoylation state of ASCT2 (a glutamine transporter known to localize to both the plasma membrane and mitochondria).183

β-Hydroxybutyrylation

The occurrence of β-hydroxybutyrylation

β-Hydroxybutyrylation (Kbhb) is a PTM in which β-hydroxybutyric acid (BHB) is covalently bound to the ε-amino group of lysine residues in proteins. It was not discovered until 2016. BHB is generally produced by the β-oxidation of fatty acids under conditions of fasting or calorie restriction, and it combines with free coenzyme A to form BHB-CoA, which eventually leads to β-hydroxybutyrylation modification on the lysine residues of proteins.184 When the body is in a state of fasting, insufficient glucose supply, or on a long-term high-fat and low-carbohydrate diet, acetyl-CoA produced by fatty acid oxidation is ultimately converted into BHB in the ketone body generation pathway.185 BHB is then activated by BHB-CoA synthetase to form BHB-CoA.186

3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA synthase 2 (HMGCS2) is a key enzyme in BHB synthesis, catalyzing the condensation of acetyl-CoA to form HMG-CoA187; subsequent cleavage of HMG-CoA by HMG-CoA lyase yields acetoacetate, which is then reduced to BHB.188The activity of HMGCS2 is regulated by multiple factors, among which SIRT3 enhances its activity by removing acetylation modifications on HMGCS2,189 and SIRT5 enhances its activity by removing succinylation modifications on HMGCS2.190 Additionally, when mTORC1 is inhibited, the activities of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα) and fibroblast Growth Factor 21 (FGF21) increase, promoting the expression of the HMGCS2 gene and thereby increasing BHB synthesis.191 Currently, it is known that P300 and CBP are acetyltransferases capable of catalyzing histone β-hydroxybutyrylation, while histone deacetylases HDAC1 and HDAC2 can remove β-hydroxybutyrylation modifications from histones.192

The relationship between Kbhb and cancer has received extensive attention in recent years. For instance, a ketogenic diet increases BHB levels, inducing β-hydroxybutyrylation at the Lys108 site of aldolase B (Aldob), inhibiting its enzymatic activity, and thereby suppressing the proliferation of liver cancer cells.24 BHB can inhibit the expression of indoleacetamide-N-methyltransferase by promoting its kbhb modification, thereby suppressing the stemness of prostate cancer cells.193

Regulation of β-hydroxybutyrylation in metabolic reprogramming

As mentioned above, Aldob is a key enzyme in the glycolytic pathway, mainly responsible for catalyzing the decomposition of fructose-1,6-bisphosphate into glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (GAP) and dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP). This reaction is a critical branchpoint in the glycolytic process, and the resulting GAP and DHAP can be further metabolized to provide energy and metabolic intermediates for cells.194 β-Hydroxybutyrylation alters the conformation of Aldob, reducing its binding ability to substrates and thereby inhibiting glycolysis.24 BHB can bind to PHD2 (prolyl hydroxylase domain 2) and specifically β-hydroxybutyrylate the lys239 and lys385 residues of PHD2. Under normal circumstances, PHD2 promotes the hydroxylation of HIF-1α, leading to its degradation and inability to function. However, the modification of PHD2 by BHB blocks the hydroxylation of HIF-1α by PHD2.195 The HIF-1α signaling pathway can upregulate the expression of multiple key glycolytic enzymes (such as HK2), enhancing the glycolytic process (Figure 6).196

In mice with long-term alcohol intake, acetyl-CoA accumulated in mitochondria is transferred to the ketone body generation pathway, resulting in elevated BHB levels. The increase in BHB levels promotes the Kbhb modification of SLC25A5 (mitochondrial adenine nucleotide translocator 2) at Lys147 and Lys166 sites. This modification can enhance the stability of SLC25A5 by inhibiting its ubiquitin-proteasome degradation pathway. The increased stability of SLC25A5 can significantly upregulate the expression level of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ), a core regulatory hub for adipogenesis, further promoting fat synthesis.197,198 Ding et al. found that 75% of the proteins and sites modified by Kbhb and acetylation overlap, suggesting that Kbhb modification may act through competition or synergy with acetylation modification.199 AMPKα2 is one of the catalytic subunits of AMPK, mainly involved in the regulation of energy metabolism.200 In AMPKα2 knockout mice, the two Kbhb sites of carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 (Cpt1b), a rate-limiting enzyme for mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation, were significantly downregulated. The downregulation of Kbhb modification sites of this enzyme may inhibit the entry of fatty acids into mitochondria for oxidation.199 When 2,3,7,8-tetrachlorodibenzo-p-dioxin (TCDD) treated mouse liver cells, the total protein β-hydroxybutyrylation level in the cells would significantly decrease. At the same time, the expression of key genes related to lipid metabolism, such as ATP citrate lyase and short-chain acyl-CoA synthetase, would also be significantly inhibited. Since these enzymes play important roles in lipid synthesis and metabolism, their inhibited expression may lead to lipid metabolic disorders.201

Conclusion and prospects

The development of cancer treatment strategies based on acylation modifications still faces significant challenges. Firstly, the metabolic reprogramming of tumor cells involves complex interactions and cross-regulations among various acylation modifications, such as succinylation, crotonylation, lactylation, palmitoylation, and β-hydroxybutyrylation. These modifications dynamically and precisely regulate the activity, stability, subcellular localization, and interactions of key metabolic enzymes, such as LDHA, PGAM1, PKM2, FASN, GLS, and CPT1A, thereby profoundly influencing core pathways such as glycolysis, fatty acid synthesis and oxidation, and glutamine metabolism, and shaping an immunosuppressive microenvironment.

The complexity of the connection between acylation modifications and metabolic reprogramming allows us to precisely target specific tumor-promoting acylation modifications, such as interfering with the SIRT5-mediated desuccinylation of ECHA, which can suppress fatty acid β-oxidation, thereby reversing PARP-inhibitor resistance in ovarian cancer202; or inhibiting p300-catalyzed histone H3K18 lactylation, which eliminates its competition with acetylation at the same lysine residue, attenuating tumor immune evasion and potentiating responses to immune checkpoint blockade.203

However, this complexity also makes it extremely difficult to avoid interfering with deacylation processes that have physiological functions or potential tumor-suppressing effects. For instance, SIRT5-mediated ECHA desuccinylation is essential for maintaining fatty acid β-oxidation. Similarly, competitive modifications, such as the competition between lactylation and acetylation at the same lysine site, add another layer of difficulty.

Secondly, although significant progress has been made in understanding major acylation modifications, the precise regulatory mechanisms of novel and less-studied acylation types remain unclear. For example, β-hydroxybutyrylation’s “writers” and “erasers” have not been fully identified, and its complex crosstalk and hierarchical relationships with other classical PTMs, such as phosphorylation and ubiquitination, require in-depth exploration. Additionally, the key microenvironmental factors driving non-enzymatic acylation are still poorly understood. These factors include metabolite concentration thresholds, local pH, and hypoxia levels, which can induce modifications through high concentrations of succinyl-CoA, lactate, or β-hydroxybutyryl-CoA. Their roles in tumor spatiotemporal heterogeneity represent important directions for future research.

Thirdly, the same acylation modification may exert opposite biological effects in different contexts. For example, SIRT5-mediated desuccinylation can both inhibit GLS activity and limit glutamine metabolism, while simultaneously activating ME2 to promote glutamine decomposition into the TCA cycle. Meanwhile, changes in metabolites within the TME, such as lactate, ketone bodies, and short-chain fatty acids, can dynamically regulate the levels of acylation modifications such as lactylation and β-hydroxybutyrylation. This increases the difficulty and unpredictability of targeted intervention.

But acylation modifications also show strong regulatory potential in translational processes for tumor clinical treatment. Acylation modification directly participates in the glycolysis, fatty acid synthesis, and glutamine metabolism of tumor cells by regulating the activity of multiple key metabolic enzymes, such as LDHA, PKM2, GLS, and FASN, shows significant potential for the development of therapies for tumor clinical treatment.204 For instance, targeting erasers or writers related to acylation modification can be an effective therapeutic strategy. For example, inhibiting the desuccinylase SIRT5 can increase the succinylation level, thereby inhibiting the activity of GLS and blocking glutamine metabolism205; while inhibiting the lactylation transferase p300/CBP can disrupt the positive feedback loop of TTK/BUB1B-MYC driven by histone lactylation, thereby inhibiting tumor progression.206

Regulating acylation modification can also synergistically enhance the sensitivity of existing therapies. For example, inhibiting P300, which catalyzes crotonylation, lactylation, and β-hydroxybutyrylation, can enhance the cytotoxicity of chemotherapy drugs such as gemcitabine.206 Additionally, palmitoylation at Cys272 of PD-L1 inhibits its lysosomal degradation and promotes immune escape. Targeting ZDHHC3 or APT1 can restore the membrane stability of PD-L1 and enhance the efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors.207

In addition, acylation modification is also an ideal liquid biopsy marker. For example, serum lactate levels are positively correlated with the H3K18la level in tumor tissues and can be used to predict the response of patients with PDAC to chemotherapy206; the mRNA level of PGK1 is significantly elevated in various cancer cells such as breast cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, and lung adenocarcinoma and is associated with poor prognosis.206

The subcellular compartmentalization of acyl-CoA determines whether it can participate in tumor protein acylation.208 During tumor metabolic reprogramming, although most acyl donors such as succinyl-CoA, propionyl-CoA, and crotonyl-CoA, are produced in large quantities in mitochondria, they cannot be directly used for histone or non-histone acylation modifications in the nucleus due to the physical barrier of the mitochondrial membrane.208 In addition, the relative concentration differences of different acyl-CoAs in each compartment can also lead to competition among different acylation modifications: when the concentration of propionyl-CoA, butyryl-CoA, or crotonyl-CoA in the nucleus increases, they can compete with acetyl-CoA for the same lysine site.209 Therefore, targeting the regulation of compartmentalized generation, transport, and local metabolism of acyl-CoA will provide new ideas for deciphering the cross-talk between tumor metabolism and epigenetics and developing precise treatment strategies.

The identification of protein acylation modifications mainly relies on two major technical systems: mass spectrometry and immunoassays. These two systems complement each other, but each has its own advantages and disadvantages. Mass spectrometry, especially liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), has become one of the most powerful tools in the study of protein acylation modifications.210

This technique identifies protein sequences and modification sites by measuring the mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) of peptide ions. Its advantage lies in providing precise site identification information, accurately determining the exact position of acylation modifications in the protein sequence, which many immunological methods cannot achieve.211 Additionally, mass spectrometry has the advantage of high throughput, capable of simultaneously analyzing thousands of protein sites and providing a comprehensive acylation modification proteomic map.212

However, current mass spectrometry technology still faces challenges in detecting low-abundance proteins or low-level modifications. To address this issue, the enrichment of the target modification is usually required before mass spectrometry analysis.213 Currently, the enrichment of acylation modifications mainly relies on antibody affinity and acyl-biotin exchange, but both have their drawbacks: β-hydroxybutyrylation easily cross-reacts with other acylation modifications, such as acetylation, causing false positives24; low-abundance acylation modifications such as lactoylation have poor reproducibility due to limited antibody efficiency214; and palmitoylation in the acyl-biotin exchange method requires multiple steps, and the acyl group is prone to loss.215

Immunological methods, represented by ELISA and Western blot, use antibodies to recognize specific acylated epitopes for rapid and low-cost semi-quantitative or quantitative analysis and can be completed under conventional equipment conditions for single-target validation.216 However, cross-reaction or insufficient affinity of antibodies can introduce false positives, and antibodies usually can only recognize a single acylation type, making it difficult to simultaneously analyze multiple acylation events in complex samples.216,217

Looking ahead, a deeper understanding of the dynamics, specificity, and interactions of the acylation modification network will be crucial. Utilizing spatially resolved multi-omics technologies, such as proteomics, metabolomics, and epigenomics, to map the acylation modification landscape in different tumor types and TME regions will help identify more specific therapeutic targets. Developing highly selective and bio-barrier-penetrating targeted tools is the cornerstone of translation. Exploring combined strategies of drugs targeting acylation modifications with existing therapies, such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immune checkpoint inhibitors, or other metabolic pathway inhibitors such as glycolysis, fatty acid synthesis, or glutamine metabolism inhibitors, is expected to overcome drug resistance and enhance therapeutic efficacy through synergistic effects. For example, inhibiting pro-glycolytic lactylation or succinylation may sensitize chemotherapy, and blocking PD-L1 palmitoylation can enhance immune therapy response. Additionally, regulating key acyl donors through dietary intervention, such as a ketogenic diet to modulate β-hydroxybutyrylation, or pharmacological means to indirectly influence acylation modification levels, is also a promising auxiliary treatment approach. In-depth research into the value of specific acylation modification profiles as diagnostic, classification, prognostic, and treatment response prediction biomarkers is also of great significance.

In this context, we systematically expounded the core roles and therapeutic potential of various acylation modifications in tumor metabolic reprogramming. It is expected that with a deeper understanding of the complex regulatory network of acylation modification, including its crosstalk with metabolite dynamics, other PTMs, and integration of microenvironmental signals, the understanding of cancer metabolic reprogramming can be further deepened, and a solid theoretical foundation and innovative directions can be provided for the development of new, efficient, and low-toxicity precision targeted therapeutic strategies.
