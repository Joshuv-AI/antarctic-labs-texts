Impact of High-sensitivity C-reactive protein testing on statin use in primary prevention: A national multicenter analysis
Authors: Danh Q. Nguyen, Anand Rohatgi, Amit Khera, Parag H. Joshi, Michael S. Garshick, Antonio Abbate, James A. de Lemos, Brittany N. Weber
Published: 2027

Introduction

High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) is a marker of systemic inflammation with established prognostic value for incident atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) [1]. The 2018 American Heart Association (AHA)/American College of Cardiology (ACC)/Multisociety Cholesterol Guideline identified hsCRP ≥2 mg/L as a risk-enhancing factor that may support initiation or intensification of statin therapy in adults at borderline-to-intermediate 10-year ASCVD risk by the Pooled Cohort Equations (PCE) [2]. More recent guidance continues to recognize hsCRP as a risk-enhancing factor while incorporating the newer Predicting Risk of Cardiovascular Disease EVENTs (PREVENT)-ASCVD equations [3,4]. However, the extent to which elevated hsCRP influences statin prescribing in routine clinical practice remains unclear. To address this gap, we used a large, multicenter electronic health record (EHR)-derived dataset to examine the association between elevated hsCRP and statin initiation or escalation to high-intensity statins among adults with borderline-to-intermediate ASCVD risk.

Methods

Data source

Data were obtained from TriNetX, a federated research network that aggregates de-identified EHR data from >150 healthcare organizations worldwide. The UT Southwestern Institutional Review Board approved this study.

Study population

We identified US adults aged 40–75 years across 48 health systems who underwent first-recorded outpatient hsCRP testing within the TriNetX Research Network between January 1, 2019, and October 31, 2025. The index date was the date of first hsCRP measurement. To focus on patients in whom hsCRP could plausibly inform statin decision-making, we restricted the analytic population to those with borderline-to-intermediate 10-year ASCVD risk (5% to <20%) by the PCE—the predominant ASCVD risk estimation tool in clinical practice during the study period—and excluded patients with missing PCE variables, established ASCVD, end-stage kidney disease, LDL-C ≥ 190 mg/dL, or hsCRP >10 mg/L.

To enrich for patients undergoing cardiovascular risk assessment, we required concurrent lipid testing (lipid panel, lipoprotein[a], apolipoprotein B) and excluded patients with immune-mediated inflammatory diseases (inflammatory arthritis, psoriasis, lupus, inflammatory bowel disease) or concurrent inflammatory (erythrocyte sedimentation rate, antinuclear antibody, complement C3/C4, rheumatoid factor, anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide, extractable nuclear antigen panel, anti-double-stranded DNA) or infectious (procalcitonin, blood culture) testing. Patients receiving high-intensity statins or statins of unknown intensity were also excluded. From the remaining patients (n = 12,254), we defined two analytic cohorts: Cohort 1 included patients not receiving any statin (n = 9532), and Cohort 2 included patients receiving low- or moderate-intensity statins (n = 2722).

Outcomes and measurements

The primary outcomes were initiation of any statin among Cohort 1 and escalation to high-intensity statin therapy (atorvastatin ≥40 mg daily or rosuvastatin ≥20 mg daily) among Cohort 2 within 90 days. Covariates included age, sex, race, ethnicity, body mass index (BMI), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), tobacco use, family history of stroke or ischemic heart disease, and comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension, kidney disease). Comorbidities were identified using ICD-10 codes recorded up to three years before hsCRP measurement, while BMI and LDL-C were defined as the most recent value recorded up to one year before hsCRP measurement. Medication exposure was based on EHR-recorded prescriptions.

Statistical analysis

We conducted two primary analyses. First, patients with elevated hsCRP (2–10 mg/L) were matched 1:1 to those with normal hsCRP (<2 mg/L) using nearest-neighbor propensity score matching on all covariates with a caliper of 0.1 pooled SDs. Outcome proportions were calculated within each matched hsCRP group, and risk differences (RDs) were estimated using generalized linear models with cluster-robust standard errors. Second, to evaluate dose-response relationships within the full unmatched cohorts, we modeled hsCRP continuously as restricted cubic splines using multivariable logistic models adjusted for all covariates.

In a series of supplementary analyses using the same propensity score matching approach used in primary analyses, we evaluated outcomes among patients with persistently elevated vs. normalized hsCRP on repeat testing, across BMI and LDL-C strata, at 180-day follow-up, and among patients with borderline ASCVD risk defined by the PREVENT equations, consistent with the 2026 ACC/AHA/Multisociety Dyslipidemia Guideline [5].

Missing values for BMI (n = 1869; 13.3%) and LDL-C (n = 80; 0.5%) were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE package, R). Twenty imputed datasets were created with a maximum of 5 iterations per chain. Effect estimates and standard errors were pooled across imputed datasets using Rubin’s rules.

All analyses were performed using Stata/SE 15.0 and R version 4.4.1. Two-sided P-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. ICD-10 code lists, a study flow diagram, and complete results from supplementary analyses are available in the online supplementary materials.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of 155,220 adults with hsCRP testing identified in the TriNetX Research Network, 13,928 met eligibility criteria and comprised the primary prevention risk assessment cohort. Among the 3059 matched pairs in Cohort 1, elevated hsCRP was associated with a small but statistically significant increase in statin initiation within 90 days compared with normal hsCRP (12.7%vs. 9.7%; RD 3.0%; 95% CI 1.3% to 4.7%; Fig. 1B). Among 831 matched pairs in Cohort 2, rates of escalation to high-intensity statin therapy were not different between hsCRP groups (5.4%vs. 5.1%; RD 0.3%; 95% CI −2.1% to 2.6%).

In adjusted cubic spline analyses, we observed a nonlinear relationship between hsCRP and statin initiation (P for nonlinearity <0.001), with predicted probability rising with increasing hsCRP up to approximately 7 mg/L and declining thereafter (Fig. 1C). A qualitatively similar but non-significant pattern was observed for intensification of existing statin therapy.

Supplementary analyses of statin initiation and intensification were broadly consistent with the primary findings.

Discussion

In this large, multicenter retrospective cohort of adults undergoing primary prevention risk assessment, elevated hsCRP was associated with a modest increase in statin initiation but not escalation to high-intensity statin therapy within 90 days. After matching, the absolute difference in initiation across hsCRP groups was small (3.0%), with no measurable effect on intensification of existing therapy. Associations were materially similar across supplementary analyses, including among individuals with borderline ASCVD estimated risk using the PREVENT-ASCVD equations.

These observations highlight a gap between the evidentiary framework supporting hsCRP-guided statin treatment and observed prescribing patterns. The JUPITER trial demonstrated that rosuvastatin 20 mg reduced cardiovascular events among individuals with elevated hsCRP [6], and subsequent ACC/AHA guidelines have recognized hsCRP as a risk-enhancing factor for more than a decade. While recent calls for universal hsCRP screening have been grounded in the premise that “clinicians will not treat what they do not measure [7],” our data suggest that clinicians often do not treat elevated hsCRP even when it is measured.

In adjusted regression analyses, statin initiation increased with hsCRP to approximately 7 mg/L and declined at higher values. Very high hsCRP levels may prompt evaluation for acute inflammatory or infectious processes rather than chronic vascular inflammation, shifting attention away from cardiovascular risk reduction. These findings suggest that clinicians interpret hsCRP in a more nuanced manner than a simple threshold of ≥2 mg/L.

Several limitations merit consideration: (1) residual confounding from unmeasured clinician- and patient-level factors is possible; (2) although concurrent lipid testing and exclusion of inflammatory or infectious evaluations were intended to approximate a primary prevention risk assessment context, we could not verify the clinical indication for hsCRP testing. Consequently, some degree of misclassification of testing indication cannot be excluded; (3) medication data may not capture prescriptions outside of participating health systems; (4) Exclusion of patients receiving statins of unknown intensity may have introduced selection bias; and (5) prescribing practices may vary across health systems, clinician specialties, and calendar years, and incomplete adjustment for these factors could bias observed associations.

In conclusion, among adult outpatients without ASCVD, elevated hsCRP was associated with only a small increase in statin initiation and no meaningful change in statin intensification. These findings suggest a disconnect between hsCRP measurement and downstream statin treatment decisions in routine clinical practice. Further research is needed to identify modifiable clinician- and patient-level barriers that limit translation of hsCRP measurement into treatment action.
