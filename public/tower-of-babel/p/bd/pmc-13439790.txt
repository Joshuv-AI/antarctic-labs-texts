Mechanical stretch loading of BMSCs-PLCL composite scaffolds accelerate diabetic wound healing by protecting endothelial cells and promoting angiogenesis
Authors: Zhe Liu, Congying Zhao, Haowei Zhou, Zhen Shi, Rong Huang, Lirong Xu, Junwei Su, Gaoyan Chen, Zehui Zhao, Yuqian Li, Jing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImpaired angiogenesis is a critical factor that delays diabetic wound healing. Although bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) have therapeutic potential, their paracrine functions are suppressed in hyperglycemic environments. This study aimed to construct a stem cell-scaffold construct by integrating BMSCs with poly(L-lactic-co-ε-caprolactone) (PLCL) nanofiber scaffolds and applying cyclic mechanical stretch to enhance the function of BMSCs, thereby promoting angiogenesis and tissue repair in diabetic wounds.MethodsThe efficacy of mechanically stretched BMSCs-PLCL (MS-BMSCs-PLCL) composite scaffolds was evaluated in a full-thickness skin defect model using diabetic rats. Immunofluorescence staining was used to assess the expression of CD31, α-SMA and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) to evaluate angiogenesis and vascular maturation. The in vivo fate of PKH26-labeled BMSCs was tracked post-transplantation. Additionally, CCK-8 assay, EdU staining, scratch assay, Transwell assay, and tube formation assay analyzed the effects of mechanical stretch-preconditioned BMSCs and their conditioned medium (CM) on high-glucose-injured rat umbilical vein endothelial cells (RUVECs). RNA-seq was performed to elucidate the mechanism underlying the pro-angiogenic enhancement of BMSCs under mechanical stretch, followed by functional validation via knockdown of the identified Postn gene.ResultsThe MS-BMSCs-PLCL composite scaffolds significantly enhanced the healing process of diabetic wounds. Immunofluorescence staining showed that MS-BMSCs-PLCL upregulated CD31, α-SMA, and VEGF expression, promoting angiogenesis and vascular maturation. Cell tracing confirmed the transplanted BMSCs were co-localized with the endothelial marker CD31. In vitro, both mechanically stretched BMSCs and their CM improved the proliferation, migration, and tube formation of high glucose-injured RUVECs. RNA-seq analysis identified key genes involved in angiogenesis and tissue repair that were altered by mechanical stimulation. Functional analysis confirmed that silencing Postn attenuated the pro-angiogenic function of mechanically stretched BMSCs.ConclusionMechanical stretch enhanced the paracrine function of BMSCs, thereby promoting angiogenesis in diabetic wounds and reversing endothelial cells damage from high glucose. These findings support a novel strategy that combines mechanical preconditioning, biomaterials, and stem cells for improving diabetic wound healing.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-026-05072-8.Keywords: Mechanical stretch, BMSCs, Angiogenesis, Diabetic wounds, Poly(L-lactic-co-ε-caprolactone)

Introduction

Diabetic wounds, particularly diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs), represent one of the most serious chronic complications of diabetes. Epidemiological studies have shown that approximately 19% to 34% of diabetic patients will develop DFUs over the course of their disease. Among these patients, the incidence of lower limb amputation approaches 20%, while the five-year mortality rate can escalate to between 50% and 70%, posing a significant challenge to global public health systems [1]. The current clinical management strategies for diabetic wounds encompass glycemic control, infection management, wound debridement, advanced wound dressings, hyperbaric oxygen therapy, and negative pressure wound therapy. Despite these interventions demonstrating some efficacy in enhancing wound healing outcomes, they are frequently associated with high costs, potential safety risks, and suboptimal success rates, collectively undermining the quality of life for affected patients [2].

A primary pathological factor contributing to impaired wound healing in diabetic patients is vascular endothelial dysfunction [3]. Persistent hyperglycemia induces substantial damage to vascular endothelial cells, dysregulates the expression of angiogenic growth factors, and triggers an imbalance in the immune microenvironment, all of which impair vascular regeneration and tissue repair processes at the wound site [4]. This dysfunction not only compromises the delivery of oxygen and essential nutrients but also exacerbates inflammatory responses and oxidative stress, thereby establishing a vicious cycle that further impedes healing [5]. Consequently, the development of therapeutic strategies aimed at effectively promoting angiogenesis, particularly through the restoration of endothelial cell function, becomes critical for the management of diabetic wounds.

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have emerged as a focal point of research in tissue regeneration and angiogenesis therapy, attributed to their multi-lineage differentiation potential, robust paracrine activity, and low immunogenicity [6, 7]. A growing body of evidence supports the notion that MSCs facilitate angiogenesis through a myriad of mechanisms. Specifically, MSCs possess the capacity to differentiate into endothelial cells or pericytes [8–10] and can modulate the vascular microenvironment through the secretion of extracellular vesicles, extracellular matrix (ECM) components, a diverse array of growth factors, including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), and hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) [11–13]. Among these, bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), the first type to be isolated and characterized, have been the most extensively investigated, yielding a substantial corpus of evidence from both fundamental research and clinical applications; thus, they are regarded as an ideal cell source for the treatment of diabetic wounds [14]. However, the post-transplantation efficacy of BMSCs is often compromised by the diabetic microenvironment, characterized by hyperglycemia, hypoxia, and chronic inflammation, leading to reduced paracrine activity, impaired homing capacity, and increased apoptosis [15, 16]. This presents a significant challenge for the clinical translation of stem cell therapies.

Given the hostile tissue microenvironment in diabetic wounds, which adversely affects the survival of directly injected stem cells [17], electrospun nanofiber scaffolds have garnered significant attention in recent years for wound dressing and tissue engineering applications due to their high surface area-to-volume ratio and ECM-mimicking structure (18). Poly(L-lactide-co-ε-caprolactone) (PLCL), a biodegradable copolymer approved by the US FDA, is an ideal material for constructing tissue engineering scaffolds [18]. Its mechanical properties and degradation rate can be finely tuned by adjusting the copolymer ratio [19, 20]. Studies have demonstrated that PLCL nanofiber scaffolds exhibit excellent biocompatibility with dental pulp stem cells, facilitating their adhesion, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation [21]. Furthermore, the considerable potential of PLCL-based scaffolds for wound treatment applications has been validated in multiple studies [22–24]. However, hyperglycemic conditions can still induce MSCs senescence and apoptosis. Relying solely on PLCL scaffolds is insufficient to fully enhance the therapeutic efficacy of stem cells within the diabetic microenvironment. Therefore, it is imperative to explore effective stem cell preconditioning strategies.

Existing studies on MSCs pre-treatment have predominantly concentrated on hypoxic conditions, genetic engineering, and chemical stimulation methods [25–28]. Notably, the mechanical microenvironment plays a crucial regulatory role in determining stem cell fate. The application of mechanical forces can effectively simulate physiological or pathological conditions, transmitting signals to cells that closely mimic their native environment, thereby offering distinct advantages such as being non-invasive, exhibiting high safety, and allowing for precise parameter control [29, 30]. Additionally, BMSCs exhibit inherent responsiveness to mechanical stimuli [31]. Research indicates that mechanical stimulation, as a pivotal physical signal in the cellular microenvironment, can directly regulate stem cell proliferation, migration, differentiation, and paracrine functions through molecular mechanisms such as activating the integrin-focal adhesion kinase pathway and regulating YAP/TAZ nuclear translocation [32–34]. Additionally, mechanical stretch (10% strain, 0.5 Hz) has been demonstrated to upregulate VEGF-A expression in BMSCs via the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby enhancing their angiogenic and anti-apoptotic capacities [35]; Similarly, mechanical stimulation simulating brachial artery waveforms (0.1 Hz, 7.5% strain) can significantly activate the Smad2/3 and YAP/TAZ signaling pathways in BMSCs. When combined with pharmacological inhibitors, this regimen can induce the expression of endothelial and pericyte markers in BMSCs, resulting in a synergistic enhancement of their pro-angiogenic capacity [36]. Our previous studies have demonstrated that mechanical stretch significantly enhances the secretion of key pro-angiogenic factors from BMSCs, including VEGF and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) [37]. These findings highlight the potential of mechanical preconditioning for optimizing MSC-based therapeutic strategies. However, the effects of mechanical stretch-preconditioned BMSCs-PLCL composite scaffolds on diabetic wound healing and angiogenesis, as well as underlying mechanisms remain unclear.

This study aimed to construct BMSCs-PLCL composite scaffolds subjected to mechanical stretch loading, to investigate their effects on diabetic wound healing, with a particular focus on angiogenesis, and to explore the underlying mechanisms preliminarily. The findings of this study are expected to lay the groundwork for a novel therapeutic strategy for diabetic wounds that integrates mechanical preconditioning, biomaterials, and stem cells therapy.

Methods

Isolation and culture of BMSCs

BMSCs were isolated from one-week-old Sprague-Dawley (SD) rat suckling pups. The pups were euthanized by decapitation, and the femurs and tibiae were aseptically dissected. The bone marrow cavities were then flushed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 1% penicillin-streptomycin to obtain a cell suspension. Following centrifugation, the resultant cell pellet was resuspended in complete MEMα medium (Gibco, USA) and seeded into 75 cm² culture flasks. The cells were cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. The initial medium change was performed 48 h post-seeding, with subsequent changes occurring every two days. Cells were passaged upon reaching 80–90% confluence, and third-passage cells were utilized for all subsequent experiments.

Preparation of PLCL scaffolds, identification of BMSCs, morphological analysis of BMSCs-PLCL complex were performed as described in Supplementary Material 1.

Mechanical stretch of cells

Mechanical stretch was applied to the cells using a custom-designed spherical gradient cell stretching device. Briefly, BMSCs were seeded onto PLCL scaffolds in six-well BioFlex™ culture plates (TTCF 5001 C, Flexcell, USA). Following a culture period of 48–72 h, the cells were subjected to mechanical stimulation using optimized parameters from our previous systematic study, specifically a 15% maximum strain, a 5-second dwell time at maximum deformation and a total of 1440 cycles [37]. Control groups were maintained under identical culture conditions but were not subjected to mechanical stretching. After a subsequent 12-hour rest period, the BMSCs-PLCL composite scaffolds were harvested for further applications in diabetic wound treatment.

Fluorescent labeling of BMSCs

BMSCs were labeled in vitro using PKH26 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol [38]. Briefly, under light-protected conditions, mix 1 mL buffer and 4 µL PKH26 dye in an EP tube. Centrifuge cells, resuspend in 1 mL buffer, then combine with the pre-mixed PKH26 solution. Incubate at room temperature for 5 min. Add 2 mL FBS to terminate staining for 1 min, supplement with 6 mL complete medium, and centrifuge cells. Seed cells onto PLCL scaffolds and continue culturing in an incubator. Capture images using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Establishment and treatment of the skin wound model in diabetic rats

The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee for Experimental Animal Center of the Fourth Military Medical University (No. 20240252) and conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health guidelines. Eight-week-old male SD rats, weighing between 250 g and 300 g, were utilized for the experiments. Prior to the initiation of the study, the rats were fasted for 12 h with free access to water. Diabetes was induced via a single intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ; Sigma-Aldrich, USA), dissolved in 0.1 mol/L cold citrate buffer (pH 4.5) at a dosage of 55 mg/kg. Rats exhibiting random blood glucose levels exceeding 16.7 mmol/L on three or more occasions within a three-week period, along with clinical symptoms such as polydipsia, polyphagia, and polyuria, were considered successfully modeled as diabetic rats [39]. The dorsal fur of the confirmed diabetic rats was shaved, and under anesthesia, a full-thickness circular skin wound with a diameter of 2 cm was created on each animal. The diabetic rats (n = 18) were then randomly (RAND; Microsoft Excel) allocated into three treatment groups: (1) Control group (n = 6): wounds received a perilesional injection of 200 µL PBS at four sites; (2) BMSCs-PLCL group (n = 6): wounds were covered with a PLCL scaffold seeded with BMSCs; (3) MS-BMSCs-PLCL group (n = 6): wounds were covered with a BMSCs-loaded PLCL composite scaffolds subjected to mechanical stretch. To prevent skin contraction, the wound edges and underlying subcutaneous fascia were sutured to a silicone ring fitted to the perimeter of the wound. Following treatment, the wounds were covered with vaseline gauze to prevent infection and secured with a bandage to avoid dislodgement (Fig. S3B). All animals were housed individually post-operatively. Wound areas were photographed and measured on days 0, 5, 7, 9, 11, 14, 18, and 21 post-surgery. Wound area was performed by an investigator blinded to treatment groups. Wound size was quantified using ImageJ software. Rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 3% pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg body weight) prior to all surgical procedures. Rats were euthanized with carbon dioxide (CO2) on days 7, 10, 14, and 21 for subsequent tissue collection.

Histological observations

Collected tissue samples were fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 4–6 μm thick histological sections. These sections underwent hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Masson’s trichrome staining. Morphological analysis was performed under an optical microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Immunofluorescence staining

Tissue samples from days 7, 14, and 21 were stained to evaluate angiogenesis using anti-CD31, anti-α-SMA, and anti-VEGF antibodies. After dewaxing and antigen retrieval, sections were permeabilized, blocked, and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by fluorescent secondary antibodies and DAPI.

For in vivo evaluation of BMSCs differentiation, day 10 samples were embedded in OCT, frozen, and sectioned. After fixation and blocking, sections were incubated with CD31 antibody and visualized via confocal and fluorescence microscopy. Differentiation was assessed by colocalization of PKH26 (red) and CD31 (green), with appropriate controls included for specificity.

Acquisition of conditioned medium

BMSCs were seeded on PLCL scaffolds at a density of 5 × 10⁵ cells per scaffold and cultured for 3 days. The culture medium was replaced with serum-free DMEM (Gibco, USA) and cells were either subjected to mechanical stretch or left untreated as a control. After a 48-hour incubation, the supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris.The serum-free medium was concentrated using a 3 kDa ultrafiltration centrifuge tube (Millipore Corporation, USA) at 3000 × g for 1 h at 4 °C. The concentrated medium was then sterilized by filtration through a 0.22 μm membrane filter, resulting in conditioned medium from mechanically stretched BMSCs (MS-BMSCs-CM) or untreated BMSCs (BMSCs-CM). The prepared media were stored at − 80 °C for later use.

Cell experimental grouping and treatment

Rat umbilical vein endothelial cells (RUVECs, CP-R232, Pricella, China) were cultured in complete medium (CM-R232, Pricella, China) with 5% FBS, 1% endothelial growth factor, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and passaged at 80–90% confluence. To simulate a diabetic microenvironment in vitro, RUVECs were cultured under high glucose (HG) conditions. During the logarithmic growth phase, the cells were divided into four experimental groups: (i) Control: standard medium with 5.5 mM glucose; (ii) HG: medium with 30 mM glucose; (iii) BMSCs-CM: 30 mM glucose mixed with BMSCs-CM (1:1); (iv) MS-BMSCs-CM: 30 mM glucose mixed with MS-BMSCs-CM (1:1).

Cell proliferation assay

RUVECs in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded at 2 × 10⁴ cells per well in a 24-well plate. After cell attachment, cultures were treated according to the experimental groups for 48 hours. Cell proliferation was assessed using a 5-Ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay kit (Beyotime, China), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Images were captured with a fluorescence microscope. The proliferation rate was calculated using ImageJ software with the formula: Proliferation Rate (%) = (Number of EdU-positive cells/Total number of cells) × 100.

Scratch assay

RUVECs in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells per well in 12-well plates and cultured until they reached 90–100% confluence. A linear scratch wound was subsequently created in each well using a sterile 200 µL pipette tip. Following gentle washing with PBS, the cells were treated with the respective culture media corresponding to the aforementioned experimental groups. Images of the scratch wounds were acquired using an optical microscope immediately (0 h) and after a 24-hour incubation period. The scratch wound area was measured, and the percentage of cell migration was calculated using ImageJ software.

Transwell assay

RUVECs were seeded at 4 × 10⁵ cells per well in a 6-well plate. After a 48-hour treatment period, cells were harvested via trypsinization. Subsequently, 4 × 10⁴ cells were resuspended in 100 µL serum-free medium and placed in the upper chamber of a Transwell insert (8 μm pore size), with the lower chamber filled with 500 µL complete medium. After 24 h, non-migrated cells on the upper surface were removed with a cotton swab. Migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and imaged using an optical microscope. The number of migrated cells was quantified using ImageJ software.

Tube formation assay

After a 48-hour treatment according to the experimental groups, cells were harvested via trypsinization. Pre-chilled Matrigel (30 µL per well) was evenly spread in 24-well plates, allowed to partially solidify at 4 °C, and then fully polymerized at 37 °C. Subsequently, 150,000 pretreated RUVECs suspended in 500 µL serum-free medium were seeded onto the gel. After a 6-hour incubation, images were captured from random fields of view using an inverted microscope. Tube formation was quantitatively assessed using ImageJ software by analyzing the number of nodes, junctions, and total segment length.

Co-culture experiment of BMSCs and RUVECs

A Transwell co-culture system was established to investigate the effects of BMSCs on RUVECs. RUVECs in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded at 2 × 10⁵ cells per well in the upper chamber of Transwell inserts (0.4 μm pore size) and maintained in endothelial cells culture medium with either 5.5 mM glucose (normal glucose) or 30 mM glucose (high glucose). BMSCs seeded onto PLCL scaffold were subjected to mechanical stretch and placed in the lower chamber.

The experimental groups included: (i) Control: RUVECs under normal glucose in the upper chamber, DMEM complete medium in the lower chamber; (ii) HG: RUVECs under HG in the upper chamber, DMEM complete medium in the lower chamber; (iii) BMSCs: RUVECs under HG in the upper chamber, a PLCL scaffold with BMSCs in the lower chamber; (iv) MS-BMSCs: RUVECs under HG in the upper chamber, a PLCL scaffold with mechanically stretched BMSCs in the lower chamber. After a 48-hour co-culture, the medium was replaced with CCK-8 working solution. Absorbance (optical density, OD) at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. To assess migration, a parallel experiment was conducted using Transwell inserts (8 μm pore size) during the same 48-hour co-culture. cells that migrated to the lower surface were fixed, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and imaged. The number of migrated cells was quantified using ImageJ software.

RNA sequencing analysis

Total RNA was isolated from mechanically stretched and untreated BMSCs using a Trizol® reagent kit and purified via chloroform-isopropanol precipitation. RNA integrity and purity were assessed with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA) and a NanoPhotometer. Sequencing libraries were prepared from ≥ 1 µg RNA using the NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit. Library quality was verified through quantification with a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer, size distribution analysis on the Bioanalyzer, and quantitative PCR, ensuring concentrations exceeded 2 nM. Validated libraries underwent paired-end sequencing (150 bp reads) on an Illumina platform.

Differential gene expression analysis was performed with DESeq2 (v1.16.1), using the Benjamini-Hochberg method to adjust p-values for false discovery rate. Significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined by an adjusted p-value (padj) < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 0.585. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses for DEGs were conducted with the clusterProfiler R package (v3.14.3). The bubble heatmap were generated using the R package “ggtree“(v3.6.2). A protein-protein interaction (PPI) network for DEGs was visualized from the STRING database (v12.0). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was also performed using clusterProfiler to evaluate predefined gene sets.

Adenovirus silencing and cell grouping

Adenoviral vectors expressing a short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting rat Postn (Ad-shPostn) and a negative control vector (Ad-shNC) were constructed and packaged by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), and subsequent experiments were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Transduction was performed at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10. After 48 h, Postn knockdown efficiency was confirmed by qRT-PCR. For functional assays, BMSCs were divided into three groups: a static negative control (Ctrl‑siNC), a mechanical‑stretch negative control (MS‑siNC), and a mechanical‑stretch Postn‑silenced group (MS‑siPostn). Subsequently, the MS‑siNC and MS‑siPostn groups were subjected to mechanical stretch. CM was collected from all groups and applied to endothelial cells cultured under high‑glucose conditions to evaluate endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis ability.

ELISA assay

Conditioned medium was collected from the treated cells. The concentrations of VEGF and bFGF in the supernatants were quantified using ELISA kits (Abcam, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol.

RT-qPCR analysis

Following mechanical stretch of the cells, total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (DP451, Tiangen, China) and reverse transcribed into cDNA with the M5 Super qPCR RT Kit (MF166-Plus-01, Mei5 Biotechnology, China). Quantitative PCR was carried out in 20 µL reactions containing Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q712-02, Vazyme, China), gene-specific primers, and cDNA template. Amplification was performed on a Bio-Rad CFX Real-Time PCR System under the following conditions: 95 °C for 3 min; 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 30 s. Gene expression levels were normalized to GAPDH and analyzed via the 2 − ΔΔCT method. Primer sequences are provided in Table S1.

Western blot analysis

Protein was extracted from cells using RIPA lysis buffer. After determining the concentration with a BCA assay, equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Postn and GAPDH. Following incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, protein bands were visualized using an ECL substrate. Band intensity was quantified with ImageJ software and normalized to GAPDH.

Statistical analysis

All experiments included a minimum of three independent biological replicates. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.2) and presented as mean ± standard deviation Normality was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test. For comparisons between two independent groups, an unpaired Student’s t-test was used. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference post hoc test was applied for comparisons across three or more groups. Two-way ANOVA was utilized for experiments involving two independent variables. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

MS-BMSCs-PLCL composite scaffolds accelerate diabetic wound healing

To evaluate the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of the MS-BMSCs-PLCL composite scaffolds, we established a full-thickness skin defect model in diabetic rats. The experimental design is shown in Fig. 1A. During wound treatment, we continuously monitored blood glucose levels to ensure they remained consistently above 16.7 mmol/L, maintaining a stable hyperglycemic state (Fig. 1B). Prior to the in vivo experiments, we validated the multidirectional differentiation potential and surface marker expression of BMSCs through a series of in vitro experiments (Fig. S1). Furthermore, we also confirmed that the PLCL scaffold exhibits excellent biocompatibility, effectively supporting the adhesion of BMSCs and extracellular matrix deposition (Fig. S2).

Macroscopic photographs of the wounds healing (Fig. 1C), overlay images of wound healing (Fig. 1D), and quantitative analysis of the wound healing rates (Fig. 1E) showed that at 14 days post-operation, the wound healing rate in the MS-BMSCs-PLCL group was 89.70 ± 2.25%, significantly higher than the Control (60.90 ± 4.69%) and BMSCs-PLCL group (79.78 ± 3.19%). By day 21, the MS-BMSCs-PLCL group exhibited nearly complete closure, while the BMSCs-PLCL group reached 95.37 ± 1.14%, and the Control group had a healing rate of 86.52 ± 1.89%. These results demonstrate that transplantation of MS-BMSCs-PLCL composite scaffolds significantly accelerates the healing process of diabetic wounds.

To evaluate the quality of wound healing, histological analysis was conducted on postoperative tissue specimens. H&E staining revealed that the MS-BMSCs-PLCL group had the narrowest wound width, consistent with healing rates. In the Control group, the epidermal layer was not fully closed, showing abnormal thickening and irregular junctions. The BMSCs-PLCL group exhibited reduced epidermal clefts and thickness, while the MS-BMSCs-PLCL group achieved complete re-epithelialization, though the neo-epithelial layer remained thicker than normal skin (Fig. 1F, G and H). Masson’s trichrome staining indicated sparse and disorganized collagen deposition in the Control group. The BMSCs-PLCL group showed increased collagen, but some disorganization. In contrast, the MS-BMSCs-PLCL group displayed abundant, well-organized collagen resembling mature dermal tissue (Fig. 1F, I).

MS-BMSCs-PLCL composite scaffolds promote angiogenesis in diabetic wounds

To assess the pro-angiogenic effects of MS-BMSCs-PLCL scaffold, immunofluorescence staining for CD31 (nascent vessels), α-SMA (mature vessels), and VEGF was performed. CD31 and α-SMA staining (Fig. 2A, D and E) showed the Control group had the lowest vessel density at days 7 and 14, with increased vessel numbers by day 21, indicating suppressed angiogenesis under diabetic conditions. In contrast, both BMSCs-PLCL and MS-BMSCs-PLCL groups significantly enhanced the density of nascent and mature vessels, with the MS-BMSCs-PLCL group showing the most pronounced effects. Notably, a reduction in vessel density by day 21 in the MS-BMSCs-PLCL group suggests a transition to the remodeling phase. VEGF staining at day 14 revealed significantly higher intensity in the MS-BMSCs-PLCL group compared to the Control and BMSCs-PLCL groups (Fig. 2F, G). These findings indicate that mechanical stretch-preconditioned MS-BMSCs-PLCL composite scaffolds promote neovascularization and vascular maturation, facilitating diabetic wound healing.

Transplanted BMSCs possess the potential to differentiate into endothelial-like cells within the diabetic wounds

To trace the in vivo fate of exogenous BMSCs during diabetic wound healing, cells were fluorescently labeled with PKH26, seeded onto PLCL scaffolds, and cultured prior to transplantation. Immunofluorescence staining at day 10 post-transplantation revealed that a subset of PKH26-labeled BMSCs had migrated to the deep tissue layers of the wound (Fig. 2B). Further analysis demonstrated colocalization of PKH26 (red) and CD31 (green) fluorescence signals (Fig. 2C), indicating that some of the transplanted BMSCs may have acquired an endothelial-like phenotype in vivo.

Mechanical stretch-preconditioned BMSCs enhance proliferation, migration and angiogenesis capacity of RUVECs

To investigate the effects of mechanical stretch-preconditioned BMSCs on endothelial cells function under diabetic conditions, we established an in vitro hyperglycemic model by culturing RUVECs in medium containing 30 mM D-glucose. EdU assay indicated that HG impaired endothelial cells proliferation; however, both BMSCs-CM and MS-BMSCs-CM significantly enhanced it, with MS-BMSCs-CM showing the most pronounced effect (Fig. 3A, B). In the scratch assay, RUVECs treated with BMSCs-CM and MS-BMSCs-CM exhibited higher migration rates than the HG group after 24 h, with the MS-BMSCs-CM group surpassing the BMSCs-CM group and showing no difference from the control group (Fig. 3C, D). A similar trend was observed in the Transwell migration assay (Fig. 3E, F). Furthermore, the tube formation assay demonstrated that the tube-forming capacity of endothelial cells was severely impaired in the HG group compared to the control. Both BMSCs-CM and MS-BMSCs-CM enhanced tubule formation, with the MS-BMSCs-CM group exhibiting superior node counts, junction counts, and total segment length compared to both the HG and BMSCs-CM groups (Fig. 3G–J).

Subsequently, we established a co-culture system of BMSCs and RUVECs (Fig. 4A). Potential mechanisms of MS-BMSCs-PLCL composite scaffolds in promoting angiogenesis of diabetic wounds

The results demonstrate that MS-BMSCs-PLCL composite scaffolds significantly accelerate diabetic wound healing by promoting neovascularization and vessel maturation while effectively reversing high-glucose-induced endothelial dysfunction in vitro. To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the enhanced pro-angiogenic effects of mechanical stretch on BMSCs, we performed RNA sequencing to analyze mRNA expression changes 24 h post-stretching compared to untreated controls.

A volcano plot showed differential gene expression analysis results, identifying a total of 18,278 genes, with 109 significantly upregulated and 63 significantly downregulated (Fig. 5A). The expression patterns of these DEGs were visualized in a heatmap, highlighting distinctions between the control and stretched groups (Fig. 5B). GO enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment in biological processes related to “response to mechanical stimulus”, “collagen-containing extracellular matrix”, “extracellular matrix”, “vasculogenesis”, and “endothelial cell chemotaxis”, emphasizing their role in extracellular matrix remodeling and vascular responses (Fig. 5C). KEGG pathway analysis indicated significant enrichment in pathways including purine metabolism, calcium signaling pathway, nucleotide metabolism, and vascular smooth muscle contraction (Fig. 5D). Differential gene network analysis identified four ‘hub’ genes: the upregulated matrix gla protein (Mgp), periostin (Postn), and cellular communication network factor 3 (Ccn3), along with the downregulated transforming growth factor beta receptor 3 (Tgfbr3), proposed as core regulators of BMSC-mediated angiogenesis (Fig. 5E). Bubble heatmaps illustrated the expression patterns of these pivotal genes (Fig. 5F). A PPI network constructed from the DEGs revealed close interactions among Mgp, Postn, Ccn3, and Tgfbr3 (Fig. 5G). GSEA further confirmed the upregulation of the calcium signaling and vascular smooth muscle contraction pathways, consistent with the KEGG analysis (Fig. 5H). Subsequently, we examined the altered expression levels of Mgp, Postn, Ccn3, and Tgfbr3 using RT-qPCR. The results were consistent with our RNA-seq data, reinforcing the reliability of our sequencing analysis (Fig. 5I).

These findings elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which mechanical stretch-preconditioned BMSCs promote ECM remodeling and angiogenesis, mediated by the regulation of pivotal genes and associated signaling pathways, including calcium signaling and vascular smooth muscle contraction.

Postn knockdown in BMSCs attenuates the pro-angiogenic effects induced by mechanical stretch

Among the identified hub genes, we prioritized Postn for in-depth functional investigation based on its well-established mechanosensitivity and its direct, multifaceted role in orchestrating tissue remodeling and angiogenesis [40, 41]. We successfully knocked down Postn in BMSCs using shRNA delivered by an adenoviral vector. Knockdown efficiency was verified by RT-qPCR (Fig. 6A). Knockdown of Postn resulted in lower protein expression after mechanical stretch compared to the MS-NC group, and was lower than that in the Control-NC group (Fig. 6B, C). Importantly, the Postn knockdown led to a significant decrease in the secretion of the pro-angiogenic factors VEGF and bFGF from BMSCs using ELISA (Fig. 6D). We then collected conditioned medium and applied it to endothelial cells. Functional assays demonstrated that, compared to the MS-NC group, MS-siPostn attenuated the pro-angiogenic effects, as shown by reduced cell proliferation, migration, and tube formation. However, these capacities in the MS-siPostn group remained higher than those in the Control-NC group, indicating that Postn silencing partially attenuated the protective benefits conferred by mechanical stimulation. Discussion

Wound healing progresses through four overlapping phases: hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling. Disruption at any stage can lead to delayed healing or chronic wounds, particularly in diabetic patients [42]. Normal healing relies on angiogenesis to supply oxygen and nutrients essential for tissue repair. However, impaired endothelial function, dysregulated growth factor expression, and persistent inflammation in diabetics contribute to inadequate angiogenesis, significantly hindering the healing process [43]. In this study, we fabricated a cell-scaffold composite by seeding BMSCs onto PLCL scaffolds, which were then subjected to mechanical stretch preconditioning to optimize BMSCs paracrine functions. This preconditioned composite was applied to diabetic wounds to enhance the pro-angiogenic capacity of BMSCs, facilitating the healing process.

Although MSCs have significant potential for promoting wound angiogenesis, traditional delivery methods face limitations. Systemic intravenous injection disperses MSCs broadly; however, entrapment in the pulmonary circulation restricts the number that reaches distal wound sites [44]. Local injection of MSCs, on the other hand, leads to short survival at the wound site, limiting their sustained therapeutic efficacy [45]. To overcome the drawbacks of systemic or local injections, various biomaterial-based delivery systems have been developed in recent years, such as hydrogels, fibrous scaffolds, and acellular dermal matrices [46]. Recent studies have shown that PLCL exhibits excellent biocompatibility with various cell types, including fibroblasts [47], cardiomyocytes [48], and dental pulp stem cells [21]. Therefore, we employed a topical delivery strategy using BMSCs loaded onto PLCL nanofiber scaffolds. Light microscopy and scanning electron microscopy confirmed robust adhesion, spreading, and survival of BMSCs on the PLCL scaffolds, suggesting it provides a favorable microenvironment for the growth of BMSCs. However, the hyperglycemic and hypoxic microenvironment of diabetic wound compromises the survival and functional activity of transplanted cells [45].

Currently, various pretreatment methods have been employed to enhance the resistance of transplanted stem cells to the stress of the host environment. Among these, mechanical stretch, as a form of physical stimulation, offers distinct advantages by mimicking the intrinsic biomechanical microenvironment in vivo. In terms of safety, it avoids the potential toxicity associated with pharmacological agents and the ethical as well as safety concerns linked to genetic modification [28]. Regarding controllability, parameters of mechanical stretch, such as amplitude, frequency, and duration, can be precisely controlled and are straightforward to implement. Furthermore, it is noteworthy that MSCs possess a unique “mechanical memory,” enabling them to retain response patterns to prior mechanical stimuli. This memory effect may durably influence their subsequent functionality, offering a promising target for cell-based therapeutic strategies [49].

Our study demonstrated that MS-BMSCs-PLCL composite scaffolds significantly accelerated wound healing in a diabetic rat model by enhancing re-epithelialization, collagen deposition, angiogenesis, and vascular maturation. This effect is likely due to the synergistic actions of mechanical tension and the PLCL scaffold, enhancing paracrine functions and improving cell delivery. VEGF, a key pro-angiogenic factor, stimulates vascular endothelial cell proliferation and migration [50]. However, in diabetic wounds, VEGF and other pro-angiogenic proteins are downregulated, leading to impaired angiogenesis [51]. BMSCs promote neovascularization by activating endothelial cells proliferation and migration through the paracrine secretion of growth factors such as VEGF and b-FGF [52, 53]. Our findings revealed significant upregulation of VEGF expression in the MS-BMSCs-PLCL group compared to control groups. During early wound healing, the density of both nascent and mature microvessels was significantly greater in the MS-BMSCs-PLCL group, indicating that mechanical stretch enhances BMSCs’ capacity to promote angiogenesis and vascular maturation. By day 21, we noted a marked reduction in vessel density, which prevented excessive angiogenesis and suggested the transition into the remodeling. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that mechanical stretch pretreatment significantly accelerates diabetic wound healing by augmenting the proangiogenic capacity of BMSCs.

Research indicates that differentiation of BMSCs into endothelial cells may represent a potential pathway for angiogenesis [10]. In our experiments, PKH26-labeled BMSCs were found to co-localize with the endothelial marker CD31, exhibiting characteristics suggestive of endothelial differentiation. Although evidence for adult stem cell differentiation into endothelial cells in vivo is limited, our findings support the possibility that BMSCs contribute to angiogenesis, at least in part, through a differentiation mechanism within the diabetic wound microenvironment, which is consistent with the previously reported endothelial differentiation potential of BMSCs [44, 54].

The proliferation and migration of endothelial cells are essential for angiogenesis [55]. Chronic exposure to hyperglycemia leads to endothelial dysfunction in diabetic patients [56]. Thus, restoring endothelial cells capabilities under high-glucose conditions is vital for promoting vascularization. Our in vitro hyperglycemic model demonstrated that treatment with MS-BMSCs-CM significantly enhanced RUVECs proliferation, migration, and tubule formation compared to other groups. Similar conclusions were drawn from co-culture experiments.

Finally, RNA-seq analysis further elucidated the impact of mechanical stimulation on the gene expression profile of BMSCs, underscoring the pivotal role of mechanical signals in promoting wound vascularization through the regulation of BMSC paracrine functions. A subsequent network analysis identified several “hub” genes, including significantly upregulated Mgp, Postn, and Ccn3, as well as significantly downregulated Tgfbr3, which may promote angiogenesis by modulating extracellular matrix composition, particularly the expression of proangiogenic factors, and by altering cellular responses to mechanical forces. Previous research indicates that Mgp influences vascular endothelial cells migration, proliferation, and tubulogenesis by modulating bone morphogenetic protein and Notch signaling pathways [57]. In diabetic wounds, miR-181a-5p may influence the functions of human dermal fibroblasts and their mediated processes of ECM remodeling and angiogenesis by targeting and suppressing the expression of Postn [58]. Ccn3 belongs to the CCN family of cysteine-rich secreted matrix proteins. Studies indicate that Ccn3 promotes endothelial cells adhesion, migration, and survival, through its binding to integrins αvβ3, α5β1, and α6β1 [59]. For instance, in systemic sclerosis, reduced Ccn3 levels correlate with impaired angiogenesis [60]. Conversely, Tgfbr3, an accessory receptor in the TGF-β signaling pathway, functions as a negative regulator of angiogenesis. Studies indicate that exosomes derived from E2F1-deficient adipose stem cells promote collagen deposition and angiogenesis by upregulating miR-130b-5p, which targets and inhibits Tgfbr3, thereby increasing the wound healing rate to 72.5% compared to 30.5% in the control group [61]. Collectively, the upregulation of Mgp, Postn, and Ccn3 alongside the downregulation of Tgfbr3 may synergistically enhance the pro-angiogenic capacity of BMSCs.

Our functional validation confirmed the critical role of Postn. Postn expression is regulated by matrix topology and mechanical forces, aligning with our focus on the mechanical microenvironment [40]. Furthermore, as a secreted matricellular protein, Postn directly promotes angiogenesis by binding to integrin αvβ3, which activates the TGF-β1/SMAD signaling pathway and enhances VEGF secretion; its knockout has been shown to suppress synovial angiogenesis [41]. Postn is also known to engage such as PI3K/Akt, Wnt, NF-κB, and MAPK, thereby playing a role in various pathological processes [62]. In our research, silencing Postn in the mechanically stretched BMSCs reduced the secretion of VEGF and bFGF and weakened the ability of the conditioned medium to promote endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and tube formation. Importantly, however, its pro-angiogenic capacity remained higher than that of the non-stretched control group. This indicates that while Postn is an essential mediator, the pro-angiogenic effect of mechanical preconditioning likely involves additional factors. These findings collectively demonstrate that Postn is indispensable for the enhanced pro-angiogenic effects induced by mechanical stretch pretreatment of BMSCs.

KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that pathways such as “Calcium signaling pathway,” “Vascular smooth muscle contraction,” and various Metabolism-related pathways were significantly enriched in mechanically stretched BMSCs. This finding aligns with previous reports. For instance, studies demonstrate that cyclic mechanical stretch activates the Piezo1-YAP axis in BMSCs, which regulates mitochondrial dynamics and energy metabolism (e.g., promoting glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation), thereby integrating mechanical and metabolic signals to enhance smooth muscle differentiation and pro-angiogenic capacity in vivo [63]. Moreover, mechanical stretch pretreatment in ADSCs activates the Piezo1-ATP pathway, inducing pronounced metabolic reprogramming and activating mitophagy, which enhances oxidative stress resistance and significantly improves cell survival in wound microenvironments [64]. Together, these studies reveal a novel mechanism by which mechanical signals directly regulate stem cell energy metabolism, providing important insights into the role of mechanobiology in modulating stem cell function.

In terms of clinical translation, this study still has several limitations. First, scaffold scalability remains a critical hurdle: although electrospun nanofiber scaffolds effectively mimic the extracellular matrix to support cell adhesion and differentiation, their large-scale, reproducible manufacturing processes are not yet fully established. Furthermore, standardized production protocols and a robust quality control system are still lacking [65, 66]. Second, in our study, the in vitro cell stretching device was operated using a rigorously optimized set of stimulation parameters, which ensured high reproducibility of mechanical conditions throughout the experiment. However, it does not fully replicate the complex mechanical microenvironment of the human body, where tissues are typically subjected to multi-modal mechanical stimuli, such as tension, compression, and shear, simultaneously. Finally, immune rejection remains an unavoidable and critical biological hurdle, as the host may mount an immune response against the implanted stem cells, affecting their long-term survival [67]. This poses significant challenges to the clinical application of stem cell therapy, including issues related to cellular heterogeneity, long-term efficacy stability, and safety assessment. Future research directions should focus on innovating scaffold design and manufacturing (such as 3D-printed scaffolds); constructing dynamic mechanical models that more closely mimic physiological conditions; exploring alternative strategies such as stem cell-derived extracellular vesicles; and conducting strong pre-clinical validation.

Conclusions

In this study, we engineered a novel cell-scaffold composite by incorporating mechanically stretched pretreated BMSCs onto PLCL scaffold. When deployed in diabetic wound animal models, this composite demonstrated a significant enhancement in angiogenesis. This pro-angiogenic effect is correlated with the mechanical stretch pretreatment, which enhances the secretion of pro-angiogenic factors from BMSCs and effectively preserves endothelial cells function under hyperglycemic conditions. These findings offer a robust rationale for the development of innovative therapeutic strategies aimed at addressing the challenge of impaired angiogenesis in diabetic wounds, thereby advancing the field of regenerative medicine.
