Strengthening partnerships, accountability, resource mobilisation for better health outcomes in Africa: the example of the WHO Africa Regional Office Transformation Agenda
Authors: Andrea Luciani, Pamela Drameh-Avognon, Matshidiso Moeti, Younhong Min, Rosalia Framil Carpeño, Ayah Ahmed, Olushayo Oluseun Olu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe WHO Africa Region Transformation Agenda (2015–2024) significantly enhanced health outcomes across Africa by strengthening strategic partnerships. By decentralising external relations, engaging non-state actors and hosting the Africa Health Forum, the Agenda boosted WHO Regional Office for Africa’s capacity to mobilise resources and support Member States.The Agenda’s success is evident in increased funding, improved stakeholder engagement and enabling WHO to better contribute to building more resilient health systems in Africa, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic. By aligning initiatives with national health priorities and focusing on sustainability, the Transformation Agenda has laid a strong foundation for achieving Universal Health Coverage and the Sustainable Development Goals in Africa.

Introduction

The Transformation Agenda (TA), a flagship initiative of Dr Matshidiso Moeti, the WHO Regional Director for Africa aimed to ensure that WHO is more responsive and effectively supports African Member States, to accomplish universal health coverage (UHC) and the Sustainable Development Goals.1

A key pillar of the TA was to strengthen strategic partnerships and resource mobilisation for better health outcomes, thus the WHO Regional Office for Africa (AFRO) deliberately made financial and human resource investments in this area. These included strengthening country offices and decentralising external relations (EXR) and resource mobilisation activities, boosting engagement with non-state actors (NSAs) and creating and hosting the Africa Health Forum among others.1

The TA success was evident in increasing funding for health at country and regional level, improving engagement with partners, strengthening WHO financial solidity and increasing the capacity of WHO to strengthen health security and respond to health emergencies, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic.2 Particularly, regional and country office teams were repositioned to better respond to and deliver impactful results on health for the Member States of the WHO African Region.

Through the TA, WHO was able to enhance its capacity to establish strategic partnerships, build wide alliances and extend its outreach to better support health development across the continent.

Strengthening WHO Country Offices in EXRs and partnerships

To strengthen WHO/AFRO’s EXRs functions, an initial mapping exercise was conducted. This mapping revealed that WHO’s EXR resources were heavily concentrated at headquarters, with 67% of specialists working in this area based in Geneva and only 16% in Country Offices3. This demonstrated the need to strengthen WHO Country Offices, the WHO/AFRO deployed EXRs and partnership staff to all WHO Country Offices across the Region.4 This decentralisation significantly enhanced WHO/AFRO’s capacity to engage with stakeholders at country level, increasing transparency and responsiveness of the organisation. The joint effort of EXRs and partnerships specialists on the ground and the coordination unit in WHO/AFRO facilitated the growth and diversification of funding streams, including from new philanthropic, international financial institutions and private sector sources.2

Shifting from resource mobilisation to partnerships

Historically, resource mobilisation was seen as a unidirectional process in which WHO/AFRO ‘donors’ provided financial resources and WHO oversaw the implementation. Through the TA, this was transformed into bidirectional relationships where both WHO/AFRO and its ‘partners’ have an active role in cocreating and contributing to improved health outcomes in the region according to their comparative advantage.3 This approach allowed WHO/AFRO to increase funding from both its legacy and new partners. The organisation developed comprehensive engagement plans at regional and country levels to ensure critical relationships with existing and new partners are developed and maintained. Leveraging digital tools such as the Contributor Engagement Management system was critical in obtaining analytics and business intelligence on financing sources, which enabled the organisation to leverage various funding sources and diversify its funding base. All these investments combined resulted in an 84% increase in resources mobilised from $387 millions in the 2014–2015 biennium to $714 millions in 2022–2023 (figure 1). This increase in funding has been crucial in strengthening technical assistance to Member States in critical areas such as immunisation, neglected tropical diseases, non-communicable diseases, health emergencies, health systems strengthening, sexual and reproductive health, maternal and child health, nutrition among others.

Engaging NSAs

The Framework of Engagement with Non-State Actors (FENSA),5 adopted by the World Health Assembly in 2016, was also a key enabler of strengthened partnerships with NSA. FENSA’s goal is to anchor and streamline WHO’s relationships with NSAs, including non-governmental organisations (NGOs), foundations and private sector partners. These actors are uniquely positioned to financially and technically complement WHO/AFRO’s actions financially and technically particularly at the community level. Since 2020, WHO/AFRO significantly improved its engagement with NSAs, with a 193% increase in engagement with NSAs4 (figure 2).

The African Health Forum: a catalyst for new partnerships

The convening of the first Africa Health Forum (AHF) in 2017 in Kigali, Rwanda, marked a significant milestone in WHO/AFRO’s partnership strategy. The forum, themed Putting People First: The Road to Universal Health Coverage in Africa, brought together over 800 participants, including representatives from governments, the private sector, civil society and youth organisations.6 The discussions culminated in the Kigali Call to Action, emphasising the need for renewed commitment to achieving UHC through strategic partnerships.

The success of the first forum led to the second Africa Health Forum in 2019, held in Cape Verde under the theme Achieving UHC and Health Security in Africa.7 This event further solidified WHO/AFRO’s role as a key convener of partnerships, bringing together leaders and policymakers from across the continent to discuss practical solutions for Africa’s health challenges.

COVID-19: a test of partnership resilience

The COVID-19 pandemic presented an unprecedented challenge to global health, highlighting the critical role of partnerships in crisis response.8 In this context, WHO/AFRO played a pivotal role working closely with national and regional partners to enhance contact tracing, increase oxygen supplies and provide comprehensive case management training to medical staff, just to mention a few.8 The TA’s emphasis on strengthening partnerships proved invaluable during the pandemic. WHO/AFRO’s collaboration with Member States and other stakeholders facilitated the rapid mobilisation of resources and the implementation of effective response strategies. WHO has shown leadership in sharing information and in colaunching the Access to COVID-19 Tools (ACT) Accelerator, a global collaboration to accelerate development and equitable access to diagnostic tests, treatments and vaccines.8 WHO advocated for global financial solidarity by establishing the COVID-19 Solidarity Response Fund in April 2020 and the external independent WHO Foundation in May 2020. These efforts not only helped mitigate the impact of COVID-19 but also laid the groundwork for a more resilient health system in Africa, capable of responding to future crises.

Ensuring sustainability of change

The TA also emphasised the importance of monitoring and evaluation and capacity development at country level as a tool for ensuring sustainability. In this regard, Key Performance Indicators to track the progress of the work on EXRs and partnerships were developed, allowing for continuous improvement and adaptation. The use of interactive live data dashboard to monitor reporting in real-time further enhanced compliance, accountability and transparency, which are critical to the sustainability of partnerships. This resulted in a reduction of overdue technical and financial reports to partners to only 3% of the total due in 2024,8 compared with over 12% in 2020.

Additionally, a Masterclass programme in EXRs, Partnerships and Resource Mobilization for Health was established to strengthen the capacities of all the WHO/AFRO staff in EXRs and partnerships. This programme was rolled out in most WHO offices targeting over 500 technical and administrative staff, the training enhanced EXRs and partnerships skills in the Region.

Conclusion

The WHO AFRO TA was a visionary initiative that has had a profound impact on the organisation’s ability to support health development in Africa. By focusing on strengthening partnerships, the TA has enabled WHO AFRO to mobilise significant resources, enhance its engagement with stakeholders and improve health outcomes across the continent.

The TA’s success is evident in the growth and diversification of WHO AFRO’s funding base, the enhanced capacity of its Country Offices to engage with stakeholders and the strategic alignment of its initiatives with national health priorities. The organisation’s approach to resource mobilisation, coupled with its emphasis on sustainability, has positioned WHO AFRO well to continue supporting Member States in their efforts to achieve equity, UHC and the SDGs.

Despite this positive progress, the road to sustainable, predictable and flexible WHO financing to effectively carry out our work, address global health threats and meet the expectations of Member States for better health for all at all ages is still long. WHO’s work continues to be hindered in critical underfunded areas by lack of long-term predictable and flexible funding. The unfinished business of the TA as it relates to partnerships and resource mobilisation needs to be tackled to ensure a stronger WHO, more transparent, accountable and ready to respond to global health challenges.

In the current context of the global health climate of funding constraints and competing priorities, while there is also increased pressure on multilateral organisations such as WHO, the lessons learnt from the TA would be of utmost importance in guiding investments in partnership models that are sustainable, strengthen national sovereignty in the Africa Region and unlock untapped resources from multiple sectors (public and private sectors) to respond to health challenges and needs. Additionally, exploration of strategies aimed at ensuring more efficient use of organisational resources would be critical in ensuring that the organisation could achieve more impact with less resources in the current austere circumstances.

Moving forward, it is essential to sustain the momentum generated by the TA by continuing to reinvest the resources mobilised in partnership functions as well as programme management and operational functions to ensure that the increased resources are timely and effectively used. The continued involvement of WHO AFRO’s leadership in partnership development and stakeholder engagement will be crucial in maintaining the organisation’s progress and achieving long-term health improvements in Africa.
