Quantitative Approaches to Accelerate MASH Drug Discovery and Development
Authors: Yasmeen Abouelhassan, Shailendra Tallapaka, Ramin Mehrani, Scott Q. Siler, Li Qin, Zeyuan Wang, Maria E. Trujillo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatohepatitis (MASH) is a widespread liver condition that leads to cirrhosis, hepatic carcinoma, and increased mortality rates. In MASH, the diagnosis and accelerated approval of drugs are dependent on changes in histological endpoints; full approval requires lengthy outcomes trials. This requirement for biopsies complicates and constrains clinical development. While the use of noninvasive biomarkers to aid in diagnosis and prognosis is prevalent in MASH trials, interpretation of these data is not straightforward. Due to the complexity of the disease and heterogeneity of the patient population, a single biomarker may not fully capture the changes in the pathobiology with disease progression or with pharmacological interventions. No biomarker or collection of biomarkers can be used in lieu of histological evaluations for accelerated approval. These challenges may be mitigated in part through the application of quantitative approaches and model informed drug discovery and development (MID3). In this review, we demonstrate how MID3 approaches can be applied alone or together to facilitate decision making. For example, quantitative systems pharmacology (QSP) modeling can predict the physiological effects of MASH drugs and identify opportunities for combination therapy. Model‐based meta‐analysis (MBMA) can benchmark molecules in early development and aid in biomarker interpretation by establishing relationships between biomarkers and histological endpoints. Artificial intelligence and machine learning (AI/ML) methods can aid in the identification of participants that meet histological criteria and reduce screen failure rates. Together, these quantitative approaches can be used strategically to accelerate MASH drug development.

INTRODUCTION

Metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), previously known as non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), represents the most common chronic liver condition around the world, with a prevalence of 25–30% worldwide. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 The disease encompasses a wide range of liver pathology for these patients presenting with steatosis. Up to 40% of MASLD patients can further progress to MASH, previously known as non‐alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH). 3 MASH is a progressive form of MASLD characterized by hepatic steatosis and inflammation with hepatocyte injury (ballooning) with or without fibrosis. MASH is a chronic and progressive condition that can potentially result in cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and increased risk of both liver transplants and all‐cause mortality. 3 These varying features of MASH observed at different stages of disease progression result in disease heterogeneity among MASH patients.

Liver biopsies remain the primary diagnostic method for MASH. 5 Liver biopsies are used to characterize steatosis, inflammation, and hepatocyte ballooning via a composite NASH activity score (NAS), and fibrosis stage via the MASH Clinical Research Network (CRN) fibrosis score. The outcome and prognosis of patients with MASLD or MASH worsen significantly with the severity of the disease and worsening of fibrosis. 6 , 7 Due to the strong association between the severity of histological readouts and outcomes, improvement in histology remains the primary efficacy and registration endpoint for drugs. In 2018 and 2019, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) released guidance outlining requirements for developing drugs to treat MASH. 8 , 9 For full approval in both pre‐cirrhotic and cirrhotic MASH, the FDA emphasized the need for clinical outcome trials that demonstrate the drug’s ability to prevent progression to cirrhosis and cirrhosis complications, reduce the need for liver transplantation, and improve survival. These outcome trials are lengthy, often requiring 5 years or more. Recognizing the significant time investment required for these trials, the FDA granted accelerated approval for pre‐cirrhotic MASH based on specific histological endpoints. These endpoints include (1) resolution of steatohepatitis on overall histopathological reading and no worsening of liver fibrosis. Resolution of steatohepatitis is defined as absent fatty liver disease or isolated or simple steatosis without steatohepatitis and a NAS of 0–1 for inflammation, 0 for ballooning, and any value for steatosis; (2) improvement in liver fibrosis greater than or equal to one stage NASH CRN fibrosis score and no worsening of steatohepatitis (defined as no increase in NAS for ballooning, inflammation, or steatosis); or (3) both resolution of steatohepatitis and improvement in fibrosis. The European Medicines Agency (EMA) guidance aligns with the FDA regarding intermediate histological endpoints for accelerated approvals of pre‐cirrhotic MASH. However, in contrast to the FDA, the EMA mandates a significant improvement in both histological endpoints rather than just one. 10

Based on the FDA guidance, histological endpoints were not sufficient for approval in cirrhotic populations. However, in a 2021 webinar entitled “Regulatory Perspectives for Development of Drugs for Treatment of NASH,” the FDA announced that a clinical outcome trial in cirrhotic MASH (F4) could convert accelerated approval for pre‐cirrhotic MASH to full approval. 11 Most recently, in 2023, the FDA has agreed to an accelerated approval pathway in cirrhotic patients using histology based on a press release from 89bio. 12 This flexibility demonstrates the FDA’s responsiveness and adaptability to challenges in MASH drug development and highlights the unmet needs of this patient population. The EMA aligns with the FDA’s recent flexible approaches as it clearly outlines accepting intermediate endpoints for accelerated approvals in patients with compensated cirrhosis. 10 These intermediate endpoints include reversal of cirrhosis, defined as an improvement of at least one fibrosis stage to non‐cirrhotic liver disease. The EMA also emphasized on the need for support by secondary outcomes including histological assessments, noninvasive disease markers, and descriptive data on decompensation events, liver transplantation, and mortality. 10

The biopsy requirements for MASH drug approval complicate clinical development. Liver biopsies are invasive, heterogeneous, resource‐intensive, and typically only assessed at the beginning and end of a trial, meaning longitudinal data are absent. Baseline liver biopsies, including prespecified NAS and fibrosis score requirements, are part of the entry criteria for clinical trials, which results in slow enrollment and high screen failure rates in MASH trials. Furthermore, histological changes required for accelerated approval necessitate improvements in all the underlying pathologies in MASH: steatosis, inflammation, hepatocyte cell death, and fibrosis. It is worth noting that, in addition to the requirement for liver biopsies for the approval of agents, both FDA and EMA guidance encouraged sponsors to assess biomarkers in MASH trials to identify novel biochemical and noninvasive imaging biomarkers that could eventually replace liver biopsies due to their inherent limitations.

Though resmetirom is the first drug to meet the criteria for accelerated approval 13 ; many trials have failed. The reason for the failure remains uncertain, as it could be due to the therapy being ineffective, the trial too short, the patient population not appropriate, or other factors. This quandary has led to an onslaught of efforts toward identifying biomarkers that aid in diagnosis as well as prognosis in MASH. However, due to the inherent complexity of the disease and the need for biopsy or outcomes to validate biomarkers, establishing diagnostic and prognostic criteria for noninvasive biomarkers for MASH has been challenging.

Model‐informed drug discovery and development (MID3) approaches have been widely incorporated in various stages of drug development. 14 , 15 , 16 When strategically applied, MID3 can both inform decision making and accelerate development, especially in emerging fields such as MASH, where data collection is constrained by disease heterogeneity and the invasiveness of diagnostic/prognostic procedures. In this review, we demonstrate three different MID3 approaches: quantitative systems pharmacology (QSP) modeling, model‐based meta‐analysis (MBMA), and artificial intelligence/machine learning techniques (AI/ML) to inform the discovery and development of therapies for MASH. These quantitative tools differ in part in the data used to build them and how they integrate knowledge. Together, these three tools provide a mechanism to improve the efficiency and probability of success of clinical trials that may increase the availability of medicines for MASH patients.

QUANTITATIVE SYSTEMS PHARMACOLOGY MODELING

Quantitative systems pharmacology (QSP) modeling is a mechanism‐based approach that describes both the pathophysiology of a disease and how pharmacological interventions can modify these mechanisms. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 What makes QSP modeling a particularly valuable tool is that it can provide mechanistic insights and facilitate decision making by quantitatively integrating mechanistic data from multiple sources to produce readily interpretable results in the form of clinically relevant endpoints. The applications of QSP are growing and increasingly used for decision making. 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 These applications include, but are not limited to, understanding the mechanism of action of drugs, identifying biomarkers, optimizing clinical trial design, prediction of physiological effects, and identifying ideal combination therapies.

Several QSP models characterizing hepatic steatosis, hepatic fibrosis, and MASLD/MASH have been reported. 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 In this review article, we focus on NAFLDsym, a model first described by Siler, 20 and its applications to inform the discovery and development of therapies for MASH. The NAFLDsym model is a very powerful tool for MASH drug development because it contains sub‐models representing the interplay of each of the important pathophysiological characteristics of MASH including steatosis, lipotoxicity, inflammation, and fibrosis. 20 NAFLDsym is commercially available software, at the time of writing this manuscript, that is available for free to non‐profit academic institutions but needs to be licensed by for‐profit corporations. 30 Although many of the NAFLDsym model equations have not been discussed in Siler et al., this model is based on the DILIsym model whose equations have been published. 31 , 32 Importantly, this model has the capability to predict changes in several clinical measures including markers of hepatocyte injury (e.g., alanine transaminase [ALT], aspartate transaminase [AST]), immune mediators (e.g., tumor necrosis factor α [TNFα], transforming growth factor β [TGFβ]), and histological measures (e.g., NAS, fibrosis stage). 20 This model has the capability to predict changes in several important clinical labs and biomarkers including markers of hepatocyte injury (e.g., alanine transaminase [ALT], aspartate transaminase [AST]), immune mediators (e.g., tumor necrosis factor α [TNFα], transforming growth factor β [TGFβ]), and histological measures (e.g., NAS, fibrosis stage).

NAFLDsym also includes a virtual population of 1,794 participants that represent the variability in the pathophysiological pathways described in the model. Described in greater depth by Siler, 20 these pathways include hepatic de novo lipogenesis, adipose fatty acid release, hepatocyte triglyceride lipolysis, oxidative stress, activation of hepatic stellate cells, and collagen synthesis in stellate cells. Variability across these key pathophysiologic components of MASH enables evaluation not only of disease status but also of the diverse response to treatment within MASH cohorts. These include allowing for investigation of experimental therapies/lifestyle modifications (e.g., reduced food intake) on different populations (e.g., individuals of varying fibrosis stage or liver fat levels). This model has previously been used to describe the effects of various molecules on MASH endpoints including cenicriviroc, pegbelfermin, acetyl‐CoA carboxylase (ACC) inhibitors, diglyceride acyltransferase‐2 (DGAT2) inhibitors, and 5′ adenosine monophosphate‐activated protein kinase (AMPK) activators. 33 , 34 , 35 , 36

The value of NAFLDsym is that it can characterize the drug and disease interaction, allowing for validation in the clinic. This is especially useful in early drug development when data are limited, and decisions are based on pharmacodynamic effects on biomarkers, not histology. Predictions of longitudinal changes in biomarkers or metabolites can aid in early clinical trial design, such as determination of study duration and dose. Further, incorporation of clinical biomarker data from competitor molecules into early development can accelerate the development of molecules within the same drug class and advance our understanding of the pharmacological mechanism of action.

To showcase how mechanistic and clinical data can be integrated into NAFLDsym, simulations of resmetirom, semaglutide, and ervogastat are provided below. Resmetirom, a thyroid hormone receptor β (THR‐β) agonist, is the first drug approved for MASH. 13 , 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 Semaglutide, a glucagon‐like peptide‐1 (GLP‐1) agonist, is approved for diabetes and weight loss but has also shown promise in MASH. 37 , 38 , 39 , 41 Ervogastat, a DGAT2 inhibitor, is under development for MASH. 39 , 42 , 43 These three molecules are antisteatotic agents and may in part ameliorate the pathobiology of MASH through reductions in liver fat. In constructing these representations in NAFLDsym, insights into the mechanism of action were gleaned as well as the dose and time course of drug‐induced changes in clinical measurements. This information is invaluable to the design of clinical studies evaluating other molecules within these drug classes as well as drug combinations including these molecules. One major assumption in most of these simulations was that the effects of these drugs on liver fat were entirely drug‐related. In the clinical trials used to calibrate these representations, placebo effects of ~10–20% were observed, possibly due to lifestyle changes during the trial or other random effects not captured by the model. Due to the heterogeneity of patient populations and of disease progression/regression within these populations, capture of the placebo effects in MASH remains challenging, for example, NAFLDsym is not a disease progression model. Thus, it is the modeler’s discretion whether the drug representations are calibrated to either placebo adjusted or relative change from baseline.

Resmetirom is a selective agonist of THR‐β whose effects on mitochondrial respiration, sterol regulatory element‐binding protein (SREBP) activation, and carbohydrate‐responsive element‐binding protein (ChREBP) expression result in a net decrease in liver fat and overall improvement in MASH‐related histological outcomes. 13 , 42 , 44 , 45 , 46 The resmetirom model in NAFLDsym was constructed using published data and includes liver exposure from a physiologically based pharmacokinetic model of resmetirom and a combination of mechanistic data from several studies that used thyroid hormone to activate THR‐β along with in vitro potency data comparing several THR‐β agonists. 47 The results of the model were compared to data from a phase II trial in which 384 participants with biopsy‐confirmed MASH (fibrosis stages 1–3) were treated with resmetirom for 36 weeks (Table 1 ). 48 The model was able to recapitulate liver fat reduction in all participants as well as in those with certain fibrosis scores and predicted plasma biomarker changes including triglycerides and ALT. It is worth noting that the standard error of the simulated results was numerically lower than observed data; however, without having access to individual participants’ data, it is not feasible to identify the sources of variability that contribute to the difference in standard error between observed and simulated populations.

NAFLDsym was also used to simulate ervogastat, a DGAT2 inhibitor. 40 , 43 , 51 to diacylglycerol acyltransferase 2 (DGAT2). 40 , 43 , 51 This enzyme catalyzes the final step of triglyceride synthesis leading to a reduction in De novo lipogenesis (DNL). DGAT‐2 inhibition results in increases in β‐oxidation and decreases in SREBP 1/2, further leading to decreased very low‐density lipoprotein‐triglyceride (VLDL‐TG) secretion. The DGAT2 inhibition model was built using mechanistic data and calibrated using data from Phase I and IIa trials of ervogastat. The model was calibrated using the Phase I and IIa data to achieve half‐maximal effective concentrations at 30 mg BID and maximum effects at 300 mg BID. The model was then used to simulate the Phase IIb MIRNA trial, demonstrating simulations that matched the clinical data for 300 mg BID at all timepoints. 43 , 52 However, these simulations underestimated the effects of the lower doses, suggesting that additional mechanisms may be involved in the reduction of liver fat with long‐term treatment (> 6 weeks). In the MIRNA trial, it is unclear why there was a lack of dose–response to DGAT2 inhibition; more clinical and mechanistic data are needed to understand these clinical results and enhance these predictions.

While it is currently unclear which single therapeutic is most ideal for MASH patients, integration of data on different molecules in development for MASH into NAFLDsym allows evaluation of these molecules as monotherapy and in combination in silico. This can aid in identification of combinations for co‐administration and/or inform the impact of treatment on top of the standard of care.

We conducted a sensitivity analysis of the different aspects of the underlying pathophysiology present in MASH. 53 The goal of this exercise was to understand the therapeutic value of pharmacological interventions to each aspect as a monotherapy as well as to identify ideal combination therapies. The evaluated aspects included a decrease in food intake (FI) or −200 kcal/day or −400 kcal/day leading to 5% and 10% weight loss, 90% inhibition of DNL with a 60% increase in fatty acid oxidation (FAO), inhibition of hepatic stellate cell (HSC) activation by 30%, and reductions in lipotoxicity/reactive oxygen species (ROS) by 40%. To evaluate the effectiveness of these as potential drug targets, the proportion of virtual patients from a population of MASH patients (Table S2 ) that showed improvement was assessed. Improvement was determined by reductions of fibrosis stage (≥ 1 stage) and NAS (≥ 2 points) predicted after 52 weeks of pathway interventions relative to initial values.

The results of this sensitivity analysis showed that decreases in FI or DNL/FAO resulted in comparable improvements in NAS. Approximately 10% and 20% of the population showed improvements with a reduction in FI of −200 and −400 kcal/day, and 20% of the population showed improvements with changes in DNL/FAO, respectively. The combination of reductions in FI and DNL/FAO resulted in 50% and 60% of the population showing improvement, demonstrating that combination therapies increased the number of responders to therapy (Figure 2 ). Effects of DNL/FAO on fibrosis improvement were greater than those of FI, with approximately 40% of the population showing improvement with changes in DNL/FAO vs. 20% and 40% with reductions in FI, and there too the combination increased the number of responders showing improvement to 60% and 100% of the population. The reduction in HSC activation had no effect on NAS when administered as monotherapy. ROS reduction led to minor reductions in NAS. Both HSC and ROS reduction improved fibrosis. The effects of decreased FI or DNL/FAO on fibrosis score were further improved when combined with the inhibition of HSC activation or ROS reduction. These explorations confirm there are many pathways that contribute to the pathophysiology of MASH. Agents that reduce hepatic fat are foundational for MASH patients, and adding those that reduce ROS and/or HSC activation may be key to optimal therapy across the spectrum of MASH. Although these findings on effects on NAS are in line with what has been observed with antisteatotic agents (such as resmetirom, ervogastat, and semaglutide), it does appear that the simulations predicted a larger proportion of responders to fibrosis improvements than was observed clinically. This could be due to the higher propensity of this specific virtual population toward improvement in fibrosis as all the individuals were in F3, whereas the NAS was more varied. This highlights the importance of the population characteristics on results in both simulations and clinical trials.

Currently, the combination of the two most advanced mechanisms for MASH, THR‐β and GLP‐1 agonists, is thought to be complementary to each other. 54 Based on the simulations above (Figure 2 ), we hypothesize that the combination of mechanisms causing a reduction in food intake and an increase in DNL should result in improvements in histology beyond either agent alone. However, in contrast to our predictions, the phase III study MAESTRO‐NASH shows that histological responses were equivalent in a subset of diabetic participants treated with resmetirom regardless of concomitant GLP‐1 therapy. 55 These results should be interpreted with caution because the most common dose of semaglutide co‐administered with resmetirom in MAESTRO‐NASH was the 1 mg dose, which is much smaller than the 2.4 mg dose used in studies for MASH. In addition, the study was not powered or designed to test the combination hypothesis. In the future, dedicated clinical trials testing the benefit of combination therapies such as resmetirom and semaglutide or other therapies vs. monotherapy are needed to inform prescribers so they may best manage patients. Until then, QSP modeling may aid in identifying combinations to invest in and ways to more efficiently evaluate whether not accelerate the development of these combination therapies.

QSP models such as NAFLDsym are valuable quantitative approaches in the evaluation of drugs of varying mechanisms for the treatment of MASH. Current caveats to NAFLDsym reside in the complexity of the model. For example, the NAFLDsym model consists of ~700 ordinary differential equations (ODEs) and over 1,000 closed‐form equations describing macronutrient metabolism, hepatocyte health, immunology, and fibrosis. Representative equations and ontological descriptions have been included in numerous publications. 56 , 57 , 58 , 59 Model simulations are technically challenging to implement and take significant processing time. Importantly, implementation requires a deep understanding of the underlying biology and pathophysiology. QSP models also require maintenance and should be updated with emerging preclinical or clinical data to both improve and validate model predictions. Regardless of these challenges, there is immense value with this approach for MID3 in MASH.

MODEL‐BASED META‐ANALYSIS

Model‐based meta‐analysis (MBMA) is a quantitative approach that allows researchers to synthesize insights from heterogeneous data (e.g., clinical trials, observational studies) to inform decision making, clinical trial design, and drug development strategies. 60 , 61 , 62 In brief, MBMA involves data aggregation and exploration of clinical data using statistical methodology and pharmacological plausibility to address specific questions and provide insights when data in the landscape are available but internal data are limiting. Tutorials detailing MBMA methodologies with examples are reviewed here. 63 , 64 Examples of insights from MBMA include benchmarking molecules across the competitive landscape, clinical pharmacology, and the interrelationships between biomarkers and efficacy/ safety.

In MASH, MBMA can be leveraged to evaluate and compare assets in various stages of clinical development. This is of particular value when assessing molecules in early clinical development prior to the collection of histological endpoints via biopsy, where a change in noninvasive biomarkers is the main decision‐making factor. 65 , 66 , 67 , 68 Currently, several consortia have formed to study the diagnostic and prognostic value of key biomarkers in MASH. 65 , 67 However, the results of these efforts have not been available in time to inform the many ongoing clinical trials highlighting a knowledge gap around the use of biomarkers to support the current state of drug development. In addition, due to the complexity of the underlying pathophysiology of MASH, drug developers have been encouraged to evaluate a constellation of biomarkers to guide decision making. Though this suggestion is valid, the exact combination of biomarkers to use is debated. 69

Currently, to inform clinical trial design and interpretation and to assess the probability of success for assets in early development, correlations between individual biomarkers and histological changes are frequently used to guide drug development and may benefit from the application of quantitative approaches such as MBMA. Thus, we share how MBMA approaches may be applied using a dataset of clinical trials conducted in participants with MASLD/MASH generated as of October 2023. The dataset was created by and purchased from Certara®, and it was inclusive of all agents studied in MASLD/MASH. Studies from the dataset that fit the desired endpoints of changes in liver fat, enhanced liver fibrosis (ELF), and corrected T1 (cT‐1) were included in the analysis of each biomarker, and these changes were related to corresponding changes in histological endpoints to inform clinical development of MASH therapeutics. A list of the studies included in each biomarker analysis is available in Data S1 .

In the below, we share the results of simple regression analyses of a select group of biomarkers and the relationship with histological endpoints in MASH trials. From these results, one can refine the analysis and build models suited to the class, mechanism, or patient population of interest. Once the appropriate data and ensuing relationship between the biomarkers and endpoints have been established, this MBMA model can be used to aid in the predictions of the effect that different mechanisms, molecules, doses, and regimens may have on histology based on their effects on the biomarker of interest. Establishing these data‐based connections between biomarkers and histology is incredibly valuable to aid in decision making as molecules transition from early to late development, where the cost and resource investment increases substantially. Further, due to the aggregation of data involved in the process, the predictions for one asset may be compared across the competitive landscape, further informing the value of the asset among competitors as well as among other assets in the development portfolio.

Liver fat content

Liver fat, a measure of triglyceride accumulation in hepatocytes, is most commonly assessed by magnetic resonance imaging proton density factor (MRI‐PDFF), which is the most accurate and reliable noninvasive method. 70 , 71 , 72 The MRI‐PDFF technique capitalizes on the differences in resonance frequencies between water and fat protons in the liver, enabling the precise calculation of fat fraction in the liver tissue, expressed as a percentage. Liver fat content ≥5% is a pathophysiological condition clinically known as hepatic steatosis. 72 Liver fat content of ≥ 5–10% is frequently used as entry criteria in MASLD/MASH trials. As patients progress from MASLD to MASH when fibrosis becomes severe, liver fat levels decline, resulting in a U‐shaped relationship for liver fat over time. 73 Though it is not clear why this occurs, this is why liver fat alone cannot serve as a diagnostic or prognostic biomarker in MASH without additional supporting evidence. To date, baseline liver fat in most clinical trials in MASH was ~20% and did not include participants with advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis (F4). Of note, not all assets under development for MASH are antisteatotic in nature, and thus the use of a decrease in liver fat to evaluate assets in MASH should be done judiciously.

As a prognostic biomarker, relative reductions in liver fat (RRLF, the percent change in liver fat from baseline) ≥30% were shown to be predictive of clinically meaningful responses in NAS, 74 and for MASH resolution without worsening of fibrosis. 71 In a retrospective analysis of 264 participants with MASH, there was a strong correlation between liver fat and NAS; correlation between liver fat and fibrosis was weak. 73 Using MBMA, we observed a linear correlation between RRLF and histological endpoints (i.e., MASH resolution and improvement in fibrosis score, Figure 4 a,b ). These data can be used to compare efficacy across drug classes and can also evaluate the effects of baseline characteristics, drug class, dose, and study duration on liver fat. Of note, though the placebo effect on RRLF across MASH trials was ~11%, drug effects on RRLF in these analyses did not include placebo correction.

Corrected T1

MRI iron‐corrected T1 (cT‐1) is a noninvasive imaging biomarker that consists of a proprietary MRI‐based measure that can detect changes in the fraction of extracellular free‐water content in the liver, which is then corrected for MRI field strength and the presence of liver iron. 75 Normal cT‐1 values are typically within 650–800 ms. 70 , 76 , 77 , 78 Elevated levels of cT‐1 have been correlated with liver injury and fibrosis, indicating that it might reflect the underlying mechanisms of liver inflammation and metabolic stress. Based on the within‐scanner repeatability, a change of cT‐1 ≥ 46 ms is considered a true change. In a study with 197 participants with compensated liver disease, clinical outcomes were predicted by liver cT‐1 > 825 m. 76 In a retrospective analysis of 264 participants in MASH studies, a cT‐1 change of 88 ms was related to a two‐point difference in overall NAS. 73

In MASH, the data characterizing the changes in cT‐1 are limited to a few studies spanning various mechanisms. 79 , 80 , 81 , 82 Exploration of the changes in cT‐1 revealed a strong correlation with changes in liver fat (assessed by MRI PDFF) both in the magnitude and timing of effects (Figure 5 a ), calling into question whether changes in cT‐1 in MASH are dependent on changes in liver fat. Work to deconvolute the cause and effect of changes in cT‐1 in MASH due to changes in liver injury/fibrosis vs steatosis is needed. Furthermore, only four studies measure cT‐1 and histological endpoints (Figure 5 b,c ). Of those, two studies tested anti‐inflammatory mechanisms (galectin 3 inhibitor and chemokine receptor CCR2/CCR5 antagonist), both of which are no longer under development due to underwhelming clinical results. Even with the sparse data, cT‐1 is a promising biomarker for MASH; however, more data from studies measuring cT‐1 and histological endpoints are needed to better interpret cT‐1 biomarker changes in MASH. 73

Enhanced liver fibrosis

Enhanced liver fibrosis (ELF) is a noninvasive blood test measuring three specific fibrosis biomarkers: hyaluronic acid (HA), type III procollagen peptide (PIIINP), and tissue inhibitor of matrix metalloproteinase 1 (TIMP‐1). 83 The test is noninvasive and does not require imaging machinery, thus being incorporated into many trials. ELF is commonly used to assess the prognosis of patients with advanced fibrosis and uses an algorithm of the components to present the ELF score. 84 , 85 , 86 , 87 Patients with an ELF score below 9.80 are considered at lower risk for progressing to cirrhosis and experiencing liver‐related clinical events. An ELF score between 9.80 and 11.29 is categorized as moderate risk, while an ELF score above 11.29 indicates a high risk. It is thought that the magnitude of ELF change to observe an improvement in fibrosis is −0.5 and −0.85 for an improvement of patient‐reported outcome. 88

The baseline levels for ELF have been found to be a significant predictor of disease progression in MASH clinical trials. A study conducted by Younossi et al. 88 in 2020 examined 2,155 patients with MASH‐related advanced fibrosis stage F3 and F4 and indicated the association between baseline ELF levels and disease progression as defined by histologic progression to cirrhosis, hepatic decompensation, liver transplant, and death. The findings demonstrated that among F3 patients, those who progressed to a higher fibrosis stage had higher baseline ELF levels (mean ELF score was 10.6 ± 0.1 for progressors vs. 9.9 ± 0.9 for non‐progressors; P < 0.0001). Similarly, in F4 patients, those who showed progression had higher baseline ELF levels with a mean score of 11.5 ± 1.2 compared to 10.6 ± 1.0 for non‐progressors; P < 0.0001.

In the context of MASH drug development, ELF has been one of the most explored biomarkers. A few studies measured ELF at multiple timepoints, and of those, there was no trend on how time on treatment affected ELF change, which limits the ability to draw meaningful conclusions regarding the changes in ELF over time. Our analysis shows a positive correlation of ELF and histological endpoints (Figure 6 a,b ). This finding reinforces the value of ELF as an early predictor for fibrosis changes with treatment, though the impact of the duration of treatment on ELF remains unclear. Few studies have shown improvements with treatment in participants with baseline fibrosis stage F4, limiting the prognostic value of ELF in this population. Overall, the ELF test has emerged as a promising biomarker in the field of MASH research, and future efforts to explore the relationship between ELF and histology may increase the utility of this assessment.

The limitations of the MBMA for MASH are largely due to the dearth of data. True, there have been many studies since 2017 when the FDA and EMA aligned on using histological endpoints as a surrogate for efficacy in MASH trials. 8 , 9 , 10 However, of these trials, few have shown the required changes in these endpoints necessary for accelerated approval; no drug has achieved traditional approval, making it difficult to determine the biomarker changes predictive of approval via histological surrogate endpoints or outcomes in MASH. In addition to this challenge, MASH clinical trials have varied in the baseline characteristics of the patient population, the duration of treatment, and other features of trial design and clinical conduct. This complicates the interpretation of the predictive power of biomarkers because any or all these features may influence the relationship between histological endpoints and biomarkers, as well as the effects of the MASH therapy tested. These complications, together with the fact that molecules under development for MASH are of varying mechanisms of action, make MBMA for MASH even more difficult. Efforts to limit the number of assumptions underlying the analyses are needed when using this data to inform MASH therapies, and results should be interpreted with caution. In the future, new trials will provide a more complete dataset for analysis of this complex heterogeneous disease.

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE/MACHINE LEARNING

Artificial intelligence/machine learning (AI/ML) is a quantitative tool that utilizes statistical techniques along with advanced algorithms to identify nonlinear relationships between data to predict outcomes of interest. 89 Compared to classical statistical methods such as linear regression and logistic regression, ML methods rely on learning from data with no/few assumptions about data distribution or functional forms of the model. 90 This flexibility allows ML algorithms to effectively explore relationships involving complex patterns of data sets and discover the hidden nonlinear relationships in the data. 90 Together, these attributes have resulted in a growing interest in applying algorithms to explore and predict outcomes using high‐dimensional and complex datasets 91 , 92 and the prediction of the histological endpoints in MASH trials is an ideal use case for this application, as evidenced by a growing number of efforts in this domain. 93 , 94 The application of AI/ML in MASH has been accepted by regulatory agencies to aid pathologists scoring liver biopsies in clinical trials during enrolment and follow‐up visits. In fact, the EMA has mentioned that the use of AI/ML to minimize bias in histological assessments is acceptable so long as there is appropriate justification of the scoring tool. 10 , 95

In MASH, despite several inherent limitations such as invasiveness and sampling variability, liver biopsy remains the gold standard for assessing the stage and severity of MASLD and MASH. 96 Thus, to identify participants for MASH trials where entry criteria include histological measures, clinicians rely on patient characteristics (e.g., age, body mass index [BMI] > 25, metabolic risk factors of elevated triglycerides and hypertension), laboratory values (e.g., ALT, AST), and biomarkers (e.g., ELF) to increase the likelihood of identifying eligible participants prior to conducting the biopsy. 97 Using these methods, the screen failure rates for clinical trials are often high, some exceeding 70%. 98 This high failure rate is most often associated with failure to meet the histological criteria, resulting in prolonged periods of enrollment and high patient burden. Therefore, the use of AI/ML to improve the successful identification of participants that meet study criteria including histology prior to liver biopsy not only saves time and resources but also avoids subjecting participants to an unnecessary invasive procedure.

To demonstrate the potential application of ML in predicting MASH‐related histological endpoints, we explored multiple ML classification techniques, including the Support Vector Classifier (SVC), Random Forest (RF), and Gradient Boosting Method (GBM) to identify participants who meet the entry criteria for a particular trial that was previously conducted. 99 The dataset for this investigation consisted of 443 data points (participants) selected from a screening population with available histology readouts. The input for all algorithms, termed features, is comprised of baseline demographics, select clinical laboratory measurements, and a selection of MASH‐related biomarkers (e.g., procollagen, collagen, ALT, and AST). The histological criteria applied to this exercise included participants with a fibrosis score of 2 or 3 and a NAS≥ 4 with steatosis, ballooning, and lobular inflammation ≥1 (225 participants). Participants not meeting the trial entry criteria were defined as participants with a fibrosis score of 0 or 1 or a NAS score < 4 with steatosis, ballooning, or lobular inflammation < 1 (218 participants). To ensure an unbiased evaluation of the model, the dataset was split 4:1 into a training set and a testing set. We reduced the number of inputs after calculating the pairwise correlation matrix of features using Spearman correlation matrix 100 and maintaining the feature with a higher ranking identified by the RF feature importance method.

Evaluation of the model’s accuracy in this study showed that the three ML techniques tested can use participant characteristics, laboratory measurements, and noninvasive biomarker data to identify participants who meet the inclusion criteria prior to biopsy with 73% to 77% accuracy (Figure 7 a ). Among all classification techniques tested, the random forest classifier showed the best performance with an area under the curve of receiver operating characteristic (AUCROC) of 0.81 (Figure 7 b ). In contrast, the most informative input features (taken from feature ranking provided by the random forest algorithm) as a predictor of this specific inclusion criteria showed an AUCROC value of 0.72.

Despite differences between the dataset used in this study (smaller, balanced dataset, where each group has a fairly similar number of observations) in comparison with other related publications (larger imbalanced dataset where the number of observations in each classification group is different from one another), 94 , 101 we observed many commonalities between the important features identified by random forest algorithms and what has been reported by other similar publications. In addition, the accuracy of our model also aligns closely with that reported in similar studies. 94 , 101 These findings suggest that ML techniques are better predictors of inclusion criteria than any single biomarker or patient attribute collected during screening for this clinical trial. While this work is limited by the relatively small sample size and the need for validation, it illustrates the potential of ML techniques as a tool in identifying participants who meet the inclusion criteria prior to biopsy. When applied to larger datasets, AI/ML can be useful in identifying participants with varying fibrosis and NASs dependent on the entry criteria for the clinical trial of interest.

As mentioned above, this AI/ML model has the potential to reduce the high screen failure rates in MASH trials and reduce the burden on participants interested in enrolling in MASH trials by identifying participants who might fit the biopsy criteria based on data acquired during screening before the biopsy is performed. There are many groups working on expanding the application of AI/ML to address similar challenges associated with liver biopsy in MASH trials 93 , 94 , 101 and despite their initial success, there are some limitations that need to be addressed before fully incorporating AI/ML‐based methods into clinical trial protocols. It should be noted that like any other applications of ML methods, extrapolation of the prediction beyond the range of training data is unreliable. Also, the lack of transparency and explainability of AI/ML methods could undermine their application when it comes to critical decision making in clinical trials. In addition to these limitations, training an AI/ML model to predict histological endpoints in MASH trials requires a large and comprehensive dataset to minimize inherent biases when training a model. For instance, utilizing datasets from a single trial could introduce biases due to the lack of variability in patients’ characteristics and clinical trial conditions. To quantify the impact of these limitations and enhance model generalizability, it is essential that MASH clinical trials move towards more unified trial entry criteria allowing data from multiple sources with similar patients’ characteristics to be combined to facilitate these analyses and the use of AI/ML in screening.

THE POWER OF COMBINED APPROACHES

Building a quantitative toolbox requires investment and dedication. Development of each of the models/approaches requires extensive data collection coupled with comprehensive engineering of models that describe the ecosystem from which these data are collected. Each model should be evaluated in ways appropriate for each tool, by how well it predicts outcomes of interest, and where the lack of data prohibits predictions. However, it is possible to augment the capability of any of these tools and hence our knowledge and decision making with the power of combined approaches. When more than one quantitative tool is available, there are opportunities to combine approaches in ways that may provide greater insights than one approach alone. In the below, we provide some examples of how we may combine approaches to enhance our predictions and improve MID3 in MASH.

QSP and MBMA

QSP modeling can aid discovery and early development by identifying MASH biomarkers such as liver fat, N‐terminal propeptide of type III collagen (Pro‐C3), and other metabolic changes that may be observed when modulating pathways using novel therapeutics. To assess these outcomes and the appeal of the new asset, MBMA can be utilized to benchmark the biomarker changes predicted against other assets in clinical development. In addition to direct comparisons of assets using biomarkers, the MBMA may also be used to translate the level of biomarker‐related changes simulated with histological changes based on an aggregate of clinical trials. Thus, the combination of QSP modeling and MBMA can aid in the appraisal of novel targets in MASH before clinical data are generated.

AI/ML and MBMA

MBMA approaches require the aggregation of large amounts of data from publications. In areas of active research such as MASH, the addition of clinical trials to the database may need to occur as frequently as every 3–6 months to maintain current assessments of the competitive landscape. Thus, efforts involved in maintaining a clinical database for MBMA are laborious and expensive. Advances in AI/ML techniques have improved automated search and retrieval techniques used in the identification, collection, and integration of relevant data from publications. 102 These enhancements to the dataset build‐up and maintenance may lower the cost to dataset subscriptions and expand the use of MBMA in drug discovery and development.

AI/ML and QSP

Developing QSP models necessitates a profound understanding of disease biology and curation of extensive literature to identify relevant mechanisms for representation within the model. Here, large language models can significantly expedite the development process by efficiently identifying pertinent mechanisms and supporting data, thereby streamlining model construction. Furthermore, the computational demands of large QSP models can be alleviated by training AI/ML models on simulation results, enabling the compression of complex models and substantially reducing simulation times. Additionally, AI/ML techniques can be employed to analyze simulation results across virtual populations characterized by numerous parameters, facilitating the rapid identification of sensitive parameters and critical mechanistic pathways. Combining QSP and AI/ML approaches can help accelerate drug development by improving the efficiency of QSP modeling and fostering a deeper understanding of model predictions.

CONCLUSION

MASH drug development is challenging due to the complexity and heterogeneity of the disease, limited data on successful therapies, and lack of established diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers. For this emerging field, MID3 approaches such as QSP modeling, MBMA, and AI/ML are quantitative tools that can be employed to inform decision making and accelerate drug development. Each approach presents unique opportunities and limitations. Careful conduct and interpretation of simulations resulting from these approaches is necessary to avoid misguidance. Though many of the limitations in MID3 for MASH are due to the limits of available data, recent studies have achieved positive outcomes, 39 , 103 , 104 where several new therapies may have data sufficient for accelerated approval in the next few years. Analysis of data from these putative therapeutics will provide invaluable insights for MASH drug discovery and development, some of which may best be realized using QSP, MBMA, and AI/ML techniques. Hosting a suite of these quantitative tools is an absolute competitive advantage. Taken together, learning across drug classes and clinical studies, and integrating mechanistic and pharmacological data on MASH facilitates data‐driven decisions in the discovery and development space. Strategic implementation of quantitative approaches and resultant data‐driven decisions advance the science in our pharmaceutical industry and increase the speed and efficiency with which we provide therapies for patients.
