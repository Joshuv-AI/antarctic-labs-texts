Native long-read RNA sequencing of human monocytes reveals activation-induced alternative splicing toward functional isoforms
Authors: Alejandra Bodelón, Maurice J. H. van Haaren, Paula Sobrevals Alcaraz, Lyanne J. P. M. Sijbers, Rianne C. Scholman, Lucas W. Picavet, Aafke de Ligt, Daphne van Ginneken, Remco G. A. Erkens, Harmjan R. Vos, Jorg J. A. Calis, Sebastiaan J. Vastert, Jorg van Loosdregt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Alternative splicing is a key mechanism for expanding transcriptomic and proteomic complexity, yet its role in innate immune activation remains incompletely understood. Here, we applied Oxford Nanopore native RNA-sequencing to generate an isoform-level transcriptome of primary human monocytes before and after activation with lipopolysaccharide. We identify over 24,000 expressed isoforms, including thousands of previously unannotated variants. Activation induced widespread isoform-specific expression changes, leading to extensive isoform switching events, which were validated using matched short-read RNA-Seq. These activation-induced shifts enhanced transcript immune-regulatory functions: activated monocytes preferentially express longer, coding-competent isoforms with complete open reading frames, fewer retained introns, and increased domain complexity. By integrating matched Ribo-seq and proteomic data, we demonstrate that these isoform modulations are associated with enhanced translation of immune effector proteins. Together, our findings position alternative splicing as a dynamic and functional regulator of monocyte activation, emphasizing the need for isoform-level resolution to fully understand immune cell function.

Introduction

Upon encountering pathogens, immune cells rapidly initiate a cascade of molecular events to mount an effective defense. This swift immune response is driven by dynamic alterations in gene and protein expression, which are critical for combating infections and limiting pathogen spread. While the relevance of these changes is well-established, the underlying molecular mechanisms controlling this rapid adaptation remain incompletely understood.

Alternative RNA splicing plays a pivotal role in regulating gene expression and expanding protein diversity. This post-transcriptional process allows a single gene to generate multiple transcript variants, called isoforms, contributing to functional specialization and cellular heterogeneity. In humans, nearly 90% of genes undergo alternative splicing, giving rise to an extensive repertoire of isoforms that modulate diverse biological processes1,2.

In immune cells, alternative splicing is increasingly recognized as a key regulator of functional plasticity, particularly during activation in response to pathogens3. Several studies have highlighted differential splicing of immune-related genes such as CD45, IRF5, and LILRA2, underscoring the capacity of splicing events to influence immune cell behaviour4–6. These findings align with observations of splicing quantitative trait loci (sQTLs) associated with immune-mediated diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus and multiple sclerosis3,7. Despite these insights, the full extent of the splicing landscape in immune cells, especially in the context of activation, remains incompletely defined.

Important studies employed short-read RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) to assess the splicing landscape in in vitro models of monocytes/macrophages, revealing numerous unannotated exon–exon junctions and alternative splicing events during differentiation8,9. Notably, current studies have been constrained by the limitations of short-read RNA-seq, as it captures only fragmented portions of transcripts and lacks the resolution to assess isoform diversity across entire genes. As a result, many potentially important isoforms could remain mischaracterized or entirely undetected with these techniques.

Recent advancements in long-read RNA-seq have revolutionized the study of alternative splicing by enabling the direct sequencing of native full-length RNA molecules. Unlike short-read approaches, long-read RNA-seq provides comprehensive transcriptome-wide insights by reading entire transcripts in a single pass. This capability greatly enhances accurate isoform identification, the assessment of alternative splicing events across entire genes, and the reconstruction of full-length isoforms, offering a much deeper understanding of gene expression and regulation10–12. Another advantage of long-read RNA-seq is that native RNA can be analysed, thereby avoiding amplification bias and reducing the introduction of artefacts, resulting in improved isoform detection and quantification. Several recent studies have now demonstrated the utility of long-read RNA-seq in uncovering complex splicing landscapes across diverse tissues and cell types13,14. Nonetheless, a comprehensive study using native long-read RNA-sequencing to assess how the splicing landscape in primary human monocytes is affected by activation is still lacking.

To address this gap, our study employs native long-read RNA-seq to systematically profile the alternative splicing landscape in primary human monocytes, both at rest and following activation (Supplementary Fig. 1). Through this approach, we identified thousands of previously uncharacterized splicing isoforms and revealed widespread activation-induced isoform switching. These shifts favoured longer, coding-competent isoforms more likely to be translated into functional proteins. These findings highlight alternative splicing as a dynamic regulator of immune responses and uncover critical insights into the proteomic consequences of alternative splicing during monocyte activation. A deeper understanding of these isoform-level changes may ultimately support the development of novel therapeutic strategies for immune-related diseases.

Results

Monocyte activation triggers splicing-related gene expression

To identify key molecular pathways regulating monocyte activation, primary human CD14+ monocytes were isolated from the blood of eight healthy donors, stimulated in vitro with LPS for 3 h and sequenced using short-read RNA-Seq. Principal component analysis (PCA) showed that LPS treatment could explain 98% of the total variance between samples (Fig. 1a). More than half of the expressed genes were differentially expressed following LPS activation: 25.51% (3057 genes) were overexpressed and 27.99% were underexpressed (3354 genes, Fig. 1b). Notably, upregulated genes exhibited greater expression changes (mean log2FC of overexpressed: 3.10, mean log2FC of underexpressed: −1.75). As expected, immune-related genes showed the most pronounced changes (selected gene names in Fig. 1b). Besides the expected identification of immune-related processes, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of overexpressed genes revealed a strong overrepresentation of processes involved in splicing, most of which were also significantly upregulated using Gene Set Enrichment Analyses (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). Indeed, the expression of the splicing-related genes involved in the significant pathways of Fig. 1c was increased after monocyte activation (Fig. 1d). Unsupervised hierarchical clustering of these splicing-related genes clearly separated samples by activation status, whether using all splicing genes or only those with significant expression changes (Fig. 1e, f). Within these, we identified two distinct clusters representing genes with mild downregulation (cluster 1) and genes with strong or moderate upregulation (cluster 2) following LPS stimulation (Fig. 1f). Although both clusters were involved in gene expression and mRNA splicing, cluster 1 was also associated with DNA repair and cluster 2 was involved in protein and mRNA transport, cell cycle and translation. To evaluate whether these findings extended to in vivo conditions, we analysed a publicly available transcriptomic dataset (GSE87290) of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from healthy volunteers challenged with LPS15. To functionally validate our findings that splicing is involved in monocyte activation, we aimed to modulate splicing in human monocytes by modulating the activity of serine/arginine-rich splicing factors (SRSF) through targeting their phosphorylation. Here, human monocytes were stimulated with LPS in the presence of inhibitors of CLK (CLK-IN-T3) or SRPK1 (SRPIN340) to suppress SRSF phosphorylation16–19, or an inhibitor of PP2A (LB-100) to increase SRSF phosphorylation20, thereby modulating the splicing process. Indeed, CLK and SRPK1 inhibitors reduced SRSF phosphorylation, whereas PP2A inhibition had little effect (Supplementary Fig. 3a), possibly because SRSF is already highly phosphorylated after activation, rendering additional changes difficult to detect. TNFα expression, a canonical marker of monocyte activation, was measured at both the protein and mRNA level via flow cytometry and qPCR, respectively. These inhibitors altered TNFα expression dose dependently without affecting viability, indicating that interference with splicing modulates monocyte activation (Fig. 1h–k and Supplementary Fig. 3b, c). Taken together, these data demonstrate that splicing-related gene expression increases upon monocyte activation and suggest that changes in the splicing process are functionally relevant for monocyte activation.

Identification of novel isoforms in human monocytes

As we observed extensive changes in the expression of splicing-related genes upon monocyte activation, we next aimed to determine whether these changes also translated into alterations of expressed isoforms. For a comprehensive analysis of all expressed isoforms, we employed Oxford Nanopore native RNA sequencing on RNA isolated from primary human monocytes, both before stimulation (0 h) and after in vitro activation with LPS for 3 h. Transcript detection and quantification, including both annotated and novel isoforms (isoforms with novel splice junction chains and/or different TSS/TES), were performed using full-length alternative isoform analysis of RNA (FLAIR) (Fig. 2a). In total, we identified 24,939 expressed isoforms in monocytes, of which 62% were absent from the GENCODE reference annotation (Fig. 2b). In addition, we detected 1238 novel expressed genome regions (loci) that were not previously annotated (Fig. 2b), 631 predicted to be coding (Supplementary Fig. 4a). To assess the novelty of our transcriptome more broadly, we compared our isoforms with TRAILS, a previously published long-read transcriptome of blood immune cells13, and with FLIBase, a curated database of isoforms expressed in normal and cancerous tissues21. Strikingly, 74% and 68% of the novel isoforms in our dataset contained splicing junction chains absent in TRAILS and FLIBase (Supplementary Fig. 4b, c), respectively. Isoforms in our dataset were also significantly longer than those in the GENCODE reference (two-sided Wilcoxon test, p < 2.2 × 10−¹⁶; Fig. 2c).

We next classified the type of splicing events of our transcriptome by their similarity to annotated isoforms, and we detected 4.50% of isoforms with alternative 3’ or 5’ end (FSM) and 13.07% with different 5’ or 3’ exon (ISM; Fig. 2d). Notably, 42.9% of isoforms were completely novel, either containing new combinations of known splice junctions (NIC) or incorporating novel splice sites (NNC). To validate our findings, we analysed splice junctions in two independent short-read RNA-seq datasets: our own (Fig. 1) and GSE103501, a publicly available dataset of monocytes from healthy donors consisting of five 0 h and four 6 h LPS-stimulated samples22. We were able to validate all splice junctions from 83.5% of the isoforms identified in our long-read dataset (Supplementary Fig. 4d), including 65.9% of the fully novel NIC and NNC isoforms. Analysis of splicing event types revealed that novel isoforms contained a higher frequency of alternative 5′ and 3′ splice sites compared to annotated isoforms (Fig. 2e). Moreover, we detected a greater number of isoforms per gene than previously appreciated (Fig. 2f), with 64.6% of the genes containing at least one novel isoform and 46% of the genes expressing a greater number of novel isoforms than annotated in monocytes. Together, these findings indicate that the currently annotated monocyte transcriptome substantially underestimates the diversity of isoforms expressed in this cell type and underscore the importance of cell type- and condition-specific transcriptome profiling using native long-read RNA sequencing.

Monocyte activation induces a shift in isoform usage

Next, we aimed to determine how activation reshapes isoform-specific transcriptomic expression in monocytes. We observed condition-specific isoform expression, with 16.8% of isoforms expressed exclusively after LPS stimulation and 24.6% expressed only before activation (Fig. 3a). Notably, a greater proportion of novel isoforms were expressed before activation (26.3% vs. 20.3% in LPS). Furthermore, isoform-specific expression was assessed between conditions. Upon monocyte activation with LPS, we detected 2915 differentially expressed genes (2524 annotated and 391 novel) and 7100 differentially expressed isoforms (2842 annotated and 4258 novel), with a general tendency towards upregulation (mean log2FC for differential gene and isoform expression were 0.45 and 0.54, respectively, Fig. 3b, c). We also identified distinct categories of expression patterns among isoforms of the same gene. For example, PTX3 exhibited uniform upregulation across all isoforms, while NLRP3 exhibited isoform-specific induction upon stimulation with LPS (Fig. 3c). Interestingly, genes such as PPP2R2D showed no change in total expression but underwent a clear shift in the isoform used after activation. To assess these different isoform switching events, we compared the relative abundance of the gene’s dominant isoform before and after activation. For example, the primary isoform of PPP2R2D switched following activation (Fig. 3d), and NLRP3 shifted from expressing two main isoforms (one known and one novel) to three different isoforms (one known and two novel). In total, we detected 686 isoform switching events (333 annotated and 353 novel, Fig. 3b). Among these, six events spanning distinct functional categories were validated using an electrophoretic separation of PCR products (Supplementary Fig. 5). As expected, functional enrichment analyses of genes undergoing significant isoform shifting revealed overrepresentation in immune-related processes, including innate immune activation and cytokine production (Fig. 3e; most specific functions and pathways are described in Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). Notably, several isoform-switching events occurred in genes involved in the regulation of mRNA splicing, suggesting feedback within the splicing machinery itself.

To validate the isoform switching events detected in our long-read dataset, we analysed two independent short-read RNA-Seq datasets: our own short-read datasets and the public dataset GSE10350122. Both datasets confirmed the same LPS-induced isoform switching patterns, with half of those changes reaching significance in at least one of the short-read RNA-seq datasets (Fig. 3f, Supplementary Fig. 6c). Indeed, we found a significant correlation (p < 0.05) between our long-read and short-read datasets for both gene and isoform expression (Supplementary Fig. 6d). We then compared specific splicing events that differed between LPS-treated and 0 h conditions (Fig. 3g). This analysis revealed a reduction in intron retention and an increase in exon inclusion events following activation, for example within the NLRP3 gene. In total, we detected 214 differently spliced events after activation (118 annotated and 96 novel, Fig. 3b). To identify splicing factors potentially involved in these changes in exon exclusion/inclusion (SE), we performed a motif enrichment analysis in regions flanking exons that showed significantly increased inclusion after activation, and compared them with exon-skipping events that were unchanged between conditions. This analysis revealed enrichment of motifs bound by SFPQ and CPEB2, both of which are highly overexpressed after LPS activation (Supplementary Table 1). Taken together, these data demonstrate that in addition to gene-level changes, monocyte activation induces widespread alterations in isoform usage, particularly in genes associated with immune function. These findings support a model in which alternative isoform usage plays a regulatory role in monocyte activation.

Monocyte activation results in longer and more productive isoforms

Having established that monocyte activation induces widespread isoform switching, we next aimed to understand the functional consequences of these changes. Analysis of the isoform switching events revealed a significant shift post-activation toward longer isoforms, isoforms with a greater amount and more distal exons, as well as those containing longer and complete open-reading frames (ORFs), longer 3’ UTRs, and fewer retained introns (Fig. 4a). Correspondingly, read length significantly increased after 3 h LPS activation (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Furthermore, we observed a significant increase in the usage of isoforms with predicted coding potential, accompanied by a decrease in the usage of isoforms predicted to be susceptible for nonsense-mediated decay (NMD) (Fig. 4b). Monocyte activation also increased isoform topology complexity, along with number and length of protein domains. Post-activation isoforms were enriched for extracellular regions and intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs), features often associated with dynamic regulatory and signalling functions. After identifying these changes in the characteristics of the isoforms used before and after activation, we aimed to further understand what impact these changes might have on functionality. Therefore, we classified isoforms based on their coding capacity: the isoform(s) per gene encoding for the longest protein were defined as “longest-ORF”, and those with ORFs lacking premature stop codons were considered “productive”. We observed a marked increase in the usage of both longest-ORF and productive isoforms following LPS activation (Fig. 4c, d). This was further supported by the increased usage of the Ensembl canonical transcript per gene (Supplementary Fig. 7f), and by a rise in the usage of isoforms encoding the greatest numbers of protein domains (Supplementary Fig. 7g). As expected, longest-ORF, productive and Ensembl Canonical isoforms contained significantly more protein domains than their non-functional counterparts (Supplementary Fig. 8a).

To assess the relevance of these isoform shifts on translation, we analysed publicly available Ribo-Seq and matched RNA-Seq data from a THP-1 monocytic cell line before and after 3 h LPS stimulation, including five Ribo-Seq and four RNA-Seq replicates per condition23 (GSE208041). THP-1 is a well-used human monocytic model that retains key functional and molecular characteristics of primary monocytes. We identified isoforms expressed in THP-1 cells based on uniquely mapped RNA-Seq reads and confirmed their translation by corresponding Ribo-Seq signals. Mapping these isoforms onto our dataset revealed a significant increase in the usage of translated isoforms after activation (Fig. 4e). Notably, both longest-ORF isoforms and those with the highest number of protein domains per gene were strongly associated with ribosomes (Supplementary Fig. 8b).

To examine the impact of isoform usage changes on protein expression, we employed mass-spectrometry analyses on proteins isolated from primary human monocytes, both at 0 h and after in vitro activation with LPS for 3 h. Genes with more than 25% increase in usage of longest-ORF, productive, or Ensembl Canonical isoforms after activation from our long-read dataset (upper left red and blue dots in Fig. 4c, d and Supplementary Fig. 7f), exhibit a significantly increased protein abundance after stimulation compared with the other genes (Fig. 4f). These findings were validated using a publicly available mass-spectrometry data (PXD004352) from 12 h LPS activated and non-activated human monocytes24, suggesting that isoform shifts toward functional transcripts are associated with enhanced protein production (Fig. 4f). Reinforcing this idea, we observed a substantially higher correlation between 3 h LPS mRNA and 12 h LPS protein levels for genes with more than 25% increase in usage of longest-ORF, productive, or Ensembl Canonical isoforms after activation compared to other genes (Supplementary Fig. 8c). Indeed, unique peptides predominantly originated from longest-ORF, productive, Ensembl canonical and translated isoforms (Supplementary Fig. 8d).

Finally, we used the ESM1b Protein Language Model (PLM) to assess the pseudolikelihood of each isoform’s protein sequence, a metric correlated to evolutionary fitness25. We accounted for expression dynamics per condition by weighting the PLM pseudolikelihood score by the isoform usage fraction observed before and after activation. We grouped the isoforms into two groups: belonging to the genes showing over a 50% increase in usage of the longest-ORF, productive, or Ensembl Canonical isoforms after activation, and those from all other genes. The first group exhibited higher weighted pseudolikelihood after activation than before (Fig. 4g). Correspondingly, we detected a significantly higher PML pseudolikelihood score for the predominant isoform used after activation than before for genes that switched their most used isoform between conditions (Supplementary Fig. 8e). Similarly, productive and Ensembl Canonical isoforms had higher mean pseudolikelihood scores (Supplementary Fig. 8f). Taken together, these results indicate that monocyte activation drives a shift toward the usage of more functional isoforms contributing to an enhanced translation of biologically relevant proteins.

NLRP3, a case study of activation-induced changes in isoform usage

To demonstrate the ability of long-read sequencing to detect functional isoform usage changes upon monocyte activation, we selected one gene of particular interest: NLRP3. This gene is the central regulator of the NLRP3 inflammasome in monocytes and is associated with severe autoinflammatory disorders, such as cryopyrin-associated periodic syndrome, gout, and atherosclerosis26,27. Notably, “NLRP3 inflammasome complex assembly” was among the specific pathways significantly altered by isoform switching after activation (Supplementary Fig. 6b). NLRP3 encodes a well-characterized intracellular sensor that detects exogenous and endogenous danger signals, triggering the formation and activation of the inflammasome complex27. A previous study has shown that inclusion of exons 5 and 7 in NLRP3 is essential for its functionality26. Using Oxford Nanopore native RNA sequencing, we identified nine novel NLRP3 isoforms not annotated in the GENCODE reference annotation (Fig. 5a), many of which exhibited unique combinations of splicing events. Specifically, we detected intron retention between exons 1 and 2 in several isoforms (ENST00000366496.7, 4 and 5), and multiple exon-skipping events involving exons 5, 6 and 7 (ENST00000366496, 3, 4, 5 and 8). Following LPS activation, both NLRP3 gene expression (Fig. 5b) and isoform usage (Fig. 5c, d) were altered. Specifically, we observed a shift from isoforms lacking one or more central exons to isoforms containing all canonical exons (Fig. 5c, d). This switch included a decrease in intron retention and reduced usage of the alternative 3’ end isoforms after activation (Fig. 5a, c, d). Although all isoforms were classified as productive, activation induced a significant increase in the usage of isoforms encoding for the longest ORF (Fig. 5e), particularly ENST00000697350.1 and Isoform 1 (Fig. 5c, d). These isoform switching events were reversed by splicing inhibitors (Supplementary Fig. 9a) and validated by using two independent short-RNA-Seq datasets from human monocytes (Fig. 5f). Further experimental validation was performed via qPCR using isoform-specific primers, confirming differential expression patterns (Fig. 5g). We also confirmed alternative splicing of exon 5, 6 and 7 by electrophoretic separation of PCR products amplified using primers located in exon 4 and 8 (Fig. 5h). Additionally, isoform switching was confirmed at the protein level by Western blot (Supplementary Fig. 9b). This revealed a clear shift from shorter isoforms to longer isoforms containing all exons following activation.

As an additional example, an increase in the usage of the longest-ORF isoform by exon 6 inclusion after 3 h LPS activation was validated at the mRNA and protein levels for the DNA repair enzyme TDP2 (Supplementary Fig. 9c–f), and this isoform switching event was reversed by splicing inhibitors (Supplementary Fig. 9g). Taken together, NLRP3 and TDP2 represent compelling examples of how native long-read RNA sequencing enables the detection and quantification of both validated and novel splicing events, many of which are difficult or impossible to resolve with short-read RNA-seq technologies.

Discussion

Making use of recently developed techniques, such as Oxford Nanopore native RNA sequencing, our findings establish alternative splicing as a key, yet often underappreciated, layer of gene regulation during monocyte activation (Supplementary Fig. 1). While changes in total gene expression have been extensively studied in immune responses, our data show that activation also involves widespread isoform switching, resulting in a shift toward more functional, translatable isoforms. This dual-level regulation, at both the gene and isoform level, adds critical nuance to our understanding of immune cell modulation.

Numerous immune-related genes are known to undergo alternative splicing in response to activation4–6. Consistent with this, we observed upregulation of splicing-related genes in monocytes activated in vitro, as well as in PBMCs from LPS-challenged individuals in vivo, suggesting a broader role for splicing modulation in innate immune activation. Importantly, inhibition of splicing factor phosphorylation16–19 impaired monocyte activation, supporting a functional role for splicing regulation in this process. Splicing regulators such as Muscleblind Like Splicing Regulator 1 (MBNL1) and quaking (QKI) have been shown to contribute to alternative splicing in monocytes/macrophages8,9. In addition, we detected an enrichment of motifs bound by SFPQ, which has been implicated in exon inclusion28, and CPEB2, a member of a protein family that modulates translation29, in exons more included after activation. However, additional research on these and other immune-specific splicing factors could improve our understanding of the activation mechanisms and inform therapeutic strategies for autoimmune diseases.

Long-read RNA sequencing has previously been demonstrated to outperform short-read technologies in capturing the full isoform landscape, particularly for novel or complex transcripts13,14,30. Here, we used Oxford Nanopore RNA sequencing on native RNA, which avoids amplification bias and captures full-length transcripts directly31. As a result, longer isoforms can be detected, which is further supported by the increased number of alternative 3’ and 5’ splicing events observed in the novel isoforms. One limitation of long-read RNA sequencing is that isoform annotation can be affected by RNA degradation. To mitigate this, we applied stringent criteria by annotating only isoforms initiating at known transcription start sites (TSSs) identified in the human genome by CAGE experiments, reducing false positives32,33. In addition, we incorporated samples from multiple donors, and we match our data with short-read RNA-seq for validation and correction, ensuring high reproducibility despite the relatively high error rates of long-read platforms34. Our results further support the notion that isoform expression is highly cell- and condition-specific13,14. Indeed, when compared to two recently published datasets: TRAILS, a long-read transcriptome atlas of resting immune cells13, and FLIBase, a long-read and short-read transcriptome of a range of normal and cancerous tissues and cells21, we identified thousands of novel isoforms, highlighting the dynamic and context-dependent nature of isoform expression. While our study focused on monocytes, future studies combining long-read sequencing with single-cell or spatial transcriptomics could explore isoform dynamics across various innate and adaptive immune cells, subtypes or tissue microenvironments, providing a more integrated view of splicing-regulated immunity.

Our analyses show that monocyte activation is not only characterized by increased transcription but also by a shift in isoform usage, favouring transcripts with increased coding potential, more exons, fewer introns and more complete ORFs. Indeed, reduced intron retention along with increased exon inclusion has been previously reported in human macrophages following bacterial infections35. Interestingly, in vitro activation of human macrophages has also been reported to increase alternative first exon usage. This pattern was not observed in our monocyte data, suggesting a slightly different isoform usage upon activation compared to their differentiated counterparts36. This implies that gene-level analyses may miss critical changes occurring at the isoform level. Previous studies in monocytes and other immune cells using short-read RNA-seq have reported differential splicing under inflammatory or immune-related conditions, but isoform-resolved approaches provide a more granular view. This level of resolution could also help identify genetic variants that modulate isoform ratios, offering new insights into the pathogenesis of immune and infectious diseases37–39.

Functionally, our data suggests that isoforms used after activation are more likely to be translated into proteins. This shift includes isoforms with longer ORFs, more protein domains, and features linked to translation efficiency, such as longer 3′ UTRs and reduced intron retention. Although ORF length does not universally predict functionality, since short isoforms can play important regulatory roles, we also observed increased usage of productive isoforms with intact ORFs lacking premature stop codons. We also detected an increase in the usage of isoforms associated with ribosomes. Although THP-1 cells were used to assess isoform translation, and this human monocytic model may have altered and less diverse transcriptional profiles, the association of these isoforms with ribosomes still suggests their potential for translation. These findings align with previous work showing that alternative splicing can regulate both protein domain composition and translation in response to immune stimuli39,40. We also observed that splicing impacts protein structure and that sequences used post-activation exhibit higher evolutionary fitness, as measured by PLM pseudolikelihood25. Taken together, these data support a model where, under resting conditions, immune-related genes tend to express more non-functional isoforms (e.g., with intron retention or exon skipping), and upon activation, rapid isoform switching enables a swift proteomic response, even prior to substantial transcriptional upregulation. Future proteomic studies of immune cells in both resting and activated states will be essential to fully characterize this dynamic regulation.

To illustrate the utility of long-read sequencing in resolving biologically relevant isoform changes, we highlighted one example: NLRP3, a central component of the inflammasome. Our approach for NLRP3 captured both known and novel splicing events, including exon skipping, intron retention, and alternative 3′ ends. We observed a clear shift toward full-length, functional isoforms upon activation, consistent with prior reports that inclusion of exons 5 and 7 is required for proper inflammasome assembly and NLRP3 activity26. These results demonstrate that long-read sequencing enables high-resolution characterization of gene function regulation at the isoform level, particularly for genes with immunological importance. Given that splicing alterations can modulate immune function, expanding these investigations to a disease context could uncover new avenues for therapeutic modulation of splicing, via kinase inhibitors or antisense oligonucleotides, as strategies to fine-tune immune responses13,41.

This study provides the first evidence of an increase in functional isoform usage in monocytes following 3 h of in vitro LPS stimulation. Although only a single time point was analysed, validation using a publicly available dataset at 6 h post-LPS in vitro stimulation22 with different experimental conditions than ours suggests that this increase in functional isoforms reflects a generalizable effect over time. Indeed, we validated the greater increase in protein abundance for genes using 25% more functional isoforms after activation than the rest of genes with a publicly available dataset of monocytes stimulated 12 h with LPS (Fig. 4f). Longitudinal analyses of isoforms expression following LPS stimulation could provide deeper insight into the temporal dynamics of monocyte activation governed by alternative splicing.

While we were able to validate the majority of the novel isoforms that we identified using short-read RNA-seq, there was also a proportion of isoforms that were not identified using this technique. Previous studies have suggested that long-read and short-read sequencing techniques are largely complementary rather than redundant, as their distinct characteristics make them better suited to detect different isoform-switching events42,43, leading to higher correlations at the gene level than at the isoform level. Such differences make direct comparisons challenging. Nonetheless, the increase in functionality after LPS stimulation was consistently detected regardless of the sequencing technique, strengthening our main conclusion. Additional long-read RNA-seq studies in monocytes with different immune stimuli could further expand and validate the atlases of expressed isoforms and provide insights into stimulus-specific responses.

In summary, we reveal alternative splicing as a dynamic and functional regulator of monocyte activation, orchestrating a shift toward isoform expression profiles that enhance protein production and immune functionality (Supplementary Fig. 1). Our findings highlight the importance of studying isoform-level regulation in immune responses and open new avenues for identifying immune-related disease mechanisms and therapeutic targets through isoform-resolved transcriptomics.

Methods

Cell culture

Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were obtained from healthy donor blood (Supplementary Table 2), after informed consent was obtained, in sodium heparin tubes and isolated using Ficoll-paque plus (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) density gradient media. Ethical oversight of the study and the use of human study participants was conducted and approved by UMC Utrecht and Sanquin (Amsterdam), and conducted in accordance with the criteria set by the Declaration of Helsinki. Primary human CD14+ monocytes were isolated from PBMC using automatic magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS, Miltenyi Biotech, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) using the human CD14+ monocyte isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) according to the manufacturer's protocol. 0 h samples were collected at this stage, before in vitro culturing, to avoid any stimulation from in vitro culture. Primary monocytes were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (Thermo Fisher, Hampton, NH, USA) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated foetal calf serum (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MI, USA), 1% L-glutamine (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA), 100 U/mL of penicillin and 100 mg/mL of streptomycin (Gibco, New York, NY, USA). Cells were cultured at 37 °C in 5% CO2 and stimulated with 100 ng/mL LPS (Sigma-Aldrich) for 3 h in culture medium. For the inhibition experiments, isolated monocytes or gated monocytes from PBMCs (eBioscience Fixable Viability Dye eFluor506, Invitrogen, Cat: 65-0866-14. Lot: 3093358, 1:1000 dilution; CD14-PerCP-Cy5.5, Biolegend, Cat: 325622, clone HCD14, Isotype: Mouse IgG1 κ, 1:100 dilution) were preincubated with the inhibitor for 1 h and then stimulated with LPS for 3 h (a figure exemplifying the gaiting strategy is provided as a Source Data). The following inhibitors were used: CLK-IN-T3 (1, 5 and 10 µM; MedChemExpress, NJ, USA)18,19, SRPIN340 (20, 50 and 100 µM; MedChemExpress, NJ, USA)16,17 and LB-100 (1, 5 and 10 µM; MedChemExpress, NJ, USA)20.

Flow cytometry

Cells were washed in FACS buffer (PBS with 2% FBS and 0.1% NaN3), fixed and permeabilized with a fixation/permeabilization solution kit (BD Cytofix/Cytoperm: Cat: 51-2090 KZ) and stained with mouse anti-human TNF-APC (Biolegend, Cat: 502912, Lot: B375241, clone Mab11 - Isotype Mouse IgG1 κ, 1:400 dilution) antibody. Measurements were performed using the BD FACSCanto™ II flow cytometer, and FlowJo v10 was used for data analysis.

(q)PCR

Cells were washed in PBS and lysed in RLT buffer (RNeasy kit, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). RNA was isolated using the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA synthesis was performed using the iScript cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). cDNA was stored at −20 °C. qPCR analysis was performed on the QuantStudio 3 real-time PCR system with SYBR Select master mix (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) and the primer sets can be found in Supplementary Table 3. Briefly, primers were designed by selecting the coordinates of exons of interest (exons involving splicing events and exons with equal expression in both conditions for gene expression normalization) using IGV and the Nanopore long-read RNA-Seq alignment files. UCSC genome browser was used to obtain the DNA sequence from these coordinates and the NCBI Primer designing tool was used to design the primers. Wherever possible, one exon-spanning primer was selected per primer pair. qPCR results were normalized for housekeeper/s expression. PCR amplification was performed using Phusion HF DNA polymerase (Fisher Scientific, 10024537). PCR products were stained with SYBR Safe DNA Gel Stain (Invitrogen, S33102), loaded on a 1% agarose MP gel (Roche, 74078100), and visualized on a BioRad GelDoc.

Western blot

Monocytes were counted, and equal amounts were lysed in Laemmli lysis buffer (0.12 M Tris (pH 6.8), 20% glycerol, 4% SDS), heated at 99 °C for 10 min, and stored at −20 °C until the western blot procedure. After thawing the cell lysates, reducing 6× Laemmli SDS sample buffer (ThermoFisher Scientific, J61337.AD) was added, and the samples were heated again at 99 °C for 2 min. The samples were run on a 12% SDS−PAGE gel and transferred to a PVDF membrane (Immobilon®-P PVDF Membrane, Merck, IPVH00010) using the wet transfer technique. After the transfer, the membrane was blocked in 5% milk (Merck, 1153630500) in TBS-T for 30 min and incubated overnight with the primary antibody (TTRAP Polyclonal antibody, Proteintech, rabbit, 12203-1-AP, Cat: 12203-1-AP, 1:500 dilution; NLRP3 monoclonal antibody adipogen, Cat: AG-20B-0014-C100, Lot: A41812012, clone Cryo-2, 1:1000 dilution; GAPDH monoclonal antibody (GA1R), ThermoFisher Scientific, Cat: MA5-15738, Lot: 21401261, clone GA1R, 1:1000 dilution; or α-tubulin monoclonal antibody, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat: T9026, Lot: 099M4773V, Clone DM1A, 1:1000 dilution) on a roller bench at 4 °C. Next, the membrane was washed 3 × 10 min with TBS-T and subsequently incubated with the secondary antibody (polyclonal swine anti-rabbit immunoglobulins/HRP, DAKO, Cat: P0217, Lot: 41830203; 1:2000 dilution in 0.5% milk in TBS-T and Cat: P0260, Lot: 41326080; 1:1000 dilution in 0.5% milk in TBS-T for GAPDH) for 60 min at room temperature. Afterwards, the membrane was again washed 3 × 10 min with PBS, followed by 1 × 10 min in PBS, after which the protein expression was imaged on the Bio-Rad ChemiDoc imaging system using ECL Western Blotting Substrate (Avantor, RPN2236).

RNA extraction and short-read and long-read RNA-sequencing

Monocytes were lysed in TRIzol LS reagent (Thermo Fisher) directly or after LPS activation. RNA was isolated using the Dynabead mRNA Purification Kit (Thermo Fisher) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For short-read RNA-Seq, the concentration of RNA was quantified using a Qubit RNA HS assay and Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher). Polyadenylated messenger RNA was isolated using Poly(A) beads (NEXTflex), and sequencing libraries were made using the Rapid Directional RNA-seq kit (NEXTflex). Libraries were sequenced at the Utrecht Sequencing Facility using the Nextseq500 platform (Illumina), which produced single-end reads of 75 bp. For Native RNA-Seq, 1 µM mRNA per sample was loaded on MinION flowcells FLO-MIN106 and ran for ~2 h at the Utrecht Sequencing Facility. Raw data was interpreted by Nanopore Technologies sequencing base calling software (Guppy v3.2.10), and reads that passed minimum quality were kept for the analysis. Quality control of the data was assessed using NanoPlot44 (Supplementary data 1).

Short-read RNA-Seq pipeline

Reads were trimmed using Trim Galore v0.6.10 with default parameters and aligned to the reference genome (GENCODE: Release v46 (GRCh38.p14), primary assembly) using STAR v2.7.11b45 in two-pass mode, basic and --quantMode to count reads per gene using htseq-count. Genes with at least a sum of 10 reads in the minimum of samples per group were selected to continue the analysis and biomart v2.60.146 was used for gene annotation. DESeq2 function from the R Bioconductor package DESeq2 v1.44.047 was used to normalize read counts, using the median of ratios method. DESeq read counts were modelled using a negative binomial distribution with the formula ~treatment + donor_id, to correct condition-specific effects by donor-specific differences. DESeq was used to identify differentially expressed genes, performing a Wald test: p-values were obtained using the results function and adjusted for multiple testing, using the false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. log2FC were shrunken using the default and recommended apeglm v1.26.148 algorithm of the lfcShrink function. Genes with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and at least a double difference of expression between evaluated conditions (|shrunken log2FC| > 1) were considered as differentially expressed. Functional enrichment analysis was performed for overexpressed genes using gprofiler2 v0.2.349 correcting for multiple testing by FDR, and significance was considered with an adjusted p-value < 0.05. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis was performed using fgsea v1.30.0. For our short-read RNA-Seq analysis, 8 biological samples per condition (0 h and LPS activation) were used, achieving a power of detection of true positives of 0.889, using RNASeqPower v1.44.0 with an average gene coverage depth of 16.49 and a coefficient of variation of 0.28 and 0.42 for 0 and 3 h LPS, respectively. The same bioinformatics pipeline was used for publicly available inpatient endotoxin challenge data (GSE87290)15.

Oxford Nanopore long-read RNA pipeline

flair v2.0.050 was used to identify a confident full-length isoform-level reference transcriptome in monocytes. Specifically, flair align was used to align long reads to the reference genome (GENCODE: Release v46 (GRCh38.p14), primary assembly) using minimap2 v2.2451 with long-read-specific parameters and --nvrna (recommended for native-RNA). Quality control of the alignment was assessed using NanoPlot44 (Supplementary data 1). flair correct with --nvrna parameter was used to correct misaligned splice sites using the reference genome annotations and short-read splice junctions. Short-read splice junctions were extracted from the STAR junction output of our short-read RNA-Seq dataset, with a custom script that keeps strand-specific information. flair collapse was used to collapse corrected reads of all samples in high-confidence isoforms using the criteria: transcripts that have at least 3 full-length supporting reads (80% coverage and spanning 25 bp of the first and last exons) in total with the --stringent option, enforcing a 4 out of 6 bp coverage around each splice site and no insertions greater than 3 bp with the --check-splice option, keeping isoforms that are a proper subset of another isoform with the --filter comprehensive option, and transcripts whose 5′ end was located within 100 bp from the TSS annotated by refTSS32 and/or TSSclassifier (“relaxed” or “strict”), which are based on the FANTOM CAGE (Cap Analysis of Gene Expression) peak33 with the --promoters option. This strict last option was used to avoid false-positive TSS annotations caused by RNA degradation, frequent in long-read RNA-sequencing. Supporting the novel transcriptome TSS annotations, most reads initiated within ±50 bp of the annotated TSS (Supplementary Fig. 10a, b), and significantly more reads started within ±50 bp of the annotated TSS than at the internal position of the isoform with the highest read density not corresponding to a TSS (Supplementary Fig. 10c). Isoforms supported by at least 3 strand-specific full-length reads were classified as novel when they contained novel splice junction chains and/or different TSS/TES. Isoforms were then assigned to genes based on the annotated gene sharing the greatest number of splice junctions with the isoform. Isoforms that could not be assigned to any annotated gene were grouped in novel loci.

SQANTI3 v5.2.152, including annotated TSS33 and poly(A) sites53, was used to assess the quality of our novel transcriptome and to predict ORF using GeneMarkS-T v5.154. SQANTI3 was also used to filter and rescue (with default filtering, except RT-Switching) to create the final novel transcriptome. Isoform productivity was detected by using the flair v2.0.050 additional programme mark_productivity. SUPPA v2.355 was used to detect splicing events (generateEvents) and to perform the differential splicing event analysis (psiPerEvent and diffSplice) using tpm values per isoform obtained using the flair v2.0.050 quantify (--tpm) command. Exon-skipping (SE) events detected by SUPPA were analysed for splicing-related motif enrichment. Genomic regions spanning ±250 bp around the start and end of the included exon, as well as the end of the upstream exon and the start of the downstream exon, were extracted. Motif enrichment was assessed with default parameters (CISBP-RNA database for Homo sapiens) using Motif Enrichment Analysis (AME, v5.5.9) from the MEME Suite56 for SE events showing increased exon inclusion after activation compared with 0 h. All SE events without significant changes after activation were selected as the control set. Gffcompare v0.12.657 was used to compare our transcriptome with TRAILS13 and FLIBase21.

Differential gene and isoform expression, and switching analysis

Both Oxford Nanopore long-read RNA sequencing and short-read RNA-Seq sequences (ours and GSE10350122) were re-aligned to the novel transcriptome (including both novel and annotated isoforms) using salmon v1.10.358 (--ont parameter for long-read sequencing quantification of minimap2 aligned reads), thereby ensuring improved comparability across datasets. tximport v1.32.059 was used to import transcript abundance at the gene and isoform level. Differential gene and isoform expression were performed with DESeq2 v1.44.047 as explained before. Differential Isoform Switching was performed using IsoformSwitchAnalyzeR v2.4.060, with the default pre-filtering (cutoff of isoform usage of 0.01 and minimum gene and isoform expression of 1 and 0, respectively), and analysis was performed via DEXSeq61 with the recommended q-value < 0.05 and absolute dIF > 0.1 cutoffs. Sequence and structural consequences of the isoform switching events were analysed using IsoformSwitchAnalyzeR60 and the analysis was expanded by including predicted protein domains using Pfam v1.662, predicted coding potential using CPAT v3.0.563, protein topology predictions using TMHMM v2.064, intrinsically disordered regions (IDR) and intrinsically disordered binding regions (IDBR) predictions using IUPred365 and signal peptides using SignalP v6.066.

Ribo-Seq and matching RNA-Seq data—GSE208041

Ribo-Seq and RNA-Seq23 from THP-1 cells were used, a well-used human monocytic model that retains key functional and molecular characteristics of primary monocytes. Reads were trimmed using Trim Galore v0.6.10 with default parameters. Ribo-Seq reads were filtered for contaminants by aligning reads to Human 5S DNA (GenBank: X12811.1) and Human ribosomal DNA complete repeating unit (GenBank: U13369.1) using STAR v2.7.11b45. Ribo-Seq and RNA-Seq were aligned to the novel transcriptome (novel and annotated isoforms detected using nanopore long-read RNA-Seq). For Ribo-Seq data, bowtie v1.3.167 was used to get the best uniquely mapped read (-m1 --best --strata). For RNA-Seq data, bowtie2 v2.5.468 with the --very-sensitive option was used, and uniquely mapped reads (minimum alignment quality of 30) were kept using samtools v1.2069 (-q 30). samtools v1.2069 idxstats was used to count reads per isoform. To evaluate the effect of the use of uniquely mapped reads, and considering that THP-1 may express slightly different isoforms than monocytes, THP-1 RNA-Seq reads were also aligned to the reference transcriptome using salmon v1.10.358. Excluding isoforms considered not expressed in THP-1 (0 count using salmon), we were able to detect ~80% of the remaining isoforms using uniquely aligned reads in the RNA-Seq (Supplementary Fig. 10d), and these detected isoforms were then assessed for their translation ability in the Ribo-Seq. Considering that isoforms not detectable using this approach were significantly longer than isoforms detected as transcribed, there was no bias in this approach due to isoform length (Supplementary Fig. 10e).

Mass spectrometry data

Proteins (n = 1 for 3 h LPS and 0 h) were denatured and alkylated in 50 µL 8 M urea, 1 M ammonium bicarbonate (ABC) containing 10 mM TCEP (tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine hydrochloride) and 40 mM 2-chloro-acetamide and separated from the beads. After 4-fold dilution with 1 M ABC and digestion with trypsin (20 µg)(Promega), peptides were desalted with homemade C-18 stage tips (3M, St. Paul, MN), eluted with 80% acetonitrile (ACN) and, after evaporation of the solvent in the speedvac, redissolved in Britton & Robinson buffer composed of 20 mM acetic acid, 20 mM phosphoric acid, and 20 mM boric acid titrated with NaOH to pH 11 for Sax fractionation and loaded on a stage tip with SAX material (3M, St Paul, MN). 7 Fractions (pH = 11, 8, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2) of 50 µL were loaded subsequently on a c18 stage tip after acidification with 20 µL 20% formic acid. Peptides were eluted with buffer B (80% ACN, 0.1% FA) and dried in the speedvac and resolved in A (0.1% formic acid). After separation on a 30 cm pico-tip column (75 µm ID, New Objective) in-house packed with C-18 material (1.9 µm aquapur gold, Dr. Maisch) using a 270 min gradient (7–80% ACN, 0.1% FA), delivered by an easy-nLC 1200 (Thermo), peptides were electro-sprayed directly into an Orbitrap Eclipse Tribrid Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Scientific), which was run in DDA mode with a cycle time of 1 s, with the FAIMS compensation voltage (CV) switching between −45 and −65 V. The full scan (400–1400 mass range) was performed at a resolution of 240,000. Ions reaching an intensity threshold of 10E4 were isolated by the quadrupole with a 0.7 Da window, fragmented with a HCD collision energy of 30% and measured in a de Iontrap in rapid mode with a maximum injection time of 40 ms.

After splitting the raw files into the two CV channels with Freestyle Software (Thermo), analysis of the raw data was performed with MaxQuant v2.6.4.0, using the standard settings and the Uniprot database for Homo sapiens (December 2021) and a monocyte-specific analysis with the predicted protein sequences from our novel transcriptome. Oxidation of Methionine, and Acetylation of protein N-termini were set as variable modifications, while carbamidomethylation (C) was set as a fixed modification, and 1 missed cleavage was allowed. Precursor mass tolerance was set to 4.5 ppm and fragment mass tolerance was set to 0.5 Da. Peptide length was confined to 7–25 amino acids, and the allowed FDR level on both the peptide and protein level was set to 1%.

For PXD00435224, mass spectrometry (MS) raw files were re-analysed using MaxQuant software v2.5.1.0, the parameters described in the paper from which the data were obtained and using as a reference all Uniprot human protein sequences and the predicted protein sequences from our novel transcriptome. LFQ protein abundance per gene was calculated from the proteinGroups.txt output by summing the label-free quantification intensities of proteins mapping uniquely to a single gene, when considering only “reviewed” UniProt human protein entries and predicted protein sequences from our novel transcriptome in the ‘Majority protein IDs’ column. For the publicly available dataset, values for each condition were obtained by computing the median LFQ intensity across all Classical Monocyte replicates (n = 3 for LPS and n = 4 0 h) in which the protein was detected. Functional peptides were extracted from the peptide.txt output by identifying the predicted protein sequences from our novel transcriptome to which each peptide belongs, and then assigning each sequence to its corresponding isoform functionality. Only peptides associated with a single functionality were considered ‘unique’. Pearson correlation was then calculated between LFQ intensity values per gene (computed as described above) from PXD004352 and RPKM values (htseq-coun quantification) from our short-read RNA-Seq data for 0 h and LPS (12 h protein abundance and 3 h mRNA amounts) conditions, separately.

PLM-based pseudolikelihood scores

The PLM pseudolikelihoods were calculated by summing up the log-scaled probability of every residue and dividing this by the sequence length based on the PLM ESM1b70. The following pipeline was used to get the ESM1b pseudolikelihoods: https://github.com/dvginneken/PLM-pipeline. Next, the isoform PLM pseudolikelihood scores were weighted by the usage of that isoform to compute the weighted PLM pseudolikelihood score.
