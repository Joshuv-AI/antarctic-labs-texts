Generation and applications of an expandable and mature hiPSC-derived liver organoid
Authors: Sen Liu, Ze Wang, Liuyang Zhu, Libo Wang, Tianyu Zhao, Pinsheng Han, Yongdeng Xu, Long Yang, Lei Hu, Fengying Yan, Xiaoliang Wang, Tao Cui, Chunli Li, Baofeng Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Organoids have emerged as a powerful tool for modeling liver diseases, drug screening, and personalized treatments. However, they have a limited capacity to generate functional hepatocytes in a reproducible and efficient manner. Here, we designed a novel method to efficiently and reproducibly establish a protocol for generating functionally mature SB-HEOs (SB431542/BMP4-hepatic endoderm organoids) from hiPSC-derived hepatic endoderm (HE) cells. The maturation of these organoids was confirmed through transcriptome analysis and functional expression detection. We extended this culture system to various biomedical applications. This system can be used to identify hepatotoxicity with DILI drugs, model disease using OA stress, metabolize drugs using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry, and repopulate FRG mice. These organoids have both expansion and maturation characteristics, high drug metabolism ability to prolong the survival of FRG mice, can accurately identify hepatotoxic and non-hepatotoxic drugs, and mimic metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease. Thus, SB-HEO provide a new cellular system for toxicology testing, drug metabolism, modeling liver diseases, and regenerative medicine. Especially benefiting from the high expression of CYP450 activity, SB-HEO shows high potential in the fields of drug testing and regenerative medicine.

Introduction

The liver maintains metabolic and physiological homeostases by performing critical functions of metabolism, synthesis of proteins and bile1. Given the liver's unique regenerative capability in vivo, the quintessential model for assessing liver metabolism in vitro is primary human hepatocytes (PHHs), although they have limited proliferation capacity and long-term functional loss2. This underscores the urgent need for cell-based, individualized in vitro liver models to test efficacy and toxicity in preclinical drug development. hPSC-derived liver lineages are considered an optimal source for such therapy and drug development3,4.

In recent years, the hepatocytic differentiation of hPSCs has been achieved and improved5, 6, 7. However, substantial hepatocyte populations are difficult to obtain, especially for drug efficacy and safety evaluation. Hepatic progenitor cells (HPCs) can self-renew and differentiate into hepatocytes and cholangiocytes. hPSC-derived HPC expansion has been reported8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13. The 3D culture facilitates hepatic differentiation of hPSCs14, 15, 16. Three-dimensional organoids originate from stem cells that can self-renew and organize to simulate organ structure and function17. Highly expandable sources of hepatic endodermal organoids have been generated from hPSCs18, 19, 20. Choi's group21 have reported a protocol for directly converting PHHs from healthy donor livers to bipotent progenitor cells, which provide a new personalized stem cell-mediated system for toxicology testing, regenerative medicine, and modeling liver diseases22,23. However, it is difficult to induce mature hepatocytes from these liver stem/progenitor cells, resulting in fewer ALB-positive cells with low metabolic activity. Low expression levels of metabolic genes characteristic of adult liver function hamper their use in studying metabolic regulation and in trials of liver disease treatments.

In this study, SB431542 and BMP4 were used instead of the commonly used FGF2 and BMP4 in the HE generation step to establish an expandable HEO with multiple functional properties. The use of SB431542 makes SB-HEO more characteristic of hepatocytes and more suitable for metabolic and disease modeling studies. These liver organoids displayed higher maturity than the 2D hepatocytes and organoids generated using other methods. Notably, this system offers a reproducible robust hepatic model for toxicology testing, modeling liver diseases, drug metabolism, and regenerative medicine.

Materials and methods

Reagents

Troglitazone (HY-50935), benzbromarone (HY-B1135), acetaminophen (APAP, HY-66005), trovafloxacin (HY-A0170), cyclosporin A (HY-B0579), rifampicin (HY-B0272), rosiglitazone (HY-17386), levofloxacin (HY-B0330), elafibranor (HY-16737), liraglutide (HY-P0014), metformin (HY-B0627), obeticholic acid (HY-12222), saroglitazar (HY-19937), Nile red (HY-D0718), and sodium oleate (HY-N1446B) were purchased from MCE.

Human iPSC culture

Human iPSC (F1 hiPSC, Cellapy, China) was derived from human dermal fibroblasts. The CB, PM3, PM342, PM849 and PM853 were gifted by Prof. Tao Cheng, Institute of Hematology and Blood Diseases Hospital of Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College (Tianjin, China). The hiPSCs were maintained in mTeSR Plus (Stem Cell Technologies, USA) on Matrigel-coated dishes at 37 °C in 5% CO2, and passaged as described previously20.

Small molecule-mediated hepatic differentiation

HPCs were generated from hiPSCs24. First, definitive endoderm (DE) induction was started. Briefly, when hiPSCs approached 70% confluence, the medium was substituted with RPMI-1640 (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 1 × B27 without insulin (Invitrogen, USA), 3 μmol/L CHIR99021, 5 μmol/L IDE1, and 0.75 μmol/L PD0332991 (CIP) for 1 day, and then cultured for the next 2 days in the same medium with 1 μmol/L CHIR99021. For differentiation to HPCs, DE cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 containing 1 × B27 and small-molecule cocktail VDF24 for 4 days. For the maturation of hepatocytes, HPCs were cultured as previously described24. The small molecules were procured from MCE.

hiPSC-derived liver organoid generation and expansion

HE and HLCs mediated by small molecules and cytokines were detached by Accutase (Gibco, USA) at 37 °C for 10 min. Detached HLCs were embedded in 40 μL Matrigel (BD Biosciences, USA) at 5000 cells/well in 48-well plates (Corning, USA) and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min to allow gelation. Thereafter, generation medium (GM) was added, which comprised of Advanced DMEM/F12 with specific concentrations of supplements as described previously20. GlutaMAX, HEPES, N2, and B27 were purchased from Gibco. N-Acetylcysteine, human gastrin I, nicotinamide, A83-01, Forskolin, and CHIR99021 were purchased from MCE. FGF10, HGF, R-spondin1, and EGF were purchased from Novoprotein. The GM was changed every 3 days. The cells were cultured for 14–15 days at 37 °C in 5% CO2.

Organoid passaging from HEO, preservation and culture were conducted as described previously20. HEO were passaged using expansion medium (EM) with GM minus R-spondin 1 and EGF. The ROCK inhibitor was purchased from MCE.

PHH and HepG2 culture

PHHs and HepG2 were cultured as described previously20. MEM, fetal bovine serum and penicillin–streptomycin were procured from Gibco.

Immunofluorescence staining of 2D cells and organoids

For the immunocytochemical analysis of 2D cell cultures, the cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in DPBS for 15 min at room temperature (RT). Thereafter, the cells were permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 for 15 min at RT. Samples were blocked with 4% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in DPBS for 1 h at RT and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The samples were stained using a 0.05% solution of Tween-20 (Sigma–Aldrich, USA) in DPBS and subsequently incubated with Alexa Fluor®-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at RT. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (Sigma–Aldrich, USA). Fluorescence images were captured with an Olympus microscope.

For immunofluorescence staining of organoids, they were isolated from Matrigel with cell recovery solution (Corning, USA), fixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4 °C, and subsequently paraffin-embedded. After deparaffinizing, rehydrating, and incubating in a sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for antigen retrieval, the samples were permeabilized, blocked with 5% normal serum (Gibco, USA), incubated with each primary antibody overnight at 4 °C, followed by fluorescein-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h. Nuclei were stained with DAPI. Images were taken by Leica STELLARIS 5 microscope. Supporting Information Table S1 shows the antibodies used in these experiments.

RT-PCR and real-time PCR

TransZol Up Plus RNA kit (TransGen Biotech, China) was used for extracting total RNA from each sample that was subsequently reverse-transcribed utilizing Transcriptor first strand cDNA synthesis kit (Roche, Switzerland). FastStart Universal SYBR Green Master (Rox) (Roche, Switzerland) was used for qPCR on LightCycler 480 II (Roche, Switzerland). The PCR results of each sample in triplicate are displayed as relative fold changes quantified by 2–ΔΔCt compared to the control. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used for normalization. The ΔCt (ΔCt2) was calculated as the target gene Ct minus that of GAPDH. The reference (ΔCt1) was that of control cells. Primers used in these experiments are listed in Supporting Information Table S2.

Flow cytometry

Suspensions of single cells were prepared with Accutase at 37 °C for 3–5 min and stained in DPBS for 30 min at 4 °C for analyzing surface antigens. Thereafter, 4% PFA-fixed and 0.1% Triton X-100-permeabilized cells were stained for intercellular protein analyses. Blocking was performed with 10% goat or donkey serum. The cells were incubated with conjugated primary antibodies for 1 h at 4 °C, and strained with a 70 μm cell strainer. BD FACSCelesta (BD Biosciences, USA) was used for sorting the stained cells, and subsequent data analysis was performed using FlowJo software. Supporting Information Table S3 shows the details of antibodies and isotype controls.

Functional studies

Indocyanine green (ICG) uptake and release was observed as described previously23. To analyze glycogen storage, paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated. Samples were stained with a periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) stain kit (Solarbio, China), as per the manufacturer's instructions. For albumin (ALB), alpha-1 antitrypsin (AAT), and urea quantification, 48 h after the medium change, the medium was collected and tested with a human ALB ELISA kit (Abcam, UK), a human AAT ELISA kit (Elabscience, China), and a urea assay kit (Abcam, UK), respectively. The absorbance was measured on SpectraMax M3 (Molecular Devices, USA), and normalized by the cell number. CYP3A4 activity measurements were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions (Promega, USA). For the CYP3A4 induction experiments, 20 μmol/L of rifampicin was used for 48 h. Data were normalized by cell number.

Transplantation of SB-HEO and PHHs into FRG mice

All experiments were under IACUC approval and adherence to the institutional guidelines of the Committee on the Ethics of Animal Experiments of Shenyang Pharmaceutical University (Approval number: IACUC-2312001). For implanting SB-HEO and PHHs into male, 6–8 weeks old, Fah−/−/Rag2−/−/IL2rg−/− (FRG) mice (Charles River, USA), 1 × 106 cell suspension of SB-HEO or PHHs in Matrigel (30 μL) was injected into the hepatic left lower lobe (SB-HEO: n = 10, PHHs: n = 10). Post-transplantation, NTBC was progressively decreased in drinking water from 100%, 25%, 12%, 6%, and finally stopped. After the mice lost nearly 20% weight, 50% NTBC was given for three days. FRG mice were sacrificed 3 months post-transplantation. Human serum ALB ELISA kit (Abcam, UK) was used to measure ALB levels.

Measurement of CYP450 activity by LC–MS/MS

CYP450 activity was measured in SB-HEO, PHHs, HepG2, and 2D HLCs by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) as described previously20.

RNA-seq and data analysis

To obtain high-quality cDNA libraries for transcriptome analysis, the 3′-end polyA structure of mRNA and various molecular biology techniques were used for mRNA separation, fragmentation, double-stranded cDNA synthesis, cDNA fragmentation modification, magnetic bead purification and fragmentation sorting, library amplification and other processing on the six complete total RNA samples in this project. A sequencing library appropriate for the Illumina platform was obtained after testing and quality control.

For the analysis of RNA-seq data, the Trimmomatic software26 was used to perform quality control on the original reads and remove adapter sequences and low-quality reads. The trimmed reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) with the HISAT2 software27 and annotated and quantified using the StringTie software28. The R package edgeR29 was used for differential gene expression analysis on samples from different groups. Genes that meet the false discovery rate (FDR) corrected P-value <0.05 and the absolute value of fold change >4 are defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the R package clusterProfiler30, and the reference gene set was from the MSigDb database31. Thereafter, GO and KEGG functional enrichment analyses of the DEGs were conducted. A corrected P-value <0.05 demonstrated statistical significance. The RNA-Seq data are accessible through the NCBI (accession number: PRJNA1282801).

Astral DIA quantitative proteomics

Sample preparation

SDT buffer comprising of 4% SDS and 100 mmol/L Tris-HCl (pH 7.6) was added to the samples directly. The lysates were sonicated and boiled for 15 min. The supernatant was obtained by centrifuging at 14,000×g for 40 min, and the protein concentration was measured with the BCA protein assay kit. Subsequently, 15 μg of each protein sample was mixed with 5 × loading buffer, boiled for 5 min, separated by SDS-PAGE (4%–20% gel, 180 V, 45 min), and stained with Coomassie Blue R-250.

Filter-aided sample preparation

Samples were treated with 40 mmol/L DTT at 37 °C for 1.5 h with mixing (600 rpm), then cooled to RT before adding 20 mmol/L IAA to block reduced cysteine residues, followed by incubation for 30 min in the dark. The samples were transferred to individual filters (microcon units, 10 kDa), washed three times with 100 μL of UA buffer, followed by two washes with 100 μL of 25 mmol/L NH4HCO3 buffer. The samples were incubated overnight at 37 °C after treatment with trypsin at 1:50 (w/w) trypsin-to-protein ratio. The filtrates containing the peptides were desalted with C18 cartridges (Empore™ SPE Cartridges MCX, 30 μm, Waters), concentrated by vacuum centrifugation, and reconstituted in 0.1% (v/v) formic acid (40 μL). The absorbance was measured at 280 nm using UV spectroscopy to determine the peptide concentration. For data-independent acquisition (DIA) experiments, indexed retention time (iRT) calibration peptides were spiked into the sample.

Mass spectrometry assay for data-independent acquisition

Peptides were analyzed in the DIA mode by Orbitrap™ Astral™ mass spectrometer coupled to a Vanquish Neo system liquid chromatography (both from Thermo Scientific). Precursor ions were scanned at m/z 380–980, MS1 resolution: 240,000 at m/z 200, maximum IT: 5 ms, normalized AGC target: 500%. In MS2 scanning, 299 windows were set for DIA mode, HCD collision energy: 25eV, isolation window: 2 m/z, maximum IT: 3 ms, normalized AGC target: 500%.

Mass spectrometry data analysis

DIA-NN 1.8.1 was used to analyze the DIA data. The main software parameters were established as follows: trypsin was selected as the enzyme, and a maximum of one missed cleavage was allowed. Carbamidomethyl (C) was designated as the fixed modification, while acetylation (Protein N-terminus) and oxidation (M) were chosen as dynamic modifications. All reported protein identifications have an FDR ≤1% (99% confidence level).

PCA

The processed data were normalized to total peak intensity and uploaded to SIMCA-P (version 14.1, Umetrics, Umeå, Sweden), and thereafter underwent multivariate data analyses, including Pareto-scaled principal component analysis (PCA).

Cluster analysis

Hierarchical clustering analysis was performed with Cluster 3.0 (http://bonsai.hgc.jp/mdehoon/software/cluster/software.htm) and Java Treeview softwares (http://jtreeview.sourceforge.net). The Euclidean distance algorithm was selected for measuring similarity. The average linkage clustering algorithm, which utilizes centroids of observations, was chosen for hierarchical clustering. A heat map was frequently presented as a visual aid in addition to the dendrogram.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq)

The cell suspension was loaded into Chromium microfluidic chips with 3′ v3 chemistry and barcoded with a 10 × Chromium Controller (10X Genomics). RNA from the barcoded cells was subsequently reverse-transcribed and sequencing libraries constructed with reagents from a Chromium Single Cell 3′ v3 reagent kit (10X Genomics) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Sequencing was performed with Illumina (NovaSeq) according to the manufacturer's instructions (Illumina, USA).

For scRNA-seq analysis, the fastq files were processed using Cell Ranger (v5.0.1) with default settings. The reads were then aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38). Cell doublets were removed using Scrublet. Downstream analysis was performed with Seurat (v5.0.1). Cells with fewer than 2000 detected genes or more than 20% of UMIs mapped to mitochondrial genes were excluded. The filtered gene expression matrix was normalized using the “NormalizeData” method. The top 2000 most variable genes were identified using the FindVariableFeatures function. The normalized data were then scaled using the ScaleData function. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the identified highly variable genes. Unsupervised cell clustering was conducted using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was used for visualizing the identified cell clusters. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in each cluster were identified using the FindAllMarkers function, comparing each cluster to all other cells. Gene ontology (GO) analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler package. For the liver tissue samples obtained from GEO (GSE115469), the gene expression matrices were integrated with those of organoids using Seurat. Batch correction, data scaling, clustering, and visualization were then carried out. The scRNA-Seq data are accessible through the NCBI (accession number: PRJNA1307746).

ATP assay and reduced (GSH) and oxidized (GSSG) glutathione quantification

Enhanced ATP Assay Kit (Beyotime, China) was used to measure changes in ATP levels and the data were normalized to protein concentration. GSH and GSSG levels were measured using the GSH/GSSG Assay kit (Promega, USA) and the data were normalized using the number of cells per million. Perform experiments according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Statistical analysis

The figures display representative data as the mean ± standard error of mean (SEM) from three or more independent biological replicates. For PCR, toxicity test, ELISA, and Nile red staining, triplicate samples (technical replicates) were used. Inter-group comparisons were made with Student's t-test. Results

Establishing expandable SB-HEO from hiPSC

Hepatic development-based differentiation protocols outlined by Gao24, Hay25, and Park20 were employed with minor modifications to obtain HE and HLCs from hiPSC. Briefly, hiPSC was differentiated stepwise into DE-, HE-differentiated cells, and HLCs (Fig. 1A). Among the organoids generated by several methods, SB-HEO had the highest expression of hepatic progenitor cells and was selected for subsequent experiments (Fig. 1B). Supporting Information Fig. S1A shows bright-field images of each stage in the SB-induced protocol, revealing the differences and characteristics between them. Each stage-specific differentiation marker expression was assessed at the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. S1B and S1C). The cells in each stage were heterogeneous (Fig. S1D). Supporting Information Fig. S2 presents bright-field images for each stage of the SM and FB induction protocols. To demonstrate the robustness of the SB induction protocol, umbilical cord blood monocyte-derived hiPSC (CB) and peripheral blood monocyte-derived hiPSC (PM3, PM342, PM849, PM853) were selected for differentiation at various stages (Fig. S2). Approximately 17 days post-differentiation, organoids were generated in GM and expanded in EM. In the Matrigel-embedded EM culture, 3D organoids exhibited significant enlargement over a five-day culture and robust regrowth following freeze-thaw cycles (Fig. 1C). Immunofluorescence assays revealed that the SB-HEO were EPCAM, AFP, and CK19 positive (Fig. 1D). The organoids demonstrated self-renewal in Matrigel and expansion (more than 20 times passaging) in long-term culture (over 5 months) (Fig. 1E). The HPC stage of SB-HEO was confirmed by the expression of SOX9, EPCAM, CK19, HNF4A, and AFP (Fig. 1F).

Hepatic differentiation potential of SB-HEO in vitro

The potential in vitro differentiation of SB-HEO into functional hepatocytes was investigated and the culture conditions were established by modifying a previously reported method32. SB-HEO showed an enlarged spherical structure, while the Differentiation medium-cultured organoids (SB-HEO-DM) showed smaller and denser morphology (Fig. 2A). In contrast to undifferentiated SB-HEO and 2D HLCs, liver-specific genes (AFP, HNF4A, AAT, TAT, G6PC, TF, ALB, and MRP2), urea cycle enzyme genes (CPS1, ASS1, OTC, ARG1, and ASL), and drug metabolism genes (CAR, PXR, FXR, CYP2C9, CYP3A4, and CYP2E1) were upregulated in SB-HEO-DM, and some of these genes showed comparable expression levels to that in PHHs (Fig. 2B). Immunostaining also showed that SB-HEO-DM expressed mature liver markers (ALB, HNF4A) (Fig. 2C). Key indicators of hepatocyte-specific functions were observed in SB-HEO-DM, namely significantly higher ICG uptake and release, as well as glycogen storage (PAS) (Fig. 2D and E). Furthermore, ALB secretion, AAT secretion and urea production were significantly increased in the SB-HEO-DM compared to the SB-HEO, yet did not reach the levels found in the PHHs (Fig. 2F), indicating that the induction of mature hepatic phenotypic traits needs improvement. It is worth noting that the mature functions of PHHs disappear over time (Supporting Information Fig. S3). Moreover, the DM-cultured organoids were enriched in the pathways of bile acid metabolism and drug metabolism cytochrome P450, which is characteristic of maturation (Fig. 2G and H). Detailed comparison of these populations was performed by analyzing selected collections of transcripts, including a subset of phase I and phase II drug metabolism enzymes, transporters, general liver enzymes, lipoproteins, coagulation factors and nuclear receptors. The results indicate higher expression of many genes in DM-cultured organoids compared to EM-cultured organoids (Supporting Information Fig. S4). Transcriptome and proteome analyses of SB-HEO

We referred to an RNA sequencing-based LiGEP algorithm developed by Cho's group33, which can quantitatively assess and present the liver similarity degree. While 2D HLCs showed a liver similarity of 26.88%, SB-HEO and SB-HEO-DM showed 51.61%, and up to 62.37% similarity, respectively (Fig. 3A). Fig. 3B shows the gene expression levels in each sample, which are listed in Supporting Information Table S4. The mulberry plot shows some metabolic pathways, and a few representative genes in the pathways are shown on the left (Fig. 3C). The DEGs between the two groups (SB-HEO/2D HLCs) were enriched by GO analysis, which showed that the enriched items included not only xenobiotic metabolic process and lipid metabolism reflecting mature function but also DNA replication reflecting proliferation ability (Fig. 3D). The seven liver-associated gene sets are shown in heat maps reflecting the liver functional maturation of SB-HEO (Fig. 3E). The GSEA plot also shows that SB-HEO are more mature than 2D HLCs (Supporting Information Fig. S5A). Notably, compared with 2D HLCs, SB-HEO have self-renewal ability (Fig. S5B). The proteomic data demonstrate the scalability and maturity of SB-HEO at the protein level (Supporting Information Fig. S6). Therefore, SB-HEO serve as a relevant functionally mature hepatic system to examine various downstream applications. These applications are performed in EM because EM-cultured organoids have sufficiently mature liver function in long-term culture. Single-cell transcriptome analysis of SB-HEO

To investigate the cellular composition of SB-HEOs, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to analyze the transcriptome of SB-HEOs. The cluster-wise expression patterns revealed 22 cell clusters (Fig. 4A). Based on gene expression patterns, cells in SB-HEOs were divided into four distinct cell clusters, representing hepatocytes, cholangiocytes, fetal hepatocytes, and liver mesenchymal cells (Fig. 4B and C). respectively, and each cell cluster expressed its own lineage-specific genes (Fig. 4B and C). Based on this cell type classification, SB-HEOs contained 57.94% HLCs, 18.36% biliary-like cells, 11% fetal hepatic-like cells, and 12.7% hepatic mesenchymal-like cells (Fig. 4D). Representative markers for each lineage were expressed almost exclusively in each population (Fig. 4E). To compare cells composed of SB-HEOs and human liver, we integrated the SB-HEOs single-cell data into a previously published single-cell transcriptome dataset of human tissue (GSE115469). Prediction of hepatotoxic drugs using SB-HEO

The value of SB-HEO as a cellular system for toxicity testing was determined by comparing its drug responsiveness with other cell models in vitro. Dose–response curves were used to compare the cytotoxicity of six hepatotoxic drugs between SB-HEO, SB-HEO-DM, HepG2 cells, PHHs, and 2D HLCs (Fig. 5A). For the six drugs tested, SB-HEO showed the highest sensitivity at the lowest concentrations and closest hierarchical clustering among the tested models (Fig. 5B). The bright-field images of the different concentrations corresponding to the dose–response curve are shown in Supporting Information Fig. S8. The hepatotoxic specificity in SB-HEO was determined by comparing DILI-negative with positive structural analogs, namely rosiglitazone vs. troglitazone and levofloxacin vs. trovafloxacin. Troglitazone is an antidiabetic drug that was withdrawn from the market owing to severe idiosyncratic hepatotoxicity34. The organoid SB-HEO developed in this study could identify its toxicity (Fig. 5C). Thereafter, the effects of trovafloxacin and levofloxacin35,36 were compared23. As anticipated, levofloxacin did not exhibit cytotoxicity at concentrations exceeding the maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) of 23.8 μmol/L in SB-HEO cells (Fig. 5C). However, trovafloxacin significantly reduced organoid viability (Fig. 5C), which was not observed in conventional 2D models34. Meanwhile, levofloxacin exhibited toxicity at 25 × Cmax, which resulted in a 65% reduction in organoid cell viability (Fig. 5C). Regenerative and inflammatory responses in SB-HEO

Since changes in regenerative capacity are a key indicator of liver damage, inflammatory and regenerative responses of the organoids were assessed following large-dose APAP treatment (Fig. 6A). Following five days of APAP (20 mmol/L) treatment, the organoids exhibited significant morphological injury (Fig. 6B). However, notable recovery on Day 7 was observed post-medium change on Day 4, 48 h post-APAP exposure (Fig. 6A). The size of damaged organoids recovered (Fig. 6C). Epithelial responses like the activation of damage-associated molecular patterns induces inflammation, which subsequently promotes either regeneration or pathological damage37. Prolonged detrimental conditions may block autophagy-dependent recovery. Although this organoid system lacks niche cells, including immune cells, it recapitulates the inflammatory response post-injury. In the APAP-treated organoids, there was a pronounced induction of proinflammatory IL1B and IL8, as well as the pathological mediator TNFA. Notably, the anti-inflammatory IL10 expression was significantly elevated by Day 5 of the recovery phase (Fig. 6D). SB-HEO exhibited a pattern analogous to that of ATP content (Fig. 6E) and the GSH/GSSG ratio (Fig. 6F). Hence, SB-HEO could be used to examine inflammatory and regenerative responses post-hepatotoxicity. HMGB1 is a marker of cell necrosis. After 48 h of APAP treatment, high expression of HMGB1 was observed in organoids, indicating that APAP induced necrosis of SB-HEOs (Fig. 6G). Drug screening and steatosis in SB-HEO

The liver is responsible for detoxification and maintaining metabolic homeostasis. We investigated the organoids as a potential model for NAFLD, which is characterized by excessive lipid accumulation in the liver. To model these conditions in vitro, free fatty acids uptake and storage as lipid droplets by hepatocytes are essential. Hence, SB-HEO were incubated for 3 days with 200, 400, 600 or 800 μmol/L oleic acid (OA) or with fatty acid-free BSA (vehicle control). Exposure to the four tested concentrations of OA did not alter cell viability measured as ATP levels (Supporting Information Fig. S9A), but increased the intracellular lipid accumulation compared to the BSA control (Fig. 7A). The intracellular triglyceride concentration was significantly elevated following OA treatment compared to the BSA control (Fig. 7B). Triglyceride concentration increased after OA administration compared with the control group (Fig. 7C). Having observed the induction of intra-HEO steatosis and the accumulation of triglycerides, we investigated whether this condition could be mitigated by five compounds that efficiently treat MASH, namely elafibranor, liraglutide, metformin, obeticholic acid, and saroglitazar. All five compounds inhibited steatosis development by at least 20% (P < 0.05) when compared to untreated controls exposed solely to OA (Fig. 7D and E). Only obeticholic acid at physiologically relevant concentrations failed to prevent spheroid fat accumulation. Therapeutic potential of SB-HEO in vivo

To assess functional and stable integration of SB-HEO into the liver parenchyma, single cells were isolated and transplanted into the livers of Fah−/−/Rag2−/−/Il2rg−/− (FRG) mice, which are an established model for in vivo liver transplantation experiments41. All untransplanted FRG mice died within 4 weeks (Fig. 9A). In contrast, 7 out of 10 SB-HEO-transplanted FRG mice survived for over 2 months (Fig. 9A), which was comparable to the PHH-transplanted FRG mice. The early weight loss observed in SB-HEO-transplanted mice was alleviated 5 weeks post-transplantation, and the body weight stabilized afterwards, indicating that transplanted SB-HEO progressively matured and revived the liver function of FRG mice (Fig. 9B). A marked decrease in serum hepatocytic injury markers, specifically ALT and AST, underscores the significant role of SB-HEO in promoting liver maturation and function recovery (Fig. 9C). In the serum samples collected from SB-HEO-transplanted mice, the average secretion of human ALB at 2 months was approximately 83 μg/mL (Fig. 9D). Human ALB (hALB), CYP2E1, FAH, and GS-positive staining demonstrated reaggregation in liver specimens at 3 months post-transplantation (Fig. 9E). Discussion

Normal human hepatocytes have exceptional regenerative capacity42, 43, 44, which is lost in vitro. Therefore, the source of human hepatocytes remains a major challenge in many research areas and clinical therapy. Previous studies on hepatocyte expansion involved addition of growth factors, hormones, serum, and vitamins in vitro45, 46, 47, 48. Notably, Shan et al.49 identified small molecules that can induce proliferation of human hepatocytes in vitro. However, they did not use animal rescue experiments to establish the highest expansion potential and function of the treated hepatocytes. Pan et al.50 examined the relationship between relevant signaling pathways and proliferation of hiPSC-derived HPCs and developed a chemical cocktail to synergistically regulate relevant signaling pathways and optimize the balance between HPC proliferation capacity and stem cell maintenance. In their study, CHIR99021, which can activate Wnt/β-catenin signaling, significantly improved HPC proliferation. The authors demonstrated that concurrently inhibiting TGF-β and activating hedgehog signaling enhances the proliferation, self-renewal, and maintenance of bipotency in HPCs. BMP signaling also has a crucial role in the proliferation and bipotency maintenance of HPCs. Xiang et al.51 used SB431542 to inhibit TGF-β signaling in PHHs and noticed E-cadherin epithelial expression after two weeks of culture. Next, they tested gene expression using several small molecules and observed that Forskolin24 decreased EMT markers but preserved hepatocyte functions. Based on Forskolin-SB431542, similar results were obtained with DAPT (Notch inhibitor), LDN193189 (BMP inhibitor), and IWP2 (Wnt inhibitor) for 4 weeks. The culture medium reported by Mun et al.23 to generate functionally mature and expandable hPSC-derived hepatocyte-like liver organoids contains Forskolin and A8301 (an alternative TGF-β inhibitor to SB431542). FGF2 facilitates proliferation of hepatic progenitor cells52. Based on the above studies, we used three methods to induce hepatic progenitor cells: (1) SB (SB431542 and BMP4); (2) FB (FGF2 and BMP4); (3) VDF (vitamin C, Dihexa and Forskolin). The results suggest that the SB induction scheme facilitates maturity and stemness.

To validate the viability of our organoids, we used transcriptomics and proteomics to analyze their molecular basis at the mRNA and protein levels. The quantifiable similarity of our organoids with human liver tissue was first established using LiGEP33. Since hepatocytes account for nearly 80% of liver tissue, a 51.61% similarity in EM-cultured organoids and 62.37% in DM-cultured organoids is meaningful to achieve with an in vitro differentiation system. Second, detailed transcriptome analysis demonstrated that the organoids exhibited mature characteristics, as evidenced by the enrichment of gene sets associated with phase I drug metabolism enzymes, phase II drug metabolism enzymes and transporters. Notably, the cell cycle-related gene set was also highly expressed. The above conclusions were also confirmed in the proteome, and hence, we chose SB-HEO.

Severe adverse events, such as liver failure, are the leading cause of drug attrition during clinical development or withdrawal of marketed drugs. DILI is a key challenge in drug development53. Currently, the pharmaceutical industry's best option is to use PHHs in sandwich culture to recreate liver function in vitro. However, these detection platforms face reproducibility and throughput challenges due to the variability and limited availability of PHHs, as well as the need for complex quantitative algorithms. HLCs differentiated from hPSCs have similar phenotypes to PHHs and nearly unlimited and fairly consistent availability. Notably, the genetic profile of these cells matches that of their original donors. Hence, iPSC-derived HLC hepatotoxicity model is suitable for toxicological applications54. To improve HLC maturity and thus improve the chemical toxicity prediction accuracy, various strategies have been explored in recent years. Studies have shown that 3D culture mimics the hepatic cell-matrix contacts and cellular interactions, thereby enhancing HLC function and improving the prediction of culture models23,55,56. Moreover, 3D cultures are more sensitive to toxins than 2D cultures23. Compared to other cell culture models, SB-HEO have similar or higher sensitivity to established DILI drugs and drug specificity to their negative structural analogs. Importantly, SB-HEO accurately reflect this pathological process in the APAP-induced inflammatory response.

HLCs generated from hiPSC using a 2D differentiation strategy were found to be effective in treating various liver diseases57, 58, 59. Disease modeling with hiPSC has made tremendous progress with the establishment of hiPSC-derived organoids60, 61, 62, 63. Our group successfully established a metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease model in vitro using SB-HEO to better understand the pathophysiology of the disease and develop new therapeutic strategies. 3D organoids have evolved from 2D culture and represent a more mature liver disease model to reliably explore complicated liver disease mechanisms for individualized therapy. However, our current model has limitations in recapitulating the further progression of severe liver injury due to the absence of niche cells within the liver, such as Kupffer cells, hepatic stellate cells, and sinusoidal endothelial cells, which are important for triggering inflammation and fibrosis64. In this study, SB-HEO showed more efficient drug metabolizing activity compared to other cell culture models, as evidenced by the detection of the corresponding active metabolites by LC–MS analysis. It is necessary to determine specific drug metabolism-related enzymes for predicting drug–drug interactions65. Despite the limitations of SB-HEO compared to PHHs, they show higher sensitivity to drug metabolism compared to 2D HLCs and HepG2 cells. Taken together, these results support the use of SB-HEO to study CYP450-dependent drug metabolism.

The clinical potential of the organoids was explored by transplanting SB-HEO into diseased FRG mouse livers. SB-HEO were found to be the most effective in repopulating diseased livers and achieving functional liver recovery. SB-HEO injection improved survival and ALB secretion in mice, demonstrating the value of SB-HEO in cell therapy. However, our SB-HEO also has several limitations. First, our SB-HEO did not form a complete bile duct network. Second, SB-HEO did not show liver metabolic zoning. Finally, our SB-HEO did not have liver non-parenchymal cells. The above limitations may not be so physiologically similar when simulating certain liver functions in vivo and in vitro, which needs to be solved in subsequent studies.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that SB431542 and BMP4-induced formation of HPCs is a robust and renewable source for organoid production. The combination of scalability and mature functionality makes this new organoid system ideally suited for research purposes. In particular, our SB-HEOs have high CYP450 activity and can serve as a high-fidelity model for drug safety assessment and a promising cell source for the treatment of human liver diseases.
