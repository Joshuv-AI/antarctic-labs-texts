A NASSS-based framework for AI-driven prevention of non-communicable diseases in Iran: a qualitative study

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-Communicable Diseases (NCDs) are recognized as the leading cause of mortality worldwide, accounting for approximately 74% of all deaths. In Iran, this figure exceeds 80%, underscoring the critical need for innovative preventive strategies. Artificial Intelligence (AI), with its capabilities in early detection, risk prediction, and the design of targeted interventions, holds significant potential for mitigating the burden of NCDs. However, the successful integration of AI into a health system necessitates a thorough understanding of local cultural, organizational, and structural contexts. Grounded in the NASSS (Non-adoption, Abandonment, Scale-up, Spread, and Sustainability) theoretical model, this study aimed to design and validate a localized framework for the application of AI in NCD prevention within the Iranian health system.MethodThis study employed an exploratory-sequential qualitative design to develop and validate a localized framework for the application of AI in preventing Non-Communicable Diseases (NCDs) within the Iranian health system. In the initial phase, data from 34 semi-structured interviews with experts in health, technology, ethics, and policy were analyzed using a hybrid inductive-deductive thematic analysis. In the subsequent phase, the preliminary framework was refined and finalized using a modified Delphi method and two focus group discussions with 13 experts, yielding a high overall content validity index of 0.91.ResultsAnalysis guided by the NASSS model identified five principal themes and 19 initial subthemes. Following Delphi validation and framework refinement, the final framework comprised five major dimensions and 18 operational components tailored to the Iranian health system. The framework emphasizes algorithmic transparency, data standardization, digital health literacy, and workforce empowerment as key requirements for successful AI implementation in NCD prevention.ConclusionsThe finalized framework provides a locally validated model for the ethical, sustainable, and context-sensitive integration of AI into NCD prevention. It offers practical guidance for policymakers, health system managers, and researchers in Iran and other countries with similar healthcare contexts. The framework may assist decision-makers in prioritizing digital infrastructure investments, strengthening data governance, and implementing context-sensitive AI strategies for NCD prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s41043-026-01379-7.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Non-communicable diseases, Digital health, NASSS model, Local framework, Iran’s health system

Background

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are among the most important public health challenges worldwide, and their prevalence has been increasing rapidly in recent decades due to changing lifestyles, an aging population, and shifts in dietary and behavioral patterns [1, 2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) report in 2023 shows that these diseases are responsible for about 74% of all deaths worldwide, which represents a heavy health and economic burden on health care systems and societies [3]. Cardiovascular diseases, cancers, chronic respiratory diseases, and diabetes are among the major NCDs that claim millions of lives worldwide each year, leading to chronic disabilities and reduced quality of life [3, 4].

In economic terms, these diseases impose enormous costs on health systems [5, 6]. According to estimates by Chen et al. in 2019, the direct and indirect costs of NCDs worldwide amount to $25 trillion and are expected to double by 2030, which is a wake-up call for policymakers [7]. In contrast, targeted investment in the prevention and management of these diseases can significantly reduce the economic and social burden they impose and contribute to the sustainable development of societies [8].

From a national perspective, Iran, like many other countries, is facing an increasing burden of NCDs. According to the Ministry of Health, more than 80% of deaths in the country are due to these diseases, which reinforces the importance of addressing this issue as a key priority for health policies [9]. The increasing trend of risk factors such as obesity, inactivity, unhealthy diet, tobacco use, and air pollution has further highlighted the need to develop preventive and modern strategies based on scientific evidence [10].

A 2023 study by T. Fotis, highlighted the potential of swarm intelligence-based AI frameworks in improving the management of non-communicable diseases (NCDs). Their findings emphasized enhanced diagnostic accuracy, personalized treatment, continuous monitoring, and better clinical decision-making, demonstrating the importance of integrating trustworthy and adaptive AI systems into healthcare environments [11].

Also, A 2025 study by M. Behzadifar et al. examined the challenges of implementing artificial intelligence in Iran’s health system. The findings identified major barriers including weak infrastructure, regulatory and ethical issues, poor data quality, shortage of skilled professionals, and difficulties integrating AI into health policymaking. The study emphasized that successful AI adoption in Iran requires legal reform, infrastructure development, workforce training, and a balance between technological innovation and human-centered healthcare [12].

In these situations, artificial intelligence (AI) is recognized as a powerful tool for preventing and managing NCDs, thanks to its advanced machine learning algorithms, big data analysis, and innovative information-processing methods [13]. This technology can quickly analyze vast amounts of medical data, which supports more accurate predictions, early diagnoses, optimal treatments, and ongoing patient monitoring [14]. Several studies have shown that incorporating AI into health systems not only enhances the accuracy of disease diagnoses but also reduces healthcare costs and improves the effectiveness of preventive interventions [15].

in the context of NCD prevention, AI applications may operate across different levels of disease prevention. At the primary prevention level, AI can support population risk stratification, behavioral prediction, and personalized lifestyle interventions aimed at reducing exposure to risk factors such as obesity, smoking, and physical inactivity. At the secondary prevention level, AI may facilitate early detection and screening through predictive analytics, automated interpretation of clinical data, and identification of high-risk individuals before symptom progression. At the tertiary prevention level, AI applications can assist in long-term disease monitoring, complication prediction, treatment adherence assessment, and personalized chronic care management.

In this study, AI applications were operationally defined as machine learning–based, data-driven systems capable of analyzing longitudinal clinical, behavioral, or population-level health data to support preventive decision-making, risk prediction, early intervention, and health system management related to NCD prevention.

One of the most widely used frameworks for analyzing the complexity of digital health implementation is the NASSS framework (Non-adoption, Abandonment, Scale-up, Spread, and Sustainability). By examining dimensions such as technology, adopters, organizations, and wider system contexts, the NASSS model provides a robust theoretical basis for understanding the implementation and sustainability of emerging technologies in healthcare settings [16]. Distinctive Contributions of the Proposed Framework Compared with Existing Digital Health Implementation Model

Despite the significant potential of AI, the successful and sustainable implementation of AI technologies in health systems depends on multiple contextual factors, including cultural, structural, ethical, legal, and technological conditions [17]. The adoption and scale-up of AI-based interventions require a comprehensive understanding of local challenges and opportunities to ensure alignment with the specific realities of each health system [18].

To better position the proposed framework within the existing body of AI-healthcare implementation research, a comparative analysis was conducted against prominent international frameworks and implementation models. As shown in Table 1, although existing frameworks provide valuable guidance regarding AI governance, ethics, technology adoption, and trustworthy AI development, they offer limited attention to the operational requirements of AI-driven NCD prevention in resource-constrained healthcare settings. The proposed framework addresses these gaps by integrating algorithm localization, longitudinal health data integration, digital empowerment, cultural adaptation, and inter-sectoral governance into a unified implementation model tailored to the Iranian health system.

Although the NASSS framework has been widely applied to examine the adoption and sustainability of digital health technologies, existing applications have primarily focused on telemedicine, electronic health records, and high-income healthcare settings. Current implementation models provide limited guidance for the integration of artificial intelligence into the prevention of non-communicable diseases (NCDs), particularly in low- and middle-income countries where infrastructural fragmentation, regulatory uncertainty, cultural resistance, and data heterogeneity are more pronounced. An important contribution of the present study is its emphasis on ethical governance as a central prerequisite for sustainable AI implementation. Unlike many technology-focused implementation models, the proposed framework treats ethical oversight, algorithmic transparency, informed consent, and protection of patient privacy as operational components rather than secondary considerations. Participants highlighted that without transparent governance structures and culturally sensitive ethical regulations, AI adoption may increase distrust, reinforce healthcare inequalities, and expose patients to risks associated with biased or non-interpretable algorithms. Consequently, the framework recommends the establishment of national AI ethics committees, continuous monitoring of algorithmic fairness, and explicit policies regarding data ownership, accountability, and patient rights.

However, despite the growing international interest in AI-enabled health interventions, no locally validated framework currently exists for the implementation of AI in NCD prevention within the Iranian health system. Existing digital health implementation models provide limited guidance regarding the cultural, organizational, ethical, and infrastructural complexities specific to Iran and similar middle-income healthcare settings.

The present study extends existing NASSS-based approaches. It develops a context-sensitive and operational framework specifically tailored to AI-driven NCD prevention within the Iranian health system. Unlike prior digital health implementation models, the proposed framework incorporates locally emergent dimensions identified through qualitative inquiry, including algorithmic trust and transparency, indigenous data governance challenges, localization of predictive algorithms, and the interaction between digital literacy and cultural acceptance.

Therefore, the framework is not merely an adaptation of NASSS; rather, it represents a theoretically informed and empirically grounded extension of AI implementation science for complex chronic disease prevention in resource-constrained health systems. Taken together, these gaps indicate the absence of a context-specific, empirically validated framework for implementing AI in NCD prevention within the Iranian health system, thereby justifying the need for the present study. Recognizing the importance of adapting AI technologies to the cultural, social, and structural context of Iran, this study aimed to develop and validate a comprehensive framework for the use of AI in preventing NCDs. The framework was designed using qualitative data obtained from physicians, policymakers, health system managers, medical ethicists, and information technology specialists. The proposed model is intended to support policymakers and health managers in improving preventive care, facilitating responsible technology adoption, and strengthening evidence-based decision-making within the Iranian health system.

Methods

Type of study

This study employed an exploratory-sequential qualitative design with quantitative validation components to develop and validate a localized framework for the application of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in preventing Non-Communicable Diseases (NCDs) within the Iranian health system. The research was structured in two sequential, complementary phases to ensure both the conceptual depth required for framework development and a rigorous assessment of its scientific and practical validity.

The first phase, dedicated to framework development, utilized in-depth, semi-structured interviews analyzed through a thematic analysis approach. The objective was to identify key themes, conceptual dimensions, and critical factors influencing the acceptance and implementation of AI technologies. Data analysis followed a hybrid inductive-deductive approach. The inductive analysis allowed for the emergence of concepts directly from the raw data, thereby preserving the authenticity of local contexts, while the deductive analysis mapped these findings onto the dimensions of the overarching theoretical model to ensure a coherent theoretical structure.

In the second phase, focused on framework validation and refinement, the findings from the initial phase were synthesized into a preliminary conceptual model. This model was subsequently evaluated and finalized using a modified Delphi method and focus group discussions. A panel of experts in digital health, medical ethics, health system management, and epidemiology participated in this phase. The goal was to assess the framework’s content validity, conceptual clarity, structural comprehensiveness, and practical implement ability.

The study was guided by the NASSS (Non-adoption, Abandonment, Scale-up, Spread, and Sustainability) framework, a validated model for analyzing the complexities associated with the adoption, diffusion, and sustainability of digital health technologies [16]. The NASSS framework facilitated a comprehensive, systematic, and interdisciplinary examination of AI adoption by considering its core dimensions: the technology, the condition, the value proposition, the adopters, the organization, and the wider societal context. This approach allowed for a nuanced analysis of the integration of AI within the specific complexities of the Iranian health system.

The research was conducted between February 2024 and July 2025, in strict adherence to ethical principles for human subject’s research. Throughout all stages of the study, participation was voluntary. The protocols rigorously upheld the principles of confidentiality, informed consent, and the right of participants to withdraw at any time without penalty.

Phase one: framework design (thematic analysis)

The study population comprised specialists, experts, and policymakers operating at the intersection of health and technology. Participants were purposively selected from Type 1, 2, and 3 medical universities, the Ministry of Health, and research centers specializing in Non-Communicable Diseases (NCDs). This sampling strategy aimed to capture multidisciplinary perspectives spanning managerial, technological, ethical, and epidemiological domains, thereby ensuring a comprehensive exploration of the factors influencing AI integration in the health system.

The inclusion criteria for participants were as follows:

Exclusion and Sampling Criteria:

Participants were excluded from the study if they demonstrated an unwillingness to cooperate, failed to engage meaningfully in the interview process, or were identified as having a conflict of interest with the research objectives. To bolster the transferability and richness of the data, a purposive sampling strategy was employed to secure a diverse participant pool. This diversity encompassed variations in professional expertise, organizational role, academic background, gender, and geographic location.

Sampling

Sampling was conducted using a purposive strategy aimed at achieving maximum variation to capture a wide spectrum of perspectives, expertise, and managerial experiences. The selection of participants was guided by their professional field, decision-making level, categorized as policymaker, middle manager, or technical expert, and their prior experience with digital health technologies.

Following initial analysis, theoretical sampling was incorporated to address emergent gaps in the conceptual framework. This iterative process involved recruiting additional participants based on analytical needs to refine and clarify developing categories. Theoretical saturation was attained after 29 interviews. Data collection continued, however, until a total of 34 participants were engaged to reinforce the robustness, stability, and replicability of the identified thematic patterns.

How to conduct interviews

All interviews were conducted by the principal investigator and a trained research assistant between February 2024 and July 2025. Prior to each session, the research objectives and ethical protocols were explained in detail, and written informed consent was obtained. Interviews were held in quiet settings, including private offices and research centers, or via secure online platforms such as Google Meet and Sky Room, based on participant availability and preference.

The duration of interviews ranged from 45 to 65 min. With participants’ permission, all sessions were audio-recorded. Immediately following each interview, detailed field notes were taken to document non-verbal cues, tonal nuances, and the researcher’s initial interpretive impressions. The audio recordings were transcribed verbatim and subsequently cross-checked against the original files to ensure accuracy. To enhance the trustworthiness of the data, a member-checking procedure was implemented, whereby a transcript of each interview was provided to the respective participant for review, correction, or confirmation. All collected data were stored securely under confidential conditions and were numerically coded to preserve participant anonymity. All interviews were conducted in Persian, which was the native language of both participants and interviewers. For reporting purposes, selected participant quotations were translated into English by the research team and subsequently reviewed to ensure conceptual and linguistic accuracy. Particular attention was paid to preserving the original meaning and contextual nuances during the translation process.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed following Braun and Clarke’s six-step methodological framework for thematic analysis [27] using MAXQDA software (version 20). A hybrid inductive, deductive, analytical approach was employed. Inductive coding was used to derive emergent concepts directly from the interview transcripts, while deductive analysis was applied to map these concepts onto the pre-established dimensions of the NASSS framework.

The coding and theme development process was conducted through several iterative stages. Initially, all interview transcripts were transcribed verbatim and reviewed repeatedly to achieve immersion in the data. In the first stage, open coding was independently performed by two members of the research team to identify meaningful units and preliminary concepts related to AI implementation in NCD prevention. Codes with conceptual similarity were subsequently grouped into broader categories through constant comparison and iterative discussions among the researchers.

In the second stage, axial coding and thematic synthesis were conducted to establish relationships between categories and to organize them within the conceptual dimensions of the NASSS framework. Themes and subthemes were continuously refined through collaborative discussions until conceptual coherence and data saturation were achieved. Disagreements between coders were resolved through consensus meetings and re-examination of the original transcripts.

To enhance the trustworthiness and rigor of the findings, several strategies were employed, including prolonged engagement with the data, peer debriefing, member checking with selected participants, maintenance of an audit trail, and independent review of coding decisions by qualitative research experts. Inter-coder reliability was assessed using Cohen’s Kappa coefficient, which yielded a value of 0.84, indicating a strong level of agreement between the researchers. These procedures strengthened the credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability of the study findings.

Reflexivity was maintained throughout the study to minimize the potential influence of researcher assumptions and prior professional perspectives on data interpretation. The research team included experts in digital health, health policy, qualitative research, and medical ethics, which enabled multidisciplinary reflection during data analysis. To reduce researcher bias, coding decisions and theme development were discussed collaboratively, and interpretations were continuously compared against the original interview transcripts. Researchers also maintained reflective notes during data collection and analysis to increase awareness of potential subjective influences on interpretation. Several measures were additionally employed to minimize participant bias. Participants were informed that their responses would remain confidential and would not affect their professional positions or institutional relationships. Interviews were conducted in a neutral and non-judgmental manner to encourage open discussion and reduce social desirability bias. Furthermore, maximum variation purposive sampling was used to capture diverse professional perspectives and minimize the dominance of any single viewpoint in the findings.

Through this analytical process, five primary themes and nineteen subthemes were ultimately identified.

Upon completion of this phase, the thematic analysis findings were synthesized to construct a preliminary conceptual framework. This initial model was subsequently evaluated and refined in the study’s second phase, utilizing a modified Delphi technique and focus group discussions.

Ethical considerations

All stages of the research were conducted in accordance with ethical principles and were approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences, number IR.SUMS.REC.1404.073. Prior to each interview, written informed consent was obtained from all participants. They were assured of the complete confidentiality and anonymity of their data throughout the analysis and subsequent reporting. All collected data were encrypted and stored in a secure location, with access limited exclusively to the principal investigator. Participation in the study was entirely voluntary, and participants retained the right to withdraw at any point without penalty.

Beyond conventional research ethics considerations, this study also addressed broader ethical dimensions associated with the implementation of AI in health systems. Particular attention was given to issues related to patient privacy, algorithmic bias, informed consent, data ownership, transparency of automated decision-making, and accountability in AI-assisted clinical processes.

Participants consistently emphasized that the absence of clear national ethical guidelines for AI governance in Iran may increase the risk of misuse of patient data, opaque algorithmic decisions, and unequal healthcare outcomes across different social groups. Concerns were also raised regarding potential algorithmic bias resulting from incomplete, non-representative, or fragmented health datasets. To minimize these risks, the proposed framework incorporates principles of explainable AI, continuous ethical oversight, localized algorithm development, and transparent governance mechanisms.

Furthermore, informed consent in AI-supported healthcare was recognized as extending beyond routine clinical consent procedures. Participants emphasized that patients should be adequately informed regarding how their health data are collected, analyzed, stored, and utilized by intelligent systems. Therefore, the framework recommends the establishment of dedicated ethical governance structures and multidisciplinary AI ethics committees to supervise the safe, fair, and accountable use of AI technologies within the Iranian health system.

Phase two: framework validation via a modified delphi method

Phase one: expert validation

A two-round modified Delphi method was prospectively designed and employed to validate the conceptual and content dimensions of the preliminary framework. The number of Delphi rounds was determined a priori based on methodological recommendations for achieving expert consensus in conceptual framework validation studies. In the first round, a comprehensive document detailing the initial framework, including its core dimensions, constituent components, and their interrelationships, was distributed to a purposively selected panel of 12 key experts and stakeholders. The panel was constituted to ensure multidisciplinary expertise in digital health, medical ethics, health policy, and epidemiology. The final composition was as follows:

Four faculty members specializing in digital health and health information technology.

Panel members were asked to evaluate the proposed framework using a Content Validity Index (CVI) questionnaire, which assessed five key indicators: appropriateness, clarity, necessity, simplicity, and comprehensiveness. The instrument utilized a four-point Likert scale:

The CVI for each component of the framework was calculated as the proportion of experts rating it as a 3 or 4 on the scale. This provided a quantitative measure of content validity for each element. The acceptance criterion for each component was CVI ≥ 0.80, according to Lynn’s (1986) criterion [29]. Any component with a CVI score below the established threshold was revised. In the initial Delphi round, seven of the framework’s 19 components were identified as requiring modification. These components were subsequently refined based on the panel’s qualitative feedback, which included suggestions to clarify conceptual definitions, reconfigure inter-dimensional relationships, and simplify the framework’s graphical representation.

The revised framework was then redistributed to the expert panel for a second round of evaluation. No additional Delphi rounds were deemed necessary after consensus thresholds had been achieved. The study protocol prospectively planned two Delphi rounds, and both rounds were completed as scheduled. In this round, the level of consensus was measured using the intra-group agreement index, which yielded a score of 0.87, denoting a strong consensus among the experts. The final overall Content Validity Index (CVI) for the entire framework was 0.91, confirming excellent content validity.

Framework refinement through focus group discussions

Subsequently, to finalize the validation process and assess the framework’s operational applicability, two focus group sessions were conducted with 13 key stakeholders. The participants were purposively selected and included representatives from medical universities, health technology specialists, managers in NCD prevention, and experts in data science and medicine.

During these sessions, the refined framework was presented using a visual diagram and contextualized through scenario-based examples. The ensuing discussions were guided by a semi-structured protocol centered on the following themes:

The sessions were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim upon receiving participant consent. The resulting data were analyzed using a directed content analysis approach, which was structured around the pre-defined themes from the preliminary model. This process yielded final refinements to the framework, including the merger of two overlapping components, “data capacity” and “technological infrastructure,” and the addition of a new component, “algorithmic trust and transparency,” which emerged directly from the focus group discussions.

Summary of the design and evaluation process

The development and validation of the framework proceeded through three sequential phases: initial formulation, a modified Delphi process for validation, and a final review via focus group discussions. This comprehensive process involved 25 expert participations across the Delphi and focus group phases. Evaluation results demonstrated that the final framework achieved a high level of desirability across key criteria, including conceptual comprehensiveness, contextual suitability to local conditions, clarity of its constituent components, and practical implementability within the structure of the Iranian health system.

Consequently, the framework was ratified as a localized model to guide the application of AI for NCD prevention in Iran. It is thus recommended for subsequent field testing and pilot implementation in real-world health settings to further assess its utility and impact.

Results

Findings of the first phase: framework design (thematic analysis)

This study analyzed the demographic and professional composition of the 34 key experts and stakeholders interviewed. The cohort had an average age of 41 years (range: 33–50), representing the most active professional groups in the relevant fields.

The sample was 53% female and 47% male, providing a gender balance that supports the generalizability of the findings. Participants were drawn from key domains aligned with the research topic, including medicine, health information technology, medical ethics, health system management, and policy-making.

Nineteen of the 34 interviewees held university faculty positions (from assistant to full professor). Their deep scientific knowledge and experience were instrumental in guiding the study’s scientific direction. The remaining participants held managerial, policy, clinical, and specialist roles, such as executives, IT specialists, and health policymakers.

On average, participants had 13 years of professional experience in their respective fields, underscoring their considerable expertise. This demographic and professional diversity enabled a multifaceted examination of the challenges and opportunities in adopting AI within the Iranian health system. Theme 1

Technological challenges emerged as a primary barrier to the successful integration of AI for NCD prevention. Participants highlighted those deficiencies in infrastructure, data quality, interoperability, and model interpretability significantly limit the effectiveness and adoption of AI solutions within the Iranian health system. These challenges indicate the critical need for robust, integrated, and user-centered technological frameworks.

Sub-theme 1.1

Fragmented and inconsistent health data were identified as a major obstacle to AI implementation, undermining prediction accuracy and national-level modeling. Data were often incomplete, scattered across multiple systems, and recorded in incompatible formats.

“When we collected data from several hospitals, none had the same structure…we dedicated half our time to cleaning the data” (Interviewee 9).

Establishing standardized data collection and validation mechanisms is essential for enabling reliable AI-driven interventions.

Sub-theme 1–2

Participants reported the absence of unified protocols for exchanging health data across hospitals, universities, and insurance systems, leading to isolated data silos.

“Each system operates independently, making national model development nearly impossible” (Interviewee 22).

Implementing interoperable data exchange standards (e.g., HL7, FHIR) is critical for scaling AI applications across the health system.

Sub-theme 1–3

Advanced AI models require substantial computational infrastructure and multidisciplinary expertise, which are often unavailable in practice. Pilot studies frequently failed to translate into clinical settings due to server shortages, weak networks, and limited human resources.

“We designed a deep learning model for diabetes prediction, but infrastructure gaps prevented its implementation” (Interviewee 16).

Capacity building for both hardware infrastructure and cross-functional teams is a prerequisite for operationalizing AI in healthcare.

Sub-theme 1–4

The black-box nature of AI algorithms was noted as a barrier to clinician trust and accountability. Clinicians were reluctant to rely on outputs they could not interpret, raising ethical and legal concerns.

“When a doctor wants to use AI, they need to know why the system flagged a patient at risk. Otherwise, they cannot rely on it” (Interviewee 15).

Developing explainable AI models is crucial to promoting clinician adoption, ethical compliance, and transparency in AI-driven decision-making.

Theme 2

Patient and disease-related factors play a pivotal role in shaping AI implementation for NCD prevention. The complexity of chronic conditions, variability in patient behavior, and socio-cultural influences all affect the feasibility, acceptance, and effectiveness of AI interventions.

Sub-theme 2 − 1

The long-term nature of NCDs requires continuous monitoring, but intermittent data collection impedes reliable AI prediction. Participants noted that irregular patient follow-ups generate fragmented datasets that limit trend analysis.

“Diabetic patients should be followed up regularly, but in practice, they only come when symptoms are severe. This makes the data discontinuous, so the AI model cannot find the real pattern” (Interviewee 19, epidemiologist).

Continuous patient tracking systems are essential to support longitudinal data collection and accurate AI modeling.

Sub-theme 2.2

Variation in risk factors across gender, age, and social groups limits the generalizability of AI models trained on non-local data. Participants highlighted that cultural, dietary, and lifestyle differences affect disease patterns in Iran.

“The risk pattern differs between men and women…if models do not see these differences, predictions will be misleading” (Interviewee 25, Full Professor of Public Health).

AI algorithms must be localized to reflect demographic and cultural heterogeneity, ensuring accurate predictions for all population groups.

Sub-theme 2–3

Participants emphasized that health records are scattered, partial, and inconsistently transferred between centers, reducing analytical reliability.

“When a patient moves from one center to another, the data is not transferred…longitudinal analysis is not possible” (Interviewee 12, Faculty of Health Technology).

Integrating fragmented datasets and designing mechanisms for continuous monitoring are prerequisites for valid AI analyses.

Sub-theme 2.4

The absence of national-level longitudinal databases prevents AI models from identifying disease trends and causal relationships. Most available data are cross-sectional and insufficient for long-term predictions.

“For chronic diseases like cancer or diabetes, we do not have longitudinal data…AI can only guess, not predict” (Interviewee 10, medical ethicist).

Developing national longitudinal data banks is crucial not only for AI prediction but also for evidence-based policymaking in NCD prevention.

Theme 3

Experts emphasized the potential of AI to enhance healthcare efficiency and improve preventive strategies for NCDs. While AI offers significant promise, its benefits depend on robust data infrastructure, effective digital health management, and context-sensitive implementation.

Sub-theme 3 − 1

AI was viewed as a key tool to alleviate repetitive administrative tasks and reduce clinician burnout, enabling staff to focus on specialized clinical decision-making.

“I see maybe fifty patients a day…If an AI system could transcribe my conversation or voice, it would be a great help” (Interviewee 5, internal medicine specialist).

Automating routine processes can increase efficiency, reduce errors, and allow healthcare professionals to allocate time to higher-value clinical tasks.

Sub-theme 3 − 2

AI can move interventions from generic population-level programs to targeted strategies for high-risk individuals by analyzing behavioral, clinical, and environmental data.

“AI can predict who is at risk more accurately…we can design specific training programs for them” (Interviewee 19, epidemiologist).

Targeted interventions enhance the effectiveness and personalization of preventive measures, optimizing resource allocation.

Sub-theme 3.3

Participants highlighted AI’s role in supporting evidence-based decisions in both clinical and managerial domains. It can uncover hidden patterns, accelerate early diagnosis, and guide policy and resource allocation.

“AI helps us base our decisions on real data, rather than emotions…we can make better national decisions” (Interviewee 17, Medical Ethics Specialist).

Participants consistently indicated that AI’s main value lies in reducing workload, enabling targeted interventions, and strengthening data-driven decisions. Achieving these benefits depends on adequate data infrastructure, localized algorithms, and user-centered design to ensure effective adoption and sustainability within the health system.

Theme 4

The readiness and engagement of human users emerged as a critical determinant of AI adoption in NCD prevention. Participants emphasized that successful implementation depends more on user acceptance, trust, and interaction than on technical advancement. Key barriers included physicians’ limited understanding of AI principles, low digital literacy among high-risk populations, patients’ cultural resistance to automated systems, and the need for continuous training and empowerment.

Subtheme 4 − 1

Lack of knowledge about algorithmic logic and outputs generates distrust and resistance among clinicians.

“When I tested a heart attack risk prediction software, it showed a risk number but had no explanation on what it was based on… I cannot trust it” (Interviewee 30, cardiologist).

Integrating AI education into medical curricula and offering practical workshops are essential to build trust and competence among healthcare professional.

Sub-theme 4 − 2

Older adults, chronically ill, and low-education groups struggle to use apps or remote monitoring systems, limiting AI effectiveness.

“Many of my patients still do not know how to connect their phones to the internet…they do not understand how to use these tools” (Interviewee 7, Health Network Manager).

Tailored digital literacy programs are needed to enable equitable access and engagement with AI-based interventions. Limited digital literacy among older adults, rural populations, and some healthcare providers may substantially reduce equitable access to AI-enabled preventive services.

Subtheme 4 − 3

some patients distrust automated systems and prefer human decision-making, expressing concerns about privacy and replacement fears.

“When we tell a patient that an intelligent system is going to check their condition, they feel like it is replacing the doctor” (Interviewee 25, Public Health Professor).

AI adoption must consider cultural beliefs and patient expectations, emphasizing technology as supportive rather than a replacement.

Subtheme 4–4

Ongoing training for clinicians, healthcare providers, and patients is essential, including both technical and ethical components.

“AI training should have two parts: technical and ethical…both are necessary” (Interviewee 10, medical ethicist).

User readiness and engagement are pivotal for AI adoption. Addressing gaps in physician knowledge, digital literacy, cultural acceptance, and continuous empowerment is critical to ensure effective and sustained implementation of AI interventions for NCD prevention.

Theme 5

Organizational and macro-level factors emerged as critical determinants of AI adoption and sustainability in NCD prevention. Participants highlighted that structural and policy-level challenges, including fragmented decision-making, insufficient coordination among stakeholders, weak legal and ethical frameworks, and unstable financial resources, limit the scalability and long-term impact of AI interventions.

Sub-theme 5 − 1

The highest frequency in this theme (27%) was related to legal gaps and the lack of local frameworks for using AI in healthcare. Participants believed that the country’s laws currently focus more on public information technology and do not adequately address issues such as data ownership, liability for algorithm errors, and patient privacy.

“In the area of privacy, we have a general law, but there is no specific framework defined for digital health data. For example, where is AI data stored? Who has the right to access it? These are still unclear.” (Interviewee 13, IT Manager).

Subtheme 5 − 2

In about 24% of the codes in this theme, participants mentioned the insular nature of decision-making and the lack of coordination between different institutions. According to them, the Ministry of Health, technology companies, universities, insurance companies, and research centers each operate independently, and there is no precise mechanism for data sharing or joint planning. Participants stated that:

In successful countries, there is a central coordinating body that guides the national strategy for AI in health. We do not have that; everyone does their own thing.” (Interviewee 22, Epidemiologist).

Sub-theme 5 − 3

In about 23% of the extracted codes, interviewees emphasized the critical role of ethical considerations and social trust in the success of health technologies. Many acknowledged that there are still no clear ethical guidelines for the use of patient data, how algorithmic decisions should be made, or the boundaries of human responsibility. According to the Participants:

“In developed countries, there are specific ethics committees to monitor algorithms, but we are still at the level of scattered projects. Ethics should be a constant part of the development process, not the end of the story.” (Interviewee 3, medical ethics faculty).

Sub-theme 5 − 4

In this subtheme, 26% of the codes were dedicated to the issue of budget instability and lack of institutional support for the continuation of AI projects. Participants explained that most existing projects in the country are implemented as research projects or short-term pilots and are discontinued once the financial support ends. According to Participants:

“AI projects usually start with research funding and are abandoned after a year or two, because there is no ongoing funding for maintenance or development.” (Interviewee 12, Faculty of Health Technology).

Developing the initial framework

Developing the initial framework

In the initial phase, the research team, comprising four specialists in digital health, health system management, non-communicable diseases (NCDs), and qualitative analysis, aligned the first-stage findings with the dimensions of the NASSS framework. The team subsequently developed a preliminary draft of the framework, which was structured around five core dimensions: “Technology,” “Disease Characteristics,” “Value Proposition,” “Human Users,” and “Organizational and Macro-Level System.” The framework was articulated in both visual and textual formats, with the relationships between components designed to reflect a logical data flow from the micro to the macro level, as well as the corresponding policy feedback from the macro to the micro level. The NASSS framework was selected because it provides a comprehensive and multidimensional structure for examining the adoption, implementation, scale-up, and sustainability of complex health technologies within dynamic health systems. Unlike conventional technology acceptance models that primarily focus on individual user behavior, NASSS incorporates technological, organizational, contextual, policy, ethical, and system-level determinants that are particularly relevant in fragmented and resource-constrained healthcare environments such as Iran. Localization of the NASSS dimensions was achieved through an iterative hybrid inductive-deductive analytical process. While the primary thematic structure was initially informed by the original NASSS domains, context-specific themes emerging from stakeholder experiences were integrated to adapt the framework to the organizational, cultural, legal, technological, and governance realities of the Iranian health system.

Final components of the indigenous framework for the use of AI in the prevention of NCDs

Discussion

This study contributes to the literature beyond a contextual application of the NASSS framework. While NASSS provided the theoretical foundation for organizing the analysis, several dimensions of the final framework emerged inductively from stakeholders’ experiences and reflected challenges that are insufficiently addressed in existing digital health implementation models. In particular, the framework emphasizes algorithmic transparency, localized data governance, culturally mediated technology acceptance, and the integration of longitudinal multi-source health data for AI-enabled NCD prevention. These elements extend prior implementation frameworks by operationalizing AI-specific governance and trust mechanisms within a middle-income healthcare context. Participants consistently emphasized that transparency and interpretability are essential for clinician trust in AI-supported decision-making. Explainable AI approaches that provide understandable reasons for algorithmic recommendations may improve clinical acceptance, accountability, and integration into routine preventive care.

The findings also highlight the importance of developing AI systems using representative and context-sensitive datasets. Variations in risk patterns across demographic, socioeconomic, cultural, and geographic groups suggest that predictive models trained on limited or non-representative data may perform unequally across populations. This concern is particularly relevant in NCD prevention, where lifestyle, environmental exposures, and health-seeking behaviors differ substantially among population groups. Therefore, the use of diverse and longitudinal datasets, combined with the localization of predictive algorithms, can improve model fairness, reduce algorithmic bias, and enhance the equitable delivery of AI-supported preventive services. These findings reinforce the need for data governance strategies that prioritize inclusiveness and representation throughout the AI development lifecycle.

Another important consideration is the need for robust data protection and cybersecurity measures in AI-enabled health systems. Given the extensive use of sensitive health information in AI applications, participants highlighted concerns related to data privacy, unauthorized access, and the potential misuse of health data. Effective implementation therefore, requires comprehensive data governance policies, secure data storage and exchange mechanisms, cybersecurity risk management strategies, and continuous monitoring of system vulnerabilities. Integrating security-by-design principles into AI development and deployment processes may strengthen public trust, protect patient information, and support the safe and sustainable use of AI in NCD prevention.

Effective implementation of AI within health systems also depends on its seamless integration with existing digital health infrastructures. The proposed framework highlights the importance of interoperability between electronic health record systems, national health information platforms, and other digital health technologies through standardized data exchange mechanisms such as HL7 and FHIR. Participants emphasized that AI solutions are more likely to be adopted when they are embedded within existing clinical workflows rather than introduced as stand-alone technologies. Strengthening institutional coordination, promoting interoperability standards, and ensuring compatibility with existing health information systems can facilitate scalable and sustainable integration of AI into routine NCD prevention services. Consideration should also be given to potential rural–urban disparities in access to AI-enabled healthcare services. While AI has the potential to improve preventive care and expand access to health information, its benefits may not be distributed equally across populations. Differences in digital infrastructure, internet connectivity, availability of health technologies, and digital literacy may limit the adoption of AI-supported services in rural and underserved areas. Addressing these disparities requires targeted investments in digital health infrastructure, capacity-building initiatives, and inclusive implementation strategies to ensure that AI contributes to reducing, rather than exacerbating, existing health inequalities.

This study aimed to design and validate a localized framework for the application of artificial intelligence (AI) in preventing non-communicable diseases (NCDs) within the Iranian health system, using the NASSS model as a foundation. A qualitative analysis of 34 semi-structured interviews revealed that the acceptance and sustainability of AI in healthcare are contingent upon the complex interaction of technological, human, organizational, and cultural factors. The findings were categorized into the five principal dimensions of the NASSS model, encompassing 19 conceptual sub-themes. The most frequently cited challenges included data heterogeneity (29%), the chronic nature of diseases and the consequent need for continuous monitoring (27%), a lack of local legal frameworks (27%), physicians’ unfamiliarity with AI principles (28%), and the potential for reducing human resource workload (26%).

Comparison with existing frameworks

Comparison with international AI-healthcare frameworks further highlights the distinctive contribution of the present study (Table 1). While frameworks such as the WHO Guidance on Ethics and Governance of AI for Health, OECD AI Principles, and FUTURE-AI primarily emphasize ethical governance, trustworthy AI, and technical validation, the proposed framework extends these perspectives by addressing implementation challenges identified through stakeholder experiences in the Iranian health system. In particular, the framework incorporates localized algorithm development, integration of fragmented longitudinal health data, digital literacy enhancement, cultural acceptance, and inter-sectoral coordination. These context-sensitive components provide practical guidance for implementing AI in NCD prevention and may also be relevant for other low- and middle-income countries facing similar health system constraints.

The results indicate that the primary obstacles to AI adoption in Iran pertain to health system readiness, user culture, and digital governance infrastructure, rather than purely technical limitations. Consequently, the final research framework was designed as a dynamic model emphasizing the necessary synergy between technology, users, organizational structures, and macro-level policies. A comparison with international studies suggests that while technical challenges are ubiquitous, cultural and institutional factors play a disproportionately significant role in determining the success of technology deployment in Iran. Therefore, the development of localized, ad hoc frameworks to align technological requirements with the specific realities of the country’s health system is essential. Such a coordinating body should adopt a multi-stakeholder governance approach involving policymakers, healthcare organizations, technology developers, patient representatives, and other relevant actors to facilitate accountability, transparency, and implementation feasibility.

Technology features

In this dimension, key challenges included the poor quality of health data (29%), the lack of standards for data exchange between systems (24%), the complexity of implementing deep learning algorithms (23%), and the lack of interpretability of models for users (24%). These barriers suggest that the success of AI in the Iranian health system depends mainly on the maturity of data and infrastructure. According to the findings, poor quality and heterogeneity of data were identified as the most important barriers. Health data are stored in multiple and non-standard systems, which reduces the accuracy and generalizability of AI models. The present finding is consistent with the results of Topol et al. (2019) [15] and Esteva et al. (2019) [30], who emphasized the importance of data integrity and model interpretability. The lack of standards for data exchange between health systems is also a structural challenge that prevents the formation of national databases, similar to the finding of Kaushik (2025) [31], who identified it as a significant obstacle in developing countries. In addition, the complexity of implementing deep learning models and the lack of multidisciplinary human resources have increased the gap between research and practical application. Ultimately, the uninterpretable nature of algorithms leads to distrust among physicians and restricts the clinical application of this technology. As Pierce et al. emphasized in 2022, explain ability is a prerequisite for clinical acceptance of AI [32]. Therefore, for the successful deployment of this technology in Iran, the policy focus should be on data standardization, strengthening information exchange between systems, and developing explainable algorithms to provide the basis for trust and scalability of the technology. In addition to data-related barriers, participants highlighted infrastructural limitations such as unstable internet connectivity, insufficient digital network coverage in underserved regions, and limited computational capacity within some healthcare institutions. These infrastructural weaknesses reduce the reliability and scalability of AI-supported healthcare services, particularly in remote and resource-constrained settings.

An additional consideration for the successful implementation of AI in NCD prevention is the representativeness of training datasets. Participants emphasized the importance of using comprehensive and high-quality health data; however, data quality alone may not be sufficient to ensure equitable AI performance. AI systems trained on datasets that underrepresent specific demographic, geographic, socioeconomic, or vulnerable population groups may generate biased predictions and exacerbate existing health disparities. Therefore, the development of AI models should incorporate representative and diverse datasets that reflect the heterogeneity of the target population. Continuous monitoring of algorithmic performance across different population groups, together with periodic bias assessment and model recalibration, is necessary to promote fairness, equity, and inclusiveness in AI-driven healthcare interventions. These considerations are particularly important in the Iranian health system, where substantial variations exist across regions, socioeconomic groups, and levels of access to healthcare services.

Disease characteristics

Within this dimension, the chronic and multifaceted nature of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) was identified as a critical factor influencing the effectiveness of AI in the health system. The findings indicate that the principal challenges are the chronicity of NCDs and the consequent need for continuous management (27%), the diversity of risk patterns across populations (25%), incomplete data (24%), and the absence of national longitudinal data (24%). These challenges collectively demonstrate that AI models are effective in preventing NCDs only when supported by continuous, accurate, and demographically representative health data. This result aligns with the work of Rajkomar et al. (2018)[33] and Beam et al. (2016) [34], who emphasized that machine learning models for chronic diseases require reliable longitudinal data to perform effectively.

However, this study also uncovered a significant local dimension: global models often fail to adequately account for unique risk patterns within the Iranian population, such as those related to gender, climate, and lifestyle. Similar issues regarding the limited generalizability of international models have been reported in other developing countries, as noted in studies by Stonbraker et al. (2024) [35] and Canfell et al. (2022) [36]. In summary, the findings indicate that the absence of a national longitudinal database and the fragmentation of health data in Iran not only compromise the accuracy of predictive models but also challenge the fairness and generalizability of their results. Consequently, establishing integrated data systems and localizing algorithms to reflect Iran’s unique epidemiological characteristics are essential prerequisites for the successful application of AI in NCD prevention.

Value proposition

In the value proposition dimension, the most frequent ones were related to reducing human workload (26%), targeting interventions for high-risk groups (25%), and strengthening data-driven decision-making (24%). The findings showed that the value of AI in the Iranian health system lies in increasing the efficiency, accuracy, and fairness of preventive services rather than replacing humans with technology. In conditions of human resource shortage, AI can improve the efficiency of the health system through automated screening and early prediction. This result is consistent with the findings of a 2017 study by Jiang et al. [13], which demonstrated that the application of AI in clinical decision-making can enhance the accuracy and speed of screening. Wahl et al. also reported in 2018 [37] that integrating deep learning algorithms into clinical processes leads to more intelligent allocation of health resources and reduced human errors.

On the other hand, Ting et al. (2018) showed that the use of AI in the prevention of chronic diseases can significantly reduce the time spent on repetitive tasks [38]. In this regard, the findings of Esteva et al. (2017) also confirm that machine learning-based models, in addition to higher accuracy in diagnosis, facilitate clinical work and reduce staff workload [39]. Accordingly, the present study highlights a new aspect that has been less considered in the global literature: the role of AI in reducing job fatigue and improving the satisfaction of health system employees through the automation of repetitive tasks and workload optimization. Focusing on the human dimension of technology’s value can significantly increase the acceptance and sustainability of innovative technology implementation within the Iranian health system.

Human users

In the human user dimension, the findings showed that physicians’ unfamiliarity with the logic and limitations of algorithms (28%), patients’ cultural resistance to machine decision-making (26%), low digital literacy (23%), and the need for continuous user training (23%) were among the most important barriers to the adoption of AI technologies in the Iranian health system. These results are consistent with a 2024 study by Hassan et al. [40], which showed that a lack of understanding of model performance and inadequate training reduce physicians’ trust in the use of AI. Weingott et al. also reported in 2025 that patients’ concerns about privacy and human-machine replacement are serious cultural barriers to the adoption of innovative technologies [41]. Similarly, Longoni et al. (2019) demonstrated that the adoption of innovative technologies in healthcare increases when patients perceive that final clinical decisions remain under physician supervision [42].

The present study additionally revealed a context-specific dimension of cultural readiness in Iran, emphasizing the importance of multi-level ethical and educational interventions targeting physicians, caregivers, and patients to strengthen trust and reduce resistance toward AI-supported healthcare services. The findings suggest that, within the Iranian context, educational strategies should extend beyond technical skill acquisition to include ethical awareness, transparency regarding algorithmic decision-making processes, and responsible human-technology interaction.

Cultural and social acceptance also emerged as critical determinants of successful AI implementation within the Iranian health system. Participants emphasized that public trust in AI-supported healthcare is strongly influenced by cultural beliefs regarding physician authority, the importance of human interaction in clinical care, and concerns about replacing human judgment with automated systems. In many cases, patients were more willing to accept AI-assisted recommendations when they were supervised or validated by healthcare professionals rather than presented as fully autonomous decisions.

In addition, varying levels of health literacy, digital literacy, and familiarity with emerging technologies may significantly influence patient attitudes toward AI-driven interventions. Patient experiences should also inform the design and implementation of AI-enabled preventive services. Collecting and incorporating patient feedback throughout the development, deployment, and evaluation phases can help identify usability barriers, improve user satisfaction, and ensure that AI applications remain responsive to users’ needs and expectations. Furthermore, accessibility considerations, including language preferences, age-related limitations, disability-related needs, and unequal access to digital technologies, should be integrated into system design to promote equitable adoption and long-term engagement across diverse population groups. Concerns related to transparency, fairness, confidentiality, and the potential depersonalization of healthcare were also identified as barriers to social acceptance. Therefore, culturally sensitive communication strategies, public awareness programs, transparent governance mechanisms, continuous user training, and patient-centered implementation approaches are essential to improve trust and facilitate the sustainable adoption of AI technologies in healthcare.

Organization and macro-level system

In the organization and macro-level system dimension, four main challenges were identified, including the lack of local legal and regulatory frameworks (27%), unstable financial resources and over-reliance on ad hoc projects (26%), lack of coordination among health system stakeholders (24%), and a gap in ethical and regulatory policymaking (23%). These findings suggest that the primary barriers to the adoption of AI in the Iranian health system are structural and governance-related, rather than technical or technological in nature. An important contribution of the present study is its emphasis on ethical governance as a central prerequisite for sustainable AI implementation. Unlike many technology-focused implementation models, the proposed framework treats ethical oversight, algorithmic transparency, informed consent, and protection of patient privacy as operational components rather than secondary considerations. Participants highlighted that without transparent governance structures and culturally sensitive ethical regulations, AI adoption may increase distrust, reinforce healthcare inequalities, and expose patients to risks associated with biased or non-interpretable algorithms. Consequently, the framework recommends the establishment of national AI ethics committees, continuous monitoring of algorithmic fairness, and explicit policies regarding data ownership, accountability, and patient rights.

A study by Greenhalgh et al. in 2017 also showed that the lack of institutional coherence and instability of executive policies are among the main factors contributing to the failure of digital health technology deployment [16]. Similarly, Morley et al. (2020(emphasized that the lack of transparent ethical policies and independent regulatory institutions hinders the formation of public trust and the sustainability of AI-based projects in health systems [43]. Furthermore, Neupane et al. (2025) stated that the lack of coordinated national mechanisms and financial continuity in digital policymaking leads to the failure of transformative health initiatives in developing countries [44].

At the organizational and policy level, the findings indicate that financial instability and short-term policymaking are the primary causes of AI project failure in Iran. Many initiatives are discontinued after the pilot phase due to managerial turnover, the cessation of funding, or the absence of a clear governing body. Consequently, the sustainable deployment of AI in the Iranian health system necessitates establishing a national coordinating body, developing explicit legal and ethical frameworks, and designing stable, long-term financial mechanisms.

In addition, regulatory mechanisms such as national standards for health data interoperability, algorithm validation and certification procedures, independent ethical oversight committees, and accountability frameworks for AI-related clinical decisions should be established to ensure the safe, transparent, and trustworthy deployment of AI technologies in healthcare [22, 45].

This comprehensive approach can pave the way for a gradual transition to an innovative and sustainable health system by enhancing stakeholder coordination, promoting policy coherence, and fostering institutional trust.

Limitation

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the framework was developed within the context of the Iranian health system; therefore, the transferability of the findings to other health systems should be interpreted cautiously. Second, purposive and theoretical sampling may have introduced selection bias, as participants were primarily experts and stakeholders with prior experience or interest in digital health and artificial intelligence. In addition, frontline healthcare workers, including nurses and primary healthcare providers, as well as patients and community representatives, were underrepresented in the study. Consequently, the findings may reflect the perspectives of experts, policymakers, and academic stakeholders more strongly than those of end users and frontline implementers. Future studies should incorporate a broader range of stakeholders to enhance the practical relevance and inclusiveness of the framework. Third, the findings relied on self-reported perceptions and experiences obtained through interviews and focus group discussions, which may be influenced by subjective interpretations or social desirability bias. Finally, although the framework achieved high expert consensus and content validity, it has not yet been empirically tested in real-world implementation settings. Future studies should evaluate the framework through pilot implementation and longitudinal assessment.

Conclusions

This study developed and validated a localized framework for the use of AI in the prevention of NCDs within the Iranian health system. Drawing on the NASSS framework and informed by the experiences of experts from different disciplines, the study identified a range of technological, organizational, human, and policy-related factors that shape the successful implementation of AI in preventive healthcare. The findings suggest that the adoption of AI for NCD prevention is not solely a technological issue. Rather, its success depends on the interaction between reliable data infrastructure, user readiness, organizational support, ethical governance, and sustainable financing. Participants consistently emphasized the importance of data quality, interoperability, transparency of algorithms, digital literacy, and coordinated governance as key prerequisites for effective and sustainable implementation. An important contribution of this study is the extension of the NASSS framework to the context of AI-enabled NCD prevention. While the original framework provided the conceptual foundation, several context-specific components emerged from the qualitative findings, including algorithmic trust and transparency, localization of predictive algorithms, integration of longitudinal health data, and continuous digital empowerment of users. These elements reflect challenges that are particularly relevant in middle-income health systems and add to the growing body of knowledge on AI implementation in healthcare.

The proposed framework also offers practical implications for health policymakers and managers. The findings highlight the need for stronger data governance mechanisms, clear legal and ethical regulations for AI applications, investment in digital infrastructure, and continuous capacity building for healthcare professionals. Establishing coordinated governance structures and promoting collaboration among healthcare institutions, policymakers, researchers, and technology developers may facilitate the responsible integration of AI into preventive health programs.

Although the framework was developed in the Iranian context, many of the challenges identified in this study, such as fragmented health data systems, limited interoperability, workforce readiness, and regulatory uncertainty, are shared by other low- and middle-income countries. Therefore, the framework may provide useful guidance for similar healthcare settings seeking to incorporate AI into NCD prevention strategies.

Future studies should evaluate the framework in real-world implementation settings and examine its impact on healthcare processes and preventive outcomes. Quantitative validation of the framework components, assessment of implementation barriers and facilitators, and comparative studies across different health systems could further strengthen the evidence base. Additional research is also needed to explore the long-term effectiveness, scalability, and cost-effectiveness of AI-supported interventions for NCD prevention.

In conclusion, the proposed framework provides a context-sensitive approach for integrating AI into NCD prevention and offers a foundation for future implementation efforts. By combining technological, human, organizational, and governance dimensions, it contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of how AI can support preventive healthcare in Iran and other health systems facing similar challenges.
