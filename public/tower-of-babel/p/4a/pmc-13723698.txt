Genetic model for fluorite mineralization at the AGS deposit, St. Lawrence, Newfoundland, Canada: REY patterns, fluid inclusion analysis, Nd-Sm dating, and C, O and S isotopes
Authors: Zsuzsanna Magyarosi, James Conliffe, Daniel J Kontak, Robert Creaser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFluorite mineralization in the AGS deposit occurred in an epithermal setting from fluids exsolved from the associated St. Lawrence Granite and mixed with meteoric water. Veins of the deposit cut Cambrian sedimentary rocks, and sills and a massive variant of the granite. The mineralization is characterized by banded, colloform and breccia textures, and is divided into three stages: Early, Main and Late. The magmatic origin of the fluorite is supported by the Nd-Sm age of ~ 361 Ma, ƐNd values (1.7 to 2.9), strong negative Eu anomalies and REY contents (~ 5 to 1000 times chondrite values) of the fluorite, all comparable to the associated granite. Interaction of a vapour-rich magmatic fluid with oxidized surface-derived fluids formed an acidic fluid that led to greisenization and fracturing of the overlying rocks prior to fluorite precipitation. Fluorite precipitated between ~ 130 and 180 °C from H2O-NaCl-CaCl2±FeCl2±MgCl2 fluids with salinities of 9.7–27.5 eq. wt% NaCl. Carbon isotope data (δ13C between − 6.5 and − 5.6‰) on calcite intergrown with fluorite indicate a magmatic carbon source and δ18O (8.6 to 19.3‰) indicate mixing of magmatic fluids with meteoric water. Main-stage sphalerite returned δ³⁴S values of ~ − 32‰, consistent with biogenic sulfur from the host sedimentary rocks or oxidized magmatic sulfur, whereas Late-stage sphalerite returned δ³⁴S ≈ − 1‰, indicating a dominantly magmatic source. Increasing circulation of fluids in the enveloping sedimentary rocks in the Main and Late stages resulted in smaller negative Eu anomalies in the fluorite, more reducing conditions reflected in the disappearance of hematite and appearance of sulfides, and change in the fluid chemistry (addition of FeCl2 and/or MgCl2).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00126-026-01457-0.Keywords: Fluorite, Peralkaline, Epithermal, REY (lanthanides + Y), Nd-Sm, Isotopes

Introduction

Fluorite is a critical mineral included on the critical minerals list of many jurisdictions including Canada due to its high-risk supply, a variety of uses essential to the modern economy (e.g., lithium batteries), a lack of viable substitutes, and difficulty in recycling (https://www.crmalliance.eu/critical-raw-materials). Fluorite deposits have recently been reclassified by Magotra et al. (2017) based on their lithological association into the following host-rock associations: (1) felsic igneous rocks; (2) carbonatites and associated alkaline rocks; and (3) carbonates and other rocks. Fluorite deposits in the St. Lawrence area of eastern Newfoundland belong to the first group and are associated with the Late Devonian polyphase, peralkaline to metaluminous St. Lawrence Granite (SLG; Figs. 1 and 2a). Fluorite in this area was first discovered in the 1800s and mined intermittently between 1933 and the early 1990s (Van Alstine 1944, 1948; Edwards 1991; Sparkes and Reeves 2015). The deposits of the St. Lawrence area were one of the largest fluorite resources known in Canada, and important on a global scale, supporting one of Canada’s longest-lived mining operations. In 2013, renewed exploration led to the discovery of the AGS deposit and active mining ensuing from 2018 to 2022. Operations restarted in 2025 with the first shipment at the end of August (USGS: https://minerals.usgs.gov/minerals/). The AGS deposit is named for Arthur Gordon Stollery (1947–2011), the founder of Canada Fluorspar Inc. In contrast to most of the fluorite veins in the St Lawrence area, which are hosted in the SLG, the AGS deposit is hosted within the Inlet Group sedimentary rocks, consisting of shales, porphyritic sills of the SLG that intrude the Inlet Group and also a massive equigranular unit of the SLG at ~ 300 m sub-surface (Fig. 2a and b).

This paper summarizes the results of a detailed study of the AGS fluorite deposit that is focused on the relationship of the mineralization to the SLG and the surrounding Inlet Group and the source reservoir(s) of the mineralizing fluids. Previous studies on the fluorite veins were regional and typically included limited sampling from individual veins (Van Alstine 1944; Alstine 1948; Williamson 1956; Strong et al. 1984; Collins 1992; Gagnon et al. 2003; Reeves et al. 2016). In contrast, this study presents a detailed geological and geochemical study of an individual deposit and highlights the value of detailed studies in resolving questions posed by regional-scale studies. Collectively, these new results contribute to our understanding of fluorite mineralization at the AGS deposit and will aid in exploration for similar deposits. In addition, results provide important clues regarding the complexing of rare earth elements (REY: lanthanides + Y) in F-rich fluids, which is considered important in granite-hosted REY mineralization, especially in peralkaline rocks.

Geological setting

The Avalon Zone is the most easterly tectonostratigraphic terrane of the Appalachian Orogeny in Newfoundland (Fig. 1; Williams et al. 1974; Williams 1995). It consists of fault-bounded blocks of Neoproterozoic, arc-related volcanic and sedimentary rocks and minor calc-alkaline to alkaline granitoid rocks that were subsequently deformed during the Neoproterozoic Avalonian Orogeny (Landing 2004; van Staal and Barr 2012). Cambrian‒Early Ordovician platformal sedimentary rocks disconformably and unconformably overlie these varied Precambrian rock types. Late Devonian post-orogenic granites, including the SLG (O’Brien et al. 1996), intrude all of these older rock types, including Cambrian sedimentary rocks. The metamorphic grade ranges up to lower-greenschist facies (Papezik 1974).

In the St. Lawrence area, the oldest strata are the Tonian Burin Group composed mainly of mafic volcanic rocks with tholeiitic signatures (Fig. 2a; Strong et al. 1976, 1978; Krogh et al. 1988; O’Driscoll et al. 2001; Murphy et al. 2008). The Ediacaran Marystown Group, composed of bimodal volcanic rocks, underlies much of the Burin Peninsula (Strong et al. 1976; Sparkes and Dunning 2014). The Cambrian Inlet Group overlies rocks of the Burin and Marystown groups and is composed of fine-grained clastic sedimentary rocks with locally abundant limestone nodules and minor limestone beds, with the abundance of limestone units increasing towards the top (Strong et al. 1978). The AGS fluorite deposit is partially hosted in the Inlet Group that in this area consists primarily of dark shale (Fig. 2a and b). Limestone-rich units are absent from the Inlet Group where fluorite veins occur, although minor carbonate-rich layers and lenses may not outcrop on the surface. The youngest stratigraphic unit in the area is the Late Devonian Rocky Ridge Formation, which outcrops as a discontinuous sequence of riebeckite- and aegirine-bearing felsic volcanic rocks and is interpreted to be the volcanic equivalent of the SLG (Fig. 2a; Strong et al. 1978). Volcanic and volcaniclastic rocks of the Grand Beach complex occur west of the SLG and Dostal et al. (2024) relate these to the SLG, based on a closely similar U-Pb zircon age (375.6 ± 1.1 Ma). However, these rocks lack the peralkaline signature that characterizes parts of the SLG. Other intrusive rocks in the area include the Mount Margaret Gabbro, Loughlins Hill Gabbro, Anchor Drogue granodiorite, and SLG (Fig. 2a; Strong et al. 1978). Except for the SLG, these intrusions have not been dated; however, O’Driscoll et al. (1995) interpreted the Anchor Drogue granodiorite and the Loughlins Hill Gabbro as Neoproterozoic and the Mount Margaret Gabbro as Devonian.

St. Lawrence Granite (SLG)

The SLG is an A-type, peralkaline to metaluminous granite that has been subdivided regionally into the East and West lobes and the Winterland porphyry to the north (Fig. 2a; Teng and Strong 1976; Strong et al. 1978; Kerr et al. 1993a, b, 1995; Miller 1994). Numerous sills and dykes intrude the Inlet Group between the East and West lobes and include the AGS porphyry that partially hosts the AGS deposit. Zircon U-Pb dating provides ages of 374 ± 2 Ma for the East lobe (Kerr et al. 1993b) and 377.2 ± 1.3 Ma for the AGS porphyry (Magyarosi et al. 2019). A quartz-fluorite-molybdenite vein in the West lobe of the SLG was dated at 365 ± 2.8 Ma using Re-Os analysis of molybdenite (Lynch et al. 2012).

The SLG is considered a high-level intrusion based on the presence of miarolitic cavities, the abundance of porphyritic sills and dykes, tuffisites, the presence of a volcanic equivalent (Rocky Ridge Formation; Teng and Strong 1976; Collins 1992; Kerr et al. 1993b), and textures of the vein fluorite that are similar to those in epithermal settings (e.g., banded, colloform, crustiform; Magyarosi et al. 2019; Magyarosi 2022). These various attributes are more common towards the south, where most of the fluorite mineralization occurs. This suggests that the southern part of the SLG intrusion lies closer to the original top of the magma chamber. The East lobe was estimated to have crystallized at a pressure of 0.61 kbar, based on the quartz-geobarometry method of Yang et al. (2021), which equates to a depth of 1.6 km assuming lithostatic conditions, and temperature of about 954 °C using the zircon saturation method (Magyarosi 2022).

The East lobe is the only peralkaline unit of the SLG exposed on surface (Magyarosi 2022). The primary minerals are quartz, perthitic feldspar, and < 5 vol% sodic amphibole and/or aegirine. In contrast, the West lobe lacks these sodic minerals and instead contains < 5 vol% biotite that is mostly altered to chlorite. The AGS porphyry is a quartz-K-feldspar porphyry with a groundmass consisting of quartz, K-feldspar and albite. The Winterland porphyry contains quartz, perthitic feldspar and rare pyroxene phenocrysts in a groundmass of quartz and K-feldspar with sodic-calcic amphibole and pyroxene.

Varying degrees of alteration affected all units of the SLG (Magyarosi 2022). In the East lobe, it ranges from weak to strong with intensity increasing southwards with the most pervasive alteration observed at Chambers Cove at the southernmost tip of the East lobe (Fig. 2a). Types of alteration include albitization of both feldspars, conversion of sodic amphibole to aegirine+magnetite or quartz+hematite±chlorite, and hematization of aegirine and magnetite; a late carbonatization overprints the earlier alteration types. Strong alteration in the AGS porphyry is represented by albitization, chloritization of biotite, hematization of magnetite, and rare carbonatization. Moderate alteration in the West lobe consists of albitization, hematization of magnetite and chloritization of biotite. Greisenization is observed around fluorite veins in all SLG units and is manifested as sericitization of K-feldspar. This is attributed to condensation of an early vapour-rich fluid, exsolved from the granite, mixing with down-welling meteoric water to create a strongly acidic fluid (Magyarosi 2022).

Fluorite mineralization

There are more than 40 known fluorite veins in the St. Lawrence area that range in size from a few cm to 30 m width and up to 3 km length (Reeves et al. 2016). All economic quantities of fluorite are associated with the peralkaline East lobe and the AGS porphyry. The lack of economic fluorite concentrations in the West lobe was attributed to its relatively lower initial Ca content (Magyarosi 2022). Fluorite veins are hosted in the granite, except for the AGS deposit that is partially hosted in the Inlet Group (Fig. 2a and b). The fluorite veins are fault-controlled, have three main orientations (EW, NNW and NW), several generations of fluorite fill (Van Alstine 1948; Strong et al. 1984; Magyarosi et al. 2019), and are typically brecciated, with later generations cementing earlier ones. Locally, extensional zones host high-grade massive fluorite mineralization.

AGS fluorite deposit

The AGS deposit was discovered in 2013 by Canada Fluorspar Inc. (CFI; Sparkes and Reeves 2015; Reeves et al. 2016). Although the Grebe’s Nest occurrence located in the western end of the AGS deposit was known prior to this discovery, the extent of the fluorite mineralization in the area was not realized until renewed exploration by CFI in 2013 (Sparkes and Reeves 2015). The vein system constituting the deposit is approximately 1.85 km long, occurring along a well-developed, NW trending, sinistral strike-slip fault; and consists of several veins running parallel or oblique to each other, with local pinching and swelling (Magyarosi et al. 2019). The individual veins range in width from 2 to 30 m and in length from 400 to 700 m. The deposit is hosted in the Inlet Group sedimentary rocks and the intruding AGS porphyry, but the veins cut a massive equigranular unit of the SLG that was intersected by drilling between 250 and 300 m sub-surface (Fig. 2a and b). Fluorite deposition occurred under dominantly brittle conditions, according to the “fault-valve model” of Sibson et al. (1988). Brittle conditions are indicated by the abundance of breccia textures and the presence of Riedel shears developed oblique to the main fault. The calculated fluorite resource is 9,389,049 tonnes at 32.88% fluorite (NI 43–101 compliant, Sparkes and Reeves 2015).

Fluorite mineralization in the deposit is divided into Early, Main and Late stages, which are further subdivided into 10 substages, based on crosscutting relationships and the sequence of infill in the veins (Table 1; Fig. 3; Magyarosi et al. 2019). Fluorite is the main vein mineral and its colour varies - purple, yellow, green, blue, grey, white, red and red-brown, pink and tan. Fluorite textures indicate syntaxial growth and include hydrothermal breccia, stockwork veins, massive, crustiform, colloform, cockade, comb and zonal crystals, typical of an epithermal setting. Calcite is locally important, particularly in the “High Carbonate Zone” in the eastern part of the deposit, where the amount of calcite locally exceeds 50% of the vein (Sparkes and Reeves 2015). Where present, calcite mostly occurs early in the paragenesis either with purple fluorite or as layers intergrown with banded, fine grained and yellow fluorite. The amount of calcite relative to fluorite decreases in the Main stage but increases again with green fluorite later in the paragenesis. Quartz is common in the Early stage and in the latest part of the last stage. Hematite occurs in the Early stage and in reddish grey fluorite in the Main stage, but it disappears in the later grey fluorite, suggesting a decrease in fO2. Galena and sphalerite are present in the Main and Late stages only. Rare barite and pyrite occur in the Early stage and pyrite also in the Late stage.

The Early stage of fluorite mineralization consists of barren breccia, purple fluorite, and banded fine-grained fluorite alternating with yellow, coarse-grained fluorite (Table 1; Fig. 3a-d). The breccia consists of strongly altered and comminuted pieces of the host rocks (Fig. 3a). Purple fluorite cements altered fragments of host rocks or lines thin veins with a crackle-like breccia texture (Fig. 3b). The next substage is rhythmically banded, mm-scale layers of fine-grained variably coloured and coarser yellow fluorite and calcite (Fig. 3c), resembling colloform textures in places, locally represented by bands of hematite, purple fluorite and quartz (Fig. 3d).

The Main stage is the most important economic phase of fluorite mineralization as it contains the highest grades (~ 98% fluorite; Sparkes and Reeves 2015). It consists of variably banded, reddish brown and grey fluorite and comb-textured grey fluorite separated by a 1- to 2-cm-thick, fine-grained sulfide layer of dark sphalerite and minor galena (Table 1; Fig. 3e). The reddish colour of the first substage is due to the presence of fine-grained hematite (Fig. 3e). The fluorite in this stage is typically massive and occurs in local extensional zones, but in the western end of the deposit it forms hydrothermal breccias.

The Late stage of the paragenesis consists of green fluorite, blue or clear fluorite and quartz with trace pyrite and chalcopyrite (Table 1; Fig. 3f and g). The green fluorite contains minor white and blue layers, locally abundant calcite, galena in the eastern part of the deposit and sphalerite in the western part of the deposit. This is followed by quartz veins or “blastonite” (Fig. 3g) composed of fragments of earlier fluorite stages in a matrix of quartz and fine-grained fluorite. Locally, late pyrite and chalcopyrite occur as crusts on the quartz. Late-stage mineralization typically occurs along late faults and Riedel shears developed oblique to the main vein.

Analytical methods

A total of 115 samples were collected from outcrops at the AGS deposit during the 2017 and 2018 field seasons. The analytical methods for this study included: (1) trace element analysis of fluorite; (2) fluid inclusion analysis; (3) Nd-Sm isotope analysis; (4) carbon and oxygen isotope analysis of calcite; and (5) sulfur isotope analysis of sphalerite. A brief description of the analytical methods is provided below. Additional details are included in the Supplementary Material.

Rare earth element and selected trace element concentrations in fluorite were determined in situ by LA–ICP–MS on nine samples representing all substages of fluorite mineralization. Two to four areas were selected in each sample with five spots analyzed per area. Analyzed elements included the REY (REE and Y, except Pm), Na, Ca, Rb, Sr, Zr and Ba.

Fluid inclusion analyses were conducted on 13 samples, representing every substage of fluorite mineralization. Petrographic study incorporated the Fluid Inclusion Assemblage (FIA) approach whereby demonstrated petrographically-related inclusions are interpreted to represent a fluid-trapping event (Goldstein and Reynolds 1994; Bodnar 2003). The temperature of homogenization (Th), eutectic melting (Te), hydrohalite melting (Tm(hh)) and final ice-melting (Tm(ice)) were measured in two-phase (liquid (L) + vapour (V) inclusions (Shepherd et al. 1985). The cations present (e.g., Na, Ca, Fe) in inclusion fluids were inferred from first melting or Te values compared with the known eutectic temperatures of common aqueous‒salt systems (Steele-MacInnis et al. 2016). Salinities were calculated as equivalent weight% NaCl (eq. wt% NaCl) using Tm(ice) and the equation of Bodnar (1993), whereas salinities of inclusions with more complex chemistries (eq. wt% NaCl + CaCl2) and their Na/Na/(Ca + Na) ratios were determined using Tm(hh) and Tm(ice).

Neodymium-Sm isotope analyses were completed on six fluorite samples from all substages of mineralization. Fluorite was weighed and spiked with a calibrated, known amount of mixed 150Nd-149Sm tracer solution (Wasserburg et al. 1981). The sample was dissolved, Nd and Sm were separated by conventional cation and HDEHP-based chromatography and the isotopic compositions were analyzed in static mode by Multi-Collector ICP- Mass Spectrometry (Creaser et al. 1997; Unterschutz et al. 2002; Schmidberger et al. 2007).

Carbon and oxygen isotope analyses were conducted in calcite intergrown with fluorite in seven samples from three substages of fluorite mineralization where calcite was present. In addition, carbon and oxygen isotope analyses were carried out on calcite from an altered granite sample from Chamber’s Cove. Two µg of material was removed from every sample via micro-drilling and analyzed using a Thermo Gas Bench II - Delta V IRMS. Isotopic compositions are reported in delta (δ) notation, with carbon isotope analyses reported relative to the Vienna Peedee belemnite (VPDB) standard and oxygen isotope analysis reported relative to the Vienna Standard Mean Ocean Water (VSMOW).

Sulfur isotope analyses were completed on sphalerite associated with fluorite in five samples from the Main and Late stages of fluorite mineralization. Individual sphalerite crystals were sampled via micro-drilling, SO2 was separated then analyzed in a Delta XP isotope ratio mass spectrometer (Thermo Finnigan, Germany). All sulfur analyses are reported using the standard δ notation in units of ‰ relative to the international Vienna-Canyon Diablo Troilite (V-CDT) standard.

Results

REY patterns in fluorite and host rocks

The trace element data of fluorite and host rocks are included in ESM Table S1. The ΣREY of the fluorites show an enrichment of between 5 and 1000 times chondritic values (highest in the grey fluorite from the Main stage and the lowest in the blue fluorite from the Late stage), except for Eu and Y (Fig. 4). Overall, the patterns are characterized by heavy REE (HREE) enrichment relative to light REE (LREE); negative Eu anomalies and positive Y anomalies (Fig. 4).

Chondrite-normalized REY plots (McDonough and Sun 1995) of fluorites. The number under the Eu anomaly is Eu/Eu*. Averages from the last plot represent the average compositions of fluorite from each substage and host rocks

In contrast to the fluorite samples, the REY patterns of the SLG and Inlet Group are characterized by a weak negative slope from La to Lu, indicative of LREE enrichment relative to HREE (Fig. 5). The SLG also displays a strong negative Eu anomaly, some samples display variably weak positive or negative Ce anomalies, and some display a very weak negative Y anomaly. The Inlet Group samples display a weak negative Eu anomaly and one sample that contains several < 1 mm thick fluorite veins has a weak positive Y anomaly.

Chondrite-normalized REY plots (McDonough and Sun 1995) of host rocks

The size of the Eu anomaly is largest in the SLG (average Eu/Eu*=0.03) and smallest in the Inlet Group sedimentary rocks (average Eu/Eu*=0.23; Figs. 4 and 5). The Eu/Eu* is consistent at 0.04 in Early- and Main-stage fluorites, but it is lower in the green fluorite (Eu/Eu*=0.10) and the blue fluorite (Eu/Eu*=0.17).

Based on the amount of Y relative to Ho and La, the fluorites in the AGS deposit can be divided into four generations: (1) Early stage and reddish grey fluorite from the Main stage (Y/Y*=4.17–5.05); (2) grey fluorite (Y/Y*=2.58); (3) green fluorite (Y/Y*=4.83); and (4) blue fluorite (Y/Y*=6.07; Fig. 6a; Bau and Dulski 1995; Loges et al. 2013). The first generation may be further subdivided based on changing La/Ho ratios, although there is significant overlap between them.

Fluid inclusion studies

Fluid inclusions were studied in all fluorite stages. Fluid Inclusion Assemblages (FIA) define trails parallel to growth zones in fluorite crystals and thus primary in origin (P-type), or occur in trails crosscutting growth zones and thus of secondary origin (S-type). Some inclusions also occur as large, randomly dispersed arrays or as isolated inclusions, and have been classified as inclusions of indeterminate origin (I-type) since evidence of P- or S-type origin is lacking (Bodnar 2003); representative images are shown in Fig. 7. Most inclusions are two-phase liquid-vapour (L-V) type with liquid comprising 85–95% by volume. Vapour-rich inclusions are rare, but where observed occur along with liquid-rich inclusions as part of assemblages comprised of elongate inclusions oriented perpendicular to growth zones in fluorite (Fig. 7a), particularly in finely banded colloform layers. No liquid phase was observed in these inclusions, but some minor liquid component may be present on the walls of inclusions. Some P- and I-type inclusions also contain solids, including small hematite grains and cubic minerals (possibly fluorite; Fig. 7b). These minerals do not appear in all inclusions within an individual FIA, and show no phase changes upon heating to 350 °C, and are attributed to accidental entrapment versus being daughter minerals that precipitated post-fluid entrapment.

The results for microthermometry are included in ESM Table S2. All measurements are for L-V type inclusions with no phase changes observed in rare V-rich types. The P- and S-type inclusions show a narrow range for homogenization temperature (Th) values within an FIA (generally < 5 °C) and consistent with the trapping of a single phase homogenous fluid versus an unmixed fluid and that post-entrapment re-equilibration (e.g., necking, stretching, and leakage; Fall and Bodnar 2018) did not occur. The I-type inclusions commonly have similar degrees of fill and Th values as P-types in the same sample, which also precludes their post-entrapment modification. However, some large, isolated inclusions containing ~ 30–50% vapour record much higher Th values to 400 °C, which overlap some of the data reported previously by Strong et al. (1984). Careful petrographic examination of these inclusions reveals small cracks at their edges that suggest leakage (e.g., Zhang and Audétat 2023), therefore these higher Th values are not considered indicative of entrapment because they do not define an FIA (e.g., group of inclusions) and also violate one of Roedder’s rules, in this case inclusions not remaining isochoric post-entrapment (Bodnar 2003).

The L-rich inclusions were divided into two main types (i.e., 1 and 2) based on microthermometric data and derived compositions (Fig. 8). Type 1 inclusions, only recorded as P-types in Early-stage fluorite mineralization (i.e., purple, yellow and hematite-fluorite phases), were characterized by Th values ranging from 93.2 to 160.8 °C and eutectic temperature (Te) values from − 48.6 to −55.9 °C, thus consistent with a bulk composition in the H2O-NaCl-CaCl2 system. Inclusion salinities vary from 10.1 to 27.5 eq. wt% NaCl (Fig. 8a-f) and based on the hydrohalite melting temperature (Tm(hh)) and the final ice-melting temperature (Tm(ice)) values their Na/(Na + Ca) ratios are estimated at 0.12 to 0.65.

Bivariate plots of salinity vs. first ice melting temperatures (a, b, c) and salinity vs. homogenization temperatures (d, e, f) for Type 1 and Type 2 inclusions in Early-, Main- and Late-stage fluorites

Type 2 inclusions are recorded as both P- and I-types in the Main- and Late-stage fluorite, and as S-types in Early- stage fluorite. They have similar Th values as Type 1 inclusions that ranged from 101.2 to 141.1 °C in the Main stage and 105.7 to 183.1 °C in the Late stage (Fig. 8d-f). However, their freezing behavior suggests a different bulk composition (Fig. 8a-c) with Te values of −33.6 to −44.4 °C indicating the presence of salts such as MgCl2 and FeCl2, in addition to NaCl and CaCl2 (Steele-MacInnis et al. 2016). These inclusions also have a narrower range for salinities (9.7 to 16.3 eq. wt% NaCl) compared to Type 1 inclusions.

Discussion

REY patterns of fluorite

The enrichment of fluorite in HREE relative to LREE is due to the lanthanide contraction (a subtle progressive decrease in ionic radii from La to Lu) that results in the HREE forming more stable fluoride complexes than the LREE (Wood 1990; Bau and Dulski 1995; Möller et al. 1998; van Hinsberg et al. 2010) indicating the strong control of complexation processes. The overall positive chondrite-normalized slopes (Fig. 4) indicate that REE were mostly transported as F complexes rather than Cl complexes, as the latter would result in negative slopes (Wood 1990; Payne et al. 2023).

The origin of the negative Eu anomalies is attributed to an inheritance from the SLG, typical of fluorite associated with felsic magmatism (Magotra et al. 2017). The decrease in the size of the Eu anomaly in Late-stage fluorites requires the fluid to contain more Eu3+, because only Eu3+ is compatible in the fluorite structure due to the similar sizes of the ionic radii of Eu3+ and Ca2+. Reducing conditions (below the HM buffer and the SO4:H2S line) in this stage, as suggested by the lack of hematite and appearance of sulfides, in a slightly acidic fluid should reduce the Eu3+ to Eu2+ even at low temperatures (Bau 1991). However, complexation extends the Eu3+ stability towards lower fO2, therefore reduction to Eu2+ is limited to more reducing environments (Wood 1990; Bau 1991). Therefore, the smaller negative Eu anomaly is interpreted to be the influence of the surrounding Inlet Group sedimentary rocks, which are characterized by a much smaller negative Eu anomaly than the SLG (Fig. 4b). Other smaller veins in the St. Lawrence area that are not entirely hosted by the granite, such as the Anchor Drogue and Lawn Barite veins, also display smaller negative Eu anomalies than the typical granite-hosted veins (Collins 1992).

The positive Y anomaly is typical of fluorite associated with felsic rocks and is attributed to Y forming more stable di-fluoride complexes than the REE in F-rich fluids (Loges et al. 2013). Additionally, the size of the Y anomaly strongly depends on the composition of the fluid (Bau and Dulski 1995; Loges et al. 2013) and indicates two major changes: one in the Main stage from the reddish grey to the grey fluorite and another in the Late stage from the green to the blue fluorite.

Most of the fluorite from all substages are primary hydrothermal, except the blue fluorite, which plots along the secondary crystallization trend (Fig. 6b), suggesting possible REE remobilization from earlier fluorite with the relative enrichment of Tb over La due to its higher stability as a fluoride complex (Williams-Jones et al. 2012). This is also consistent with the significantly lower ΣREE content of the blue fluorite (Fig. 4).

The REY patterns presented are similar to those of previous studies on fluorites from the Grebe’s Nest occurrence (Strong et al. 1984; Collins 1992; Gagnon et al. 2003), located at the western end of the AGS deposit. Patterns mostly resemble those for green and blue fluorites, which were the ones exposed at the time of those studies. However, some of the green fluorite of Collins (1992) show enrichment in LREE relative to HREE, which was attributed to relatively earlier, less fractionated fluids, also suggesting that small-scale variations even within one substage can be significant. The REY patterns of fluorites from other veins in the St. Lawrence area are overall very similar to those here displaying strong negative Eu and positive Y anomalies and, in general, having enrichment in the HREE over the LREE (Collins 1992).

Fluid inclusion analysis

Both Type 1 and 2 inclusions are characterized by Th values between ~ 100 and 150 °C but require pressure correction to estimate true trapping temperatures using the depth of vein formation and type of confining pressure (i.e., lithostatic versus hydrostatic). Isochores for the average fluid inclusion compositions from all fluorite stages were constructed using the computer package FLUIDS (Bakker 2003). These indicate entrapment temperatures at 0.61 kbars, the crystallization pressure estimated for the East lobe (Magyarosi 2022), are ~ 30 °C higher than Th value at lithostatic conditions, thus ~ 130 to 180 °C. If the confining pressure was partly hydrostatic, which is very likely given the evidence for some involvement of meteoric-sourced fluids (see below), then the latter temperatures would be lowered accordingly.

The presence of co-existing L- and V-rich inclusions in some FIAs suggests the heterogeneous trapping of end-member fluid densities of an unmixed parental fluid. These observations indicate that the precipitation of fluorite was associated with intermittent boiling that could be explained by rapid depressurization related to pressure cycling between lithostatic and hydrostatic confining pressures which is also consistent with the vein textures that show they formed at shallow crustal levels in an epithermal environment. Pressure cycling could account for some of the variation in Th values with inclusions trapped at lower pressure conditions have higher Th values, although the temperature of entrapment was the same for all inclusions. Additionally, variable cooling of the fluids could also account for the difference in Th values.

The difference in fluid chemistry between Type 1 and 2 inclusions suggests possible changes in the mineralizing fluids from the volumetrically minor Early stage to the Main and Late stages of fluorite mineralization. Type 1 inclusions are restricted to the Early stage and have salinities between 10.1 and 27.5 eq. wt% NaCl with variable Na/(Na + Ca) ratios, suggesting the involvement of different fluid reservoirs or interaction of the fluids with wall rocks during the Early stage of fluorite mineralization. Type 2 inclusions occur as primary types in the Main- and Late-stage fluorites as well as in secondary trails cutting earlier fluorite generations and are interpreted to represent those associated with the main generation of fluorite mineralization at the AGS deposit. Compared to Type 1 inclusions, Type 2 have a narrower range in salinity (9.7 to 16.3 eq. wt% NaCl) with Te values of −33.6 to −44.4 °C that indicate these fluids contain divalent cations such as MgCl2 and FeCl2, in addition to NaCl and CaCl2, although the mineralogy of the granite (i.e., Fe-rich amphibole and pyroxene) suggests Fe is more likely.

The above interpretation in part contrasts with some of the conclusions of previous workers. In their earlier study, Strong et al. (1984) reported a wider range for Th values (100 to 450 °C) than herein and further suggested fluid unmixing. Subsequently, Collins (1992) presented results from a more detailed study of granite-hosted fluorite veins, as well as the Grebe’s Nest occurrence in the western end of the AGS deposit. He also reported a wide range for Th (50 to 500 °C), but with a pronounced maximum ~ 100 °C (i.e., 90% of data between 70 and 140 °C) with no evidence of boiling reported. The latter is consistent with our results with only rare evidence for intermittent entrapment of heterogeneous L- and V-rich fluids during pressure cycling. These earlier studies pre-dated the FIA protocol approach applied herein and only limited data on the setting of measured inclusions is reported. Therefore, the much higher and varied Th values reported by these authors likely relate to post-entrapment modification, as interpreted for anomalously high Th values for isolated inclusions during this study. Both Strong et al. (1984) and Collins (1992) also recorded a similar range of fluid salinities to this study (6 to 25 eq. wt% NaCl and ~ 12 and 25 eq. wt% NaCl, respectively), and Collins (1992) reported Te values having similar ranges to those recorded from Type 1 and Type 2 inclusions above. This suggests that the fluids associated with the formation of the AGS deposit were analogous to those responsible for mineralization hosted within the SLG based on these earlier studies. Further detailed fluid inclusion studies focusing on mineralization in the SLG are required to determine if fluid boiling was an important process during the earlier magmatic stages of fluid evolution in the SLG, as suggested by Strong et al. (1984).

Nd-Sm isotope analysis

The ~ 361 Ma Nd-Sm age defined by four of the six fluorite samples is considered to represent the time of fluorite crystallization. Of the two points that deviate, the blue fluorite likely relates to modification due to interaction with post-vein formation fluids as also suggested by its lower ΣREY values and elevated Tb/La ratio with no change in the Tb/Ca ratio (Fig. 6b). As for the second analysis, the current data provide no insight at present. The 361 ± 14 Ma age overlaps within error the age of the East lobe of the SLG (373.4 ± 1 Ma; Kerr et al. 1993b; replotted using IsoplotR of Vermeesch 2018), but potentially much younger by ~ 13 Ma.

The mineralization in the AGS deposit is inferred to be genetically associated with the massive equigranular unit of the SLG underlying the AGS area and cut by the fluorite veins (Magyarosi 2022), however, as no samples of this granite could be obtained as part of this study its precise age remains unknown. This granite is either part of the East lobe, which is the only exposed phase of the SLG associated with fluorite mineralization, or geochemically similar to the East lobe but represents a younger magmatic event. This scenario is not unlike that found in the Cornubian batholith of SW England where several phases and related hydrothermal activity spanned over 25 million years (Chen et al. 1993; Chesley et al. 1993; Moscati and Neymark 2020) and where fluorite mineralization postdated the host granite by ~ 40 million years and is interpreted to be related to younger, unexposed phases of the batholith (Chesley et al. 1993).

If the underlying granite is the same age as the East lobe, then the mineralization is probably still related to the intrusion of the SLG. According to a detailed study by Zhao et al. (2004) on the cooling rates of granite and syenite associated with uranium mineralization, hydrothermal mineralization was noted to lag ~ 25 to 30 Ma behind the crystallization age of the associated granite. The plutons studied by Zhao et al. (2004) are considered “high-heat producing” (HHP) due to their elevated Th, U and Th/U values and associated uranium mineralization. The SLG is also mostly a HHP pluton based on its Th and U concentrations and Th/U ratios (Magyarosi 2022) and a related U occurrence (Fig. 2a). A potentially younger timing for hydrothermal activity related to vein fluorite formation versus an age of the SLG is also suggested by a Re-Os age of 365 ± 2.8 Ma obtained for molybdenite intergrown with vein fluorite sampled from the West lobe (Lynch et al. 2012).

The ƐNd values at ~ 361 Ma range between 1.7 and 2.9 (except the blue fluorite), which compares well to the ƐNd value for the SLG of 3.2 (Kerr et al. 1995) and suggests that both the fluorite mineralization and the SLG originate from a common mantle reservoir; the slightly lower values for fluorite are attributed to the influence of the surrounding Inlet Group sedimentary rocks. Although ƐNd values of the Inlet Group have not been determined, Middle Cambrian clastic sedimentary rocks in New Brunswick have negative ƐNd values, suggesting that the source for these rocks in Avalonia was crustal (Satkoski et al. 2010).

Carbon and O isotope analysis

The isotopic data for calcites from different stages are similar and thus interpreted together below. The δ18OVSMOW values cover a wide range and partially overlap those measured by Coleman et al. (1984) in calcite from altered SLG (13.1–18.7‰). Using the fractionation equation of O’Neil et al. (1969) and estimated temperatures of 130 to 180 °C (pressure corrected fluid inclusion values), this indicates δ18OH2O values of −5.6 to 5.1‰ and − 2.0 to 8.7‰, respectively (Table 3). The lower range overlaps with previously published data for three quartz samples from Big Meadow Woods fluorite vein reported by Strong et al. (1984) which yielded δ18OVSMOW values of 13.9 to 14.5‰ and equate to δ18OH2O of −3.9 to −2.9 and 0.9 to 1.5‰ for temperatures of 130 and 180 °C (Zheng 1993). These calculated δ18OH2O values implicate meteoric water as one component with at least one other reservoir having a minimum δ18OH2O value of about 10‰. Strong et al. (1984) also reported δ18OH2O data based on analyzing fluid inclusions released via decrepitating fluorite samples which yielded values between − 5.2 and − 10.1‰ which overlaps with the low δ18O nature of one of the fluids based on the calcite data.

High δ18O fluid values suggest two possible reservoirs: one magmatic and the other sedimentary. The upper end of the range for normal magmatic fluids is about 10‰ (Sheppard 1986), which exceeds the available data for the SLG based on δ18Oquartz values of 6‰ for four samples in Strong et al. (1984); the latter implies δ18OH2O values slightly below this by 1‰. However, these samples are from a part of the SLG that records extreme alkali metasomatism (Strong 1982) and therefore may not represent the primary signature (see discussion in Strong et al. 1984). Regarding the potential sedimentary reservoir, the AGS deposit area is surrounded and overlain by the Inlet Group, a siliciclastic unit that includes locally abundant limestone beds, which would provide a possible 18O-enriched reservoir. However, there is no evidence for the presence of significant amounts of carbonate strata present in the AGS area currently. We conclude therefore that the elevated δ18OH2O values likely have a magmatic component but cannot exclude at present the contribution of some other component, albeit of lesser significance.

Calcite in the deposit formed from an oxidized fluid where H2CO3 was the dominant carbonate species versus a reduced form (i.e., CH4), as inferred from the presence of hematite and trace barite in the fluorite veins. As there is no fluid-related fractionation between calcite and H2CO3 at 130 to 180 °C (Ohmoto 1972), it can be assumed, therefore, that δ13Ccalcite ≈ δ13 ≈ δ13Cfluid with the latter values between − 6.5 and − 5.6‰. This very narrow range indicates that the fluid had a uniform δ13C value with little variation in its intensive parameters during vein formation (pH, T, fO2). The δ13C data overlap with the expected signature for carbon sourced from a mantle reservoir (−7 to −5‰, Keller and Hoefs 1995; −8 to −2‰, Cartigny 2005) or an average continental crust signature (Ohmoto and Rye 1979). An alternative model could source carbon from the Inlet Group, such as carbonate and clastic component which mix to give the above value; although, as noted above, there is no evidence for the occurrence of significant amounts of carbonate strata currently in the vicinity of the AGS deposit.

Sulfur isotope analysis

The δ34S for sphalerite indicate two very different signatures at ~−32‰ in sulfides from the Main stage and − 1‰ in sulfides from the Late stage. The depleted δ34S value is most simply explained by a biogenic source of sulfur for the Main stage, whereas the − 1‰ value suggests a magmatic source for the sulfur in the Late stage. However, the presence of hematite, lack of sulfides in the Early stage and in the first substage of the Main stage and the disappearance of hematite and appearance of sulfides thereafter, is consistent with a change from oxidizing to reducing in the Main stage, which is marked by the rapid precipitation of sphalerite and galena (Fig. 3e). Therefore, sulfur could have originated from a magmatic source of −1‰ and underwent transient oxidation which caused fractionation of sulfur to higher SO4:H2S ratios to generate H2S with a depleted ≈ δ34S signature in the Main stage (e.g., Gammons et al. 2020; Hastie et al. 2023). A return to more reducing conditions in the later vein stages lowered the SO4:H2S ratio such that fluid-mediated oxidation did not occur with sphalerite reflecting the primary δ34S value of the magmatic reservoir.

Formation of mineralized veins in the AGS deposit

Early stage

A magmatic contribution to the Early-stage fluorite is suggested based on the ƐNd values, REY patterns of fluorites, the δ13C values around − 6‰, and elevated δ18OH2O values of up to 10‰ for calcite. Some data can also be interpreted to argue for a model that involved convection of surface-derived (i.e., meteoric) fluid driven by a cooling, high-level intrusion. However, based on overwhelming evidence from this and previous studies we prefer a dominantly magmatic origin for the ore fluids while acknowledging input from external sources.

The mineralizing fluid was initially acidic, as reflected by greisenization marginal to the fluorite veins and in the barren breccia that predated fluorite-rich material in veins (Fig. 3a). This is attributed to condensation of a V-rich fluid, exsolved early from the granite and mixed with down-welling oxidized meteoric water to create a strong acidic fluid (Magyarosi 2022). Oxidizing conditions throughout the Early stage are indicated from fine-grained hematite and trace amounts of barite intergrown with fluorite and from the alteration of arfvedsonite to aegirine in the granite (Magyarosi 2022). The characteristics of this fluid were consistent with a magmatic fluid exsolved from the SLG during its later stages of crystallization, albeit one that has cooled substantially to < 200 °C. Detailed fluid inclusion studies at Strange Lake, a peralkaline complex and suggested older analogue to the SLG (Magyarosi 2022), indicated that the initial fluid exsolved from the cooling magma was aqueous-carbonic with hydrocarbons as the main carbon species (Vasyukova and Williams-Jones 2018). This fluid oxidized upon cooling with conversion of CH4 to CO2 and resulting in the fluid becoming more acidic due to the availability of H. The appearance of calcite with purple fluorite in the Early stage suggests that the fluid became less acidic over time, because the solubility of calcite was high in low pH and decreased with increasing pH (Coto et al. 2012). This was also indicated at Strange Lake (Vasyukova and Williams-Jones 2018). A magmatic origin for C in the AGS Deposit is indicated by the C isotope analysis of calcite intergrown with fluorite, and is consistent with other studies that note that carbon commonly occurs as a volatile in peralkaline granites (see above, Vasyukova and Williams-Jones 2018; Dostal et al. 2014; Ducharme 2020).

The source of Ca was from alteration of primary Ca-bearing minerals in the SLG (White et al. 2005; Magyarosi 2022), although minor additional sources, such as carbonate-rich beds in the Inlet Group which have not been analyzed for δ13C, cannot be ruled out. The enrichment of the fluid in Ca ultimately resulted in fluorite formation which, based on experimental studies (Richardson and Holland 1979a, b) may have occurred due to decrease in T (except in very dilute aqueous solutions below 100 °C); fluid dilution due to mixing with meteoric water, as suggested by the δ18OH2O values; or an increase in pH of original acidic fluid (pH ≤ 3). The fluid inclusion data for the Early stage record cooling fluids (i.e., from originally higher temperatures) and possible fluid mixing based on the large range of salinity and Na/(Na + Ca) ratios.

Main stage

The Main stage represents the peak of hydrothermal activity as reflected by the more massive fluorite and less obvious banding compared to the Early stage. In places, fluorite precipitated in extensional zones up to 30 m that suggests a very high fluid-rock ratio (Fig. 3e). The change in accessory mineralogy from hematite to sphalerite and galena indicates a decrease in fO2 (Table 5). The decrease in the Y anomaly in the grey fluorite, the smaller range in salinity and addition of Fe and/or Mg chlorides in addition to NaCl and CaCl2 in the mineralizing fluid suggest changes in the fluid chemistry. These changes are interpreted to result from widening of the fault zone, which enabled increased circulation of the mineralizing fluid with the enveloping Inlet Group sedimentary rocks. Fluorite precipitation occurred between 130 and 180 °C and was most likely due to changes in pH rather than fluid mixing, although the latter cannot be ruled out.

A summary of the evolution of the AGS deposit

Increased influence of the Inlet Group is consistent with the mobilization of bacteriogenic-mediated sulfur from the enclosing sedimentary rocks, which is supported by the first appearance of sulfides in the Main stage, Pb isotopic data for galena which implicates the Inlet Group as the source (Pochereva 2019), and the more common occurrence of sulfides in peripheral and sedimentary-rock-hosted veins in the St. Lawrence area (Van Alstine 1948; Williamson 1956; Wilson 2000; Reeves et al. 2016). If the sulfur is magmatic then the data reflects an oxidized fluid with high SO4:H2S that generated a depleted δ34SH2S signature in the fluid inhered by the sphalerite.

Late stage

The Late stage represents waning of hydrothermal activity resulting in a decrease in the fluid-rock ratio. Fluorite occurs along faults separating material formed in the Early and Main stages, and also in late cross-cutting faults and shears oriented at oblique angles to the main vein. The blue fluorite, representing the latest fluorite substage, appears to be remobilized from earlier mineralization (Fig. 6b). Late-stage vein fluorite indicates a more significant influence from the Inlet Group sedimentary rocks, as shown by the decreasing size of the Eu anomaly in REY patterns (Table 5). There was also a decrease in the amount of fluorite and appearance of locally abundant calcite. However, there is no demonstrable change in fluid composition from the Main stage to the Late stage according to the fluid inclusion analysis.

The δ34S in sphalerite from the Late stage indicates a magmatic source for sulfur. The absence of variation in calcite δ¹³C values during the Late Stage indicates a magmatic source for carbon as well (Ohmoto and Rye 1979). Similarly, δ18OH2O values are comparable to those of the Early Stage, suggesting continued mixing of magmatic and meteoric fluids during the Late Stage. The overall increase in the abundance of calcite in Late-stage mineralization may be due to progressive decrease in F in the fluids exsolved from the associated granite magma compared to the earlier stages. Additionally, the decrease in F would make these hydrothermal fluids less acidic, which would stabilize carbonate. This is also supported by the observation that carbonate alteration overprints earlier types of alteration within the SLG (Magyarosi 2022).

Deposit model and conclusions

This research represents the most comprehensive study of any fluorite vein in the St. Lawrence area and highlights the importance of more detailed versus regional studies in this area and elsewhere. Neodymium-Sm analysis, REY analysis, microthermometry of fluid inclusions, and stable isotopes (O, C, S) of relevant mineral species in well documented samples from all ore stages of the AGS deposit support the following deposit model. Fluorite mineralization relates to magmatic fluids exsolved at depth from an inferred parental peralkaline unit of the A-type St. Lawrence Granite, with a maximum constrained age of about 374 Ma. The interaction of an early magmatic V-rich fluid with oxidized surface-derived fluids formed an acidic fluid responsible for widespread fracturing of the overlying rocks and greisenization marginal to and predating vein fluorite. Permeable zones in the overlying sedimentary country rocks (i.e., Inlet Group) allowed the continued ingress of meteoric water and its mixing with the magmatic-sourced fluids. An apparent lack of Ca in this early fluid pulse prevented fluorite precipitation, in contrast to posited later exsolved, magmatic fluid that partitioned residual F as HF and reacted with already crystallized Ca-bearing minerals, dissolving Ca and transporting it as CaCl2. This F-bearing fluid ascended to the upper parts of the system where it was episodically released, likely due to fluid overpressuring akin to the Sibson et al. (1988) fault-valve model. Fluid inclusion and isotopic data indicate that the main stages of vein fluorite mineralization formed from mixing of cooled saline magmatic fluids with meteoric fluids, which together with the vein textures, suggest an epithermal-type setting.
