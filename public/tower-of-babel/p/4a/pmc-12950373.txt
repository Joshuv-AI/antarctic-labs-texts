Foot and ankle biomaterials: a comprehensive review of current applications, challenges, and future directions
Authors: Peter Elvin, Amayrani Sanchez, Anthony Allen Reyes, Hagner Andrade, Meelad Karami, Hooman T Mir, Javier La Fontaine, Danieli Rodrigues, Claudia Biguetti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetallic biomaterials enable successful reconstruction and fixation of skeletal tissues by supporting repair, load-bearing function, and anatomical alignment in foot and ankle surgery. However, the unique anatomic and biomechanical demands of this region, combined with challenging pathologies such as flatfoot and Charcot neuroarthropathy, present distinct challenges, with effective intervention requiring targeted biomaterial solutions and precise indications. Furthermore, metallic biomaterials currently represent the most predominant and clinically validated fixation systems in foot and ankle surgical reconstructions, while also generating the largest body of outcome-based evidence. This review summarizes and discusses their contemporary applications, outcomes, limitations, unmeet needs, and future directions based on clinical literature between 2020 and 2024. Emerging innovations, including 3D-printed titanium implants for patient-specific reconstructions, surface-engineered alloys designed to improve osseointegration, with infection mitigation properties for at risk patient populations, and the exploration of bioresorbable/biointegrative magnesium, as well as non-metallic alternative materials (e.g. polymerics) were examined. Persistent unmet needs identified in the literature include hardware challenges in osteoporotic bone and neuropathic patients, ion leeching, cyclic fatigue, economic burden, imaging artifact interference, and lack of long-term data or clinical trials on innovative implant designs and manufacturing approaches. Lastly, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging of titanium, nitinol, and stainless steel is provided to offer an analysis on biomaterial-specific microstructural features, such as surface roughness and porosity, that play a role in influencing tissue integration, corrosion behavior, and mechanical performance in reconstructive surgeries. The Translational Potential of this Article: This review provides clinically actionable guidance for selecting metallic biomaterials tailored to the biomechanical and pathological demands of foot and ankle surgery, supporting evidence-based decisions of selection of hardwares, improved fixation strategies, and reduced complication rates. It also adds recent insights to inform the development and optimization of next-generation devices based on patient- and site-specific requirements, such as 3D-printed constructs and surface-engineered implants, designed to enhance fusion, reconstruction, and limb salvage outcomes.Keywords: Ankle, Biomaterials, Foot, Implants, Orthopaedics, Reconstruction

Introduction

The use of orthopaedic biomaterials has significantly advanced the field of foot and ankle reconstruction, providing surgeons with essential devices for tissue repair, load-bearing support, and functional recovery. However, while a substantial body of research has explored biomaterials for general orthopaedic applications - such as joint replacements, spinal devices, and fracture fixation - there remains a notable scarcity of reviews that specifically address the current knowledge on biomaterials’ performance in the reconstruction or restoration of function in the lower extremity.

Similar to alternative subdisciplines in orthopaedics, foot and ankle surgery applications of orthopaedic devices require meticulous selection of biomaterials, with particular emphasis on materials that possess superior mechanical performance in terms of resistance to fatigue, wear, and corrosion [1,2]. Given these considerations, materials such as titanium (Ti) and its alloys (mainly Ti-6Al-4V), stainless steel, cobalt-based alloys, nitinol, and magnesium-based compounds are frequently favored over alternatives such as polymers and ceramics [3]. Therefore, this comprehensive review provides the current landscape of metallic biomaterials used in lower extremity orthopaedics, outlining their advantages, disadvantages, unique properties, and clinical applications from literature ranging from January 2020 until December 2024 and indexed in PubMed/MEDLINE. In addition to clinically focused studies, non-clinical and cross-disciplinary literature (including engineering, materials science, and translational research reports) were incorporated in the discussion to contextualize mechanistic considerations, implementation challenges, unmet clinical needs, and emerging research opportunities relevant to orthopaedic practice applicable to the foot and ankle. Although this review emphasizes metallic biomaterials due to their predominance and extensive clinical outcome reporting in osseous-based foot and ankle surgical reconstruction, non-metallic alternatives (including polymers, ceramics, carbon-based constructs, and allografts) are also discussed when clinically relevant to provide a more comprehensive and contextualized view of the current biomaterials landscape. Overall, this review summarizes current clinical applications according to relevant clinical literature, critically examines material-specific limitations, implementation challenges, and emerging translational directions providing insights for the future of foot and ankle biomaterials.

Our synthesis (Table 1) shows that the most reported biomaterials in peer reviewed clinical articles include titanium alloys (e.g., Ti-6Al-4V), stainless steel, nitinol, and magnesium alloys (e.g., MgYREZr). Most studies focus on applications in the forefoot and ankle, while titanium alloys and stainless steel are reported across multiple anatomical sites of the lower extremity.

Biomaterials in forefoot applications

Titanium

Most Ti-based devices in forefoot surgery over the past decade have been titanium plates, primarily used for 1st metatarsophalangeal joint (MPJ) arthrodesis to correct hallux valgus deformities [4]. Hallux valgus, commonly known as a bunion, is a progressive deformity characterized by lateral deviation of the big toe and medial prominence of the first metatarsal head, leading to pain, inflammation, and altered biomechanics of the foot [5]. Surgical correction, often referred to as a bunionectomy (Fig. 2E), involves correcting the deformity by reestablishing anatomical alignment of the 1st MPJ. Titanium implants and screws have been frequently used to stabilize the osteotomy or fusion site during this procedure [6].

In addition, Ti has also been utilized to compose frames and pins for procedures such as arthrodesis, the surgical fusion of a pathological joint [6]. Ehredt et al. (2021) demonstrated no differences in the strength of a 3-screw fixation method to a 5-hole locking plate method, consisting of titanium alloy and stainless steel, used in the fusion of the 1st tarsometatarsal joint [6].

In addition to Ti alloy plates, Ti-based interfragmentary compression locking screws have also been employed in metatarsal base procedures to correct hallux valgus deformities, demonstrating satisfactory mechanical properties [6,7]. While implant design can vary depending on what joint is being replaced, a typical construction consists of a Ti alloy stem anchored and integrated into the bone and a highly polished articular surface, such as ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene (UHMWPE), designed to replicate the function of the native joint [8]. This application is further illustrated in Fig. 2G.

Finally, Ti-based hardware has been used in more complex applications within forefoot surgery, including trauma. For instance, fractures at the metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction of the 5th metatarsal (Fig. 2D) (known as a Jones fractures), frequently require open reduction internal fixation (ORIF, Fig. 2F) [9]. A study by Li et al. (2020) reported 88.9 % of the 18 patients demonstrated satisfactory radiographic results after Ti-based implants use in these fractures, with no secondary displacement, foot deformities, or post-traumatic arthritis [10].

Nitinol (Ni-Ti)

Nitinol, also known as Ni-Ti, is a nickel and titanium metal alloy known for its high elasticity and shape-memory properties, and it is commonly utilized in procedures to correct hallux valgus deformities (commonly known as bunions) [11,12]. The shape-memory properties are considered advantageous in bunionectomies, as the deformity is often driven by a hypermobile 1st tarsometatarsal joint (TMTJ) and medial forefoot instability [13]. Contributing factors to hallux valgus can include wearing narrow or tight shoes, excessive pronation, and systemic inflammatory conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis [14]. Studies have demonstrated the use of Nitinol in staple form, taking advantage of its continuous compression capability at the fusion site for these deformities. Ravenell and Doh (2024) demonstrated the use of Nitinol in staple form for correction of these deformities, with a high success rate, where 94 % of patients achieved radiologic consolidation at 12 weeks with immediate full weightbearing, without increasing the rate of nonunion or revision surgeries [11]. However, the biomaterial performance and procedure outcomes seem to vary based on the severity of the deformity, with most severe cases presenting in long-term inflammatory destruction of the joints. In more advanced or arthritic cases, where chronic inflammation leads to significant joint destruction, complications have been reported. This indicates that while Nitinol staples may be appropriate for routine or mild-to-moderate cases, alternative fixation methods and materials might be necessary for more complex hallux valgus deformities. Additionally, a biomechanical study on cadaveric feet evaluated two Nitinol staple constructs for 1st MPJ joint arthrodesis [12]. Schafer et al. (2022) demonstrated that orthogonal Nitinol staples failed early under cyclic loads, whereas combined Nitinol staple-screw constructs showed greater durability [12].

While the material itself is a promising option for surgical applications, combined Nitinol staple-screw constructs provided greater mechanical durability and better maintained joint stability during simulated weightbearing. In more recent podiatric applications, Nitinol has been indicated in the distal forefoot, such as intramedullary implants of the phalanges, which are intended to integrate into the shaft of the metatarsals to make an artificial joint in hammertoe arthroplasties, also characterized by contractures at the proximal interphalangeal joint (PIPJ), resulting in a dorsally prominent joint [13].

Magnesium

Bioabsorbable materials have recently gained attention in foot and ankle surgery due to several advantages over non-bioabsorbable options such as titanium. These include reduced stress shielding, enhanced biological integration, and the elimination of the need for hardware removal when compared to non-bioabsorbable materials such as titanium [15]. Among bioabsorbable options, particularly magnesium alloys (magnesium, ytterium, and zirconium alloy (MgYREZr)), have been highlighted as promising alternatives to conventional titanium-based hardware [16,17].

One retrospective analysis demonstrated that magnesium-based hardware presented comparable outcomes to titanium hardware in hallux valgus procedures [17]. These findings were consistent with a meta-analysis by Fu et al. (2023), which included two randomized controlled trials and three non-randomized studies comparing bioabsorbable magnesium screws to titanium screws in bunion surgeries at the distal metatarsals (Fig. 2E). Across all studies, there were no statistically significant differences in surgical outcomes between magnesium and Ti-based screws [16].

Magnesium-based implants have also been used successfully in other bunionectomy procedures, including Scarf osteotomies, Akin procedures, and modified closing base wedge osteotomies. A single study evaluated an intramedullary PIPJ implant stem (Figure G) made of magnesium, ytterium, and zirconium alloy (MgYREZr) MAGNEZIX® CS made from Syntellix AG [18]. Döhner et al. (2023) compared the MgYREZr implant to a titanium counterpart and found comparable performance under torsional, weight-bearing, and cyclic load conditions.

Biomaterials used in midfoot applications

As previously outlined, multiple biomaterials, including titanium alloy and stainless steel, are widely employed across various surgical applications in podiatry, including those involving the midfoot. Although these materials differ in mechanical and surface properties, both titanium alloy and stainless steel are grouped in this section due to their use in multiple pathologies involving the midfoot , such as midfoot Charcot and pes planus.

One key midfoot application is the correction of pes planus (flatfoot) deformities. This condition often necessitates complex fixation, involving not only the midfoot, but also the forefoot and hindfoot with the ultimate outcome of restoring the foot arch [19]. Among these procedures, the Cotton osteotomy involves the placement of a dorsal wedge in the medial cuneiform to plantarflex the first metatarsal (Fig. 2B). These wedges are frequently constructed from titanium alloys or cadaveric bone allografts, which are intended to remain permanently in place, allowing for the 1st metatarsal head to be closer to the ground [19].

In addition, proper reconstruction of flatfoot also requires attention to soft tissue structures such as spring ligament (plantar calcaneonavicular ligament), which is commonly injured in individuals with pes planus deformities. This ligament supports the talar head at the talonavicular joint and is critical for maintaining medial arch rigidity during the stance phase of the gait cycle [19,20]. To address this aspect of reconstruction, one study utilized Ti-based wedges for spring ligament repair and demonstrated excellent functional and radiographic results, with superior bone integration and stabilization in patients with pes planus deformities [21].

Arguably one of the most severe midfoot pathologies is Charcot neuroarthropathy. This debilitating condition, most commonly associated with advanced diabetes mellitus, is characterized by progressive joint dislocation, fractures, and collapse of the foot's arch due to neuropathy-induced loss of protective sensation and chronic inflammation [22]. While non-operative options, such as off-loading techniques and bisphosphonate therapy have been proposed [23], limb salvage and reconstructive surgery, using internal or external fixation, remains the definitive treatment [22,24]. Wukich et al. (2021) compared titanium and stainless steel hardware in Charcot reconstruction and concluded that more ridging and mechanically stable constructs were associated with better fusion rates and biomechanical outcomes [25]. The choice of incorporating implant stiffness directly influenced the success of the reconstruction, with stiffer constructs showing a lower risk of failure [25].

In addition to material choice, surface modifications of titanium implants have shown promise in Charcot foot procedures. While most titanium-based devices in Charcot neuroarthropathy are polished, a recent study by Mehlhorn et al. (2020) highlighted the advantages of modified titanium surfaces using high-profile threaded fusion bolts with grit-blasted surface over standard fusion bolts (Fig. 2H) for stabilizing the medial column in patients with unstable feet due to Charcot. Compared to standard titanium bolts, the modified implants demonstrated superior outcomes, including improved osseous consolidation at 3 months, fewer complications (e.g., only one bolt breakage and no dislocations), and greater improvement in Meary's angle, an indicator of medial longitudinal arch restoration. These findings suggest that surface and design enhancements in titanium implants can significantly improve medial column stability and long-term foot reconstruction outcomes [26].

Biomaterials in hindfoot applications

Titanium

In reconstructive hindfoot surgery, addressing bone gaps is critical to achieve successful outcomes. Traditionally, these gaps have been managed using structural bone, which often require supplemental stabilization with screws or nails to prevent collapse. However, complications such as nonunions may arise when compression is inadequate or when distraction occurs, even with locking intramedullary nails supported by compression devices. It is important to emphasize that in specific hindfoot pathologies, such as Charcot neuroarthropathy, pilon fractures, and revision total ankle replacements (Fig. 2A), severe bone resorption, destruction, or implant failure may result in limb shortening [[27], [28], [29], [30]]. In these cases, titanium-based cages serve as a structural block stabilization to maintain limb length during subtalar joint and ankle fusion. Another example is a case study which involved a 3D-printed bespoke titanium porous block in conjunction with an intramedullary tibiotalocalcaneal nail in the reconstruction of an unstable hindfoot Charcot deformity. This reconstruction strategy with titanium implants has the potential of correcting a rigid deformity with notable shortening of the limb while simultaneously restoring stability [31].

In addition to complex reconstructions, titanium is also employed in subtalar joint arthrodesis, a procedure involving fusion between the calcaneus and talus [32]. This procedure is generally indicated for patients suffering from subtalar joint osteoarthritis, post-traumatic arthritis, and various other arthropathies [33]. Niazi et al. (2020) utilized porous titanium wedges to fill bone deficits in subtalar joint distraction arthrodesis for four patients with post-traumatic arthritis [32]. The procedure demonstrated favorable radiographic and surgical outcomes [32]. Compared to traditional autografts and allografts, which carry risks of donor site morbidity and nonunions [34], titanium wedges showed equal success rates without such risks [32].

Magnesium

Magnesium-based bioabsorbable screws, particularly those composed of MgYREZr alloy, have emerged as promising alternatives to titanium in hindfoot procedures. A study by Acar et al. (2020) compared magnesium screws to titanium screws in biplanar chevron medial malleolar osteotomy for treating osteochondral lesions of the talus [35]. The findings indicated no significant differences in clinical or radiological outcomes between the two materials, suggesting that magnesium screws are a viable and safe option for this type of fixation in hindfoot surgery [35].

Biomaterials utilized in ankle applications

Titanium, nitinol and stainless steel

Several studies in ankle applications utilize Ti, Nitinol, and stainless steel, warranting their inclusion under the same subsection. Ti is the most frequently cited material, particularly in complex reconstructive surgeries involving 3D-printed cages and trusses for large critical-sized bone defects [36,37]. These applications are commonly reported in cases of chronic infection [38], bone defects due to avascular necrosis [37,39], and Charcot neuroarthropathy [40]. Indeed, 3D-printed titanium cages have been successfully employed to salvage procedures of destroyed ankle joints due to chronic talar and peritalar infections, offering promising long-term outcomes with optimal functionality follow ups, including a pain-free, functional gait without assistive devices [38]. Considering avascular necrosis cases, 3D printed titanium cages have been used in conjunction with tibiotalocalcaneal arthrodesis [39,41,42]. For example, Ramhamadany et al. (2021) evaluated the efficacy of a novel, keystone-shaped, 3D-printed titanium truss in treating severe AVN of the talus, a condition traditionally challenging to treat due to significant bone loss, reduced vascular supply, and risk of collapse. Three patients underwent tibiotalocalcaneal arthrodesis using a custom-made implant and a hindfoot intramedullary nail, which achieved radiological union by one year. Authors concluded that the keystone design of the truss contributed to preserving bone stock and conforming it to the native shape of the calcaneus, providing a strong structural support and resisting collapse and subsidence [39]. Similarly, Wang et al. (2021) used a custom 3D-printed titanium cage for tibiotalocalcaneal arthrodesis in Charcot foot deformity correction [40]. Their two-year follow-up showed successful joint fusion and restoration of a plantigrade foot, confirmed by radiographic union and patient satisfaction, demonstrating that combining 3D printing with Ti and arthrodesis can be a viable alternative for deformity correction in Charcot neuroarthropathy [40].

Titanium is frequently used in revision surgeries as well. Lewis et al. (2024) discussed the prospective use of custom patient-specific 3D-printed titanium truss arthrodesis implant in failed total ankle replacements [43,44]. In their prospective observational study, six patients underwent the revision procedure and modest level of improvements in the mean Manchester–Oxford Foot Questionnaire (MOXFQ) Index, European Quality of Life 5 Dimensions 5 Level Version (EQ-5D-5L) Index, and EuroQol Visual Analogue Scale (EQ-VAS) were observed [43]. However, authors concluded that larger-scale studies are needed to validate these outcomes.

Titanium has also been incorporated into total talus replacements and total ankle replacements (TAR), as depicted in Fig. 2A. Nielsen et al. (2024) investigated the use of the Scandinavian Total Ankle Replacements (STAR) systems with surface coatings to the tibial tray [45]. STAR represents a type of ankle replacement system composed of three components, typically made of a tibial tray, a talar component, and a polyethylene bearing. This three-part construction allows for an upper and lower articulation around the polyethylene bearing, ultimately reducing shear stresses around the joint. STAR total ankle replacements were analyzed to see which part of the implant lasted the longest and which ones lasted the shortest. The authors found that the titanium tibial trays with double calcium-phosphate coating showed the greatest longevity, followed by the talar component, with the polyethylene bearing demonstrating the shortest [45,46].

While less frequently utilized than titanium, stainless steel remains relevant in ankle surgery. Liang et al. (2023) compared conventional steel plates and custom-contoured steel plates in trimalleolar fractures. The two groups consisted of 24 patients each, and both exhibited comparable healing outcomes and follow-up times, though custom plates yielded improved fixation and joint function scores [46]. Gu et al. (2022) reported positive clinical outcomes in the effectiveness of a modified posterolateral approach using one incision, two windows, and three steel plates for treating complex 2-part posterior malleolus fractures along with medial and lateral malleolus fractures in twelve patients. Patients initiated weight-bearing at an average of 13 weeks, reported reduced pain, and achieved good-to-excellent functional outcomes [47].

Nitinol has emerged as a material of interest in ankle intramedullary fixation due to its compressive forces. Terrill et al. (2023) compared the effects of carbon fiber, titanium, and Nitinol elements in tibiotalocalcaneal intramedullary nails used for ankle fusion [48]. Their study demonstrated that while titanium and carbon fiber nails lost initial compression with minor bone loss and transferred only 17–22 % of gait forces to the bone, Ni-Ti nails preserved compression and transferred up to 67 % of the dynamic forces. This load-sharing capability suggests a biomechanical advantage in maintaining interfragmentary stability [48].

Bioabsorbable materials

The application of bioabsorbable materials in ankle procedures has gained attention due to their potential to eliminate the need for secondary implant removal and to reduce long-term complications associated with retained hardware. One clinical study specifically addressed the use of such materials: one employing magnesium-based screws [49]. May et al. (2020) conducted a retrospective study comparing bioabsorbable magnesium screws with conventional titanium screws for medial malleolar fracture fixation in 48 patients. Both groups achieved successful fracture fixation, however, the titanium group demonstrated a higher rate of implant removal, highlighting a key advantage of magnesium-based screws in reducing the need for secondary procedures [49]. Moreover, the bioabsorbable implants offered additional benefits such as reduced infection risk, improved biocompatibility, and the elimination of hardware removal surgeries. These findings support the use of magnesium screws as a favorable alternative to traditional metal implants, particularly in cases where future hardware removal would be undesirable or burdensome. Still, long-term studies are recommended to assess degradation profiles, inflammatory responses, and mechanical performance over time.

Miscellaneous

A small number of biomaterials in this review did not fit neatly into the previous subheadings but are nonetheless relevant in the context of ankle reconstruction. Johnson et al. (2022) conducted a systematic review to assess the outcomes of unconstrained total talus replacement for treating pathological changes in the talus and adjacent joints, examining clinical and radiographic outcomes along with safety metrics [50]. This review encompassed 22 studies involving 191 patients (196 ankles) from 2005 to 2021, focusing on various generations of implants primarily made from ceramic, cobalt chrome, or titanium. The most common complication was adjacent joint arthritis. Notably, the review found that material degradation occurred most commonly in ceramic components, followed by CoCr, with titanium demonstrating the least breakdown over time [50]. A complementary case series and literature review by West and Rush (2021) further highlighted the diversity of materials used in total talus replacements, which include aluminum ceramic, cobalt chrome, stainless steel, and titanium [51]. Their clinical study specifically reported favorable one-year outcomes for three patients treated with constrained cobalt chrome implants in combination with tibial prostheses, supporting the feasibility of this approach in selected patients [51].

Biomaterials in distal tibia and fibula applications

Although this review focuses primarily on the foot and ankle, the distal tibia and fibula are included here because these bones form the ankle joint with the talus and are routinely involved in podiatric reconstructive procedures. Accordingly, this section is limited to distal applications relevant to ankle stability, fixation, and functional restoration.

Titanium

Further, titanium has been applied in foot and ankle orthopaedics, as evidenced by the use of “Eiffel tower” double titanium elastic nails in treating congenital pseudoarthrosis of the tibia. In a case series of 17 patients treated with double titanium elastic nails, 15 achieved bony union following the primary intervention, while the remaining 2 required a second procedure to achieve successful fusion [44]. These findings highlight the versatility and efficacy of titnaium-based implants across different patient demographics and fracture complexities.

Stainless steel and carbon fiber

Stainless steel and carbon fiber have also been utilized in distal tibial and fibular fractures, particularly in complex or comminuted injuries. Wang et al. (2022) investigated the efficacy of utilizing one-third tubular stainless-steel screw and plate fixation to fix Rüedi-Allgöwer Class III pilon fractures [52]. Their findings suggested that steel plate fixation allowed for earlier postoperative weight-bearing, decreased loss of fracture reduction, and overall better clinical outcomes compared to screw-only fixation [52].

In a related study addressing pilon fractures with Tillaux fragments, bone block augmentation combined with steel plate fixation was found to provide effective stabilization [46,52]. Liang et al. (2023) compared the use of personalized custom-made steel plates with the conventional steel plates in the treatment of complex distal intra-articular trimalleolar fractures with the emphasis on improving ankle joint function [46]. A total of 48 patients (24 in each group), underwent the respective treatments. While both groups exhibited similar healing rates, personalized custom-made steel plate group demonstrated superior outcomes in fracture reduction, fewer complications, and significantly better ankle joint function scores [46].

Unspecified anatomical locations

Several studies in this review could not be categorized into specific anatomical locations, as they involved multiple lower extremity regions or presented generalized findings relevant to foot and ankle surgery. One retrospective study evaluated the use of 3D-printed titanium implants and cages across the forefoot, midfoot, and hindfoot, demonstrating their effectiveness in restoring structural bone volume, maintaining alignment, and achieving successful implantation in complex periarticular defects, independent of a single anatomical site [42]. These findings suggest that the primary advantage of such constructs lies in their ability to address large, segmental bone loss rather than region-specific biomechanical optimization. In addition, two clinical studies specifically addressed hardware-related infections in foot and ankle surgery from different but complementary perspectives [53,54]. Xia et al. (2020) evaluated patients with exposed stainless steel plates following foot and ankle fracture fixation, a setting frequently susceptible to nonviable bone and compromised soft tissue [54]. Their analysis demonstrated that microsurgical flap repair significantly improved clinical outcomes, aesthetic results, and functional recovery while reducing complication rates. Importantly, these findings underscore that successful management of hardware exposure and infection is driven primarily by restoration of soft-tissue viability, rather than implant material or fixation strategy alone [54]. In contrast, Stoffel et al. (2023) examined infection outcomes related to implant surface characteristics in a clinical setting by comparing hydroxyapatite-coated and uncoated stainless steel pins used for external fixation of tibial, fibular, and femoral pathologies [53]. Their results showed no statistically significant differences in pin tract infection rates between the two groups, indicating that enhanced bone–biomaterial interaction did not translate into a measurable clinical benefit in infection mitigation. This finding highlights the dominant influence of local soft-tissue conditions, mechanical stability, and postoperative care over implant surface properties in infection-prone fixation environments, as well as a current challenge and persistent need in biomaterial sciences.

Comparative features of common metallic biomaterials used in foot and ankle

To complement the clinical findings in this review, we highlight the importance of recognizing and critically analyzing the microstructural features of the most frequently used metallic biomaterials : commercially pure (cpTi) and Ti alloy (Ti-6Al-4V), Stainless Steel, and Nitinol. These materials are widely recognized for their favorable combinations of strength, corrosion resistance, biocompatibility, and clinical track record [[1], [2], [3],55,56]. Therefore, to visualize microstructural differences, Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) was conducted on these materials. Samples of Ti-6Al-4V, cpTi, and stainless steel were obtained from McMaster-Carr, sectioned into 5 mm discs, and manually polished using 240-grit sandpaper. Nitinol was purchased from VWR International and processed identically. SEM imaging was performed at 2 kV EHT using secondary electron (SE2) detection with a field width of 114.3 μm (∼1000 × magnification). It should be noted that samples were prepared to meet SEM size and handling; therefore, the imaged surfaces reflect the polished state rather than the intrinsic, untreated material topography.

As shown in Fig. 3A–D, SEM images display the material surfaces following preperation, while Fig. 3E summarizes the surface topography, advantages, disadvantages, and applications. Ridges and scratches were observed across all surfaces, resulting from the mechanical interaction between the polishing method and the material. The analysis focused on the surface characteristics that emerged after this preparation. In addition, Fig. 4 provides a comparative overview of the mechanical and biological behavior of cpTi, Ti-6Al-4V, and stainless steel as load-bearing metallic biomaterials.

Among titanium alloys, Ti-6Al-4V is widely used in arthrodesis, osteotomies, additive manufacturing, and salvage procedures due to its favorable strength, corrosion resistance, and relatively low elastic modulus, which minimizes stress shielding compared to other materials [39,40,42,55,57]. In our analysis, Ti-6Al-4V alloy (Fig. 3A) revealed a relatively smooth surface characterized by shallow ridges and scattered pore-like structures (circled in yellow). Furthermore, the lack of notable surface flaking indicates a strong resistance to delamination. This observation is consistent with the alloy's higher hardness and mechanical strength, as reported in the literature [1,55]. These properties likely minimized surface deformation, which is desirable in foot and ankle surgery that require load-bearing applications [1,55]. Furthermore, it is important to emphasize that the elastic modulus of Ti-6Al-4V (∼110 GPa) is lower than that of stainless steel (∼180 GPa), the mismatch with cortical bone (∼20 GPa) remains substantial and can still generate stress-shielding effects at the bone–biomaterial interface, potentially leading to bone resorption (Fig. 4A) [[58], [59], [60], [61]]. This concern is particularly relevant when hardware is contoured to patient-specific anatomy, as deformation may alter load transfer and local stress distribution. Additionally, while Ti-6Al-4V exhibits favorable fatigue properties overall, its performance can be compromised by cyclic loading, surface damage, and stress concentrations introduced during contouring, making fatigue failure a persistent clinical consideration [59,60]. Indeed, under cyclic loading, micro-cracks that develop along the surface facilitate the release of cytotoxic aluminum and vanadium ions into the surround tissue [62,63]. Therefore, improving the surface characteristics of Ti-6Al-4V hardware remains as a significant research opportunity to reduce fatigue failure by enhancing osseointegration and cellular response. In locking plate fixation, where plate–bone compression is intentionally avoided to preserve periosteal blood supply, improved screw–bone integration would be an alternative to increase axial load sharing and reduce plate fatigue.

In contrast to Ti-6Al-4V, cpTi (Fig. 3B) exhibited a noticeably rougher surface characterized by deeper grooves and scattered flake-like features (circled in yellow), consistent with surface delamination. Such features may be disadvantageous in orthopaedic and load-bearing applications, where surface integrity is critical for long-term mechanical performance [64,65]. These observations align with the intrinsic material properties of cpTi, including lower hardness, reduced mechanical strength, and greater ductility compared with Ti-6Al-4V [66,67]. Additionally, pore-like formations observed on the cpTi surface suggest wear-related fatigue introduced during the preparation process. Considering the mechanical properties, cpTi may offer several important advantages, particularly in applications where reduced stiffness mismatch with bone is desirable. Similar to Ti-6Al-4V, cpTi exhibits a relatively lower elastic modulus (∼100 GPa), which reduces stiffness mismatch with cortical bone and may mitigate stress-shielding effects associated with stiffer alloys [[68], [69], [70]]. Moreover, its superior ductility and toughness allow cpTi to undergo plastic deformation under modest loads rather than brittle fracture, potentially enhancing tolerance to minor intraoperative deformation (Fig. 4B) [68,70]. The absence of alloying elements such as aluminum, iron, or nickel further enhances biocompatibility and reduces the risk of adverse reactions or metal hypersensitivity [71,72]. However, from a clinical perspective in foot and ankle demands, although cpTi elastic modulus is closer to that of bone, it demonstrates lower overall strength, which limits its suitability for general load-bearing in these site-specific applications, particularly when compared with titanium alloys. Consequently, cpTi is more vulnerable to fatigue failure under cyclic loading, as surface defects and repetitive stresses may more readily progress to crack initiation and propagation, limiting long-term structural stability in demanding load-bearing environments [[73], [74], [75]].

Stainless steel, although used less frequently than titanium and its alloys, remains a mechanically robust material commonly employed in ORIF screw and plate fixation devices [[76], [77], [78], [79]]. In our analysis, stainless steel (Fig. 3C) exhibited shallow ridges and contained the highest density of pore-like formations (circled in yellow) across the surface. These pore-like formations are likely artifacts of the abrasive processing, where inclusions were dislodged from the matrix and are not representative of material porosity. However, this feature emphasizes the material's high ductility and susceptibility to surface scratching. For clinical applications, the benefit of stainless steel lies in high elastic modulus, ductility, ease of manufacturing, and low cost [80]. The high modulus allows for rigid fixation of components where movement must be minimized [81]. The high ductility allows the material in some cases to contour to patients without high risk of fracture [58]. However, these same features may be disadvantageous in load-bearing applications due to potential stress risers and reduced fatigue resistance (Fig. 4C) [78,79,82]. Stainless steel also has an extreme modulus mismatch with natural bone (∼200 GPa vs. ∼20 GPa, respectively), and can generate the most severe stress shielding effect of the materials compared in this analysis, leading to significant bone resorption [[68], [69], [70]]. Additionally, because of its unique microstructure, stainless steel has a high fatigue rate susceptibility, similar to cpTi in some cases [68,83]. Furthermore, because of this susceptibility, ions such as nickel can be leeched out of the hardware and can act as a hapten, ultimately leading to hypersensitivity reactions in susceptible patients.

Taken together, these considerations help explain why in contemporary foot and ankle reconstructive procedures, Ti-6Al-4V is often favored as a more compliant alternative to stainless steel, offering a balance between mechanical strength and reduced stiffness mismatch with bone (Fig. 4A–C).

Finally, clinical articles also introduced Nitinol as an innovative biomaterial to fusion procedures through its shape-memory and superelastic properties, enabling dynamic fixation [11,12,48,56,84,85]. In the SEM, Nitinol (Fig. 3D) shows a mixture of smooth surface areas and rough sublayers with small granular features (circled in yellow) distributed across the surface, likely resulting from surface oxidation following preparation. Compared to cpTi and stainless steel, Nitinol shows minimal flaking, suggesting enhanced resistance to scratching based on its superelastic properties and high mechanical durability at low forces. These unique chemical and mechanical properties displayed by Nitinol are advantageous in applications where flexibility, fatigue resistance, and long-term mechanical stability are critical, such as in 1st MPJ fusions [11,12,48,56,84,85]. Nitinol, depending on its manufacturing technique, also has a modulus of 40–75 GPa, and is much closer to cortical bone (∼20 GPa) compared to all above biomaterials [86]. Consequently, Nitinol presents a lower risk of stress shielding at the bone–biomaterial interface. Additionally, because of its superelastic properties and shape memory capability, the material is also able to flex and apply a continuous compressive force, promoting bone union and healing [87,88]. Despite these advantages, Nitinol is unsuitable for large load-bearing applicaions in foot and ankle due to its high flexibility. Finally, Nitinol is costly compared to stainless steel, cpTi, and Ti-6Al-4V due to complex processing requirements and the rigorous validation needed to assess biological risks.

Challenges and unmet clinical needs in metallic biomaterials

Corrosion, fretting, and ion release are main complications associated with orthopaedic metallic biomaterials and must be carefully considered by surgeons. Both Nitinol and stainless steel contain significant amounts of nickel, which may trigger hypersensitivity reactions following ion release [89]. This is an important concern in site-specific applications when biomaterial ions are progressively released due to friction and wear at the bone–hardware interface, potentially leading to sustained inflammatory or immunological responses that necessitate hardware removal [90]. Furthermore, corrosion and ions release may be particularly pronounced in medically compromised populations, including patients with diabetes mellitus and peripheral neuropathy [1,91]. In addition, changes in serum and interstitial fluid composition may promote increased ion leaching from metallic surfaces, including titanium-based devices.The altered physiological environment observed in diabetes, such as chronic low-grade acidosis, can further affect implant surfaces and overall hardware performance, demonstrating unmet biomaterial needs for certain patient populations [1,91].

Indeed, many of the current challenges encountered in clinical settings are related to patient comorbidities, which consistently compromise the biological environment, resulting in poor bone quality and a persistent pro-inflammatory milieu. Such unfavorable conditions at the bone–implant interface can impair osseous integration, leading to implant loosening or hardware backout due to insufficient bone adherence [[92], [93], [94]]. This complication is particularly problematic in neuropathic patients, in which prominent or loosened hardware may progress to soft tissue irritation, cellulitis, and ultimately osteomyelitis [95,96].

Another major challenge in foot and ankle surgery is peri-implant infection. Although infection is an inherent risk of all surgical procedures, immunologically susceptible populations, such as patients with diabetes, face a significantly elevated risk. Current strategies to mitigate biomaterial-related infections include strict adherence to sterile technique, systemic antibiotic prophylaxis, and the development of antibiotic-releasing and surface-modified biomaterials designed to reduce bacterial colonization and biofilm formation, but still represent an unmet clinical need [97].

Lastly, this review emphasizes important limitations of metallic materials (stainless steel, titanium, and Nitinol) inherent to their interference with medical imaging, particularly the generation of artifacts in CT and MRI scans [89]. Importantly, Stainless steel exhibits higher levels of artifact interference due to its higher composition of ferromagnetic metals, when compared to titanium [89,98]. The ferromagnetic metals can cause distortion of the image in advanced imaging. The artifact interference can allow for difficulties in analyzing patient anatomy, pathology, and healing process.

Given these challenges, the development and clinical translation of non-ferrous and non-metallic implant materials represent a compelling unmet clinical need, particularly in surgical applications that require reliable post-operative advanced imaging. Alternative implant strategies capable of preserving imaging fidelity could substantially enhance post-operative monitoring and clinical decision-making in osseous-based procedures [99,100]. Although not the primary focus of this review, emerging polymer-based and carbon fiber–reinforced implants have demonstrated promising early outcomes, offering advantages such as reduced radiographic interference and improved visualization on advanced imaging modalities [99,100]. However, despite these encouraging short-term results, robust long-term data evaluating mechanical durability, fatigue behavior, biological performance, and clinical efficacy remain limited. While the literature in innovations is abundant in preclinical sciences, it has been observed that direct comparative clinical studies between conventional metallic implants and non-metallic alternatives in foot and ankle applications are still scarce. Addressing these gaps represents a significant opportunity for future research. Well-designed, long-term, and head-to-head investigations comparing metallic and non-metallic biomaterials could redefine biomaterials selection strategies and accelerate the development of next-generation of foot and ankle biomaterials. Such efforts have the potential to improve post-operative assessment, personalize biomaterial choice (site- and patient-specific), and ultimately advance the clinical outcomes of foot and ankle reconstructions in high risk populations.

Final considerations and future research directions

This review demonstrates how Ti and its various alloys remains the most frequently utilized and versatile biomaterial across all anatomical regions of foot and ankle surgery. Its widespread application spans from 1st metatarsophalangeal joint arthrodesis to complex reconstructions in Charcot neuroarthropathy using 3D-printed truss cages [39,40], with superior mechanical strength, corrosion resistance, and an ideal elastic modulus. We have also identified innovations such as hydroxyapatite-coated surfaces [53] and porous wedge designs (see example in Fig. 2B and C) as promising candidates in optimization of Ti to enhance integration with bone and clinical outcomes. In addition to Ti, Nitinol was also a highlight in this review. Due its shape-memory properties it has shown value in arthrodesis at the metatarsophalangeal joints with continuous compression staples, and joint implants (Fig. 2G) [11]. Clinical outcomes with the use of Nitinol included angular correction of hallux valgus deformities, joint fusion and pain reduction in 1st MPJ arthritis, and hammertoe reduction with joint arthroplasties. Yet, biomechanical studies suggest utilization of screws with nitinol staple fixation in MPJ joint arthrodesis produces a more solid fusion site [12]. Finite element analysis further confirmed nitinol's superior compression maintenance under bone resorption, representing an innovation in load-sharing implants [48].

Just as important as the bulk material is the surface layer, particularly the surface modifications applied to hardware. Surface modifications appear to be underpresented in the literature of foot and ankle surgery. One of the studies reported the use of double calcium phosphate coatings being used on the tibial tray portion of total ankle replacement (Fig. 2A) for better placement in the bone [45]. Also, clinical approaches utilizing coatings of hydroxyapatite are shown to have favorable surgical outcomes [53], as well as acid etched and grit blasted titanium medial column fusion bolts (Fig. 2H) for Charcot reconstructive surgery, resulting in reduced hardware loosening [26]. Hardware coatings, such as hydroxyappatite coatings, have shown to increase osteoblastic activity and promote bone formation [101], but seems to lack any benefit to infection mitigation. Direct modification of the hardware surface by increasing roughness, such as grit-blasted and acid-etched identified in one clinical study for Charcot reconstructions, seems to fill this gap, though not well explored in foot and ankle [26]. Indeed, studies suggest that direct surface modification techniques, such as sandblasted, large-grit, acid-etched (SLA) surfaces found in other specialties such as dentistry, may reduce bacterial adhesion [102,103]. Although the direct mechanism behind the antimicrobial nature of rough surfaces remains to be fully investigated, it is hypothesized that bacterial biofilm growth can be stunted through specific surface hydrophilicities and surface charges [104]. The combination of biofilm mitigation and increased levels of osseointegration make SLA modification for titanium alloy hardware a promising candidate in diabetic Charcot neuroarthropathy foot and ankle reconstruction. Therefore, research and innovation to further optimize the surface roughness can prove to be extremely impactful in patient outcomes in high risk reconstructive surgeries. Seeking such solutions to hardware infections in the field of foot and ankle surgery is a current major need and has the potential to be a innovative direction for treating patients with immunological challenges.

It was also observed through this review that orthopaedic biomaterial research, particularly titanium, seems to be trending toward patient specific hardware. The advent of custom 3D printed implants allows for optimal fitment for patient specific anatomy during surgery. This improved fitment results in less intraoperative modifications of plates and maintenance of fatigue resistance. In detrimental foot and ankle pathology, such as Charcot neuroarthropathy or failed total ankle replacements (Fig. 2A), custom 3D-printed titanium cages or truss systems allow the limb to regain height while still maintaining stability. Porous printed implants (Fig. 2B and C), such as wedges and cages, allow for osteogenic ingrowth for increase integration and stability of the osteotomy site. Similarly, additive manufacturing modifications of the surface layer have shown to have beneficial surgical outcomes. Improving hardware surface characteristics can reduce pitting, fretting, and surface inclusions, thereby decreasing susceptibility to corrosion. Reduced corrosion limits ion release, which may ultimately lower the risk of metallosis and hardware failure in the postoperative setting.

In addition to metallic implants, bioabsorbable, functionalized, and biointegrative materials represent emerging trends toward more integrative and patient-specific biomaterial solutions in orthopaedic fields. In foot and ankle reconstruction, certain polymer-based materials, such as polyether ether ketone (PEEK) and polyetherketoneketone (PEKK), have demonstrated significant potential for future surgical applications, particularly in the management of large osseous defects of the foot and ankle. Future applications of polymeric implants can be utilized in a Charcot neuroarthropathy with patients requiring ankle fusions to salvage the limb for a plantigrade foot [105]. PEEK has also shown to be promising as a biomaterial utilized in future total ankle replacement surgeries [106].

Importantly, based on the specific clinical articles found in this review, there appears to be a lack of large multicenter registries for foot and ankle biomaterials and manufacturing modifications to existing hardware. Many independent studies are found regarding novel biomaterials as discussed in this article, such as bioabsorbable metallic implants and polymerics [18,35,105,106], but multicenter consensus and registration of these innovations does not seem to be as apparent. The independent studies of specific biomaterials also do not typically list manufacturing specifics, such as surface modifications like electroanodization, that are extremely common in modern orthopaedic surgical trays. Many manufacturing addatives, like color electroanodization, are often done to improve surgical efficiency, such as easy visualization for screw sizing or type [107]. Despite this, the downstream effects of these seemingly benign surface modifications to titanium alloy hardware may unknowingly effect the oxide layer, altering osseointegration and cellular response. These observations suggest the opportunity for improved collaboration between industry, surgeons, and the state of current reported literature.

In conclusion, biomaterial selection for foot and ankle surgery is guided by the anatomical site, patient pathology, material-specific properties, and surgical objectives, requiring a careful balance between biomechanical demands and biocompatibility (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). As highlighted conceptually, the intersection between intrinsic limitations of metallic biomaterials (e.g., elastic modulus mismatch, fatigue behavior, corrosion, and wear) and the unique mechanical and biological challenges of the foot and ankle (including high cyclic loading, stress concentration, variable bone quality, and high-risk patient comorbidities) underscores the need for patient-specific and site-specific implant solutions. While metallic biomaterials remain central to contemporary foot and ankle surgery, significant opportunities exist to address limitations related to fatigue resistance, corrosion, implant design standardization, and performance in high-risk populations through next-generation manufacturing approaches, advanced surface modification strategies, and rigorous long-term clinical evaluation. Although the literature provides insight into emerging biomaterials and implant designs (such as polymer-based systems and custom 3D printed constructs), there remains a notable gap in studies evaluating surface modification strategies specifically applied to foot and ankle hardware, emphasizing a critical area for future investigation. Finally, integrating advances in bulk biomaterial development with focused research on implant surface engineering may accelerate progress in foot and ankle surgery by enabling enhanced biomaterial functional integration at fracture and osteotomy sites, reduced fusion times, lower infection rates, and decreased ion release and material corrosion. Collectively, these efforts highlight surface modification and functionalization as a promising and current translational pathways to improve biomaterial performance and patient outcomes in complex, load-bearing foot and ankle reconstructions.
