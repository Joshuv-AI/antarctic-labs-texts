Variability of Visual Assessment in Determining Tibial Component Alignment in Total Ankle Arthroplasty
Authors: Cesar A. Ramirez, Seif El Masry, Gabrielle M. Meli, Patrick Baki, Krysta Caudle, Edward Haupt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Accurate implant positioning is essential for long-term success in total ankle arthroplasty (TAA). Although patient-specific instrumentation (PSI) and advanced imaging have improved technical precision, intraoperative alignment assessment still relies heavily on visual interpretation of fluoroscopy. The reliability of visual assessment remains poorly understood. This study evaluated the accuracy and reliability of visual assessment of tibial component alignment in TAA.Methods:Seventeen raters assessed 21 TAA cases (15 unique, 6 duplicated) using standardized anteroposterior (AP) and lateral fluoroscopic or radiographic images. True implant alignment was defined by postoperative computed tomography (CT) measurements from a previously published PSI cohort. Raters classified angular alignment as neutral (0° to 2°), 2° to 4°, or greater than or equal to 5° of deviation in varus/valgus or dorsiflexion/plantarflexion. Interobserver agreement was assessed using Fleiss κ; and intraobserver reliability was evaluated using Cohen κ and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs). Accuracy, mean absolute error, and perceptual thresholds were analyzed using mixed-effects models and segmented logistic regression.Results:Overall classification accuracy was modest (48.3% AP; 52.5% lateral), with low interobserver agreement (κ = 0.14-0.21). Mean absolute error ranged from 2.0° to 2.2°. Intraobserver repeatability demonstrated moderate agreement (ICC(2,1) 0.43-0.48). Observer performance was most variable near neutral alignment and improved with larger deviations. Years of experience did not improve accuracy and demonstrated a small inverse association with correct classification.Conclusion:Visual assessment of tibial component alignment in TAA demonstrated substantial variability, particularly when evaluating small angular deviations. Imaging characteristics, including view orientation and modality, were associated with measurement error, whereas surgeon experience had minimal impact on overall accuracy. These findings highlight the limitations of visual assessment under standard fluoroscopic and radiographic imaging conditions and support continued investigation of other strategies, such as computer-assisted navigation, 3-dimensional imaging, and robotic-assisted technologies, to improve intraoperative alignment assessment.Level of Evidence:Level III, diagnostic reliability study.

Introduction

In arthroplasty, accurate implant positioning is critical for long-term success. In total ankle arthroplasty (TAA), precise component placement is particularly important because implant alignment directly influences joint kinematics, ligament balance, and load distribution across the implant-bone interface.1,2 The ankle functions through a constrained articulation with a complex, obliquely oriented rotational axis, and malalignment may alter native motion patterns and loading mechanics. 2 Consequently, component malposition can lead to abnormal contact pressures, ligament imbalance, accelerated polyethylene wear, implant loosening, and failure.1-3 Conversely, accurate alignment, particularly in the sagittal plane, helps preserve normal ankle kinematics and facilitates smooth transitions between dorsiflexion and plantarflexion during gait.4,5

Despite advances in implant design and instrumentation, achieving optimal alignment remains technically demanding. Although patient-specific instrumentation (PSI) has been proposed to improve the accuracy of component placement relative to a preoperative plan, several comparative studies have demonstrated similar postoperative alignment accuracy between PSI and conventional instrumentation.6-9

More recently, computer-assisted navigation, robotic-assisted systems, and weightbearing computed tomography (WBCT) have emerged as potential tools for improving alignment assessment and implant positioning in TAA.10-14 These technologies provide objective 3-dimensional information that may reduce reliance on subjective visual estimation and conventional 2-dimensional imaging.14,15 However, despite these advances, visual interpretation of fluoroscopic images remains a central component of intraoperative decision making during contemporary TAA.16,17

In published series, surgeons experienced in TAA generally achieve component positioning within 5° of the intended alignment; however, achieving and verifying small alignment deviations remains challenging despite advances in instrumentation and surgeon experience.9,18,19 With either approach, surgeons continue to rely heavily on visual assessment and fluoroscopic imaging, both of which are inherently subjective and susceptible to perceptual error. Understanding the limitations of visual alignment assessment and the degree to which surgeons can reliably detect deviations from neutral positioning is therefore essential, particularly during intraoperative decision making and as emerging technologies are introduced into TAA.

To date, no study has systematically evaluated how observer performance varies across different magnitudes of varus/valgus or dorsiflexion/plantarflexion malalignment, nor has inter- and intraobserver reliability been quantified under typical operative viewing conditions. The purpose of this study is to evaluate inter- and intraobserver reliability in the visual assessment of implant alignment in TAA using both fluoroscopic and radiographic images. We further sought to characterize observer performance across varying magnitudes of angular deviation and to identify factors such as surgeon experience, imaging modality, and view orientation that influence accuracy.

Methods

Study Design

This survey study evaluated the accuracy of visual assessment in determining tibial component alignment following TAA using fluoroscopic and radiographic images, compared with the implant position as determined by computed tomography (CT). Following Institutional Review Board approval, a survey was administered to foot and ankle fellowship–trained orthopaedic surgeons who routinely perform TAA and were familiar with the implant system used (Vantage Total Ankle Arthroplasty; Advita Inc), as well as 2 graduate medical students and 1 advanced practice provider.

Cases included in this survey study were derived from a previously published cohort evaluating the accuracy of PSI in TAA. In that study, preoperative CT scans were used to generate a PSI-based surgical plan using a commercially available planning software and patient-specific guides (3D Systems). Postoperative CT scans were obtained at the onset of weight bearing and used to quantify achieved implant position relative to the preoperative plan. Postoperative CT scans and matching standing AP and lateral radiographs were obtained at the onset of weight bearing as part of the original PSI accuracy study. Corresponding intraoperative fluoroscopic images and postoperative radiographs were retrospectively collected and incorporated into a survey instrument. CT-derived alignment measurements served as the reference standard (“true” alignment) for all analyses and were derived from postoperative CT-based assessment of implant position relative to the tibial axis used in the PSI planning workflow.

A total of 17 raters participated in the study, with a mean of 18.5 ± 16.3 years of clinical experience (range, 0-50 years). The cohort consisted primarily of fellowship trained foot and ankle surgeons (14/17, 82.4%), 2 medical students, and 1 advanced practice practitioner. For each case, raters were presented with either intraoperative fluoroscopic images obtained using a standard image-intensifier C-arm system (anteroposterior [AP] and lateral views) or first postoperative standing radiographs (AP and lateral views) as separate survey questions. Only 1 imaging modality (AP and lateral pair) was displayed per question, resulting in 4 independent evaluations per case (AP/lateral fluoroscopy and AP/lateral radiographs).

Figures 1 and 2 demonstrate the standardized imaging format presented to raters and illustrate representative examples of near-neutral alignment and greater angular deviation. These examples were selected for illustrative purposes only and were not chosen based on observer performance. For each image set, raters were asked to visually estimate tibial component alignment using a predefined categorical rubric. Alignment was classified as neutral (0°-2° deviation from neutral), mildly malaligned (2°-4° deviation), or markedly malaligned (≥5° deviation) in the coronal plane (varus/valgus) or sagittal plane (dorsiflexion/plantarflexion), depending on the imaging view presented. Respondents were not permitted to use measurement tools or draw reference lines, in order to replicate typical intraoperative visual assessment conditions. Observer responses were considered correct when the selected alignment category (neutral [0°-2°], mildly malaligned [2°-4°], or markedly malaligned [≥5°]) matched the category derived from the CT reference-standard measurement. The alignment categories were selected a priori to standardize observer responses across a range of angular deviations and were not intended to represent validated clinical thresholds for acceptable or unacceptable component positioning.

In all cases included in the present analysis, the tibial component was templated to neutral alignment relative to the anatomic tibial axis in both the coronal and sagittal planes (0° varus/valgus and 0° tibial slope). Cases from the original cohort with significant extra-articular malunion or proximal tibial deformity were excluded, as these deformities could bias visual assessment on fluoroscopy or radiographs. No measurable changes in implant position were observed between intraoperative fluoroscopic imaging and the postoperative CT scan for any case.

Raters evaluated 21 total cases, including 15 unique cases and 6 duplicated cases to assess intraobserver reliability. All 15 unique cases that had known CT-derived tibial component alignment were selected for inclusion in the survey. Survey images were screened for appropriate positioning, field of view, and image clarity, and were standardized using a commercial image editing platform (Microsoft Designer; Microsoft Corp) to ensure consistent visualization across all cases. Survey images were randomized prior to distribution, and all identifying information was removed. Participants were blinded to patient identity, case duplication, and image pairing and were not informed that repeat cases were included in the survey.

Only tibial components were assessed. Tibial alignment was evaluated in the coronal and sagittal planes using the flat horizontal surface of the tibial tray as the visual reference, consistent with the design of most modern TAA systems. Talar components were excluded for 2 reasons: (1) coronal plane talar alignment is difficult to reliably assess on fluoroscopy or radiographs alone, and (2) sagittal talar alignment (talar slope) is assessed on fluoroscopy and radiograph imaging because of the absence of a clearly defined flat surface. Excluding talar components minimized variability unrelated to human visual perception. The primary objective of this study was to assess the accuracy of visual estimation of implant alignment using standard intraoperative and postoperative imaging modalities, rather than to evaluate the accuracy of implant placement itself.

Outcomes and Statistical Analysis

The primary outcome was observer reliability in assessing TAA alignment, quantified using Fleiss κ for interobserver agreement and Cohen κ and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) for intraobserver repeatability. Agreement statistics were interpreted using commonly accepted thresholds for κ and ICCs.20,21 Secondary outcomes included the relationship between angular deviation magnitude and classification accuracy. Segmented logistic regression models were used to explore classification performance at 1°, 3°, and 5° of deviations from neutral alignment.

The influence of years of experience (YOE), imaging view (AP vs lateral), and imaging modality (fluoroscopy vs radiograph) on observer performance was analyzed using mixed effects analysis of variance (ANOVA) and logistic regression models. To account for the repeated measures study design and non-independence of observations, both models included random intercepts for rater and case. All data were analyzed using a commercial statistical software package (BlueSky Statistics, v10.3.4; BlueSky Statistics LLC). All statistical tests were 2 tailed, with an alpha level of .05 set for statistical significance.

Results

Overall observer accuracy in assigning tibial component alignment to the correct CT-derived alignment category (neutral [0°-2°], mildly malaligned [2°-4°], or markedly malaligned [≥5°]) was modest across imaging conditions (Table 1). Using AP views, raters correctly identified the CT-derived alignment category in 48.3% of assessments with an interobserver agreement (Fleiss κ) of 0.21 and a mean absolute error of 2.2° ± 1.7° relative to the CT reference standard. Lateral views demonstrated slightly higher accuracy, with 52.5% correct classifications, although interobserver agreement remained low (κ = 0.14). Mean absolute error for lateral views was 2.0° ± 1.8°. When stratified by imaging modality, fluoroscopic and radiographic images demonstrated similar overall accuracy. For AP views, fluoroscopy and radiographs yielded correct classification rates of 47.9% and 48.7%, respectively. For lateral views, accuracy was 51.0% with fluoroscopy and 54.1% with radiographs. Corresponding 95% CIs are provided in Table 1. Interobserver agreement remained low to moderate across modalities, and mean absolute error was comparable between fluoroscopic and radiographic imaging for both AP and lateral views.

The multivariable models in Table 3, mixed effects ANOVA, demonstrated that absolute alignment error differed significantly by imaging view (P = .02) and imaging modality (P = .03), with a significant interaction between view and modality (P < .01). Years of experience and case identity were not significantly associated with absolute error. In contrast, multivariable logistic regression evaluating correct alignment classification demonstrated a statistically significant inverse association between YEO and correct classification (odds ratio, 0.99; 95% CI, 0.99-1.00; P = .02). Imaging view, imaging modality, true numerical alignment angle, and the interaction between view and modality were not independently associated with correct classification. Overall, imaging characteristics had a greater influence on observer performance than years of experience. Differences in view orientation and imaging modality were associated with changes in measurement error, whereas observer experience had minimal impact on overall accuracy.

Classification performance varied across deviation thresholds (Table 4). At a 1° deviation, increasing true angular deviation was associated with a higher likelihood of correct classification (OR 3.60, P < .001). At a 3° deviation, observers were significantly less likely to correctly classify alignment (OR 0.09, P < .001). In contrast, at a 5° deviation, larger angular deviations were again associated with substantially higher odds of correct classification (OR 5.70, P < .001). Across all deviation thresholds, YEO showed a small but consistent association with classification performance, with each additional year of experience associated with a slight decrease in the odds of correct classification (OR approximately 0.99 per year; P = .01 for all models). Overall model performance was modest, with McFadden pseudo-R² values ranging from 0.07 to 0.11, and the model demonstrated best fit at the 3° threshold based on Akaike information criterion values. Overall, these findings suggest substantial variability in the visual detection of small alignment deviations, with more consistent recognition of larger degrees of malalignment.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that visual assessment of tibial component alignment in TAA has important limitations, particularly when evaluating small alignment deviations.

Inter- and intraobserver agreement were limited, categorical agreement remained only fair to moderate, and absolute errors of 1° to 3° relative to the CT reference standard were common, even under standardized imaging conditions. Observers demonstrated the greatest variability when assessing deviations near neutral alignment, suggesting increased difficulty distinguishing small angular difference. Performance was influenced primarily by imaging characteristics, specifically view orientation and modality, rather than years of experience. These findings are consistent with prior work across other orthopaedic subspecialties demonstrating that small angular deviations are difficult to appreciate visually, even for experienced surgeons, and that intraoperative fluoroscopy may be particularly limited when assessing subtle malalignment.22-25

The observed mean absolute error of approximately 2° should be interpreted with caution. Because raters evaluated fluoroscopic and radiographic images that primarily visualized the distal tibia, whereas the reference standard was derived from CT-based alignment measurements, a portion of the observed discrepancy may reflect differences between reference axes rather than visual perception alone. Furthermore, the magnitude of the observed measurement error overlaps with the range in which classification performance appeared most variable, limiting the ability to define a precise perceptual threshold for alignment detection. Consequently, the present findings should be interpreted as demonstrating limitations in visual assessment under these imaging conditions rather than establishing a specific angular cutoff at which perception deteriorates.

Although lateral radiographs were commonly classified correctly in our study and have demonstrated high intra- and interobserver reliability for angular measurements, they are less reliable for linear measurements and may be affected by technical factors such as patient positioning and projection.26,27 Lateral fluoroscopic images were more frequently classified correctly, and prior studies comparing intraoperative fluoroscopy with standing radiographs for hindfoot alignment have demonstrated strong correlation between the 2 modalities. 28 The improved performance observed in the sagittal plan may partially reflect differences in the visualized tibial reference axis. The CT reference standard incorporated the full tibial axis, whereas fluoroscopic and radiographic images visualized only the distal portion of the tibia. Because standard ankle imaging provides a limited field of view, coronal plane assessment may be more susceptible to the effects of tibial bowing and variation in the visible anatomic axis than sagittal plan assessment.

However, fluoroscopic images are taken under nonweightbearing conditions, and prior studies have demonstrated that angular differences between intraoperative and postoperative imaging in PSI-assisted TAA are typically within 2° to 3°.19,28-30 In the present study, mixed-effects ANOVA identified image view and modality as the primary determinants of measurement error, with lateral radiographs demonstrating lower error than AP projections or fluoroscopy. These findings may reflect both differences in image quality and limitations of the visualized tibial reference axis available on standard ankle imaging.23,27,28,31,32

In TAA, studies using PSI demonstrate that postoperative AP weightbearing alignments fall within ±3° of predicted CT scan–derived surgical plans in the coronal plane.19,30 However, sagittal plane accuracy appears even more consistent, with studies reporting 100% of implants within 2° of anticipated placement in one series, and 88.7% within 2° of predicted alignment in another.19,29 Taken together, these findings suggest that the degree of alignment precision achieved in contemporary TAA may be difficult to consistently evaluate using visual assessment alone.

An apparent discrepancy was observed between the mixed effects ANOVA and logistic regression analyses. Imaging view and modality were significantly associated with absolute alignment error but were not independently associated with correct alignment classification. This likely reflects fundamental differences between the 2 outcome measures. Absolute error captures continuous variation in alignment estimation, whereas correct classification depends on assignment to broad categorical alignment bins. Consequently, small improvements in measurement precision may reduce absolute error without necessarily altering categorical classification. Additionally, the modest model performance and limited sample size may have reduced power to detect differences in classification outcomes.

Notably, surgeon experience was not associated with reduced alignment error and demonstrated only a small inverse association with correct classification in logistic regression. However, the magnitude of this effect was minimal (OR 0.99 per year of experience) and should be interpreted cautiously. Given the limited number of raters and repeated measures study design, this finding is unlikely to represent a clinically meaningful effect and should not be interpreted as evidence that greater experience reduces alignment assessment accuracy. Several explanations may account for this finding. However, because cognitive mechanisms were not directly measured, any explanation remains speculative. It is possible that experienced observers rely on different visual assessment strategies than less experienced observers, although the observed association may also reflect statistical variability given the small effect size and limited number of raters. These findings suggest that years of experience alone may not predict visual alignment assessment performance, although the mechanisms underlying the observation remain uncertain. Although studies in other subspecialties have found that level of training does not improve visual accuracy, other studies have shown trends toward improved implant placement accuracy and reduced complications based on YOE.33,34 In hip arthroplasty Woerner et al 35 found that intraoperative cup inclination, anteversion, and stem torsion visual estimations differed significantly from 3D-CT but was not influenced by patient characteristics or experience. Although accurate implant placement may be influenced by patient characteristics, there is no direct evidence that surgeon age or experience affects visual alignment accuracy.

Segmented logistic regression demonstrated variability in classification performance across deviation magnitudes. Although classification performance appeared lower at the 3° threshold than at the 1° and 5° thresholds, this pattern was not consistent with a discrete perceptual threshold and may reflect case distribution, categorical alignment groupings, limited sample size, differences in case difficulty, or model instability. Accordingly, these findings are better interpreted as demonstrating variability in the visual detection of small alignment deviations rather than establishing a specific angular threshold at which perception deteriorates. Additionally, because the observed measurement error overlaps with the range in which classification performance appeared most variable, the present data do not permit identification of a precise perceptual cutoff. Although malalignment of 3° or greater is often considered clinically relevant and has been associated with inferior outcomes in arthroplasty literature,36-39 the present study was not designed to identify a precise perceptual threshold. Rather, the results suggest that visual assessment of subtle alignment deviations remains challenging under standard fluoroscopic and radiographic imaging conditions. These findings are consistent with prior work demonstrating poor agreement between visual assessment and objective measurements in orthopaedic imaging.40,41

As computer-assisted navigation, robotic systems, intraoperative 3-dimensional imaging, and advanced fluoroscopic technologies continue to evolve, objective assessment methods may help reduce observer-dependent variability in implant alignment evaluation. However, the present study did not directly compare these technologies with visual assessment and therefore cannot determine whether they improve alignment accuracy or clinical outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although images were standardized and randomized, the survey based design does not fully replicate real-time intraoperative decision making, where tactile feedback, dynamic fluoroscopy, and visual context may influence perception. Second, the number of cases and raters, although adequate for reliability analysis, may limit generalizability to broader surgeon populations and all implant systems. No a priori power calculation was performed. Consequently, the precision of the reported reliability estimates may be limited, and some analyses may have been underpowered to detect small but potentially meaningful effects. In addition, the relatively small number of independent cases and raters, combined with the repeated measures study design, may have contributed to model instability, reduced precision of effect estimates, and potential overfitting in multivariable analyses. Accordingly, findings from exploratory modeling analyses should be interpreted cautiously.

Third, the reference standard for alignment was derived from postoperative CT-based measurements of achieved implant position obtained as part of a prior PSI accuracy study. Although postoperative CT provides highly accurate 3-dimensional assessment of component position, the reference standard was derived from the tibial axis used in the PSI planning workflow, which incorporates the full tibial anatomy available on CT imaging. In contrast, observers assessed alignment using fluoroscopic and radiographic images that visualized only the distal portion of the tibia. Consequently, differences between the full tibial anatomic axis and the distal tibial axis visible on standard ankle imaging may have contributed to observed measurement discrepancies independent of visual perception. Additionally, observers evaluated static 2-dimensional images rather than dynamic fluoroscopic sequences available during surgery. As a result, the study environment may not fully reflect real-world intraoperative decision making and may underestimate performance achievable when surgeons can adjust imaging, obtain additional views, and integrate operative context.

Although the majority of raters were fellowship-trained foot and ankle surgeons, 2 medical students and 1 advanced practice provider were included. The inclusion of these non-surgeon raters may have influenced overall accuracy and reliability estimates and may limit the generalizability of the findings to surgeon-only populations.

An additional limitation relates to the use of image-intensifier fluoroscopy. Image-intensifier systems are susceptible to geometric distortion, including pin-cushion and sigmoidal distortion, which may vary across the image field. 42 Such distortion could potentially influence visual assessment of implant alignment and may not affect all regions of the image uniformly. 43 Although the magnitude of this effect cannot be quantified retrospectively, it may have contributed to observer variability.

Conclusion

Although an exact angular threshold for visual detection of malalignment in TAA has not been defined, our findings indicate that visual assessment of tibial component alignment demonstrates substantial variability, particularly when evaluating small angular deviations. Imaging characteristics, including view orientation and modality, were associated with measurement error, whereas surgeon experience had minimal impact on overall accuracy. These findings highlight important limitations of visual assessment under standard fluoroscopic and radiographic imaging conditions and support continued investigation of other strategies such as computer-assisted navigation, intraoperative 3-dimensional imaging, and robotic-assisted technologies for alignment assessment in TAA.
