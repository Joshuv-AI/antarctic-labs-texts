Poly-ubiquitylated transmembrane proteins outcompete other cargo for limited space inside clathrin-coated vesicles
Authors: Hao-Yang Liu, Susovan Sarkar, Feng Yuan, Grant Ashby, Carl C Hayden, Jon M Huibregtse, Jeanne C Stachowiak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYEndocytic recycling of transmembrane proteins is essential to cellular function. The intracellular domains of transmembrane proteins are frequently ubiquitylated, a modification that is recognized by adaptor proteins during clathrin-mediated endocytosis. Recent work suggests that transmembrane proteins compete for space within highly crowded endocytic structures, suggesting that enhanced internalization of one group of transmembrane proteins may come at the expense of others. Here, we show that preferential internalization of poly-ubiquitylated transmembrane proteins can result in reduced endocytosis of mono-ubiquitylated and non-ubiquitylated proteins. Further, poly-ubiquitylated receptors significantly outcompeted their less ubiquitylated counterparts for uptake of extracellular ligands. These findings suggest that clathrin-coated vesicles may act as selective filters, prioritizing highly ubiquitylated transmembrane proteins for uptake while leaving others behind. Given that poly-ubiquitylation is thought to signal protein aging and damage, these findings suggest a mechanism for selective internalization of high priority cargo, with simultaneously exclusion and protection of functional proteins lacking poly-ubiquitylation.

INTRODUCTION

Recycling of transmembrane proteins by clathrin-mediated endocytosis (CME) is crucial for maintaining homeostasis of the plasma membrane and sensitivity to the cellular environment through repeated cycles of ligand binding and signal transduction.1-4 During endocytic uptake, clathrin-coated pits nucleate at the plasma membrane where they recruit endocytic adaptor proteins. These adaptors facilitate the incorporation of transmembrane cargo into clathrin-coated vesicles.5-7 As the vesicles mature, additional transmembrane proteins and adaptor proteins are incorporated. Once the vesicle is fully formed, scission proteins such as dynamin facilitate the separation of the vesicle from the plasma membrane, allowing it to enter the cytoplasm for further processing.8

Among the diverse mechanisms regulating CME, ubiquitylation has emerged as an important post-translational modification that influences receptor internalization in both yeast and mammalian systems.9-13 This post-translational modification, which involves the attachment of ubiquitin to lysine residues within the intracellular domains of transmembrane proteins, enhances their affinity for key components of clathrin-coated vesicles.14-17 Specifically, Eps15, a protein that arrives early in the assembly of clathrin-coated vesicles, recruits ubiquitylated cargo using its tandem ubiquitin-interacting motifs (UIMs).18 These interactions stabilize the initiation of clathrin-coated vesicles, resulting in an increase in the fraction of endocytic assemblies that successfully internalize cargo.19 Similarly, Epsin, a key component of mature clathrin-coated vesicles, contains UIMs, which enable it to function as an adaptor for ubiquitylated cargo proteins.20,21

In yeast, Ste2p, a G protein-coupled receptor, becomes ubiquitylated upon binding to α-factor, a modification that is required for its recruitment to clathrin-coated pits and subsequent internalization via CME.22 However, whether mono-ubiquitylation or poly-ubiquitylation is more critical for receptor uptake in yeast cells remains controversial. Some studies indicate that mono-ubiquitylation efficiently directs cargo to cortical actin patches on the plasma membrane, the sites of clathrin-mediated uptake in Saccharomyces cerevisiae,23,24 while poly-ubiquitin chains signal proteasomal degradation.25 However, the Fur4 transmembrane protein, which is predominately poly-ubiquitylated, is also internalized by CME in yeast cells, suggesting that there may be exceptions to this dichotomy.26

In mammalian cells, the impact of ubiquitylation on uptake of transmembrane proteins through the clathrin pathway is less clear and has largely centered on internalization of receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs). Initial work from the Sorkin lab demonstrated that, while ubiquitylation of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), an RTK, is not strictly necessary for its recycling, restoration of two critical ubiquitylation sites significantly increased uptake through the clathrin pathway compared to a mutant in which all known ubiquitylation sites within the intracellular domain were removed.27 In subsequent work from the same lab, all lysine residues in the intracellular domain of EGFR were mutated to arginine, resulting in a substantial reduction in internalization through the clathrin pathway. From these results, the investigators concluded that EGFR interacts with clathrin-coated vesicles using a combination of ubiquitylation and interactions with other adaptor proteins,28 including AP2.29 Additionally, Sigismund et al. showed that the mechanism of EGFR internalization depends on the concentration of its ligand, EGF. At low EGF concentration, EGFR is internalized mainly by the clathrin pathway, and ubiquitylation is thought to be minimal. However, at high EGF concentration, EGFR is significantly ubiquitylated and appears to be internalized approximately equally through clathrin-mediated and clathrin-independent mechanisms. Interestingly, under this condition, internalization of EGFR remains dependent on clathrin adaptors that bind to ubiquitin, Epsin and Eps15.30

Additionally, a debate persists regarding whether mono-ubiquitylation or poly-ubiquitylation serves as the principal signal mediating ubiquitylation-dependent CME of mammalian receptors. Notably, mono-ubiquitylation alone has been shown to promote internalization, as exemplified by the Cbl-mediated mono-ubiquitylation of Met, an RTK that undergoes CME upon activation.31 However, other work has suggested that a single ubiquitin moiety is insufficient to function as an autonomous internalization signal in mammalian cells. Specifically, Barrière et al. demonstrated that only multiple ubiquitin units—assembled either as polyubiquitin chains or as oligomers—are efficiently recognized by clathrin adaptor proteins such as Epsin and Eps15, enabling productive cargo internalization via CME.16 Similarly, Mao et al. showed that poly-ubiquitylation of IGF-1 receptor, an RTK, is required for efficient receptor internalization.32

Importantly, these studies mainly examine endocytosis of individual transmembrane proteins in isolation from the other proteins at the plasma membrane surface. However, it is increasingly clear that the plasma membrane is densely crowded with transmembrane proteins,33,34 suggesting that concentrating target receptors at sites of endocytosis may require the displacement of other putative cargo. In particular, recent work from our lab and others suggest that endocytic structures themselves are highly crowded, such that transmembrane proteins35-39 and their associated adaptor proteins40 must compete with one another for limited space inside endocytic carriers. In particular, bulky glycosylated transmembrane proteins, which take up more space inside of endocytic structures, and proteins with lower affinity for the endocytic machinery were outcompeted by their smaller, higher-affinity counterparts during endocytic internalization.39,41 In this competitive landscape, how might the enhanced uptake of ubiquitylated transmembrane proteins impact internalization of proteins with little or no ubiquitylation?

To examine this question, we performed a series of experiments using engineered transmembrane proteins with varying degrees of stable ubiquitylation. We examined the localization of these model transmembrane proteins to endocytic sites and measured their ability to internalize ligands. Our findings demonstrate that poly-ubiquitylated proteins not only localize more robustly to endocytic structures but also exhibit higher internalization efficiency compared to their mono-ubiquitylated and non-ubiquitylated counterparts. This observation suggests that poly-ubiquitylated transmembrane proteins may be prioritized during internalization. To investigate this possibility, we developed assays in which transmembrane proteins with varying degrees of stable ubiquitylation competed for localization to endocytic sites and for ligand uptake. Our results demonstrate that poly-ubiquitylated transmembrane proteins consistently outcompeted their mono-ubiquitylated and non-ubiquitylated counterparts, indicating a selective advantage during endocytic uptake. Together, our findings suggest that CME is highly sensitive to the extent of ubiquitylation of transmembrane proteins, resulting in selective uptake of poly-ubiquitylated cargo. Given that damaged proteins and proteins that have completed their function in cell signaling are frequent targets of poly-ubiquitylation,42,43 these findings could help to explain how endocytic structures select specific populations of transmembrane proteins for rapid removal from the cell surface, leaving functional transmembrane proteins behind.

RESULTS

Poly-ubiquitylation increases endocytic uptake of model transmembrane proteins relative to mono-ubiquitylation

To evaluate the role of ubiquitylation in the partitioning of transmembrane proteins to endocytic sites, we designed chimeric membrane proteins with a defined number of enzymatically inert ubiquitins (0, 1, or 4) fused to the intracellular and transmembrane domains of the transferrin receptor (TfR), followed by an extracellular red fluorescent protein (RFP) domain for visualization (Figure 1A). TfR was selected as the model protein due to its well-characterized, strong constitutive internalization via CME.44,45 These model transmembrane proteins will henceforth be referred to as 0-Ub, 1-Ub, and 4-Ub. The native intracellular domain of TfR contains a YTRF motif, which mediates a measurable, basal level of constitutive internalization at endocytic sites by interacting with the μ2 subunit of the clathrin adaptor protein AP2.46,47 Our experiments examine the potential for increased internalization, above this basal level, upon addition of ubiquitin. Notably, by using a model receptor that undergoes constitutive endocytosis, we avoid the complexity associated with studying RTKs, where internalization may be ligand dependent or change upon ligand binding, as discussed above. However, during internalization, TfR may undergo endogenous mono-ubiquitylation by cellular ligases.48 To prevent this complication, we mutated all four lysine residues in the TfR intracellular domain and all seven lysines within the ubiquitins to arginine. Additionally, to maintain the stability of the added ubiquitins, the C-terminal GG residues of all ubiquitins were mutated to AA to prevent deubiquitylation. This design generates a linear, non-extendable ubiquitin fusion that cannot undergo further chain branching or disassembly. While our construct most closely mimics linear (M1-linked) ubiquitin chains, the intent was to mimic K63-linked chains, which are structurally similar to M1-linked chains.49 K63-linked chains are known to be common at the plasma membrane and to play important functions at endocytic sites.50-52 Thus, the 4-Ub construct serves as a model to mimic the multivalent topology of K63-linked poly-ubiquitin and to investigate its role in CME independently of dynamic remodeling. For simplicity, the chimeric construct omits the extracellular domain responsible for TfR dimerization, thereby yielding a monomeric form. Because uptake of transferrin receptor by the clathrin pathway is constitutively mediated by the intracellular domain, and unrelated to ligand binding or dimerization,53,54 the resulting monomeric model proteins were taken up by the clathrin pathway as described previously.55

Each of the model transmembrane proteins was separately expressed in SUM159 human breast cancer-derived epithelial cells. We chose these cells because of their thin lamellipodia, which are ideal for visualization of endocytic events. These cells were gene edited to incorporate a HaloTag on both alleles of the σ2 subunit of AP2, the major adaptor protein of the clathrin pathway.56,57 Notably, AP2, which is thought to be in approximately 1:1 stoichiometric ratio with clathrin, is preferred over clathrin for tracking assembly of endocytic vesicles because AP2 localizes exclusively to the plasma membrane, while clathrin is also found at endosomes and other organelles.58 For visualization of AP2 during fluorescence imaging, we used the far-red HaloTag ligand, JF646.59 We imaged the plasma membrane of adherent cells using spinning disk confocal microscopy (Figures 1B-1D and S1A). First, we examined cells expressing the 0-Ub model protein (RFP), which exhibited clear colocalization with endocytic sites (AP2), suggesting robust internalization of the model protein as expected for the transferrin intracellular domain (Figure 1B). Next, we examined confocal images of cells expressing the 1-Ub and 4-Ub model proteins. As the number of ubiquitins increased, the intensity of the model proteins (RFP) that colocalized with puncta in the AP2 channel (JF646) appeared to increase (Figures 1C and 1D).

We quantified the partitioning of each model transmembrane protein to endocytic sites using a publicly available algorithm, CMEAnalysis.60 This algorithm applies a 2D Gaussian fit to puncta in the AP2 channel, which was designated as the “master channel” to identify endocytic structures, and subsequently to the corresponding locations in the transmembrane fusion protein channel, which served as the “subordinate channel,” for estimating the intensity of each structure relative to the local plasma membrane signal. The amplitudes of these Gaussian fits report the relative number of model proteins per endocytic structure.55 The algorithm also records the average fluorescence intensity of model proteins on the plasma membrane in the immediate vicinity of each punctate structure. This intensity represents the relative concentration of model proteins on the surrounding plasma membrane.55 Using these data, we plotted the relative number of model proteins per endocytic structure as a function of the relative concentration of model proteins in the surrounding plasma membrane. The resulting plots showed that the relative number of model proteins within each clathrin-coated structure initially increased with increasing local concentration of model proteins. However, as the concentration on the plasma membrane further increased, the relative number of model proteins in endocytic sites began to plateau toward a maximum value. This maximum reflects the relative number of transmembrane model proteins necessary to fully saturate a clathrin-coated structure, as previously described55 (Figure 1E).

These data suggest that the saturated capacity of clathrin-coated structures for model transmembrane proteins significantly increased as the number of ubiquitins increased, from zero to four. To estimate the saturation value, we used a simple multivalent binding model that we have described previously (Equation 1).55 Here, the average number of model proteins per structure, denoted as 〈n〉, is determined by the maximum capacity per endocytic structure (Nmax), the relative concentration of model proteins on the plasma membrane (Cmem), and the dissociation constant (Kdeff) representing the affinity between the model protein and the endocytic structure.

This equation assumes that the number of model proteins per endocytic structure is governed by a dynamic equilibrium between model proteins inside and outside the structure. We applied this equation to the data in Figure 1E using Nmax and Kdeff as free parameters. Fitting this model to the data suggests that the relative affinity (Kdeff) of the model proteins for endocytic sites increased with the number of ubiquitins conjugated to it. Specifically, Kdeff increased by 2.3-fold for 4-Ub compared to 0-Ub and by 1.6-fold for 1-Ub compared to 0-Ub (Figure 1F). This result can be explained by multivalent interactions between endocytic proteins and ubiquitin, which likely increase the overall affinity between the model protein and endocytic structures. What was surprising was that the saturated capacity of the endocytic structures, Nmax, also increased, 3.3-fold for 4-Ub compared to 0-Ub and 2.1-fold for 1-Ub compared to 0-Ub (Figure 1F). To distinguish specific ubiquitin-binding effects from nonspecific steric effects, we used a control construct in which all four ubiquitins carried mutations (L8A, I44A, and V70A) that disrupt binding to UIM domains, such as those in Epsin and Eps15.61 This 4-Ub (4M) construct maintains the same overall size and multivalency as the original 4-Ub model protein, isolating the effect of biochemical binding from steric contributions. Interestingly, this mutant protein exhibited significantly reduced recruitment to endocytic sites, with both binding affinity and saturation capacity approaching the levels observed for the 0-Ub model protein (Figures S1B and S1C). These results support the conclusion that specific interactions between ubiquitin and endocytic adaptor proteins, rather than nonspecific steric effects of the polyubiquitin chain, were responsible for enhanced recruitment to endocytic sites. This finding suggests that endocytic sites can accommodate a substantially larger number of poly-ubiquitylated model proteins in comparison to mono-ubiquitylated or non-ubiquitylated proteins. If so, then a similar trend should exist for endocytic internalization of the model proteins. Therefore, we next sought to evaluate internalization of model proteins using flow cytometry-based assays.

Poly-ubiquitin chain length controls ligand uptake by model receptors

We next evaluated the impact of ubiquitylation of model receptors on ligand uptake by SUM159 cells. For this purpose, we designed a model receptor-ligand system, as shown in Figure 2A. Similar to the model transmembrane proteins in Figure 1, the N terminus of the model receptors began with a fixed number of stable ubiquitins (0, 1, or 4), followed by the TfR intracellular and transmembrane domains. The extracellular domain consisted of an RFP domain for visualization followed by a C-terminal domain consisting of a single-domain camelid antibody (i.e., nanobody) with nanomolar affinity for GFP.62 As before, the lysines in the intracellular domain of TfR and the linked ubiquitins were mutated to arginines. We transiently expressed each model receptor in SUM159 cells, which were subsequently incubated with a solution of 500 nM GFP for 1 h. Confocal images of these cells revealed clear colocalization between the model receptor (RFP), its ligand (GFP), and endocytic sites (AP2-JF646). The intensity of the receptor and ligand at endocytic sites appeared to increase with increased length of the ubiquitin chain (0, 1, and 4), as expected (Figures 2B-2D).

After incubation with GFP, the cells were trypsinized and thoroughly washed to ensure removal of unbound GFP from the cell surface and the culture medium.63 Before starting the experiment, the expression levels of each of the plasmids and AP2 were confirmed to be similar (Figures S2A-S2C). The total GFP uptake for each cell population was then quantified (Figures S2D and S2E). Although CME is the predominant internalization route for transferrin receptor,64,65 a possible contribution from clathrin-independent pathways cannot be ruled out in this ligand uptake assay. As shown in Figure 2E, the intensity distribution of GFP fluorescence shifted toward higher values for cells expressing the 0-Ub model receptor, compared to the untransfected control group, indicating the uptake of GFP. Comparing the distributions for the three model receptors (Figure 2F), cells expressing the model receptor with 1-Ub showed a 2.6-fold increase in GFP uptake compared to cells expressing the 0-Ub model receptor, while cells expressing the 4-Ub receptor showed a 4.8-fold increase compared to the 0-Ub group. These results confirm that the increase in localization to endocytic sites observed for ubiquitylated receptors in Figure 1 correlates with a similar increase in their internalization by cells.

Poly-ubiquitylation of model receptors increases stability of endocytic structures relative to mono-ubiquitylation

The increase in the number of ubiquitylated model proteins required to saturate endocytic structures (Figures 1E and 1F), which correlates with an increase in ligand uptake by ubiquitylated model receptors (Figures 2E and 2F), suggests that clathrin-coated vesicles have a larger capacity for ubiquitylated transmembrane proteins compared to their non-ubiquitylated counterparts. How is this increase in capacity achieved? We considered three nonexclusive explanations (Figure 3A). First, given that ubiquitin plays an important role in stabilizing endocytic sites,19,66 the presence of a significant number of ubiquitylated transmembrane proteins at the plasma membrane could cause endocytic sites to grow physically larger such that they can accommodate a larger amount of transmembrane protein cargo. Second, the increased stability of endocytic sites in the presence of ubiquitylated transmembrane proteins could result in a greater fraction of endocytic sites successfully maturing into productive endocytic vesicles that internalize cargo. Third, the increased affinity of ubiquitylated transmembrane proteins for endocytic sites could enable these proteins to effectively outcompete other transmembrane proteins for limited space within endocytic structures with a fixed capacity.

First, we assessed the impact of transmembrane protein ubiquitylation on the size of clathrin-coated vesicles. Using CMEAnalysis, we quantified the intensity of endocytic structures in the AP2 channel (JF646) for the model transmembrane proteins containing varying numbers of ubiquitins. Although AP2 intensity does not directly reflect coat size and may be influenced by factors such as turnover rates, the near 1:1 stoichiometric ratio between AP2 and clathrin allows the relative number of AP2 proteins per structure to serve as a rough proxy for the size of endocytic structures.58 Figure 3B shows the average AP2 fluorescence intensity for endocytic structures in cells expressing each of the model transmembrane proteins (0-Ub, 1-Ub, and 4-Ub). These data suggest a modest increase in the size of endocytic structures for cells expressing the 4-Ub model transmembrane protein compared to cells expressing the 0-Ub model transmembrane protein (18%). However, there was no significant increase in the size of endocytic structures in cells expressing the 1-Ub model transmembrane protein compared to cells expressing 0-Ub.

Next, we examined the impact of the 0-Ub, 1-Ub, and 4-Ub model transmembrane proteins on the stability of growing endocytic structures. For this purpose, total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) microscopy was used to image the recruitment of model proteins into growing endocytic structures in real time (Figure S3). TIRF is ideal for tracking the dynamics of endocytosis due to the shallow penetration depth (~100 nm) of the evanescent field, which selectively illuminates the plasma membrane while minimizing fluorescence from the cytoplasm and organelles. We captured images every 2 s, collecting 250 frames in total across the same two fluorescent channels used above: (i) AP2 (JF646) and (ii) transmembrane model proteins (RFP).

The time series were analyzed to track individual endocytic events from initiation to departure from the TIRF field. The lifetime of an endocytic structure on the membrane, measured as the interval between the arrival and departure of a labeled marker, can range from seconds to minutes.67 Structures lasting less than 20 s are typically classified as “short-lived” and are likely abortive, based on prior studies showing their inability to generate productive vesicles. In contrast, “productive” structures, which successfully form vesicles, typically remain on the membrane for 20 s to several minutes. Structures that persist for over 180 s are considered ‘long-lived’ and are thought to have stalled, potentially undergoing delayed internalization or failing to form vesicles (Figures 3C and 3D).68 Each of these populations was observed in our experiments, and the distribution of the lifetimes of endocytic structures for cells expressing each of the model transmembrane proteins is shown in Figure 3E. The data indicate that the fraction of “short-lived” endocytic structures progressively decreased upon expression of the 1-Ub and 4-Ub model transmembrane proteins, which corresponded to an increase in the number of “productive” structures (10% and 22% for 1-Ub and 4-Ub, respectively, in comparison to 0-Ub), while the fraction of “long-lived” structures remained low and approximately constant (Figure 3F). Further, we quantified the intensity of model proteins at EEA1-positive endosomal structures relative to the intensity of the corresponding puncta in the EEA1 channel. Both the 0-Ub and 4-Ub model proteins showed a positive correlation with EEA1, indicating that endosomal structures with higher EEA1 signals recruited more cargo (Figure S4). Notably, the slope was higher for the 4-Ub model protein compared to the 0-Ub model protein, suggesting that a higher degree of ubiquitylation enhances the efficiency of cargo delivery into early endosomes. Taken together, these data suggest that increases in the size and stability of endocytic structures upon the expression of ubiquitylated transmembrane proteins could partially explain the increased uptake of these cargo.

Poly-ubiquitylated model transmembrane proteins can outcompete mono-ubiquitylated proteins for endocytic uptake

We next evaluated the impact of ubiquitylation on competition between transmembrane proteins for space within clathrin-coated vesicles. Specifically, we co-expressed two model proteins simultaneously in SUM159 cells: one labeled with RFP and the other with GFP. The RFP-labeled model protein (TfR-RFP), which was not ubiquitylated, served as a “bystander,” while the GFP-labeled model protein served as a “competitor” and was conjugated with either 0, 1, or 4 ubiquitins, using the same domain structure as the model proteins in Figure 1 (Figures 4A-4D). Confocal microscopy showed that both RFP and GFP-labeled model proteins localized to endocytic sites (AP2 puncta in JF646 channel). However, as the number of ubiquitins on the competitor increased, the partitioning of the RFP-labeled bystander into these sites decreased. Specifically, the contrast in the RFP channel between the endocytic sites and the surrounding plasma membrane weakened, while the GFP signal contrast progressively increased. These shifts suggest that the presence of a ubiquitylated competitor reduced the localization of the non-ubiquitylated bystander at endocytic sites. These trends are quantified in Figure 4E, which indicates that the relative number of bystanders (RFP) per endocytic structure initially increased with the relative concentration of bystanders on the surrounding plasma membrane, before approaching a plateau corresponding to the saturated capacity of the endocytic structures for the bystander protein. Notably, as the GFP-tagged competitors are in close proximity to the RFP-tagged receptor on the plasma membrane, there is potential for fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET), which could reduce the intensity in the GFP channel. To avoid this potential artifact, we only analyzed the RFP channel images. Additionally, cells were selected based on their expression level of the GFP-tagged competitor, ensuring that variations in expression level were not misinterpreted as competition between the receptors.

The data clearly show that the saturated capacity of endocytic structures for the bystander protein (RFP labeled) is highest when the bystander is expressed alone and progressively decreases when it is co-expressed alongside the 0-Ub or 4-Ub competitors (GFP labeled). Fitting these data with the multivalent binding model used above (Equation 1) provides measure of the impact of the competitor on the effective affinity of the bystander for endocytic sites (Kdeff) and the effective capacity of endocytic sites for the bystander (Nmax) (Figure 4F). These trends indicate that the competitor had little impact on the affinity of the bystander for endocytic sites, as would be expected, given that the competitor is unlikely to alter the biochemistry of binding between the bystander and endocytic adaptor proteins such as AP2. In contrast, the presence of the competitor had a substantial influence on the effective capacity of endocytic sites for the bystander, lowering its saturation value by 1.2-fold for the 1-Ub competitor and 1.6-fold for the 4-Ub competitor, in comparison to the 0-Ub competitor. These results suggest that internalization of a transmembrane protein decreases substantially in the presence of more highly ubiquitylated competitors (Figure 4G). Notably, while the effect of competition on localization of receptors to endocytic sites becomes more apparent as their concentration on the plasma membrane increases (Figure 4E), the inherently crowded nature of the plasma membrane suggests that this effect is relevant at all levels of receptor expression. Specifically, as discussed in the introduction, the plasma membrane is densely inhabited by transmembrane proteins, such that transmembrane cargo faces inherent competition with endogenous proteins that must be displaced before they can enter endocytic structures. Therefore, while competition effects become easier to visualize and quantify as expression levels increase, they are likely an inherent feature of the biophysical environment at the plasma membrane.

Poly-ubiquitylated model receptors can outcompete mono-ubiquitylated model receptors for ligand uptake

Up to this point, our data suggest that the increased capacity of endocytic sites for ubiquitylated cargo can be explained by increases in both the stability of endocytic sites (Figures 3E and 3F) and favorable competition with non-ubiquitylated cargo (Figure 4), with a more minor contribution from an increase in the size of endocytic structures (Figure 3B). Next, we used flow cytometry to measure the impact of competition between ubiquitylated and non-ubiquitylated cargo during endocytic uptake of ligands. Specifically, we asked what impact a ubiquitylated “competitor” has on the ability of a non-ubiquitylated “bystander receptor” to internalize its ligand via endocytosis. For this purpose, we employed a non-ubiquitylated model receptor for GFP, identical to the one used in Figure 2A, except that the RFP domain was replaced by BFP, leaving the RFP channel available for RFP-labeled competitors. Using flow cytometry, we measured the ability of the resulting “bystander receptor” to internalize GFP when expressed either alone or in combination with RFP-tagged competitors having 0, 1, or4 ubiquitins fused to their intracellular domains. Before measuring GFP uptake, we first measured the expression levels of the bystander receptor and the competitor using flow cytometry. This step ensured that both the transmembrane proteins were expressed at comparable levels across all conditions (Figures S5A and S5B). Only cells with similar BFP and RFP expression levels were included in the subsequent GFP uptake analysis, ensuring that any differences in internalization were attributed to the ubiquitylation state of the competitor rather than variations in protein expression. As shown in Figures 5A-5D, the competitors did not bind to GFP such that the internalized GFP signal resulted entirely from internalization of the bystander receptors.

After incubation with GFP, as shown in Figures 5A-5D, we observed clear colocalization of RFP and GFP signals under confocal microscopy at the endocytic structures (AP2, JF646). In the RFP channel, the contrast of the endocytic sites increased with the number of ubiquitins in the chain, consistent with the results from Figure 1. However, the contrast of GFP puncta appeared to decrease, indicating the inhibition of GFP uptake in the presence of ubiquitylated competitors. Subsequently, we quantified GFP uptake using flow cytometry (Figures S5C and S5D). Cells were incubated with GFP at 37° for 1 h and then washed with trypsin and PBS to remove unbound GFP. As shown by the flow cytometry analysis results in Figures 5E and 5F, cells expressing the bystander receptors with GFPnb exhibited a significant rightward shift in GFP fluorescence intensity distribution compared to the untransfected control group, indicating the specific uptake of GFP. Notably, as the number of ubiquitin moieties on the competing receptor increased, the extent of this rightward shift in GFP fluorescence decreased significantly (Figures 5E and 5F). Cells expressing the competitor with 1-Ub showed a 1.4-fold reduction in GFP uptake compared to cells expressing the 0-Ub competitor, while cells expressing the 4-Ub competitor exhibited a 2.1-fold reduction. The progressive reductions in the uptake of non-ubiquitylated receptors in the presence of the 1-Ub and 4-Ub competitors demonstrate that cells prioritize ubiquitylated proteins for internalization at the expense of non-ubiquitylated proteins and their ligands.

DISCUSSION

While ubiquitylation is thought to play an important role in regulating the recycling of transmembrane proteins, important questions remain regarding the specific types of ubiquitylation required to promote endocytosis.12,69 In particular, it has remained unclear whether mono-ubiquitylation vs. poly-ubiquitylation is sufficient to prioritize transmembrane proteins for internalization.10,23,43 Here, we demonstrate that both mono-ubiquitylation and poly-ubiquitylation can significantly enhance endocytic uptake of transmembrane proteins. However, poly-ubiquitylation had a substantially greater impact on the stability of endocytic structures in comparison to mono-ubiquitylation (Figures 3E and 3F) and also conferred a larger advantage in assays of competitive localization to endocytic sites (Figure 4) and ligand uptake (Figure 5). Collectively, these results suggest that conjugation of poly-ubiquitin chains to transmembrane proteins plays an important role in CME, both in terms of stabilizing nascent endocytic sites and prioritizing specific cargo and their ligands for removal from the plasma membrane surface.

More broadly, our findings have implications for the productive assembly of endocytic sites. It has recently been proposed that multi-valent, liquid-like complexes of endocytic proteins promotes the assembly of productive endocytic vesicles.70,71 In particular, binding of ubiquitin to the early endocytic protein, Eps15, has been shown to shift the balance of endocytic events from abortive to productive.19 These findings are in line with other recent studies demonstrating that ubiquitin can stabilize liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) in diverse contexts.72-74 Our current work suggests that ubiquitylated transmembrane proteins could play an important role in stabilizing nascent endocytic sites, as suggested by data in Figure 3. In this context, poly-ubiquitylation appears to exert a more potent stabilizing effect compared to mono-ubiquitylation, suggesting that the presence of poly-ubiquitylated transmembrane cargo could promote more efficient recycling at the plasma membrane. Moreover, other studies suggest that ubiquitin E3 ligases of the Nedd4 family could localize within endocytic sites, where they could facilitate localized ubiquitylation of transmembrane proteins.75,76 This effect would be expected to further stabilize endocytic sites and prioritize uptake of locally ubiquitylated cargo.

Importantly, our studies highlight the potential of poly-ubiquitylated transmembrane proteins to out-compete their non-ubiquitylated and mono-ubiquitylated counterparts during endocytic internalization. While the relationship between ubiquitylation and protein state remains poorly understood, recent work suggests that ubiquitin ligases play an important role in plasma membrane quality control, selectively targeting misfolded or damaged membrane proteins for ubiquitin-mediated internalization and degradation.77,78 This mechanism could help to efficiently remove defective proteins from the plasma membrane, preventing their accumulation under proteotoxic stress or after exceeding their functional lifespan. From this perspective, the ability of endocytic sites to selectively internalize poly-ubiquitylated transmembrane proteins, while leaving proteins with little or no ubiquitin behind, could provide cells with a mechanism for removing damaged proteins from the cell surface while preserving functional proteins.

Limitations of the study

Our 4-Ub construct structurally mimics the multivalent topology of K63-linked ubiquitin chains by linearly fusing four ubiquitin units with all lysines mutated to arginines and C-terminal glycines to alanines, preventing chain branching or disassembly. While this design enables us to isolate the role of ubiquitin multivalency in CME, it does not fully capture the biochemical properties or dynamic behavior of native, enzymatically assembled ubiquitin chains. Further studies using linkage-specific poly-ubiquitin chains will be needed to determine how different chain architectures influence cargo sorting, and endocytic-site stability.

In addition to Eps15 and Epsin, multiple adaptor proteins such as Hrs, STAM, and Epsin family members contain ubiquitin-binding domains and may also contribute to the recognition of ubiquitylated cargo during endocytosis.79,80 Future studies involving genetic or biochemical perturbation of these proteins could clarify the specific contributions of each adaptor in regulating selective cargo uptake.

Our model receptor system uses overexpressed constructs and artificial ligand interactions, which may not fully represent the behavior of endogenous receptors. Although our imaging analyses focus on membrane-localized fluorescence, differences in protein turnover or trafficking cannot be excluded in the flow cytometry experiments.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Human-derived SUM159 cells genetically engineered to incorporate a HaloTag at the C terminus of both alleles of AP2-σ2 were a kind gift from Dr. Tomas Kirchhausen. Cells were cultured in a 1:1 mixture of DMEM (high glucose) and Ham’s F-12 medium (Hyclone, GE Healthcare), supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Hyclone), 2 mM L-glutamine, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. Additionally, the medium contained 1 μg/mL hydrocortisone (H4001; Sigma-Aldrich), 5 μg/mL insulin (I6634; Sigma-Aldrich), and 10 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.4). Cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 and passaged every 2–3 days to maintain sub-confluent growth. For imaging experiments, cells were seeded onto acid-washed glass coverslips to promote optimal surface adherence and cleanliness. A seeding density of 3 × 104 cells per coverslip was used, and cells were allowed to adhere and spread for 24 h prior to transfection. Transfections were performed using 1 μg of plasmid DNA and 3 μL of Fugene HD reagent (Promega) per coverslip, following the manufacturer’s protocol. After 24 h, HaloTagged AP2-σ2 was labeled by incubating the cells with 100 nM JF646–HaloTag ligand (Promega) for 15 min at 37°C. Cells were then washed extensively with prewarmed medium to remove excess dye and immediately imaged in live-cell imaging medium under physiological conditions.

METHOD DETAILS

Plasmids

A plasmid for expression of 0-Ub-TfR-Δecto-RFP was generated by replacing the GFP domain with mRFP of the TfR-Δecto-GFP construct, which we previously described.81 The TfR-Δecto-GFP plasmid encoded the first 88 amino acids of the human transferrin receptor, corresponding to the intracellular and transmembrane domains of TfR (identical to amino acids 1–88 of GenBank accession number AAA61153) fused to GFP. A 9-amino acid linking sequence (GKGDPPVAT) connected TfR and GFP in this construct. Here mRFP was PCR amplified from the pcDNA3-mRFP plasmid acquired through Addgene as a gift from Dr. Douglas Golenbock (Addgene #13032). To prevent ubiquitylation, all four lysine residues in the cytoplasmic domain of TfR were mutated to arginines (Kto R) to prevent ubiquitylation.

The constructs 1-Ub-TfR-Δecto-mRFP and 4-Ub-TfR-Δecto-mRFP were generated by adding one or four tandem ubiquitin (Ub) coding sequences, respectively, to the N terminus of the 0-Ub-TfR-Δecto-RFP plasmid. To stabilize the attached ubiquitin moieties and prevent unintended poly-ubiquitylation, all seven lysine residues within the ubiquitin sequence were mutated to arginines (K to R). Additionally, to prevent intracellular deubiquitylation, the C-terminal glycine residues (GG) of ubiquitin were mutated to alanine residues (GG to AA). This design ensured that the ubiquitin chains remained covalently attached and stable during cellular expression. For the 1-Ub-TfR-Δecto-RFP construct, a single mutated ubiquitin sequence was added to the N terminus of TfR-Δecto-RFP. For the 4-Ub-TfR-Δecto-RFP construct, four tandemly fused ubiquitin sequences with the same mutations were inserted at the same position. Additionally, the 4-Ub (4M)-TfR-Δecto-RFP construct was generated by introducing binding-deficient mutations (L8A, I44A, V70A) into all four ubiquitin units. These mutations disrupt interactions with ubiquitin-binding domains such as the UIM motifs of Epsin and Eps15.

To generate 0-Ub-TfR-Δecto-mRFP-GFPnb, 1-Ub-TfR-Δecto-mRFP-GFPnb, and 4-Ub-TfR-Δecto-mRFP-GFPnb, the GFP nanobody (GFPnb) coding sequence was PCR amplified from the pOPINE GFP nanobody plasmid (Addgene #49172), a gift from Dr. Brett Collins. The amplified GFPnb sequence was inserted into the C terminus of each plasmid, downstream of the mRFP coding region. To ensure flexibility and proper folding of the fused proteins, a flexible linker sequence (ESRPQYNQRRYT) was introduced between the mRFP and GFPnb coding regions. The resulting constructs were designed to retain the functional properties of both mRFP and GFPnb while allowing proper spatial arrangement for nanobody binding to GFP-tagged proteins.

To generate the constructs 0-Ub-TfR-Δecto-GFP, 1-Ub-TfR-Δecto-GFP, and 4-Ub-TfR-Δecto-GFP, the mRFP coding region in the respective 0-Ub-TfR-Δecto-RFP, 1-Ub-TfR-Δecto-RFP, and 4-Ub-TfR-Δecto-RFP plasmids was replaced with the coding sequence for GFP. To improve the monomeric properties of GFP and prevent dimerization, the A206K mutation was introduced into the GFP sequence using site-directed mutagenesis. This mutation converts alanine at position 206 to lysine, ensuring that the GFP remains monomeric under physiological conditions.

To generate the 0-Ub-TfR-Δecto-BFP-GFPnb construct, all four lysine residues in the cytoplasmic domain of TfR were mutated to arginines (K to R) to prevent ubiquitylation. The TfR-Δecto-BFP-GFPnb plasmid, which we had previously used,55 served as the template for this modification.

All plasmid constructs above were custom-designed and constructed by GenScript. The designs included specific modifications such as the addition of ubiquitin sequences with lysine-to-arginine (K to R) mutations and glycine-to-alanine (GG to AA) modifications, replacement of mRFP with GFP (A206K mutant) or BFP, point mutations (L8A, I44A, V70A) into all four ubiquitins, and the inclusion of GFP nanobody (GFPnb) with a flexible linker. After receiving the plasmids, all constructs were verified by full sequencing (Plasmidsaurus) to confirm the correct insertion, reading frame, and presence of the intended mutations.

Fluorescence microscopy

Live cell plasma membrane imaging was conducted using an Olympus spinning disk confocal microscope, which featured a Yokogawa CSU-W1 SoRa confocal scanner unit and an Olympus IX83 microscope equipped with a 100×Plan-Apochromat 1.5 NA oil-immersion objective. Fluorescence emission was captured using a Hamamatsu ORCA C13440-20CU CMOS camera. Excitation was achieved with lasers at 561 nm for RFP, and 640 nm for JF646.

Cell movies were collected on a TIRF microscope consisting of an Olympus IX73 microscope body, a Photometrics Evolve Delta EMCCD camera, and an Olympus 1.4 NA ×100 Plan-Apo oil objective, using Micro Manager version 1.4.23. The coverslip was heated to produce a sample temperature of 37°C using an aluminum plate fixed to the back of the sample. All live-cell imaging was conducted in TIRF mode at the plasma membrane 24 h after transfection. Transfection media used for imaging lacked pH indicator (phenol red) and was supplemented with 1 μL OxyFluor (Oxyrase, Mansfield, OH) per 33 μL media to decrease photobleaching during live-cell fluorescence imaging. 532 nm and 640 nm lasers were used for excitation of RFP and JF646-HaloTag ligands of AP2, respectively. Cell movies were collected over 250 frames at 2 s intervals.

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry assays were conducted using a Guava easyCyte Flow Cytometer (Millipore Sigma-Aldrich), equipped with 405 nm, 488 nm, and 532 nm lasers for fluorescence excitation and detection. Samples were prepared by resuspending cells in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to minimize cell clumping and non-specific binding. Before acquisition, the instrument was calibrated according to the manufacturer’s recommendations to ensure consistent performance and accuracy. Data acquisition was performed at a flow rate of35 μL/min, with event thresholds set to minimize background noise. Forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC) parameters were used to assess cell size and granularity, respectively, and gates were drawn on FSC versus SSC plots to exclude debris and identify the population of interest. Appropriate fluorescence compensation was applied to correct for spectral overlap between channels when necessary.

To ensure consistent expression levels across different plasmids, cells were transfected with the indicated plasmids and incubated for 24 h under standard culture conditions. Prior to GFP uptake experiments, cells were detached using trypsin-EDTA, washed with PBS, and resuspended in staining buffer (PBS with 2% FBS). The median fluorescence intensity (MFI) of the fluorescent protein linked to each plasmid was measured using flow cytometry to assess expression levels.82 If necessary, transfection conditions (plasmid concentration or transfection time) were adjusted to minimize variability between groups. Only samples with comparable mean fluorescence intensity values were used for subsequent GFP uptake assays. Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo software (Treestar). Statistical analysis of flow cytometry results was performed using at least n = 3 biological replicates.

Image analysis

Clathrin-coated structures, observed as fluorescent puncta in confocal and TIRF images, were identified using CMEAnalysis (Danuser lab).60 AP2-σ2 signal was used as the master channel to track clathrin-coated structures. Local intensity maxima in the RFP channel were fitted with 2D Gaussian functions. The Gaussian standard deviation was derived from the microscope’s physical parameters to estimate the point spread function. To ensure the accuracy of the fit, the Anderson-Darling test was applied to the residuals. The Gaussian amplitude, corresponding to the fluorescence intensity of each detected punctum, along with the punctum’s location, were recorded. For a punctum to qualify as a valid clathrin-coated structure, it had to be diffraction-limited and significantly brighter than the surrounding membrane, as described in previous work.41 For validated puncta in the master channel, a corresponding 2D Gaussian fit was applied to the puncta in transmembrane fusion protein, receptor, or ligand channels, within a 3σ pixel radius of the master channel location.83

Fluorescence lifetime measurements were performed by detecting and tracking clathrin-coated structures using CMEAnalysis in MATLAB. The point spread function (PSF) of the data was utilized to calculate the standard deviation of the Gaussian fit. The AP2-σ2 signal was designated as the master channel for tracking clathrin-coated structures. Only structures that remained visible for at least three consecutive frames were included in the analysis. Structures that were already present in the first frame of the time series were excluded to avoid pre-existing or non-endocytic events with undefined initiation time. Similarly, puncta that persisted throughout the entire imaging session without disappearance were excluded to ensure accurate lifetime measurements.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Micrograph acquisition experiments were independently repeated on separate days, producing consistent results across replicates. For CMEAnalysis, cell image data were collected independently under at least two different experimental conditions or on separate days. As described throughout this paper, all reported experimental results are based on a minimum of three independent replicates. The number of biological replicates was determined based on the experimental design and guidance from previously published studies. No statistical methods were used to predefine sample sizes. The sample size (n) is indicated in the figure captions, referring either to the number of biological replicates or technical replicates. All statistical tests were executed in GraphPad Prism. Unless noted otherwise, data are displayed as mean ± s.e.m. Pairwise comparisons were evaluated with a two sample t test. Exact p-values are given in the corresponding figure captions; significance is indicated as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and “n.s.” for p > 0.05.
