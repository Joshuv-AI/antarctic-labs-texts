Introduction

Rocky Mountain spotted fever (RMsf) is a tick-borne illness caused by Rickettsia rickettsii, classically associated with fever, rash, and vasculitis complications. Cardiac involvement is uncommon but clinically significant. Electrocardiac manifestations, such as arrhythmias and conduction disturbances, including complete heart block (CHB), have been reported in isolated case studies, complicating an already challenging diagnosis of rare infectious diseases.1,2 Lyme carditis, a well-characterized entity, accounts for approximately 1% of reported cases3 of Lyme disease compared to the rarity and underreporting of CHB in RMsf. Lyme carditis typically presents with fluctuating atrioventricular (AV) block that can rapidly progress to CHB but is usually reversible with antibiotic therapy and temporary pacing. Exclusion of reversible etiologies of CHB is critical before pursuing permanent pacemaker implantation. We present a case of RMsf in an 81-year-old woman presenting with CHB as an initial sign. Such isolated presentation makes an already tricky diagnosis of RMsf even more difficult. The authors aim to raise awareness and appreciation for electrocardiac manifestations of tick-borne diseases.

Case presentation

An 81-year-old Nigerian woman presented to the emergency room (ER) with complaints of fever, malaise, and generalized weakness. She was visiting family in the United States for the past month and reported symptoms of fevers, fatigue, and malaise for the past week. She recently noted pleuritic chest pain without a cough and denied palpitations or syncope. Medical records from Nigeria from 1 year prior included a normal echocardiogram with normal left ventricular ejection fraction and normal electrocardiogram (ECG) showing sinus rhythm with normal conduction. Medical history included hypertension, which was being treated with amlodipine 10 mg, bisoprolol 2.5 mg, and candesartan 8 mg daily.

Upon arrival in the ER, an ECG revealed sinus rhythm with CHB and junctional escape rhythm at 40 beats per minute (Figure 1A). Her blood pressure was 144/52 mm Hg, respiratory rate was 15 breaths per minute, and temperature was 97.4°F. Laboratory values were significant for a white blood cell count of 17.3 K/μL and troponin of 137 ng/L. Other laboratory values showed acute renal failure with creatinine of 4.1 mg/dl and elevated aspartate aminotransferase of 202 U/L and alanine aminotransferase of 138 U/L. N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide was 44,787 pg/ml. Chest computed tomography scan showed confluent predominant central ground-glass opacity with interlobar septal thickening of the bilateral pulmonary lobes, which was worse on the right with additional confluent areas of alveolar opacification.

A: Initial electrocardiogram demonstrating sinus rhythm with complete heart block and junctional escape rhythm. B: Electrocardiogram showing sinus rhythm with intact atrioventricular conduction after doxycycline therapy.

In the ER, she became more lethargic and developed tachypnea and hypoxia requiring intubation for airway protection. After intubation, she became hypotensive and more bradycardic, requiring atropine injection. Due to persistent bradycardia and hypotension, a dopamine infusion was initiated. Despite dopamine, her cardiac rhythm did not improve. A temporary transvenous pacemaker was placed until the etiology of her illness and sepsis was identified.

She was admitted to the intensive care unit. Her medical condition worsened over the next few days with intermittent fevers of 102.6°F and fulminant multiorgan failure. In addition to acutely progressive hepatic and renal failure requiring dialysis, laboratory evaluation showed markedly elevated transaminases (aspartate aminotransferase: 5094 U/L and alanine aminotransferase: 2664 U/L), hyperbilirubinemia (total bilirubin: 3.3 mg/L), worsening anemia (hemoglobin: 7.9 g/dL), and severe thrombocytopenia with a platelet decline from 203 K/μL to 48 K/μL. The procalcitonin level was 13.5 ng/mL and lactate dehydrogenase was 1550 U/L. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a left ventricular ejection fraction of 60%. Given her severe acute multiorgan failure, fevers, and guarded prognosis, the placement of a permanent pacemaker was deferred until recovery.

An infectious disease specialist evaluated the patient. The presence of intermittent fevers, leukocytosis, confusion, and encephalopathy pointed to the possibility of an infectious etiology of her CHB. Additionally, acute hemolytic anemia with thrombocytopenia, acute renal and liver failure, shock, and signs of meningeal irritation rendered the patient too high-risk for consideration of a permanent pacemaker. Blood and urine cultures were negative. Bacterial, fungal, and viral panels, including human immunodeficiency virus and hepatitis testing, were also negative. Empiric doxycycline was added to broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy, given the syndrome of fever with CHB and suspicion for tick-borne illness. Lyme and Salmonella serologies were negative. 2 weeks into hospitalization, R. rickettsii IgG resulted at 1:64, confirming a diagnosis of RMsf. The patient was eventually extubated and was able to confirm she had a tick bite. Subsequently, her cardiac rhythm recovered with telemetry showing improved conduction to normal sinus rhythm (Figure 1B), allowing removal of the temporary pacemaker wire.

Discussion

The patient’s clinical course was consistent with severe, complicated RMsf supported by a history of tick exposure and IgG titer of 1:64. RMsf diagnosis is obvious when the typical skin rash is present. However, it is diagnostically challenging when the rash is absent, as in our patient. A previous review identified 10.8% of reported cases of RMsf at Duke University Medical Center to be without the typical rash.4 Amongst the spotless RMsf, 61% were men and about two-thirds were of African ancestry. Additional findings included spontaneously resolving bradycardia, elevated high-sensitivity troponin and lactate dehydrogenase levels suggestive of underlying myocarditis with associated cardiogenic shock, thrombocytopenia, transaminitis, and clinical meningoencephalitis, with negative testing for other infectious etiologies.

Although tick-borne infections are known to cause bradycardia, the pathogenesis of CHB in Rickettsial infection is not well described. Rickettsial cardiac involvement is thought to stem from endothelial infection, leading to systemic vasculitis, microvascular injury, and inflammatory infiltration of the myocardium and conduction system.5,6 These mechanisms can produce arrhythmia, myocarditis, and conduction abnormalities, though such manifestations remain underrecognized. From a management perspective, excluding reversible etiologies of CHB is critical before considering permanent pacemaker implantation. Guidelines classify pacing in reversible AV block as not indicated (Class III).7 In infectious causes such as RMsf or Lyme disease, temporary pacing and targeted antimicrobial therapy are preferred.

Given the diagnostic complexity, high clinical alertness and acumen are essential to help clinicians recognize such obscure presentations. A collaborative multidisciplinary approach involving intensivists, infectious disease specialists, cardiologists, electrophysiologists and advanced practice providers improves accuracy and outcomes.8 Literature reviews and case reports emphasize that clinician awareness of these rare presentations is necessary for timely diagnosis and appropriate management.1, 2, 3,5, 6, 7, 8

Conclusion

RMsf, a tick-borne disease, has uncommon but clinically significant cardiac involvement. Diagnosis of RMsf is obvious when the typical skin rash is present. However, it is diagnostically challenging when the rash is absent. Additionally, electrocardiac manifestations such as arrhythmias and conduction disturbances, including CHB, complicate an already challenging diagnosis of a rare infectious disease such as RMsf. Tick-borne diseases such as Lyme disease can lead to Lyme carditis and can cause heart block.3 However, less is known about other tick-borne diseases. Rickettsial disease, such as RMsf causing CHB, is underreported. A multidisciplinary approach while focusing on the clinical picture is valuable in reaching an accurate diagnosis. Excluding reversible etiologies of CHB is critical before considering permanent pacemaker implantation. Cases such as this one raise awareness and help clinicians to be alerted to such rare manifestations.
