Hallucinations and cognitive impairment are associated with blood pressure fluctuations in Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Shohei Nomoto, Tomoko Oeda, Yasuhiro Tomii, Nina Kasahara, Hiroshi Uchizumi, Toshiya Ishihara, Masayuki Kohsaka, Shintaro Toda, Atsushi Umemura, Kwiyoung Park, Satoshi Tomita, Masayuki Tahara, Kenji Yamamoto, Yasumasa Yamamoto, Hideyuki Sawada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with Parkinson’s disease (PD) frequently develop cardiovascular autonomic dysfunctions, including blood pressure (BP) fluctuations and orthostatic hypotension (OH) in the early stage, and hallucinations or cognitive impairment in the middle stage to late stage. The current study aimed to determine whether OH, BP fluctuations and cerebral white matter hyperintensities (WMHs) on MRI are associated with the development of hallucinations and cognitive impairment.MethodsIn this joint cross-sectional case–control study conducted across two institutions, we analysed the systolic BP coefficient of variation (SBP-CV) and nocturnal BP dipping using ambulatory BP monitoring and OH prevalence in 121 patients with PD and 21 hospital controls. The association between these factors and hallucinations/psychosis or cognitive impairment was analysed using multivariable logistic regression models. Then, we conducted mediation analyses of the relationship between abnormal BP fluctuations and cognitive impairment in PD, with WMHs evaluated using the Fazekas scale as the intermediate factor.ResultsPatients with PD were characterised by large SBP-CV without pulse rate-CV elevation, neurogenic OH and riser pattern of nocturnal BP dipping. Neurogenic OH was significantly associated with hallucinations/psychosis (adjusted OR: 6.22; 95% CI 2.47 to 15.64; p<0.001), and large SBP-CV was significantly associated with hallucinations/psychosis (adjusted OR: 2.15; 95% CI 1.32 to 3.53/5 points; p=0.002) and cognitive impairment (adjusted OR: 1.79; 95% CI 1.15 to 2.78/5 points; p=0.010). Periventricular hyperintensity (PVH) was associated with cognitive impairment (adjusted OR: 5.80; 95% CI 1.81 to 18.61; p=0.003), but not with BP fluctuations. The ORs for SBP-CV in cognitive impairment in PD were very similar across multivariable analyses that included PVH or deep WMH as mediator variables.ConclusionsLarge BP fluctuations in PD were associated with hallucinations and cognitive impairment, but WMHs did not mediate this relationship.Keywords: PARKINSON'S DISEASE, DEMENTIA, HALLUCINATIONS

Introduction

The clinical diagnosis of Parkinson’s disease (PD) is based on motor symptoms responsive to dopamine therapy, while non-motor symptoms often appear earlier. Among non-motor symptoms, the cardiovascular autonomic dysfunction presenting with abnormal blood pressure (BP) patterns such as orthostatic hypotension (OH), supine hypertension (HT) and postprandial hypotension often manifests early in the disease. It impairs the patient’s quality of life.1 2 A recent systematic review reported OH in 39% of patients with PD. Physiological nocturnal BP dipping status was also disrupted, with 40% and 36% showing riser and non-dipper status, respectively.3

A recent 5-year prospective study involving older Japanese adults found that increased day-to-day BP fluctuations are a significant risk factor for all-cause dementia.4 Cognitive impairment and hallucinations are particularly prevalent in middle-stage to late-stage PD, suggesting an association with Lewy body pathology.5 However, research evidence linking BP fluctuations and white matter hyperintensities (WMHs) on MRI to cognitive impairment is limited,6 and the association between BP fluctuations and hallucinations in patients with PD has not been sufficiently addressed.

In this study, we investigated whether abnormal BP fluctuations, based on 24-hour ambulatory BP monitoring (ABPM), are associated with hallucinations or cognitive impairment. We also explored whether brain WMHs mediate the association between abnormal BP fluctuations and hallucinations or cognitive impairment in PD.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a joint study at Utano National Hospital and Kyoto Katsura Hospital in Kyoto, Japan. To ascertain the mechanism underlying BP fluctuations in patients with PD, we performed the Schellong test and collected ABPM data from patients with PD and hospital controls (HCs). We assigned patients with PD with hallucinations/psychosis (PDH) as cases and those without them (PDNH) as controls. Subsequently, PD patients with cognitive impairment (PDCI) and without cognitive impairment (PDNCI) were assigned as cases and controls, respectively. Finally, we explored whether brain WMHs mediate abnormal BP fluctuations, hallucinations/psychosis or cognitive impairment in patients with PD.

Participants

Consecutive patients with PD treated at the study hospitals from August 2018 to May 2022 were screened for eligibility, and those who consented were recruited. PD diagnosis was made according to the Movement Disorder Society (MDS) clinical diagnostic criteria.7 Patients who met the diagnostic criteria for dementia with Lewy bodies based on the ‘1-year rule’8 were excluded. All patients underwent brain MRI, and those with MRI findings suggestive of multiple system atrophy or progressive supranuclear palsy were excluded. In addition, patients with acute illnesses, including infection, heart failure or recent surgeries, were excluded. Patients unable to complete the 24 hours ABPM were also excluded. Age-matched and sex-matched HC patients without neurological disorders from the orthopaedic convalescent ward of Utano National Hospital were recruited as HCs. We performed neurological examinations to rule out neurological deficits among HCs.

Sample size calculation

Our preliminary data from patients with PD suggest that the systolic BP coefficient of variation (SBP-CV) follows a normal distribution with a SD of approximately 4.0. Assuming a prevalence of hallucinations of 40% in the current PD cohort, 46 patients with PDH and 69 with PDNH are required to achieve 90% statistical power at a two-sided α level of 5% to detect a 2.5-point difference in mean SBP-CV (SD=4.0) between the two groups. Based on these data, 121 patients with PD were enrolled in this study. Similarly, considering that 40% of patients would have cognitive impairment, 121 patients were deemed sufficient for the analysis of PDCI and PDNCI.

Clinical characteristics and ABPM parameters

We collected data on age, sex, body mass index (BMI) (kg/m2), estimated glomerular filtration rate (mL/min/1.73 m2), geriatric nutritional risk index (GNRI), and smoking history for all participants. Medical histories of HT, dyslipidaemia, diabetes mellitus (DM), atrial fibrillation (Af) and stroke were recorded. The definitions of these conditions are described in online supplemental methods.

Neurogenic OH was determined when vasopressor agents were required to prevent presyncope or syncope, or when a drop in SBP (ΔSBP) of >20 mm Hg and ΔPR (pulse rate)/ΔSBP ratio <0.5 bpm/mm Hg occurred within 3 min of standing from supine (Schellong test).9 For patients with PD, PD duration and Hoehn and Yahr stages (HY)10 were collected, and the levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD, mg/day) was calculated.11 The frequency of MAO-B inhibitor use and dopamine agonist use was also collected.

SBP and PR were measured every 30 min for 24 hours using an ABPM apparatus (FM-970; Fukuda Denshi, Tokyo, Japan). Means, SDs and CVs were calculated for all measurements. The CV of SBP (SBP-CV) and PR (PR-CV) were used to represent diurnal cardiovascular fluctuations.

We also collected the prevalence of nocturnal HT and nocturnal BP dipping status classified into four categories: riser, non-dipper, dipper and extreme dipper. These were defined as follows in online supplemental methods.

Evaluation of cognitive function and hallucinations/psychosis

Cognitive function was assessed using the Japanese version of the Mini–Mental State Examination (MMSE-J).12 13 Patients with an MMSE score <26 were regarded as having cognitive impairment. The psychotic symptoms were evaluated through interviews, and hallucinations and psychosis were assessed using ratings by the MDS United PD Rating Scale Part 1.2 hallucination and psychosis item.14 PD patients were considered PDH if they scored at least 2 points on the scale. Patients who were prescribed antipsychotic medication for hallucinations or psychosis were also regarded as PDH.

Brain white matter lesion evaluation

Vascular lesions in the cerebral hemispheres, such as periventricular hyperintensity (PVH) and deep WMH (DWMH), were graded using the Fazekas scale15 on a brain MRI axial fluid-attenuated inversion recovery image. The evaluation of WMHs was performed jointly by the principal researchers from the two institutions, and inter-rater reliability was ensured. WMHs were assessed using the Fazekas grade of PVH and DWMH, where each was dichotomised as 0=none or mild (Fazekas grade 0–1) or 1=moderate to severe (Fazekas grade 2–3). Cohen’s k coefficient was calculated to confirm the inter-rater reliability of the assessment of the Fazekas grade of PVH and DWMH.

Statistical analysis

Categorical data were compared using Fisher’s exact or χ2 tests, and continuous data were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test (non-Gaussian distribution) and Student’s t-test (Gaussian distribution). Comparisons between three or more groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance with Dunnett’s multiple-comparison post hoc test.

The prevalence of neurogenic OH and ABPM parameters was compared between the PD and HC groups. The relationship between SBP-CV on 24 hours ABPM and PD duration was analysed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. The association between hallucinations/psychosis and ABPM parameters or neurogenic OH was investigated in both univariable and multivariable logistic regression models. The following variables were incorporated into the multivariable regression model, considering their relative importance to clinical outcomes: age (years), sex (male/female), PD severity as assessed by HY, which was dichotomised as 0 (HY 1–3) or 1 (HY 4–5), neurogenic OH (yes/no), and ABPM parameters (SBP-CV, dipping status (riser/not-riser)). ORs for neurogenic OH and ABPM parameters, along with 95% CIs, were calculated. The ORs in the regression model were considered as a measure of the degree of association. The variance inflation factors (VIFs) of each study variable in the regression models were confirmed to be <2.0. Similar analyses were performed to evaluate the association between cognitive impairment and these explanatory parameters.

To clarify whether brain WMHs mediate the association between abnormal BP fluctuations and hallucinations/psychosis or cognitive impairment, we performed mediation analyses to assess the strength of the relationship between BP fluctuations and hallucinations/psychosis or cognitive impairment in regression models with and without WMHs as covariates.

The threshold of statistical significance was set at p<0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for V.28.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics).

Hallucinations/psychosis and abnormal BP fluctuations in patients with PD

Of the 121 patients with PD, 46 patients (38.0%) were classified as PDH. They had a longer disease duration, worse HY, higher LEDD and lower MMSE scores than those with PDNH (table 2A). No significant difference was observed in the prevalence of medications such as dopamine agonists and MAO-B inhibitors. The prevalence of vascular risk factors, including smoking history, HT, DM, dyslipidaemia and Af, was not significantly different between the two groups. Regarding BP fluctuations, SBP-CV was significantly larger in PDH than in PDNH (mean (SD): 18.1 (4.7) vs 15.3 (4.2), p<0.001). Neurogenic OH was significantly more prevalent in PDH than in PDNH (78.2% vs 37.3%, p<0.001). The riser status was more prevalent in PDH than in PDNH, with a marginal but non-significant difference (PDH vs PDNH; 56.5% vs 38.7%, p=0.056) (table 2A).

Relationship between abnormal BP fluctuations and hallucinations/psychosis or cognitive impairment in PD with brain WMHs as the intermediate factor

Brain WMHs and abnormal BP fluctuations

The inter-rater reliability of brain WMH (PVH and DWMH) assessments was high (κ=0.798 and 0.702, respectively). We investigated whether abnormal BP fluctuations were linked to WMHs, and the univariable analysis revealed a significant association between neurogenic OH and PVH (OR 3.06, 95% CI 1.03 to 9.07, p=0.043). However, the effect attenuated in the multivariable models. SBP-CV was not significantly related to PVH or DWMH in either the univariable or multivariable models (table 4A).

Discussion

Characteristics of abnormal BP fluctuations in PD

In this study, the characteristic BP abnormalities in PD were large diurnal BP fluctuations without PR compensation and neurogenic OH. These results are consistent with the most prominent features of cardiovascular dysfunction in PD.2 The prevalence (53.8%) of neurogenic OH in our patients with PD was higher than that reported in a recent systematic review (38.7%).3 This discrepancy could be due to the low BMI (20.9) in the present study, as a lower BMI has been associated with OH.16 The prevalence of neurogenic OH in our study was almost identical to the prevalence of 48.0% reported in a recent study of Japanese de novo PD.17 However, the mean PD duration of our cohort was 8.0 years. In this study, the extent of BP fluctuations did not increase with PD duration, suggesting that abnormal BP fluctuations with PR decompensation may occur at an early stage of the illness in some patients with PD. Peripheral sympathetic denervation, driven by Lewy pathology, is thought to be the primary cause of abnormal BP fluctuations during the early stages of PD. A decreased heart-to-mediastinum accumulation ratio on 123I metaiodobenzylguanidine myocardial scintigraphy, which reflects sympathetic cardiac denervation18 is an important diagnostic marker for PD <3 years after onset.19

α-Synuclein pathology develops in the peripheral and central autonomic nervous (CAN) systems in PD, affecting brain regions that comprise the CAN.20 When BP falls, neural activity in the bilateral anterior insula cortices, the anterior cingulate and medial prefrontal cortex, the amygdala, the midbrain and the mediodorsal nucleus of the thalamus decreases and disinhibits sympatho-excitatory pathways, facilitating the elevation of both HR and sympathetic vasoconstrictor tone.21 In a previous study, PD patients with OH had significantly decreased perfusion of the anterior cingulate cortex compared with those without OH.22 These results suggest that the CAN may regulate OH in PD, with its dysfunction leading to OH exacerbation.

The high prevalence (45.5%) of nocturnal BP riser status is another characteristic of PD, consistent with the 40.5% prevalence reported in a recent systematic review.3 Sodium retention in response to neurogenic OH may result in supine HT at night. In addition, circadian rhythm disruptions, such as nocturnal non-dipping BP, could be caused by disorders in the suprachiasmatic nucleus in the hypothalamus, which is agreeably the highest centre for circadian rhythms, and the hypothalamus is known for its abundance of Lewy bodies and Lewy neurites in PD.23

Abnormal BP fluctuations and hallucinations in patients with PD

This study revealed that PD hallucinations/psychosis were associated with neurogenic OH and larger SBP-CV. We confirmed this association after adjusting for cognitive impairment, known as a risk factor for hallucinations/psychosis. Few reports have focused on the connection between abnormal BP fluctuations or neurogenic OH levels and hallucination development in PD. In a study by Oka et al, the fall in BP in the head-up tilt test was significantly larger in patients with PD with visual hallucinations than in those without visual hallucinations.24 In a large-scale longitudinal study, autonomic dysfunction emerged as an independent risk factor for hallucinations in PD.25 The current results are consistent with these results.

Abnormal BP fluctuations and cognitive impairment in patients with PD

This study revealed that a large SBP-CV was significantly associated with cognitive impairment. The impact of large BP fluctuations on cognitive impairment remained significant even after eliminating the influence of hallucinations/psychosis. Patients with PD, along with disease progression, develop dementia more frequently than the general older population. In this study, 39.7% of the participants had cognitive impairment, and the mean PD duration was 8.1 years. A longitudinal study showed that dementia developed in 46% of the participants after 10 years of PD.26 Previous studies in patients with PD have shown significant associations between cognitive impairment and OH,27 28 supine hypertension, large SBP-CV and riser status in ABPM.29 30 The association between BP fluctuations and cognitive impairment was observed not only in patients with PD but also in the general older population. A prospective study demonstrated that the risks of developing all-cause dementia were significantly higher in individuals in the highest SBP-CV quartile than in those in the lowest quartile,4 suggesting large BP fluctuations constitute a significant risk for dementia.

Possible mechanisms linking abnormal BP fluctuations with hallucinations and cognitive impairment in patients with PD

This study found that abnormal BP fluctuations were independently linked to hallucinations/psychosis and cognitive impairment, although the exact mechanisms remain unclear. We hypothesised that these BP fluctuations may lead to brain WMHs, which could, in turn, cause hallucinations and cognitive impairment. However, our findings did not reveal significant associations between BP fluctuations and WMHs, suggesting that WMHs may not mediate the association between abnormal BP fluctuations and hallucinations or cognitive impairment (figure 2). In previous studies, the relationship between BP fluctuations and WMHs has remained controversial in both older adults and patients with PD. In the present study, brain WMHs were a significant risk factor for cognitive impairment but not for hallucinations/psychosis. The view that WMHs are predictors of cognitive impairment aligns with the findings in the general older population31 and in patients with PD.32

In addition to brain WMHs, several other mechanisms may link large BP fluctuations to cognitive impairment and hallucinations in patients with PD. A systematic review of 44 autopsy studies on dementia in patients with PD indicated that tau and amyloid-β pathologies independently contribute to the development and patterns of cognitive decline in PD, but α-synuclein pathology is the main contributor to dementia.33 On the other hand, visual hallucinations are specific to Lewy pathology rather than other neurodegenerative pathologies.34

Emerging evidence suggests that vascular risk factors also play a role in the pathogenesis of AD. Experimental studies indicate that amyloid-β has potent cerebrovascular effects, and hypoxia-ischaemia is a significant modulator of cerebral amyloidogenesis.35 Regarding α-synuclein pathology, animal models showed that brain ischaemia leads to the oligomerisation and aggregation of α-synuclein.36 Pilotto et al reported that patients with Lewy body disease and severe OH showed greater medial temporal lobe atrophy than those with mild or no OH.37 Chronic BP fluctuations may lead to cerebral hypoperfusion, which begins even in the early stage of PD and could exacerbate amyloid-β and α-synuclein accumulation, while potentially contributing to cognitive impairment and hallucinations in patients with PD.

Another possible mechanism is that the significant association between large BP fluctuations and either cognitive impairment or hallucinations in PD may indicate that the brain regions capable of developing these clinical symptoms are spatially proximate. The amygdala and anterior cingulate cortex, which are part of CAN, are profoundly associated with the baroreflex.21 The invasion of Lewy pathology into the CAN could result in exacerbating abnormal BP fluctuations, primarily caused by peripheral sympathetic denervation. The clinicopathological study in PD showed significant associations in the limbic system between dementia and α-synuclein burden in the amygdala, anterior cingulate cortex, entorhinal cortex and hippocampus,38 as well as between visual hallucinations and Lewy body burden in the amygdala,38 39 parahippocampal gyri39 and anterior cingulate cortex.40

The current study results suggest that BP fluctuations may affect cognitive impairment and hallucinations in PD through pathomechanisms other than WMHs. It is still unclear if the association between BP fluctuations and cognitive impairment or hallucinations arises from BP fluctuations-induced acceleration of neurodegeneration, or if it reflects the spatial proximity of the neurodegenerative lesions involved in BP fluctuations, cognitive impairment and hallucinations.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was a cross-sectional study. Second, although the overall sample size was moderate, the number of HC participants was relatively small, which might limit the robustness of comparisons with the PD cohort. Third, diagnoses were based on established criteria and ancillary imaging; however, the underlying pathology was not determined, though we rigorously applied predetermined definitions of various clinical factors, and some clinical data were derived from clinical records. Finally, the potential effects of antihypertensive medications and therapeutic interventions for OH were not fully evaluated and may have influenced the observed associations.

Conclusions

Overall, patients with PD frequently exhibited large BP fluctuations on 24 hour ABPM without compensatory PR reactions, even in the early stages. These fluctuations could be an independent risk factor for developing hallucinations and cognitive impairment, although they were not caused by brain white matter damage. Though large-scale cohort studies will be needed to verify the results of the present study, the current study findings support the possibility that intervention for BP fluctuations in patients with PD can prevent the onset of hallucinations and cognitive impairment.
