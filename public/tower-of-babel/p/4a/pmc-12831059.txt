Prevalence and associated risk factors of neck pain in the Turkish population: A population-based study
Authors: Halide Zeynep Aydın, Cengiz Güney, Kemal Aydın, Nadire Gülçin Yıldız, Hasan Aykut Karaboğa, Yohane V. A. Phiri, Fatma Kahraman Güloğlu, Serdar Aydın
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:This study aimed to determine the prevalence of neck pain in the Turkish population and its association with sociodemographic factors, work status, self-rated health, obesity, depression, physical activity, non-prescribed medications, and vitamin/nutritional supplements.Methods:A cross-sectional study was conducted using data from the 2019 Türkiye Health Survey, which included 17 084 participants aged ≥15 years. Descriptive and multivariate binary logistic regression analyses were performed to investigate the prevalence of neck pain and its associated risk factors.Results:Neck pain prevalence increased with age, peaking between 35 and 65 years old. Women were 3.2 times more likely to experience neck pain than men. Married and divorced individuals had higher risks than single individuals did. There was no significant relationship between neck pain and educational level or physical activity. Self-rated health showed a strong inverse relationship with neck pain: “good” health increased risk 11 times, “fair” 64 times, “poor” 145 times, and “very bad” 387 times compared to “very good” health. House workers had a lower risk (0.8 times) compared to employees, while pre-obese and obese individuals had a 1.2 times higher risk. Neck pain was twice as common in those using non-prescribed drugs and 1.3 times more common in those using vitamins and supplements.Conclusion:Neck pain is prevalent in Türkiye, with factors such as age, sex, marital status, employment, health status, obesity, and use of non-prescribed drugs and supplements as significant risk factors. This study provides a reference for understanding neck pain, its associated factors, and guiding future research.Level of Evidence:Level III, Prognostic Study.

Introduction

Neck pain is a prevalent musculoskeletal disorder among adults, with significant variations between countries. It is a common condition that leads to considerable pain, disability, and economic burden.1,2 The global prevalence of neck pain ranges from 17% to 75%. It ranks fourth among the leading causes of disability worldwide3 and is the second most common cause of chronic pain after low back pain. Despite fewer studies addressing the prevalence of neck pain compared to lower back pain, its significance is well recognized. In general, the prevalence is higher among women and in high-income countries than in low-income and middle-income countries, as well as in urban and rural areas. Various environmental and individual factors can affect the occurrence and progression of neck pain.4

Numerous studies have explored the impact of ergonomic, sociodemographic, individual, behavioral, and psychosocial factors that increase the risk of neck pain. Occupational ergonomic factors, such as heavy lifting, frequent bending and twisting, whole-body vibration, and a high body mass index, are associated with a greater risk of neck pain. The risk is particularly high in jobs involving prolonged desk work.5 However, research in the UK has suggested that psychological factors may be more significant predictors of neck pain than occupational or physical activities.6 A study conducted in the United States found that occupation and working hours affected the risk of neck pain among employees.7

Age is a critical factor in the development of neck pain, particularly in middle-aged individuals. Research indicates that both men and women in this age group are at a higher risk of neck pain. Studies have consistently shown a higher prevalence of neck pain among women, with the risk peaking in the 35-49 age range before gradually decreasing.8 In addition to age, psychological factors, such as long-term stress, anxiety, and depression, have been identified as significant contributors to neck pain.9 Psychological stress can play a role in both the onset and continuation of neck pain,10-12 although some studies report a weak correlation.13

Obesity is thought to be a risk factor for neck pain because of its underlying biological mechanisms. The lower back anatomically supports the upper body and bears the burden of excess body weight. However, because excess weight throws the waist out of alignment, the impact shifts to the spine, causing pain in the neck. Although the hypothesis posits a strong association between obesity and neck pain, epidemiological evidence remains inconclusive despite a growing body of research on this subject.14

In Türkiye, research on neck pain prevalence is limited compared with the more extensively studied low back pain. According to the literature review, most investigations have focused on specific occupations at the local level rather than population-based studies.5,15-17 In this review, no comprehensive population-based study investigated the prevalence of neck pain and its associated factors in Türkiye.18

This study aimed to determine the prevalence of neck pain among the general Turkish population aged ≥ 15 years and to analyze its correlations with demographic, socioeconomic, health status, obesity, depression, physical activity, and the use of non-prescribed medications, vitamins, and nutritional supplements. The analysis was based on data from the 2019 Türkiye Health Survey (THS) conducted by TURKSTAT on behalf of the Turkish government. THS is conducted every 4-5 years and is based on the European Health Interview Survey, which has been adapted to the Turkish context by a national panel of experts.

Material and methods

Data and variables

This study utilized data from the 2019 THS conducted by the TURKSTAT. The THS aims to obtain information on health indicators. The THS has been implemented to produce many indicators that cannot be compiled through administrative records in the field of health and to create a data source for decision makers and researchers. In addition to being a nationwide study, THS is important as it allows for international comparisons and sheds light on national needs.

The geographical scope of the survey is the households in all settlements within the borders of the Republic of Türkiye. The population characterized as institutionalized (military, dormitories, prisons, hospitals, nursing homes, etc.) is excluded. The survey was conducted in provinces, districts, and towns covering 12 main and 26 subregions using a multi-stage probability sampling design. The framework used in the sampling of the study is the Address Based Population Registration System. The first stage sampling unit is the blocks randomly selected in proportion to their size from clusters (blocks) containing an average of 100 household addresses; the second stage sampling unit is the household addresses systematically randomly selected from each selected cluster.

A total of 9470 household addresses were selected from 947 sample blocks in Türkiye (10 households from each block). Within the scope of the Türkiye Health Survey, data were collected from individuals living at the sample household addresses by the computer-assisted face-to-face interview method. A total of 23 199 individuals, including 17 084 individuals aged 15 years and over and 6115 children aged 0-14 years, were surveyed.

In the 2019 Türkiye Health Survey, information on the diseases that children aged 0-14 years had during the 6 months preceding the survey date and data on child disability were obtained. For individuals aged 15 and over, information on general health status, chronic diseases, functional ability to carry out daily activities, personal care, use of health services, use of medication, vaccinations, and measurements, height, and weight values, were collected. Detailed information on the sampling methods, data collection, and response rates is available online.19,20

According to the THS results, 3884 (22.4%) of the 17 084 individuals aged 15 years and over who were surveyed in Türkiye reported experiencing neck pain, neck disorders, or other chronic neck defects in the past 12 months. Of the 3834 participants experiencing neck pain, 1076 (13.8%) were male and 2758 (29.7%) were female. In the second step, to establish a control group, an equal number of participants without neck pain, another 3834 participants were randomly selected, yielding a total sample size of 7668 individuals evenly distributed across both groups. In sum, 3834 participants with neck pain and another 3834 participants without neck pain (a total of 7768 participants) were included in the analysis as a control group from the survey conducted with a total of 17 084 people. The participants without neck pain were randomly selected from the survey to match the number of participants with neck pain, which enhances the representativeness of the sample.21,22 Thus, 7768 of the total 17 084 participants were included in the analysis.

Neck pain was identified based on a “yes” response to the question: “During the past 12 months, have you had neck pain, neck disorder or other chronic neck defects?” Variables included demographic data (age, education level, marital status, employment status), health status, mental health (PHQ-8 for depressive symptoms), body mass index (BMI), and an 8-item physical activity module.

Sociodemographic variables included age (categorized into 7 groups), education level (primary or below, secondary, high school, university or above), marital status (single, married, widowed, divorced), and employment status (employee, housewife, jobseeker, student, retired, disabled). Notably, 77% of the participants in the housework category were women and 23% were men. Body mass index was calculated from height and weight measurements and then categorized as normal weight (18.50 ≤ BMI < 25), pre-obese (25.00 ≤ BMI < 30), and obese (BMI ≥ 30). Due to the low proportion of underweight participants (3.4%), the underweight and normal weight categories were combined for analysis.

The THS includes the PHQ-8 (Patient Health Questionnaire) short module. The PHQ-8 (8-item Patient Health Questionnaire) was used to diagnose and measure the severity of depressive symptoms for the assessment of depressive symptoms, a validated tool in both international literature23 and Turkish studies.24 PHQ-8 measures the frequency of depressive symptoms over the past 2 weeks using a Likert scale. Scores ranged from 0 to 24, with a cut-off point of 10 indicating a depressive disorder.

Physical activity was measured by using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ). Activity levels were categorized as inactive (<600 METs/week; METs: Metabolic equivalent), minimally active (600-3000 METs/week), moderately active (3000-6000 METs/week), or highly active (>6000 METs/week) based on MET values (IPAQ Research Committee, 2004). The validity and reliability of this module in Türkiye were confirmed by Özkeskin and Özden.25

Statistical analyses

As explained previously, the 2019 THS included 17 084 adults aged ≥ 15 years across Türkiye. According to the survey results, 22.4% (3834 individuals) reported experiencing a neck disorder or other chronic neck defects in the past 12 months. To ensure reliable results and accurate predictions, an equal number of participants, with and without neck pain, were included in the study. Thus, the sample consisted of 3834 individuals with neck pain and 3834 randomly selected individuals without neck pain.

A multivariate binary logistic regression model was used to identify the variables affecting neck pain.26 Independent variables included socioeconomic and demographic factors such as sex, age, education level, employment status, depressive disorder, obesity, health status, physical activity level, non-prescribed medication use (over-the-counter medication), and vitamin and supplement intake. The results are presented as P-values (significance was set at P < .05), odds ratios, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The frequencies of the independent variables are presented in Tables 1 and 2, and the results of the multivariate binary logistic regression analysis are presented in Table 3.

Results

The demographic and health characteristics provided in Tables 1 and 2 provide a detailed overview of the study population, highlighting the diverse factors that may influence the prevalence and risk factors of neck pain. This comprehensive dataset is crucial for understanding the prevalence and factors associated with neck pain across different demographic segments in Türkiye.

As shown in Table 1, the study involved a comprehensive analysis of 7668 individuals, with a higher proportion of females (n = 4344, 57%) than males (n = 3324, 43%). Age distribution among participants varied, with the largest group being those aged 35-44 years (n = 1549, 20%), followed by individuals aged 45-54 years (n = 1448, 19%), and those aged 55-64 years (n = 1276, 17%). Participants aged 25-34 years (n = 1180, 15%) and 15-24 years (n = 1043, 14%) were also well represented, whereas those aged 65-74 years (n = 787, 10%) and 75+ years (n = 385, 5%) were less common.

Regarding marital status, the majority of participants were married (n = 5302, 69%), with singles constituting 20% (n = 1492), widowed 3% (n = 262), and divorced 8% (n = 612). In terms of educational attainment, almost half of the participants had an education level of primary school or below (n = 3605, 47%). Secondary school education was reported by 16% (n = 1205), high school and equivalent education by 19% (n = 1450), and college education or higher by 18% (n = 1408). Employment status varied significantly, with 36% (n = 2756) employed, 36% (n = 2734) engaged in housework, 15% (n = 1144) retired, 7% (n = 542) continuing education, 5% (n = 372) job seekers, and 2% (n = 120) disabled.

In Table 2, health-related characteristics show that nearly half of the participants stated their health as good (n = 3761, 49%), while 31% (n = 2402) rated it as fair. A smaller proportion of participants stated that their health was very good (n = 547, 7%), bad (n = 823, 11%), or very bad (n = 135, 2%). Physical activity levels varied, with 43% (n = 3261) reporting mild activity, 32% (n = 2443) reporting moderate activity, 17% (n = 1313) reporting no activity, and 9% (n = 651) reporting intense activity.

According to the depression scores obtained from the PHQ-8 module, 93% (n = 7149) of the participants had no depressive symptoms, and 7% (n = 519) had depression symptoms with a PHQ-8 score of 10 or above. The obesity prevalence indicated that 40% (n = 3041) were of normal weight, 37% (n = 2797) were pre-obese, and 24% (n = 1830) were obese. Regarding the use of non-prescribed medications, 29% (n = 2244) of the participants reported using them, while 71% (n = 5424) did not. Additionally, 8% (n = 644) of the participants used vitamins and nutritional supplements, whereas 92% (n = 7024) did not.

The multivariate logistic regression analysis presented in Table 3 identified several significant factors associated with neck pain, providing a comprehensive understanding of the risk factors. The model used men, the 15-24 age group, married individuals, those with primary education or below, employed individuals, those with normal weight, no depressive symptoms, inactive status, very good health status, no use of non-prescribed medicine, and no use of vitamins or nutritional supplements as the reference categories. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test indicated a good fit (P = .370).26 The model explained 52% of the variance in neck pain, with a classification success rate of 78% for patients with neck pain, 80% for those without neck pain, and 79% overall.

As can be seen from the model, sex was a significant predictor of neck pain. Females were significantly more likely to report neck pain than males (odds ratio [OR] = 3.152, 95% CI: 2.679-3.709, P < .01). Therefore, females are more than 3 times as likely to report neck pain compared to males. This finding aligns with the existing literature suggesting that women are generally at higher risk for musculoskeletal disorders, including neck pain, possibly due to differences in physical structure, hormonal influences, and psychosocial factors.4,27 Similar to sex, marital status also showed a significant relationship with neck pain. Married participants had higher neck pain (OR = 1.284, 95% CI: 1.015-1.624, P < .05), as divorced individuals reported (OR = 1.645, 95% CI: 1.143-2.368, P < .01), compared to single individuals. Other studies have not found a relationship between neck pain and marriage. However, a significant relationship was identified in this study and another population-based study conducted in the USA. Married individuals participate in the labor market at a higher rate than those in other marital status groups. Neck disorders are more common among those who are employed. Therefore, marriage and working life may overlap and have a significant effect on neck pain.11,28

Age was a critical factor, with all age groups over 25 years showing significantly higher odds of reporting neck pain compared to the 15-24 age group. The highest risk was observed in the 45-54 age group (OR = 2.616, 95% CI: 1.867-3.666, P < .01). The progressive increase in risk with age underscores the degenerative nature of musculoskeletal conditions and the cumulative impact of occupational and lifestyle factors over time.4 However, the education level was not a significant predictor (P = .587). There was a trend indicating that higher educational attainment may be associated with lower odds of neck pain. For instance, individuals with a college education or above had an OR of 0.877 (95% CI: 0.729-1.056, P = .167), although this was not statistically significant. This suggests that higher education correlates with better health awareness and access to health resources.29

Neck pain is also closely related to working conditions and working style.10 The work status showed some significant effects. House workers who worked as unpaid family workers were 0.8 times less likely to report neck pain than employees (OR = 0.811, 95% CI: 0.673-0.977, P < .05). This might reflect differences in physical demands and stress levels between the various types of work. Although job seekers and those continuing education/training showed no significant effects, the disabled group had higher, albeit not statistically significant, odds of neck pain (OR = 1.763, 95% CI: 0.871-3.571, P = .115).

In general, self-rated health status is a significant predictor of neck pain. Participants who rated their health as very bad had an extraordinarily high OR and were 387 times more likely than those in the very good category (95% CI: 106.108-1410.718, P < .01), followed by those who rated their health as bad (OR = 145.051, 95% CI: 80.810-260.363, P < .01), fair (OR = 63.647, 95% CI: 37.319-108.552, P < .01), and good (OR = 11.212, 95% CI: 6.626-18.972, P < .01). This gradient suggests a strong link between perceived health and neck pain, emphasizing the role of overall health in musculoskeletal conditions.30 In contrast, depression was not significantly associated with neck pain. Although typically associated with depression, increased pain perception, and chronic pain conditions,30 no significant effect on neck pain was observed. Similarly, physical activity did not show a significant association (P = .129). Mild, moderate, and intense activities had ORs close to 1, suggesting no substantial impact on the risk of neck pain in this sample. The relationship between physical activity and neck pain did not show clear results in the studies.29,31

Obesity is also a significant factor in neck pain. Compared to normal-weight subjects, the pre-obese and obese groups were approximately 1.2 times (95% CI: 1.048-1.459, P < .01) more likely to report neck pain. These results suggest that excess weight contributes to neck pain,32 likely through mechanical stress and inflammatory pathways.33 In the latter finding, those using non-prescription medications were significantly 2.10 times more likely to report neck pain (OR = 2.101, 95% CI: 1.844-2.394, P < .01), indicating that individuals experiencing neck pain may be more likely to self-medicate. Similarly, those who used vitamins and nutritional supplements were 1.3 times more likely to report neck pain than those who did not (OR = 1.267, 95% CI: 1.017-1.579, P < .05), possibly reflecting a proactive approach to managing health conditions.34

In summary, multivariate analysis identified significant predictors of neck pain, including sex, age, marital status, work status, self-rated health status, obesity, and the use of non-prescribed medicines and vitamin supplements (P < .05). In the model adjusted for sociodemographic and other variables, educational degree, depression, and physical activity levels were insignificant. Females, older individuals, married and divorced individuals, those with poor self-rated health, and those who were obese showed higher odds of experiencing neck pain. These findings highlight the importance of considering demographic, lifestyle, and health-related factors for the prevention and management of neck pain.

Discussion

This study represents the first population-based investigation into the prevalence of neck pain in Türkiye, revealing that neck pain affects 22% of the population, making it the second most common musculoskeletal condition after low back pain (32%). The prevalence of neck pain in Türkiye, as indicated by population-based studies, falls within a range similar to that reported in Brazil (24%)3 and Spain (20%)9 at approximately 24%-29%. However, these rates are notably lower than those observed in other countries such as Greece (29%),35 the United States (31%),7,11 and Germany (45%).36 Despite its high prevalence, there has been a noticeable gap in population-based research on this issue within the Turkish context. Therefore, the findings of this study contribute significantly to the existing literature and offer new insights into the epidemiology of neck pain in Türkiye.

The analysis identified several key factors significantly associated with neck pain, including sex, age, marital status, employment status, general health status, obesity, and use of non-prescribed medicines, vitamins, and nutritional supplements. Collectively, these variables accounted for 52% of the variance in neck pain. These results are consistent with international findings, where neck pain is strongly related to age, sex, obesity, general health status, and occupational factors.2,8

As in other studies, women in Türkiye were found to have a significantly 3 times higher risk of neck pain than men. The incidence of neck pain also increases with age and peaks in middle-aged adults. This aligns with the degenerative nature of musculoskeletal conditions and the cumulative impact of occupational and lifestyle factors over time.

Marital status emerged as a notable factor, with married and divorced individuals reporting higher rates of neck pain than singles. Employment status also played a crucial role: employees had a higher prevalence of neck pain, whereas those engaged in housework, predominantly women, were less likely to report neck pain. This could be due to the physical demands and stress associated with the different types of work.

This study found a strong association between self-rated health status and neck pain, with individuals reporting poor health being significantly more likely to experience neck pain. Obesity has also emerged as a significant risk factor, likely due to additional mechanical stress and inflammatory processes associated with excess weight. An interesting finding was the association between neck pain and the use of non-prescribed medicines, vitamins, and nutritional supplements. While these may be considered consequences rather than causes of neck pain, data suggest that individuals with neck pain frequently resort to over-the-counter medications and supplements. This raises the possibility that self-medication may not always be effective and could potentially exacerbate the condition, warranting further investigation using more specific experimental and longitudinal studies.

Unlike many international studies that have reported a strong association between depression and neck pain,9,10 this study did not find a significant relationship between these variables in the Turkish population. While some of the studies conducted in Türkiye at the local and clinical levels were related to depression and neck pain,12 no relation was found in others.16,37 The lack of association may reflect cultural or methodological differences and suggests that depression may not play a prominent role in neck pain in Türkiye as it does in other conditions, such as low back pain, hypertension, and diabetes. Similarly, although physical activity is widely recognized as a key factor in health and disease prevention, its relationship with neck pain was not statistically significant in this study. The physical activity paradox, in which occupational physical activity negatively impacts health outcomes, might explain these findings.38 In the analysis, the risk of neck pain decreased as physical activity increased; however, the results were not statistically significant. Given these complexities, further longitudinal and experimental studies are necessary to better understand the role of physical activity in neck pain.

This study was limited by the lack of data on the frequency, intensity, and duration of neck pain, as well as its impact on the daily life of participants. This study shows that neck pain is a common complaint in Türkiye. It is associated with age, sex, marital status, obesity, and employment status. Despite these limitations, the findings underscore the significant burden of neck pain in Türkiye and highlight the importance of targeted prevention and management strategies. This study also lays the groundwork for future epidemiological research in this area for the Turkish population.

The rising prevalence of neck pain in Türkiye represents an increasingly significant concern for public health, underscoring the need for specialized strategies in prevention and management. This study highlights the importance of addressing key risk factors such as obesity and promoting ergonomic practices to mitigate the long-term impact of musculoskeletal disorders. Building effective interventions relies on a coordinated approach among healthcare providers, policymakers, and adults, which can help reduce healthcare system demands and enhance the well-being of individuals with chronic neck pain. Emphasizing early detection and prompt intervention could help lower both the frequency and severity of neck pain, ultimately contributing to improved health outcomes and decreased healthcare demand.

The findings from this study highlight the need for research that aligns with Türkiye’s specific socioeconomic and cultural context. Long-term studies on ergonomic adjustments in the workplace could provide insights into their effectiveness in managing neck pain over time. Furthermore, research that integrates physical and psychological rehabilitation approaches may reveal a more holistic framework for chronic pain management. Digital health innovations and telemedicine also hold promise for enhancing the remote monitoring and treatment of neck pain, particularly in regions with limited access to healthcare services. Given the increasing prevalence of obesity in Türkiye, examining public health initiatives aimed at obesity prevention could offer a proactive approach for reducing musculoskeletal problems. These efforts could form the basis of national interventions and offer a model for other countries dealing with similar public health challenges.

In conclusion, neck pain is a prevalent condition in Türkiye and is influenced by a combination of demographic, lifestyle, and health-related factors. The findings presented here provide essential insights for healthcare professionals and policymakers as they work to develop effective approaches for the prevention and management of neck pain. Given the limited data from population-based studies in Türkiye, this research provides a critical addition to the field and offers a basis for further study and public health actions.
