Evolution of transcranial magnetic stimulation services in psychiatry in India
Authors: Samir Kumar Praharaj
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) has emerged as the pivotal non-invasive neuromodulation technique for the diagnosis and treatment of various neuropsychiatric disorders worldwide. In India, the adoption and evolution of TMS services have been marked by significant research contributions and clinical advancements over the past three decades. This review synthesizes the historical development, current applications, and prospects of TMS services in psychiatry in India, drawing on a comprehensive analysis of all relevant Indian publications. The growth of TMS research, institutional contributions, clinical protocols, and challenges in implementation is explored, while highlighting the potential for personalized and scalable TMS interventions in the Indian healthcare landscape.

INTRODUCTION

Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS) is a non-invasive neurophysiological technique that used electromagnetic induction to modulate neuronal activity in targeted brain regions. Since its development in 1985 by Anthony Barker in Sheffield,[1] TMS has gained global recognition for its investigational and therapeutic potential in various neuropsychiatric disorders, including major depressive disorder (MDD), obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), addiction, and migraine. In India—where approximately one in seven individuals is affected by mental health conditions—TMS has emerged as a promising intervention, particularly for treatment-resistant cases. This review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of the development of TMS services in India, particularly in psychiatry, highlighting research trends, clinical applications, and future directions through a systematic analysis of Indian publications.

The first FDA-approved TMS device was NeuroStar TMS Therapy system, approved in 2008 for the treatment of MDD in patients who had not responded to “at least one course of antidepressant treatment with an adequate dosage and duration.”[2] Since then, several other TMS systems have received FDA approval for both investigational and therapeutic use in a range of neuropsychiatric disorders [see Supplementary Table 1].

This review was conducted through a comprehensive search of all TMS-related publications from India using PubMed, supplemented by a manual search. Two key publications provide foundational context: a bibliometric analysis of Indian TMS research by Godi and Tikka,[3] and the Clinical Practice Guideline on TMS by Tikka et al.,[4] both of which summarize the existing Indian literature. Based on the patterns of usage and availability of TMS in the Indian context, TMS research can be broadly categorized into three overlapping phases [Figure 1]. However, these phases are not clearly demarcated, with considerable overlap observed across them.

Phases of TMS Services in India

EARLY TRANSCRANIAL MAGNETIC STIMULATION IN NEUROLOGY

The first reported use of TMS in India is credited to Banaras Hindu University in 1997, where it was used to assess corticospinal excitability in malnourished children,[5] as noted in the bibliometric analysis by Godi and Tikka.[3] However, an earlier study by Kumar et al.[6] from the Department of Neurology, Nizam’s Institute of Medical Sciences, Hyderabad, investigated motor evoked potentials by using TMS in patients with ischemic stroke to predict clinical outcomes, making it likely the first published TMS study from India. Subsequently, TMS became available in several neurology departments across the country, as evidenced by research publications from institutions such as the All India Institute of Medical Sciences, Delhi,[78910] the Postgraduate Institute of Medical Education and Research, Chandigarh,[11] the National Institute of Mental Health and Neurosciences (NIMHANS), Bengaluru,[12131415] the Sanjay Gandhi Postgraduate Institute of Medical Sciences, Lucknow,[16] and the Sree Chitra Tirunal Institute for Medical Sciences and Technology (SCTIMST), Thiruvananthapuram.[171819] Notably, the earliest report on theta burst stimulation (TBS) in India was also from SCTIMST,[1718] which examined cortical plasticity changes with levodopa treatment in patients with Parkinson’s disease.

THERAPEUTIC TRANSCRANIAL MAGNETIC STIMULATION TRIALS IN PSYCHIATRY

In contrast to neurology, the adoption of TMS in psychiatry in India occurred somewhat later. A series of therapeutic rTMS trials were conducted in the early 2000s, primarily at the Central Institute of Psychiatry (CIP), Ranchi, located in eastern India.[2021222324252627] Among these, the study by Ray et al.[26] represents the first therapeutic TMS study in psychiatry from India, initiated in 2003. It investigated adjunctive high-frequency rTMS applied to the left prefrontal cortex in patients with depression. This was followed by a study by Goyal et al.,[20] which examined the effects of rTMS on positive and negative symptoms in schizophrenia, and a randomized controlled trial by Praharaj et al.[21] assessing the efficacy of prefrontal rTMS in mania. The first trial investigating rTMS for alcohol craving was conducted by Mishra et al.,[24] demonstrating the effectiveness of dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) stimulation. While the study on obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) by Sarkhel et al.[23] yielded negative findings for OCD symptoms, it did show improvement in comorbid depressive symptoms.

INVESTIGATIVE TRANSCRANIAL MAGNETIC STIMULATION IN PSYCHIATRY

The first investigative TMS study in psychiatry from India was conducted by Muralidharan et al.[28] at NIMHANS, Bengaluru. This study examined abnormalities in cortical and transcallosal inhibitory mechanisms in individuals at high risk for alcohol dependence and reported cortical silent period (cSP) and ipsilateral silent period (iSP), indicating impaired corticocortical and transcallosal inhibition. However, prior to this, other investigative TMS studies had already been carried out in the Department of Neurophysiology (e.g. Udupa et al.[12]). Other notable investigative TMS studies include the use of the mirror neuron paradigm as a marker of social cognition deficits in schizophrenia and related disorders.[29303132]

NEWER DEVELOPMENTS IN TRANSCRANIAL MAGNETIC STIMULATION RESEARCH

Subsequently, several studies from India have explored newer treatment protocols for schizophrenia, including novel targets such as the cerebellum,[33343536] the inferior parietal lobule,[37] priming rTMS stimulation,[38] and emerging treatment modalities such as deep TMS,[3940] neuronavigated TMS,[3637] and robotized neuronavigated TMS.[41] Additionally, several studies have employed multimodal approaches combining TMS with transcranial ultrasound,[4243] dense array electroencephalography (EEG),[44] functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI),[4546] and magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS).[47]

SAFETY ASPECTS OF TRANSCRANIAL MAGNETIC STIMULATION

The first reported case of a seizure associated with TMS in India was from NIMHANS, Bengaluru, where a patient with bipolar disorder developed a seizure during motor threshold estimation using single-pulse TMS.[48] Similarly, low-frequency rTMS was associated with a seizure in a patient with stroke.[49] Seizure induction has also been reported with high-frequency rTMS[5051] and TBS.[5253] Notably, Bagati et al.[50] documented the safe administration of rTMS in a patient with epilepsy when used under antiepileptic drug cover.

FROM ACUTE PHASE TMS TO MAINTENANCE TMS

The effects of rTMS are often short-lasting, thereby necessitating continuation and maintenance treatment protocols. One of the earliest published reports of maintenance TMS from India was by Thirthalli et al.[54] at NIMHANS, Bengaluru. The study described an 8-month maintenance regimen in a patient with antipsychotic-refractory auditory hallucinations, involving weekly rTMS sessions for 6 weeks, followed by fortnightly sessions for 3 months, and then monthly sessions for another 3 months. Subsequently, maintenance TMS has also been reported in cases of OCD[55] and depression.[5657] A large study on maintenance TMS across various disorders—including OCD, pain disorders, depressive disorders, and tinnitus—was published by Shouan et al.[58] from PGIMER, Chandigarh. However, evidence for the effectiveness of maintenance TMS is currently limited. Additionally, there are logistical and cost-related challenges which limit its widespread use.

TRANSCRANIAL MAGNETIC STIMULATION IN PRIVATE PRACTICE

TMS services began to be offered by private practitioners in India from the mid-2000s, initially concentrated in metropolitan cities. Over time, the availability of TMS expanded, with treatment centers now established in smaller cities as well. Some centers, such as Asha Neuromodulation Clinics (https://ashahospital.org/neuro-modulation-clinic/), have not only provided clinical services but have also contributed to training and research in the field. A diverse range of TMS modalities, including deep TMS, is now available in private practice, with real-world observational data emerging to support its use.[59] However, several concerns persist regarding TMS services in the private sector, including variability in standards of care, high treatment costs, and limited accessibility for the general population.

TRAINING IN TRANSCRANIAL MAGNETIC STIMULATION

Several institutes in India have initiated training opportunities in the field of neuromodulation. TMS workshops have been regularly conducted at NIMHANS, Bengaluru. In addition, NIMHANS offers a one-year fellowship in Non-Invasive Brain Stimulation (NIBS) since 2016, aimed at developing clinical and research expertise in the area. Similarly, the All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS), Delhi, has established the Centre for Advanced Research and Excellence (CARE) in Neuromodulation (https://neuromodulationaiims.in/), which provides clinical services and organizes structured training programs. The CIP, Ranchi, also offers periodic training programs in NIBS.

At the institutional level, several other centers now conduct short-term training workshops in TMS and other neuromodulation techniques. A notable initiative is the postgraduate training program at Kasturba Medical College (KMC), Manipal, which includes a dedicated 1-month posting in NIBS for psychiatry residents since 2023. During this posting, residents are actively involved in the clinical administration of ECT, TMS, and transcranial electrical stimulation (tES). Their responsibilities include pre-NIBS’ evaluation of patients, supervised administration of treatment sessions, and serial monitoring of treatment outcomes and adverse effects.

NEUROMODULATION SOCIETY OF INDIA (NSI)

The Neuromodulation Society of India (NSI) was established in 2018 with the mission “to advance the understanding, research, and application of neuromodulation techniques for the betterment of human health and well-being” (https://neuromodulationsociety.co.in/). The first annual conference of the NSI, titled NSICON 2024, was held at NIMHANS, Bengaluru, in collaboration with the Clinical Research Centre (CRC) for Neuromodulation in Psychiatry[60] and the Neuromodulation Specialty Section of the Indian Psychiatric Society (IPS). As of now, the society comprises over 50 members and continues to promote interdisciplinary collaboration, training, and research in the field of neuromodulation.

MULTICENTER COLLABORATION STUDIES ON TRANSCRANIAL MAGNETIC STIMULATION

The CRC for Neuromodulation in Psychiatry is a multi-center initiative aimed at advancing the field of interventional psychiatry in India.[60] It was established through a collaboration between NIMHANS, Bengaluru; the CIP, Ranchi; and KMC, Manipal, with funding support from the DBT/Wellcome Trust India Alliance (https://www.neuromodulation-crc.ipsych.in/). The initiative comprises two major components. The first is the CRCs, which have undertaken three major clinical trials in the domain of neuromodulation.[616263] The second is the Clinical Research Training Programmed (CRTP), a fellowship initiative designed to develop physician-scientists and strengthen the clinical and public health research ecosystem in India.[60] Under this program, 10 fellows have been trained—six at NIMHANS, two at CIP, and two at KMC. As part of this collaborative effort, a dedicated neuromodulation laboratory was also established at KMC Manipal, further strengthening research and training infrastructure in the field.

STANDARDS IN TRANSCRANIAL MAGNETIC STIMULATION

With TMS becoming widely available across India, there is a clear need to establish minimum standards of care to ensure safety, consistency, and efficacy. The efficacy of TMS varies depending on the equipment used, coil type, stimulation parameters, and the clinician’s expertise in administering the treatment. TMS clinicians should be aware of these nuances rather than relying on rigid protocols for specific conditions. Additionally, they should educate patients accordingly to help prevent the indiscriminate use of TMS. Developing structured basic and advanced certification programs—for both beginners and experienced practitioners—can help achieve this goal. These training programs, offered through recognized institutions, could lead to formal certification, and eventually align with national accreditation frameworks. Such programs may also be integrated into broader interventional psychiatry training initiatives.[64]

FUTURE OF TRANSCRANIAL MAGNETIC STIMULATION IN INDIA

Over time, TMS services are expected to become widely available across medical colleges, district hospitals, and private clinics, improving accessibility. Advancements in technology are likely to lead to the development of high-quality, indigenous TMS machines, reducing treatment costs. Accelerated treatment protocols may shorten treatment duration and enhance patient acceptability. The expansion of short-term training programs will help build the necessary workforce to operate TMS clinics. TMS could also be adopted by the government as an additional somatic treatment alongside ECT and integrated into the District Mental Health Programmed (DMHP) or at the taluka level. Soon, domiciliary TMS using portable devices with remote monitoring by clinicians may also become a reality.

WHAT NEXT IN TRANSCRANIAL MAGNETIC STIMULATION?

The exact role of TMS for the treatment of psychiatric disorders remains uncertain, although it appears to offer benefits in mild to moderate cases, particularly for the short-term treatment of depression. As further research emerges, TMS is likely to find a more defined place in the overall management of psychiatric conditions. Standardized, evidence-based protocols may eventually become integrated into routine psychiatric care, providing effective alternatives for a range of disorders.

Advancements such as neuronavigation will enable precise targeting of brain regions, supporting personalized neuromodulation strategies. Quality control in both clinician training and clinical service delivery is likely to become standard practice. There will be an increasing emphasis on accounting for individual brain states when planning stimulation protocols. Controllable TMS (cTMS) is being explored to optimize waveform characteristics and stimulation parameters.

A stepped-care approach is likely to evolve, incorporating conventional rTMS or TBS, accelerated protocols, and deeper stimulation techniques. These may include double cone coils targeting regions such as dmPFC, dACC, OFC, as well as H-coils targeting insula and ACC. Stepwise treatment progression using different coils and stimulation protocols will be guided by research evidence, clinical need, availability, and cost considerations.

CONCLUSIONS

TMS in India has progressed significantly—from isolated institutional studies to widespread clinical use, including investigational applications and rapid research growth. A broad range of protocols is now being explored across psychiatric disorders, with a growing focus on continuation and maintenance treatments beyond acute interventions. As TMS becomes more accessible, key challenges lie in enhancing its affordability and acceptance as a standard treatment option. Technological advancements, particularly the development of high-quality indigenous devices, are expected to address these barriers soon. TMS clinicians must be equipped with the necessary knowledge and expertise to apply this modality judiciously and effectively in clinical practice. They should also educate patients about the risks of indiscriminate use, especially as TMS services proliferate, sometimes offering suboptimal care.
