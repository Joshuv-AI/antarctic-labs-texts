Effectiveness of exoskeleton training on turning-while-walking and balance control in subjects with incomplete spinal cord injury: protocol for a randomised clinical trial
Authors: Ming Chen, Chad Wing-nga Chan, Peter Yan Wang, William Wai-nam Tsang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIndividuals with incomplete spinal cord injury (iSCI) often experience impaired balance control and turning-while-walking ability, which increase the risk of falls and limit their community mobility. While overground exoskeleton training has shown promise in improving gait and balance, evidence from randomised controlled trials (RCTs) on ambulation of individuals with iSCI remains limited. This protocol outlines a cluster RCT investigating the effectiveness of overground exoskeleton-assisted gait training compared with conventional training and usual care in improving turning-while-walking performence, balance control, and functional mobility in individuals with iSCI.Methods and analysisThis multicentre, cluster RCT will compare 12-week interventions across three groups: (1) overground exoskeleton training group, (2) conventional training group and (3) usual care group. Participants with ambulatory iSCI will be recruited from three centres in Hong Kong SAR and mainland China. Primary outcomes include turning duration and number of steps during a 2-m turning-while-walking test. Secondary outcomes include static and dynamic balance, sensorimotor integration, gait performance, muscle strength, spasticity, quality of life, self-efficacy and fall incidence. Assessments will be conducted at baseline, mid-intervention, postintervention and at the 1-month follow-up.Ethics and disseminationThis study has received approval from the Research Ethics Committees of Hong Kong Metropolitan University, the Affiliated BenQ Hospital of Nanjing Medical University, and Changzhou Sunshine Rehabilitation Hospital. Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants before enrolment. Study findings will be shared through peer-reviewed publications, conference presentations and summaries available to participants upon request.Trial registration numberNCT06971510.

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) disrupts the complex neural pathways that transmit signals between the brain and the body, resulting in motor and sensory impairments.1 Approximately 70% of SCI cases are anatomically incomplete, retaining some neural connections and offering potential for partial motor recovery.2 Among individuals with SCI, restoration of walking ability is a primary rehabilitation goal.3 This includes the ability to perform turning-while-walking, a biomechanically complex and functionally critical skill for daily ambulation.4

Turning-while-walking is essential for navigating environmental constraints, yet it poses a greater challenge than linear walking, particularly for individuals with mobility impairments.4 In individuals with incomplete SCI (iSCI), turning is associated with a higher risk of falls, even greater than in older adults or those with neurological conditions such as Parkinson’s disease or peripheral neuropathy.5 Despite its clinical significance, turning-while-walking remains under-represented in SCI rehabilitation research, where the emphasis has largely been on straight-line gait training.6 7

Biomechanically, turning involves coordinated movements of the head, trunk, pelvis, and lower limbs.8 It requires asymmetrical limb movements, with the inner leg bearing more load to initiate and complete a turn.9 10 These dynamic demands necessitate intact sensorimotor integration, postural controlm, and muscular strength, functions often compromised in individuals with iSCI.1113 Psychological barriers, including fear of falling and low confidence, may further impair performance.14 15

Robotic exoskeletons have emerged as promising technologies for restoring functional ambulation in individuals with SCI.1619 Exoskeleton-assisted walking has been shown to improve weight-shifting control by increasing centre of mass excursion in both mediolateral and anteroposterior directions during overground walking20 . Such devices may also promote neuroplasticity and muscle activation. For instance, trunk muscle activity during exoskeleton-assisted walking in individuals with chronic SCI closely resembled electromyographic patterns observed in able-bodied individuals.21 Moreover, exoskeleton use can replicate the number and structure of muscle synergies observed in able-bodied gait, enhancing motor coordination in iSCI.20

Additionally, exoskeleton training has been associated with improved lower extremity muscle strength,22 reduced spasticity,23 and enhanced motor relearning24 in individuals with iSCI. In addition to physiological benefits, exoskeletons offer psychosocial advantages to iSCI survivors by enabling upright mobility, facilitating eye-level social interaction and contributing to improved self-esteem and emotional well-being.25

While most existing studies on exoskeleton training among iSCI survivors focus on general balance control and gait parameters, few have specifically examined task-oriented functions such as turning-while-walking. Furthermore, underlying mechanisms, including sensorimotor integration and psychosocial factors like fear of falling, remain underexplored in this context.

Therefore, this study explores the potential of exoskeleton training to improve turning-while-walking performance, balance control, gait function, muscle strength, spasticity, quality of life, self-efficacy, and fall incidence in people with iSCI. This study aims to fill a critical gap in the task-specific rehabilitation literature and inform evidence-based practice among the iSCI population.

Objectives

The primary objective of this randomised clinical trial (RCT) is to evaluate the effectiveness of overground exoskeleton-assisted gait training on turning-while-walking performance in individuals with iSCI.

Secondary objectives are to assess its effects on balance control, gait function, lower limb strength, spasticity, quality of life, self-efficacy, and fall incidence and to compare outcomes with conventional training and usual care, including short-term retention of training at 1-month follow-up.

Hypotheses

Primary hypothesis

Overground exoskeleton-assisted training will significantly improve turning-while-walking performance in individuals with iSCI.

Secondary hypotheses

Methods and analysis

Study design

This study is a three-arm cluster RCT, assigning participating centres to the exoskeleton training group (ETG), conventional training group (CTG), or usual care group (UCG) to minimise contamination between groups and reflect real-world clinical implementation. Participants in the ETG and CTG will complete 24 sessions of 1 hour over 12 weeks, while those in the UCG will maintain their usual activities. Outcomes will be assessed at baseline, mid-intervention (week 6), postintervention (week 12), and 1 month postintervention. A 1-month follow-up is chosen to assess short-term retention after supervised training ends. Few studies have evaluated the persistence of gains after overground exoskeleton-assisted gait training in iSCI, and no consensus exists on optimal follow-up.6 Evidence from locomotor and robotic gait training suggests walking and balance gains may decline within 3–4 weeks without continued therapy.7 26 Thus, 1 month is deemed appropriate to detect early retention or attenuation. Longer follow-ups are considered but excluded due to multicentre feasibility constraints, including higher attrition and variability in post-trial rehabilitation.

Study settings and participant recruitment

The interventions will be conducted at three centres: (1) Department of Physiotherapy, Hong Kong Metropolitan University; (2) Department of Rehabilitation Medicine, the Affiliated BenQ Hospital of Nanjing Medical University, and (3) Changzhou Sunshine Rehabilitation Hospital. Participants with iSCI will be recruited through hospitals, non-governmental organisations, and local patient self-help groups for individuals with iSCI. Recruitment is voluntary and independent from participants’ ongoing clinical care to avoid coercion. All participants will be informed that they may withdraw at any time without penalty, and refusal to participate will not affect their medical treatment.

Patient and public involvement

A small feasibility pilot was conducted before the trial to confirm the protocol’s safety and practicality. Informal participant feedback was collected, leading to minor adjustments, specifically, additional familiarisation time and enhanced therapist cueing to improve comfort and safety. Study results will be made available to participants upon request, via plain-language summaries provided by the participating centres. Findings will also be shared with relevant patient organisations and rehabilitation centres to support knowledge translation and clinical practice.

Randomisation

The three centres, Hong Kong Metropolitan University, Nanjing BenQ Hospital, and Changzhou Sunshine Hospital, will be randomised via a computer-generated allocation table into (1) ETG, (2) CTG, and (3) UCG, via a computer-generated allocation table by a research assistant who does not know the information about the trial, with participants allocated at a 1:1:1 ratio at each centre. To mitigate potential inter-rater error, all data will be collected by the same research team using standardised procedures and consistent explanations across all groups.

Sample size calculation

To date, no RCTs have specifically assessed the effects of overground exoskeleton-assisted gait training on turning-while-walking performance in individuals with iSCI; thus, effect sizes from prior studies were not directly applicable to the primary outcome of the present trial. Therefore, prior to launching the full trial, the team conducted a three-case pilot and calculated the sample size using G*Power (V.3.1.9.7). The pilot suggested large effects for turning-while-walking outcomes (turning duration: η²=0.89; number of steps per turn: η²=0.90). However, this pilot was exploratory and underpowered for precise effect size estimation, and its findings have not been published. To avoid inflating the required sample size based on this small pilot, a more conservative medium effect size of 0.31, aligned with recommendations for multicomponent rehabilitation interventions, was adopted.29 Assuming 80% power, a 0.05 alpha level and three groups, 32 participants with iSCI are required. Allowing for 30% attrition, at least 45 participants with iSCI will be recruited.

Intervention

Exoskeleton training

Participants will undergo overground exoskeleton training using the EksoNR exoskeleton (EksoNR Bionics, San Rafael, California, USA) for 12 weeks, two sessions per week, totalling 24 sessions. Each session lasts 60 minutes, including 10 min of warm-up, 45 minutes of active walking and 5 minutes of cool-down, excluding setup and donning/doffing. During the first three practice sessions, participants will familiarise themselves with the EksoNR device and gait pattern without a minimum step requirement. From the fourth session onwards, participants are required to walk a minimum of 300 steps per session. The training focuses on weight shifting, stepping, straight walking with symmetrical gait and turning with asymmetrical gait. Progression through training phases is based on achieving performance targets, including swing assist value, safe turning, and balance control. Participants who do not meet these targets within the designated sessions will remain in the current phase, receiving additional practice or support as needed. Advancement is determined by safety, participant readiness, and therapist judgement rather than strictly on session count. Turning-while-walking is emphasised in the exoskeleton group. Using the EksoNR, participants practised overground walking with programmed turning tasks that varied in angle, direction, and speed as a dedicated protocol component. All deviations from the planned progression will be documented. Online supplemental file 2 illustrates the progression plan for ETG training.28 30 All ETG training sessions will be conducted by a physiotherapist who is a certified researcher and trained in operating the EksoNR exoskeleton. Participants will be required to report the perceived training intensity using the Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE) scale (10-score RPE scale), which quantifies the perception of effort during exercise at the end of every training session.31

Conventional training

Participants in this group will receive 24 sessions of conventional exercise (two sessions per week for 12 weeks). Online supplemental file 3 illustrates the detailed conventional exercise content. Each session lasts 60 minutes, including 10 minutes of warm-up and 5 minutes of cool-down. The conventional group received standard therapy emphasising mobilisation and stretching exercises, balance control, gait practice, lower-limb/trunk strengthening, and a traditional turning-while-walking training module. The sessions will be supervised by another trained physiotherapist who will provide guidance and cues to the participants throughout.

Usual care group

Participants in this group continue with daily activities as normal for over 12 weeks. No new gait training, mobility therapy, or medications related to the condition under study will be initiated as part of the trial; however, any changes in daily activities or therapies during the study period will be recorded. Participants in this group come to the study sites for evaluations at baseline, 6 weeks, 12 weeks, and 1 month follow-up. Participants randomised to the CTG and UCG are offered EksoNR training sessions after the completion of all the assessments.

Outcome measures

Demographic data, including age, gender, height, weight, and history of iSCI, for all participants will be collected before the intervention. Outcome measures are selected to comprehensively capture changes in turning-while-walking performance, balance control, gait function, neuromuscular capacity, balance-related functional outcomes, and health-related quality of life in individuals with iSCI.

Primary outcome

Turning-while-walking test

The participants are instructed to walk a 2-m straight line, execute a 180° turn and subsequently return to the starting position as quickly and safely as possible. The test is repeated three times on each side, and one practice trial will be given before the test. Participants may use any required walking aids and wear their ankle–foot orthoses as needed during all subsequent tests. To maintain safety standards throughout the assessment, a research assistant closely monitors and accompanies the participants.

Two cameras are positioned in the sagittal and frontal planes to capture a full view of the participant during turns. A light flash on the ‘Go’ command provides a timing reference, and the movements of participants are recorded and analysed in both planes.

A turn is defined as starting and completing a 180° change in walking direction at a marked turn spot on the floor. The start of the turn is determined as the last foot contact prior to the initiation of the noticeable direction change. The end of the turn is determined when both feet are pointing towards the starting point.32 An independent assessor, not involved in randomisation or data collection, will analyse the videotaped turns using Kinovea (http://www.kinovea.org). Each trial will be time-coded to 0.01 s to determine turn duration. Temporospatial parameters, including the number of steps per turn and turn duration, will be extracted, and the averages of three trials on each side will be computed for analysis.

A test–retest intrarater reliability analysis was conducted in a subsample of nine participants with iSCI. All participants performed the turning-while-walking test on two separate occasions with a 2-week interval. The same assessor analysed the videotaped trials from both sessions using identical procedures. Intrarater reliability was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC, two-way mixed-effects model, absolute agreement). The turning outcomes demonstrated high intrarater reliability, with an ICC of 0.92 (95% CI 0.81 to 0.97, p<0.001) for the number of steps per turn and 0.98 (95% CI 0.94 to 0.99, p<0.001) for turn duration.

Secondary outcome

Balance involves multiple interacting domains, dynamic weight shifting, sensory integration, anticipatory/reactive postural control, and task-level function, so complementary performance-based measures are needed to capture domain-specific effects of exoskeleton-assisted gait training, especially during turning-while-walking. Functional outcomes (fall frequency and fear of falling) and health-related quality of life are included to assess real-world relevance, capturing behavioural and psychosocial dimensions distinct from objective balance capacity. This framework links mechanistic changes to functional performance and patient-centred significance.

Limits of stability (LOS) test

A laboratory-based dynamic balance assessment was performed using the Biodex Balance SD (Biodex Medical Systems, New York, USA). The LOS measures the ability of the participants to lean or shift their centre of gravity (COG) in different directions without losing balance.33 The test–retest reliability of the LOS test has an ICC of 0.72.34 Participants are asked to stand on the fully stable platform and position their feet to maintain balance over the platform’s centre. The coordinates are recorded to maintain foot placement for subsequent trials. The Biodex LOS test measures the time and accuracy with which participants transfer their estimated COG (from ground reaction force and height data), moving the cursor to intercept each of eight successive targets on the display screen. The targets are positioned at 45° intervals around a central target that represents the participant’s centre of pressure under static conditions. Each target is randomly highlighted, and participants are instructed to reach it by leaning and returning to the centre position before the next target is selected and displayed on the screen as quickly and accurately as possible. The test is completed when all eight targets have been reached. Target placement is preset by the manufacturer at 50% of the LOS, based on each participant’s height. The participant will complete practice trials prior to the test trials. Participants are allowed to hold their arms comfortably and adopt their desired strategy for hitting the targets, as well as to grasp the handles for less than 2 s to recover balance if needed; however, if participants are unable to complete the task without holding onto the handles, the test will be terminated. The time (seconds) and directional control (based on 100% being a straight line from the centre of pressure to the intended target) will be calculated.34 Three trials will be recorded, and the average values will be used in the statistical analysis.

Modified clinical test of sensory integration and balance (m-CTSIB)

The m-CTSIB is assessed using the Biodex Balance SD (Biodex Medical System). This test evaluates the effects of sensory integration on standing balance across four conditions: eyes open on a firm surface, eyes closed on a firm surface, eyes open on a foam surface, and eyes closed on a foam surface. Participants are positioned on a stable platform and instructed to maintain a stable vertical posture. Each condition lasts for 30 s, with a minimum 10 s rest period between trials as needed.35 Before the test, practice trials will be given to familiarise participants with the procedure. Balance performance is quantified using the Sway Index (SI), with higher values indicating greater instability. The SI will be recorded for each condition. Three trials will be conducted for each condition, and the averages across conditions will be used for data analysis.

Berg Balance Scale (BBS)

BBS is used to assess functional mobility and balance. The BBS is a clinical balance measure comprising 14 tasks that assess both static and dynamic balance.36 Each task is graded on a five-point ordinal scale ranging from 0 to 4, with 0 indicating the lowest level of function and four the highest, and then summed to a possible maximal score of 56. Scoring is based on the ability to meet certain time or distance requirements and to perform the items independently. Participants will wear the same orthotics and shoes as usual at all assessment points if needed. Walking assistive devices will not be allowed. For participants who are unable to stand unsupported, a score of zero will be given. A research team member will provide standby assistance when conducting the test. The BBS is both valid and reliable in the SCI population.37 38

10-Meter walk test (10MWT)

10MWT is used to assess the walking speed of participants.39 Participants will be asked to walk at their maximum walking speed, with their usual walking aid if needed, for 10 m. The initial and last two metres of the test will be allowed for acceleration and deceleration. The time required to walk the intermediate six metres will be recorded. The average of three trials will be used for data analysis.40 The 10MWT demonstrated excellent validity and reliability in the SCI population.41 42

The timed up-and-go test (TUGT)

TUGT involves various mobility activities common in daily life. When the test starts, the participants should stand up from a seated position, walk straight forward for 3 m, turn around, walk back to the starting position, and then sit down. The test will be repeated three times. The average time taken to complete the whole process will be used for data analysis.43 The TUGT demonstrated excellent validity and reliability in the SCI population.26 42

Muscle strength

Muscle strength of bilateral lower limbs, including hip flexors/extensors/abductors/adductors, knee flexors/extensors, and ankle dorsiflexors/plantar flexors, will be tested with a hand-held dynamometer according to the position and procedures described in Avers and Brown.44 The trunk muscle strength, including trunk flexors/extensors/rotators, will be tested and graded according to the method described in Kendall et al.45 Each of the above muscle strength tests will be measured three times. The average values will be used for data analysis.

Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS)

MAS is used to assess spasticity of the following muscles bilaterally: hip flexors/extensors/adductors, knee flexors and extensors, and ankle dorsiflexors and plantar flexors.46 The MAS scale was converted to discrete categorical scores (0, 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5).47

36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36)

The SF-36 will be administered to participants to assess their quality of life. Participants will complete the survey, which consists of 36 questions covering eight health dimensions: physical functioning, social functioning, role physical, role emotional, mental health, vitality, bodily pain, general health, and health transition perceptions. Responses will be scored on a standard 0–100 scale, where higher scores indicate better health-related quality of life.48 Data analysis will involve calculating overall total scores as well as individual scores for each dimension. The SF-36 has shown high validity and reliability in SCI populations, confirming its effectiveness in evaluating health outcomes.49

Falls Efficacy Scale International (FES-I)

The FES-I is used to assess participants’ concerns about falling during various daily activities. This scale comprises 16 items, each rated on a four-point Likert scale from 1 (not at all concerned) to 4 (very concerned), resulting in a total score ranging from 16 to 64, with higher scores indicating greater concern about falling.50 It has been validated for use in ambulatory individuals with SCI.38 In this study, the Chinese-translated FES-I Scale is employed, demonstrating high reliability with an ICC of 0.89 (95% CI 0.79 to 0.95) among older adults.51 The total score of the FES-I will be used as an outcome measure.

Fall history

Fall history will be assessed with a semistructured interview by asking the participants about the occurrence and frequency of falls during the assessment period.38 A fall is defined as any fall, including a slip or a trip, in which the participant came to rest on the floor, ground or a lower level.52 All falls are recorded through weekly self-reports by participants during the intervention and follow-up.

Physical Activity Scale for Persons with Physical Disabilities (PASIPD)

PASIPD is used to assess physical activity participation. The PASIPD is a 13-item questionnaire quantifying the level of physical activity participation.53 Participants will recall their leisure, household, and occupational activities over the past 7 days at baseline. A total score will be calculated for data analysis. The PASIPD was validated in people with SCI.54

Reporting of adverse events

Although rare, previous studies have reported adverse effects when employing an exoskeleton in rehabilitation for people with SCI. The adverse effects include falls, fractures, hypotension, autonomic dysreflexia, musculoskeletal injury, skin breakdown, and lower limb swelling.55 56 All these risks will be minimised through careful setting of inclusion and exclusion criteria, adequate training of research personnel, and close monitoring of the assessment and intervention processes. Although various measures will be taken to minimise the risk of adverse events throughout the study, any adverse events that might happen will be recorded.

Monitoring and feasibility

Given the low-risk nature of the interventions, a formal Data Monitoring Committee will not be established. The study team, including the principal investigator and certified researchers, will oversee ongoing monitoring of participant safety, protocol adherence, and data integrity. The principal investigator has purchased clinical trial insurance for all participants to cover potential adverse events. All exoskeleton sessions will be under supervision to ensure safety. Any adverse events, including falls, musculoskeletal pain, or other complications, will be recorded.

Adherence and retention

Participant attendance will be tracked, and missing more than 20% of scheduled sessions will be defined as non-compliance. Participants who cannot progress due to safety concerns, persistent non-compliance, or medical complications will be withdrawn, with all reasons documented. Retention strategies include baseline orientation, regular progress feedback, and practical support such as flexible scheduling or transportation coordination.

Early identification and support

Weekly review of adherence logs will be conducted to identify participants at risk of drop-out. Flexible scheduling, encouragement, and caregiver/family member involvement will be used to support continued participation.

Criteria for withdrawal

Participants unable to progress due to safety concerns, persistent non-compliance, or medical complications will be withdrawn. All withdrawals will be documented.

Trial conduct monitoring

Regular team meetings will review adherence to intervention and assessment protocols. No interim analyses or formal stopping guidelines are planned. However, the principal investigator may pause or terminate the trial if serious adverse events occur. All monitoring activities and potential conflicts of interest will be documented.

Data analyses

Data collection and storage

All outcome measures will be recorded using standardised assessment forms. Turning-while-walking trials will be video-recorded and analysed using Kinovea software. For assessing the balance control, gait performance, muscle strength, spasticity, quality of life and self-efficacy, the results will be documented electronically. All data will be stored on a secure, password-protected hard drive at Hong Kong Metropolitan University and the affiliated BenQ Hospital of Nanjing Medical University. Video files and electronic records will be deidentified with unique participant codes, and access will be restricted to authorised researchers. Data management procedures strictly adhere to the Hong Kong Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance (Cap. 486) and the People’s Republic of China Personal Information Protection Law. Physical documents, including consent forms, will be stored in locked cabinets within secured research facilities and retained for at least 5 years after study completion. Participants will not be identified in any publication or dissemination materials.

Data analysis

Data processing and analysis will be conducted with Statistical Package for the Social Sciences V.26 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, New York, USA). Descriptive statistics are used to characterise the demographic and impairment characteristics of participants. For participants unable to perform standing or walking balance tests due to safety or mobility limitations, outcomes will be recorded as zero to reflect their functional limitations. Participants in the three groups will be compared at baseline with one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous data and χ2 values for categorical data. Two-way mixed repeated measures ANOVA (3×4) will be conducted to investigate any significant group effect (between-group), time effect (within-group) and interaction effect (group×time) for each outcome measure. For any significant group effect, one-way ANOVA and follow-up post-hoc analyses will be used for further between-group comparison. For any significant time effect, repeated measures ANOVA with contrast analyses will be employed to test the within-group changes in each group. The significance level will be set at 0.05, with Bonferroni adjustments to be applied in all follow-up analyses. Any missing data will be treated by carrying the last observation forward with the intention-to-treat approach. Sensitivity analyses will assess the influence of missing or incomplete data.

Ethics and dissemination

This protocol (V.1, 11 August 2021) was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Hong Kong Metropolitan University (reference number: HE-OT2021/16), the Research Ethics Committee of the Affiliated BenQ Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (reference number: 2024-QL018, V.1, 20 June 2024), and the Ethics Committee of Changzhou Sunshine Rehabilitation Hospital (reference number: RT-2024–07).

All participants will be provided with written consent before enrolment (online supplemental file 4). The consent process includes a detailed explanation of the study’s aims, procedures, potential risks, and participant rights, delivered in plain language. Participants will have the opportunity to ask questions and will sign a written consent form only after confirming their complete understanding. Each participant will retain a copy of the signed consent form, and the original will be securely stored by the research team for at least 5 years. If a participant is unable to provide written consent due to physical limitations, a legally authorised representative, family member, or caregiver may provide consent on their behalf. To protect this vulnerable population, participation is entirely voluntary, and modest travel reimbursements are provided solely to offset transportation costs without constituting undue inducement. All study procedures comply with the Declaration of Helsinki (1964 and its subsequent amendments) and local regulations in both Hong Kong and Mainland China.

The findings of this study will be registered and updated on ClinicalTrials.gov following the publication of the primary study outcomes. Results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journal publications and presentations at relevant national and international conferences. Summaries will be shared with participating centres and made available to participants upon request. The dissemination strategy aims to inform clinicians, researchers, and policymakers to support evidence-based practices in the rehabilitation of individuals with iSCI.

Discussion

This multicentre, cluster RCT is designed to evaluate the effectiveness of overground exoskeleton-assisted gait training on turning-while-walking performance and balance control in individuals with iSCI. While previous studies have reported benefits of exoskeleton use for improving gait parameters in this population,6 21 57 limited evidence exists regarding its effects on more complex locomotor tasks, particularly on turning-while-walking, which is essential for community ambulation and fall prevention.4 58 59

The cluster design, assigning entire centres to intervention arms, reduces contamination risk between groups and enhances the external validity by reflecting real-world clinical settings. Including both CTG and UCG allows a comprehensive comparison across rehabilitation approaches. Furthermore, the use of follow-up assessments will help to evaluate the short-term retention of training effects after the intervention.

However, some limitations exist. Due to the nature of the intervention, blinding of participants and assessors is not feasible, potentially introducing performance and detection bias. To mitigate the impact of this limitation, objective outcome measures and standardised assessment protocols will be employed. Video recordings of turning-while-walking will be analysed by a researcher independent of randomisation and data collection. All other outcomes, including objective performance measurements and questionnaire data, will be recorded by a research assistant. All assessment instructions will be kept consistent across sessions and participants.

This study will provide critical evidence on the functional benefits and safety of exoskeleton-assisted training in the iSCI population. The findings are expected to inform clinical guidelines and contribute to advancing task-specific neurorehabilitation strategies aimed at improving mobility and quality of life.
