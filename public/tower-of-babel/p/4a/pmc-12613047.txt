Pan-cancer single-cell transcriptomic analysis reveals CD83 as a hallmark of tumor-associated neutrophils with senescent and pro-tumor properties
Authors: Yimin Wang, Yuan Meng, Kanchao Chen, Aihua Li, Jinqiang Yan, Jie Ding, Bingwu Yang, Linlin Tang, Bin Ding, Siyuan Chen, Song Chen, Fengyu Chen, Tong Chen, Yan Zhang, Qin Zheng, Chao Chen, Sizhou Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeutrophils, the most prevalent and efficient immune responders to pathogens, play vital roles in tumor progression and metastasis. Sustained neutrophil infiltration into tumor has been consistently linked to unfavorable clinical outcomes. Despite recent advances, critical knowledge gaps persist concerning the heterogeneity of neutrophils in the tumor microenvironment and throughout carcinogenesis and the reliable marker sets that define protumoral neutrophil subsets. Here, we constructed a comprehensive pan-cancer single-cell transcriptomic atlas of human neutrophils across 12 types of cancer, and revealed substantial heterogeneity among neutrophils. Notably, we identified CD83 as a hallmark of tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs) and found that CD83+ neutrophils are significantly enriched in cancerous tissues during carcinogenesis. Furthermore, CD83+ TANs represented a more senescent state, as confirmed by both bioinformatics analysis and the detection of SA-β-galactosidase activity. Additionally, CD83+ senescent TANs could regulate an immunosuppressive microenvironment by suppressing the activation and cytotoxicity of T cells; and their abundance exhibited significant association with poor prognosis and immunotherapy resistance. Finally, a therapeutic strategy, HDAC inhibitor romidepsin, was explored for its potential of specifically eliminating CD83+ senescent protumoral TANs. In summary, our study underscores the association between CD83+ senescent protumoral TANs and poorer clinical outcomes, offering novel perspectives on neutrophil-based therapeutic and prognostic approaches.Keywords: Single-cell RNA sequencing, Pan-cancer analysis, Neutrophils, CD83, Cellular senescence, Biomarker

Introduction

Neutrophils are major contributors to the bridging and activation of adaptive immunity, in addition to being key players in innate immunity. Upon infiltrating tumor sites, neutrophils exhibit complex and context-dependent roles within the tumor microenvironment (TME). Neutrophils possess dual and contradictory functions in tumor progression and metastasis, supporting their classification into anti-tumor (N1) or protumoral (N2) phenotype. In addition to directly killing tumor cells, N1 neutrophils can facilitate the recruitment and activation of CD8⁺ T cells through the release of cytokines and chemokines. In contrast, N2 neutrophils have protumoral capacity by producing arginase1, ROS, and iNOS [1], [2]. However, an increasing number of studies have found that neutrophils exhibit substantial heterogeneity in the bone marrow, blood, spleen, and other tissues [3], [4]. These results further imply that neutrophils in tumor conditions may exhibit a more complex population composition, rather than a simple binary classification.

Recently, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has yielded valuable information to deepen the understanding of the heterogeneity of intratumoral neutrophils and elucidate subpopulations with distinct functions. As evidenced by a pioneering study, neutrophils in liver cancer were associated with an inferior prognosis, with protumoral capacity observed in CCL4+ and PD-L1+ neutrophils [5]. Another landmark study demonstrated that neutrophils migrating into tumors undergo epigenetic, transcriptional and proteomic changes to converge into a terminally differentiated dcTRAIL-R1+ subpopulation. Consequently, tumor growth was accelerated by reprogrammed dcTRAIL-R1+ neutrophils through their pro-angiogenic effects [3]. Meanwhile, neutrophils could adopt 10 distinct transcriptional states associated with inflammation, angiogenesis, and antigen presentation across multiple cancer types. Among these, the HLA-DR+ neutrophils exhibit antigen-presentation features and potentiate neoantigen-specific T-cell responses. Augmenting or delivering this subset enhances responses to immunotherapy and fine-tunes TME [6]. Collectively, these findings underscore the utility of scRNA-seq in delineating the heterogeneity and functional specialization of tumor-infiltrating neutrophils. Moreover, it may also facilitate the identification of molecular markers for neutrophil subsets with significant regulatory roles in TME.

Accordingly, this study was conducted to establish a comprehensive human neutrophil atlas and explore the heterogeneity of TANs based on a wide range of published scRNA-seq data. More importantly, CD83+ senescent protumoral neutrophils with poor clinical outcomes were identified and a strategy for eliminating senescent neutrophils was developed. It is anticipated that findings in this study may serve as a valuable resource for understanding neutrophil properties in cancer, thereby shedding light on neutrophil-based tumor biomarkers and therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Patient sample collection

This study was conducted following the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of both West China Hospital of Sichuan University and Liaocheng People's Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from each patient. For solid tumor compartment, fresh tumor and adjacent normal (at least 2 cm from tumor tissues) tissues were collected within 1 h after the operation. Blood samples were collected before the surgery.

ScRNA-seq data quality control, processing, integration and annotation

First, re-quality control was performed on the obtained public single-cell data. Given the low transcript counts reported in neutrophils [7], we set the filtering criteria for the unfiltered raw count matrix to features > 100 and mt.pct < 25 % and the genes detected in more than 3 cells were retained. As for the filtered raw count matrix, it was used directly in this study. Then, potential doublets were identified and removed by employing the scDblFinder method. Furthermore, a total of 1514 genes were excluded (Supplementary Table 4) to minimize dissociation-induced expression artifacts and background noise [5]. For data integration, individual samples were merged into a single object. Due to partial differences in genome annotations across the datasets, we re-mapped gene identifiers on the latest version of HGNC gene symbols using the https://mygene.info API. Genome annotations were standardized as previously described [8]. After that, all cells were integrated according to sample ID by using harmony. Following unsupervised clustering, cell types were annotated using classical markers, and only neutrophils (defined by S100A8, CSF3R, FCGR3B, CXCR2, and G0S2) were retained. After obtaining high-quality neutrophil data, we computed the marker genes using the FindAllMarkers function of Seurat and further annotated the heterogeneous subpopulations of neutrophils based on these marker genes.

Inference of Cell Fate by Trajectory Analysis

Following downsampling of the dataset to 20,000 cells, Monocle 2 was applied to reconstruct the developmental trajectories of neutrophil subpopulations. The ordering genes in the trajectory analysis were determined according to each gene's expression (mean_expression > 0.05) and variance level (dispersion_empirical > 1 × dispersion_fit). To validate the trajectory, scTour was performed to infer the pseudotime of neutrophil subsets.

Enrichment analysis

GO term enrichment analyses were performed separately on DEGs across neutrophil subpopulations and on pseudotime-associated genes using clusterProfiler (v3.0.4) with default settings.

SCENIC analysis

The analysis of transcription factor (TF) regulatory networks at the single-cell level was conducted using the SCENIC R package with default settings.

Enrichment analysis of neutrophil clusters across trajectories and tissue types

To assess the enrichment of neutrophil clusters across tissues (PB, AL and tumor) and different differentiation pathways (Path I, Path II, Pre-branch), we calculated the Ro/e ratio. The Ro/e value was obtained by dividing the observed value by the expected value. The chisq.test() function in R was used to calculate both the expected and observed values.

Inference of metabolic fluxes

Potential metabolic differences among neutrophil clusters were assessed using flux balance analysis implemented in the METAFlux R package.

Flow cytometry

For senescence assay, SA-β-galactosidase activity was detected with the Cell Event Senescence Staining Kit (Thermofisher; Cat No. C10840) following the manufacturer's protocol. For the analysis of surface molecules, cells were incubated with appropriate antibodies at 4°C for 15 min, and then washed with PBS (400 g, 5 min). Samples were run on a BD FACSCanto™ for acquisition, and downstream analyses were conducted in FlowJo software. The following antibodies were used: CD45 PE-CY7 (QuantoBio 104511), CD66B PE (BD 561650), CD83 BV421 (BD 562630), HLA-DR FITC (Beckman Coulter IM1638U), CD16 FITC (BD 561308), 7-AAD (BD 559925), HLA-DR APC-CY7 (BD 335796), CD45 AmCyan (BD 339192), Cd62l FITC (BioLegend 104406), Ly6g PerCP-Cy5.5 (BioLegend 127653), Cd11b PE-Cy7 (BioLegend 101215), Cxcr2 APC (BioLegend 149311), and Cxcr4 Bv421 (BioLegend 146511).

Calculation of gene signature scores based on scRNA-seq data

Gene signature scores for individual cells were calculated based on scRNA-seq data by utilizing the AddModuleScore function. Genes subjected to calculation were presented in Supplementary Table 3.

SenCID

For each scRNA-seq dataset, both continuous and binarized senescence scores were obtained by the Python package SenCID (version 1.0.0) with default settings, using the gene expression count matrix as input.

Neutrophil isolation

Peripheral blood (5 ml) was collected from healthy donors into heparin anticoagulant tubes. Then the whole blood diluted with PBS was layered on the top of Ficoll Paque Plus (Cytiva) and centrifuged at 500 g for 20 min without brake. The subsequent neutrophil/erythrocyte suspension was inverted gently several times for adequate mixing with 5 % Dextran solution (Sigma-Aldrich). Then, blood was left untouched for 20–30 min before pipetting the supernatant into a new Falcon tube. Finally, cells were lysed with RBC lysis buffer (ACK, Gibco) for 5 min to completely remove red blood cells and washed.

Survival analysis

The prognostic value of TAN across different tumors was assessed by TCGA data of each cancer type. Kaplan-Meier survival analyses were performed with the survival R package and survminer R package. The cutoff value of continuous variables in the survival data was determined by the surv_cutpoint function of survminer R package. The labeled P-values were calculated by the log-rank test.

Multicolour Immunohistochemistry (mIHC)

Following collection, the harvested fresh human tissue specimens were fixed with a 95 % ethanol solution after sectioning to a thickness of 4 μm. Subsequently, the fixed sections were embedded in Tissue-Tek OTC (4583, Sakura Finetek) and processed according to the standard histological techniques. After hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, each section was evaluated by two or three experienced pathologists. For multiplex immunofluorescence staining, the following primary antibodies were utilized: (1) CD83 (1:1000, sc - 55535, Santa Cruz Biotechnology); (2) CD66b (1:1000, gtx19779, GeneTex). Subsequently, the specimens were stained using the AlphaTSA Multiplex IHC Kit (AXT34050011, AlphaX). After counterstaining the nuclei with DAPI (1:10000, C1005, Beyotime), multispectral images were scanned with the PerkinElmer Vectra 3 system.

Cell culture

Cancer cell lines (HCT116, HeLa, MCF7) were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultured in complete RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS, HyClone) and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C with 5 % CO2. Human PB neutrophils were purified as described above and co-cultured with the aforementioned cancer cell lines or with tumor-conditioned medium for 24 h. Subsequently, neutrophils were harvested and washed for flow cytometry. For the anti-PD-L1 blockade experiments, atezolizumab, an anti-human PD-L1 antibody (Selleck Chemicals, USA), was employed.

qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted with Trizol RNA Isolation kit (Invitrogen). cDNA was synthesized with the Reverse Transcription Reagents kit (TIANGEN). qPCR was run in triplicates on an AriaMx Real-Time PCR System (G8830A). CD83 expression was calculated by the comparative Ct method (2 −ΔΔCt) with YWHAZ as endogenous control. Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 5.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism. Data were presented as mean ± SD. Differences between two groups were assessed using Student’s t test, while comparisons among more than two groups were evaluated by one-way ANOVA.

Results

Construction of a human pan-cancer neutrophil landscape at single-cell resolution

To build an integrated single-cell transcriptomic atlas of tumor-infiltrating neutrophils across multiple cancer types, we acquired and integrated scRNA-seq data from 28 published papers (Supplementary Table 1). After initial rigorous quality-control filtering, we performed principal component analysis and graph-based cluster analysis on the filtered data. Based on neutrophil markers (S100A8, CSF3R, FCGR3B, CXCR2 and G0S2), the neutrophil cluster was extracted for further quality control and downstream analysis. Finally, we assembled a dataset of 71,225 neutrophils spanning 12 cancer types, comprising 378 samples from 262 individuals. Samples were collected from tumor tissue, adjacent non-tumor tissue, PB, normal tissue, and other tissues such as the peritoneum (Fig. 1A-B).

Analysis of neutrophil heterogeneity

Following batch-effect correction, dimensionality reduction, and clustering, neutrophils were resolved into 10 subclusters (Fig. 1C-D, Supplementary Table 2). Neu_c1 was identified as a relatively immature neutrophil subset, defined by high expression of secondary granule genes such as CAMP and LTF. Neu_c2 appeared to be a transcriptionally intermediate subset between Neu_c1 and Neu_c3. The marker genes of Neu_c3 were NFE2, CYP4F3 and NCF1. Furthermore, the expression levels of INKA2 and PGGHG were relatively higher in Neu_c4. Consistent with prior reports of neutrophil subsets with high expression of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs), we observed pronounced expression of ISGs in Neu_c5-c7. Notably, the ISG repertoires of these three subgroups were not identical. The ISGs predominantly expressed in Neu_c5 were IFIT1 and IFIT3, while Neu_c6 preferentially expressed GBP1 and GBP5. The currently reported studies on the functions of IFITs mainly focus on antiviral infection [9], [10], while GBPs, the breadth of GBPs’ antiviral activity is still being delineated, mainly play a crucial role in host defense against bacteria and parasites [11]. Alongside elevated expression of GBPs, Neu_c7 exhibited pronounced upregulation of MHC class II molecules, suggesting a potential capacity for antigen presentation. Neu_c8 expressed higher levels of SYAP1, PLAUR and PTGS2. Neu_c9 comprised an activated, inflammatory subset marked by upregulation of the neutrophil activation marker CD69 [12], [13] and high expression of inflammation-associated genes IL1B [14] and PDE4B [15], [16]. A distinct transcriptional program was also evident in Neu_c10, characterized by high expression of chemokine CCL3, pro-angiogenic factor VEGFA [17], and autophagy-associated gene MAP1LC3B [18].

To further investigate the characteristics of each neutrophil subpopulation, pathway enrichment analysis was conducted. The function of Neu_c1-c3 were predominantly associated with canonical anti-pathogen programs of neutrophils, including oxidative phosphorylation, ROS metabolic process, antibacterial responses, and phagocytosis. The differentially expressed genes of Neu_c5-c7 were mainly enriched in pathways related to antiviral responses, response to type I interferon and antigen processing and presentation. Neu_c8-c10 displayed high expression of genes involved in cellular response to IL-1, response to hypoxia, glycolysis and macroautophagy (Fig. 1E). We further assessed classical neutrophil-related functions across subgroups (Supplementary Table 3). In line with the GO analysis, Neu_c1-c3 represented subpopulations exhibiting canonical neutrophil functions (Fig. 1F).

As a short-lived cell type, cell death is also one of the key characteristics of neutrophils. There are various ways for neutrophils to die, each resulting in different consequences in health and disease. To date, apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, NETosis and ferroptosis have been described [19]. To examine whether forms of cell death varied across subgroups, we calculated scores for neutrophil clusters based on curated gene sets corresponding to defined cell-death types (Supplementary Table 3). We found that apoptosis-related genes were predominantly expressed in Neu_c2, while necroptosis- and pyroptosis-related genes were mainly expressed in the Neu_c6. Meanwhile, NETosis- and ferroptosis-related genes were primarily expressed in Neu_c7-c10 (Fig. 1F). These findings suggest that neutrophil heterogeneity extends to cell-death behavior, with subpopulations preferentially engaging discrete cell-death pathways. An increasing number of studies suggest that different forms of neutrophil death may play significant roles in specific physiological or pathological contexts [20], [21], which likely reflects context-dependent enrichment of neutrophil subpopulations with corresponding death biases. Nevertheless, further investigation is required.

SCENIC analysis revealed pronounced heterogeneity in transcription factor activity across neutrophil subgroups. Notably, RFX5 showed elevated activity in Neu_c7. Given that RFX5 is a key transcriptional regulator of MHC-II gene expression [22], this finding further supports an antigen-presenting phenotype for Neu_c7. In Neu_c10, HES4, MAFG, PPARG, and BHLHE40 exhibited higher activity. MAFG has been found to be associated with hypoxia [23]. PPARG, in addition to being regulated by HIF1A, can also enhance glucose uptake and regulate lipid metabolism [24]. BHLHE40 has been reported to regulate the expression of LDHA, while overexpression of LDHA further enhanced the glycolytic activity of dHL-60 cells (Fig. 1G, Supplemental Figure 1 A) [25]. Collectively, these findings suggest that neutrophil heterogeneity also extends to metabolism, with subgroups exhibiting distinct metabolic profiles. Accordingly, we applied the computational framework MetaFlux [26] to infer the potential metabolic fluxes of neutrophil subgroups. The inferred flux profiles indicate that Neu_c10 exhibits elevated glucose uptake and glycolysis, whereas Neu_c7-c10 show higher fatty acid synthesis flux scores relative to the other subgroups (Supplemental Figure 1B-D).

Collectively, scRNA-seq analyses reveal pronounced neutrophil heterogeneity, spanning differences in biological functions as well as variation in cell-death programs and energy metabolism.

Trajectory analysis revealed that neutrophils terminally differentiated into two distinct states

To delineate the hierarchy and developmental relationship between subsets, monocle2 [27] was used to construct a pseudotime map of neutrophil state trajectory. Given its high expression of immature neutrophil markers (CAMP and LTF), we designated Neu_c1 as the starting state [4], [28]. The findings unexpectedly revealed two distinct differentiation pathways of neutrophils (termed Path I and Path II) (Fig. 1H). Neu_c1–3 were enriched in Pre-branch (Fig. 1I). Path I-enriched clusters were Neu_c5–7, with Neu_c5 transitioning through Neu_c6 and eventually converting into Neu_c7 (Fig. 1I, Supplemental Figure 1E). Given Neu_c5 primarily expressed IFITs with antiviral infection functions, while Neu_c6 predominantly expressed GBPs with anti-bacteria/parasite functions, which suggested that the expression of antiviral-related ISG genes and antibacterial/parasite-related ISG genes might be built separately and sequentially during neutrophil development. Furthermore, since Neu_c6 highly expressed IFR1, a key regulator of MHC-II genes [29], this further demonstrated the developmental potential of Neu_c6 transitioning into Neu_c7 (Fig. 1D). In Path II, neutrophils ultimately differentiated into Neu_c10 (Supplemental Figure 1E). Consistently, Slingshot identified two discrete trajectories terminating in Neu_c7 and Neu_c10, respectively (Supplemental Figure 1 F, G). To reduce potential bias from manual definition of the root state, scTour was performed to estimate the pseudotime of each cluster, which operates without specifying a starting cell. Similarly, scTour identified Neu_c1 as the root state on the basis of its low pseudotime, with Neu_c7 and Neu_c10 representing more terminal subpopulations (Supplemental Figure 1H, I) [30]. Additionally, pathway enrichment analysis was performed on genes specifically expressed in the two differentiation trajectories. As a result, Path I was primarily associated with antiviral responses, responses to IFN-γ, and the regulation of innate immune responses; while Path II was mainly related to lipid storage, autophagy, regulation of angiogenesis, and glucose metabolism (Fig. 1J). Collectively, the above findings supported two functionally distinct developmental trajectories during the differentiation and maturation of neutrophils.

CD83 and HLA-DR discriminate tumor neutrophil states in solid tumors

Building on our observation of pronounced heterogeneity among neutrophil subpopulations, we investigated whether their distribution exhibits tissue-specific preference by comparing subpopulation compositions in PB, adjacent normal tissue, and tumor tissue. The results showed that Neu_c2-c6 subpopulations were predominantly distributed in PB, Neu_c8 and Neu_c9 were mainly enriched in adjacent tissues, while Neu_c7 and Neu_c10 expanded in tumor tissues (Fig. 2A-C, Supplemental Figure 2 A). Given their tumor-specific enrichment, we designate Neu_c7 and Neu_c10 as TANs. We next cross-referenced our neutrophil subpopulations with published scRNA-seq datasets. First, we compared our defined clusters with neutrophil subpopulations reported in PB and tumor tissues from cancer patients [7]. Neu_c1-c6 closely matched the PB-derived clusters, whereas Neu_c7-c10 primarily corresponded to intratumoral neutrophil populations, consistent with our findings on tissue-preference patterns for Neu_c1-c10. We then compared our clusters with neutrophils from healthy human PB, observing that hG5a-c aligned predominantly with Neu_c2-c3, Neu_c5-c6, and Neu_c4, respectively (Supplemental Figure 2B) [4].

To determine whether specific surface markers can identify TANs as a whole and further discriminate Neu_c7 from Neu_c10 within TANs, we conducted a detailed screening of the differentially expressed genes in Neu_c7 and Neu_c10. Consequently, CD83 and HLA-DRA were pronouncedly recognized. CD83 was specifically expressed in TANs (Neu_c7, Neu_c10), supporting its use as a hallmark of TANs. HLA-DRA further discriminated Neu_c7 from Neu_c10 within the TAN population (Fig. 2D). Observation of mIHC sections from cervical and colorectal cancer tissues confirmed the presence of CD83+ TANs (Fig. 2E). To corroborate the presence of Neu_c7 and Neu_c10, we performed flow cytometry to assess the expression of CD83 and HLA-DR on tumor-infiltrating neutrophils from breast, colorectal, and cervical cancers. Given that PB neutrophils from cancer patients show undetectable CD83 and HLA-DR (Fig. 2F), matched PB neutrophils from the same patients were used as negative controls. Indeed, we found that, compared to PB neutrophils from cancer patients, neutrophils within tumor tissues of breast cancer, colorectal cancer, and cervical cancer exhibited higher levels of CD83 expression. Among CD83+ neutrophils, both HLA-DR+ and HLA-DR- subpopulations were detected (Fig. 2G). Additionally, tumor-infiltrating neutrophils in CRC and CESC showed broadly elevated CD83 expression, yielding a high proportion of CD83+ neutrophils. Considering that Neu_c7 and Neu_c10 constitute the predominant CD83+ subsets in our single-cell data, the increased CD83 levels are plausibly driven by intratumoral enrichment of these subsets. This pattern aligns with the enrichment of Neu_c7 and Neu_c10 subsets observed in certain neutrophil scRNA-seq datasets (Supplemental Figure 2 C).

Given that other immune cells, such as dendritic cells (DCs) and macrophages, are also capable of expressing CD83 [31], an additional analysis was performed to determine whether CD83 expression within tumor tissues is specific to neutrophils. For this purpose, pan-cancer datasets from previously published studies on DCs and macrophages were analyzed [32], [33]. It was found that CD83 expression was detected in macrophages and DCs within tumor tissues. However, it should be noted that CD83 expression was also observed in macrophages and DCs from PB (Supplemental Figure 2D). This pattern differs from that seen in neutrophils, as CD83+ neutrophils were detected in tumor tissues but not in PB from both healthy donors and cancer patients (Fig. 2F, G).

Altogether, although CD83 expression within tumor tissues is not specific to neutrophils, CD83 could still serve as a hallmark to distinguish TANs from other tumor-infiltrating neutrophils. Furthermore, TANs could be further subdivided into HLA-DR⁺ and HLA-DR⁻ subpopulations.

Neutrophil heterogeneity during carcinogenesis

Building upon our prior characterization of neutrophil heterogeneity across tissues from cancer patients, we next sought to delineate how this heterogeneity evolves across discrete stages of carcinogenesis. As the datasets analyzed in this study also included samples from normal cervical tissues, high-grade precancerous cervical lesions, and cervical cancer tissues, we conducted a preliminary analysis of stage-dependent changes in neutrophil clusters across carcinogenesis. Comparative analysis of neutrophil composition across normal cervical tissue, high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (HSIL), and tumor tissue revealed that CD83⁺ neutrophils (Neu_c7 and Neu_c10) were preferentially enriched in tumor tissue (Fig. 3A-C). This further confirmed that CD83+ neutrophils represented tumor-associated neutrophils. Therefore, we sought to further investigate factors driving the preferential expansion of CD83+ neutrophils in tumor tissues during carcinogenesis. GM-CSF levels have been reported to rise in parallel with disease progression, from NILM/LSIL to HSIL and cervical cancer [34]. This raises the possibility that GM-CSF contributes to the generation of CD83⁺ neutrophils. To explore this, we reanalyzed published data investigating the regulation of GM-CSF on neutrophil gene expression [35]. Our analysis revealed that, compared to control group, GM-CSF significantly induced the expression of CD83 in neutrophils over time (Fig. 3D). Moreover, by quantifying the dynamic changes in CD83 mRNA in neutrophils cultured for 0, 6, 12, 18, and 24 h with or without GM-CSF stimulation, we observed a time-dependent increase that was augmented by GM-CSF (Fig. 3E-F). To further validate these findings at the protein level, we performed flow cytometry. In unstimulated controls, the proportion of CD83⁺ neutrophils rose gradually over time and then approached a plateau, whereas GM-CSF elicited a more pronounced and sustained increase across all time points (Fig. 3G-H). These results are consistent with our re-analysis of published datasets shown in Fig. 3D. These findings indicated that GM-CSF was able to promote generation of CD83+ neutrophils, highlighting the upregulation of GM-CSF during cervical carcinogenesis as a potential key factor driving the production of CD83+ neutrophils. This provided a foundational basis for further investigation into the potential role of CD83+ neutrophils in the process of cervical cancer development.

CD83⁺ TANs represent a more senescent subpopulation

Based on pseudotime analysis of CD83+ TANs, we found that CD83+ neutrophils represented a more terminal state (Supplemental Figure 1 F). We therefore hypothesized that CD83⁺ neutrophils constituted a senescent subpopulation and next assessed the state of tumor-infiltrating neutrophils. Firstly, the expression of CXCR4 (higher expression in aged neutrophils), CXCR2 and SELL (lower expression in aged neutrophils) was examined [4], [36]. Consequently, the expression of CXCR4 was significantly upregulated in Neu_c7 and Neu_c10 (CD83+ TANs), while CXCR2 and SELL were markedly downregulated (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, we assessed the senescence levels of different neutrophil subpopulations by leveraging a published gene set for predicting cellular senescence [37], and found that CD83+ (Neu_c7, Neu_c10) TANs exhibited higher senescence scores (Fig. 4B). Similarly, CD83⁺ neutrophils (Neu_c7, Neu_c10) exhibited higher senescence scores calculated by SenCID (Fig. 4C) [38]. Consistent with their senescent state, CD83⁺ neutrophils (Neu_c7, Neu_c10) displayed upregulation of senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) genes (Fig. 4D).

In addition to bioinformatic assessment of senescence in CD83⁺ TANs from solid tumors, we measured senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) activity using flow cytometry. To simulate the state of neutrophils within solid tumors, neutrophils were co-cultured with cervical, breast, and colorectal cancer cell lines for 24 h, after which CD83 expression on neutrophils was quantified. Results showed that, compared to neutrophils cultured alone, neutrophils co-cultured with tumor cells significantly upregulated CD83 and CD83+ neutrophils exhibited higher SA-β-Gal activity (Fig. 4E-F). Additionally, the whole neutrophils also exhibited higher SA-β-Gal activity under tumor stimulation (Supplemental Figure 3A-B). These findings suggest that tumor cells can induce significant CD83 upregulation in neutrophils and CD83+ neutrophils displayed more pronounced senescent characteristics. Furthermore, we examined CD83 expression in neutrophils co-cultured with tumor-conditioned medium (TCM) and found that TCM also significantly induced more surface CD83, although the effect was notably weaker than that induced by direct contact with tumor cells (Fig. 4E, Supplemental Figure 3 A). It could be interpreted that tumor cells could induce the generation of CD83+ senescent neutrophils through both paracrine signaling and direct cell-cell interactions. We also compared CD83 expression levels in neutrophils from PB, peritumoral tissue, and tumor tissues of patients with cervical, breast, and colorectal cancers. We observed a progressive elevation of CD83 in neutrophils from PB to peritumoral and ultimately intratumoral tissue, reflecting the temporal build-up of senescent characteristics during neutrophil trafficking into TME (Fig. 4G).

In conclusion, we emphasized that CD83+ neutrophils exhibit more pronounced senescent features.

Senescent state of tumor-derived neutrophils also conserved in mouse

To determine whether the senescent phenotype of intratumoral neutrophils is conserved in murine models, we performed senescence-related scoring on single-cell sequencing data of mouse neutrophils from BM, PB and tumor [37], and found that intratumoral neutrophils indeed exhibited higher senescence scores (Fig. 5A-B). However, we did not observe the expression of Trem2, which has been suggested as a marker of senescent neutrophils in prostate cancer (Fig. 5C) [39]. Additionally, flow cytometry analysis of Cxcr4, Cxcr2, and CD62l expression levels in neutrophils from PB and tumors in 4T1 and MFC mouse models revealed that, compared to PB neutrophils, intratumoral neutrophils exhibited higher Cxcr4 and lower Cxcr2 and CD62l levels, indicating a more pronounced aged state (Fig. 5D-E).

The association of TANs with unfavorable prognosis, immunotherapy resistance and higher disease risk

Previous studies have demonstrated that inflammation is closely associated with both patient prognosis and response to immunotherapy [40], [41], [42]. As key inflammatory cells, sustained neutrophil accumulation within tumors has been consistently associated with unfavorable clinical outcomes [43]. In light of the high expression of pro-inflammatory genes and the preferential enrichment within tumor tissues, CD83+ TANs (Neu_c7 and Neu_c10) may also play an important role in disease progression and therapeutic response. To verify this, we next evaluated their prognostic significance across multiple cancer types. Our findings revealed that patients with higher levels of Neu_c7 and Neu_c10 exhibited worse survival outcomes across multiple cancer types, indicating a significant association between TANs and poor prognosis in cancer patients (Fig. 6A). We further investigated the relationship between CD83+ TANs and efficacy of immunotherapy [44]. Under neoadjuvant immunotherapy, the proportions of senescent TANs (Neu_c7 and Neu_c10) were significantly elevated in NMPR patients compared to MPR patients, with the most pronounced increase in Neu_c7 (Fig. 6B-D). Additionally, CD274 expression was markedly elevated in Neu_c7 of NMPR patients, suggesting that Neu_c7 may contribute to immunotherapy resistance via CD274 (Fig. 6E). Moreover, in NMPR samples, we observed higher expression of CXCR4, CD83, and senescence-associated gene CDKN1A, indicating that neutrophils in NMPR samples displayed a more senescent state compared to treatment-naive and MPR samples (Fig. 6F). In a urothelial carcinoma cohort treated with PD-L1 blockade [45], Neu_c7 scores showed no significant difference between CR/PR and SD/PD groups, whereas Neu_c10 scores were significantly elevated in the SD/PD group (Fig. 6G). These findings suggest that CD83⁺ senescent neutrophils may contribute to immunotherapy resistance.

CD83+ senescent TANs lead to immunosuppressive microenvironment

Since CD83+ senescent TANs (Neu_c7 and Neu_c10) were associated with poor prognosis in cancer patients, it suggested the protumoral effects of CD83+ senescent TANs. This motivated us to explore the mechanisms underlying the protumoral functions of CD83+ TANs. Moreover, as senescent CD83+ TANs can be further divided into two distinct subsets, Neu_c7 and Neu_c10, we sought to determine whether their protumoral mechanisms also differed. To investigate this, we analyzed the differential genes identified along two neutrophil differentiation trajectories derived from pseudotime analysis. During the development toward Neu_c7, expression level of CD274 gradually increased. Cell-cell communication analysis further predicted that Neu_c7 could interact with CD8+ T cells via CD274 - PDCD1 and LGALS9 - HAVCR2 pathways (Fig. 7A-B). This suggested that the primary protumoral mechanism of Neu_c7 might involve suppressing the cytotoxic function of CD8+ T cells through CD274 - PDCD1 and LGALS9 - HAVCR2 pathways, thereby promoting tumor progression. In contrast, during development toward Neu_c10, the expression of VEGFA increased, suggesting a critical role in promoting intratumoral angiogenesis and tumor progression. Additionally, Neu_c10 exhibited higher expression of BHLHE40 and LDHA (Fig. 7A). Previous studies have shown that BHLHE40 drove the protumoral phenotype of neutrophils in pancreatic cancer [25]. To further confirm the protumoral effect of CD83+ senescent TANs, the expression level of PD-L1 in CD83+ and CD83- neutrophils was assessed. Accordingly, CD83+ neutrophils exhibited higher PD-L1 expression than CD83- neutrophils (Fig. 7C). Moreover, considering the close interaction between CD83+ senescent TANs and T cells (Fig. 7B), we co-cultured CD83+ TANs with T cells (Fig. 7D). CD83+ TANs exerted a suppressive effect on T cell activation, and led to a significant reduction in T cell cytotoxicity against tumor cells (Fig. 7E-G).

It has been shown that the enrichment of CD83⁺ neutrophils is associated with resistance to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 immunotherapy (Fig. 6B-G). To further substantiate this observation, we first induced the generation of CD83⁺ neutrophils using TCM and subsequently co-cultured these induced neutrophils with T cells isolated from healthy donors. We found that TCM-induced neutrophils exhibited a markedly stronger suppressive effect on the activation of both CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells. Although administration of anti-PD-L1 blocking antibody partially alleviated this suppression, the activation of T cells was not fully restored (Fig. 7H-J). These findings suggest that, in addition to the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway, other immunosuppressive mechanisms, such as LGALS9-HAVCR2 axis (Fig. 7B), may also contribute to T-cell inhibition. This observation may, at least in part, explain the association between CD83⁺ neutrophil enrichment and resistance to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 immunotherapy.

In summary, CD83+ senescent TANs exhibited stronger protumoral activity, characterized by higher expression of immunosuppressive molecules and suppression of T cell function.

Selective elimination of CD83+ senescent TANs by HDAC inhibitor

To assess whether selective clearance of senescent CD83+ protumoral TANs could suppress tumor progression to some extent, we selected romidepsin (histone deacetylase [HDAC] inhibitors) that has been reported to clear senescent neutrophils in a murine prostate cancer model [39]. Given that romidepsin itself exhibits anti-tumor activity, we co-cultured TCM, which was also capable of expanding CD83+ senescent neutrophils, with freshly isolated PB neutrophils to evaluate whether romidepsin could effectively kill CD83+ senescent neutrophils (Fig. 8A). Consequently, exposure to TCM significantly upregulated CD83 in neutrophils. However, provision of romidepsin significantly reduced the CD83 levels in neutrophils. In addition, neutrophil CD83 expression was significantly lower in PBN with HDACi group than in PBN group (Fig. 8B). Elevated CD83 levels observed in PBN group may be attributable to the spontaneous senescence of neutrophils. Given that other immune cell types also express CD83 [31], it remains unclear whether romidepsin exerts effects on other CD83⁺ immune cells. To investigate whether romidepsin specifically targets CD83⁺ neutrophils, PB-derived immune cells were treated with TCM. Increased CD83 expression was observed in other immune cells, excluding neutrophils and T cells, following TCM stimulation. However, administration of romidepsin under these conditions did not alter CD83 expression levels in these immune cells (Supplemental Figure 4 A, B). In contrast, CD83⁺ neutrophils appeared to be more sensitive to the pro-apoptotic effects induced by romidepsin, a phenomenon that has been reported in various tumor contexts [46], [47].

Collectively, these results indicated that romidepsin was able to selectively eliminate CD83+ senescent neutrophils (Fig. 8B).

Discussion

Pan-cancer analysis, owing to its unique advantages, transcends the limitations of single-cancer studies and enables the exploration of both shared molecular mechanisms and cancer type-specific features at a systems-wide level. This integrative approach has emerged as a key strategy in contemporary cancer research [48], [49], [50], [51], [52]. scRNA-seq technology, as a powerful tool for investigating the heterogeneity of TME, has been widely applied to deepen the understanding of tumorigenesis and to facilitate the development of novel therapeutic strategies. In this study, we comprehensively profiled human neutrophil populations from PB, tumor and adjacent normal tissues by adopting an unbiased, systematic approach. In doing so, we delineated 10 neutrophil populations with distinct molecular signatures, which collectively displayed pronounced heterogeneity in phenotype, function, cell-death programs, and metabolism. Neutrophil differentiation and maturation trajectories facilitated to explore dynamic transition between neutrophil subpopulations, which promoted researchers to know the role of different subpopulations in tumor initiation and development. Trajectory analysis revealed that neutrophils differentiated into two distinct pathways. One pathway started from Neu_c5 to Neu_c6 and ended with Neu_c7. Another pathway terminally differentiated into Neu_c10. Until now, many studies explored neutrophil developmental trajectories and terminals under tumor condition and found that neutrophils had one developmental terminal [3], [8], [25]. For example, Ng LG’s work demonstrated that neutrophils in tumors undergo significant changes to converge into a terminally differentiated dcTRAIL-R1+ state. The possible reason for different results was that terminally differentiated neutrophils were senescent neutrophils with high expression of SASP genes, which might mislead the identity of neutrophil subpopulations.

For tissue specificity, PB preferentially harbored six neutrophil subsets (Neu_c1-c6), tumor-adjacent tissue contributed Neu_c8 and Neu_c9, and tumor tissue was selectively enriched for Neu_c7 and Neu_c10. Among these subsets, Neu_c5, Neu_c6, Neu_c7 subpopulations expressed a set of ISGs, which implied these three subpopulations might play essential roles in infection and tumor immunity. Although ISG-high neutrophils have been reported in several studies, our analysis further revealed clear heterogeneity within this population, with Neu_c5 showing preferential expression of IFITs involved in antiviral defense, whereas Neu_c6 predominantly expressed GBPs. More interestingly, except for GBPs, Neu_c7 highly expressed MHC-II, which suggested this subpopulation might possess antigen-presenting function. It was reported that antigen-presenting neutrophils induced CD4+ T cells to exacerbate inflammation in sepsis and could boost immunotherapy and fine-tune TME. On the contrary, we found that Neu_c7 was linked to poor prognosis and resistance to immunotherapy. The possible reason for different results was that Neu_c7 also highly expressed PD-L1, a well-known immune checkpoint. A lot of literature showed that PD-L1 expressing neutrophils have protumoral capacity [5], [53], [54]. Furthermore, many groups have found the existence of MHC-II and PD-L1 double positive neutrophils [55], [56], [57], [58] and even some studies reported it was able to inhibit T cells [59]. Negative feedback mechanism inhibiting overactive T cells might explain this. In addition, it has been reported that CD83 expressed on the surface of mature DCs promotes the expression of MHC class II and CD86 [60]. Furthermore, by inhibiting MARCH-8 in cTECs, CD83 ensures MHC-II expression and antigen presentation to developing CD4⁺ T cells, which is a prerequisite for proper positive selection [61]. Collectively, these findings highlight the critical relationship between CD83 and MHC-II. As both CD83 and MHC-II are expressed in Neu_c7, it is plausible that CD83 may also play an important role in maintaining MHC-II expression in neutrophils. However, given that this subset also expresses PD-L1, with potential immunosuppressive activity, it further suggests that CD83 may be particularly important in sustaining a subset of neutrophils that preferentially suppress CD4⁺ T cells. Nevertheless, these possibilities warrant further investigation.

Besides Neu_c7, Neu_c10 was also a terminally differentiated subpopulation enriched in tumor. They both displayed an aged phenotype, high expression of CXCR4 and low expression of CXCR2 and SELL [4]. It was demonstrated that CXCR4hiCD62Llo aged neutrophils robustly promoted tumor migration and supported metastasis [36], [62]. However, senescent neutrophils in tumors could not be precisely identified using these classical aging markers, as the CXCR4hi aged G5c subpopulation has been shown to exist in PB from both human and mouse under homeostasis. In another study, TREM2 has been described as a marker of senescent neutrophils in murine prostate cancer [39]; however, intratumoral TREM2⁺ neutrophils were not observed in several tumor-bearing mouse models assessed in this study. It was supposed that senescent TREM2+ mouse neutrophils were limited to prostate cancer. Therefore, it is necessary to find more precise markers to identify human senescent neutrophils in tumors.

Fortunately, a specific protumoral CD83+ neutrophil subpopulation was identified in our study, which was correlated with poor prognosis and resistance to immunotherapy. CD83, a member of the immunoglobulin (Ig) superfamily, exists in both membrane-bound and soluble forms. The membrane-bound form is expressed on various activated immune cells, including B and T cells, monocytes, dendritic cells, and neutrophils [31], [63]. PMN from healthy donors showed that while IFN-γ and GM-CSF induced expression of CD83, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α selectively induced de novo synthesis of CD83 [64]. Similarly, Salcher et al. discovered higher expression of CD83 in neutrophils from NSCLC tumors than adjacent normal tissues, but the expression of CD83 in neutrophils from other cancer types and the characteristics of CD83+ neutrophils were not explored [8]. Here, we profiled the dynamics of CD83 expression in neutrophils from peripheral blood to peritumoral tissue and into the tumor core in patients with CESC, CRC, and BRCA. We observed a progressive upregulation of CD83 as neutrophils infiltrated the tumor, a pattern that was largely consistent across cancer types. Notably, the stepwise increase from blood to peritumoral and intratumoral compartments was more pronounced in CRC than in CESC or BRCA, suggesting a shift in the composition of neutrophils. The observed divergence may be driven by the heterogeneity between different cancer types. In addition, disease stage may also be an important contributor, consistent with our findings on neutrophil heterogeneity during carcinogenesis (Fig. 3). These observations underscore the value of systematically comparing neutrophil subset heterogeneity across tumor types and along the carcinogenic trajectory to refine our understanding of neutrophil biology and to inform neutrophil-targeted therapies. Further characterization of CD83+ neutrophils revealed a pronounced senescence-associated phenotype of this subset. Cellular senescence is a state characterized by growth arrest, structural and metabolic alterations, and persistent DNA damage responses [65]. As our result, it was shown that senescent neutrophils with increased glucose usage contributed to tumor support and radiotherapy resistance [66]. Importantly, Alimonti’s group discovered that the HDAC inhibitor romidepsin was able to eliminate senescent neutrophils in murine tumor models, leading to improved therapeutic efficacy [38]. As expected, CD83⁺ senescent neutrophils were selectively depleted by romidepsin. Therefore, eliminating senescent neutrophils might be an efficient strategy to inhibit tumor growth. To further validate these findings, in vivo studies evaluating the effects of romidepsin on senescent neutrophils and on tumor progression driven by senescent neutrophils are warranted.

Although CD83⁺ TANs were found to exhibit senescent features (Fig. 4B, C, F), it remains unclear whether CD83 functions as a driver or merely serves as a marker of neutrophil senescence. At present, studies directly addressing the causal relationship between CD83 and cellular senescence are largely lacking. Nevertheless, activation of NF-κB signaling pathway in senescent cells has been reported to induce the expression of multiple pro-inflammatory genes [67]. Notably, CD83 expression has also been shown to be transcriptionally triggered by NF-κB activation [68]. Conversely, several studies have suggested that CD83 may exert anti-inflammatory effects during inflammatory responses [69], [70]. Taken together, it is conceivable that signaling pathways activated during cellular senescence may simultaneously drive inflammatory processes and upregulate CD83 expression, whereas CD83 might act as a negative feedback regulator to mitigate senescence-associated inflammation. However, the precise causal relationship between CD83 and senescent neutrophils, as well as the potential biological functions of neutrophil-derived CD83, remain to be further elucidated.

In summary, a comprehensive human neutrophil atlas was constructed and the heterogeneity of neutrophils across cancer types, tissues and carcinogenesis was explored in this study. Most importantly, CD83 was identified as a marker of senescent protumoral neutrophils partially induced by GM-CSF and a strategy for eliminating senescent neutrophils was developed (Fig. 8C). These findings offer a valuable resource for elucidating neutrophil characteristics in cancer and inform potential neutrophil-targeted immunotherapies.

Limitations of the study

There are several limitations and outstanding questions remaining from this study. Our analyses were primarily based on scRNA-seq integrated with in vitro experimental validation. The scRNA-seq analysis relied on the integration of 28 publicly available datasets, which varied in library preparation protocols and tumor sample processing. These differences inevitably introduced batch effects among datasets. Although standard and rigorous quality control procedures and batch-effect correction were applied, this process may also reduce or obscure true biological differences across datasets. Consequently, certain meaningful heterogeneity among neutrophils derived from different tissues or cancer types might be masked, potentially preventing the identification of unique neutrophil subpopulations with important biological functions. Therefore, further studies are warranted to more comprehensively define novel neutrophil subsets.

In addition, while valuable insights into the cellular heterogeneity of TME have been provided by scRNA-seq, the spatial architecture and distribution of cells within tumor tissues are also recognized as critical determinants of tumor progression. Through spatial transcriptomics (ST), the spatial context of gene expression can be preserved within intact tissue sections, allowing spatial organization and cell-cell interactions to be investigated. With the continuous development of ST analytical algorithms and their integration with other omics technologies [71], [72], [73], [74], [75], including scRNA-seq, ATAC-seq, and proteomics, a more comprehensive understanding of the spatial and functional characteristics of TANs can be achieved.

Moreover, although the immunosuppressive role of CD83⁺ TANs, their contribution to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 immunotherapy resistance, and the feasibility of strategies targeting senescent CD83⁺ TANs have been partially demonstrated through in vitro experiments, further in vivo validation is warranted to substantiate these findings and confirm their translational potential.

Conclusion

Our findings reveal the heterogeneity of tumor-infiltrating neutrophils with distinct functions and identify CD83+ senescent neutrophils as important contributors in tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment. Furthermore, these results also underscore the potential of CD83+ senescent neutrophils to be a biomarker for prognosis and target for therapeutic intervention.
