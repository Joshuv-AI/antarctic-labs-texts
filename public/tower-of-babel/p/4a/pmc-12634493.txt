Reconsidering Patellofemoral Outcomes in Medially Stabilized Total Knee Arthroplasty: The Role of Implant Design and Rotational Strategy in Kinematic Alignment
Authors: Chia-Hao Hsu
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

I read with great interest the article by King et al., which compared kinematic alignment (KA) and mechanical alignment in patellar tilt and patient-reported outcomes using the GMK Sphere medially stabilized total knee arthroplasty (MS-TKA) system [1]. The finding that KA increased patellar tilt without worsening 1-year Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index pain or function scores adds to the ongoing discussion on alignment philosophy in MS-TKA. I respectfully offer 3 considerations to refine the interpretation and broaden the relevance of these findings.

The use of a single prosthesis limits applicability across implant systems. The evaluated implant has patellofemoral features likely to influence tracking—a lateralized trochlear groove, flattened medial trochlear wall, and medialized dome patellar component—which may facilitate more physiological movement and reduce retinacular tension. These features might mitigate KA-induced tilt, potentially masking complications that could occur with other MS-TKA designs lacking them. As prosthesis design can substantially alter patellofemoral mechanics [2], future studies comparing KA across multiple MS-TKA and conventional TKA systems are warranted.

MS-TKA promotes femoral external rotation during flexion, as the lateral condyle translates posteriorly while the medial compartment remains constrained. This may reduce the Q angle in flexion, potentially improving patellar tracking. Since KA and MS-TKA both aim to replicate native kinematics, combining them might offer complementary effects, as suggested by reports that integrating KA with medial stabilization can improve function and more closely replicate native motion [3]. MS-TKA also shows superior mid-flexion anteroposterior stability compared with posterior-stabilized designs, correlating with better outcomes [4], which could contribute to consistent patellar behavior throughout motion. However, benefits likely depend on compatibility between KA-induced axes and trochlear geometry, and may vary with less anatomical designs.

The role of femoral component rotational alignment warrants further consideration. The authors indicate the use of a calipered KA technique referencing Howell et al. [5], which does not prescribe fixed external rotation but instead seeks to match the prearthritic joint lines. This individualized approach may deviate substantially from the conventional 3° external rotation used in mechanical alignment protocols. In the context of MS-TKA—where native femoral rollback is preserved and implant design might already promote external rotation—the optimal rotational strategy remains to be determined. Excessive external rotation could paradoxically exacerbate patellar tilt, especially in lateralized trochlear designs, whereas under-rotation might compromise femoral balancing. Future KA studies should explicitly report femoral component rotation angles and assess whether adjusted rotation targets are warranted when using MS-TKA systems.

In summary, while the findings of King et al. provide valuable insight into KA in one MS-TKA system, their applicability to other implant designs should be interpreted with caution. Implant-specific patellofemoral features, intrinsic kinematics, and rotational strategies may all influence patellar biomechanics under KA. I commend the authors for their important contribution and hope that future studies will further clarify optimal KA protocols across different implant platforms. Such efforts could ultimately support safer, more tailored alignment strategies that optimize patellofemoral function for diverse patient populations. These considerations may also guide future implant design modifications to optimize patellofemoral mechanics under KA.
