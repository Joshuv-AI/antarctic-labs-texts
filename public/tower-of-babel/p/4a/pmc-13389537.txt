Genome editing-based refinement of GPCR visualization in mice using the oxytocin receptor as a model
Authors: Yukiko U Inoue, Eon Kurumiya, Ryosuke Tany, Eriko Koike, Junko Asami, Ikuo Masuho, Takayoshi Inoue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractG protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) mediate diverse physiological functions and are major drug targets, yet their in vivo visualization remains challenging due to poor antigenicity. In this study, we integrate structural prediction, functional assay, and genome editing to systematically refine epitope-tagging strategies for GPCRs in mouse models. Using the oxytocin receptor (Oxtr) as a representative GPCR, we compare conventional triple-HA tagging with two recently developed high-sensitivity tags, Spaghetti Monster fluorescent protein (smFP) and the ALFA tag. Each tag is fused to the Oxtr C-terminus, and in vitro G protein activation assays show that all three variants retain ~ 70% of wild-type activity, consistent with structural predictions from AlphaFold 3. We then generate knock-in mice employing genome editing to evaluate in vivo performance. smFP markedly improves the signal-to-noise ratio in tissue staining, whereas the ALFA tag disrupts trafficking without enhancing sensitivity. Primary cultured neurons derived from smFP knock-in mice clearly visualize the distribution of Oxtr along dendrites down to their terminals at subcellular resolution. Collectively, the refined animal models advance GPCR biology and inform the design of future genome editing approaches.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50956-7.Keywords: GPCR, Oxytocin receptor, Epitope tagging, Genome editingSubject terms: Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Neuroscience

Introduction

G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) are the most abundant family of cell surface receptors, mediating a broad range of extracellular signals, including odors, hormones, neurotransmitters, and chemokines1. They regulate nearly all physiological processes and are major pharmacotherapeutic targets2. Thus, it is crucial to determine the cell-type specificity and abundance of GPCR protein expression—beyond RNA-based measures—and to clarify how their plasma membrane localization is regulated in vivo under physiological and pharmacological conditions. However, GPCRs are notoriously difficult to detect in native tissues because their lipophilic seven-transmembrane structure restricts epitope accessibility and hinders the generation of antibodies.

To address this limitation, epitope tagging3 has long been a promising strategy, in which a short antigenic peptide is fused to a protein of interest (POI) and detected with a tag-specific antibody (Fig. 1A). Conventional epitope tags such as HA or FLAG are frequently employed, and in mice, both N- and C-terminally tagged GPCR knock-in models have been reported4–6. Oxytocin receptor (Oxtr) is an attractive choice for such methodological refinement because it is a well-characterized GPCR with key roles in endocrine and neural systems7. Besides its peripheral actions in parturition and lactation8, oxytocin modulates social behaviors in the brain9, drawing broad interest from researchers and the public. Its properties also allow in vivo verification of whether knock-in tagging affects normal receptor function, which can be assessed through established reproductive and behavioral readouts.

The DNA sequences of three variants are available at Zenodo (https://zenodo.org/records/17446559) and DDBJ (Accession numbers: LC897328, LC897329, and LC897330), and the genomic DNA sequences for three knock-in lines are also supplied at Zenodo (https://zenodo.org/records/17446592) and DDBJ (Accession numbers: LC897331, LC897332, and LC897333).

As for a drawback of epitope tagging, conventional tags often lack sufficient sensitivity. For example, we previously developed C-terminally tagged Oxtr knock-in lines, demonstrating their utility for visualizing GPCR expression4. Although clear immunohistochemical signals were obtained in young mice with high Oxtr expression and low background, detection sensitivity was inadequate in adult brain tissues with lower expression4. A comparable limitation was reported for C-terminally tagged Gpr4 knock-in mice, where low-expressing cell populations remained poorly detectable6. These findings illustrate sensitivity-related challenges inherent to conventional epitope tags such as HA or FLAG.

To overcome these barriers, two next-generation tags have been developed. The first is the Spaghetti Monster fluorescent protein (smFP)10, which embeds ten HA epitopes strategically inserted at the N- and C-termini and an internal loop of a GFP scaffold, ensuring antibody accessibility and markedly enhancing the detectability for weakly expressed proteins. The second is the ALFA tag11, a rationally designed α-helical sequence (SRLEEELRRRLTE) that is absent in common eukaryotic proteomes, allowing for low background and sensitive detection with fluorophore-conjugated nanobodies, which are compatible with super-resolution microscopy. Both ALFA and smFP have been validated through the transfection of cultured mammalian cells and by viral or electroporation-based expression in mouse brains; however, their utility as knock-in alleles for visualizing endogenous GPCRs in native tissues remains untested. Given their relatively large size, these tags may disrupt receptor conformation or signaling, underscoring the need for careful pre-validation.

In this study, we employ CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing to swiftly generate Oxtr knock-in mice carrying HA, smFP, or ALFA tags. Prior to in vivo application, we assess potential effects by performing three-dimensional (3D) structural prediction of C-terminally tagged Oxtr and in vitro G protein activation assays. We then evaluate the detectability and localization of tagged Oxtr in native tissues, focusing on whether two next-generation tags improve sensitivity over the conventional HA tag. This genome editing-driven refinement, combined with structural predictions and in vitro functional analyses, establishes a mouse model for more reliable visualization of Oxtr in their physiological context and may inform similar approaches for other GPCRs.

Materials and methods

Design of C-terminally tagged Oxtr

Three types of C-terminally tagged Oxtr constructs were generated as follows (Fig. 1B and C). In Oxtr-3xHA, three tandem HA tags (YPYDVPDYA) separated by glycine linkers were fused immediately upstream of the stop codon. In Oxtr-smFP, the smFP sequence10 was directly fused to the C-terminal end of the Oxtr coding sequence. In Oxtr-3xALFA, three copies of the minimal ALFA tag (SRLEEELRRRLTE), each flanked by proline residues as described in the original publication11, were arranged in tandem with AGA linkers between them and fused to the C-terminal end of Oxtr. The corresponding nucleotide sequences are available at Zenodo (https://zenodo.org/records/17446559) and DDBJ (Accession numbers: LC897328, LC897329, and LC897330).

Protein structure prediction and visualization

3D protein structures of epitope-tagged Oxtr and the corresponding Oxtr–Gq complexes were predicted using AlphaFold 3 via the AlphaFold Server (DeepMind/Isomorphic Labs; https://alphafoldserver.com)12 with default parameters. Input amino-acid sequences were supplied as plain, continuous single-letter strings. For complex predictions, each chain (Oxtr with the indicated epitope tag, Gαq (NP_002063.2), Gβ1 (NP_002065.1), and Gγ2 (NP_444292.1)) was entered as a separate sequence within a single job. For each target, the server produced five model files. For figure preparation, one of these five models was selected arbitrarily and shown as the representative. Resulting coordinate files (PDB/mmCIF) were downloaded and visualized in PyMOL, and static images exported from PyMOL were used for figure preparation.

Genetic constructs for in cellulo GEF assay

To generate pcDNA3.1(+) plasmids (NM_001081147) encoding wild-type Oxtr and its C-terminally tagged variants, the Oxtr cDNA was PCR-amplified from B6C3F1 mouse genomic DNA. The 3xHA and 3xALFA sequences were synthesized de novo by Eurofins Genomics, and the smFP sequence was PCR-amplified from Addgene plasmid #59759. These DNA fragments were assembled using the In-Fusion cloning system (Takara Bio). The corresponding nucleotide sequences are available at Zenodo (https://zenodo.org/records/17446559) and DDBJ (Accession numbers: LC897328, LC897329, and LC897330).

The pCMV5 plasmid encoding human Gαq (NM_002072) was kindly provided by Dr. Hiroshi Itoh. A fusion protein of Venus (amino acids 156–239) linked via a GGSGGG linker to the N-terminus of Gβ1 (NM_002074), with the initial methionine removed (Venus 156–239-Gβ1), and a complementary construct consisting of Venus (amino acids 1–155) fused similarly to the N-terminus of Gγ2 (NM_053064)13 were used as BRET sensor components. Both constructs were generously provided by Dr. Nevin A. Lambert. The masGRK3ct-Nluc-HA construct, used as a BRET donor, has been previously described14. The construct encoding the S1 catalytic subunit of pertussis toxin (PTX-S1; AJ920066) was described in a prior study15.

Cell culture

HEK293T/17 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Corning, Cat No. 10–013-CV) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), minimum Eagle’s medium non-essential amino acids, and antibiotics (100 units/ml penicillin and 100 µg/ml streptomycin). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. HEK293T/17 cells, a high-transfectability clone of the HEK293T cell line, were used because of their suitability for transient expression assays16.

Transfection protocol for in cellulo GEF assay

Before cell seeding, each well of a 96-well plate was pre-incubated with 50 µl of a diluted growth factor-reduced Matrigel solution (approximately 10 µg/ml in culture medium, Corning) and incubated at 37 °C for at least 5 minutes. Subsequently, 50 µl of a cell suspension was added to each well at a density of 0.5 × 105 cells/well.

Two hours after seeding, cells were transfected with a total of 0.25 µg plasmid DNA per well using PEI Max (1 mg/ml) as the transfection reagent, with 0.25 µg PEI added per well. The following plasmids were transfected: Oxtr (1), Gαq (2), Venus 156–239-Gβ1 (1), Venus 1–155-Gγ2 (1), and masGRK3ct-Nluc-HA (1). Numbers in parentheses represent relative DNA ratios, where 1 corresponds to 10.5 ng/well.

To block the potential coupling of endogenous Gi/o proteins to Oxtr, the PTX-S1 construct (1) was co-transfected. Empty pcDNA3.1(+) was added as needed to equalize total DNA amounts across all wells.

In cellulo GEF assay

Real-time monitoring of agonist-induced BRET changes between Venus-Gβ1γ2 and masGRK3ct-Nluc-HA was performed in live HEK293T/17 cells, following procedures conceptually similar to those previously described17,18. Approximately 24 h after transfection, cells were rinsed once with BRET buffer consisting of Dulbecco’s Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS) supplemented with 0.5 mM MgCl2 and 0.1% glucose. Furimazine (NanoLuciferase (Nluc) substrate; Promega) was added according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

BRET measurements were carried out at 25 °C using a microplate reader (FLUOstar Omega; BMG Labtech) equipped with dual-emission photomultiplier tubes, enabling simultaneous detection at two wavelengths with a temporal resolution of up to 20 ms per data point. The BRET ratio was calculated as the emission at 535 nm (Venus; 30 nm bandwidth) divided by the emission at 475 nm (Nluc; 30 nm bandwidth).

To quantify net BRET changes (ΔBRET), the average baseline signal recorded before agonist stimulation was subtracted from each data point. The peak ΔBRET was defined as the maximum BRET amplitude. For kinetic analysis, the rising phase of the BRET curve was fitted to a single exponential function using Clampfit 10.3, and the rate constant was calculated as 1/τ.

Intracellular Ca2+ mobilization and β-arrestin recruitment assays

The transfection protocol was identical to that used for the in cellulo GEF assay, except for the plasmid combinations. For the intracellular Ca2+ mobilization assay, cells were transfected with Oxtr (1) and Calflux VTN (1)19. For the β-arrestin recruitment BRET assay, cells were transfected with Oxtr (1), β-arrestin1-Nluc or β-arrestin2-Nluc (0.1), and mVenus-KRas (2). The BRET assay was then carried out in the same manner as the in cellulo GEF assay.

Experimental animals

All animal care and experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the National Institute of Neuroscience (approval number: 2023004). All experiments were performed in accordance with the Guidelines for Animal Experiments of the National Institute of Neuroscience and with the relevant national regulations, including the Act on Welfare and Management of Animals in Japan. The study was conducted in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines. Mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions in a controlled environment (22 ± 2 °C; 12-h light/dark cycle; ad libitum access to food and water).

One-cell stage zygotes of C57BL/6J mice were obtained by in vitro fertilization using males and females purchased from Charles River Japan. Fertilized eggs of B6C3F1 mice were collected by mating stud males with females purchased from SLC Japan, where females were hormonally treated to induce superovulation. ICR female mice as surrogate mothers were purchased from SLC Japan, and C57BL/6J mice for back-crossing were purchased from Charles River Japan. Pronuclear injections and mouse transgenesis experiments were carried out following standard protocols20, and fertilized egg electroporation was performed using established procedures21.

For surgical procedures, mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection (i.p.) of a mixed anesthetic consisting of medetomidine (0.3 mg/kg; Kyoritsu Seiyaku Corporation), midazolam (4.0 mg/kg; Maruishi Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.), and butorphanol (5.0 mg/kg; Meiji Animal Health Co., Ltd.). For the collection of oocytes and sperm, mice were euthanized under deep anesthesia by cervical dislocation, followed by laparotomy to collect oviductal embryos and epididymides. For oviductal embryo transfer, mice were anesthetized with the same mixed anesthetic. Embryos were then transferred into the oviducts, and anesthesia was antagonized with atipamezole (1 mg/kg, i.p.; Kyoritsu Seiyaku Corporation) to allow recovery and continued breeding.

Preparation of CRISPR components

To insert epitope tags into the Oxtr locus via the CRISPR/Cas9 system, the guide RNA sequence closest to the stop codon TGA in the second exon was selected (Fig. 1B). Two parts of the CRISPR guide RNA, crRNA and tracrRNA, were chemically synthesized (Integrated DNA Technologies), and recombinant Cas9 protein (S.p. HiFi Cas9 Nuclease V3) was purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies. For pronuclear injections, equimolar crRNA and tracrRNA were mixed and annealed (94 °C, 2 min, then at room temperature for 10 min).

Generation of the Oxtr-3xHA mouse line

To insert three tandem copies of HA tag sequences immediately upstream from the stop codon, a single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) donor containing three HA tag sequences flanked by 36- and 37-base homology arms on each side was designed and chemically synthesized by Eurofins Genomics. The mixture of crRNA, tracrRNA, Cas9 protein, and ssDNA donor was electroporated into C57BL/6J one-cell stage zygotes at concentrations of 50, 50, 100, and 500 ng/µl, then the zygotes were transferred into the oviducts of surrogate mothers to raise pups.

Generation of the Oxtr-smFP mouse line

To directly fuse the smFP sequence10 at the C-terminal end of Oxtr coding sequence, the targeting vector containing the smFP sequence flanked by 1 kb homology arms on both sides was constructed by the In-Fusion cloning system (Takara Bio). The mixture of crRNA/tracrRNA complex, Cas9 protein, and the targeting vector was injected into B6C3F1 fertilized eggs at concentrations of 17.5, 35, and 10 ng/µl, and the eggs were transferred into the oviducts of surrogate mothers to raise pups.

Generation of the Oxtr-3xALFA mouse line

To fuse three copies of ALFA tags to the C-terminal end of Oxtr, the DNA construct containing 3xALFA tag sequences flanked by 130 bp homology arms on both sides was generated by using the In-Fusion cloning system (Takara Bio). By using this construct as the template, a long single-stranded DNA (long ssDNA) donor was prepared with the Phospho-PCR method (Guide-it Long ssDNA Production System v2, Takara Bio)22. The mixture of annealed crRNA/tracrRNA, Cas9 protein, and long ssDNA donor was injected into B6C3F1 zygotes at concentrations of 50, 100, and 20 ng/µl, and the zygotes were transferred into the oviducts of surrogate mothers to raise pups.

Validation of the founder knock-in mice and their backcrossing

Genomic DNAs were prepared from newborns’ tails by proteinase K treatment in Lysis Buffer (Takara Bio). Knock-in mice were screened by PCR using Tks Gflex DNA polymerase (Takara Bio). To exclude the possibility that the donor DNAs had been randomly integrated into the genome, a subset of genotyping primers was designed external to the donor DNAs’ homology arms. For knock-in candidates, PCR products were analyzed by Sanger sequencing to confirm carrying the correct insertions. The obtained founders were backcrossed with C57BL/6J to get F1 heterozygous progenies, which were again sequence-verified to harbor the designed knock-in allele. F1 heterozygous mice were then intercrossed to generate homozygous knock-in mice. For histological analyses, homozygous knock-in mice after F6 generations were used.

Generation of the Oxtr knockout (KO) mouse line

Two guide RNAs (Guide RNA1 and Guide RNA2 depicted in Supplementary Fig. 1A) were designed to completely delete the Oxtr gene locus (~ 20 kb), which lies adjacent to the Cav3 gene locus, using CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing. A mixture of crRNA, tracrRNA, and Cas9 protein was electroporated into B6C3F1 one-cell stage zygotes, then the zygotes were transferred into the oviducts of surrogate mothers to obtain pups. Oxtr KO mice were screened by PCR using three primers indicated in Supplementary Fig. 1 A and 1B. PCR products were analyzed by Sanger sequencing to confirm the presence of the designed deletion (Supplementary Fig. 1C). The obtained founder was backcrossed with C57BL/6J to generate F1 heterozygous progeny. This line was maintained by mating homozygous males with heterozygous females because homozygous Oxtr KO mothers were unable to nurse their pups. This phenotype is consistent with previously reported Oxtr KO mice23, indicating that the line generated in this study faithfully recapitulates the known Oxtr KO phenotype. Homozygous male mice at the F16 generation were used for analysis.

Antibodies

For the detection of HA tags in Oxtr-3xHA and Oxtr-smFP mice, Rabbit anti-HA-tag antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, C29F4, #3724S) was used at 1:2000 dilution as the primary antibody, followed by Donkey anti-rabbit IgG H&L Alexa Fluor 647 antibody (abcam, ab150075) at 1:250 dilution as the secondary antibody. For the detection of ALFA tag in Oxtr-3xALFA mice, FluoTag-X2 anti-ALFA Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated single-domain antibody (NanoTag Biotechnologies, N1502-AF647-L) was used at 1:150 dilution. For the detection of microtubule-associated protein 2a and 2b (MAP2) as a dendritic marker in primary cultured cells, Mouse anti-MAP2 antibody (Clone AP-20, M1406, Sigma-Aldrich) was used at 1:1500 dilution as the primary antibody, followed by Donkey anti-mouse IgG H&L Alexa Fluor 488 antibody (abcam, ab150109) at 1:250 dilution as the secondary antibody.

Immunohistochemistry

Brain and pancreatic samples from 7-week-old male homozygous knock-in mice, as well as brains from homozygous Oxtr-smFP males at postnatal day 0 (P0), were collected for immunohistology. The mice were euthanized under deep anesthesia with the same mixed anesthetic described above, followed by intracardiac perfusion with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4). The brains and pancreases were dissected, post-fixed in the same fixative for 16 h, cryoprotected in 30% sucrose in PBS, and embedded in OCT compound (Sakura Finetek Japan) for freezing. Mammary gland tissues were obtained from 11–12-week-old female homozygous mice at 10–19 days postpartum. After deep anesthesia with the same mixed anesthetic and perfusion fixation using the same procedure, the fourth mammary glands were dissected, post-fixed in the same fixative for 24 h, cryoprotected in 30% sucrose, and embedded in OCT compound. Brains from 7-week-old mice were coronally sectioned at 40 μm using a Leica cryostat, and the free-floating sections were subjected to immunostaining. Brains from P0 mice were sectioned at 18 μm and mounted on glass slides for immunostaining. Pancreatic and mammary gland samples were sectioned at 14 μm, mounted on glass slides, and processed for immunostaining.

Sections were treated in blocking buffer (3% normal donkey serum, 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 1 h at room temperature. They were then incubated with either the anti-HA primary antibody or the fluorescently labeled anti-ALFA single-domain antibody in blocking solution (1% normal donkey serum, 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS) overnight at 4 °C. For HA-tag staining, sections were washed with PBS and subsequently incubated with the Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibody and DAPI for 70 min at room temperature. For ALFA-tag staining, sections were washed with PBS and incubated with DAPI for 20 min. After final washes, sections were mounted with VECTASHIELD Vibrance antifade mounting medium (Vector Laboratories). Fluorescence images of brain sections from 7-week-old mice were acquired using a BZ-X710 fluorescence microscope (KEYENCE) equipped with a 10× objective lens. Image tiling and stitching were performed using BZ-X Analyzer software (KEYENCE). Higher-magnification images were obtained using an OLYMPUS SpinSR10 spinning-disk confocal microscope with a 20× objective lens. For subcellular localization analysis of tagged Oxtr in neurons, pancreatic cells, and myoepithelial cells, images were acquired using the SpinSR10 microscope with a 40× objective lens. For comparisons between mouse lines, images were obtained using identical microscope settings and acquisition parameters at a single focal plane without Z-stacking.

Quantification of HA-stained neurons in the primary somatosensory cortex (S1)

Six mice per genotype (Oxtr 3xHA/3xHA and Oxtr smFP/smFP) were analyzed. Coronal brain sections (40 μm) were collected in four serial sets (1/4 series; 160 μm interval), and four sections around bregma − 1.82 mm were used for quantification. Images were acquired using an OLYMPUS SpinSR10 with a 20× objective lens under identical acquisition settings for all samples. In each section, both the left and right S1 regions were imaged, yielding eight images per animal. HA-stained neurons were counted across the entire confocal field of view (0.439 mm²) in each image and converted to the number of neurons per mm². Cell counting was performed using ImageJ (version 1.54 g) with the Multi-point tool, as exemplified in Supplementary Fig. 2. For each animal, the mean value from the eight images was calculated and used as a single biological replicate. The resulting values from six animals per genotype were compared using an unpaired t-test.

Primary culture of Oxtr-expressing neurons derived from Oxtr-3xHA and Oxtr-smFP mouse brains

Brain tissue fragments, including the Oxtr-expressing regions (i.e., whole olfactory bulb and orbitofrontal cortex, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 3A), were dissected out from the P0 Oxtr3xHA/3xHA mice and OxtrsmFP/smFP mice that were euthanized under deep anesthesia with the same mixed anesthetic described above. They were immediately immersed in 2 ml of ice-cold HEPES-buffered Ca2+-Mg2+-free solution containing 1 mM EDTA. After removing the pia from these tissue fragments, they were minced with scissors to be treated with 0.25% w/v trypsin (Sigma-Aldrich) for 25 min at 37 °C with gentle agitation (~ 60 rpm). DNAs from dead cells were then completely digested by adding 150 µg/ml of DNase I (Sigma-Aldrich) and 5 mM MgCl2 for an additional 10 min at 37 °C with gentle agitation. These trypsinized cells were dissociated into single cells by thorough pipetting on ice, and the trypsin was inactivated by adding an equal volume of the DH10 medium containing the Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle medium (Shimadzu Diagnostics) and HAMF12 medium (Shimadzu Diagnostics) half by half with 10% v/v fetal calf serum. After centrifuging at 1,500 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, the cell pellet was resuspended in 2 ml of DH10, and larger tissue debris was removed using a Falcon cell strainer (#2340, Corning). After centrifuging the strained cell suspension at 1,500 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, the cell pellet was finally resuspended in 2 ml of DH10 and transferred to a Nunc Lab-Tek Chamber Slide (4-well, 177399, Thermo Fisher Scientific) coated with poly-L-lysine (Sigma-Aldrich). Cells were maintained in a water-jacketed CO2 incubator at 37 °C, and half the volume of the medium was replaced daily. At day in vitro 7 (DIV7), the cells were fixed and co-stained with anti-HA and anti-MAP2 antibodies using a standard protocol. Fluorescent images were acquired using an OLYMPUS SpinSR10 microscope with a 40× objective lens, and super-resolution images were obtained using a 60× oil-immersion objective lens. For both genotypes, all images were acquired under identical acquisition settings at a single focal plane without Z-stacking.

Quantification of the signal-to-noise (S/N) ratio for detecting HA-tagged Oxtr proteins on dendrites in primary cultured neurons

Images of Oxtr-expressing neurons were obtained from P0 primary cultures (DIV7) prepared from Oxtr-3xHA and Oxtr-smFP mice (Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5). A circle with a radius of 50 μm centered on the soma was drawn, and dendritic segments intersecting the circle were imaged at super-resolution using an OLYMPUS SpinSR10 microscope equipped with a 60× oil-immersion objective lens. In the acquired images, a single HA-tagged Oxtr protein punctum located on a dendrite at the intersection with the previously defined 50-µm-radius circle was selected. A 5-µm line perpendicular to the dendrite and passing through the punctum was drawn, and the fluorescence intensity profile was obtained using the Plot Profile tool in ImageJ (Supplementary Fig. 4). Within the same field of view, a background region devoid of cells or visible structures was selected, and a 5-µm line of equal length was drawn to obtain the background intensity profile. The signal profile was fitted with a Gaussian curve using the Curve Fitter function in ImageJ, and the goodness of fit was evaluated by the R² value. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 4C,a indicates the baseline, and b indicates the peak intensity. The S/N ratio was calculated as b − a (peak amplitude) divided by the standard deviation (SD) of the background signal. In addition to the representative neuron shown in Supplementary Fig. 4, five additional neurons were analyzed (Supplementary Fig. 5). For each genotype, measurements were performed at three locations per neuron across six neurons, and the mean value per neuron was used as a biological replicate. The resulting values from six neurons per genotype were compared using an unpaired t-test.

Statistical significance was evaluated using two-tailed unpaired t-tests for comparisons between two groups, one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA as appropriate for comparisons involving one or two independent variables, respectively, followed by multiple-comparison tests. Results

Structural comparison of C-terminally tagged Oxtr variants

To evaluate the advantages and limitations of smFP and ALFA tags relative to the conventional HA tag, we generated three Oxtr knock-in designs (Fig. 1). Oxtr-3xHA served as the benchmark, while Oxtr-smFP and Oxtr-3xALFA represented new tagging strategies. Given that Oxtr possesses a relatively long and flexible C-terminal tail (Fig. 2A and B), we considered this region a suitable site for tag insertion. However, C-terminal tagging could still potentially influence receptor coupling to G proteins. To assess this possibility, we first predicted 3D protein structures of each C-terminally tagged Oxtr in complex with Gq using AlphaFold 3 (Fig. 2C–F). Structural modeling suggested that none of the three tags interferes with Gq coupling and that their impact on signaling is expected to be minimal (Fig. 2C, D, and F and Supplementary Fig. 6). To complement these structural predictions, we subsequently built up the three tag-inserted constructs and sought to examine actual protein dynamics both in vitro and in vivo.

Five independent models are overlaid to illustrate structural consistency in the transmembrane core and variability in flexible regions, particularly the relatively long C-terminal tail. (C) Oxtr-3xHA–Gq complex, (D) Oxtr-smFP–Gq complex, (E) overall structure of the smFP tag, and (F) Oxtr-3xALFA–Gq complex. C-terminal tagging does not abolish Gq-coupling activity of Oxtr

To assess whether the addition of tags at the C-terminus affects the function of Oxtr, we compared the signaling activity of untagged Oxtr with constructs carrying three different C-terminal tags. Among several available methods for evaluating GPCR activity, we chose a Bioluminescence Resonance Energy Transfer (BRET)-based assay that directly measures G protein activation (Fig. 2G)17, enabling the most direct assessment of Oxtr function. Since Oxtr is known to couple primarily to the Gq family of G proteins24, Gq was used in this study. Because this unique BRET-based approach quantifies the ability of GPCRs to activate G proteins as Guanine nucleotide Exchange Factors (GEFs) in living cells, it is hereafter referred to as an in cellulo GEF assay, in accordance with our previous work25.

To ensure accurate interpretation of the results, we first optimized the amount of wild-type Oxtr plasmid used for transfection by titrating the DNA input and identifying conditions that yielded maximal responsiveness (Supplementary Fig. 7A and 7B). Under these optimized conditions, we observed a robust increase in BRET signal in response to oxytocin only when Gαq was coexpressed with Oxtr, confirming the specificity of the Oxtr-Gq coupling (Supplementary Fig. 7C). Similar Gq-specific coupling was confirmed for all three C-terminally tagged Oxtr constructs (Supplementary Fig. 7D–7F).

Using these conditions, we then compared the signaling activities of the tagged constructs. All three constructs showed the ability to activate Gq to a similar extent as the wild-type receptor (Fig. 2H). To more precisely evaluate receptor activity, we quantified both the maximal Gq activation (Fig. 2I) and the GEF activity of Oxtr by measuring the initial rate of Gq activation (Fig. 2J). While the maximal Gq activation induced by the tagged constructs was comparable to that of the wild-type receptor, their GEF activity was uniformly reduced to approximately 70% of wild-type levels. These results indicate that despite a modest reduction in GEF activity, all tagged Oxtr constructs retain sufficient functionality and are likely to be suitable for in vivo applications.

smFP provides superior sensitivity for Oxtr detection in adult mouse brains compared with 3xHA and 3xALFA

Following the results of the in vitro G protein activation assay, we generated three Oxtr knock-in mouse lines with distinct C-terminal tags, namely 3xHA, smFP, and 3xALFA (Fig. 1B and C), using our rapid and accurate genome-editing approach4,22. In our previous epitope-tagged mouse models4, Oxtr proteins were detectable with reasonable sensitivity in the juvenile brain, where their expression is high26; however, the detection sensitivity was insufficient in the adult brain, as Oxtr levels decline. To assess whether this limitation could be overcome, we compared immunostaining signals in adult (7-week-old) male brains using antibodies against each tag.

As a benchmark control, we observed that the Oxtr-3xHA line displayed detectable signals in brain regions containing many cells with strong Oxtr expression, such as the paraventricular thalamus (PVT)26,27, posteromedial cortical amygdaloid area (PMCo)26, and ventromedial hypothalamus (VMH)28,29 (Fig. 3A), reproducing our previous results4. However, signals were weak in most other regions. In the primary somatosensory cortex (S1), neurons with high Oxtr expression were labeled, but neurons with lower Oxtr levels were often undetectable, leading to an underestimation of the total number of Oxtr-positive cells (Fig. 3B).

In the Oxtr-smFP mice (C–D), staining signals are markedly stronger compared with Oxtr-3xHA (A–B) and Oxtr-3xALFA mice (F–G), in regions previously reported to express Oxtr, including the primary somatosensory area (S1), hippocampal dentate gyrus (DG) and anterior CA2/CA3, paraventricular thalamus (PVT), posteroventral part of the medial amygdala (MePV), posteromedial cortical amygdaloid area (PMCo), and ventromedial hypothalamus (VMH). Oxtr-smFP showed a significantly higher number of labeled neurons than Oxtr-3xHA, demonstrating the superior sensitivity of smFP for detecting GPCRs in adult brain tissue.

By contrast, Oxtr-smFP mice exhibited a pronounced enhancement in detection sensitivity (Fig. 3C) compared with the 3xHA mice (Fig. 3A). We observed strong signals in multiple brain regions previously reported to express Oxtr, including the S126, hippocampal dentate gyrus (DG) and anterior CA2/CA330, PVT, posteroventral part of the medial amygdala (MePV)31,32, PMCo, and VMH. Although these regional expressions had been detectable in juvenile brains of our earlier knock-in lines4, robust labeling in the adult brain had not been achieved until this time. In the S1, neurons spanning a wide range of expression levels were readily visualized (Fig. 3D). By counting the number of HA-stained neurons in S1 corresponding to Fig. 3B and D from N = 6 mice (see Supplementary Fig. 2 for details), we found that more neurons were labeled in Oxtr-smFP mice than in Oxtr-3xHA mice (Fig. 3E); notably, no specific signal was detected in wild-type mice and Oxtr KO mice under the same staining conditions (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Thus, in the adult brain, where traditional tags lacked sensitivity, the hyper-antigenic smFP tag provided a substantial improvement in the S/N ratio. To our knowledge, this marks the first clear detection of Oxtr in the adult mouse brain, highlighting the effectiveness of smFP tagging for in vivo visualization of GPCRs.

Next, in mice carrying three copies of the de novo rationally designed ALFA tag, which is absent in common eukaryotic proteomes and thus minimizes background, we observed that staining signals exhibited a relatively high signal-to-noise ratio, as predicted (Fig. 3F). However, contrary to our expectations, this line failed to yield a substantial improvement in detection sensitivity compared with the 3xHA mice (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, in the enlarged view of S1, we found that the signals appeared to be unevenly distributed within neuronal somata (Fig. 3G). As described later, this observation raised the possibility that the 3xALFA tag influenced the intracellular localization of Oxtr.

C-terminally fused 3xHA, smFP, and 3xALFA do not inhibit peripheral Oxtr function in lactation

Based on the in cellulo GEF assays, we found that C-terminal tagging of Oxtr with 3xHA, smFP, or 3xALFA caused only a modest reduction in GEF activity while retaining sufficient receptor functionality. To evaluate the in vivo functionality of these tagged receptors, we examined lactation as a representative peripheral effect of oxytocin. In Oxtr KO mothers, pups reportedly die shortly after birth due to maternal defects in nursing23. As shown in Fig. 4A–C, pups born to mothers with any of the three C-terminal tags had stomachs filled with milk, and the mothers’ mammary glands were well developed, with prominent nipples suitable for nursing (Fig. 4D–F). These observations indicate that C-terminal tagging does not disrupt peripheral oxytocin signaling, including its crucial role in lactation, which is consistent with the in vitro results. However, the effects of these tags on oxytocin function in the central nervous system have not yet been fully evaluated and would require further investigation.

C-terminal 3xALFA tag disrupts Oxtr trafficking to the plasma membrane

In addition to potential effects on G protein signaling, C-terminal tagging may alter the subcellular localization of GPCRs. As described in the previous section, Oxtr-3xALFA mice showed intracellularly biased localization in cortical neurons, raising concerns about impaired trafficking. To examine this more comprehensively, we analyzed three cell types known to express Oxtr: neurons in the cerebral cortex26, pancreatic islet cells33, and mammary myoepithelial cells34, using high-magnification imaging of homozygous adult mice.

In cortical neurons, we found that Oxtr-3xHA was distributed relatively uniformly along the plasma membrane (Fig. 5A and B). Oxtr-smFP also showed strong membrane signals, albeit with slight polarization, likely reflecting the enhanced detection conferred by the ten copies of HA epitopes (Fig. 5C and D). Consistent with our previous observations in the juvenile brain4, both Oxtr-3xHA and Oxtr-smFP were detectable in neuronal processes. By contrast, Oxtr-3xALFA exhibited pronounced intracellular accumulation, as noted above, and no signal was detected in neuronal processes (Fig. 5E and F).

In homozygous Oxtr-3xHA mice, 3xHA-tagged Oxtr exhibits uniform distribution along the cell membrane surrounding the cell body (A, B, G, H, M, N). In homozygous Oxtr-smFP mice, membrane signals are generally uniform in neurons (C, D) and mammary cells (O, P), although some heterogeneity is observed. In pancreatic cells (I, J), intracellularly invaginated signals occasionally appear (white arrows), suggesting that in certain cell types the smFP tag partially interferes with membrane trafficking. In contrast, in homozygous Oxtr-3xALFA mice, 3xALFA-tagged Oxtr shows pronounced intracellular retention in all three cell types (E, F, K, L, Q, R, white arrowheads), indicating severe impairment of receptor trafficking and membrane insertion.

In pancreatic islet cells, we observed that Oxtr-3xHA was consistently localized along the plasma membrane (Fig. 5G and H). Oxtr-smFP largely followed this pattern, although occasional intracellular invaginations were observed in some cells (Fig. 5I and J, indicated by white arrows). In sharp contrast, we found that Oxtr-3xALFA exhibited considerable mislocalization, with diminished membrane signals and prominent intracellular accumulation (Fig. 5K and L, marked by white arrowheads), indicating a strong trafficking defect.

In mammary myoepithelial cells, we detected membrane-enriched labeling around the cell bodies for both Oxtr-3xHA (Fig. 5M and N) and Oxtr-smFP (Fig. 5O and P). However, Oxtr-3xALFA consistently showed weaker membrane signals and abnormally concentrated intracellular labeling (Fig. 5Q and R), further confirming a pronounced disruption of receptor trafficking.

These results indicate that while 3xHA and smFP tags preserve proper Oxtr localization across multiple cell types, the C-terminal 3xALFA tag substantially impairs receptor trafficking and membrane insertion.

Oxtr-smFP enables high-resolution mapping of subcellular Oxtr localization in neurons

Since Oxtr-smFP mice showed a markedly improved S/N ratio in brain tissue staining compared with Oxtr-3xHA mice, we next derived primary neuronal cultures from these two lines and investigated Oxtr distribution at the subcellular level. To efficiently culture cells expressing Oxtr, we first examined the regional distribution of Oxtr-positive neurons in Oxtr-smFP mouse brains at postnatal day 0 (P0), as this developmental stage is optimal for preparing primary neuronal cultures—older tissues are more difficult to dissociate and yield lower cell viability. At P0, smFP labeling was relatively sparse in the cerebral cortex, whereas the olfactory bulb and orbitofrontal cortex contained abundant Oxtr-positive cells suitable for culture (Supplementary Fig. 3). We accordingly dissociated cells from these regions, cultured them, and fixed them at day in vitro 7 (DIV7), followed by co-staining with anti-HA and anti-MAP2 antibodies. MAP2, which localizes primarily to somata and dendrites but not axons in mature neurons, served as a dendritic marker.

Among cultured cells from the Oxtr-3xHA line, HA-positive (Oxtr-expressing) neurons were sparsely distributed and detectable by anti-HA staining, although background staining was relatively high (Fig. 6A and A’). Using super-resolution imaging, we observed that 3xHA-tagged Oxtr proteins were distributed along MAP2-positive dendrites (Fig. 6a1–6a3) and throughout the soma (Fig. 6a4), with noticeable background signals. In contrast, Oxtr-expressing neurons in the Oxtr-smFP line were readily detected with higher sensitivity (Fig. 6B and B’). Super-resolution imaging revealed distinct punctate signals of smFP-tagged Oxtr proteins along MAP2-positive dendrites (Fig. 6b1–6b3), and we were able to trace these signals toward distal dendritic regions. Signals were also broadly distributed throughout the soma (Fig. 6b4).

Using the method described in Supplementary Fig. 4, we quantified the Gaussian fit of the signal intensity profiles (R² value) and the S/N ratio for dendritic puncta of 3xHA-tagged and smFP-tagged Oxtr proteins from N = 6 neurons. Both the R² values and S/N ratios were higher for Oxtr-smFP than for Oxtr-3xHA neurons (Supplementary Fig. 4D and 4E), indicating that the smFP tag enables high-resolution mapping of subcellular Oxtr localization in neurons.

Although we had previously reported the localization of epitope-tagged Oxtr in neuronal somata and processes using juvenile brain slices from our earlier knock-in lines4, the resolution in those studies was somewhat limited, and Oxtr distributions were not sharply defined. The present analysis demonstrates that primary neuronal cultures from the Oxtr-smFP line enable more precise and reliable monitoring of Oxtr distribution at the subcellular resolution, providing a powerful approach for studying GPCR localization.

Given this superior performance in detection sensitivity and subcellular visualization, we further examined whether Oxtr-smFP also preserved receptor function in greater detail (Supplementary Fig. 9). These additional analyses showed preserved sensitivity to oxytocin, largely intact downstream signaling including Ca2+ influx, and no major defect in arrestin recruitment. Taken together, the overall findings of this study support Oxtr-smFP as the most suitable design among the tagging strategies examined here.

Discussion

Detecting GPCRs as proteins in their native tissues remains a technically challenging task. The Oxtr knock-in mouse lines we previously reported4 represented valuable resources, yet their application in the adult brain was limited by insufficient sensitivity. In the present study, we updated our resources through genome editing-driven refinement.

A notable aspect of this work is the rigorous reassessment of how C-terminal tagging affects downstream G protein activation. We employed the in cellulo GEF assay, a technically demanding but uniquely powerful method that directly monitors G protein activation with high temporal resolution17. Because smFP is a bulky tag, almost as large as the receptor itself (Fig. 2D and E), we initially expected that it would interfere with G protein coupling more than smaller tags such as 3xHA (Fig. 2C) or 3xALFA (Fig. 2F). Contrary to our expectations, however, all three tagged Oxtr variants exhibited a similar and uniform reduction in GEF activity to approximately 70% of wild-type levels. According to the current structural model of GPCR-G protein interaction, the outward movement of transmembrane segment 6 (TM6) generates an intracellular pocket into which the α5 helix of Gα inserts, stabilized by intracellular loop 2 (ICL2), ICL3, and adjacent TM regions35–37. Our AlphaFold 3-based structural prediction of Oxtr-smFP (Fig. 2D) suggests that smFP does not block the intracellular pocket, ICL2, and ICL3. Instead, the conformation appears to leave Gα access at least partially feasible, consistent with the ~ 70% residual GEF activity observed (Fig. 2J).

These data indicate that while all C-terminal tags tested reduce signaling to some degree, even a large tag such as smFP retains sufficient functionality for in vivo applications. Importantly, the Oxtr-smFP knock-in mouse established here combines preserved receptor function with markedly enhanced sensitivity in detecting Oxtr compared with conventional epitope tags. Using primary cultured neurons derived from Oxtr-smFP mouse brains and super-resolution imaging, we visualized the subcellular localization of smFP-tagged Oxtr proteins more clearly than in previously reported mouse lines. Our findings thus provide the first in vivo demonstration that the hyper-antigenic smFP tag can overcome longstanding sensitivity limitations of traditional tags, representing a key achievement of this study.

Such enhanced performance can be better understood by considering a fundamental limitation of conventional tagging approaches: antibody affinity is often a bottleneck in epitope tagging, and even multimeric repeats of small epitopes are frequently insufficient for detection when the POI is weakly expressed. Unlike simple tandem arrays, smFP represents an innovative design in which ten HA epitopes are strategically arranged within the GFP scaffold, with three at the N-terminus, four within internal loops, and three at the C-terminus. Each of these epitopes has been validated for accessibility by anti-HA antibodies10. Previous in vitro work demonstrated that smFP outperforms conventional 1x–3x HA repeats in labeling efficiency10. Here, we extended these findings by applying smFP for the first time to knock-in mice and demonstrating that its ten epitopes significantly enhance GPCR detection in vivo. Moreover, for epitope tag-based studies, the availability of high-affinity primary antibodies is a critical requirement. In the case of smFP, this condition is well met, as reliable anti-HA clones such as CST C29F4 (rabbit monoclonal) and Roche 3F10 (rat monoclonal) are commercially available and well validated. By contrast, for one of the most commonly used traditional tags, the FLAG tag, anti-FLAG antibodies often suffer from clone-dependent variability and are prone to high background staining in brain tissue38. These considerations further underscore the value of the HA-type smFP as an excellent add-on to the neuroscientist’s toolkit for studying the brain.

A second important contribution of this study is the systematic in vivo analysis of tagged Oxtr subcellular localization across multiple cell types, which allowed us to assess potential trafficking effects. The ALFA tag, a compact and rationally designed synthetic epitope11, was expected to be optimal among available tags. Surprisingly, however, the Oxtr-3xALFA knock-in line displayed abnormal intracellular accumulation in vivo. To interpret the results, two technical aspects warrant discussion: tag copy number and linker design. Firstly, we used three tandem copies of the ALFA tag, while the original report employed a single copy11. This might have been excessive. Secondly, we selected AGA as the linker connecting the three ALFA tags; however, other commonly used linkers include GAG, GSG, and GGG. This specific combination of three ALFA tags and the AGA linker may have altered the localization of Oxtr. It is essential to note that our findings do not preclude the potential utility of ALFA tags in general. Their compact design remains attractive, and single-copy insertions or alternative linkers may yield better outcomes in future experiments. Thus, while our results indicate that 3xALFA is not suitable for Oxtr visualization, it may still be appropriate for other types of molecules beyond GPCRs, and the experimental parameters for its optimal use should be addressed in future investigations.

At the same time, the selective appearance of impaired anterograde trafficking in the Oxtr-3xALFA variant, but not in the Oxtr-3xHA or Oxtr-smFP variants, suggests that the phenotype may not be explained solely by general tagging effects and that additional mechanism-specific factors may be involved. Although altered glycosylation maturation cannot be excluded, one possible explanation is that the 3xALFA tag interferes with C-terminal trafficking determinants or their associated trafficking machinery, thereby impairing transit through the secretory pathway and contributing to intracellular accumulation.

Taken together, these findings highlight the broader significance of developing reliable approaches to visualize GPCRs at the protein level with preserved functionality, because proteins—not transcripts—ultimately mediate physiological functions. Compared with RNA sequencing or classical in situ hybridization, our approach provides clearer insights into GPCR biology. Beyond developmental and disease-associated changes in receptor abundance, repeated pharmacological treatments are known to trigger receptor internalization, leading to desensitization39, which represents a significant obstacle in long-term pharmacotherapy. Given that GPCRs remain among the most important drug targets, our method establishes not only a means of identifying cell types that express specific GPCRs but also a starting point for in vivo analyses of disease mechanisms and the molecular basis of therapeutic efficacy and desensitization.

In summary, this study provides two key insights. First, smFP enables sensitive detection of Oxtr in vivo without compromising receptor function, overcoming a major limitation of conventional tags. Second, evaluation of ALFA-tagged Oxtr revealed unexpected trafficking defects, emphasizing the need for empirical validation of each tagging strategy. Overall, our results demonstrate that genome editing-driven refinement of tagged GPCR knock-in mice offers a versatile experimental tool for more precise studies of GPCR localization. By integrating in vitro functional validation with in vivo analyses, this work provides valuable guidance for the rational design of future genome-edited animal models, thereby broadening their applications in life sciences and translational research.

Limitations in interpreting tag-dependent effects and structural models

While these results provide useful guidance for the design and evaluation of endogenously tagged Oxtr models, several limitations should be taken into account when interpreting tag-dependent differences in visualization, signaling, and structural compatibility.

One limitation of the present study is that Oxtr expression levels were not quantitatively compared across the three C-terminally tagged knock-in mouse lines. Reliable antibody-based quantification of endogenous Oxtr in the mouse brain remains challenging40. Accordingly, we cannot exclude the possibility that differences in visualization efficiency among the tagged lines reflect, at least in part, differences in receptor abundance in addition to tag-dependent detection sensitivity.

A related limitation applies to the interpretation of the in cellulo GEF assay. The reduced signaling observed for the tagged Oxtr constructs may reflect not only altered intrinsic receptor signaling but also the effects of C-terminal modification on receptor abundance, conformation, or assay readout.

The structural interpretations based on AlphaFold models should likewise be regarded as supportive rather than definitive. Because the Oxtr C-terminal tail is likely flexible, the apparent positioning of the tags in the predicted structures may not represent a single fixed arrangement in vivo. Thus, functional analyses remain essential for evaluating the impact of C-terminal tagging on receptor signaling.
