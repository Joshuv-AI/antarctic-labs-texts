Adaptive testing for smartphone‐based cognitive screening: Reducing patient burden while maintaining classification accuracy
Authors: Stephanie Ruth Young, Julia Yoshino Benavente, Elizabeth McManus Dworak, Michael S Wolf, Cindy J Nowinski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONRemote cognitive screening for primary care must balance accuracy with patient burden. We evaluated whether adaptive task administration could reduce testing while preserving classification accuracy.METHODSUsing data from older adults (N = 277; 100 mild cognitive impairment [MCI]; 177 normal cognitive aging) who self‐administered MyCog Mobile, we developed a stepped protocol in which the final task is skipped when preceding tasks classify the patient with confidence. Leave‐one‐out cross‐validation (LOO‐CV) assessed agreement with the full‐battery classification and accuracy relative to the reference diagnosis.RESULTSThirty‐three percent of participants were classified at the initial decision point, and only 17% required the full battery. LOO‐CV showed a negligible change in area under the curve (ΔAUC = 0.008; bootstrapped 95% confidence interval [−0.004, 0.021]) using the adaptive battery.DISCUSSIONApproximately one third of older adults can be accurately classified as with MCI using only two MyCog Mobile tasks under an adaptive approach. Prospective validation, in which the adaptive battery is administered in real time, is warranted.Keywords: adaptive testing, cognitive screening, mild cognitive impairment, patient burden, smartphone assessment

INTRODUCTION

Early detection of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is critical for identifying treatable causes, planning appropriate care, and initiating disease‐modifying therapies that are most effective in the earliest stages of cognitive decline. 1 , 2 Remote, self‐administered cognitive screening tools offer a promising approach to increasing detection rates in primary care, a common setting in which older adults access health care, yet up to 90% of MCI cases are missed. 3 Primary care clinicians face significant barriers to cognitive screening that contribute to missed diagnoses, especially limited time and resources in busy clinics. 4 Moreover, a fundamental tension exists between the comprehensiveness of screening assessments and patient burden: longer assessments capture more precise information but reduce completion rates and engagement, particularly among older adults. 5 , 6

MyCog Mobile is a novel National Institutes of Health–funded cognitive screener enabling patients to self‐administer measures on their smartphones prior to primary care visits. 7 MyCog Mobile integrates directly with the electronic health record (EHR), efficiently overcoming many common workflow barriers in primary care. A major goal of the MyCog Mobile paradigm is ease and accessibility of remote self‐administration, and thus a brief but sensitive battery is paramount. The primary MyCog Mobile detection algorithm was designed for parsimony and demonstrated strong clinical accuracy (area under the curve [AUC] = .828). 8 The model retained three cognitive measures—MySorting (executive function), two MyFaces subtests (associative memory), and MyPictures (episodic memory), plus years of education.

Although this validation reduced testing time, the remaining battery still requires 15 to 20 minutes, and still includes the MyPictures task, which patients have found frustrating given its length and difficulty. Given that MySorting and MyFaces captured most of the model's predictive information (Wald χ2 ), with all three MyPictures components contributing just 16.4%, we investigated whether MyPictures could be skipped when earlier tasks classified patients with high confidence.

METHODS

The institutional review board at Northwestern University (STU00214921) approved this study. All participants provided informed consent. The sample and data collection procedures are described in detail by Young et al. 8 Briefly, 277 older adults self‐administered MyCog Mobile on study‐provided iPhones under standardized conditions. The MCI group (n = 100; Mage = 74.9 years, standard deviation [SD] = 6.9; 62% male; 90% White, 3% Black; 95% non‐Hispanic; 32% graduate degree) had EHR‐confirmed diagnoses of MCI excluding more advanced dementias. The normal cognitive aging (NCA) group (n = 177; M age = 72.8 years, SD = 4.9; 46% male; 79% White, 17% Black; 84% non‐Hispanic; 19% graduate degree) was screened with the Mini‐Cog (score ≥ 3).

Measures

MyCog Mobile comprises three self‐administered cognitive tasks described in detail elsewhere. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 Briefly, MySorting measures executive function via a dimensional‐change card sorting task. MyFaces assesses associative memory through two subtests. First, there is a learning trial, then after a delay, the tasks include free recall of the first letter of a learned name (First Letter) and matching the correct name to a face (Matching). MyPictures is an episodic memory task requiring participants to encode and then reorder a sequence of images. It includes an 8‐image practice with feedback, followed by two 14‐image live trials without feedback. Live trial scores were combined in the original validation, but here MyPictures was disaggregated into three sequential components—practice, Trial 1, and Trial 2—to allow for incremental stopping points.

Adaptive protocol and evaluation

The full battery protocol used all seven previously validated predictors with MyPictures disaggregated (education, MyFaces First Letter, MyFaces Matching, MySorting, and MyPictures practice, Trial 1, and Trial 2), 8 which achieved comparable discrimination to the original model (AUC = 0.826; calibration slope = 0.91; Youden threshold = 0.332). The clinical threshold was recomputed within each leave‐one‐out (LOO) fold to avoid information leakage; fold‐specific thresholds were stable (median 0.331, range 0.308–0.342).

We used a multi‐stage adaptive protocol in which cognitive tasks were administered in order of their contribution to the original prediction model, starting with the most informative, as determined by the Wald χ2 statistics, as well as characteristics of the task. First, as MyFaces requires a presentation of the faces and names followed by a delay for recall, the stimuli were presented first in the order. Then, the single most informative predictor, MySorting (31.17%), was slotted in the delay, along with the education item, which was relatively less informative (14.00%) but non‐burdensome. The MyFaces subtests (Matching: 19.63% and First Letter: 18.77%) were administered next, followed by the three MyPictures subtests (practice: 10.57%; Trial 1: 3.97%; Trial 2: 1.89%). This ordering is analogous to information‐maximizing selection in computerized adaptive testing. 11 After MySorting and MyFaces, the model combined these scores with education to evaluate whether MyPictures was needed (Figure 1).

For each participant, we sampled 100,000 simulated reference MyPictures profiles with replacement, each a complete set of practice, Trial 1, and Trial 2 scores from a single training participant drawn at random (without conditioning on participant characteristics). Sampling whole profiles preserves the natural correlation among components, while sampling them at random reflects the full empirical range of MyPictures performance. Each MyPictures profile was combined with the held‐out participant's scores on the completed tasks to predict their probability of MCI, yielding a distribution of 100,000 possible probabilities. If ≥ 95% fell on the same side of the clinical threshold, the participant was classified and MyPictures was skipped; otherwise, the MyPictures components were administered sequentially (practice → Trial 1 → Trial 2), with the procedure repeated at each step.

To avoid circularity, we used LOO cross‐validation (CV) to evaluate the protocol. 12 For each participant, the model, reference score distribution, and Youden‐derived clinical threshold were recomputed from the remaining 276 participants within each fold before classifying the held‐out individual. To test model stability, we re‐ran the full LOO procedure across 10 random seeds and quantified the range of exit‐stage counts across seeds.

RESULTS

Discrimination increased cumulatively with each MyPictures component (Figure 1). The initial model (education, MyFaces, MySorting) achieved AUC = 0.811; adding practice, Trial 1, and Trial 2 contributed 0.008, 0.001, and 0.005, respectively, for a collective gain of ≈ 0.014. These are apparent, full‐sample discrimination values describing each component's incremental contribution; they are distinct from the cross‐validated classification performance (Table 1), in which the full‐battery and adaptive protocols achieved LOO‐CV AUCs of 0.802 and 0.795, respectively.

Under LOO‐CV, 92 participants (33.2%) were classified at the initial decision point with 79.3% accuracy relative to reference diagnosis (95% confidence interval [CI: 71.0%, 86.8%]). An additional 71 (25.6%) were classified after the practice item (cumulative accuracy of those classified through this stage: 81.0%), 68 (24.5%) after Trial 1, and 46 (16.6%) required the full battery. In total, 231 of 277 (83.4%) avoided Trial 2. Among those classified at the initial decision point, 52 were truly impaired and 40 truly normal, confirming the adaptive protocol classified participants in both directions. The number of participants requiring the full battery was identical across 10 random seeds with any single stage's count varying by ≤ 6 participants.

Full agreement between adaptive and full‐battery classifications was consistent with the adaptive rule design, and performance metrics relative to reference diagnosis were identical across protocols (Table 1). AUC difference between the models was 0.008 (95% CI [−0.004, 0.021]), slightly in favor of the full battery but with a CI containing zero. The three false negatives at the initial decision point were also misclassified by the full‐battery model, confirming MyPictures would not have changed their classification.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates that adaptive task administration meaningfully reduces testing burden in smartphone‐based cognitive screening without altering classifications produced by the full battery. The adaptive rule classifies a participant only when nearly all plausible MyPictures outcomes point to the same diagnosis, so high agreement with the full battery is built into the design; the empirical question is how often the rule reaches that level of certainty. For 33% of older adults, the initial tasks (MySorting, MyFaces, and education) provided sufficient information to classify MCI status, making completion of MyPictures unnecessary. Only 17% of participants needed to complete the entire battery. The cost of skipping MyPictures is small (ΔAUC = 0.008, 95% CI [−0.004, 0.021]), suggesting the adaptive strategy may be acceptable for MyCog Mobile, a primary care screening tool focused on binary classification for triage. Comprehensive evaluation is still required for differential diagnosis.

The present study builds on the validated MyCog Mobile prediction model, providing an adaptive protocol that requires no additional models, item response theory calibration, nor complex infrastructure, only a decision rule applied to the existing model at each stage. This task‐level approach is particularly suited to established screeners like MyCog Mobile, in which modifying existing measures for item‐level adaptive testing is not feasible (e.g., 30 fixed trials for MySorting). While MyPictures contributed only modest incremental information for classification, it may improve precision in larger samples and may still be important for domain‐specific memory concerns.

We present a small companion validation study which strengthens the findings reported by Young et al., 8 and warrants consideration of several limitations. We used the same dataset for model development and evaluation; we used LOO‐CV to mitigate risks of circularity, yet this design does not establish real‐world performance. As discussed in the primary validation study, 8 the sample was relatively highly educated and not fully representative of diverse primary care populations. The reference standard relied on EHR‐extracted diagnoses, with absence of diagnosis and Mini‐Cog scores the only criteria for normal cognitive status. Prospective validation in which the adaptive battery is administered in real time, using a large and diverse sample of participants evaluated with standardized diagnostic procedures is needed to confirm true clinical performance.

In conclusion, adaptive task administration offers a practical approach to reducing testing burden in multi‐task cognitive screening paradigms like MyCog Mobile. These findings support precision screening in which cognitive assessments adapt to individual patient needs, reducing testing burden and facilitating engagement with remote, self‐administered screening paradigms.
