New legal category of ‘advanced regenerative medicine treatment’ in Korea’s amended regenerative medicine law: comparative lessons from Japan
Authors: Jinah Yoon, Sukhyang Lee, Jung-Hyun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSouth Korea’s Act on the Safety of and Support for Advanced Regenerative Medicine and Advanced Biological Products, enacted in 2019, was recently amended. This article examines the new advanced regenerative medicine treatment (ARMT) category, focusing on patient access, review processes, safety monitoring, ethical safeguards, and lessons from Japan’s comparable framework.

Introduction

In South Korea, the Act on the Safety of and Support for Advanced Regenerative Medicine and Advanced Biological Products was enacted in 2019 to promote access to advanced regenerative therapies under centralized governmental oversight. However, the original framework was limited to clinical research, resulting in limited implementation. To improve patient and developer access, the law was amended to introduce a new legal category, advanced regenerative medicine treatment (ARMT), which allows investigational regenerative medicine to be provided in clinical practice at approved institutions, with patients being charged. This study examines the newly implemented ARMT framework in Korea and analyzes its regulatory design and anticipated implications, drawing comparative lessons from Japan’s experience under the ASRM for the sustainable and responsible integration.

Global regulatory approaches for accelerating patient access to regenerative medicine

Considering the high level of innovation and therapeutic potential of regenerative medicine, the market authorization process may delay patients’ access to these treatments. Therefore, global regulatory bodies established fast-tracks to accelerate patient’s access. In the United States, the 21st Century Cures Act enables the designation of certain therapies such as regenerative medicine advanced therapy, allowing products supported by early-phase clinical data to enter accelerated or priority review pathways and receive expedited approval, with post-market real-world evidence used to confirm long-term safety and efficacy (Chhina et al., 2022; Lee, 2025). In addition, the HCT/P regulatory framework distinguishes between Section 361 HCT/Ps, which may be used clinically without pre-market approval if they meet strict criteria, such as minimal manipulation, homologous use, and no systemic effect, except in autologous or closely related contexts. In Europe, the ATMP Hospital Exemption provides a controlled access route for ATMPs manufactured on a non-routine basis, customized for individual patients or small populations, and administered within the same member state hospital under the supervision of a medical specialist. Conditional Marketing Authorization mechanisms also facilitate earlier patient access to innovative therapies. In this framework, marketing approval may be granted based on less comprehensive clinical data than is typically required for full marketing authorization, provided that the immediate availability of the therapy outweighs the uncertainties stemming from the limited evidence base (Chhina et al., 2022; Lee, 2025; Yoon et al., 2024). In Japan, the Act on the Safety of Regenerative Medicine (ASRM), together with the revised Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Act, implements a risk-tiered regulatory framework (Classes I–III) and allows a conditional and time-limited approval system for regenerative medical products, under which lower-risk or medium-risk therapies are reviewed by certified private committees and delivered in authorized medical institutions. In Korea, the Act on the Safety of and Support for Advanced Regenerative Medicine and Advanced Biological Products (Advanced Regenerative Bio Act) was designed to promote clinical access to advanced therapies. Advanced regenerative medicine refers to medical interventions using human cells or related materials, such as cell therapy, gene therapy, tissue-engineered therapy, or combined therapies intended to regenerate, restore, or form human body structures or functions or to treat or prevent disease (MOHW, 2019). The clinical research track in the original version of the law and the newly introduced advanced regenerative medicine treatment (ARMT) track in the amended version of the law enable efficient access to regenerative therapies for patients with serious and intractable diseases (Yoon et al., 2024).

New category of ‘advanced regenerative medicine treatment’ in the amended Advanced Regenerative Bio Act in Korea

The Advanced Regenerative Bio Act (MOHW, 2019) was designed to promote the development of cutting-edge advanced regenerative medicine technologies, while ensuring ethical and scientific oversight. Initially, the law established only a clinical research framework for advanced regenerative medicine, applying it to a limited subset of patients with serious, life-threatening diseases for which no alternative treatments exist and to patients with rare diseases (as defined in the Rare Disease Control Act). Advanced regenerative medicine (Article 2) refers to procedures prescribed by the Presidential Decree, such as cell therapy, gene therapy, and tissue-engineered therapy, that utilize human cells or related materials to regenerate, restore, or form human bodily structures or functions, or to treat or prevent diseases. Under the original clinical research framework, advanced regenerative medicine could be administered solely within approved research protocols (Article 12), and patients could not be charged for receiving investigational treatments (Article 3). As of November 2025, only 50 clinical research plans had been approved (Supplementary Table 1) (MOHW, 2025; Portal(K-ARM), 2025). Most interventions were cell-based therapies administered in advanced general hospitals.

To improve patient access, the Advanced Regenerative Bio Act was revised in February 2024 (MOHW, 2024b) and took effect in February 2025, with the dual aim of facilitating the clinical uptake of regenerative medicine more practically and enhancing patient safety. The major changes included: (1) extending the scope of clinical research to cover all treatments and not just those serving patients with a rare and life-threatening or severely debilitating condition with no current treatment option, and (2) introducing a new legal category, “ARMT” (Article 2). ARMT refers to the use of advanced regenerative medicine as part of medical procedures and clinical treatment, as defined in Article 12 of the Medical Service Act (MOHW, 2024a). This implies that ARMT applies cell therapy, gene therapy, and tissue-engineered therapy utilizing human cells or related materials during medical procedures. Unlike the clinical research framework, which is investigational and subject to strict cost prohibitions, the ARMT category allows providers to charge patients for procedures. In South Korea’s National Health Insurance system, medical services are classified as either reimbursable, partially covered with patient cost sharing, or non-reimbursable, requiring full out-of-pocket payment. ARMT is classified as a non-reimbursable item under regulations on the criteria for Health Insurance Benefits (Article 9(1)). Therefore, patients are responsible for covering the full cost of the treatment. Authorized institutions are permitted to apply for approved ARMT and charge patients for 5 years (Article 12(2)). To extend this, institutions must resubmit their treatment plans for review and reapproval (Figure 1). Overall, ARMT is intended to function primarily as investigational but, a non-reimbursed clinical practice within licensed hospitals.

Explanation of the ARMT review process

Compared with the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS) market approval track, ARMT shortens the pathway to market access.

Review the process of the treatment plans

Under this revised regulatory framework, ARMT is administered by institutions designated as Advanced Regenerative Medicine Providers (Article 10). These institutions must submit a detailed treatment plan outlining the purpose, methods, and safety management strategies of the proposed therapy to the Ministry of Health and Welfare (Article 10). The submitted plan undergoes a comprehensive review process, which varies depending on the type of therapy, by the Committee for Deliberation on Advanced Regenerative Medicine and Advanced Biopharmaceuticals (Article 13, enforcement decree Article 15) (MOHW, 2025). Only plans approved by the review committee can proceed with implementation. The review committee consists of 25 members, including medical professionals, scientists, and experts in ethics and social issues (Article 13). In addition, the subcommittee, called the expert committee, has up to 40 members, comprising experts in gene and cell therapy, tissue-engineered therapy, and clinical medicine) (MOHW, 2025). The committee will review the technical points of the plans before the deliberation committee reviews them (Enforcement Decree Article 15) (MOHW, 2025).

Risk classification of advanced regenerative medicine treatment

ARMT can be administered to patients with life-threatening or intractable diseases, including rare diseases, as defined by the Rare Disease Control Act (Article 2(6)) (MOHW, 2017). ARMT is classified into three categories based on the level of risk posed to human life and health (Article 2(5)): (1) high risk, referring to treatments with potentially significant or uncertain effects on human life and health, such as human embryonic stem cells or induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cells; (2) moderate risk, referring to treatments with potential adverse effects requiring substantial caution, such as manipulated autologous cells (except minimally manipulated autologous cells), minimally manipulated allogenic cells or artificial tissue for the regeneration; and (3) low risk, referring to treatments with well-established safety and minimal health risks, such as minimally manipulated autologous cells. The requirements for submitting a treatment plan vary according to the risk level. For high-risk procedures, a treatment plan may be submitted for review only when clinical research with the same purpose and content has already been completed, and only the regenerative medicine institution that conducted that research is eligible to apply. For medium-risk procedures, prior clinical research with the same purpose and content must have been completed, but eligibility to apply extends not only to the institution that conducted the research but also to other institutions. By contrast, for low-risk procedures, a treatment plan may be submitted for review even in the absence of previously completed clinical research (Figure 1). This means that it is not mandatory for the applying institution to have conducted the prior clinical research itself; if sufficient supporting literature exists from other groups, the treatment may still be eligible for application.

Safety monitoring and adverse event reporting

Authorized institutions are required to implement safety monitoring to analyze the occurrence and trends of adverse effects and report the results to the Ministry of Health and Welfare (Articles 20 and 21). Article 21 of the Act provides provisions on safety management and long-term follow-up only for clinical research; however, there is no requirement for the long-term follow-up of ARMT interventions. Instead, under Article 13(4) of the Act and Article 14(2) of the Enforcement Decree, the Review Committee may request the submission of progress data on ARMT interventions, and institutions are required to submit interim and final reports that include an analysis and evaluation of the safety and effectiveness of the treatments administered. In addition, to ensure patient safety, institutions must retain all records related to the treatment process, including electronic documents that must be uploaded to the Safety Management Information System, in accordance with Article 23(1) of the Enforcement Decree and Article 5(1) of the Enforcement Rules (Article 3(2) and Draft Treatment Plan Guidelines) (MOHW, 2025; Portal(K-ARM), 2025).

Under Article 20 of the Act and as stipulated in the Draft Guidelines (Article 3(3)), institutions are also obligated to cooperate with requests from the Safety Management Agency, including safety monitoring, adverse event reporting, and other measures necessary to protect the safety of treatment recipients. Overall, the revision of the Advanced Regenerative Bio Act has expanded patient accessibility while enhancing patient safety by strengthening oversight and regulatory mechanisms.

Ethical and patient information considerations

In accordance with statutory requirements, any ARMT implementation plan must secure informed consent using a standardized consent form at the time of human cell or tissue collection. The consent document must explicitly describe (i) the intended purpose of using the cells or tissues to be obtained, (ii) the specific type and quantity to be collected, (iii) the retention period for both the collected materials and any cellular products manufactured therefrom, and (iv) whether the collected materials may be transferred to other institutions or used for purposes beyond the initially approved scope. Furthermore, a separate informed consent form for treatment recipients must be prepared pursuant to Articles 11-2(1) and 11-2(4) of the Act, including details of the measures to protect personal information. This consent form must clearly specify the nature of the therapeutic intervention, indicated condition(s), expected benefits, treatment procedure, duration, and associated costs. All consent forms obtained must be retained by the implementing medical institution for a minimum of 10 years, as required under Article 8-2 of the Enforcement Rule. However, the current regulatory framework does not mandate informing patients that the intervention constitutes an unapproved and investigational treatment, potentially limiting transparency regarding its regulatory status. Accordingly, there is a global opinions that patients need to be informed that if the interventions are investigational, administered outside the formal clinical trial framework, and involve market-unapproved medicine (YOKOHAMA Declaration 2025 and ISSCR, 2025).

Potential implications and points to consider regarding the new advanced regenerative medicine treatment category compared with Japan

The Japanese ASRM has been in effect for the past decade and served as the model for Korea’s ASRM track of the Advanced Regenerative Bio Act. ASRM aims to set structural requirements that clearly distinguish non-scientific, unproven stem cell therapy from responsible medical practice so that patients, governments, and industry can access scientifically valid care. However, Japan has encountered several challenges in implementing and operating the ASRM framework (Konomi et al., 2015). A report by Ikka et al. described potential structural weaknesses in the review system in Japan’s Ministry of Health and Labor for moderate-risk cell therapies, primarily involving the use of autologous mesenchymal stem cells or somatic cells (Ikka et al., 2024). The current Japanese review system permits the approval of therapies despite unresolved scientific and safety concerns. For example, physicians may submit provision plans outside their areas of specialization, relying on limited scientific evidence to support safety claims, and committee members with potential conflicts of interest may review the plans. The report also noted procedural deficiencies, including duplicative documentation requirements and application language that overstated the robustness of the supporting data. In addition, a recent study revealed significant gaps in the scientific and clinical expertise among review committee members (Hosoya et al., 2025; Yokomizo et al., 2025). Unlike Japan, where regenerative medicine review committees are organized by individual medical institutions, except for ‘class 1 (highest risk) therapies, which must also be reviewed by a centralized review committee of the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare (MHLW), Korea adopted a centralized governance model. This approach was intended to prevent potentially inconsistent evaluation of treatment plans by establishing a centralized 25-member review committee with broad authority. The review committee was tasked with evaluating supporting evidence for the safety and efficacy of proposed treatments, including (1) existing data and outcomes from similar regenerative medicine procedures, (2) systematic literature reviews, and (3) clinical trial-related data. Although the Korean review committee is centralized, the review process can be improved. The reviewer needs to evaluate the evidence submitted for safety and efficacy assessments to meet standards required under global regulatory guidelines, such as those issued by the ICH-GCP, Food and Drug Administration, or European Medicines Agency. To do that, the review committee needs expertise on regulatory science, typically expected of competent national regulatory authorities. In addition, more detailed and robust guidelines for evaluating safety and efficacy are required to be developed and implemented to improve the standardized regulatory review process in accordance with regulatory body standards. We can consider the recommendation of Takashima et al. that risk classification be updated to include factors beyond cell source, such as the administration site and the risks associated with surgical procedures and delivery, in developing standardized regulatory review guidelines (Takashima et al., 2021).

Furthermore, if clinical data from ARMT are intended to serve as supporting evidence for further development and marketing the authorization of advanced therapy products in Korea, we consider that safety and efficacy data, as well as clinical data, must clearly meet the subsequent requirement of clinical trials under formal regulatory pathways. According to the Guidelines for the Review and Preparation of Advanced Regenerative Medicine Treatment Plans issued by the Korean MOHW, treatment plans across all risk categories—high, moderate, and low risk—are required to include evidence on safety and efficacy derived from systematically collected literature, and the results of previously conducted clinical studies. However, it is not mandatory that all types of non-clinical test data be submitted. Depending on the characteristics of each treatment, selected appropriate testing methods can adequately demonstrate safety and efficacy (Office of the Advanced Regenerative Medicine and Advanced Biopharmaceuticals Review Committee, 2025). We consider that the assessment of safety and efficacy data must be comprehensive regarding the quality of the product and patient’s safety. In addition, the accumulation of real-world clinical safety data over years of ARMT implementation will strengthen the safety and efficacy evidence of the ARMT, which lead to market authorization smoothly.

Conclusions

Since the enactment of Japan’s Act on the Safety of Regenerative Medicine (ASRM) in 2014, more than 1,000 regenerative medicine provision plans have been approved, and over 10,000 patients have received such treatments annually (ISSCR, 2025). If South Korea follows a similar trajectory under its amended Advanced Regenerative Bio Act, low- and moderate-risk regenerative therapies are likely to expand at a significantly faster rate, thereby extending potential benefits to a broader patient population. Importantly, however, Japan’s framework has come under increasing scrutiny, with growing calls for regulatory reform to strengthen patient protection. Notably, on the day that Korea’s amended Advanced Regenerative Bio Act—closely resembling Japan’s ASRM—came into effect, the International Society for Stem Cell Research (ISSCR) submitted a formal letter to the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labor, and Welfare outlining critical concerns regarding Japan’s current framework (ISSCR, 2025). In this letter, the ISSCR Scientific Committee recommended strengthening oversight of the review process for regenerative medicine provision plans and called for more rigorous and cautious approval procedures. For the successful implementation of ARMT in South Korea, based on lessons learned from the Japanese ASRM, sustained efforts to ensure comprehensive scientific review, transparent governance, long-term post-treatment follow-up, safety data management, and patient awareness of the non-market-approved status of the therapy with reimbursement to patients will be essential for ARMT to fulfill its original legislative intent of bringing regenerative therapies closer to patients.
