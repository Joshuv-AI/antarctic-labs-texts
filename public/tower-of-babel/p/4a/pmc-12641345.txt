Contemporary Surgical Management of Colorectal Cancer in Lynch Syndrome: Considering the Implications of Known Genotype
Authors: Ashley Jenkin, Kim‐Chi Phan‐Thien
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

It is associated with an elevated lifetime CRC risk of 30%–80% and predisposes individuals to various extra‐colonic malignancies. Background

A diagnosis of Lynch Syndrome (LS) has significant implications for surgical management and surveillance. As our understanding of the genetic basis of the condition expands, so should our capacity to tailor surgical options, always keeping the patient at the center.

Lynch syndrome is the most common hereditary colorectal cancer (CRC) predisposing syndrome, and accounts for 10%–25% of familial CRC [1]. LS is an autosomal dominant germline variant affecting the DNA mismatch repair (MMR) genes MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, or PMS2. This genetic change results in microsatellite instability (MSI), defined by changes in the length of repetitive DNA sequences within neoplastic tissue compared to normal, a marker of the impacted cell's inability to self‐repair [2]. The MMR genes exist as heterodimers where the dominant genes (MLH1 and MSH2) are stabilized by the ‘mino’ genes (PMS2 and MSH6, respectively) [1, 3]. The “mino” genes rely on the dominant for protein expression, and pathogenic variants in MSH6 and PMS2 tend to result in a lower risk of malignancy [1, 4]. LS can also manifest indirectly through the deletion of the EPCAM gene and the resultant methylation of the adjacent MSH2 promoter. MLH1, in contrast, is more commonly inactivated by promoter hypermethylation without germline mutation, resulting in sporadic MSI in up to 30% of patients with CRC and a clinical picture inconsistent with LS [1, 3]. MMR genes are also subject to physiological regulation with variable expression across cell types [1]. Thus, not all patients with MSI‐high tumors will be a result of LS, and the LS phenotype can vary depending on the pathogenic variants [1, 5, 6].

LS is responsible for 1%–3% of all CRC diagnoses and results in a lifetime risk of CRC of 30%–80% [7, 8]. CRC in LS tends to occur at a younger age than sporadic cases, with a trend toward more proximal tumors and accelerated carcinogenesis [1, 8, 9]. Fortunately, although LS is associated with poorly differentiated CRC with mucoid or signet‐cell features, patients with LS tend to have improved survival compared to sporadic CRC [1]. While the main clinical consequence of LS is CRC, the resultant high volume of mutations resulting from a failure in genetic repair across cell types also increases the risk of extra‐colonic malignancies, including endometrial, ovarian, gastric, small bowel, urinary tract, pancreatic, brain, and cutaneous gland tumors [10, 11, 12, 13].

Screening

Access to MMR deficiency screening in Australia has improved, reducing clinicians' reliance on clinical diagnostic criteria to detect LS [9, 14, 15, 16]. In fact, if clinical criteria were still used in isolation for the detection and diagnosis of LS, the majority of affected patients would be missed [13, 17, 18]. This may be a product of smaller family sizes or the result of LS creating a constellation of phenotypes with many patients and families demonstrating atypical patterns [17]. Routine testing on all colorectal and endometrial specimens is recommended at any age, as endorsed by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network Guidelines and others [19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. In Australia, this is most commonly performed through immunohistochemistry, followed by BRAFV600E somatic testing if MLH1 deficiency is demonstrated [14, 17]. Patients without evidence of MLH1‐promoter methylation on further assessment are then referred for somatic genetic testing and counseling [2, 14]. The frequency of germline variants with non‐selective testing has been shown to be 2%–4%, with de novo pathogenic variants being even less common [17, 19]. This nonselective screening strategy has however been reported to be both sensitive and cost‐effective in diagnosing LS [2, 14, 15, 17, 26].

Impact of Routine Mismatch Repair Deficiency Screening

Since screening for MMR deficiency and MSI became routine on CRC specimens, the resultant increase in surveillance has reduced the incidence of CRC and mortality associated with LS over time [2, 14, 17, 27, 28, 29]. Furthermore, the advanced ability to correlate genotype and phenotype in LS based on germline testing implies an improved ability to tailor surveillance and surgical management according to the specific genetic alteration and family history [27]. For CRC in Australia, initial MMR testing is performed on the endoscopic tumor biopsy specimens where possible, which has implications for surgical planning with a role for expedited somatic testing [13, 18]. These formal results can facilitate pre‐operative genetic counselling before the definitive procedure, which can influence surgical management.

Surgical Planning

The surgical management of LS is increasingly nuanced, and segmental versus extended resection remains a topical area of discussion [9, 30, 31]. The surgical imperative is to limit the risk of cancer‐related death while managing the risk of future malignancy, with the goal of minimizing impact on quality of life by reducing bowel dysfunction. LS is currently considered a spectrum of disease, the idiosyncrasies of which are difficult to capture within international guidelines posed to provide a broad, informed direction rather than an adaptable recommendation for individual patient care.

Resection Type and Risk of Metachronous Cancer

Patients with LS have an increased risk of metachronous cancers following segmental resection compared to extended colectomy, with reported rates of 10%–45% at 10–15 years [1, 6, 9, 30, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39]. This risk increases over time and is estimated to be 62% at 30 years post‐resection [35]. The mean time to developing metachronous cancer following segmental surgery is approximately 5 years [33, 40] and may be higher in patients with left‐sided tumors as the index presentation [36]. The relationship between metachronous CRC risk and disease or patient characteristics has been challenged [35, 41]. Although meta‐analysis suggests extended resection is associated with a significantly reduced relative risk of metachronous cancer, no significant difference has been shown in mortality [30, 37, 42]. The risk of further cancer of the rectum remains 10% at 10 years, and annual surveillance of the residual rectum is still required [32, 33, 34, 35, 38, 43]. For patients presenting with a primary rectal cancer, there is a similarly high rate of metachronous colon cancer following proctosigmoidectomy [8, 41]. However, total proctocolectomy is far more technically complex, particularly in the context of neoadjuvant radiation, and has poorer functional outcomes [8, 41, 43]. The risk of metachronous CRC can be reduced by up to 31% for every 10 cm of colon removed [44], but this needs to be considered in the context of the primary's location, pre‐existing sphincter function, age, and comorbidities of the patient, and predicted compliance with the recommended post‐operative surveillance [9, 30, 35, 43, 44, 45]. Most international guidelines continue to recommend total abdominal colectomy with deference to increased patient age or underlying sphincter dysfunction (see Table 1) [12, 13, 20, 22, 25], and extended resection is certainly endorsed once metachronous cancer develops [20, 22].

Resection Type and Functional Outcomes

Extended resection can have a negative impact on functional outcomes [48], though quality of life does not seem to vary significantly between surgical approaches [6, 43, 48, 49, 50]. As a result, previous recommendations have supported a more aggressive resection approach with total abdominal colectomy and ileorectal anastomosis as prophylaxis against the development of further malignancies, particularly for young patients [27, 37, 51, 52]. Younger patients are more likely to adapt to the increased stooling with better‐preserved continence than older patients [8, 9, 48]. Rectal preservation is still worth considering in this group where possible, as ileoanal pouch anastomosis has a higher risk of urinary incontinence, sexual dysfunction, and reduced fecundity than an ileorectal anastomosis [8, 48]. Thoughtful consideration in offering such extensive surgery is worthwhile in younger populations where the prophylactic benefit of preventing further cancer is stronger, but the negative functional consequences have an arguably more profound impact on quality of life [8, 43, 52]. The impact of metachronous CRC and its diminished impact on overall survival makes functional outcomes increasingly relevant [6, 30, 37, 42]. Patients are increasingly aging with their Lynch syndrome diagnosis, surviving multiple operations for Lynch‐associated cancers, without the additional toll of chemotherapy, making risk‐reducing surgery less necessary [4]. This is particularly relevant with respect to the specific genetic variant a patient carries, as their overall risk of metachronous CRC is not equal (see Table 2) [5]. The latest Mallorca guidelines recommend more conservative surgery via segmental resection for MSH6 or PMS2 carriers for primary CRC [22], which is a better reflection of current perspectives on the role for gene‐specific recommendations, but this is not currently common to other international guidelines [12, 25, 44].

Surveillance

Colonoscopy remains the main secondary prevention technique of CRC in LS [4, 40, 45, 54, 55, 56]. Approximately 80% of patients who develop CRC in LS under regular surveillance will develop the cancer within 2–3 years following their preceding colonoscopy [4, 6]. Most guidelines internationally recommend endoscopic assessment every 1–2 years based on evidence suggesting earlier diagnosis of CRC [12, 13, 20, 23, 25, 29, 57]. Increased surveillance does not necessarily reduce the incidence of metachronous cancer following segmental resection, but paradoxically may increase it [4, 39, 43, 49, 54, 58, 59]. The high rates of cancer development at short intervals may be associated with the aforementioned accelerated carcinogenetic process demonstrated in LS, or attributed to cancers developing directly as invasive malignancy rather than through a traditional adenoma‐carcinoma pathway with polyp formation [4, 54]. This is thought to occur despite normal‐appearing mucosa through MMR‐deficient crypt foci that are undetectable at endoscopy and at histopathology unless MMR protein staining is performed [3, 54]. This suggests that colonoscopic screening may not be able to detect a resectable precursor lesion, which limits the usefulness of more frequent colonoscopy. Fortunately, despite high rates of cancer detection in LS, overall survival is preserved [4, 5, 29].

The quality of mucosal interrogation may also vary between centres [45, 56, 59, 60]. Factors such as adenoma‐detection rate (ADR), quality of mechanical bowel preparation, withdrawal time, caecal intubation rate, and polypectomy technique, among others, contribute to this variability [56]. Although it is believed that not all LS‐associated CRC arise from a discernible precursor adenoma, polyps in LS, when found, tend to exhibit high‐grade dysplasia more frequently and at a smaller size (< 5 mm), making adequate ADR increasingly relevant [56]. Missed adenomas are not uncommon and represent a plausible explanation for the high rate of developing CRC in LS between colonoscopies [56, 61, 62]; though there are no studies thus far demonstrating that a high ADR prevents CRC in LS. The relevance of missed polyps and the increased progression to carcinoma likely also varies based on the pathogenic variant the patient carries [56]. As further information is available with respect to the pathogenesis of cancer formation in LS, particularly in the context of the varied LS phenotypes, the role and timing of colonoscopic surveillance may change.

Tailoring Surveillance Recommendations

Colonoscopy is an invasive procedure and is not without risk [63, 64, 65]. Surveillance recommendations may also need to be tailored to reflect the age and gender of the patient, previous tumor and polyp burden, operative management, and potentially the specific genetic variant found on somatic testing [4, 56, 59, 66, 67, 68]. Australian recommendations in LS suggest patients with MSH6 or PMS2 variants begin surveillance at 35 years or 5 years before the earliest diagnosed CRC in a relative; 10 years later than those with MLH1 or MSH2 pathogenic variants [12]. This is endorsed by international guidelines (see Table 1) [13, 20, 22, 25, 46]. Shorter intervals of less than second‐yearly endoscopic surveillance have not demonstrated a clinical benefit, including to the stage of CRC diagnosed [4, 54, 59]. LS‐related tumors are strongly immunogenic, and more frequent endoscopic assessment may detect lesions that would otherwise be cleared by the immune system or regress, which may predispose to over‐treatment [54, 69, 70, 71, 72]. Reducing the required surveillance is also an important consideration in some patients where geographic or social constraints limit access to endoscopy, in which case extended surgical resection may be considered [39, 45, 73, 74]. Previous cancer diagnosis has been demonstrated to be a strong predictor of compliance with endoscopic surveillance, as well as surveillance for extra‐colonic malignancies [74, 75, 76].

Surveillance Timeframes

Extended periods without colonoscopic surveillance at intervals beyond 3–3.5 years are not currently recommended due to the aforementioned high rate of cancer detection in this patient cohort [4, 20, 40, 54, 55, 59]. Prolonged periods of time between screening may also erode compliance with recommended surveillance protocols [74, 75]. Previous reports have suggested poor adherence to LS surveillance guidelines in Australia [77]. The Mallorca guidelines, however, suggest a reduced frequency for all carriers of LS, with 2–3‐yearly colonoscopy, with carriers of PMS2 variants in particular undergoing only 5‐yearly colonoscopy [22]. This surveillance protocol, while arguably more representative of current perspectives on the influence of gene‐specific recommendations, is currently not supported in Australia [12]. This reduced surveillance does not apply to patients with a previous history of LS‐associated malignancy [22]. There are currently also no variations recommended between men and women with LS [4, 12, 20, 22, 25, 68].

Phenotypic Considerations

A patient's particular genetic variant has also been shown to be highly relevant to surgical management; the pattern of genetic change predicts the patient's risk of malignancy (see Table 2) [4, 5, 6, 27, 41]. A cancer risk algorithm is available to predict the remaining lifetime risk of Lynch‐associated cancers specific to the patient's age, gender, and genetic variant on the Prospective Lynch Syndrome database website (www.plsd.eu) [58]. MLH1 and MSH2 are the most commonly affected variants and result in a substantially higher risk of CRC at a younger age compared to carriers of other pathogenic variants [4, 6, 27]. Unfortunately, many CRCs detected occur in LS despite colonoscopic surveillance, but this has not resulted in worse survival compared to sporadic cases, nor a crude survival difference between genetic variants [4, 6, 39, 43, 49, 54, 58, 59]. Overall, the higher risk of developing a metachronous cancer in LS is largely attributed to patients with MLH1 and MSH2 variants specifically [30]. MSH2 carriers are at particularly high risk of metachronous cancers [37, 40], with MSH6 and PMS2 variants experiencing a markedly lower risk [5, 66, 67, 78]. In fact, CRC in patients with MSH6 may appear phenotypically more similar to sporadic cases [66, 67], and patients with PMS2 variants tend to follow an adenomatous pathway of carcinogenesis [22, 79]. This impacts surveillance and surgical recommendations with consideration for a longer surveillance interval and less extensive surgery in the setting of lower risk variants (see Figure 1) [31, 56, 66, 78]. The link between genetic variants and surveillance schedule requires further study [4, 54, 59]. Furthermore, while surveillance recommendations are starting to reflect this shift to gene‐specific recommendations internationally, resection is still often endorsed with a blanket approach to extended surgery [12, 13, 20, 23, 25]. This persists despite there currently being no high‐quality evidence to support extended resection in patients with MSH6 or PMS2 variants, as noted in the Mallorca guidelines [22]. Family history, age at first Lynch‐related cancer diagnosis, use of chemoprophylaxis, and environmental and behavioral exposures also influence the development of CRC in LS and can result in substantial variability in risk, highlighting the importance of a tailored approach to surgical management [4, 22, 31, 78].

Future Directions

The surgical management of CRC in LS does not occur in isolation, and as medical management and surveillance techniques improve, the role for extended resections will become more limited. The role of chemoprophylaxis in LS may be a means of risk reduction that may absolve the need for more extensive surgery. Thus far, high‐dose aspirin has been suggested to result in risk reduction with a 10‐year latency period [80, 81]; but further study is required to determine the optimal dose, duration, and timing [80, 81]. Immunotherapy in MMR‐deficient patients with CRC may also shift the surgical decision‐making away from more aggressive surgery, but evidence for its use in non‐metastatic disease is limited [82, 83, 84, 85]. Furthermore, endoscopic detection techniques continue to improve with the use of narrowband imaging, chromoendoscopy, and prolonged withdrawal times [44, 60, 61, 62, 86, 87, 88]. The use of artificial intelligence programs to aid in adenoma detection has demonstrated mixed but promising results and remains an area of further study [56, 89, 90, 91]. Surveillance in LS may be shown to be more effective in preventing primary and metachronous CRC in the future, again limiting the value of extended surgical resection. Other biomarkers in blood and stool for adenomas and CRC in LS are being investigated, though no consistently accurate test has yet been identified [45]. As phenotypic profiling in LS continues, many of these recommendations for chemoprophylaxis, surveillance, and neo/adjuvant therapies may become increasingly gene‐specific as well.

Conclusion

Lynch syndrome can no longer be considered a single disease entity, and the pattern of genetic change is predictive of risk. Patient‐tailored treatment needs to extend beyond functional outcomes and also consider the specific cancer risk for both primary and metachronous CRC based on gene‐specific recommendations. Surveillance patterns should also reflect this patient‐focused, gene‐specific pathway. In this way, many patients would benefit from a more conservative resectional approach than previously supported.
