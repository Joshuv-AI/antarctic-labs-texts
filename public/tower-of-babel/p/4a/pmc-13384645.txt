The Interface of Artificial Intelligence and the Electronic Medical Record in Orthopaedic Surgery: Current Applications and Future Directions
Authors: Logan M. Good, Matthew R. Magro, Jeremy M. Adelstein, Margaret A. Sinkler, Abigail M. Braden, John M. Apostolakos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Artificial intelligence (AI) has a broad impact on health care across orthopaedic surgery and numerous other subspecialties through various applications, including the enhancement of diagnostic imaging, robotic assisted surgeries, and rehabilitation protocols. More recently, AI-powered electronic medical record integration with platforms, such as DeepScribe, Augmedix, Suki, Abridge, Nuance Dragon Ambient eXperience, and Clinical Notes AI, have enabled hospital systems to enhance their operational value while streamlining patient care and reducing burnout for clinicians. Although these systems can notably reduce clinician workload while accurately and efficiently transcribing clinical encounters, concerns exist regarding patient confidentiality and data encryption, cost, and ease of integration within healthcare systems. Further studies are needed to elucidate the long-term impact and outlook of AI healthcare integration, particularly within the electronic medical record system.

Artificial intelligence (AI) has rapidly transformed health care since its integration in the 1990s, affecting patient care through a multifaceted approach.1,2 The utilization of AI through diagnostic imaging, robot-assisted surgeries, and rehabilitation applications has been well described in many different medical and surgical subspecalities.3-6 However, more recently, many hospital systems are attempting to adopt AI technology that enables the use of electronic medical record (EMR) data to enhance their operations and streamline patient care.7,8 Many of these systems use ambient capture and automatic speech recognition to record and process conversations between patient and clinician, converting these interactions into AI-generated clinical encounter documentation.9 Many of these systems also have live, hybrid human medical support and scribes to virtually assist with the documentation and its necessary medical coding and orders.7-9 Given the notable financial and time considerations associated with manual chart review and documentation for patient encounters, it is imperative to further investigate the impact of automated technologies to alleviate these burdens.10,11 The purpose of this review is to outline the current state of AI utilization in the clinical setting across medical and surgical subspecialties, particularly orthopaedic surgery. We then aim to comprehensively review the integration of various AI systems into the EMR to further characterize their impact on patient encounters. Finally, we discuss concerns associated with these AI technologies in health care and highlight future directions for further integration into patient care.

History of AI and Current Applications

The development of AI and machine learning (ML) first began in the 1950s, but more recently, it has emerged into the healthcare sphere since the 1990s.1,2 Implementation of these systems largely stems from the need to automate repetitive tasks and streamline workflows.2 However, this warrants the question—how does AI and ML improve health care? Artificial neural networks (ANNs), the most widely used AI in health care, consist of networks of interconnected computer processors called “neurons” that perform simultaneous tasks to process data.3 ANNs have an exceptional ability to learn from historical data, analyze complex information patterns, and process information, ultimately improving the quality of medical care patients receive.2,3 These networks assist in image analysis, disease detection, general interpretation of medical data, histopathology analysis, and much more.2,3 Although ANNs have a robust scope of practice, some challenges include algorithmic biases, regulatory concerns, human interaction, and human experiences.2

In the current healthcare setting, AI has benefited many different medical subspecialties in various ways. ANNs have been employed in histopathological tumor classification, abdominal pain and appendicitis diagnostics, cytology of various cell types, interpretation of various imaging types including plain radiographs, ultrasonography, CT, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and radioisotope scans among others.2-5 The use of AI has become increasingly used for precise diagnostic imaging and assisting surgeons in decision making based on patient-specific factors.12 AI has also improved intraoperative guidance and enhanced surgical outcomes, patient risk assessments, and predictions of postoperative complications.13 Park et al4 recently reviewed current AI healthcare applications, highlighting the use of AI-powered programs in robotics for surgical subspecialties, continuous patient monitoring and nursing alerts, and, notably, voice recognition for EMR transcription. They concluded that although AI technologies have demonstrated outstanding results in regard to accumulating medical data, diagnosis accuracy, and assisting with treatment decision making, various issues still exist relating to patient privacy and systematic reproducibility and reliability.

AI in Orthopaedic Surgery

AI has also integrated into various subspecialities within orthopaedic surgery, including spine, adult reconstruction, trauma, and oncology.12 Its utilization has become particularly important for precise diagnostics, predicting fragility risk, staging and grading orthopaedic conditions, and assisting surgeons in decision making based on patient-specific factors.12 Muehlematter et al used machine learning to detect the risk of vertebral insufficiency fractures, whereas Tanzi et al designed deep learning approaches for fracture classifications.14,15 Furthermore, an analysis by Wei et al16 with ANN modeling revealed clinically important factors such as preoperative sodium, anemia status, and age among others in predicting patients who can undergo safely undergo same-day rehabilitation and discharge from an outpatient TKA. More implications of AI in orthopaedic surgery include image interpretation of fractures, osteoarthritis, osteoporosis, and benign and malignant lesions.13,17,18 Cheng et al17 used AI to assist in the recognition of hip fractures with an excellent diagnostic performance of 98% accuracy. Do et al18 used an AI-driven model to determine whether specific bone regions around the knee were benign or malignant lesions on plan radiographs, yielding a 99% accuracy for more than 1500 samples. Furthermore, AI networks have been implemented to accurately spatialize complex limb salvage and reconstructive surgeries. Qu et al19 describes a deep learning–based method to segment pelvic tumor based on MRI scans, which was superior to several methods and decreased the workflow for complex surgical planning time from multiple days to only about 15 minutes. These implementations are a few of the many facets in which AI is drastically beginning to impact the orthopaedic surgery landscape.

AI in Clinical Encounters

Although many of these integrations have enhanced the field of orthopaedics, AI integration into the clinical setting for documentation purposes has more recently been introduced. In the past, extensive documentation requirements have has markedly contributed to physician burnout and negatively affected the quality of medical records and patient care.20 The utilization a natural language processing (NLP)–based virtual assistant within the EMR introduces the ability for a virtual assistant to ambiently record and document a clinical encounter, thereby decreasing documentation time, increasing efficiency, and allowing more time for orthopaedic providers to spend with their patients.

Although these prior interfaces have focused on many of the aforementioned applications, more recent literature has introduced the integration of AI in physician-patient dialogue to obtain clinical histories and document physical examinations.21-23 The introduction of the EMR sought to increase efficiency and enhance patient safety, but unintended negative consequences such as reduced efficiency and increasing burnout have occurred as a result of poorly designed interfaces and overburdening documentation requirements.24,25 The EMR requires notable data entry for clinical encounters, which ultimately decreases patient care time and contributes to physician burnout and care inefficiencies.24,26 Literature has demonstrated physicians spend more than half of their clinical time performing EMR documentation, with more than 75% of physicians identifying the EMR as a notable stressor contributing to burnout symptoms.27-29 One study reported 20% faster note-taking and 30% less work after hours, while noting improvement in documentation quality and patient engagement.30 Another 6-week pilot study with 223 participants reported a 40% relative reduction in clinician reported burnout.31

The implementation of AI-driven documentation tools such as NLP has attempted to mitigate this burden and allow the physician to directly focus on patient care while still accurately and efficiently maintaining appropriate documentation standards.32-34 These tools may provide clinicians with the opportunity to avoid long documentation times both in and out of the workplace. Although human scribes have been used in the past for this same purpose, they often present considerably higher costs in salary and training. Many AI-driven documentation tools cost approximately $100 to 200 per month per user, compared with the average medical scribe salary costs of approximately $3000 per month.35 Furthermore, the utilization of these platforms could allow a clinician to converse with a patient while imaging and various visual aids are automatically displayed. A recent study by Feldman et al analyzed the 5C components of note quality (completeness, conciseness, contingency, correctness, clinical assessment and reasoning) with AI in the internal medicine, general surgery, pediatrics, and intensive care (ICU) departments at a single, large academic medical center.36 Overall, outside of the contingency category in the ICU department, they demonstrated that clinical documentation was markedly improved by AI technology in every 5C category for all specialties. Ultimately, these results created a lasting impact with an improvement in care quality and the 5Cs achieving more than 90% compliance one year after implementation.36 Other preliminary studies have concluded some large language models (LLMs) could assist with appropriate physical examination recommendations during clinical encounters.37 Ultimately, AI systems capable of accurately and efficiently documenting the clinician-patient encounter could increase the consistency and quality of patient care while simultaneously reducing clinician errors stemming from information overload, alert fatigue, and complex EMR systems.38

AI-Powered Clinical Documentation Platforms

DeepScribe

DeepScribe (DeepScribe, San Franscisco, CA) is a medical scribe platform using AI, ML, and NLP to capture and transcribe clinical encounters in real time by extracting relevant medical information.39 This platform uses ambient capture and speech recognition to record a clinical encounter between the provider and patient to transcribe clinical documentation, including the history and physical examination. Highlighting their optimization for specialty medicine, including oncology, cardiology, and orthopaedics, the platform integrates with numerous EMR systems including Epic and athenahealth and more recently introduced automated Evaluation and Management (E/M) coding capabilities. However, this program does not advertise any imaging analysis or ordering capabilities.

Augmedix

Augmedix (Augmedics Inc, Arlington Heights, IL) is a virtual scribe that uses automatic speech recognition (ASR) along with NLP and LLMs to generate medical notes for the clinician, highlighting their versatility across many specialties and human-assisted support.40 Various customizations exist including the self-serve option Augmedix Go, which instantaneously drafts a medical note. Augmedix Assist is their hybrid option with including coding and order capabilities, as well as support if needed from a medical documentation specialist (M.D.S.). Augmedix Live incorporates all of these functionalities along with a specialty-trained MDS for the entire clinical shift. Both the Assist and Live options include further capabilities such as the production of discharge instructions and after visit summaries, referral letters, and pending laboratory tests and imaging orders during the clinical encounter.40 All of these options are Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) compliant and integrate with multiple EMR systems including Epic, Cerner, Meditech, and athenahealth among others. Although costs vary between systems, Augmedix is generally considered more expensive due to human-assisted support options.21

Suki

The Suki (Suki AI, Redwood City, CA) platform is another virtual scribe that combines ASR with human scribes to transcribe appropriate medical documentation for clinicians, offering real time support during encounters.41 Their system integrates across many EHR systems and uses NLP, LLMs, and natural language understanding (NLU) to provide dictation, commands, ambient, and coding capabilities in one application with the ultimate goal to lift administrative burden from clinicians. Similar to Augmedix, this platform is often considered more costly due to the lack of pure-AI integration and the need for physician review and finalization. However, similarly, it appears to be a more comprehensive platform supporting more than 100 specialties by recording clinical encounters to generate a history and physical examination as well as execute order placement and coding suggestions.41

Abridge

Abridge (Abridge AI Inc, Pittsburgh, PA) is an AI-powered chart summarization platform using ASR, NLP, and ML to accurately generate documentation notes and order entry for efficient integration into many EHR systems.42 Again, similar to alternative programs, this platform records clinical conversations to generate a clinical summary documenting the history and physical examination. Abridge then summates the visit by suggesting actionable outputs including linked evidence for various interventions that the provider can review and prescribe the patient if deemed necessary. The software highlights an excellent workflow that allows clinicians to review and revise AI-generated documentation before ending each clinical encounter. They highlight upward of a 40% decrease in burnout rate with their product.42 Notably, this software integrates with leading EMRs and is the only company that maps AI-generated summaries, providing clinicians with recommendations they are able to quickly trust and verify.

Nuance Dragon Ambient eXperience (DAX)

Nuance Communications recently launched their version of AI-powered ambient clinical intelligence solution, Dragon Ambient eXperience (DAX) (Nuance Communications, Burlington, MA), which analyzes patient encounters to appropriately transcribe the history and physical examination in clinical encounters in real time, while also integrating across multiple EHR systems to provide coding suggestions automated clinical assistance.43 In addition, this platform is also able to suggest laboratory and imaging orders. However, the software does not advertise to provide any imaging analysis similar to the other platforms. DAX was recently chosen as the top platform in the ambient clinical encounter category, largely due to its streamlined workflow and ability to provide advanced capabilities such as heterogenous note templates, after-visit patient summaries, and referral letters.45

Clinical Notes AI

Clinical Notes AI (Clinically AI, San Diego, CA) is another platform optimized for telehealth visits that generates intake and assessment documents simultaneously and transcribes history and physical examination encounters in real time.44 This platform is advertised as the number one program in the behavioral health space, largely due to its functionality in telehealth capabilities. Although it interfaces with EHR systems including AdvancedMD, Alma, and CharmEHR, in contrast to other platforms, it does not interface with many common alternatives including Epic and Cerner. Furthermore, it appears to have more limited capabilities than other platforms with an inability to order laboratory or imaging requests following clinical encounters.

Limitations and Future Directions

EMR documentation has been identified as a key contributor to clinician burnout, and AI-powered technologies have the capability to alleviate this burden and improve the efficiency of clinical encounters for both providers and patients. However, numerous limitations and deficiencies still exist within the current landscape. A recent study noted that 38 out of 85 (44.7%) of clinicians using an AI-powered documentation tool reported a positive outcome, but still more than 40% did not endorse time-saving benefits or improved EHR experience.46 This could have been due to inadequate familiarity with the system but, importantly, highlights the possible need for standardization among platforms, appropriate response to user feedback for system refinements, and potentially even notable improvements to these systems before widespread integration. There is also concern for inconsistent outputs, omissions, fabrications, and substitutions during clinical documentation that raise concern.47 AI platforms should also serve to augment clinical decision making and enhance personalized medicine for patients, a process that will only improve with continual process assessment and ongoing evaluation.24

In addition, although numerous platforms endorse HIPAA compliance, the utilization of a third party interface in EMR systems introduces the need for heightened patient confidentiality. Furthermore, cybersecurity issues resulting in data breaches emphasizes the importance of robust data encryption and multifactor authentication.48 Cost is another potential limitation of implementing and maintaining an AI-powered platform in a healthcare system, particularly in low-resource settings. Although increasing billing codes or insurance claims is certainly a concern, there currently is no documented evidence that costs will increase for patients. However, estimates suggest that AI can markedly improve healthcare expenditures in the long term, saving up to 25% annually on administrative costs.49 Notably, a recent study further investigated barriers encountered by 13 healthcare leaders when implementing AI for medical history taking and examinations.50 Although this qualitative study mostly portrayed a positive sentiment among interviewees, three domains were highlighted as barriers to successful integration including professional skepticism and resistance, adapting standard units to digital care, and inadequacies in AI application functionality and system integration.50 The leaders suggested that adequate training, organizational integration of digital workshops, and anticipating long-term AI developments and advancements will seek to mitigate these concerns and improve implementation.50

Conclusion

Although still in its early stages, AI has begun to drastically impact the landscape of orthopaedic surgery and the healthcare system at large, ultimately improving patient diagnostics, treatment, and rehabilitative care. Although its implantation has demonstrated notable utilization across various subspecialities and phases of care, AI shows considerable applications in clinical encounter documentation by streamlining workflows and automating tasks to reduce burnout. Although these AI-driven processes provide the means of optimizing clinician workload while maintaining accurate and effective documentation strategies, limitations including patient confidentiality and data breeches, cost, and skepticism regarding inadequacies of functionality and system integration exist. Further studies are needed to elucidate the long-term impact and outlook of the AI healthcare interface, particularly related to EMR integration.
