Association between perioperative metabolic disorders and disease time course with pyloric morphological characteristics in infants with infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis
Authors: Baohong Zhao, Bingliang Li, Hongxia Ren, Rao Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo identify risk factors for perioperative metabolic disorders in infants with infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis (IHPS) and examine their associations with disease time course and ultrasound-measured pyloric morphology.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, consecutive surgical IHPS patients (2019–2025) were included. Data on demographics, disease timeline (age at onset, age at surgery), and preoperative ultrasound parameters (pyloric muscle thickness, canal length) were collected. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression identified independent risk factors, and a decision tree prediction model was developed.ResultsAmong 323 infants, 43.7% had preoperative metabolic disorders. Multivariate analysis identified independent risk factors: age at surgery (per 1 week delay, aOR = 1.438), pyloric muscle thickness (per 1 mm increase, aOR = 1.408), pyloric canal length (per 1 mm increase, aOR = 1.614), and pyloric index (per 10-unit increase, aOR = 1.671). In the decision tree model, pyloric canal length showed the highest predictive importance (100%), with > 20.75 mm as the primary split point. The model achieved 87.9% accuracy and 90.1% sensitivity.ConclusionPerioperative metabolic disorders in IHPS reflect both obstruction duration and pyloric structural changes. Pyloric canal length is a potentially valuable imaging predictor. The preliminary threshold of > 20.75 mm may serve as a high-risk warning for metabolic disorders; combined with metabolic laboratory assessment, it could help optimize preoperative management and surgical timing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-07077-x.Keywords: Infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis, Metabolic disorder, Pyloric canal length, Ultrasound, Risk factor

Background

Infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis (IHPS) is the most common gastric outlet obstruction in infancy, classically presenting with progressive, non-bilious projectile vomiting at 2–8 weeks of life and a male-to-female ratio of 4–6:1 [1, 2]. While earlier diagnosis via ultrasound and laparoscopic treatment have raised cure rates near 100%, preoperative metabolic disturbances—notably hypochloremic hypokalemic alkalosis—remain a key safety concern [3]. Uncorrected, these can lead to severe alkalosis, electrolyte imbalance, and hemodynamic instability, increasing perioperative risks [4, 5]. Thus, early recognition is vital for safe and efficient care.

These metabolic derangements result from gastric acid loss and renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) activation due to persistent vomiting [4]. Their persistence and variable severity in late-presenting cases suggest links to both symptom duration and obstruction severity [6, 7]. Although vomiting duration and pyloric ultrasound parameters (e.g., muscle thickness, canal length) have been associated with metabolic severity, prior studies have largely examined single indicators [8–10]. There remains a lack of research systematically integrating the dynamic morphological changes of the pylorus with metabolic outcomes over the disease time course.

We hypothesize that perioperative metabolic disorders in IHPS are the cumulative pathophysiological result of prolonged gastric outlet obstruction, and that their severity can be assessed by combining disease time course with pyloric morphology. This single-center retrospective cohort study therefore examines the characteristics of metabolic disorders in IHPS infants, focusing on the independent and combined effects of disease time course and ultrasonographic pyloric parameters. Our aim is to provide evidence for a practical risk-warning framework and to optimize surgical timing and perioperative management.

Methods

Patients

This single-center retrospective cohort study included consecutive infants diagnosed with IHPS who underwent surgical treatment at our institution between January 2019 and December 2025. Inclusion criteria were: (1) typical progressive, non-bilious projectile vomiting onset between 2 and 8 weeks of age; (2) ultrasound confirmation of IHPS (pyloric muscle thickness ≥ 3.5 mm and canal length ≥ 14 mm) [3, 11]; (3) availability of complete admission laboratory results reflecting preoperative metabolic state; (4) complete clinical and perioperative records. Exclusion criteria were: (1) major comorbidities affecting metabolism or perioperative care; (2) systematic intravenous fluid correction before admission; (3) non-surgical management; (4) perioperative death/loss to follow-up; (5) missing key data. All study procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study obtained ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Shanxi Province Children’s Hospital (Approval No. IRB-KYYN-2023-010), and informed consent was formally waived by the committee.

All infants received standardized perioperative care. Upon admission, they were kept nil per os (NPO). Blood gas and electrolyte tests were performed within 6 h. Fluid correction was guided by initial laboratory results and repeated daily for moderate or severe abnormalities. Laparoscopic pyloromyotomy was performed after metabolic correction. Postoperatively, feeding was gradually advanced from small volumes of water to full formula feeds over several days.

Data collection

Data extracted from electronic records included: baseline characteristics (sex, gestational age, birth weight, parity, delivery mode, initial feeding); disease course (age at onset, vomiting duration, admission/surgery age, surgery weight); perioperative metrics (preoperative/total stay, operative duration); and ultrasonographic parameters (pyloric muscle thickness, canal length, and calculated Pyloric Index [PI = thickness × length]) [12]. The maximum values for muscle thickness and canal length were recorded. Metabolic status: Based on the first laboratory results after admission, including: Blood gas analysis: pH, partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PCO₂), bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻), base excess (BE).Serum electrolytes: Potassium (K⁺), sodium (Na⁺), chloride (Cl⁻), calcium (Ca²⁺).Renal function: Blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (Cr).Complete blood count: Hemoglobin (Hb), hematocrit (Hct).A small amount of randomly missing data (< 5%) was assessed to have no significant impact on the primary analyses and was handled using complete-case analysis.

Patient and public involvement

This study is a single-center retrospective cohort study, with all data extracted from existing electronic medical records. Given the retrospective nature of the study design and the study population being infants who are unable to express opinions directly, there was no patient or public involvement in the design, implementation, data analysis, or reporting of this study. The dissemination plan for the study is primarily aimed at academic and clinical professionals.

Case grouping

Infants were stratified into four groups based on age at surgery: ≤21 days, 22–42 days, 43–90 days, and > 90 days. Metabolic status was classified as normal, mild, moderate, or severe based on the first blood gas and electrolyte results, applying the highest applicable severity level [5, 13].

Mild disorder was defined by any one of: pH > 7.45, BE > + 3.5 mmol/L, HCO₃⁻ >26 mmol/L, Cl⁻ 95–<100 mmol/L, K⁺ <3.4 mmol/L, or Na⁺ 130–<135 mmol/L.

Moderate disorder criteria included: pH 7.50–7.59, BE + 5.0–+6.9 mmol/L, HCO₃⁻ 30–34 mmol/L, Cl⁻ 90–<95 mmol/L, K⁺ 3.2–3.3 mmol/L, or Na⁺ 125–<130 mmol/L.

Severe disorder was defined as: pH ≥ 7.60, BE ≥ + 7.0 mmol/L, HCO₃⁻ ≥35 mmol/L, Cl⁻ <90 mmol/L, K⁺ <3.2 mmol/L, or Na⁺ <125 mmol/L. The presence of a mild or greater abnormality constituted a metabolic disorder.

Study content and analytical framework

This study tested the hypothesis that perioperative metabolic disorders in IHPS result from the cumulative effect of prolonged obstruction. The analytical framework involved stratifying infants by age at surgery to examine clinical and metabolic profiles across different obstruction durations, and by metabolic severity for risk stratification. We analyzed correlations between ultrasonographic pyloric parameters (muscle thickness, canal length, PI) and both disease time course and metabolic disorder severity. Independent risk factors were identified via logistic regression, and a decision tree model was built for hierarchical preoperative risk identification to facilitate early detection of high-risk infants and optimize management. The term “perioperative metabolic disorders” is used throughout, reflecting its critical impact on surgical timing, anesthesia risk, and postoperative recovery.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 and R 4.4.3. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR) and compared using ANOVA or the Kruskal-Wallis H test, with post-hoc Mann-Whitney U tests and Bonferroni correction as needed. Categorical variables are presented as n (%) and compared using χ² or Fisher’s exact test. Variables were screened via univariate logistic regression (P < 0.10 for inclusion); multivariate logistic regression then identified independent risk factors, reporting adjusted odds ratio (aOR) with 95% confidence interval (95%CI). Collinearity was assessed (variance inflation factor (VIF) > 10 indicated severe multicollinearity). All tests were two-sided with P < 0.05 considered significant. The relationship between pyloric muscle thickness and canal length was assessed using Spearman correlation. A Classification and Regression Tree (CART) model was built to predict metabolic disorders, with a 7:3 sample split (training: test) via stratified sampling, optimized by cost-complexity pruning. Model performance was evaluated by accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and area under the curve (AUC), with internal validation using 10-fold cross-validation and external validation using the test set. Bootstrap sampling (1000 iterations) provided confidence intervals for performance metrics. No large language models such as ChatGPT were used in the conception, design, data analysis, or writing of this manuscript.

Results

Cohort characteristics and metabolic status

A total of 323 infants with IHPS were included, predominantly male (280, 86.7%; ratio 6.5:1). The median age at surgery was 39.0 days (IQR 32.0–53.0), with a peak incidence at 22–42 days (43.3%). Preoperative metabolic disorders were present in 141 infants (43.7%), classified as mild (n = 73, 22.6%), moderate (n = 48, 14.9%), or severe (n = 20, 6.2%). Hypochloremia was the most common electrolyte disturbance (40.9%), followed by hyponatremia (13.6%) and hypokalemia (9.3%) (Table 1; Fig. 1).

Baseline characteristics by age at surgery

Stratified by age at surgery (≤ 21, 22–42, 43–90, > 90 days), no significant differences were found in perinatal baseline characteristics (sex, parity, delivery mode, gestational age, birth weight, feeding method). However, the median age at symptom onset, age at admission, and duration of vomiting increased significantly across later surgery groups (all P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 1).

Clinical and ultrasonographic features by age at surgery

Both admission and surgical weight increased with later surgery age (P < 0.001). Total hospital stay differed significantly (P < 0.001), being longest in the ≤ 21 days group. Pyloric muscle thickness, canal length, and the Pyloric Index (PI) all showed a significant increasing trend with later surgery age (P = 0.047, P = 0.040, P = 0.005, respectively). The proportion of infants with muscle thickness ≥ 4 mm did not differ among groups (Supplementary Table 2).

Metabolic and laboratory indicators by age at surgery

Acid-base balance indicators and hypochloremia incidence did not differ across groups. Serum sodium concentration varied (P = 0.040), but hyponatremia incidence did not. Hypokalemia incidence was highest in the > 90 days group (22.2%, P = 0.043). Hemoglobin and hematocrit decreased significantly with delayed surgery (both P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Characteristics by metabolic disorder severity

With increasing metabolic disorder severity, age at admission, age at surgery, and vomiting duration were significantly prolonged, while admission weight decreased (all P < 0.001). Pyloric canal length and PI increased significantly with worsening severity (both P < 0.001), with the severe group showing the highest values. Pyloric muscle thickness did not differ among severity groups (Table 3).

Correlation between ultrasonographic parameters

A moderate positive correlation was found between pyloric muscle thickness and canal length (r = 0.388, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Collinearity diagnostics confirmed low variance inflation factors (1.28 and 1.31), allowing both parameters to be included in subsequent regression models.

Logistic regression analysis of risk factors

Univariate analysis identified age at surgery, age at onset, admission weight, pyloric muscle thickness, canal length, and PI as associated with metabolic disorders. Multivariate analysis retained four independent risk factors: age at surgery (per 1-week delay, aOR = 1.438, 95%CI 1.158–1.785), pyloric muscle thickness (per 1-mm increase, aOR = 1.408, 95%CI 1.149–1.727), pyloric canal length (per 1-mm increase, aOR = 1.614, 95%CI 1.213–2.147), and PI (per 10-unit increase, aOR = 1.671, 95%CI 1.231–2.272). All were significant (P < 0.001) (Table 4; Fig. 2).

Decision tree model performance

The decision tree model indicated that pyloric canal length had the highest predictive importance (100%). A preliminary cutoff value of 20.75 mm was identified for distinguishing metabolic disorder from normal status. On the full dataset, the model achieved an AUC of 0.818 (80.0% sensitivity, 76.6% specificity). However, external validation on an independent test set revealed reduced specificity (56.7%) and an average cross-validation AUC of 0.619, indicating some overfitting and limited generalizability (Table 5; Fig. 3).

Discussion

This study systematically analyzed 323 surgically treated infants with IHPS to identify key factors influencing perioperative metabolic disorders. The cohort was predominantly male (86.7%, male-to-female ratio 6.5:1), exceeding the commonly reported ratio of 4–6:1 [2]. This discrepancy may be related to regional, referral, or specific hormonal and genetic characteristics of the sample, warranting further multi-omics validation. The median age at surgery was 39 days, with a peak incidence at 3–6 weeks of life, consistent with previous reports. However, surgery was delayed beyond 90 days in some infants, suggesting potential delays in early screening, recognition, or referral.

The pathophysiological basis of preoperative metabolic disorders primarily involves persistent vomiting leading to gastric acid (H⁺, Cl⁻) loss and secondary RAAS activation [14, 15]. In this study, the incidence was 43.7%, comprising mild (22.6%), moderate (14.9%), and severe (6.2%) cases. Hypochloremia was most common (40.9%), followed by hypokalemia (9.3%). This rate is higher than recent reports where > 90% of infants had essentially normal metabolism, reflecting a degree of delayed presentation in our cohort.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified four independent risk factors: age at surgery (per 1-week delay, aOR = 1.438), pyloric muscle thickness (per 1-mm increase, aOR = 1.408), pyloric canal length (per 1-mm increase, aOR = 1.614), and the PI (per 10-unit increase, aOR = 1.671). These results statistically clarify the central role of disease time course (age at surgery) and the degree of pyloric obstruction morphology (especially pyloric canal length) in metabolic risk assessment, supporting the view of IHPS as a progressive, quantifiably cumulative obstructive disease.

Perinatal baseline characteristics (sex, gestational age, birth weight, parity, mode of delivery, and feeding type) showed no significant differences among the surgery age groups, suggesting their direct impact on disease progression speed is limited. This finding is somewhat inconsistent with previous studies proposing that prematurity or low birth weight might accelerate the disease course [16]. A possible explanation is that most preterm infants in this cohort were late preterm with relatively preserved physiological reserve. Notably, the > 90 days surgery group had a higher proportion of formula feeding, indicating a potential association between feeding type and disease progression or delayed presentation [17]. Formula feeding itself has been identified as a risk factor for IHPS [18]. It may influence disease progression by affecting gastric emptying, gastrin secretion, or pyloric muscle development, which requires further prospective validation.

Although admission and surgical weight showed an increasing trend with later age at surgery, this reflects physiological growth rather than resolution of obstruction. Compared to healthy peers, weight gain in IHPS infants remained delayed, indicating the cumulative impact of chronic vomiting and inadequate intake. However, the risk of metabolic disorders was significantly higher in the > 90 days group, with a hypokalemia incidence of 22.2%, demonstrating that weight gain does not compensate for metabolic imbalance. Admission weight was not an independent predictor in the multivariate model; its influence may be indirectly mediated through prolonged vomiting and chronic depletion. Low-weight status, often associated with poor nutritional reserves and decreased tolerance to dehydration, may amplify the severity of metabolic disorders in the context of prolonged obstruction [19, 20]. Therefore, using weight as a measure of “compensation” can be misleading, and priority should be given to obstruction duration and ultrasonographic morphological progression.

Notably, serum creatinine decreased significantly with later surgical age, a trend mainly attributed to reduced muscle mass and endogenous creatinine production resulting from chronic malnutrition and persistent vomiting. Similarly, hemoglobin and hematocrit declined alongside prolonged disease course; these alterations are primarily caused by nutritional anemia due to chronic vomiting and inadequate oral intake, with longer obstruction duration further aggravating such changes.

Quantitative imaging assessment further revealed associations between pyloric morphological indicators and metabolic status. Both pyloric muscle thickness and canal length increased with later surgery age. However, the proportion of infants with muscle thickness ≥ 4 mm did not differ significantly among groups, suggesting that muscle thickness primarily reflects localized, transient hypertrophy and is less sensitive to cumulative progression. In contrast, pyloric canal length was significantly increased in the severe metabolic disorder group, indicating it better reflects the “structural remodeling under sustained obstruction“ [10] and thus more accurately represents the cumulative effect of disease progression. In this study, the two parameters showed a moderate correlation (r = 0.388), but VIF were low, indicating they could be included together in regression models without severe collinearity and each carried independent predictive information. This supports the unique value of pyloric canal length in predicting metabolic disorders: it is not only correlated with muscle thickness but also shows greater sensitivity over time, serving as an important imaging marker for assessing obstruction duration and metabolic risk. Although clinical practice and ultrasound diagnosis often focus on pyloric muscle thickness as a key indicator of obstruction severity [9], our results suggest that pyloric canal length may hold unique and important value in predicting metabolic disorder risk. Studies by Kim et al. [10] and Piotto et al. [21] also support pyloric canal length as one of the most valuable single ultrasound criteria for distinguishing normal from hypertrophic pylorus. Research by AlMaramhy et al. [9] and Ulusoy et al. [22] noted that pyloric muscle thickening is associated with metabolic disorders, but pyloric canal length may be a more important factor determining the severity of laboratory abnormalities.

The decision tree risk stratification model constructed in this study preliminarily explored the predictive potential of pyloric morphological parameters for perioperative metabolic disorders. The model indicated that pyloric canal length ranked highest in predictive variable importance (100%). ROC analysis identified 20.75 mm as the preliminary optimal threshold for distinguishing “any metabolic disorder” from “normal metabolic status.” These findings preliminarily support the predictive role of pyloric canal length and may help translate imaging measurements into a tentative clinical reference: infants with a pyloric canal length > 20.75 mm could be considered a suspected high-risk group, prompting timely preoperative metabolic evaluation and early risk alert. However, model validation revealed limitations: accuracy was 94.7% and sensitivity 99.1% on the training set, but specificity dropped to 56.7% on the independent test set, and the average AUC from 10-fold cross-validation was only 0.619, indicating overfitting and limited generalizability. These results show that although pyloric canal length is a valuable metabolic risk marker and the model has high sensitivity suitable for metabolic screening, its low specificity leads to more false positives. Therefore, a pyloric canal length > 20.75 mm can serve as a tentative warning signal to initiate timely metabolic laboratory testing, and this cutoff requires further external multicenter validation.

Furthermore, the PI increased significantly with delayed surgery age and worsening metabolic disorder severity. The PI integrates information from both muscle thickness and canal length, providing a more comprehensive reflection of the chronic development of pyloric obstruction [9–11]. In the multivariate analysis, after accounting for the time variable, pyloric canal length retained its independent predictive value, while the contribution of muscle thickness diminished. This indicates that indicators reflecting chronic, persistent obstruction (such as canal length and its derivative PI) are more clinically relevant for metabolic risk assessment than parameters representing only transient narrowing.

Based on these results, pyloric canal length can serve as a practical clinical indicator for identifying infants at high risk for metabolic disorders. It is recommended to complete metabolic assessment and optimize fluid and electrolyte correction early for such infants, with surgery scheduled only after metabolic parameters are stabilized [5], to reduce perioperative risk. All infants in our center received standardized management including preoperative acidosis correction (median 3 days) and stepwise postoperative feeding, which effectively reduced postoperative vomiting risk, demonstrating the importance of standardized perioperative care for surgical safety.

This study has several limitations. First, its single-center retrospective design limits generalizability. Second, key time variables (e.g., vomiting onset) relied on parental recall, potentially introducing recall bias. Potential confounders such as detailed fluid correction protocols, anesthetic techniques, and postoperative complications were not systematically analyzed. The sample sizes for the very early (≤ 21 days), very late (> 90 days), and severe metabolic disorder subgroups were small, which may affect statistical power. Although ultrasound measurements were performed by experienced sonographers, inter-operator variability remains possible. Furthermore, the decision tree model showed obvious overfitting in validation with limited generalizability, which needs further optimization. The proposed 20.75 mm pyloric canal length cutoff remains preliminary and requires further external validation in the future. Future studies should verify these results in multicenter cohorts, explore combined imaging indicators, and evaluate the long-term influence of metabolic disorders on growth and development, so as to continuously optimize the perioperative management of IHPS.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that perioperative metabolic disorders in infants with IHPS result from the cumulative interplay of obstruction duration and structural pyloric changes. Age at surgery, pyloric muscle thickness, pyloric canal length, and the pyloric index were all identified as independent risk factors. Among these, pyloric canal length emerged as a potentially valuable predictor, with its increase strongly correlated to the severity of metabolic disturbances and the duration of obstruction, thereby serving as a key imaging marker of chronic obstructive remodeling. The risk stratification model using a pyloric canal length > 20.75 mm showed high sensitivity and may facilitate early identification of high-risk infants. However, this preliminary cutoff and model require further external multicenter validation. Integrating this imaging marker into perioperative risk stratification can help optimize clinical management and improve outcomes.
