Beyond Rejection: Long‐Term Survival Following Heart Transplant
Authors: Justin C Y Chan, Randal I Goldberg
Published: 2025

Cardiac transplantation remains the gold‐standard treatment that extends survival and returns quality of life in patients with end‐stage heart failure. Continuous improvements in surgical technique and medical management have yielded improved long‐term survival. 1 As with all solid organ transplants, long‐term survival remains limited, in part, by challenges of immunology, primarily acute and chronic rejection (manifested by graft failure and cardiac allograft vasculopathy). As survival time increases, the management of preexisting health conditions such as vascular disease comes into focus beyond the classical paradigm of navigating the competing risks of infection/malignancy and rejection in transplant recipients.

In this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA), Corianò et al. 2 explore the previously undescribed relationship between left ventricular–systemic afterload mismatch and graft failure and mortality. Significantly, they report a hazard ratio of 2.51 times of cardiac mortality in patients with afterload mismatch compared with patients with nonafterload mismatch, primarily from graft failure. This was a highly selected patient population who had a left ventricular ejection fraction ≥50%, cardiac allograft vasculopathy <grade 2, and cellular rejection <2R at 1 year post transplantation. In this group, posttransplant median survival was significantly lower in patients with afterload mismatch, 17.2 years compared with >24 years in patients without.

The concept of ventricular‐afterload coupling is perhaps most elegantly demonstrated in the condition of pulmonary hypertension 3 where increasing pulmonary pressures (right ventricular afterload) eventually lead to maladaptive and disproportional responses in the right ventricle including increased right ventricular volume, mass, and diastolic stiffness. This idea translates also to the left ventricular ‐systemic vascular state and may be important in conditions where ventricular contractility is preserved, such as valvular heart disease (in predicting response to surgery 4 ). There is also increasing recognition of the role that afterload mismatch plays in the condition of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction. 5 Loss of systemic arterial compliance and aortic stiffening (most usually due to aging and hypertension) is a significant contributor to heart failure with preserved ejection fraction. 6 Thus, the concept of preexisting systemic arterial stiffness affecting cardiac function post transplantation in the long term where ventricular contractility, although normalized, cannot adapt to the systemic vascular bed is mechanistically plausible as a cause for poor outcomes.

The current study by Corianò and their group highlight several important issues. First, ventricular and systemic elastance (dependent on measures of ventricular volume and pressure) are difficult to measure noninvasively. This is perhaps why the right ventricular ‐pulmonary artery uncoupling phenomenon is more well studied, as patients with pulmonary hypertension require Swan–Ganz catheterization to diagnose and monitor their condition. Nevertheless, left ventricular end‐systolic pressure can be roughly estimated, as a fraction of noninvasively measured blood pressure and echocardiographic derived values were used to calculate the end ventricular elastance and end arterial elastance. 6 They divided their cohort into 3 specific phenotypes: low afterload, matched high afterload (where both arterial elastance and ventricular elastance were both elevated), and afterload mismatch (where arterial elastance is high and ventricular elastance is low). Exact cutoffs for these cohorts remain to be defined, but conceptually the existence of a cohort of patients with afterload mismatch makes sense.

What might lead to afterload mismatch? First, we must consider the possibility of a size mismatch between donor and recipient. This seems not to be the case in this study, with no differences in predicted heart mass mismatch or donor/recipient weight mismatch between the groups with afterload mismatch and others. Next, the role of donor/recipient sex matching is raised. The authors identified male recipient/female donor as a strong predictor of afterload mismatch (independent of predicted heart mass). Surprisingly, male donor/male recipient sex concordance was also predictive. In light of large reports of minimal/no effect on long‐term mortality with sex‐mismatched transplants 7 absent other risk factors, we strongly recommend the findings of this study should not influence the decision to accept otherwise suitable hearts for transplant in the era of donor organ shortage.

Finally, the male recipients with afterload‐mismatch (of whom this study had a disproportionate amount) were older and had a higher rate of hypertension. It is more likely that one of the main reasons for afterload mismatch seen in this study is senescence and preexisting hypertension leading to arterial remodeling in the systemic vasculature. Even though these changes tend to be more pronounced in women in later life, 8 the sex imbalance in this cohort (reflective of the state of heart transplantation in general 9 ) masks the sex‐based differences that could be identified in a more balanced population.

After transplantation, physicians have the unique responsibility of providing advanced specialized care, while simultaneously functioning as a primary care provider. Malignancy screening, infectious assessment, and preventative health maintenance all come under the purview of the transplant team. In general cardiology, prevention has become its own subspecialty due to the wealth of data of the importance of a multifaceted approach to cardiac prevention (weight loss, exercise, sleep hygiene) as well as the significance of medical interventions for hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes. Importantly, guidelines not only recommend blood pressure control but advocate for which medications are chosen to do so with thiazide diuretics, calcium channel blockers, and angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers receiving class I recommendations as first‐line therapies. 10 Unfortunately, due to the comparatively sparse numbers of recipients of heart transplant as opposed to those with cardiovascular risk factors, little evidence exists for long‐term prevention strategies. Kobashigawa et al. demonstrated that early use of pravastatin led to increased 1‐year posttransplant survival and this was maintained to 10 years post transplant as well as decreased cardiac allograft vasculopathy, even with the majority of the control group crossing over to the treatment arm after the first year. 11 , 12 Notably, the trial by Corianò et al. began recruitment before Kobashigawa's findings, but fewer than half of patients were on a statin in this trial. In these patients, the systolic blood pressure was notably equivalent between the groups with low afterload and afterload mismatch and less than the group with matched high afterload so the results cannot be explained by blood pressure alone. However, perhaps this defines a group with longstanding vascular disease and potential endothelial dysfunction, a relatively new target in the pathogenesis and prevention of ischemic heart disease. This is further suggested with the group with afterload mismatch having significantly more patients with ischemic heart disease, possibly suggestive of more vascular disease in general. Attenuation of endothelial dysfunction has been implicated in the beneficial effects of a variety of medications shown to improve mortality in patients with cardiovascular disease including angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors, statins, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, and sodium glucose transporter 2 inhibitors. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 If a patient's endothelial dysfunction was advanced enough to contribute to the need for heart transplantation, perhaps we should be more aggressive about optimizing the treatment thereafter.

This paper's strength lies in the robustness of their follow‐up, up to 30 years in some cases. However, there are also significant limitations given the highly selected group, of whom 58% with heart transplants performed were excluded from the study, as well as the small numbers. Although the group with afterload mismatch included 26% who died in follow‐up, that encompassed only 13 patients, and thus caution is needed when a small change in numbers would have drastically changed the results. In addition, over such a long period, it is hard to compare patients across different eras in part due to the changing of underlying treatment strategies. With those caveats aside, the authors entertain a valuable point, that the long‐term health of a transplant recipient may have some modifiable factors, outside of a change in immunosuppression. Early after transplantation, renal dysfunction is highly prevalent, often the goal is a safe discharge, and not the long‐term consequences that can arise from less‐than‐ideal treatment of chronic conditions. With the standard immunosuppression, particularly calcineurin inhibition and corticosteroids, these risk factors worsen over time, and the aggressiveness of preventative treatment is more guided by the treating center and not international consensus. Although the solution is unclear, there is widespread agreement in the importance of improving posttransplant long‐term survival. Although there has been an appropriate push in modifying immunosuppressive strategies as well as improving upon the assessment of acute and chronic graft rejection, perhaps there needs to be a further emphasis on the best approach to incorporate preventative cardiology best practices.
