Heterologous expression of lipase gene in Cereibacter sphaeroides 2.4.1 driven by an anaerobic Photosynthesis-Inducible promoter
Authors: Titus Trias Trapsila, Yogy Satria Ariyanto, Antonius Suwanto, Aris Tri Wahyudi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The T1.2RQ lipase gene was successfully expressed in the photosynthetic bacterium Cereibacter sphaeroides, establishing this organism as a viable and potentially energy-efficient platform for recombinant enzyme production.•The puf promoter (oxygen-regulated) demonstrated superior performance compared to the lac promoter under anaerobic photoheterotrophic conditions, confirming its effectiveness for driving high-level expression when oxygen tension is low.•The expressed T1.2RQ lipase displayed high thermostability (Optimal 65°C) and activity on long-chain substrates, suitable for industrial applications.Keywords: Anaerobic expression, Cereibacter sphaeroides, Heterologous expression, Photosynthetic induction, pRK415, Puf promoter, Thermostable lipase

Background

Lipase (EC 3.1.1.3) is a hydrolase enzyme with broad industrial applications, including food and dairy processing, biodiesel production, wastewater treatment, pharmaceuticals, cosmetics, leather tanning, and detergents [1]. Owing to advantages such as mild operating conditions, reaction selectivity, reduced by-product generation, efficient product separation, biodegradable properties, and high efficiency, the enzyme market continues to grow at a rate of about 7% per year, along with innovations to reduce the cost of its use [2].

While the industrial demand for lipase enzymes continues to grow, high costs and reliance on imported enzyme preparations pose significant challenges. Currently, the global enzyme market is highly centralized, leading to logistical and economic hurdles for local industries in developing regions [3]. In addition, although the use of lipase enzymes is considered a more environmentally friendly option 4, 5, however, it is noteworthy that the energy and raw materials required for enzyme production can sometimes exceed the potential savings achieved by their use 6, 7. The use of electrical energy for bioreactors, sterilization, and motor power for stirring is the main contribution to the overall energy consumption [7]. Therefore, one of the reasons for the high cost of enzyme production is the large energy consumption [8].

The T1.2RQ lipase gene, isolated from G. stearothermophilus T1.2 [9], can be expressed in the host systems of Escherichia coli [10] and Bacillus subtilis [11]. In the previously used host systems, T1.2RQ lipase was extracted under aerobic heterotrophic bacterial culture conditions. However, lipase production in the host E. coli or B. subtilis has drawbacks, particularly in terms of the need for electrical energy for aeration, especially in large-scale bioreactors. This can significantly increase operational costs and potentially increase undesirable environmental impacts [12].

While E. coli and B. subtilis remain the dominant platforms for recombinant protein production, their industrial application faces significant challenges regarding sustainability and scalability [13]. In these aerobic systems, high productivity is strictly coupled to intensive mechanical aeration and agitation to meet the cellular oxygen demands for energy (ATP) generation. This requirement not only translates to high electrical consumption but also complicates the scale-up process, as maintaining uniform oxygen transfer in large-scale bioreactors is technically difficult and costly [14]. Additionally, these hosts often rely on expensive chemical inducers, further increasing the economic burden of the process [15].

Beyond host selection, various strategies have been explored to reduce the economic burden of lipase production and enhance enzymatic performance. Recent studies have focused on optimizing cultivation parameters, utilizing inexpensive agro-industrial residues as carbon sources, and implementing advanced immobilization techniques such as physical adsorption onto magnetic nanoparticles or graphene oxide to improve thermal stability and reusability 16, 17. While these downstream and substrate-based approaches have improved enzyme cost-effectiveness, the development of a low-energy metabolic chassis that reduces upstream operational costs remains a critical necessity for sustainable manufacturing.

Photosynthetic bacteria, such as Cereibacter sphaeroides, offer a promising alternative as host cells [18]. C. sphaeroides is particularly attractive because it can use sunlight as an energy source for their growth and metabolism [19]. C. sphaeroides when cultured under anaerobic conditions with light and carbon in the media, it will undergo photoheterotrophic metabolism. Unlike most host systems in the biotechnology industry, this photoheterotrophic lifestyle has the advantages of reducing maintenance costs, being environmentally friendly, and supporting sustainable production through the use of organic waste [20]. However, despite the remarkable progress in molecular genetics of photosynthetic microorganisms, very few expression vectors suitable for purple bacteria are yet available, mainly caused by an unsuitable promoter [21].

A variety of plasmid vectors bearing fragments of the photosynthetic operons were used for genetic studies of photosynthesis. However, the majority of them were designed to investigate gene structure, function, and regulation, rather than expression 22, 23, 24. This gap in current metabolic engineering toolkits limits the transition of C. sphaeroides from a model organism for photosynthesis research to a robust industrial chassis for enzyme production.

Optimizing endogenous regulatory elements—specifically the puf (photosynthetic unit fixed) promoter—presents a strategic advantage. The puf promoter is a high-strength bacterial promoter naturally evolved to synchronize gene expression with environmental oxygen tension and, to a minor level, by light intensity and is classified as a strong bacterial promoter 25, 26. The puf promoter, located upstream from the puf operon on C. sphaeroides, functions to control the expression of genes involved in the photosynthesis process [27]. The puf promoter is regulated by oxygen levels in the environment so that in anaerobic (low oxygen) conditions, this promoter is active and allows transcription of the genes in the puf operon. In contrast, under aerobic conditions (high oxygen), the activity of this promoter is reduced, consequently decreasing the expression of genes in the puf operon [28]. While the puf promoter is primarily anaerobically inducible, a secondary site located 188 bp downstream allows for basal expression under aerobic conditions [28].

By leveraging this light-driven and oxygen-responsive system, this study demonstrates a “green” production platform for thermostable lipases, providing a sustainable pathway for the synthesis of biocatalysts essential for the industry. The primary significance of this work lies in harnessing the oxygen-responsive puf promoter to drive the expression of a thermostable biocatalyst, thereby establishing a resource-efficient and sustainable alternative to conventional proteobacterial expression systems. While the puf promoter is a known regulatory element, its application to express a heterologous lipase from a thermophilic source (G. stearothermophilus T1.2) represents a strategic integration of metabolic plasticity and industrial utility. Additionally, there is no reported expression of lipases in this host system to date. Therefore, this research was conducted to express the T1.2RQ lipase gene in C. sphaeroides 2.4.1 using the puf and lac promoter and to compare lipase expression under anaerobic photosynthetic and aerobic conditions.

Procedures

Materials

E. coli DH5α, E. coli HB101, and C. sphaeroides 2.4.1 were the bacterial strains used in this study. For cloning the lipase gene, E. coli DH5α was employed, while E. coli HB101 served as the helper for conjugation. For lipase production, C. sphaeroides 2.4.1. was employed. Three plasmids were employed: pGEMT-easy and pSL301 for intermediate cloning, and pRK415 as the broad-host-range expression vector. Media used in this study were Luria-Bertani (LB) medium (composed of 1% NaCl, 1% Tryptone, 0.5% yeast extract) and Sistrom’s minimal medium (SMM). The antibiotics used included ampicillin (100 μg/mL) and tetracycline (10 μg/mL for E. coli; 1 μg/mL for C. sphaeroides). For detecting lipolytic activity, tributyrin agar was used. The media contained 1% (v/v) glycerin tributyrate, 1% (w/v) tryptone, 0.5% (w/v) yeast extract, 1% (w/v) NaCl, and 1.5% (w/v) agar.

In this study, the expression efficiency was evaluated by comparing three distinct systems: the experimental construct (pRK415-puf-T1.2RQ), a reference system (pRK415-lac-T1.2RQ) for promoter strength benchmarking, and a negative control consisting of C. sphaeroides harboring the empty pRK415 vector to account for endogenous lipase activity. The gene encoding the modified Lipase T1.2RQ was derived from Geobacillus stearothermophilus (NCBI Accession No. AF237623.1). The modified nucleotide sequence used for expression has a total length of 1,179 bp, which encodes a polypeptide of 392 amino acids.

Amplification of puf promoter

Genomic DNA of C. sphaeroides 2.4.1 was isolated according to the protocol of the Wizard® Genomic DNA Purification Kit for Gram-negative bacteria. After genomic DNA was extracted, the puf promoter was amplified using the primer pair P15-DE-23_Rsph-Puf--394-F (5′-CCTCTTTCATCGCTGCCTCTTTCC-3′) and P15-DU-23_Rsph-PufB-4-R (5′- CCCTGGCGGCATGGACC-3′). This primer pair was designed to amplify the region containing the anaerobically induced puf promoter, the aerobically induced puf promoter, and the associated ribosome binding site (RBS) [28], resulting in the 459 bp PCR product (Fig. 1). The PCR conditions used were as follows: 1) Pre-denaturation at 98°C for 5 min; 2) 30 cycles of: denaturation at 98°C for 30 s, annealing at 64.25°C for 30 s, and extension at 72°C for 10 s; 3) final extension at 72°C for 5 min. The 50 μL PCR reaction mix contained 25 μL Q5® High-Fidelity 2X Master Mix (NEB), 5 μL forward primer, 5 μL reverse primer, 10 μL nuclease-free water, and 5 μL of DNA template.

Construction of recombinant plasmid

Three plasmids were constructed in this study: pRK415-puf-T1.2RQ, pRK415-lac-T1.2RQ, and pRK415-puf. Plasmid pRK415-puf-T1.2RQ contains the puf promoter and the T1.2RQ lipase gene cloned in the opposite direction to the lac promoter orientation (Fig. 2A). This configuration ensures that T1.2RQ lipase expression is regulated solely by the puf promoter. In pRK415-lac-T1.2RQ, the T1.2RQ lipase gene was inserted in the same orientation as the lac promoter (Fig. 2B). The third plasmid, pRK415-puf, only contains the puf promoter region but lacks the T1.2RQ lipase gene (Fig. 2C). pRK415 plasmid, derived from pRK404 [29], is a widely used broad-host-range vector for conjugation in Gram-negative bacteria, including C. sphaeroides 30, 31, 32.

Conjugation of E. Coli DH5α (pRK415-puf-T1.2RQ), E. Coli DH5α (pRK415-lac-T1.2RQ), and E. Coli DH5α (pRK415-puf) to C. Sphaeroides 2.4.1

Conjugation was performed using the triparental mating method, utilizing E. coli DH5α (pRK415-puf-T1.2RQ), E. coli DH5α (pRK415-lac-T1.2RQ), and E. coli DH5α (pRK415-puf) as the donor, E. coli HB101 (pRK2013) as the helper, and C. sphaeroides 2.4.1 as the recipient. The three strains were grown overnight on their respective selective media, then centrifuged, washed twice with LB, and the cell pellet was suspended in LB. The cell mixture was then spotted onto LB agar coated with sterile filter paper in three combinations (donor–recipient, recipient–helper, donor–recipient–helper) and incubated for 6 h at 30°C. Triparental mating was conducted on sterile filter paper to enhance conjugation efficiency compared to liquid-based methods [33]. After that, the cells on the filter paper were suspended in 0.85% NaCl solution, vortexed, and then spread onto SMM media with or without tetracycline for selection. The cultures were incubated at 30°C for 2–3 days to obtain transconjugants.

Recombinant lipase production

C. sphaeroides 2.4.1 colonies with recombinant plasmids pRK415-puf-T1.2RQ, pRK415-lac-T1.2RQ, and pRK415-puf were cultured in SMM supplemented with tetracycline (1 μg/mL) as a pre-culture at 30℃ with 200 rpm agitation overnight. Recombinant lipase production occurred aerobically and anaerobically. For aerobic production, the pre-culture was inoculated into 50 mL LB media supplemented with tetracycline (1 μg/mL) to an initial OD of 0.1, incubated at 30℃ with 200 rpm agitation, and harvested after 24 h. For anaerobic production, the pre-culture was inoculated into 10 mL LB:SMM (1:1) medium supplemented with tetracycline (1 µg/mL) until the OD660 reached 0.1. The cultures were then incubated at 30°C for 96 h under static, illuminated conditions. Anaerobic photosynthetic induction was performed under continuous illumination using a 25 W tungsten lamp with a light intensity of approximately 2,900 lx, measured at the surface of the culture vessel. Extracellular enzymes were recovered from the culture supernatant after centrifugation at 8,000 xg for 5 min. To extract intracellular enzymes, the resulting cell pellet was washed with Tris-Cl buffer (pH 8.0), sonicated for 10 min, and centrifuged at 8,000 xg for 10 min at 12°C. The resulting supernatant was stored as the intracellular extract.

Lipase activity test and protein concentration Measurement

Lipase activity was assessed spectrophotometrically using p-nitrophenyl palmitate (pNPP) as the substrate. The enzyme hydrolyzes the substrate, releasing p-nitrophenol, leading to a yellow color change measurable with a spectrophotometer 34, 35, 36. The 1 mL reaction mixture consisted of 940 μL Tris-Cl buffer (pH 8.0), 10 μL crude enzyme extract, 40 μL absolute ethanol, and 10 μL pNPP (p-nitrophenyl palmitate). The reaction occurred at 50℃ for 5 min and was measured at 405 nm. One Unit (U) of enzyme activity was defined as the amount of enzyme releasing 1 μmol of p-nitrophenol per minute. The total protein concentration was determined using the Bradford assay (Bradford, 1976), with bovine serum albumin (BSA) as the standard. Absorbance was measured at 595 nm using a spectrophotometer.

SDS-PAGE and zymogram of recombinant protein

Recombinant lipase expression was confirmed using 12% SDS-PAGE followed by Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 staining. For functional verification, zymogram analysis was conducted by renaturing unstained gels in 1% Triton X-100 and equilibrating them in Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0). The gels were then overlaid on 1% tributyrin agar plates and incubated overnight at 30°C. Lipolytic activity was identified by the formation of clear zones, which serves to observe the functionality of the enzyme [37].

Characterization of lipase produced by C. Sphaeroides 2.4.1

The optimum temperature of lipase was tested at 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 65, 70, 75, 80, 90, and 100 ℃ using pNP palmitate substrate at pH 8.0. The temperature stability of lipase was carried out at 50, 65, and 80℃, lipase activity was measured every 15, 30, 60, 90, 120, 150, and 180 min. The optimum pH of lipase was tested at 0.1 M citrate phosphate (pH 5), 0.1 M acetate buffer (pH 6), 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7), 0.1 M Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0–9.0), 0.2 M glycine-NaOH buffer (10–13). The pH stability of lipase was carried out at pH 5, 7, 10. Lipase activity was measured every 15, 30, 60, 90, 120, 150, and 180 min. Substrate specificity was tested on pNP butyrate (C4), pNP caproate (C6), pNP caprylate (C8), pNP caprate (C10), pNP laurate (C12), pNP myristate (C14), pNP palmitate (C16), and pNP stearate (C18). The effect of metal ions was investigated by incubating the enzyme with respective chloride salt (1:1 ratio) at 40℃ for 30 min. The chloride salts tested were MgCl2 (0.001 M), CaCl2 (0.001 M), NaCl2 (0.001 M), FeCl2 (0.001 M), MnCl2 (0.001 M), KCl (0.001 M), and ZnCl2 (0.001 M). The resistance of lipase to organic solvents was carried out by incubating the enzyme and organic solvent (1:1) at a temperature of 30℃ for 1 h. The organic solvents used were methanol, ethanol, butanol, isopropanol, acetonitrile, n-hexane, and n-heptane.

Data and Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For expression and activity assays, experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3) using independent biological samples. To ensure high precision in biochemical characterization (temperature, pH, metal ions, and organic solvents), measurements were conducted in nonuplicate (n = 9). Data were analyzed using Two-way or One-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) where appropriate. Post-hoc comparisons were performed using Tukey’s multiple comparison test to determine significant differences (p < 0.05). All analyses were conducted using OriginPro™ 2023 software.

Expression of recombinant lipase in C. sphaeroides 2.4.1

Recombinant lipase expression was performed qualitatively and quantitatively. Qualitative expression analysis was performed via a spot test of bacterial culture on LB agar supplemented with 1% tributyrin and 1 µg/mL tetracycline. Screening lipolytic activity on tributyrin media facilitates the observation of lipase activity by the formation of clear zones [38]. The expression of C. sphaeroides (pRK415-puf-T1.2RQ) was analyzed relative to two groups: the reference system (pRK415-lac-T1.2RQ) and the negative control (pRK415-puf). C. sphaeroides (pRK415-puf-T1.2RQ) formed a clear zone after 24 h (Fig. 6A), a result similarly observed for the reference system C. sphaeroides (pRK415-lac-T1.2RQ) (Fig. 6B). However, the negative control C. sphaeroides (pRK415-puf) had not formed a clear zone after 24 h (Fig. 6C).

Quantitative tests were performed on intracellular extracts. Intracellular lipase specific activity of C. sphaeroides (pRK415-puf-T1.2RQ) was significantly different from both the reference system and negative control under aerobic conditions (Table 2). The specific activity of lipase produced by C. sphaeroides (pRK415-puf-T1.2RQ) under aerobic culture conditions was 0.247 ± 0.010 U/mg, while the specific activity of the reference system T1.2RQ was 0.542 ± 0.001 U/mg. The specific activity of lipase from C. sphaeroides (pRK415-puf-T1.2RQ) was significantly different from the reference system under anaerobic conditions (Table 2). The specific activity of lipase produced by C. sphaeroides (pRK415-puf-T1.2RQ) under anaerobic culture conditions was 0.333 ± 0.044 U/mg while the specific activity of the reference system T1.2RQ was 0.108 ± 0.013 U/mg. This indicates that T1.2RQ lipase expression, under the control of the puf promoter, is upregulated under anaerobic conditions, a finding that aligns with the promoter's regulatory mechanism. However, the overall highest intracellular specific activity was achieved by C. sphaeroides (pRK415-lac-T1.2RQ) under aerobic conditions.

Characterization of lipase T1.2RQ produced by C. Sphaeroides 2.4.1

The extract used for biochemical characterization is the crude T1.2RQ extract produced by C. sphaeroides 2.4.1. The scope of this research was specifically limited to the optimization of expression systems. Therefore, biochemical characterization was conducted using crude extracts to evaluate the promoters' efficiency, while protein purification was beyond the current study's objectives. The optimal temperature for the lipase, determined by the pNPP assay, was 65℃, with a minimum temperature of 20℃. The enzyme demonstrated exceptional thermostability, maintaining 36.6% of its relative activity at 100°C. This suggests that the T1.2RQ lipase produced by C. sphaeroides remains functional even under extreme thermal conditions (Fig. 8A). Temperature stability testing was performed at three different temperatures, revealing that lipase T1.2RQ is relatively stable at an incubation temperature of 50℃. However, after incubation for 15 min at 65 and 80℃, the relative activity of lipase T1.2RQ significantly decreased (Fig. 8B). The analysis of the optimum pH for recombinant lipase T1.2RQ was conducted within the pH range of 6–13. The optimum pH obtained was pH 10, with pH 7 being the minimum pH, and pH 12 being the maximum pH for the activity of lipase T1.2RQ produced by C. sphaeroides 2.4.1 (Fig. 8C). pH stability was tested at pH 5, 7, and 10, revealing that lipase T1.2RQ is relatively stable at all three tested pH levels (Fig. 8D).

The influence of temperature on intracellular lipase T1.2RQ produced by C. sphaeroides 2.4.1 was examined. (A) Optimal temperature, (B) temperature stability, (C) optimal pH, and (D) pH stability. Discussion

The successful cloning of the T1.2RQ lipase gene into pRK415 derivatives and its subsequent introduction into C. sphaeroides laid the groundwork for this study. During conjugation, E. coli donor strains effectively transferred the pRK415-puf-T1.2RQ, pRK415-lac-T1.2RQ, and pRK415-puf plasmids into the C. sphaeroides 2.4.1 recipient, yielding tetracycline-resistant transconjugants. The efficiency of transconjugation from E. coli to C. sphaeroides is a crucial metric, underscoring the successful integration and functionality of the lipase genes within the recipient organism. The transconjugation efficiency can be influenced by various factors, both biotic and abiotic, including cell density, donor-recipient ratio, growth phase, nutrient source, pH, temperature, and mating time 39, 40, 41.

The use of the lac promoter as a reference system provided a benchmark to assess the performance of the puf promoter. By utilizing the same host and plasmid backbone (pRK415) for both systems, the observed differences in lipase activity can be specifically attributed to the regulatory strength of the promoters rather than variations in plasmid copy number or host metabolism. The lipase activity produced by C. sphaeroides under the control of the puf promoter under aerobic culture conditions was lower than that under the control of the lac promoter. This is consistent with [28], who attributed this basal expression throughout aerobic growth to a second, weaker transcription initiation site located 188 bp downstream of the anaerobically inducible puf promoter (primary transcription initiation site) position.

The specific activity (U/mg) results reveal a clear specialization for each promoter system. The puf promoter demonstrated its optimal functionality under anaerobic conditions (0.333 ± 0.044 U/mg), which was significantly higher than its aerobic performance (0.247 ± 0.010 U/mg) (p < 0.05). This indicates that T1.2RQ lipase expressed under the control of the puf promoter is relatively better produced under anaerobic conditions. This aligns with the findings of [28], who reported that the promoter located 699 bp upstream of the pufB structural gene operates optimally under anaerobic conditions.

In contrast, the lac promoter followed the opposite trend. While it reached the highest overall activity under aerobic conditions (0.542 ± 0.001 U/mg), it failed to maintain this performance in the absence of oxygen. Its anaerobic activity dropped significantly to 0.108 ± 0.013 U/mg. Although this value was significantly higher than the empty vector control (P < 0.05), it represented a substantial decrease compared to its aerobic performance. These results establish that while lac remains an effective reference for aerobic heterotrophic growth, the puf system provides significantly more robust expression under anaerobic photoheterotrophic conditions, where the standard reference system shows limited efficacy.

The utilization of a high-performing indigenous promoter does not necessarily correlate with a proportional increase in enzymatic activity. Although the puf promoter exhibited clear induction under anaerobic conditions, its peak activity remained lower than the aerobic lac reference. This discrepancy underscores that efficient transcription does not guarantee a concomitant increase in translation or the correct folding of the target protein. High transcriptional efficiency does not invariably lead to a proportional increase in functional protein, as the final yield is governed by multiple regulatory checkpoints during translation, folding, and post-translational processes [42]. In C. sphaeroides, these limitations are often linked to the complex metabolic partitioning between heterologous protein synthesis and the formation of the photosynthetic apparatus [43].

The maximum lipase activity achieved under anaerobic photoheterotrophic conditions (using the puf system) remained lower than the peak activity recorded under aerobic heterotrophic conditions (using the lac system). This suggests that the anaerobic photoheterotrophic state in C. sphaeroides imposes a significant metabolic demand. During this phase, C. sphaeroides synthesize crucial proteins for photosynthesis, such as cytochrome c2, cytochrome bc1, cytochrome cbb3, and reaction centers [44]. Additionally, the expression of genes associated with CO2 fixation, bacteriochlorophyll biosynthesis, and carotenoid biosynthesis takes place during this growth phase [45]. The observed differences in lipase production can be attributed to the distinct physiological processes or metabolic pathways activated under anaerobic photoheterotrophic conditions. The altered metabolic state of C. sphaeroides during phototrophic growth may influence the expression levels of recombinant lipase.

Based on this study, the puf promoter can be used to express the T1.2RQ lipase gene. Several studies have used the puf promoter for gene expression in C. sphaeroides, for example, in the study of Luciferase gene expression by [46]. These results demonstrated that vectors containing the puf promoter can be used for heterologous gene expression in both batch and continuous cultures. Additionally, the puf promoter has been used in research on the development of expression vectors from the pBBRBB-BioBrick™ plasmid for overexpression of membrane proteins. This vector significantly increased the expression of membrane proteins and proteorhodopsin in C. sphaeroides [21].

Lipase activity was predominantly localized within the intracellular fraction, with negligible extracellular activity (Table 2). This indicates that the T1.2RQ lipase remained largely within the cytoplasm or periplasm, likely due to the absence of a compatible signal peptide for the C. sphaeroides secretion machinery. Generally, in enzyme industries, extracellular expression is preferred as it facilitates downstream processing [47]. The limited extracellular secretion in C. sphaeroides 2.4.1 highlights a common challenge in heterologous protein production within Gram-negative hosts, which possess a complex cell envelope consisting of an inner membrane, a peptidoglycan layer, and an outer membrane [48]. For a protein to reach the extracellular medium, it must navigate specialized secretion pathways, such as the Sec or Tat systems, and additional machinery to bypass the outer membrane [49]. In this study, the absence of a specific signal peptide likely resulted in the lipase being retained in the cytoplasm or periplasm. The retention of the T1.2RQ lipase within the cytoplasm or periplasm suggests that future engineering must incorporate native C. sphaeroides signal sequences to facilitate industrial-scale extracellular production

To improve extracellular yield in future studies, several engineering strategies for Gram-negative bacteria could be implemented. These include modifying the secretion tags of the target protein, engineering the secretion systems themselves, transferring heterologous secretion systems into the host, or manipulating the genes that regulate secretion pathways [49]. Implementing these strategies would be essential to facilitate the transport of the enzyme across the complex Gram-negative cell envelope for industrial-scale production.

Furthermore, C. sphaeroides 2.4.1 strain used in this study was a wild-type (unmodified) host. Consequently, its efficiency for recombinant lipase production remains lower compared to highly optimized industrial strains like E. coli BL21 50, 51. To establish C. sphaeroides as a competitive and effective host system, further genetic engineering is essential. This includes not only the optimization of metabolic flux but also the development of specialized strains with reduced protease activity or enhanced protein folding and secretion capabilities.

Protein analysis through SDS-PAGE and zymogram visually confirms lipase T1.2RQ’s expression and consistent size around 43 kDa. The zymogram’s ability to depict lipolytic activity through clear zone formation further validates the enzyme’s functionality, providing essential certainty for subsequent applications and downstream processes [37]. The protein size and zymogram results align with the expression of lipase T1.2RQ in E. coli BL21 (DE3), reported to be 43 kDa [10].

Beyond expression, the biochemical characterization of lipase T1.2RQ delves into its functional properties. The determined optimal temperature of 65°C is favorable for typical industrial ranges, highlighting the enzyme’s applicability. Stability at 50°C indicates resilience under moderate temperatures, a critical trait for sustained enzymatic activity. Lipase T1.2RQ expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) with the pET-28a vector has an optimum temperature of 50℃ 10, 52, while the optimum temperature of the native G. stearothermophilus bacterium is reported to be 80°C [53]. This shift suggests that the C. sphaeroides cellular environment may offer a distinct folding milieu or post-translational stabilization, potentially favoring a more thermostable protein conformation compared to E. coli. Such robustness at 65°C enhances the enzyme's applicability in high-temperature industrial processes, bridging the gap between previous heterologous systems and the native G. stearothermophilus enzyme (80°C). Determining optimum and stable pH ranges provides additional insights into the environmental conditions conducive to optimal lipase performance. Lipase T1.2RQ expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) with the pET-28a vector has an optimum pH of 8 10, 52, whereas the lipase expressed by G. stearothermophilus has an optimum pH of 8.5 [53].

The optimal expression of transgenes in heterologous systems depends on the structural features of the host recipient gene, potentially causing differential expression of different genes 54, 55. For efficient expression of heterologous genes, they must be transcribed and translated efficiently by the resources of the host cell. Additionally, proteins must undergo proper processing and post-translational modifications required for them to function correctly [56]. Differences in the host used in heterologous expression can be one of the reasons for changes in the optimal temperature and pH of the expressed T1.2RQ lipase.

Substrate specificity in lipases is generally categorized by fatty acid chain length preference, regioselectivity (positional specificity on the glycerol backbone), and stereoselectivity. This study specifically evaluated the enzyme's substrate preference based on acyl chain length (C4–C18) [57]. The analysis in this study only focuses on regioselectivity. The high activity towards pNP butyrate (C4) and pNP caprylate (C8) indicates that T1.2RQ lipase possesses esterase-like activity, although it retains the ability to act on longer-chain substrates. This preference is advantageous in the food industry, where short-chain fatty acid hydrolysis can enhance flavor 58, 41. Similarly, heterologous expression of Geobacillus kaustophilus lipase in E. coli BL21(DE3) demonstrated specificity towards short to medium-chain fatty acids [59]. Metal ion effects revealed the robustness of lipase T1.2RQ, surviving and exhibiting increased activity in the presence of specific ions. The catalytic triad of lipase T1.2RQ in G. stearothermophilus has binding affinity for Ca2+ and Zn2+, contributing to enzyme stability [53]. Generally, ions like Ca2+, Mn2+, and Zn2+ enhance lipase activity and stability against temperature and pH changes 60, 61, 62. In this study, these biochemical characterizations were conducted using crude enzyme extract, as the primary objective was to evaluate the functional expression efficiency of the puf and lac promoters within C. sphaeroides. This approach provides a direct assessment of the total enzymatic potential in a new host system while avoiding potential activity loss often associated with purification steps. While crude extract is sufficient for this initial proof-of-concept, further purification methods such as ammonium sulfate precipitation or ultrafiltration will be prioritized in future studies for large-scale applications.

Lipases, in general, can catalyze various reactions in both aqueous and organic systems. The use of water as a solvent encourages lipases to catalyze hydrolysis reactions, while in organic solvents, lipases can catalyze various synthesis reactions. Lipase activity in organic solvents offers advantages such as better substrate and product solubility, a shift in thermodynamic equilibrium from hydrolysis to synthesis, easy solvent separation, enzyme elimination from the reaction owing to its insolubility, improved thermal stability, microbial contamination elimination, and the potential direct use of enzymes in chemical reactions [63]. The influence of organic solvents on lipases has been extensively studied and applied because many industrially important chemical reactions, such as ester synthesis, transesterification, synthesis of pure enantiomers, and others, have been shown to be implementable with enzymes as catalysts in non-aqueous solvents 64, 65. Lipase resistance in solvents like methanol is crucial in its application for biodiesel production 66, 67.

The potential for integrating C. sphaeroides into industrial frameworks can be explored through a sequential two-stage bioprocess, transitioning from an aerobic-dark phase to an anaerobic-light phase. This strategy aims to overcome the metabolic limitations observed in pure anaerobic-light cultivation, such as the prolonged lag phase which can reduce overall productivity [68]. By utilizing the superior performance of the lac promoter under aerobic conditions for rapid biomass accumulation, the process can subsequently be switched to anaerobic-light conditions to trigger the light-harvesting puf promoter for lipase expression. While studies on CoQ10 production suggest that a single-stage aerobic-dark process with zero dissolved oxygen may sometimes yield higher concentrations [68], the two-stage framework provides a versatile alternative for products that specifically require the induction of photosynthetic machinery or light-dependent promoters. This approach balances the need for rapid growth kinetics with the energy-efficient, light-driven biocatalysis characteristic of C. sphaeroides.

Conclusions

This study successfully demonstrated the cloning, expression, and characterization of the T1.2RQ lipase gene in Cereibacter sphaeroides, establishing its viability as a sustainable photosynthetic platform for biotechnological applications. The originality of this work lies in the utilization of the native light-inducible puf promoter, offering a sustainable and cost-effective alternative to conventional chemical induction.

The results provide a solid basis for advancing anaerobic photoinducible biocatalyst development. In terms of applicability, this system serves as a promising platform for light-driven enzyme production, particularly in bioprocesses that benefit from anaerobic conditions. However, a primary limitation of the current system is the use of an unmodified wild-type host, which possesses complex native regulatory pathways that can compete with heterologous protein expression, thereby limiting overall yield. Future work should focus on the genetic modification of the C. sphaeroides host, such as the development of specialized strains with simplified regulatory backgrounds, to fully realize its potential as an efficient photosynthetic cell factory.

Furthermore, optimizing extracellular secretion strategies represents a key direction for future research. Enhancing the transport of lipase across the complex Gram-negative cell envelope of C. sphaeroides. Future efforts should incorporate specialized signal peptides or engineered secretion pathways to enhance enzyme recovery and streamline downstream purification.
