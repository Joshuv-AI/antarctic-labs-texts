Internet gaming disorder and psychological distress among Palestinian adult online gamers
Authors: Hana Khalili, Yousef AlShaikh, Jana Abadi, Sana Abadi, Faridah Ihmoud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInternet Gaming Disorder (IGD) is considered a recently developed issue regarding behavioral health concerns internationally, especially among young adults who become more involved in internet gaming activities. Even though there is a vast number of studies conducted internationally, data from Palestine remain limited. The present study was therefore designed to investigate the prevalence rate of IGD in Palestinian adults and its relationship with psychological distress, including depression, anxiety, and stress.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted between March and May 2026 using a structured questionnaire distributed through social media platforms. The study included adults aged 18 years and older residing in Palestine who provided informed consent. Individuals outside the specified age range or with incomplete questionnaire responses were excluded. Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) and psychological distress were assessed using the validated Arabic versions of the DSM-5 IGD Scale and the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21). A total of 611 participants were included. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, bivariate tests, Spearman correlations, multivariable logistic regression to identify predictors of probable IGD, and multiple linear regression to examine associations between IGD score and DASS-21 subscale scores.ResultsThe final analysis included 611 participants recruited using convenience sampling through social media and online platforms. Probable Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) was identified in 23.5% of online gamers. Probable IGD was significantly associated with increased gaming frequency and duration, multiplayer gaming, spending money on games, attempts to reduce gaming time, and negative effects on academic/work performance, social relationships, and sleep (p < 0.05). Significant positive correlations were observed between IGD scores and depression, anxiety, and stress scores (p < 0.001). In multiple linear regression analyses, IGD score remained an independent predictor of higher depression (β = 0.348, p < 0.001), anxiety (β = 0.311, p < 0.001), and stress scores (β = 0.346, p < 0.001). In multivariable logistic regression, playing online with other players most of the time was independently associated with probable IGD (adjusted OR = 3.51, 95% CI: 1.08–11.46, p = 0.037), as were moderate and high perceived negative impacts of gaming on academic/work performance (adjusted OR = 4.38, 95% CI: 1.78–10.80, p = 0.001; and adjusted OR = 4.88, 95% CI: 1.61–14.81, p = 0.005, respectively). Higher IGD scores also independently predicted higher depression, anxiety, and stress scores in adjusted linear regression models.ConclusionThis study highlights probable IGD as an emerging behavioral health concern among Palestinian adult online gamers, particularly when gaming is linked to functional impairment and use as a coping strategy for stress or negative emotions. The findings suggest that assessment should move beyond gaming duration alone and consider gaming-related consequences, psychological distress, and coping motives. Future research should use longitudinal and clinically assessed designs to clarify temporal relationships between IGD and psychological distress. Prevention and intervention efforts should focus on healthy coping skills, early screening, and accessible support for individuals whose gaming is associated with distress or impairment.Trial registrationNot applicable.Keywords: Internet gaming, IGD, DASS, Depression, Anxiety, Stress

Introduction

In recent years, electronic games have been considered one of the most prevalent forms of entertainment and competition worldwide. This is attributable to the easy accessibility to electronic games, technological advancements and the spread of the internet and smart devices [1]. These games have become an integral part of daily life for many people, especially young individuals, because of the social interaction, engaging virtual experiences, and an environment of high competitiveness they provide. Recent data indicate the widespread global popularity of electronic games, with the number of players exceeding 3.6 billion people in 2024, which is equivalent to about 42% of the world’s population [2]. Certain types of games may enhance intellectual abilities, stimulate cognitive function [3], and promote social interaction, among other potential benefits [4]. However, excessive or inappropriate use, or the use of potentially harmful types of games, may negatively impact overall well-being, mental health, academic performance, and behavioral patterns [5].

Engaging in online gaming has been increased by the widespread smartphone technology use and internet connectivity, which may be seen as an easily accessible and viable coping mechanism for many individuals. Despite it being a coping mechanism or a form of recreational engagement, over-engagement may contribute to the development of maladaptive behavioral responses, which may contribute to mental health issues and negatively impact functioning [6, 7]. In Palestinian contexts where mental health services are limited, psychological distress may be underrecognized because of stigma, low mental health literacy, and barriers to help-seeking [8, 9]. In this context, gaming disorder may represent a clinically recognized yet underexplored public health concern, espcielly in low-resource settings [10, 11].

The American Psychiatric Association views Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) from a research perspective; it included IGD in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) as a condition that needs further research before being adopted as an official independent diagnosis. The DSM-5 characterizes IGD based on a set of clinical criteria that reflect behavioral and cognitive patterns associated with excessive gaming use [12].

Epidemiological studies reveal that gaming disorder is considered one of the emerging global public health concerns, as it has begun to receive increasing attention in research circles in recent years. Studies reveal that gaming disorder prevalence varies noticeably depending on the population and the diagnostic criteria used. The rate typically ranges between approximately 1% and 10%, with meta-analytic studies suggesting pooled prevalence rates of around 3% [13]. Adolescents and young adults, known for their frequent use of digital technologies and a greater tendency to seek rewards, show significantly higher rates of gaming disorder [14]. The present study focued on adults aged 18 years and older for several reasons. First, adults can provide independent informed consent for online survey participation. Second, IGD prevalence is frequent among adults in the Arab countirs [15], and it is clinically and socially relevant, and may intersect with work or university performance, financial spending, sleep, relationships, and stress-coping behaviors in ways that differ from adolescent samples.Third, adult online gamers remain underrepresented in Palestinian research, despite the growing relevance of online gaming and psychological distress in this population.

Research interest is not limited to prevalence, but also factors associated with problematic gaming use including behavioral, psychosocial, and functional aspects. Behaviorally, spending a long time playing games, coupled with motivations related to escapism or stress relief, is associated with an increased likelihood of developing gaming disorder. Likewise, on a psychological level, factors such as depression, anxiety, and perceived stress stand out as clearly associated with this disorder, with individuals experiencing problematic gaming use exhibiting higher levels of psychological distress compared to others. Also, recent evidence suggests that gaming may function as a maladaptive coping strategy, wherein individuals utilize gaming to manage negative emotions or to evade real-world stressors [16].

The present study was guided by the Interaction of Person-Affect-Cognition-Execution model. This model explains problematic internet-use behaviors, including gaming disorder, as the result of interactions between person-level vulnerabilities, affective and cognitive responses, coping motives, internet-related biases, and impaired behavioral control [17, 18]. This framework was selected because it aligns with the variables examined in the present study, including psychological distress, gaming as escape from stress or negative emotions, gaming intensity, and functional impairment.

Broader addiction literature also highlights the importance of psychosocial mechanisms, including hope, perceived social support, resilience, stigma, and caregiving burden, in shaping addiction-related outcomes and quality of life. Although much of this evidence comes from substance-use populations, it supports the need to examine behavioral addictions such as IGD within a wider psychosocial framework [19, 20].

Despite the growing international literature on gaming disorder, evidence from the Middle East remains limited, and Palestine is particularly underrepresented in studies using standardized diagnostic tools [15, 21]. This gap is important because local mental health research has described high levels of psychological distress and limited mental health resources in Palestine, while studies among Palestinian-Arab populations have also highlighted stigma, mental health literacy, and help-seeking barriers as relevant contextual factors [9, 22, 23]. However, local evidence on IGD among Palestinian adults remains scarce, making it difficult to estimate the burden of probable IGD or understand its relationship with depression, anxiety, and stress. Therefore, this study aimed to estimate the prevalence of probable IGD among adult online gamers in Palestine, identify gaming behavior patterns, and examine associations between IGD and psychological distress, including depression, anxiety, and stress.

Methodology

Study design and population

A cross-sectional study from [March - May 2026] using a structured questionnaire was conducted to determine the prevalence of IGD and the psychological impact among adults in Palestine. The study included adults ≥ 18 years old, currently residing in Palestine, and who were able to provide informed consent. Online gaming engagement was assessed within the questionnaire. Participants who reported current online gaming were included in the IGD-specific analyses, including gaming behavior comparisons and regression models. Participants who were younger than 18 years old, did not provide informed consent, or who had not completed scale items in the questionnaire were excluded.

The study was conducted in West Bank, Palestine using online recruitment. Participants were drawn from different residential settings, including cities, villages, and refugee camps. The questionnaire included residence type as a sociodemographic variable. Because the survey was disseminated online through social media platforms, participation was not restricted to a single district or governorate.

Sample size calculations

Sample size was calculated using Raosoft sample size calculator [24] for single population proportion estimation. The standard formula n = Z² × p(1 − p) / d² was used, where n represents the minimum required sample size, Z is the standard normal deviate corresponding to a 95% confidence level (1.96), p is the estimated prevalence of Internet Gaming Disorder, and d is the margin of error set at 5%.

In the absence of nationally representative prevalence data for Palestine, a conservative prevalence estimate of 50% (p = 0.5) was adopted to maximize sample size under maximum variability conditions. Based on these assumptions, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be approximately 385 participants.

A total of 611 participants were successfully recruited and included in the final analysis. This sample size exceeds the minimum required threshold calculated using Raosoft sample size calculator formula, thereby improving the precision of prevalence estimates and allowing for reliable subgroup and multivariable analyses. Participants with incomplete IGD or DASS-21 responses were excluded from final analysis.

Sampling technique and data collection

Participants were recruited using a convenience non-proportional sampling method. Data were collected using an anonymous online questionnaire distributed through social media platforms. The first page of the survey included the study aim, eligibility criteria, estimated completion time, confidentiality statement, and informed consent form. Participants were required to confirm eligibility and provide informed consent before accessing the questionnaire items. To reduce duplicate responses, the survey settings allowed only one submission per device or account when possible. In addition, responses were screened before analysis for duplicate timestamps, identical sociodemographic patterns, and repeated or inconsistent entries; suspected duplicates and incomplete responses were removed before final analysis.

The questionnaire consisted of 4 parts, the first was sociodemographic characteristics, such as age, gender, marital status, occupational status, educational level, residence, and living situation. The second part was regarding gaming-related variables; number of gaming days per week, number of gaming hours daily, gaming time patterns, expenditure on online gaming, gaming time-related effect on academic/ work performance, social relationships and sleeping hours.

The third part consisted of the internet gaming disorder assessment using the validated Arabic version of the internet gaming disorder (IGD) scale [25, 26]. The scale included 9 items, based on DSM-5 criteria with binary answers of yes, no, with yes answers equal to 1 point and no answers equal to 0 points.

The answers to IGD items were summed, and a cutoff point of ≥ 5 was used to indicate a probable IGD. The Cronbach’s alpha for IGD scale in our population was 0.734 indicating good reliability.

The fourth part was the psychological assessment using the Arabic validated version of the 21-item Depression Anxiety Stress Scale (DASS-21) [27–29]. Each subscale consists of 7 items rated on a (0–3) Likert scale; 0 means that the item does not apply to self, 1 means that the items somewhat applies to self or some of the times, 2 means the item significantly applies to self and occasional times, and 3 indicates the item mostly applies to self and majority of times. The higher the score the greater the symptom severity. Raw scores for each subscale range from 0 to 21 and were multiplied by 2 to obtain DASS-42-equivalent scores ranging from 0 to 42. Severity categories were defined as follows: depression, normal 0–9, mild 10–13, moderate 14–20, severe 21–27, and extremely severe ≥ 28; anxiety, normal 0–7, mild 8–9, moderate 10–14, severe 15–19, and extremely severe ≥ 20; and stress, normal 0–14, mild 15–18, moderate 19–25, severe 26–33, and extremely severe ≥ 34.

The Arabic validated version of DASS-21 has demonstrated good psychometric properties, including high internal consistency and construct validity across both clinical and non-clinical populations [30].

The Cronbach’s alpha values for DASS-21 were measured for each subscale in our population. For depression subscale it was 0.886, for anxiety subscale it was 0.875, and for the stress subscale it was 0.875, all indicating strong reliability.

Validity and reliability

A pilot study (n = 20–30) was conducted to assess clarity and feasibility. Minor adjustments were made based on participants feedback to improve the structure and ensure ease of understanding and access to accurate and reliable information. Participants who took part in the pilot study were excluded from the final analytical sample of 611 participants.

Ethical approval

The study was registered with the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of An-Najah National University (IRB Reference #: Med.Nov.2025/198). This was an observational study, and no clinical trial interventions were involved. The research was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

The participants were informed about the purpose of the study, and written consent was obtained before their participation. Confidentiality and privacy were maintained throughout the study, with access to patient data restricted to the research team.

Statistical analysis

The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27 was used to analyse the data. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the study variables; categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables are presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). The data were not normally distributed, as tested by the Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test.

The Mann‒Whitney U test was used to assess the correlation between the non-normally distributed study variables and the IGD groups. Pearson’s chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables when expected cell counts were adequate, while Fisher’s exact test or the Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test was used when expected cell counts were small. Statistical significance was defined as a P value less than the threshold of 0.05 and a confidence interval (CI) of 95%.

Univariable logistic regression analyses were first conducted to examine factors associated with probable IGD among online gamers. Variables considered clinically or theoretically relevant, and those significantly associated with probable IGD in univariable analyses, were considered for inclusion in the multivariable logistic regression model. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Variables with substantial conceptual overlap were not simultaneously retained in the final model to reduce multicollinearity and limit overfitting.

Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to assess the relationship between IGD score and DASS-21 subscales (depression, anxiety, and stress). Independent variables included in the regression model were based on significant associations observed in the bivariate analysis. The final adjusted model was constructed using IGD score, age, gender, hours per day spent gaming, days per week gaming, gaming as escape from stress or negative emotions, and perceived impact of gaming on sleep. Regression coefficients with 95% CIs and p-values were reported. The presence of multicollinearity among the independent variables was evaluated via the variance inflation factor (VIF). VIF values below 5 were considered acceptable and indicated no evidence of significant multicollinearity among the predictors.

Additional linear regression assumptions were assessed using diagnostic plots and residual statistics. Linearity was assessed by inspecting scatterplots of standardized residuals against standardized predicted values. Homoscedasticity was assessed by examining whether residuals were evenly distributed across predicted values. Normality of residuals was evaluated using histograms and normal probability plots of standardized residuals. No major violations of linearity, homoscedasticity, or residual normality were detected. Standardized beta coefficients were also calculated to allow comparison of the relative strength of predictors across models.

Spearman rank-order correlation analyses were used to examine correlations among age, age at gaming initiation, IGD score, and DASS-21 depression, anxiety, and stress scores because these variables were not normally distributed.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the participants

A total of 611 participants were included in the study. The majority of the participants were females (65.5%), while males were 34.5% of the sample. Participants mostly resided in villages (68.7%), followed by cities (29.8%), and only 1.5% of our sample were residents of refugee camps.

Median age for participants was 22 [IQR: 20–26], with the majority either holding or currently enrolled in a bachelor or a diploma program (86.1%). Students constituted 60.4% of the sample, while employment status was only found in 27%, and unemployment status was in 12.7% of participants.

The majority were single (75%), living with their families (93.3), and 51.4% reported engagement in online gaming. (Table 1)

Gaming behavior patterns among participants

314 individuals reported engagement in online gaming, which constitutes 51.4% of the sample. Among the 314, only 74 individuals had probable IGD. The sociodemographic characteristics (age, gender, residence, educational level, employment status, or living situation) were not significantly associated with probable IGD. However, there was a significant association between probable IGD status and if participants had a monthly in-game spending (31.1% vs. 15.0%, p = 0.001).

While the duration of online gaming did not differ significantly between both groups of IGD status (p = 0.553), the time spent on gaming on a typical day and on off-days differed significantly (p < 0.001); longer hours were observed among individuals with probable IGD, especially past the 6 h mark (8.1% vs. 2.5% in regular days and 25.7% vs. 4.2% in off-days). Data presented similarly in anxiety (median 14 vs. 6), and stress subscales (median 18 vs. 10). (Table 4)

Factors associated with probable IGD among online gamers

In the multivariable logistic regression model, after adjustment for selected demographic and gaming-related factors, three predictors remained independently associated with probable IGD. Playing online with other players most of the time was associated with greater odds of probable IGD compared with playing alone (adjusted OR = 3.51, 95% CI: 1.08–11.46, p = 0.037). Moderate and high perceived impact of gaming on academic or work performance were also independently associated with probable IGD. Compared with participants reporting no academic or work impact, those reporting moderate impact had more than fourfold higher odds of probable IGD (adjusted OR = 4.38, 95% CI: 1.78–10.80, p = 0.001), while those reporting high impact had nearly fivefold higher odds (adjusted OR = 4.88, 95% CI: 1.61–14.81, p = 0.005). Gaming six days per week showed borderline significance (adjusted OR = 6.86, 95% CI: 1.00–47.04, p = 0.050). The multivariable model was statistically significant overall and showed acceptable calibration. (Table 5)

Psychological distress and IGD symptoms

In multivariable linear regression analyses, higher IGD scores were consistently associated with greater depression, anxiety, and stress scores after adjustment for age, gender, gaming frequency, gaming duration, use of gaming as emotional escape, and perceived sleep impact. Each one-point increase in IGD score was associated with a 1.28-point increase in depression score, a 1.18-point increase in anxiety score, and a 1.21-point increase in stress score, all p < 0.001. Younger age was independently associated with higher depression scores, but not anxiety or stress. Greater endorsement of gaming as an escape from stress or negative emotions was independently associated with higher depression, anxiety, and stress scores.

After reverse-coding gaming as escape from stress and gaming impact on sleep, higher endorsement of gaming as an escape from stress remained positively associated with depression, anxiety, and stress scores. Standardized coefficients showed that IGD score and gaming as escape from stress were the strongest predictors across the three DASS-21 subscales. (Table 6)

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, the prevalence of IGD was assessed amongst 611 Palestinian adults and its psychological correlates using the DASS-21. In our study, 23.5% of 314 participants who reported online gaming were identified as having probable IGD, comparable to rates found in Saudi university students, where 21.5% among 843 students had a probable IGD [31].

In contrast, a meta-analysis of global prevalence of IGD showed a much lower prevalence than our study [13], indicating that our 23.5% (12.1% of total sample) might be elevated due to self-reporting, sampling frame, cutoff choice or cultural factors.

Our study shows that IGD score was significantly associated with all DASS-21 subscales; depression, anxiety, and stress (p < 0.001), results consistent with a cross-sectional study using DASS-21, which found a significant association between IGD and depression, anxiety and stress [32], and with a study of 548 participants aged 15 years and older, recruited from various educational institutions, participants with IGD reported significantly higher levels of depression, anxiety, and stress, with a notable proportion falling into the “severe” and “extremely severe” categories as classified by the DASS-21 [5], and another study which concluded individuals with IGD use less cognitive reappraisal and more expressive suppression, which can hinder emotional recovery and contribute to the onset or worsening of depression [33]. Additionally, studies demonstrated associations between higher IGD scores and levels of perceived stress, hostility and psychological well-being [34, 35].

In the current study, gaming as a way to cope with or escape from stress and negative emotions was more commonly reported among participants with probable IGD. This finding is consistent with previous evidence showing that escape-related gaming motives are associated with IGD symptoms and problematic gaming behaviors [36]. Although gaming may provide short-term stress relief [37], mood improvement, recovery from stress [38] and psychosocial benefits for some individuals [4], reliance on gaming as an emotional coping strategy may also reflect maladaptive coping and greater psychological distress.

Participants with probable IGD more frequently reported perceived negative effects of gaming on academic/work performance, social relationships, and sleep, similar to studies which have found a moderate negative correlation between IGD and academic performance [39], social relationships [40–42], and sleep [43]. This interpretation is consistent with broader addiction literature showing that psychosocial resources such as hope, perceived social support, resilience, stigma, and caregiving burden can shape addiction-related functioning and quality of life [19, 20]. This reliance on gaming as an emotional coping strategy may be particularly relevant in the Palestinian context, where limited mental health resources, economic strain, academic pressures, and restricted access to structured recreational alternatives may reduce opportunities for adaptive stress relief. In such circumstances, online gaming may provide temporary distraction, emotional relief, and social connection. However, when gaming becomes a primary coping strategy, it may reinforce avoidance-based coping and increase vulnerability to functional impairment [22].

Multiplayer gaming was significantly associated with probable IGD. This may be explained by social reinforcement, competition, peer obligations, and fear of missing out, which may promote more persistent or compulsive gaming [44, 45].

Although longer gaming duration was associated with probable IGD in univariable analysis, this association was attenuated after adjustment for other gaming-related and psychological factors, suggesting that gaming time alone may not fully explain the relationship between gaming behavior and psychological distress [46]. In contrast, perceived academic or work-related impact of gaming remained independently associated with probable IGD. This suggests that problematic gaming in this sample was not defined simply by the amount of time spent gaming, but rather by the extent to which gaming interfered with daily responsibilities and functioning. Therefore, high gaming engagement should be distinguished from probable IGD, which is better characterized by impaired control, persistence despite negative consequences, and functional impairment, consistent with DSM-5 criteria and previous evidence distinguishing screen time from addictive digital media use [12, 46] .

Recommendations and implications

The findings suggest that interventions should focus not only on reducing gaming time, but also on addressing the reasons individuals use gaming as a coping strategy. Screening programs in universities, primary care, and community mental health settings may benefit from including brief questions about gaming-related functional impairment, gaming used to escape stress, sleep disturbance, and co-occurring symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress. Because escapism was an important factor in this study, feasible interventions may include coping-skills training, stress-management education, sleep hygiene counseling, psychoeducation about healthy gaming habits, and referral pathways for individuals with high psychological distress. Public health messaging should avoid pathologizing all gaming and instead distinguish recreational gaming from gaming associated with impaired functioning or psychological distress.

Strength and limitations

This study has several strengths, including a relatively large sample size, (n = 611), which increases precision, the use of validated Arabic questionnaires for IGD and DASS-21 with detailed inquiries of gaming habits which allowed for meaningful subgroups and variable analyses. On the other hand there were limitations from the use of online convenience sampling and self-reported measures, which may limit representativeness and introduce selection or reporting bias. Also, the predominance of female and highly educated participants may also limit generalizability to the wider Palestinian adult population. In addition, because of the cross-sectional design, the direction of association between IGD symptoms and depression, anxiety, and stress cannot be determined. Psychological distress may contribute to problematic gaming as a coping strategy, but problematic gaming may also worsen distress through sleep disruption, impaired functioning, and social consequences. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the temporal and potentially bidirectional relationships between IGD and psychological distress.

The high prevalence of probable IGD should be interpreted cautiously. Convenience sampling and online recruitment may have increased the likelihood of participation by individuals who were more active online, more engaged in gaming, or experiencing psychological distress. Therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to all Palestinian adults.

Conclusion

This study highlights probable IGD as an emerging behavioral health concern among Palestinian adult online gamers, particularly when gaming is linked to functional impairment and use as a coping strategy for stress or negative emotions. The findings suggest that assessment should move beyond gaming duration alone and consider gaming-related consequences, psychological distress, and coping motives. Future research should use longitudinal and clinically assessed designs to clarify temporal relationships between IGD and psychological distress. Prevention and intervention efforts should focus on healthy coping skills, early screening, and accessible support for individuals whose gaming is associated with distress or impairment.
