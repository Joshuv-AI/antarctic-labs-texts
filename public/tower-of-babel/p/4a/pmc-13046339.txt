Use of a ceramic surgical bur for dental implant uncovering: A pilot randomized controlled trial
Authors: Marco Aurélio Mena, Caio Vinicius Roman-Torres, Yeon Jung Kim, Luiz Felipe Palma, Vinicius Resende Ferreira, Luana de Campos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Dental implant uncovering, also referred to as second-stage surgery, is a minor surgical procedure traditionally performed using scalpels, which may result in bleeding and postoperative discomfort. In recent years, alternative techniques such as the use of surgical burs have been introduced, aiming to provide more comfortable and faster procedures, along with improved esthetic outcomes. However, scientific evidence regarding these alternative methods for dental implant uncovering remains limited, and certain clinical aspects are still a concern. The present study aimed to evaluate clinical outcomes associated with the use of a ceramic surgical bur for implant uncovering – specifically, thermal changes and postoperative pain – in comparison with the conventional technique employing a circular scalpel punch as the control.Materials and Methods:Ten single-unit osseointegrated implants, fully submerged beneath gingival tissue, were included and divided into two groups: the scalpel punch group (n = 5) and the ceramic bur group (n = 5). In addition, three specimens of residual soft tissue were collected from each group for histological analysis.Results:The results revealed no differences in any of the evaluated clinical parameters between the two groups, and no thermal damage was observed in the histological specimens.Conclusion:Based on these findings, the use of ceramic surgical burs for dental implant uncovering appears to be a safe technique, demonstrating comparable outcomes to the conventional scalpel method. Nevertheless, further studies are necessary to draw more definitive conclusions.

INTRODUCTION

The surgical protocol for dental implants is typically performed in two phases. The first stage consists of implant installation into the alveolar bone, and the second stage (following osseointegration) involves surgical exposure of the implant and the placement of a healing abutment.[12] This latter stage, also referred to as implant uncovering, serves to prepare the implant site for subsequent prosthetic rehabilitation. During this phase, particular attention is given to establishing peri-implant soft-tissue stability – a critical determinant of both peri-implant health maintenance and long-term prosthetic success.[23]

Many techniques for implant uncovering have been employed over the years, each with specific characteristics regarding soft-tissue management, wound healing, and gingival architecture preservation. Among the most common approaches are the conventional technique (alveolar ridge incision using a surgical blade or circular scalpel) and the combination of flap surgery with connective tissue grafting.[2] To achieve greater efficacy and improved patient comfort, alternative techniques have been investigated, including the use of high-power lasers and surgical burs.[245]

Although surgical burs are a recent introduction in dentistry, with few published studies available, existing research confirms their efficacy for surgical interventions, particularly in periodontal soft tissues.[4567] In Brazil, the commercialization of surgical burs is even more recent, having entered the market only within the last 2 years. These burs are made of composite ceramics, including yttrium-partially stabilized zirconium dioxide and aluminum-based ceramics. They provide smooth and delicate incisions, and the generated heat appears to contribute to local hemostasis control, resulting in minimal bleeding with low necrosis risk.[46] Surgical burs appear to be faster and more comfortable to use;[45] however, there is currently no scientific evidence supporting their efficacy in implant uncovering procedures.

The present study aimed to evaluate clinical outcomes associated with the use of a ceramic surgical bur for implant uncovering – including thermal changes and postoperative pain – in comparison with the conventional technique employing a circular scalpel punch as the control.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A randomized pilot study was conducted in a Brazilian private office (São Paulo, Brazil) with a convenience sample. The study protocol was approved by a Research Ethics Committee (#5.654.833) before any clinical procedure, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Individuals aged 18 or older and requiring a second-stage implant surgery (complete implant coverage by gingival tissue) for a single molar tooth replacement were considered eligible for inclusion in the study. The following exclusion criteria were applied: systemic diseases or medications known to alter healing processes and any oncological treatment.

Ten patients were randomly assigned to receive either the conventional scalpel technique (control group, n = 5) or a technique using a ceramic surgical bur (bur group, n = 5) for second-stage surgery of fully-submerged dental implants, i.e., installation of a healing abutment. For this purpose, considering blocks of five individuals, randomization was performed using a computer-generated table, and the treatment group for each patient was allocated to a numbered, opaque, sealed envelope, which was opened immediately before surgery.

All surgeries involved intraoral antisepsis with aqueous-based 0.12% chlorhexidine and local anesthesia using a solution of 3% lidocaine and 1:50,000 epinephrine (Lidostesim AD 3%™, DLA Pharmaceutical, Brazil). A 4-mm circular scalpel punch mounted on a 20:1 surgical dental implant handpiece at 800 rpm and without cooling was used in patients of the control group, and then the incised tissue covering the implant area was gently dissected and removed. In patients of the bur group, SOFT BUR™ (Microdont, São Paulo, Brazil) was mounted on a high-speed dental handpiece at 300,000–450,000 rpm and without irrigation for performing a 4-mm rounded incision over the implant area [Figure 1]; the incised tissue covering the implant site was gently dissected and removed.

Intraoperative use of the ceramic surgical bur for implant uncovering

A healing abutment was installed following the uncovering procedure in all patients. No sutures were placed, and an analgesic was prescribed for systemic use in case of local pain. The excised mucosal tissue specimens were collected for subsequent processing and analysis.

To measure the local temperature generated during the uncovering procedures, a high-resolution thermographic camera with a resolution of 1440 × 1080 pixels (Flir One Pro®, Flir Systems, Sweden) connected to a smartphone (iPhone 11 Pro Max, Apple, USA) was used at a distance of 10 cm from the surgical site. The camera has a temperature measurement range from −20°C to 400°C, a thermal sensitivity of 70 mK, a thermal pixel size of 12 µm, and a frame rate of 8.7 Hz. Only the maximum temperatures reached during the surgical procedures were considered for statistical analysis.

Postoperative pain was assessed at 24 h, 48 h, and 7 days using a Visual Analogue Scale, consisting of a horizontal line marked from 0 (indicating “no pain”) to 10 (indicating “worst possible pain”). Patients were asked to report their pain either in person or via text message.

Histological analyses were performed for three representative samples from each group using light microscopy with hematoxylin and eosin (H and E) and Picrosirius staining. For light microscopy processing, the excised mucosal tissue specimens were rinsed in phosphate buffer and subjected to dehydration in ascending concentrations of ethanol, followed by xylene, and then embedded in paraffin. Sections of 5 µm thickness were obtained using a rotary microtome and stained with H and E and Picrosirius. Coverslips were mounted using Entellan™ (Merck, Germany), and the slides were subsequently analyzed and photographed under polarized light microscopy. Signs of thermal-induced changes were examined and thoroughly described across the various structural layers of the oral mucosa.

The patients’ demographic data, thermal measurements, and pain scores were organized using Microsoft Excel™ software and subsequently subjected to descriptive and inferential statistical analyses using the Jamovi 2.6.44™ software. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Histological findings were categorized and described separately for each group.

RESULTS

The age of patients in the control group ranged from 27 to 39 years, with a mean of 32.2 ± 6.22 years. In the bur group, patient ages ranged from 27 to 43 years, with a mean of 38.4 ± 7.06 years. Regarding sex distribution, the control group consisted exclusively of women (100%), whereas the bur group included two women (40%) and three men (60%).

In the control group, the second-stage surgeries were performed for the rehabilitation of the following teeth (according to the Universal Numbering System): 30 (n = 2), 19 (n = 2), and 14 (n = 1); and for the bur group: 30 (n = 1), 31 (n = 1), 18 (n = 1), 14 (n = 1), and 15 (n = 1) [Table 1].

No thermal effect was observed in the control group, as the baseline temperature was not altered by the use of the scalpel. The mean temperature in this group was 37.2°C ± 0.447°C, with a median of 37°C. In the bur group, the mean temperature reached 41.4°C ± 6.02°C, with a median of 40°C.

Given that the data did not follow a normal distribution (assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test) and did not meet the assumption of homoscedasticity (assessed by Levene’s test), the Mann–Whitney U-test was applied to compare the groups and revealed no statistically significant difference (P = 0.373) [Table 2].

DISCUSSION

The present study evaluated the comparative clinical outcomes associated with the use of a conventional circular scalpel and a ceramic surgical bur in dental implant uncovering procedures. Although both techniques are considered safe and effective for soft-tissue incisions in the oral cavity, current literature lacks studies directly comparing them in implant dentistry.

The use of surgical burs has emerged as a promising alternative for reducing postoperative pain when compared to conventional techniques and has shown other favorable clinical outcomes in Periodontology, particularly in procedures for gingival depigmentation.[56] A study by Mikhail et al.,[4] which compared a diode laser, surgical bur, and conventional scalpel, reported reduced postoperative pain in the first two groups. In contrast, the present findings did not reveal any significant differences in pain scores between the techniques or across the evaluated time points. Histological analysis, however, revealed areas of epithelial and connective tissue laceration in the examined specimens.

Other reported clinical advantages of surgical burs include the combination of precise cutting and the heat generated during the procedure, which may contribute to effective hemostasis and improved bleeding control.[8] These findings were supported by Negi et al.,[6] who compared bleeding outcomes following diode laser, surgical bur, and scalpel blade use in gingival depigmentation procedures, and observed that the conventional technique yielded the least favorable results. Histologically, however, no evidence of heat-induced coagulative hemostasis was observed in the present study.

The findings presented herein indicate no significant differences in intraoperative temperature variation or postoperative pain across three evaluation time points between the groups undergoing implant uncovering using either a circular scalpel punch or a surgical bur. These results may suggest that both techniques are considered clinically comparable. The authors believe that discrepancies with previous literature may be attributed to the nature and design of this pilot study, which involved a limited sample size without a prior sample size calculation.

Nevertheless, pilot studies such as this are essential for advancing knowledge on specific topics and serve as critical precursors for larger clinical trials.[9] Given the novelty of this comparison in the literature, statistical parameters such as the treatment effect size on dependent variables may now serve as a basis for future well-designed studies that will provide robust evidence to support clinical decision-making.

CONCLUSION

Within the limitations of this pilot study, both the conventional circular scalpel technique and the ceramic surgical bur demonstrated comparable outcomes in terms of safety and effectiveness for second-stage dental implant surgery. Therefore, clinicians may base their choice on other factors, such as cost, operative time, and technical preference. Further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm these preliminary findings.
