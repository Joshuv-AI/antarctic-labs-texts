Use of patient-reported outcomes version of the common terminology criteria for adverse events in oncology clinical trials
Authors: Lauren Rogak, Jamie K. Forschmiedt, Ethan Basch, Gina L. Mazza, Minji K. Lee, Eric A. Meek, Brenda Ginos, Blake T. Langlais, Brie N. Noble, Allison M. Deal, Claire Yee, Gita Thanarajasingam, Amylou C. Dueck
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The Patient-Reported Outcomes version of the Common Terminology for Adverse Events® (PRO-CTCAE®) item library enables patient self-reporting of symptoms in cancer clinical trials. It was developed as a companion to CTCAE v4.0. The aim of this analysis is to understand how PRO-CTCAE has been used since its release. Trials using PRO-CTCAE were identified on clinicaltrials.gov. Trial characteristics were descriptively analyzed. Number of trials by year posted on clinicaltrials.gov was tested for increasing trend using Poisson regression. Among 327 identified trials, 318 (97 %) were in oncology, 269 (82 %) were interventional, and 170 (52 %) were randomized. Number of trials significantly increased over time (P < .001). PRO-CTCAE has had substantial and growing use in oncology trials since its release.

The National Cancer Institute (NCI) developed the Patient-Reported Outcomes version of the Common Terminology for Adverse Events (PRO-CTCAE®) to enable patients to self-report their symptoms while undergoing cancer treatment on clinical trials. The PRO-CTCAE is a 124-item library, measuring 78 symptomatic adverse events (AEs) [1]. It was released for public use as a companion to CTCAE v4.0, which is the gold standard for clinician-based assessment of adverse events. In the original PRO-CTCAE validation work, the item library has been validated in diverse patient populations [2], translated and linguistically validated in Spanish [3], and assessed for impacts of mode of administration [4] and different recall periods [5]. Subsequently, PRO-CTCAE was embedded in cancer clinical trials to assess feasibility of collecting patient-reported symptomatic AEs [6,7]. After the completion of the validation work, PRO-CTCAE has been included in cancer clinical trials both nationally and internationally, as well as translated into over 60 languages.

PRO-CTCAE was developed due to evidence [8,9] that clinicians miss symptomatic AEs experienced by patients while enrolled in clinical trials. PRO-CTCAE was tested and validated for patient self-reporting in variable study designs, including in-between clinic visits, pre-and post-surgical procedures, during chemotherapy and radiation, and after completion of all treatment to monitor the persistence of AEs long-term.

In this analysis, the aim is to understand how PRO-CTCAE has been used since its release. The knowledge gained from understanding how PRO-CTCAE has been used can aid researchers in designing future trials.

For clinical trials, study details are entered in clinicaltrials.gov, which was queried to extract and review information about clinical trials that use PRO-CTCAE. The searching feature of clinicaltrials.gov was used in May 2024; search terms “PRO-CTCAE” and “PRO CTCAE” as quoted strings were entered into the “outcome measures” and “other terms” fields to identify possible trials. Resulting trials were exported and manually reviewed by two independent reviewers. Additionally, trials that had the protocol and/or statistical analysis plan available in clinicaltrials.gov were reviewed by two independent reviewers to further assess the characteristics of PRO-CTCAE implementation. In this subset of studies, PRO-CTCAE items selected for use, as well as mode of administration (electronic vs. paper), location of completion of item surveys (home vs. in-clinic), and planned analysis of items were reviewed and tabulated. Trial characteristics were descriptively analyzed with frequencies and relative frequencies. Number of trials by year posted on clinicaltrials.gov was tested for increasing trend using Poisson regression.

The initial search identified 353 potential trials, of which 26 were excluded for having unknown or withdrawn study status. No other studies were excluded by manual review, resulting in 327 trials that included PRO-CTCAE as an outcome. 318/327 (97 %) were in oncology (including trials for hematopoietic stem cell transplantation), with 74/318 (23 %), 60/318 (19 %), and 46/318 (14 %) enrolling patients with hematologic malignancies, breast cancers, and lung cancers, respectively (Fig. 1). 18/327 (6 %) trials enrolled patients under the age of 18. 269/327 (82 %) trials were interventional and most using randomized designs (170/327 trials, 52 %) (Supplemental Table 1). A subset of trials (36/327, 11 %) had protocols and/or statistical analysis plans available for review in clinicaltrials.gov. None of these studies were excluded from the subset analysis (Supplemental Table 2). 35/36 (97 %) were in oncology, with 7/35 (20 %), 9/35 (26 %), and 4/35 (11 %) enrolling patients with hematologic malignancies, breast cancers, and lung cancers, respectively. The one trial that was not oncology-specific was a trial that enrolled women undergoing hysteroscopy. One trial (3 %) enrolled patients under the age of 18. All 36 trials (100 %) were interventional, with most testing treatments (20/36 trials, 56 %), and most using randomized designs (28/36 trials, 78 %).

A review of the 36 protocols show that 31 (86 %) reported the PRO-CTCAE items employed in the study. These 31 trials used an average of 11.4 items, with nausea (23/31 trials, 74 %), diarrhea (16/31 trials, 52 %), and vomiting (16/31 trials, 52 %) being the most frequent (Fig. 3). Notably, 5/36 (14 %) trials reported using PRO-CTCAE items but did not specify which ones were included. An analysis of where PRO-CTCAE items were collected showed that 19/36 (53 %) studies collected surveys only in clinic, 7/36 (19 %) only at home, and 10/36 (28 %) at both; while 8/36 (22 %) collected surveys using only electronic methods (tablets, computer), 12/36 (33 %) only paper, 1 (3 %) only by telephone, and 7/36 (19 %) did not specify. In 31/36 trials (86 %) PRO-CTCAE items were collected from all participants, while 5/36 (14 %) collected PRO-CTCAE from a subset of participants. Of the 36 trials, the following methods for analysis of reporting were mentioned in their analysis plan: 14 (39 %) hypothesis testing (e.g., t-tests, chi-square tests), 7 (19 %) statistical modeling, 2 (6 %) graphical visualizations, 3 (8 %) plan for handling missing data, and 24 (67 %) descriptive analysis. Of this subset, 20/36 (56 %) were cancer-directed trials, defined as trials enrolling participants with cancer in which the purpose of the study intervention was to treat the cancer. Since its inception, there has been an increase in the use of PRO-CTCAE in clinical trials year after year. With this increase, there has been a wide diversity in the types of trial settings, patient populations and study designs, in which PRO-CTCAE is utilized. This analysis shows that PRO-CTCAE is used most often in interventional cancer clinical trials with patients diagnosed with hematological, breast, and lung cancers. However, the analysis shows that researchers are utilizing PRO-CTCAE in increasing numbers across cancer types, as well as non-interventional clinical trials and in a small but growing number of non-cancer clinical trials.

Recent efforts by the US Food and Drug Administration and the National Cancer Institute have suggested that the most relevant symptomatic AEs for clinical trials are consistently nausea, fatigue and diarrhea [10]. As a result, these AEs can be used as a core set of symptoms that should be assessed when using PRO-CTCAE, in addition to other expected toxicities based on treatments being tested [10]. This analysis supports that many trials are already regularly assessing these symptomatic AEs, in that nausea was used in 23/31 trials (74 %), diarrhea in 16/31 trials (52 %) and fatigue in 15/31 trials (48 %) (Fig. 2).

In the in-depth analysis of the subset of clinical trials with available protocols and/or statistical analysis plans, we observed that PRO-CTCAE is regularly administered at baseline and nearly always administered during treatment. There are several documented analysis approaches for PRO-CTCAE [11,12], which is reflected in the 36 protocols that were examined in detail. Most trials reported descriptive statistics, various statistical testing approaches such as t-tests for comparison of means or chi-square tests for comparison of proportions, as well as mixed modeling to account for correlation within participants. We also found the PRO-CTCAE is most completed on paper and in the clinic, but with a significant number allowing for electronic administration and for completion at home.

In addition to being used to assess AEs from the patient perspective in cancer treatment trials, PRO-CTCAE is also being used as an interventional platform as a monitoring tool in patients following surgery [13], patients receiving outpatient chemotherapy [13], and patients receiving palliative chemotherapy [14]. These studies demonstrate some of the strengths of using participant-reported symptomatic AEs allowing for frequent assessment of symptoms, and for participants to report from home on their personal devices. These studies also illustrate how the PRO-CTCAE can be incorporated into research depending on the needs of the trial as well as integrated into standard of care practices.

Further in-depth analysis of clinical trial protocols and statistical analysis plans would lead to a greater understanding of how PRO-CTCAE has been used, and the ways in which researchers can utilize it as a tool in the future. In this analysis, 36 out of 327 (11 %) had protocols or statistical analysis plans available for review in clinicaltrials.gov. The subset of clinical trials with available information in clinicaltrials.gov still had missing information that would enhance this analysis and the understanding of how PRO-CTCAE has been used. This study is limited to those that have PRO-CTCAE listed as a primary or secondary outcome and may be missing clinical trials which list PRO-CTCAE as an exploratory endpoint. Albeit limited information on optimal statistical analysis approaches were available at the time these protocols were written and future protocols should incorporate current recommended approaches such as those outlined by Dueck et al. [15] One approach to capturing symptoms not assessed in structured PRO-CTCAE items included in a given clinical trial is the inclusion of an open-ended item, also called the “free text” item. Though not systematically listed in clinical trial protocols, this item has been included in practice and reported for clinical trials such as: Chung et al. and Snyder et al. [16,17].

At its inception, PRO-CTCAE was developed as a companion to CTCAE v.4.0. Since then, CTCAE has been updated to v5.0, with an additional update (v6.0) forthcoming. An analysis of PRO-CTCAE items and the frequency of usage would be benefited by a comparison to the most up-to-date version of CTCAE and can lead to an update of PRO-CTCAE items to better collect symptomatic AEs from patients undergoing cancer treatments. Future explorations can include use in observational studies and/or cancer care, optimal reporting of PRO-CTCAE results in clinical trials, and updating the item library to account for changes to CTCAE.

PRO-CTCAE has had substantial and growing use in oncology trials since its release. The breadth of clinical trials that have incorporated PRO-CTCAE are diverse in all aspects of clinical trial design, including patient population, symptom choice, mode of administration, and statistical analysis.
