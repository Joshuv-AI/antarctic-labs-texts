A Comparison of Attitudes About Rural Practice Among Family Physicians in Japan and Oregon
Authors: Wataru Sasaki, Patricia A. Carney, Robert Jackman, Cort Cox, Melinda M. Davis, Masakazu Nakamura, Daisuke Yamashita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and ObjectiveIn Japan and the U.S., the number of physicians per population is lower in rural areas than in urban areas, and the shortage of physicians in rural areas is an urgent issue in both countries. Physicians are more likely to remain in rural areas if they are more satisfied with their work and life, but no studies have been conducted simultaneously in more than one country on this topic.MethodsThis cross‐sectional survey study simultaneously assessed the attitudes about rural practice and life in rural settings among a convenience sample of physicians living and working in rural Japan and Oregon (US). The 40‐item survey assessed demographic and rural background, clinical practice information, and satisfaction with rural work and life.ResultsIn Japan, 209 out of 592 respondents (35.3%) responded to the survey, and in Oregon, 66 out of 630 respondents (10.5%) responded to the survey. Oregon physicians were more satisfied than Japanese physicians on enjoying a broader scope of practice in rural areas (4.56 vs. 3.78; p < 0.001), being able to access the latest medical information (4.35 vs. 2.75; p < 0.001), having someone to talk to about work and life issues (3.75 vs. 3.04; p < 0.001), feeling part of the rural community where they work (4.42 vs. 3.76; p < 0.001), and perceiving their children are receiving a satisfactory education (3.66 vs. 3.04; p < 0.001).ConclusionPhysicians in Japan and Oregon were satisfied with working and living in rural areas. Japanese rural physicians' backgrounds and satisfaction levels differed significantly from those in Oregon.

Introduction

In Japan, 69.9% of the country is rural (less than 500 people per square mile), and this is where 12.0% of the population lives [1]. Overall, the number of physicians per capita in Japan has increased each year (2022: 247.2 per 100,000 people; 1982: 141.5 per 100,000 people) [2]. However, the number of physicians in rural areas remains lower than in urban areas (222.6 per 100,000 people; rural areas: 111.7 per 100,000 people in 2002) [3]. Since 1956, the national and prefectural governments have dispatched physicians to rural areas and provided career development support to alleviate the shortage of physicians working in rural areas [4]. Since 2008, many universities have established regional quotas and provided scholarships to students who enroll and work in the region after graduation [5, 6]. However, about 12% leave the region within 3 years of starting work [7], and only about 25% remain in the region after completing the nine‐year obligation [8].

In the United States (US), 97% of the country is rural, and this is where 19.3% of the population lives [9, 10], but only 8.9% of all physicians work in rural areas [11], which is similar to Japan. Several university and medical school‐based training programs educate those interested in medicine about practice in rural areas [12]. Since 1970, the number of physicians choosing rural practice has increased in response to these programs [13, 14], but the percentage of physicians working in rural areas compared to the total number of physicians in the US has actually decreased [13].

Many studies have explored how best to support the retention of physicians in rural areas [15, 16]. It has been reported worldwide, particularly in the U.S., Canada, and Australia, that physicians are more likely to remain in rural areas if they are more satisfied with both their work and personal life [17, 18, 19]. These same studies suggest that more research is needed to clarify more specifically what they are satisfied and/or dissatisfied with. A recent study has reported on factors related to burnout among physicians working in rural areas of the U.S. Midwest [20]. In Japan, a large‐scale study on the satisfaction of physicians working in rural areas was published in 2004 [21]. However, this study is now two decades old, and these findings need to be updated to reflect current status. In addition, no studies have been conducted simultaneously in more than one region on satisfaction with rural practice. Thus, it is unknown how the unique healthcare systems and lifestyles in different countries affect rural physician satisfaction.

The purpose of this study was to simultaneously assess the attitudes about rural practice and life in rural settings among physicians living and working in both rural Japan and Oregon (US). By comparing the two countries, we hoped to discover new and persistent factors related to physician satisfaction in rural areas.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This cross‐sectional study involved administering a survey to a convenience sample of primary care physicians practicing in rural Oregon and Japan. Physician assistants (PA) and nurse practitioners (NP) were excluded from the study because PAs and NPs are not licensed health professionals in Japan. We selected and recruited physicians working at “Hekichi‐Shinryojo (Rural Clinic).” Hekichi‐Shinryojo is designated by local government to resolve medically underserved areas due to lack of clinic and remote distant to hospitals [22]. Additionally physicians working at Japan Association for Development of Community Medicine (JADECOM) Practice Based Research Network located within depopulated areas were also recruited. “Depopulated areas,” are designated by the Japanese government as having a rapidly declining population, a high ratio of elderly people, and weak local government finances [23]. We considered the definition to be similar to that of Hekichi‐Shinryojyo (Rural Clinic). In Oregon, classification of physicians as rural is defined as any geographic area in Oregon that is ten or more miles from a population center of 40,000 people or more [24]. We obtained mailing address for physicians in rural practice from Oregon Office of Rural Health. Physicians practicing in rural areas for less than 3 years were excluded in both countries because we wanted to survey a stable well informed population. The area, population, and population density of each region are as follows: Japan: 378,000 km2, 124,352,000, 328.9/km2, Oregon: 248,607 km2, 4,237,256, 17.0/km2 [25, 26]. We collected postal codes of practice locations in the survey to assess the rurality, focusing on the healthcare needs of practice locations of the survey respondents. The Oregon Area of Unmet Health Care Need Report (OAUHCNR) score was used in Oregon [27]. The RIJ for Japan (RIJ) was used with permission in Japan [28].

Development and Testing of the Study Survey

The 40‐item survey had three sections: (1) Demographic and rural background (10 items) (age, gender, marital and parental status, history of living in rural area up to high school, history of rural area experience during medical school and residency, financial support for rural area employment); (2) clinical practice information (7 items) (work hours, frequency of on‐call, number of other medical clinicians, clinical areas of practice); and (3) satisfaction with rural work and life (17 items). Six additional open‐text response questions were also asked to get more detailed information on satisfaction. Questions were obtained from previous studies on satisfaction and intention to work in rural areas [15, 16]. We used the same definitions of rural to identify the study population when asking whether the participants in the US or Japan grew up in rural areas.

After the English version of the questionnaire was developed, the lead author (WS) translated it into Japanese. Next, we tested the English version of the survey using cognitive interview techniques by administering the survey to physicians familiar with primary care and asking participants to assess the readability and response scales for clarity and the question ordering to avoid using leading or otherwise biased questions [29]. The Japanese version of the survey was also pilot‐tested by bilingual Family physicians who practiced in both countries, and revisions were made based on the pilot testing. Lastly, the wording in English and Japanese was checked to be sure the questions were as close to identical as possible.

Survey Administration

The final version of the survey was administered using both an online platform (Qualtrics) and a paper version between June 2023 and September 2023. Nearly 600 (n = 592) physicians in Japan were included in the study. Of these, 568 physicians were identified through Rural clinic designation by the prefectural government [22]. Additionally, 24 physicians working in 20 clinics in the JADECOM‐PBRN that have at least one full‐time physician and are located in depopulated areas were also included in the study. All eligible physicians in Japan received an invitation to participate, a paper survey, and a postage‐paid return envelope. In Oregon, 630 physicians registered with the Oregon Medical Board (OMB) whose practice location met the Oregon Office of Rural Health (ORH) definition of a rural area were included.

The survey was constructed using the online platform Qualtrics, and participants could respond to the survey using the QR code included in the invitation letter, or they could fill out and return the survey using a paper version by mail. The study information sheet, including the purpose of the survey, the voluntary nature of participation, and the anonymity of respondents, was included at the beginning of the recruitment letter and email. The study was exempt from the written informed consent by the IRBs.

The survey asked the first question about the number of years in their current practice and excluded respondents who answered less than 3 years from the survey. The survey asked if the respondents intended to complete the survey and if the answer was “yes,” the survey opened; if the answer was “no,” the survey was terminated. Respondents could leave subsequent questions unanswered or opt out of the survey at any point during the survey.

Data Management and Analyses

We exported data from Qualtrics into SPSS v.29 and hand‐entered data from the paper version of the surveys into Excel (Microsoft Corp., WA, USA), which was then combined with the Qualtrics data uploaded into SPSS. Hand‐entry data underwent quality checks before analysis. Analyses included generating descriptive statistics, including frequencies, means, medians, modes, and standard deviations, to assess the shape of the data. We used the Mann–Whitney U test to assess for differences in continuous data and Chi‐Square for categorical data. All tests were two‐tailed with alpha set at 0.05 to determine statistical significance.

Ethics Statement

Each research team obtained IRB approval according to the regulations and laws of the relevant countries (OHSU IRB number: 25305, JADECOM IRB number: 20230413‐01).

Results

In Japan, 209/592 (35.3%) of invited subjects completed the survey. Of these, 24 were excluded because they had not been in practice for 3 years, three opted out of the study, and five were excluded because they only completed the demographic questions, leaving 177 respondents for inclusion in data analyses. In Oregon, 66 of the 630 (10.5%) subjects responded. Of these, nine were excluded because they had not been in practice for 3 years, and one was excluded because they only completed the demographic questions. This left 56 respondents for inclusion in analyses. Thus, a total of 233 surveys were included in analyses.

Oregon physicians were more likely to be responsible for inpatient care (42.9% vs. 8.5%; p < 0.001), maternity care (23.2% vs. 2.3%; p < 0.001), and prenatal care (28.6% vs. 1.7%; p < 0.001) than in Japan. Japanese physicians were more likely to be responsible for emergency care (67.8% vs. 12.5%; p < 0.001), home visits (87.0% vs. 37.5%; p < 0.001), and the local public health department (63.3% vs. 12.5%; p < 0.001). More Oregon clinics had medical students and residents in their practices (39.3% vs. 13.0%; p < 0.001).

When we compared opinions between Oregon and Japanese physicians about practicing and living in rural areas, we found that six of the 16 variables differed statistically, with Oregon physicians rating these variables higher than Japanese physicians (Table 3). These included higher scores on enjoying a broader scope of practice in rural areas (4.56 vs. 3.78; p < 0.001), being able to access the latest medical information (4.35 vs. 2.75; p < 0.001), having someone to talk to about work and life issues (3.75 vs. 3.04; p < 0.001), that their rural area is rich in natural beauty (4.71 vs. 4.12; p < 0.001), feeling part of the rural community where they work (4.42 vs. 3.76; p < 0.001), and perceiving their children are receiving a satisfactory education (3.66 vs. 3.04; p < 0.001). Many respondents in Japan and Oregon cited good relationships with patients and the community as attractive, and many respondents in Oregon cited proximity to work from home as attractive. In Japan and Oregon, respondents reported inconveniences in their lives, such as a lack of transportation and difficulty in obtaining necessities, while in Japan, many respondents reported burdens, such as not being able to take days off (Table 3).

Discussion

There were significant differences in the backgrounds of rural area physicians in Japan and those in Oregon. Oregon's rural area physicians were younger, more likely to be women, had more experience training in rural areas, received financial support, had more medical professionals to work with, and had a higher percentage of physicians involved in perinatal practice than in Japan. These differences could reflect changes in Oregon's medical education strategies that encourage physicians to practice in rural areas, such as changes in medical school admission processes, medical education, and financial aid [30, 31]. There are similar efforts in Japan, and the study supports those efforts to be considered in Japan [32, 33, 34]. A recent study showed part‐time work was correlated with less burnout in the rural area in the Midwest US [20]. Therefore, other features, such as job sharing or a system to allow part‐time employment, may also promote rural retention in Japan, given our study showed more physicians are working full‐time (10 more half days per week) in Japan.

In Japan, only one in five practicing physicians is female, and most practice in urban areas [35]. Our study showed a lower percentage of female respondents compared to the national average of physician breakdown by gender in Japan, whereas Oregon had a female percentage equivalent to the national average [36]. Investing in removing barriers to female participation in rural clinics may improve satisfaction among female clinicians and should be explored in future research in Japan. Our study also showed that rural physicians' age distribution was similar to the national average in Japan, but Oregon had fewer older physicians who responded to the survey compared to the national average [2, 36]. Further research exploring factors supporting Japanese physicians' ability to work later in their careers may provide insight into longevity or recruiting older physicians in Oregon rural practices.

While many studies have examined satisfaction with working in rural areas in different countries and regions, there has never been a study making direct comparisons of the differences in satisfaction between Japan and Oregon. Physicians working in rural Oregon were more satisfied with a “broad range of care,” “having someone to look to for advice,” “up‐to‐date knowledge,” “rich natural surroundings,” “feeling part of the community,” and “quality education for children.” However, it is important to note that the rate in these domains was high in both Japan and Oregon, except for “up‐to‐date knowledge.” Focusing on further promoting strengths that are common to both regions can encourage physicians to work in the rural areas in both regions. In regard to low satisfaction with “up‐to‐date knowledge” in Japan, according to a recent online survey not limited to rural areas in Japan, 62% of physicians reported relying on conference participation to obtain up‐to‐date knowledge, and 53% reported participation in CME or research meetings, compared to only 36% of physicians using online journals [37]. This may contribute to the difference between Japan and Oregon in that Oregon physicians can access information on demand, and not just during scheduled conferences. Interventions that reduce the physical barriers to continuous education and promote online evidence‐based medical information may improve satisfaction.

While opinions showed significant differences in satisfaction between Japan and Oregon, physicians in both regions were generally satisfied with working and living in rural areas. A high percentage of physicians in both regions reported that they could treat patients holistically and contribute to the community, suggesting that these attractions outweighed the disadvantages of inconveniences in daily life. These results were similar to those of previous studies in both regions [18, 38, 39, 40, 41].

Limitations

First, the response rate to the survey in Oregon was much lower than in Japan, which indicates the Oregon findings are not generalizable. Also, differences in recruiting Japanese and Oregon participants under the same conditions occurred because the definition of rural areas differs between Japan and Oregon. Thus, we recruited according to each country's definition of rural area, which may create confounding factors. For example, depopulated areas in Japan are designated based on criteria such as a high proportion of elderly residents and low municipal fiscal capacity, whereas rural Oregon does not consider these factors. Consequently, depopulated areas in Japan have fewer young people than rural Oregon. Third, we did not include PAs or NPs in these surveys because of different practice patterns in the U.S. and Japan for these professions. Given the role advanced practice plays in many rural settings, this may be an important professional group to engage in future studies of rural practice in the US or other countries [42, 43]. Fourth, we expect that many factors other than backgrounds affect satisfaction since the society cultures, and institutions in Japan and Oregon differ and warrant future exploration. Finally, this study compares Japan and Oregon; different findings are expected when comparing other states or the United States as a whole.

Conclusion

Physicians in Japan and Oregon included in this study reported being satisfied with working and living in rural areas. The backgrounds and satisfaction levels of Japanese rural physicians differed significantly from those in Oregon, suggesting opportunities to better inform recruitment and retention strategies for physicians through additional comparative research in both countries.
