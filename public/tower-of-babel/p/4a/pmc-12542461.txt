Comparison of the total and individual purine content of commercial maintenance dog foods and therapeutic dog foods that may be prescribed to prevent urate urolithiasis
Authors: Chie Saito Ishii, Karen C. Scott, Carolina Abrahan, Taylor A. Harmon, James C. Colee, Yann Queau, Vincent Biourge, Timothy J. Garrett, Richard C. Hill
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUrate is the third most common component of canine uroliths. Dogs with a genetic mutation or hepatic dysfunction excrete more urate salts and are predisposed to urate urolith formation. Documentation of total and individual purine content in commercial diets is clinically important because feeding diets containing fewer purines reduces urinary uric acid excretion and urolith formation.Materials and methodsNine purine metabolites were measured using ultra-high performance liquid chromatography and tandem mass spectrometry in samples of 3 batches of 14 dry and 10 canned diets from 3 manufacturers, including low-protein diets, vegetarian diets, diets designed to prevent urate uroliths, and maintenance diets. Concentrations were compared among diet groups using a linear mixed model design. Total concentrations are reported as least squares means and 95% confidence intervals.ResultsTotal purine content (μmol/Mcal) was lower (P < 0.0001) in prescription (736; 475–1053) than maintenance (2459; 1866–3133) diets, and lower in dry (464; 316–641) than canned (1704; 1327–2127) diets. Among dry prescription diets, vegetarian diets and urate urolith prevention diets contained the least purines, followed by low-protein diets; all contained < 630 μmol/Mcal purines except for one low-protein diet. Hypoxanthine concentrations were proportionately lower in vegetarian diets.ConclusionsFeeding dry vegetarian, urate urolith prevention, and some low-protein prescription diets to dogs should reduce urinary uric acid excretion more than feeding wet equivalents. However, factors that affect urinary urate crystallization, like urine pH and dilution, should also be considered. These findings will facilitate the selection of low-purine diets for individual dogs susceptible to urate urolithiasis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-025-05080-5.

Background

Urate uroliths are the third most common uroliths in dogs, accounting for 5–8% of all canine uroliths, and the second most common uroliths in dogs less than one year of age [1, 2]. Clinical signs are commonly recurrent and include urinary obstruction, haematuria and dysuria [1]. Urate uroliths are composed primarily of uric acid (UA) salts, with most being composed of ammonium urate [1, 3]. Urinary UA is a breakdown product of purines derived from the diet or de novo synthesis in the body [4]. Less soluble UA is usually metabolized by the hepatic enzyme, uricase, to more soluble allantoin, which is then excreted in dogs' urine [5–7]. However, increased urinary UA concentrations have been reported in various dog breeds, most notably Dalmatians, due to a defective urate transporter, SLC2A9, in hepatic and renal tubular cells. In addition, urinary excretion of both uric acid and ammonia increases in dogs with portosystemic shunts due to impaired hepatic conversion of UA to allantoin and decreased ammonia detoxification to urea by the liver [8–11].

Dietary modifications designed to prevent urate urolithiasis include feeding diets that contain fewer purines and also dilute and alkalinize urine to increase purine solubility [12]. Dietary purine restriction is recommended because increasing dietary purine intake has been reported to increase urinary UA excretion and the ratio of UA to allantoin in Dalmatian dogs [13, 14]. Also, a previous feeding trial conducted in healthy female Miniature Schnauzers showed that two low-purine diets resulted in lower urinary UA and allantoin concentrations than a maintenance (Maint) diet [15]. Dogs in that trial consumed on average 1090 kcal/day (personal communication), which suggests that the low purine diets contained < 630 μmol/Mcal. Research quantifying the purine content of pet foods remains limited. [16–18] A recent study measured the purine content of several dog prescription (Rx) diets, however, only 4 of the 11 diets evaluated are widely accessible in the United States [19].

Alternative dietary options are needed when Uprev diets are unavailable, unpalatable, too expensive, or a patient’s life stage or comorbidity requires a different dietary composition. Dietary purines are usually not measured nor documented on food labels of the package because purines are not essential nutrients. An alternative option is to select diets based on moisture and protein content, and that contain low purine ingredients. Nevertheless, finding appropriate combinations can be difficult because canned foods that dilute urine more than dry diets often contain animal-based ingredients, especially offal, that are high in purines. [20] Vegetarian (Veg) diets, and low-protein (LoPr) diets are likely to contain lower concentrations of purines if they do not contain organ meats, seafood, purine-rich flavors and palatability enhancers, purines from yeast, or purine-rich vegetables, such as soybeans [21, 22]. Purine content may be low in higher protein diets also if protein sources containing few purines are included. [23] Nevertheless, the purine content cannot necessarily be inferred from the protein content or ingredient list because the relative amounts of ingredients in pet foods are not documented and purines can be lost or degraded during cooking and storage [24–26].

Previous studies have only reported the total purine content in diets without distinguishing among the various purine bases, nucleosides, or nucleotides [19]. Different individual purine metabolites may vary in their capacity to affect urinary UA concentrations because they use different transporters for intestinal absorption and are interconverted [27–29]. For example, urinary UA concentrations increased more in people fed diets containing more hypoxanthine even though the total purine content did not change [30, 31]. Increased dietary total purines and hypoxanthine may also explain why dolphins in collections, but not wild dolphins, are predisposed to urate uroliths [32]. On the other hand, guanine in the diet had less influence on urinary UA concentrations than dietary adenosine monophosphate (AMP), inosine monophosphate (IMP), hypoxanthine, or adenine in people and rats [30, 31, 33].

Thus, the purpose of this study was to compare individual as well as total and relative purine metabolite concentrations in different types of commercial dog diets, to provide veterinarians more flexibility and accuracy when selecting diets to prevent urate urolithiasis. We hypothesized that total and individual purine concentrations and the relative proportions of individual purines would differ among Veg, Uprev, LoPr, and Maint diets, and between dry and canned diets.

Methods

Food selection

Dry and canned dog foods, which contain ≤ 20% or ≥ 65% moisture, respectively, were selected from three major manufacturers.1 Diet selection was from among dry and canned prescription dog foods available in each of the following categories: 1) Uprev diets designed to prevent urate uroliths2 2) Veg diets, which contain no animal-derived ingredients, 3 and 3) LoPr diets designed for the management of either liver dysfunction or chronic kidney disease, 4 and reported to contain less than 60 g of protein/Mcal ME. Urinary diets designed to acidify urine were excluded because urinary acidification is contraindicated for urate urolith prevention [12]. In the absence of alternatives, all available dry and wet formulations of Veg, Uprev, and LoPr (hepatic) diets from each manufacturer were included. For LoPr (renal) diets, which offered up to four variations in flavor and texture, a single product was selected from the available options. To select Maint diets likely to contain more purines, an additional list of the “most popular” 5 canned and 5 dry Maint diets in the US from these 3 manufacturers was created. On-line popularity was assessed in November 2020 by sorting for “best seller” or “number of reviews” on three online stores’ websites [34–36]. Maintenance diets designed for dogs in life stages other than adult were excluded.

The RAND function of Microsoft Office Excel 2019 (version 1808, Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) was used to select one dry and one canned food from the Maint and each LoPr (renal) diet category from each manufacturer.5 Bags or cans with three different batch numbers of each of the selected foods were purchased from local stores, veterinary clinics and online stores. In addition, it was postulated that hydrolysis used to produce hydrolyzed protein might degrade dietary purines and increase the proportion of purine nucleobases relative to other purines. To test this, samples from two different batches of each of two hydrolyzed protein (non-vegetarian) dry diets from one manufacturer6 were selected for supplemental analysis. A total of 70 different batches from 14 dry and 10 canned diets, were analyzed. (Suppl. 1).

Preparation of food samples

A 300-g sample of each dry diet and the entire contents of 1 large can (363–410 g) or 2 small cans (165 g) of each canned diet were individually ground through a 2 mm sieve (Wiley Mill, Thomas Scientific., Swedesboro, NJ, USA), then homogenized by hand, and numbered for identification. Samples were stored at −20 °C, and a portion of each frozen sample was sent (on dry ice) to a commercial laboratory (Dairy One, Inc., Ithaca, NY, USA) for nutrient analysis, with product names and batch numbers masked. The remainder of each sample was lyophilized to stable weight (Free Zone 2.5 Liter −50 °C Benchtop Freeze Dryers with 3-shelf drying compartment, Labconco, Kansas City, MO, USA). Dried samples were stored in polyethylene bags with zip closures, in a climate-controlled (25 °C) room and then freeze-dried again just before analysis to eliminate any moisture accumulated from ambient air.

Nutrient analysis

Nutrient analysis of crude protein, crude fat, crude fiber, ash, and moisture was performed by a commercial laboratory (Dairy One). If author CI subsequently found that the concentration of a nutrient among different batches of the same product differed by more than 20% from the mean, additional sub-samples of the food were analysed to confirm that the increased variation represented variation among batches and not analytical error. Metabolizable energy of each dog food was calculated from the nutrient composition using the predictive equation for ME of prepared dog foods recommended by the National Research Council that uses crude fiber content to estimate digestibility [37].

Purine extraction and pre-treatment

The method adapted from that described by Ardente et al. was used to extract nine purine metabolites in representative Rx and Maint dog foods [26, 38]. In short, 0.6 g of freeze-dried sample was mixed with 9.75 mL of H2O and 0.25 mL of 400 μg/mL 2,6-diaminopurine monohydrate (DAP; 97.0 + % DAP, Fisher Scientific) as an internal standard. The mixture was homogenized, sonicated, heated with a hot tub (95℃), and then cooled. The supernatant was filtered, and mixed with an equal amount of hexane, and then centrifuged. The aqueous (bottom) layer was transferred to new 2 mL tubes, and frozen at −20℃. It took nine days to complete all the sample extractions, so these extracted samples were stored for up to 16 days until analysis. An aliquot of thawed extract was mixed with stable-isotope-labeled mixed purine internal standard solution, and protein precipitation solution before analysis.

Purine analysis

Separation and quantification of eleven purine metabolites, adenine, guanine, hypoxanthine, xanthine, UA, adenosine, guanosine, inosine, AMP, IMP, and GMP were carried out using hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC)—high-resolution tandem mass spectrometry (HRMS/MS) as previously described [38]. Separation was achieved using Dionex Ultimate 3000 UHPLC (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on an InfinityLab Poroshell 120 HILIC-Z column, 150 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.9 μm (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). A total of 378 samples, each of 10 μL, were injected into the equipment, over 4 days, including 140 food samples (70 samples duplicated), external standards, and quality control (QC) samples, and additional replicate samples analyzed when duplicate values were inconsistent. Samples and QC food samples were analyzed on the same day they were extracted. Each individual sample was injected and run by HILIC-HRMS/MS twice to mitigate possible analytical errors. The order of extracted samples analyzed was shuffled randomly (RAND function of Microsoft Office Excel 2019). Authors performing the chromatographic analyses were blind to diet or batch.

Purine metabolites were identified by mass and retention time using mass spectrometry (MS) with a Q Exactive hybrid orbitrap Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with heated electrospray ionization (HESI). The peak area ratio of the external standards relative to labelled internal standards at the different concentrations of the external standards was used to create a calibration curve. The purine concentration of the sample (μM) was determined by substituting the peak area ratio of the sample to the internal standard into the equation obtained from the calibration curve. Purine concentration (μmol/100 g food DM) was calculated by adjusting for the dilution during sample preparation and then dividing the measured purine concentration in the extract by the recovery (%) of DAP. The total purine and individual purine metabolite concentrations were calculated relative to the ME content and crude protein content of each food sample.

Statistical methods

A computer program (Sample Size, Mean Difference, Version 3.01, Open Epi) [39] was used to calculate sample size based on two previous reports of the mean and variability of purine concentrations [15, 32] with a power of 80% and a type I error of 5%. Two batches of each diet (the same product with 2 different batch numbers) would be sufficient to detect a 30% difference in purine concentration among different products, however, two batches were not considered sufficient to assess the variability among different batches of the same products. Therefore, 3 batches of each of 12 dry and 10 canned diets were compared. A supplemental analysis with two hydrolyzed protein dry diets containing animal-derived ingredients included only two different batches due to time constraints.

Total purine and individual purine metabolite concentrations (μmol/Mcal ME), individual purine proportion relative to total purine (%), and the ratio of total purine (μmol) to protein (g) were compared among individual diets, between canned and dry diets, between Rx and Maint diets, and among diet categories (Uprev, Veg, LoPr, and Maint) using a computer statistics program (SAS System for Windows 9.4, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Data distribution was assessed for normality using Q-Q plots, homogeneity of variance, and a histogram. Concentrations were square root transformed if values were not normally distributed. A linear mixed model was used to evaluate the effect of diet category (Maint, Uprev, Veg, LoPro) and moisture (wet vs dry), treating manufacturers as a random effect. A linear contrast was used to test for differences among diet categories. Tukey’s multiple comparison procedure was used to make pairwise comparisons, main effects and sliced interaction comparisons. Concentrations and proportions are reported as least squares means (LSM) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) or medians and ranges. P value ≤ 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Frequency of inclusion of some ingredients in the ingredient list or inclusion within the top 5 ingredients listed (Suppl. 2) was compared between canned and dry diets, between Rx and Maint diets, and between diets with total purine content below or above 630 μmol/Mcal using Fisher’s exact test. Frequency of inclusion was assessed for chicken, meat-by products, liver, flavor (liver flavor or natural flavors) because these ingredients have been reported to contain more purines [22], and for eggs for the opposite reason. A supplemental analysis to evaluate whether hydrolysis affects the type of purine was also performed. The purine composition of the two hydrolyzed protein dry diets containing animal-derived ingredients and the dry Veg diet containing hydrolyzed protein were compared to the purine composition of three comparable dry diets (two LoPr diets and a Veg diet) containing non-hydrolyzed protein from the same manufacturers.

Protein content (per Mcal) ranged from 26 to 125 g in all diets, from 26 to 59 g in 11 Rx dry diets, and from 29 to 99 g in 7 Rx canned diets (Fig. 2). All diets met the National Research Council recommended allowance of protein for adult dogs but only a few met the recommendation for growing puppies [37]. Discussion

This study gives an indication of the differences in total purine content among and within commercial Maint and Rx diets. As expected, the total purine content (on a caloric basis) of Maint diets was much higher than in these Rx diets that might be used to prevent urate urolithiasis. Less expected was the wide variation in purine content, from 56 to 1275 μmol/Mcal among dry Rx foods, and from 343 to 4526 μmol/Mcal in canned Rx foods. In particular, the median total purine content of all the dry Rx diets was about five times less than that of all the canned Rx diets on a calorie basis. All dry Rx diets, except for one LoPr diet, contained less than 630 μmol of total purines per Mcal ME, previously reported to decrease urinary UA and allantoin concentrations in healthy dogs [15]. In contrast, among the canned Rx diets, only one contained less than this amount [15]. Purine concentrations may be lower in dry diets because dry diets tend to contain more carbohydrate and less meat and meat by-product proteins than canned diets [37]. Drying may also affect purine content [40]. Wet foods are often preferred for urolith management because their higher moisture content results in more dilute urine but these results suggest that dry food may offer an advantage in preventing urate urolithiasis especially if dry food is moistened before feeding [23]. Feeding trials are needed, however, because urine pH as well as urine dilution affect stone formation and urinary uric acid concentration and urate stone formation were not investigated here [1, 14].

Others have suggested that the total purine content of pet foods is proportional to protein content, [21] but the ratio of total purine (μmol) to protein (g) varied several fold between diets within some categories and differed significantly among dry categories (p < 0.05). This difference is likely due to variations in protein sources.

All Rx diets in this study were not labelled as being suitable to feed puppies and did not meet some nutritional requirements for puppies, such as the recommended allowances for calcium and phosphorus [37]. Nevertheless, all Veg dry diets and one Uprev dry diet met the protein requirement of puppies, 14 weeks and older [37]. Hydrolyzed protein dry diets were not included in the initial analysis, but their purine content suggests that they are an option for dogs prone to urate urolithiasis that also have food hypersensitivity. These hydrolyzed protein diets were low in total purines (least squares mean 293, 95% confidence interval 259–385 μmol/Mcal) and also met the protein requirement of puppies with only 6 μmol of total purine per gram of protein (range 5–7). On the other hand, LoPr dry diets and one Uprev dry diet that contained less protein as well as being low in purines might be better options for dogs with portosystemic shunt and hepatic encephalopathy [1, 41].

This study showed how the relative proportions of purines differ among dog foods without first using acid hydrolysis to convert purines to their four nucleobases. Hypoxanthine, which has been associated with increased uricosuria compared to other purines [6, 30, 31, 42], was the predominant purine in most diets of this study except for canned and dry Veg diets and canned Uprev diets. Meat is often a major ingredient in commercial pet food and hypoxanthine has been reported to constitute more than 50% of the purines in animal meats and offal, whereas more than 60% of the purines in vegetables are composed of other purines such as adenine and guanine [22]. Hypoxanthine is generated from the breakdown of IMP and inosine. When animals die, IMP is generated from ATP as rigor mortis develops; [43] IMP is added to diets also because it is an umami taste enhancer; [44] and, IMP then degrades over time to inosine during storage [45]. Inosine was the predominant purine in frozen stored raw fish, [32] whereas the median proportion of inosine in dog food was only 7% of total purine content in the present study. The higher hypoxanthine and lower inosine proportion in dog foods compared to frozen fish could be explained by cooking at temperatures above 100℃, which will accelerate the breakdown of IMP and inosine to hypoxanthine [46].

The higher proportion of AMP, adenosine, and adenine found in dry diets compared to canned diets in this study likely reflects the greater proportion of vegetable sources of purines, such as grains, in dry diets. The Veg diet category contained a larger proportion of adenosine and guanosine than any other categories, which was consistent with previous reports [22]. Guanosine is broken down into guanine [4, 29] that did not alter urinary UA in people and rats [30, 31, 33].

A few other differences in individual purines among diets are of note. Among the three types of hydrolyzed protein foods, one exhibited a composition in which more than 98% of the purines were present as nucleobases. It remains unclear whether this phenomenon is attributable to differences in protein sources, variations in the hydrolysis method, or other factors. Also of note, three batches of two foods were reproducibly found to contain proportionately much more UA in duplicate batches than all other batches of food. Whether this is important needs further investigation because the urate transporter, GLUT9, coded by the SLC2A9 gene, is normally expressed in the intestine as well as the liver and kidney. It is possible, therefore, that intestinal absorption of UA may be decreased in dogs with defective SLC2A9 [7, 27, 47].

Pet food labels in the United States list ingredients by declining weight regardless of water content [48]. Thus, ingredients listed early in the list may contribute more nutrients and purines to the diet, but it is impossible to determine the precise contribution of each because wet ingredients provide less nutrients than dry ingredients of similar weight. As expected, liver and chicken meat, which contain more purines, were more commonly listed in the first five ingredients of diets containing more purines. On the other hand, diets listing eggs, which contain almost no purines, were not always low in purines [22].

Previous studies of purines in dog foods have reported total dietary purine content either as weight of total purine (mg) or weight equivalent of UA (mg Eq UA), assuming that all purines in the diet are metabolized entirely to UA in urine. In the present study, the purine content is reported in moles because the molar masses of nucleosides and nucleotides are two or more times that of nucleobases even though each mole of nucleoside, nucleotide or nucleobase only generates one mole of UA. The weight of purines from previous reports can be converted to millimoles by dividing the weight of purine (mg) by the molecular mass of the nuclear bases (131–152 mg/mmol), whereas millimoles can be converted to mg Eq UA by multiplying by the molar mass of UA (168 mg/mmol). On that basis, the total purine content in the Uprev dry dog foods in the present study was about 30% of previous reports [15, 19]. Any difference may represent differences in the method of analysis or changes in processing or ingredients over time or among batches.

This study has several limitations. This report focuses on the dietary purine content of diets without considering the role that dietary mineral balance and urinary pH plays in urate solubility in urine. How the mineral and protein content of these diets might affect urine pH is published elsewhere and not included here because explaining the concept and assumptions involved detracted from the main purpose of this report [49] Feeding trials with careful patient monitoring remain the only definitive way to assess the influence of dietary purine metabolite and other nutrient concentrations on UA excretion, solubility and urolith formation.

Another limitation is that only a few diets sold at one time in the United States by three manufacturers were analyzed, so the results reported here might not be representative of all available commercial diets. Nevertheless, these three manufacturers provide most of the Rx diets available to veterinarians worldwide. Results reported here may also differ from those reported by others, because there is no standard method of measuring purine content or quality control to ensure results are comparable among different laboratories.

Conclusion

This study confirmed that diets designed to prevent urate urolithiasis and several other Rx diets contain much less total purines than Maint diets. It also showed that dry Veg, Uprev, and LoPr Rx diets may restrict total dietary purine intake more effectively compared to equivalent canned Rx diets. In addition, it showed that there are differences in the relative proportion of individual purines that may affect urinary urate concentrations. It suggests that Veg diets are useful alternatives when Uprev diets are unavailable, as they had the least purine and hypoxanthine within the same moisture category. They can also be recommended for feeding puppies because they provide more protein relative to purines. It also suggests renal dry diets that are low in purines can be reasonable alternatives to Uprev diets if protein restriction is required. The high relative UA content in some batches of food is also notable. Together, these results should help veterinarians select appropriate diets for preventing canine urate uroliths but factors that affect urate solubility, such as urine dilution and urine pH, must also be taken into account. Feeding trials and careful patient monitoring are needed to establish how such differences in total and individual purines affect urinary urate concentrations and pH in dogs.
