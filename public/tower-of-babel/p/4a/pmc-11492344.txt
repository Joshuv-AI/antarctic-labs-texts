Racial and ethnic disparities in dupilumab for pediatric atopic dermatitis in Florida
Authors: Urdur Jonsdottir, Emily S Craver, Tanvi Patel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDupilumab is an mAb that has been shown to decrease symptoms and severity of atopic dermatitis (AD). It was approved for use in adolescents and children in a stepwise manner from 2019 to 2022. Racial and ethnic disparities have been described in access to emerging therapies in many conditions, including treatment with dupilumab for AD in adult patients.ObjectiveWe sought to assess racial and ethnic disparities in moderate to severe AD treatment with dupilumab in the pediatric population.MethodsThis retrospective study identified 12,918 patients with AD aged 0 to 17 years who had at least a 6-month follow-up period between January 2020 and September 2023. The primary end point of dupilumab prescription was compared between racial and ethnic groups and a reference group of non-Hispanic White patients while adjusting for confounders.ResultsAmong the patients, 18.1% were Black, 40.5% Hispanic, 28.9% non-Hispanic White, and 12.4% Other race. Black (odds ratio, 0.43; P = .006) and Hispanic (odds ratio, 0.46; P < .001) patients had significantly lower odds of receiving dupilumab compared with the reference group.ConclusionsThis study may indicate a racial and ethnic disparity negatively affecting access to treatment with dupilumab for Black and Hispanic children and adolescents with AD. Because previous studies have not indicated decreased severity of AD in these patient populations, less frequent use is likely due to other underlying factors such as differential access to care, cultural differences, language barriers, and socioeconomic factors. The contributing factors must be further identified and addressed to ensure health equity in pediatric AD.Key words: Atopic dermatitis, dupilumab, eczema, race and ethnicity, disparity, health equity, socioeconomic status

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a condition caused by the breakdown of the epidermal barrier, causing dry, pruritic, and inflamed skin. Poor control of AD symptoms may lead to complications such as viral or bacterial superinfections as well as negative effects on sleep, academics, and mental health.1 AD is one of the most common chronic childhood illnesses, and its treatment is often time-consuming and costly to patients and cargivers.2,3

The routine management of AD is stepwise in nature and involves both nonprescription and prescription treatments.4 Trigger and irritant recognition and avoidance are important components of AD care. In addition, proper routine skin care with regular baths and application of hydrophilic, lipid-containing topical emollients for the maintenance and protection of the epidermal barrier is essential. When these measures do not suffice to control AD symptoms, patients are treated with topical steroids of varying potencies as well as topical nonsteroidal medications depending on the degree of symptoms and response of each patient. In recent years, biologic medications have been emerging for the treatment of refractory moderate to severe AD. Among these, dupilumab is an mAb that has been shown to decrease symptoms and severity of AD.5 It was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for treating moderate to severe AD in adolescents in 2019,6 children aged 6 to 11 years in 2020,7 and children aged 6 months to 5 years in 2022.8

Racial and ethnic disparities have been consistently reported in AD. Black patients have been shown to have an increased risk, prevalence, and severity of AD.9, 10, 11 Increased health care utilization has been shown in Black and Hispanic patients with AD.12 Racial and ethnic disparities have been described in access to emerging therapies in many conditions, including treatment with dupilumab for AD in adult patients.13 A recent study indicated that among adult patients, Black and Hispanic patients are less likely to be prescribed dupilumab as well as other novel and standard-of-care advanced treatment options for AD.13

With its recent approval for use in the pediatric population, data on racial and ethnic disparities in the use of dupilumab in children and adolescents are lacking. It is important to understand patterns in emerging treatments for AD in this age group because it is a large proportion of the patients who are affected. This study aimed to assess racial and ethnic disparities among pediatric patients who are treated with dupilumab for moderate to severe AD.

Methods

The Nemours Children’s Health Institutional Review Board deemed this study exempt (April 18, 2023; reference no. 2028331). The requirement for obtaining informed consent/parental permission/assent and authorization for use and disclosure of protect health information was waived on the basis of the applicable federal regulation.

Study subjects

The study population included children younger than 18 years with a diagnosis of AD who were seen at any Nemours Children’s Health location in Central Florida between January 2020 and September 2023. Nemours Children’s Health is a nonprofit organization with Central Florida locations, including a free-standing children’s hospital, outpatient specialty care, primary care, and urgent care clinics. Patients were excluded if they did not have at least a 6-month follow-up period or if information on race and ethnicity was missing from the electronic medical record (EMR). A total of 12,918 patients were included in this retrospective study. There were 2,344 Black patients, 5,237 Hispanic patients, 3,739 non-Hispanic White (NHW) patients, and the remaining 1,598 patients who identified as Asian, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, American Indian, or Alaska Native. If they identified as “some other race,” they were classified into the Other group.

Information was collected through EMR review, including patient characteristics (age, sex, insurance, asthma, food allergies, allergic rhinitis, and follow-up time). Information on race and ethnicity was parentally reported. The primary outcome was presence of dupilumab prescription. The secondary outcomes were other AD-related prescriptions (nonsteroidal and steroidal topical medications).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as median (Q1, Q3) and mean ± SD, whereas categorical variables were reported as frequency (%). Comparisons of patient characteristics according to racial and ethnic groups were made using a Kruskal-Wallis test (for continuous variables) or a chi-square test (for categorical variables).

The occurrences of dupilumab and steroidal and nonsteroidal topical medications prescribed were compared between the groups using logistic regression models. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs were estimated and interpreted as the multiplicative increase in the odds of the given outcome for each group (Black, Hispanic, and Other) compared with the NHW group. The number of topical steroidal medications was compared between the racial and ethnic groups and the reference group of NHW patients using negative binomial regression models given the count nature of this variable. Ratio of means and 95% CIs were estimated and interpreted as the multiplicative increase in the mean topical steroid prescriptions for each group compared with the NHW group. Multivariable models were adjusted for age at first visit, insurance, asthma, food allergies, allergic rhinitis, and follow-up time.

P values less than .05 were considered statistically significant, and all statistical tests were 2-sided. Statistical analyses were performed using R Statistical Software, version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Baseline patient characteristics

A comparison of patient characteristics among the racial and ethnic groups is provided in Table I. There was a significant difference in age among the 4 groups (P = .021). Hispanic children were the oldest, with a median age of 3.5 years, and NHW children were the youngest, with a median age of 2.9 years. There was also a significant difference in insurance providers among the groups (P < .001). NHW patients were more likely to have private insurance (56.5%); Black, Hispanic, and Other patients were more likely to have Medicaid insurance (67.4%, 69.0%, and 51.4%, respectively). In addition, there was a significant difference found for asthma comorbidity with Black and Hispanic groups, both having the highest rates reported among the 4 race groups (P < .001; Black: 20.7%, Hispanic: 17.2%, NHW: 15.1%, Other: 15.8%). There were also significant differences found for food allergies (P < .001; Black: 10.4%, Hispanic: 8.0%, NHW: 9.1%, and Other: 10.7%) and allergic rhinitis (P < .001; Black: 14.8%, Hispanic: 16.2%, NHW: 12.2%, and Other: 14.1%).

Patient outcomes

Comparisons of patient outcome variables between the racial and ethnic groups are provided in Tables II and III. After adjusting for potential confounders, the odds of being prescribed dupilumab were significantly less in the Black group (OR = 0.43; P = .006) and the Hispanic group (OR = 0.46; P < .001) compared with the NHW group. The odds of a prescription for nonsteroidal topical medication were significantly higher in the Other group compared with the NHW group (OR = 1.29; P = .018). No statistically significant difference was found in the odds of nonsteroidal medication prescriptions for the Black and Hispanic groups compared with the NHW reference group. The mean count of steroidal topical medications prescribed for the Black group was 1.10 times that of the NHW group (P = .008). A similar result was found for the Other group compared with the NHW group (ratio of means, 1.12; P = .004). No other statistically significant differences were found in the mean number of prescriptions for topical steroidal AD medication.

Discussion

This study shows less frequent prescriptions for dupilumab in Black and Hispanic children and adolescents with AD in Central Florida. This may indicate a racial and ethnic disparity affecting access to the newly approved biologic medication dupilumab in pediatric AD. These findings are consistent with literature examining similar topics in adult populations.

Bell et al13 demonstrated racial and ethnic disparities among adults in access to newly approved standard-of-care treatments for many of the most common dermatologic diseases. Their results indicated that Black patients were prescribed emerging treatments for acne, AD, and psoriasis less often, whereas Hispanic patients were less likely to receive certain standard-of-care acne medications.13 Among the results, Black patients with AD were significantly less likely to be prescribed dupilumab, desonide, tacrolimus, pimecrolimus, or crisaborole.13

Sanfilippo et al14 studied racial and ethnic differences in the severity and treatment patterns of mostly adult patients with AD. Their analysis did not show any consistent differences in severity or treatment patterns apart from dupilumab being more common among multiracial/other race patients. The high quality of faculty training and experience with diverse patient populations within their institution and focused efforts to eliminate structural racism were cited as possible reasons for their findings.

Our findings could be interpreted to indicate a lesser AD severity in Black and Hispanic patients compared with NHW patients, resulting in reduced need for advanced treatments such as dupilumab. These retrospective data did not offer consistent and reliable grading of AD severity because of their reliance on each provider documenting mild, moderate, or severe AD. However, lesser AD severity in Black and Hispanic patients would not be consistent with previous research on the topic. Black patients have been consistently shown to have increased severity and health care utilization for AD.9,11,15 The Hispanic population has been less studied, but increased severity and health care utilization have been reported.12,16,17

In our study, similar patterns were not seen in other forms of treatment such as nonsteroidal and steroidal topical medications. The factors that differentiate dupilumab from other forms of AD-related medications are its novelty and that it is mostly or only prescribed by subspecialists. This may reflect differential access to specialty care, that is, dermatology and allergy specialist visits.

Another possible contributing factor may be language barriers, particularly among the Hispanic population. A review study18 evaluating the barriers faced by Latinx patients in the health care system provides insight into possible contributing factors. A large proportion of Latinx or Hispanic people in the United States report limited English proficiency, which can affect their quality of care and their trust toward the health care system and providers.18 In addition to these challenges, many report immigration status, cultural traditions, and socioeconomic factors as barriers to health care.18

The use of dupilumab is an advanced treatment, and it is mostly prescribed by subspecialty providers. It is approved for moderate to severe AD, but the decision to start dupilumab is dependent on each individual provider’s assessment of AD severity and response to previous treatments. Within our system, dupilumab is prescribed only by providers in the allergy department. The providers in our allergy department are well trained in the management of patients with various skin colors and cultural and ethnic backgrounds. A large proportion of our providers speak Spanish and interpreter services are readily available. Despite this, an implicit racial and ethnic bias among providers must be considered when health care disparities are observed.

Financial factors also may play a role in the observed difference. Because of the baseline differences in insurance coverage between the racial and ethnic groups, insurance status must be considered as an underlying cause for the observed disparity. However, our multivariate analysis did adjust for insurance status. In addition, dupilumab is covered by Medicaid, which is the primary source of insurance for 3 of the 4 racial and ethnic groups. When covered by private insurance, copays may vary. According to the manufacturer, more than 40% of patients covered by private insurance pay more than $100 per month for dupilumab. This can be a substantial financial burden for some patients and families. Our analysis was not able to quantify socioeconomic status and thus we do not have information to compare financial means between the racial and ethnic groups.

This study has several key strengths. To our knowledge, this is the first study evaluating disparities in the prescription of dupilumab for pediatric patients, a novel advanced treatment for moderate to severe AD. Our inclusion of a large Black and Hispanic patient population provides valuable insight into the health care disparities affecting historically marginalized and underserved populations. The use of a large EMR database facilitates the analysis of a large patient population while minimizing recall bias.

This study has some limitations. Because of the recent approval of dupilumab for pediatric patients, the follow-up period is relatively short at a mean of 26 months. The recent approval of dupilumab also limits each provider’s experience and may affect prescribing practices. An AD diagnosis relies on the clinical assessment of health care providers, whose experience in diagnosing AD may vary. Therefore, a portion of misdiagnosed patients may have been included in the study population. The retrospective data also did not include consistent documentation of AD severity; therefore, we could not account for severity in our analysis. Our study population does not directly mirror demographic characteristics outside of Central Florida, limiting the generalizability of the results. However, these results provide important additions to the growing body of evidence on racial and ethnic disparities throughout diverse populations and medical conditions, particularly among a large Hispanic population. In addition, baseline characteristics (age at first visit, insurance status, and comorbidities) differed significantly between patient groups. However, these were accounted for in our multivariate analysis when comparing outcomes.

This study may indicate a racial and ethnic disparity negatively affecting access to emerging treatment with dupilumab for Black and Hispanic children and adolescents with AD. Because previous studies have not indicated decreased AD severity in these patient populations, less frequent use is likely due to other underlying factors such as differential access to care, cultural differences, language barriers, and socioeconomic factors. Further research and targeted interventions are required to fully understand and effectively address the possible disparities. With increasing emphasis on health equity in research and policymaking, various organizations have formulated recommendations to reduce disparities, including in atopic diseases.19 These include enhanced provider training for diverse skin tones, culturally aware medical education, multilingual resources, interpreter services, and access to subspecialty care in underserved communities. The results of this study serve as a further call to action to improve health equity for pediatric AD.

Clinical implications

This study highlights the need to further identify and address underlying factors contributing to racial and ethnic disparities in pediatric AD.
