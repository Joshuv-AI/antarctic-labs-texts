Blinatumomab Along With Combined Antifungal Agents for Refractory Adult Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia With Invasive Aspergillosis: A Case Report
Authors: Yutong Liu, Fan Xia, Wenzhi Cai, Ying Zhang, Jiawei Zhu, Zhen Shen, Jiaxin Li, Qiannan Yang, Qi Qu, Jia Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThere is an increased risk of invasive aspergillosis (IA) during the induction of acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) because of the use of cytoreductive chemotherapy and high‐dose steroids. This case demonstrates the utility of metagenomic next‐generation sequencing and repeat sampling in clarifying complex infections and highlights blinatumomab as an effective antileukemic option when conventional chemotherapy is limited by active infection. Together, these strategies enabled the successful management of refractory ALL in a patient with pulmonary IA and fungal DNAemia.Keywords: acute lymphoblastic leukemia, drug resistance, fungal infections, invasive aspergillosis, multiple infections

Introduction

Invasive aspergillosis (IA) is a serious fungal infection, and the lungs are the most common site of involvement, accounting for approximately 71.9% of cases [1]. The mortality rate of IA remains high because of confusing clinical manifestations, difficulties in early diagnosis, and limited treatment options [2]. The risk of IA significantly increases for patients with hematological malignancies when they are exposed to specific risk factors, including the administration of multiagent chemotherapies comprising high‐dose steroids, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, cellular therapies, etc. [3]. However, 10%–20% of newly diagnosed acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) patients who are resistant to induction chemotherapy may have a prolonged immunocompromised period [4], which is usually associated with fatal infections, such as IA [5]. Here, we report a refractory ALL patient who developed pulmonary IA and fungemia during induction. Targeted antileukemia therapy and combined antifungal agents were employed to rescue the patient, which suggested a feasible protocol for such intractable patients.

Medical History

The patient was a 52‐year‐old female admitted to the local hospital for the main complaint of “weakness for 1 month.” She denied any prior medical conditions. She had no known familial medical disorders and denied any significant personal history, such as smoking, alcohol abuse, or other risky behaviors. Physical examination revealed generalized pallor. A complete blood count (CBC) suggested severe pancytopenia (leukocyte count of 1.61 × 109/L, neutrophil count of 0.3 × 109/L, lymphocyte count of 1.30 × 109/L, hemoglobin concentration of 40 g/L, and platelet count of 2.8 × 1010/L), and a peripheral blood smear suggested an elevated blast ratio of 82%. Bone marrow aspiration was conducted. Blasts accounted for 93% of the marrow and were lymphoid in appearance. Multiparameter flow cytometric (MFC) detection revealed an abnormal cell population comprising 45% of cells, characterized by the expression HLA‐DR, CD10, CD19, CD22, CD34, CD58, CD128, cCD79a, and TdT. Karyotype analysis failed, and no gene rearrangement was detected. Gene mutations were screened using a next‐generation sequencing (NGS) panel, which revealed IDH2 (c.419G>A p.R140Q) and TLX3 (c.727C>A p.L243M) mutations. The variant allele frequency (VAF) was 33.09% and 57.67%, respectively.

The patient was definitively diagnosed with Philadelphia chromosome (Ph)‐negative ALL and received an induction of the VDLP regimen on December 30, 2024, after the contraindications to chemotherapy were excluded. The VDLP regimen was composed of 40 mg of vincristine (D8, D15), 60 mg of doxorubicin (D8–D10), 3750 IU of L‐asparaginase (D8), and 10 mg of prednisone (D1–D16). After a course of chemotherapy, a response assessment was conducted, which revealed a blast ratio of 83% in the marrow. On January 16, 2025, the patient developed febrile symptoms. She was in a state of severe granulocytopenia, with a neutrophil count of less than 0.2 × 109/L. Her liver and kidney functions were normal. However, no imaging examinations were conducted at the local hospital. Her multinational association for supportive care in cancer (MASCC) index score [6] was 18, indicating high risk (as seen in the Supporting Information). Cefoperazone sulbactam was empirically administered, and the fever was controlled.

Investigation, Diagnosis and Treatment

Clinical Assessment on Admission

The patient was transferred to our hospital on January 20, 2025. As shown in Figure 1, the patient presented with persistent fever but no respiratory or gastrointestinal symptoms. Physical examination revealed normal blood pressure and no obvious dry or wet rales. A CBC revealed worsened pancytopenia with severe neutropenia (0 × 109/L) and transfusion dependency of both erythrocytes and platelets. Bone marrow aspiration revealed extremely hypoproliferative nucleated cells in the marrow and a blast ratio of 55%, characterized by the expression of CD34, DR, CD10, CD19, CD33, cCD79a, CD22, and CD38 (weakly). The karyotype was normal.

Initial Microbiological Workup and Treatment

Metagenomic NGS (mNGS) of peripheral blood revealed Enterococcus faecalis (sequence number: 30), Klebsiella pneumoniae (sequence number: 19), Aspergillus flavus (sequence number: 337), human herpesvirus type 1 (sequence number: 2), and human herpesvirus type 7 (sequence number: 2). Several Galactomannan (GM) tests were positive. On January 21, 2025, chest CT (Figure 2A1–3 ) revealed lesions mainly in the right upper lobe. Despite a lack of histopathological confirmation, the patient met the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) criteria for probable invasive aspergillosis on the basis of immunosuppression, radiological findings, and positive mNGS and GM tests [7].

Serial chest CT images illustrating the temporal evolution of pulmonary infection and its relationship with treatment. On January 21, 2025 (A1–A3), chest CT revealed infectious lesions predominantly in the right upper lobe. Follow‐up imaging on February 5 (B1–B3) and February 14 (C1–C3) demonstrated progression of the pulmonary lesions under the initial treatment regimen. By March 10, 2025 (D1–D3), after switching the treatment strategy, chest CT showed partial absorption of the lesions, indicating marked improvement of the infection.

A mixed infection was established; therefore, a combined anti‐infection therapy was applied, namely, isavuconazole and amphotericin B cholesterol sulfate complex for Aspergillus, meropenem in combination with linezolid for bacteria, and acyclovir for viruses. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and thymidine were given intermittently as immunostimulatory therapy. Although the patient's fever was briefly controlled, a repeat chest CT one week later suggested the progression of pulmonary infection.

Management of Leukemia and Anti‐Infection Adjustment

Persistent neutropenia due to ALL was considered the main cause of uncontrolled infection. However, antileukemia salvage therapy aggravated immunosuppression and worsened the mixed infection. Blinatumomab, a CD3/CD19 bispecific antibody, was administered on January 27, 2025 (9 μg d1–d7, 28 μg d8–d14), to address this dilemma.

During treatment, the patient presented with a continuous fever, with intermittent high fever. On the one hand, the cause of the persistent fever could not be ruled out as being related to a mixed infection. On the other hand, on the basis of the application of the CD3/CD19 bispecific antibody and the high tumor burden of this disease, the levels of cytokines, especially interleukin‐6 (with a maximum concentration of 965 pg/mL; reference ≤ 5.9 pg/mL), significantly increased, which did not rule out the possibility of cytokine release syndrome (CRS). She presented normal circulation and respiratory function and was assessed as having grade 1 CRS [8]. Therefore, antipyretic measures were implemented. However, the patient gradually developed a cough, along with bloody and purulent sputum. Sputum culture revealed Enterobacter aerogenes and Burkholderia cepacia neoformans, and the procalcitonin (PCT) level of the patient was elevated. Despite adjusting the anti‐infection regimen according to drug susceptibility, fever persisted (Figure 1). Dynamic imaging evaluation was performed on February 5 and 14 (Figure 2B1–3,C1–3 ), which suggested continuous lung progression. Bronchoscopic alveolar lavage was performed on February 8, and mNGS of the bronchoscopic alveolar lavage fluid (BALF) revealed Burkholderia cepacia onionis, Aspergillus flavus, human herpesvirus type 7, and rhinovirus C. An additional mNGS of peripheral blood was performed on February 14, revealing Aspergillus flavus (sequence number: 66). PET–CT ruled out extrapulmonary fungal dissemination. On February 19, the antifungal regimen was switched to amphotericin B liposomes in combination with voriconazole.

Outcome

Thereafter, the infection symptoms were significantly alleviated, and the pancytopenia gradually improved (Figure 3). The GM test became negative, and the peripheral blood mNGS was negative on March 4, 2025. Compared with the previous imaging, chest CT (Figure 2D1–3 ) on March 10 revealed that the infection was partially resolved. Bone marrow assessment on February 21 indicated complete remission with measurable residual disease (MRD) less than 8.2 × 10−5. Subsequent treatment consisted of three cycles of consolidation therapy with blinatumomab, the third of which was combined with methotrexate (MTX). The patient tolerated the chemotherapy well and did not develop fever. CT imaging revealed no progression of the pulmonary infection.

Discussion

Invasive fungal disease (IFD) is a frequent and severe complication of hematologic malignancies [9], especially for ALL patients who usually get high‐dose therapy and accordingly broad‐spectrum antibiotics. The use of high‐dose steroids in intensive chemotherapy increases the risk of IFD, and prophylactic antifungal strategies are recommended in such settings [10]. However, in this case, antifungal prophylaxis was not administered during the first cycle of chemotherapy, which might have contributed to the IFD. In addition, antifungal stewardship programs have been shown to reduce inappropriate drug use, lower the risk of antifungal resistance, and decrease infection‐related mortality [11].

The detection of infectious pathogens remains challenging, particularly for fungal infections. In recent years, mNGS has been suggested as a promising method with significant advantages in the diagnosis of complex infections—especially for the detection of hard‐to‐cultivate bacteria, fungi, and mixed infections—facilitating a rapid shift from empirical treatment to targeted treatment. When the anti‐infection regimen does not achieve the expected response, as measured with mNGS, repeating the test with a different sample (e.g., BALF for pulmonary infection) could be necessary [12]. BALF is a good sample of pulmonary infection for mNGS because it has high sensitivity and specificity [13]. For this patient, mNGS with BALF further confirmed the infectious pathogens, providing a reliable basis for adjusting anti‐infective treatment.

In this case, initial antifungal therapy with isavuconazole combined with amphotericin B was selected because of its favorable safety profile [14] and potential synergistic activity [15, 16]. However, persistent detection of Aspergillus by mNGS and progressive pulmonary lesions suggested unsatisfactory efficacy. Given recent evidence supporting superior pulmonary tissue penetration and clinical efficacy of voriconazole against Aspergillus [17], the regimen was adjusted accordingly, resulting in marked clinical improvement. This highlights the importance of considering tissue permeability drug interactions when selecting treatment. The treatment should be tailored to the patient's condition, and both clinical manifestations and biomarkers—such as PCT and IL‐6—are essential for monitoring disease progression and treatment response [18].

Leukemia cell drug resistance is among the main reasons for treatment failure in ALL [19], and the selection of a salvage regimen is particularly complex and challenging. Currently, treatment options for relapsed/refractory (R/R) ALL include intensive chemotherapy, chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR‐T) cell therapy, inotuzumab ozogamicin (INO), and CD3/CD19 bispecific antibodies. Intensive chemotherapy damages hematopoiesis and anti‐infection immunity [20]. CAR‐T cell therapy is an emerging approach that is promising for R/R ALL patients. Nevertheless, in addition to the risk of CRS and neurotoxicity [21], it takes several weeks to produce the cell products, and the management of patients with immunosuppression is challenging. INO is associated with a risk of myelosuppression; thus, it is unfavorable for patients with active infection [22].

The blinatumomab used in this case has the unique advantage of demonstrating significant efficacy against Ph‐negative, resistant B‐ALL. As shown in the TOWER study, a survival benefit over standard chemotherapy has been suggested in patients undergoing first salvage therapy [23], in addition to a higher rate of remission and fewer myelosuppressive effects [24]. Mechanistically, blinatumomab may activate rather than expend T cells, which play an immunomodulatory role and balance antitumor and anti‐infection functions [25]. Additionally, immune support therapy, such as thymopeptide therapy and IVIG, which promote T‐cell maturation [26] and improve nutritional status [27], can help control the infections.

This is a single case report that suggested a potentially feasible solution rather than strong evidence for such patients. Histopathological confirmation of fungal infection was not obtained because of clinical constraints. Additionally, antifungal drug concentrations were not monitored, and the efficacy of blinatumomab in this setting cannot be compared directly with that of other salvage treatments.

In summary, fungal prophylaxis should be prioritized during chemotherapy involving high‐dose steroids. For patients with febrile neutropenia, mNGS enables rapid identification of complex pathogens and guides targeted anti‐infective therapy. Repeat peripheral blood and affected organ samples need to be collected with clinical implications, which favor the early diagnosis and assessment for complicated infections. In patients with IA, especially those with pulmonary involvement, the combination of voriconazole and amphotericin B may be considered an alternative option if the initial treatment is unsuccessful. Unlike conventional chemotherapy, blinatumomab offers antileukemic efficacy with minimal immunosuppressive effects, making it a valuable therapeutic option for vulnerable leukemia patients intolerant to intensive chemotherapies.
