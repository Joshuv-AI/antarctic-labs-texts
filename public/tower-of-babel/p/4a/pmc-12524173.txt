Simultaneous determination of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and their nitrated and oxygenated derivatives in dried tea by GC–MS/MS
Authors: Dan-Bi Kim, Yong-Suk Kim, Min-Sun Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) and their nitrated and oxygenated derivatives (NPAHs and OPAHs, respectively) are toxic contaminants commonly found in food, including tea. However, existing analytical methods often overlook these derivatives. Herein, a GC–MS/MS method was developed to simultaneously determine 11 PAHs, 13 NPAHs, and 5 OPAHs in dried tea, after acetonitrile extraction and cleanup using C18, Z-Sep+, and MgSO4. The method exhibited excellent linearity (R2 > 0.99), low detection (0.10–1.99 ng g−1) and quantification limits (1–10 ng g−1), recoveries of 70.9–103.0 %, and relative standard deviations below 15 %. The concentrations of four PAHs (BaA, Chr, BbF, and BaP) were < LOQ–322.2 ng g−1, whereas those of NPAHs and OPAHs were < LOQ–86.6 and 2.4–116.6 ng g−1, respectively. This validated method for determining the concentrations of PAHs and their derivatives in tea will ultimately support more robust food safety management strategies.Keywords: Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, Cleanup, Method validation, Simultaneous determination, Tea

Introduction

Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) are aromatic compounds composed of carbon and hydrogen atoms, characterized by the linear or condensed fusion of two or more benzene rings (Fig. 1). Based on their molecular weight, these compounds are broadly classified as low (2–3 rings), medium (4 rings), or high (5–7 rings) molecular weight PAHs. An increase in molecular weight results in higher hydrophobicity and diminished water solubility and vapor pressure, which contributes to the increased recalcitrance of the compounds (Jelic et al., 2015). These molecular characteristics are crucial, as their toxicity also correlates with both the number of carbon atoms and the molecular weight (Geier et al., 2018). The toxicity of PAHs is categorized into groups by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), as shown in Table 1. These compounds originate from various sources, including industrial activities, combustion processes, environmental pollution, and food processing (Balcıoğlu, 2016). Consequently, PAHs are widely distributed in the environment and can enter the food chain as environmental pollutants. Owing to their high carcinogenicity, they constitute a crucial concern in food safety and require rigorous quality assessment (Martorell et al., 2010). However, beyond these parent compounds, it is equally important to consider their derivatives, such as nitrated PAHs (NPAHs) and oxygenated PAHs (OPAHs). These derivatives exhibit distinct physicochemical properties and toxicities compared to their parent PAHs due to structural modifications. NPAHs consist of compounds in which one or more nitro groups are attached to the aromatic ring, incorporating a nitrogen atom. Meanwhile, OPAHs are PAH derivatives featuring oxygen-containing functional groups such as ketones or quinones. These derivatives primarily arise from combustion processes and are characterized by their strong adsorption to atmospheric fine particles (Fig. 1) (Han et al., 2025). Notably, PAH derivatives are often more polar than their parent PAHs and are considered particularly harmful to human health due to their potential to cause mutations, developmental abnormalities, and cancer (International Agency for Research of Cancer (IARC), 2010). Several studies have demonstrated that some NPAHs and OPAHs display significantly higher toxicity than benzo[a]pyrene (BaP) (Cunha et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2016).

Tea is a globally cherished beverage, closely tied to health, culture, tradition, and industry. In 2023, global tea consumption reached approximately 7.3 billion kg and is projected to rise to 8.3 billion kg by 2029 (DePaula et al., 2025). Given its widespread and frequent daily consumption, tea represents a significant potential pathway for human exposure to various environmental pollutants. Numerous studies have reported that tea leaves can be contaminated with several dangerous compounds, such as pesticides, heavy metals, or PAHs, owing to their extensive leaf surface area and morphological characteristics that facilitate the deposition and retention of airborne particles (Górka et al., 2025; Yaqub et al., 2018). These contaminants originate from environmental pollution (including atmospheric deposition, water absorption, and soil absorption), fertilizer application, sewage irrigation, or manufacturing processes, and an increase in PAH content during the drying stage of tea processing has also been reported (Lin and Zhu, 2004). The European Union (EU) strictly manages PAHs in dried herbs by setting maximum levels. Specifically, the individual content of BaP must not exceed 10 μg kg−1. Furthermore, the sum of four PAHs, including benzo[a]anthracene (BaA), chrysene (Chr), benzo[b]fluoranthene (BbF), and BaP, must not exceed 50 μg kg−1 (European Commission, E, 2023). These regulations were established with the aim of protecting consumer health, considering that PAHs can increase the risk of cancer in humans. Previous studies have reported Σ4 PAH levels (benzo[a]anthracene, chrysene, benzo[b]fluoranthene and BaP) of 37.64–106.90 μg kg−1 in Chinese tea (Guo et al., 2022), while black and Turkish teas (black tea and Turkish tea) showed total PAH levels of 317.0–452.3 μg kg−1 (Celik-Saglam et al., 2022). Given that PAHs may substantially contribute to tea leaf contamination, their derivatives (NPAHs and OPAHs) could also be present in significant amounts. Despite the importance of elucidating the potential health risks associated with human exposure to NPAHs and OPAHs, research investigating their presence in food products remains limited. Moreover, the PAH derivatives are typically present at lower levels in the environment than either parent PAHs or other aliphatic hydrocarbons. Therefore, analyzing these compounds in food requires analytical techniques with higher sensitivity and precision.

Gas chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (GC–MS/MS) is widely used for the quantitative analysis of multiple analytes owing to its high sensitivity and selectivity (Xu et al., 2021). Given the complex composition of food matrices, sample extraction and pretreatment are essential prior to GC–MS/MS (Futigami et al., 2024; Thabit et al., 2022). Various sample preparation techniques have been used to determine PAH levels in food and environmental samples, including solid-phase extraction (SPE), liquid–liquid extraction (LLE), solid-phase micro-extraction (SPME), and stir bar sorptive extraction (SBSE); however, these techniques present limitations such as lengthy processing times and the consumption of large volumes of organic solvent (Krüger et al., 2011, Titato and Lanças, 2005). Dispersive solid-phase extraction (d-SPE) has emerged as an innovative extraction technique that can overcome these drawbacks. This approach is designed to eliminate interfering substances by strategically selecting dispersive adsorbents that match the specific physicochemical characteristics of the target analyte and the sample matrix, ultimately improving both the sensitivity and precision. The method has been successfully used to detect PAHs in coffee, tea, fishery products, meats, and wheat flour (Fei et al., 2023; Urban & Lesueur, 2017).

The present study aimed to establish effective sample preparation and analytical methods for the simultaneous determination of PAHs, NPAHs, and OPAHs in dried tea samples, and to acquire foundational data through the analysis of commercial tea products.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Naphthalene, acenaphthene, phenanthrene, fluorene, anthracene, chrysene, pyrene, benz[a]anthracene, 1-nitronaphthalene, 2-nitrofluorene, 9-nitroanthracene, 3-nitrofluoranthene, 6-nitrochrysene, 1-nitropyrene, and 6-nitrobenzo[a]pyrene were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Benzo[a]pyrene, 5-nitroacenaphthene, 9-nitrophenanthrene, 7-nitrobenz[a]anthracene, anthraquinone, and 9-fluorenone were purchased from Dr. Ehrenstorfer (Augsburg, Germany). 2-Nitroanthracene, 3-nitrophenanthrene, and benz[a]anthracene-7,12-dione were purchased from AccuStandard (New Haven, CT, USA). Benzanthrone was obtained from Toronto Research Chemicals (Toronto, Canada), fluoranthene from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan), and 4-nitropyrene and 9,10-dihydrobenzo[a]pyrene-7(8H)-one from Chiron AS (Trondheim, Norway). The purities and supplier information for all the standards are given in the Supplementary Material (Table S1).

LC-Florisil SPE (Florisil) and Supel QuE (Z-Sep+) were purchased from Merck KGaA (Darmstadt, Germany). Bulk sorbents, including primary secondary amine (PSA), C18 end-capped sorbent (C18), and graphitized carbon black SPE bulk sorbent (GCB), were sourced from Agilent Technologies (Lake Forest, CA, USA). Analytical-grade anhydrous MgSO4, NaCl, and polyvinylpolypyrrolidone (PVPP), and all organic solvents, were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). All organic solvents used in this study were of chromatographic grade (>99 %).

Standard solution preparation

Standard stock solutions (10 and 100 ng g−1) were prepared by dissolving PAHs and OPAHs in dichloromethane and dissolving NPAHs in toluene. The stock solutions were stored in a refrigerator at −20 °C until use. Working standard solutions were prepared by diluting each stock solution in acetonitrile and were used for the operation of GC–MS/MS, investigation of the parameters, and spiking tests. External calibration was performed with matrix-matched analytical curves constructed using six concentrations: 0.25–20 ng g−1 for PAHs and OPAHs and 1.25–100 ng g−1 for NPAHs.

Instrumentation

PAHs, NPAHs, and OPAHs were analyzed utilizing a Trace 1310 gas chromatograph (GC) coupled with a TSQ 9000 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). Chromatographic separation was conducted on a DB-5MS UI GC column (30 m × 0.25 mm ID, 0.25 μm; Agilent Technologies, USA). Helium (>99.999 % purity) served as the carrier gas and argon (>99.999 % purity) as the collision gas. The GC oven temperature program was: 70 to 200 °C at 15 °C min−1, then 5 °C min−1 to 310 °C, then held for 3 min; the total run time was 35.67 min at a constant flow rate of 1.2 mL min−1. The inlet temperature was 300 °C, and the injection volume was 1 μL in the splitless mode. The mass spectrometer was used in the electron ionization (EI) mode at 70 eV. The transfer line and ion source were maintained at 280 and 250 °C, respectively. Quantification was performed in the selected reaction monitoring (SRM) mode. MS/MS experiments were performed using molecular ions as precursor ions in all analyses. Collision energies were varied from 15 to 45 V, depending on the analyte, and the product ions were analyzed in the m/z range of 102–267. The optimized GC–MS/MS parameters for each SRM transition and retention time are listed in Table 1. The SRM chromatogram of a blank tea sample spiked with PAHs and their derivatives is presented in Fig. S1.

Sample preparation

Sample extraction

The initial step involved extracting the analytes using a variety of organic solvents (such as acetonitrile, hexane, ethyl acetate, and acetone) in combination with NaCl as the extraction salt. A portion of the homogenized samples (1 g) was accurately weighed and transferred to a 50 mL Teflon centrifuge tube, followed by the addition of 5 mL of water and soaking for 10 min. For each sample, 10 mL of the selected organic solvent was added and mixed for 30 s using a Vortex-Genie 2 T system (Scientific Industries, Bohemia, NY, USA). Then, 1 g of NaCl was added, and the mixture was shaken for 30 min on a rotary shaker (MyLab SLRM-3, SeouLin Bioscience, Seongnam, Republic of Korea). Samples were subsequently centrifuged at 9190 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C (Himac CR22N, Hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The resulting supernatant was collected for use in the d-SPE purification process.

d-SPE cleanup

The d-SPE cleanup step was performed using six purification sorbents (C18, PSA, Florisil, Z-Sep+, PVPP, and GCB). The supernatant (1 mL) was transferred to a 1.5 mL polypropylene tube containing the selected d-SPE sorbent. The sample was agitated for 1 min and then centrifuged at 15,294 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C (5810/5810 R; Eppendorf, Leipzig, Germany). The resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.2 μm PTFE filter, and the final solution was injected into the GC–MS/MS system. Chromatograms for the blank tea samples after application of each sorbent are presented in Fig. S2.

Method validation

The validation of the developed method was conducted in accordance with Commission Decision, 2002/657/EC (Decision, 2002) for selectivity, linearity, accuracy, precision (repeatability and reproducibility), limits of detection (LODs), and limits of quantification (LOQs). Each compound was identified and confirmed on the basis of retention times and relative ion ratios corresponding to the selected SRM transitions. Linearity was evaluated using a calibration curve and expressed as a coefficient of determination (R2). Accuracy (recovery, %) and precision (relative standard deviation, RSD, %) were determined by analyzing blank dried tea samples spiked with standard solutions at three concentration levels (low, medium, and high) (n = 5). Repeatability and reproducibility were evaluated by calculating both the intraday (same day, n = 5) and interday (three consecutive days, n = 15) precision. The LODs were established using a signal-to-noise (S/N) ratio of 3, using the lowest spiked concentration in blank dried tea samples. The LOQ is defined as the minimum validated concentration that satisfies the mean recovery and RSD criteria specified by this analytical method. Application in dried tea samples

A total of 40 dried tea samples were collected from local markets in Korea; information on the tea types and their origins is provided in Table S2. The samples were pulverized using a blender (SFM-C555SP; Shin-Il Inc., Korea), immediately transferred into polyethylene bags, and then sealed and stored at −20 °C until analysis. The samples were analyzed according to established methods.

Data expression and statistical analyses

The results are presented as mean ± standard deviation from three or five replicates. The statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 20.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), which included one-way ANOVA with Duncan's test for multiple comparisons and Pearson's correlation analysis. The significance level was set to p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Comparison of extraction solvent

The selection of an appropriate solvent is crucial for improving sample recovery by maximizing the solubility and affinity of the target compounds (Kokosa, 2019). In this study, four extraction solvents were compared for their efficiency in extracting PAHs and their derivatives. Optimization experiments were performed using blank dried tea samples spiked with each compound at a concentration of 50 ng g−1. The extraction efficiencies are presented in Fig. 2, and details are listed in Table S3. Recoveries of PAHs and their derivatives were 55.3–107.5 % for acetonitrile, 44.6–104.5 % for n-hexane, 51.2–96.6 % for ethyl acetate, and 55.8–112.7 % for acetone. Among the 29 components tested, 11 showed the highest recovery rates with acetonitrile. Numerous studies have also demonstrated the effectiveness of acetonitrile in extracting PAHs and their derivatives from Chinese tea and honey (Jiang et al., 2024; Mandelli et al., 2023). Thus, acetonitrile was selected as the extraction solvent for the subsequent experiments.

Recoveries of PAHs and their derivatives using different extraction solvents. The statistical differences were tested by one-way ANOVA via Duncan's test; different lowercase letters indicate significant differences between the recoveries obtained with different extraction solvents (p < 0.05).

Selection of d-SPE cleanup sorbent

The dried tea samples used in this study contained pigments, polyphenols, organic acids, and various volatile compounds (Chaturvedula & Prakash, 2011). Therefore, d-SPE was performed using purification sorbents, which can remove interfering matrix substances present in dried tea samples. Several sorbents (C18, PSA, Florisil, PVPP, Z-Sep+, and GCB) have been reported to be effective for tea matrix removal (Gao et al., 2020; Huertas-Pérez et al., 2024). Fig. 3A shows a heatmap of the purification efficiencies of the six sorbents. The control sample (without sorbent) yielded an average recovery of 74.9 ± 13.6 %, whereas Z-Sep+, Florisil, PSA, PVPP, and C18 showed markedly improved average recovery rates of 96.7, 93.7, 91.4, 89.3, and 86.7 %, respectively. Although PSA, Florisil, and PVPP facilitated excellent matrix cleanup, they were excluded from further use because several NPAHs (1-NNap, 2-NAnt, 9-NAnt, 4-NPY, 6-NChr, and 6-NBap) were strongly retained, leading to unacceptably low recoveries (<70 %). Additionally, GCB exhibited poor recovery rates for most PAHs. The GCB sorbent is primarily used for the effective removal of coextracted pigments (e.g., carotenoids and chlorophyll) (Fig. S2); however, the planar structure of GCB is known to interact strongly with PAHs that have planar or near-planar molecular structures (Belešová et al., 2023). Fig. S3 shows the GC–MS/MS TIC chromatograms of blank tea samples after d-SPE with each sorbent. Three peaks were identified with retention times of 12.12, 14.36, and 24.29 min and were marked as peaks 1, 2, and 3, respectively. C18 effectively reduced the matrix interference for peaks 1 and 3, while Z-Sep+ showed superior performance for peaks 1 and 2 compared to other adsorbents. The capability of C18 to eliminate nonpolar interferences, such as lipids and fats, improved the detection of analytes in complex matrices. Notably, this removal was achieved without substantially affecting the responses of the target compounds. Moreover, Z-Sep+ can also reduce the amount of pigments, lipids, carboxylic acids, and proteins extracted from various complex matrices (Rutkowska et al., 2020).

Optimization of sorbent-based cleanup procedures. Purification efficiencies of six sorbents (A), Sorbent volume optimization for C18 and Z-sep+ (B), Purification efficiencies of four sorbent combinations (C) and photographs of raw tea extract and extracts using the four sorbent combinations (D).

Therefore, we envisioned that the combination of C18 and Z-Sep+ would leverage their complementary properties and enhance the removal of interfering matrix substances. The amount of purification sorbent is crucial for the recovery of the target compounds (Maranata et al., 2021). Thus, we evaluated the recovery rates by varying the amounts of C18 and Z-Sep+ (25, 50, 100, and 150 mg). The results, shown in Fig. 3B, C, and D, demonstrate that the purification efficiency of most compounds increased as the doses of C18 and Z-Sep+ were increased from 25 to 50 mg. However, when the doses were increased from 50 to 150 mg, the recoveries decreased. Therefore, 50 and 100 mg of C18 as well as 25 and 50 mg Z-Sep+ were selected for the subsequent experiments.

In this work, four combinations of sorbents (Sets 1–4), based on the ratios of C18, Z-Sep+, and MgSO4, were compared to determine the optimal cleanup method. The combinations were as follows:

(a) Set 1 contained 50 mg C18, 25 mg Z-Sep+, and 150 mg MgSO4.

(b) Set 2 contained 50 mg C18, 50 mg Z-Sep+, and 150 mg MgSO4.

(c) Set 3 contained 100 mg C18, 25 mg Z-Sep+, and 150 mg MgSO4.

(d) Set 4 contained 100 mg C18, 50 mg Z-Sep+, and 150 mg MgSO4.

As shown in Fig. 3C and detailed in Table S4, Set 4 resulted in 59.5–69.3 % recoveries for some PAHs (BbF, BaP, 2-NAnt, 3-NFln, 4-NPy, 7-NBaA, 6-NChr, 6-NBaP, and BaPO). Using Set 2, the recoveries of the major compounds were 77.8–105.0 % (except for 2-NAnt, 6-NBap, and 6-NChr). When Set 3 was used, the recoveries of BbF and BaPO were < 70 %. In the case of Set 1, the recoveries of all compounds were 72.4–108.6 %. Thus, we concluded that the Set 1 conditions were the most suitable for extracting and analyzing PAHs and their derivatives in dried tea samples.

Method validation

Selectivity, linearity, LOD, and LOQ

The selectivity of the method was evaluated to confirm that PAHs and their derivatives were adequately separated from other matrix components using a DB-5MS UI column. Matrix interference at the analyte retention times was negligible in the blank sample, as shown in Fig. S1. The concentration ranges, linearity, LODs, and LOQs obtained during validation are summarized in Table 2. The linearity (R2) for all compounds was consistently greater than 0.991. The LODs and LOQs were determined on the basis of the slopes of the calibration curves. The LODs ranged from 0.10 to 1.99 ng g−1, and the LOQs ranged from 1 to 10 ng g−1. These results demonstrate that the developed method reliably detected and quantified PAHs and their derivatives at the relevant concentrations in tea samples.

Accuracy and precision

Table 2 summarizes the results for accuracy and precision. Accuracy was evaluated by calculating recovery rates within the same day (intraday) and across different days (interday), with values ranging from 70.9 to 101.1 % and 71.0 to 103.0 %, respectively. Precision was assessed by calculating RSD (%) values for repeatability (intraday) and reproducibility (interday), which were 0.8–12.0 % and 0.8–12.6 %, respectively. According to Commission Decision, 2002/657/EC guidelines, acceptable analytical performance parameters are defined by mean recoveries within 70–120 % and precision with RSD values ≤20 % (Decision, 2002). The obtained results meet these criteria, confirming the reliability of the method.

Matrix effect

Matrix effects were classified according to the following criteria: soft (−20 to 20 %), medium (−50 to −20 % or 20 to 50 %), and strong (<−50 % or > 50 %) (Ferrer et al., 2011; Kmellár et al., 2008). Negative values indicate ionization suppression, whereas positive values signify ionization enhancement. The detailed results are presented in Table 2. The matrix effect assessment demonstrated that 55.2 % of the analyzed compounds exhibited a soft matrix effect, and 34.5 % showed a medium matrix effect. Notably, 10.3 % of the compounds displayed a strong matrix effect, which was specifically associated with nitrated compounds (5-NAce, 1-NPy, and 4-NPy). Therefore, the established method can be successfully applied to tea sample analysis, with matrix-matched calibration curves recommended to ensure more precise quantification.

Applicability to real samples

Tea varieties are categorized based on their processing methods, which typically involve five main steps, although not all teas complete every step and some may repeat certain stages. Following harvest, tea leaves undergo a withering process to reduce moisture content and prepare for rolling. During rolling, intense pressure physically distorts and disintegrates the tea leaves, destroying cellular integrity and promoting the release of internal constituents such as aromatic compounds, pigments, and enzymes. Subsequently, enzymatic oxidation occurs, primarily catalyzed by polyphenol oxidase. Depending on the tea type, a drying process is employed to control this oxidation by removing moisture from the tea leaves and halting enzyme activity. This drying step plays a crucial role in regulating tea fermentation. Green tea is a representative non-oxidized tea, while oxidized (fermented) teas include Oolong tea (semi-oxidized), Black tea (fully-oxidized), and Dark tea (post-fermented). Therefore, the tea samples were categorized into two primary groups, specifically green tea and oxidized/fermented tea. Table 3 summarizes the detection rate, detection range, and total concentrations of 4 PAHs (Σ4 PAHs), NPAHs (ΣNPAHs), and OPAHs (ΣOPAHs) in 40 dried tea samples. Overall, parent PAHs were detected at higher concentrations in oxidized/fermented teas compared to green teas. In the oxidized/fermented tea group, Phe was detected at the highest concentration (15.7–322.2 ng g−1), followed by Fln (3.9–174.1 ng g−1). These results are consistent with those reported by Guo et al. (2022), who observed Fln contamination levels ranging from ND (i.e., <LOD) to 179.6 μg kg−1 in Oolong tea (n = 51) and from ND to 162.6 μg kg−1 in black tea (n = 14). The European Union has established maximum levels for PAHs in dried herbs: 10.0 ng g−1 for BaP and 50 ng g−1 for the sum of BaA, Chr, BbF, and BaP (Σ4 PAHs) (European Commission, E, 2023). Values in excess of the regulatory limit for Σ4 PAHs were observed in some samples. One green tea sample exceeded the limit with a concentration of 62.7 ng g−1, while five oxidized/fermented tea samples also surpassed the limit, ranging from 61.2 to 98.3 ng g−1. Furthermore, one oxidized/fermented tea sample contained BaP at 11.8 ng g−1, slightly above the maximum allowed level (10 ng g−1). Another study analyzing 45 ripened Pu-erh tea samples detected BaP in 31.11 % of samples, with residue levels ranging from ND to 22.1 μg kg−1 (Fei et al., 2023).

The graphical representation in Fig. 4 illustrates the correlation between the detected levels of PAHs and their derivatives. A significant linear correlation (R = 0.7908, p < 0.001, 95 % CI: 0.641, 0.886) was observed between ΣPAHs and ΣOPAHs (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, a positive correlation (R = 0.6136, p < 0.001, 95 % CI: 0.367, 0.774) was found between Σ4 PAHs and ΣOPAHs (Fig. 4B). In contrast, NPAHs showed negligible correlation with parent PAHs (R = 0.0092, p = 0.949, 95 % CI: −0.302, 0.321). This difference in correlation suggests distinct formation pathways. Although NPAHs are primarily formed through high-temperature combustion processes or reactions of environmental PAHs with nitrogen oxides (Bandowe & Meusel, 2017), tea production involves drying (e.g., pan, basket, sun, and others) or oxidation/fermentation processes (Ahmed & Stepp, 2013; Stodt et al., 2014). Because these processing methods are more conducive to oxidation than high-temperature combustion, the formation of OPAHs is more prevalent in tea, leading to their co-occurrence with parent PAHs. However, it should be noted that differences in contaminant levels between green and oxidized/fermented tea cannot be attributed solely to processing. In our dataset, most green tea samples were sourced from Korea, whereas only two of the 14 oxidized/fermented tea samples originated from Korea. This means that variations may also reflect different environmental conditions, such as air or water pollution at the places of origin, rather than processing methods alone. Despite these limitations, these findings underscore the importance of monitoring PAHs, particularly the four regulated PAHs; however, they also demonstrate the value of assessing the levels of their derivatives (NPAHs and OPAHs) in tea products.

Comparison with other studies

The method developed in this study was compared with previously reported studies to objectively assess its performance in determining PAHs and their derivatives in tea. Table 4 summarizes the key aspects of the analytical methods, including the extraction techniques, target compounds, instrument, run time, LOQs, recoveries, and determination levels. Compared to studies published in the last five years, the method proposed in this study enabled the simultaneous analysis of a greater number of PAH derivatives (NPAHs and OPAHs), thus providing baseline data for the risk assessment of derivative contamination in tea samples (Cutillas et al., 2024; Deng et al., 2021; El-Deen & Shimizu, 2021; Li et al., 2023). Furthermore, this study demonstrated satisfactory sensitivity and accuracy, with LOQs for PAHs, OPAHs, and NPAHs of 1, 1, and 10 ng g−1, respectively, and recoveries ranging from 70.9 to 103.0 %, which are comparable or superior to analytical figures of merit in existing research (Cutillas et al., 2024; Fei et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2023). In conclusion, this study successfully established a simple and reliable analytical method for the determination of PAHs and their derivatives in dried tea.

Conclusion

The proposed methodology successfully identified and quantified 11 PAHs, 2 NPAHs, and 4 OPAHs across 40 diverse tea samples. The developed method demonstrated excellent sensitivity and selectivity, and allowed accurate and repeatable measurements of the concentration of the target analytes in dried tea matrices. Notably, BaP and 4 PAHs were detected in concentrations exceeding the established maximum permissible levels in some of the 40 tea samples. Furthermore, 1-NNaP was found to be the predominant PAH in both green and oxidized/fermented teas, suggesting the potential contribution of specific contamination sources. The detection of other PAH analogues such as 9-Flu, AntQ, BZT, and 7,12-BaAQ also emphasizes the importance of further assessment. Thus, the method can be effectively utilized in monitoring programs or risk assessments of PAHs and their derivatives in teas, contributing to food safety management. This methodological approach shows considerable promise for the analysis of plant-based foods, particularly those characterized by high pigment content such as tea. However, its applicability to complex matrices with lipids or proteins, including animal-derived products, requires further validation.
