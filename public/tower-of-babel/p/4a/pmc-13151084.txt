Higher red blood cell distribution width to platelet count ratio is associated with increased cardiovascular events in peritoneal dialysis patients
Authors: Jiadi Yu, Liuzhen Li, Na Tian, Qingdong Xu, Xiaojiang Zhan, Fenfen Peng, Xiaoyang Wang, Ning Su, Xiaoran Feng, Xingming Tang, Xianfeng Wu, Qian Zhou, Jianbo Liang, Yueqiang Wen, Jun Dong, Jiao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRed blood cell distribution width to platelet count ratio (RPR) has garnered increasing attention as a novel inflammation marker. However, its association with cardiovascular events (CVE) in end-stage renal disease patients undergoing peritoneal dialysis (PD) is largely unknown.Method1,222 PD patients, from 4 centers, were retrospectively recruited between January 1, 2012-December 31, 2017. Baseline data were collected ~ 3 months after starting PD treatment, and patients divided into 2 groups (low RPR [n = 710], high RPR [n = 512]), based on the optimal RPR cut-off of 0.084 identified by receiver operating characteristic curve analysis. The relationship between RPR with new CVE, cardiovascular disease mortality, and all-cause mortality was analyzed using restricted cubic spline (RCS) and Kaplan-Meier survival curve analyses. Associations between RPR and patient characteristics were identified using uni- and multi-variate Cox logistic regression analyses, adjusted for baseline patient characteristics, co-morbidities, and laboratory parameters. Competitive risk analysis was conducted to assess the effects of other follow-up endpoint events on CVEs.Results77 new CVEs and 212 deaths occurred during the follow-up period. High RPR, versus low, had significantly higher rates of new CVEs under Kaplan-Meier analysis; this was still present even after adjusting for specific baseline characteristics, co-morbidities, and laboratory parameters under multivariate Cox regression analysis. RCS analysis also revealed that the relationship between RPR and CVE was non-linear, with RPR ~ 0.06–0.15 being associated with higher CVE risk.ConclusionHigher RPR may serve as an independent prognostic marker for CVE risk in PD patients, providing a non-invasive, cost-effective marker for early CVE detection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-04925-x.

Introduction

Average life expectancies worldwide have increased owing to medical advances [1], but increased cardiovascular disease (CVD), a leading cause of morbidity and mortality, has also become progressively evident [2]. One significant CVD co-morbidity is chronic kidney disease (CKD), which is a significant exacerbating factor, as well as being an independent predictor of cardiovascular events (CVE) [3]. CKD itself is a significant public health challenge, particularly in Asia. There, dialysis-dependent populations are expanding at an alarming rate, with > 900,000 (218 patients/million) currently receiving maintenance dialysis; furthermore, the number of individuals needing renal replacement are projected to double by 2030 [4, 5]. With respect to the final stage of CKD, which is end-stage renal disease (ESRD), peritoneal dialysis (PD) is considered an essential therapeutic approach, and strategies to mitigate cardiovascular events and mortality in PD patients are of great importance to improve outcomes [6, 7].

A potential target to improve PD patient outcomes may involve inflammation, which has been noted to play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of coronary heart disease, where it contributes to atherosclerotic plaque instability and rupture [8, 9]. One consequence of increased inflammation could be elevated red blood cell (RBC) distribution widths (RDW), which has been identified as an independent CVD mortality risk factor in hemodialysis (HD) patients [10]. Indeed, RDW, being a routinely available hematologic index reflecting erythrocyte size heterogeneity, has increasingly been recognized as a marker of systemic inflammation and oxidative stress. More specifically, chronic inflammation can disrupt iron homeostasis and erythropoiesis, shortening red cell survival, and in turn, increasing anisocytosis and RDW [11]. Consistent with these observations, elevated RDW has been associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes and mortality across diverse populations, including patients with CKD and those receiving dialysis [12, 13]. Thus, RDW-based composite indices, such as the RDW to platelet count ratio (RPR), have gained interest as a potential cardiovascular risk stratification marker in PD patients [13]. It is also worth noting that platelets themselves have been observed to be integral for CVE occurrence and progression, likely via their interactions with endothelial cells, which initiates inflammatory responses, in turn inducing thrombus formation within coronary arteries [14, 15]. In fact, a number of recent investigations have indicated that RPR could serve as an inflammatory burden measure in PD patients [16, 17]. This possibly stems from inflammatory and neurohormonal pathways being activated in these patients, leading to disruptions in RBC membranes and impaired maturation, subsequently yielding increases in RDW, and thus RPR [16, 17].

As a result, based on the established roles of RDW as a marker of systemic inflammation, and platelet counts as a mediator of thrombo-inflammatory processes, we hypothesized that RPR could serve as an integrated biomarker reflecting the vulnerability to adverse CVEs in PD patients. In this study, we investigated whether RPR was associated with increased CVE risk in a multi-center cohort of continuous ambulatory PD (CAPD) patients. We found that higher RPR was associated with greater risk for new CVE occurrences in the CAPD patients, albeit in a non-linear fashion. This was still present even after adjusting for the baseline patient characteristics of sex, age, height, weight, co-morbidities, and standard laboratory biomarkers. Additionally, owing to age, diabetes, and hypertension being considered as well-established determinants of cardiovascular risk in CAPD patients, we examined whether subgroup stratification affected the association between RPR and CVE, and found that higher RPR was associated with CVE occurrence between different age, diabetes, and hypertension subgroups, indicating that it was independent of these potential confounders. Therefore, higher RPR could serve as a prognostic biomarker for CVE risk in PD patients, as they are reflective of elevated inflammation and platelet activation.

Methods

Patient recruitment and recording clinical characteristics

This was a retrospective study, involving an initial cohort of 1,419 CAPD patients, enrolled at 4 centers between January 1, 2012-December 31, 2017. The following exclusion criteria were applied: (1) Being < 18-years-old, (2) Receiving maintenance CAPD < 3 months, (3) Having severe liver functional abnormalities [18], malignancies, rheumatic, or hematological diseases, (4) Treated with glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants or chemotherapy, (5) Taken platelet and coagulation-affecting medications < 1 week pre-admission, (6) Having active infection < 3 months, or (7) Significant blood loss or transfusion history < 6 months, after which a total of 1,222 CAPD patients were recruited for this study (Fig. 1). The study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards (REB# LYZX-2025-184-01) of the 4 CAPD centers. The requirement for written informed consent was waived, as the study was retrospective in nature; all patient data were collected from existing records.

In terms of patient characteristics, for diabetes and hypertension, their presence was defined either by a formal diagnosis being recorded, or by the patient currently being administered hypoglycemic (for diabetes)/anti-hypertensive (for hypertension) medications. With respect to new CVE, they were defined as any of the following conditions recorded post-CAPD initiation: acute coronary syndrome (ex. myocardial infarction [MI], unstable angina hospitalization), heart failure, coronary revascularization (ex. percutaneous coronary intervention, coronary artery bypass grafting), arrhythmia requiring treatment, as documented by treating physicians (ex. pharmacological treatment, electrical cardioversion, hospitalization), as well as cerebrovascular accidents, or other major ischemic or hemorrhagic events. CAPD patients who died from these CVEs were considered to have died from CVD. Additionally, laboratory parameters, such as for complete blood count (CBC), RDW, platelet distribution width (PDW), and standard bio-markers (ex. serum albumin, alkaline phosphatase [ALP], etc.) were obtained (Table 1). Total Kt/V ([dialyzer clearance of urea×dialysis time]/total body water), a quantification of PD treatment adequacy, was also calculated using PD Adequest 2.0 (Baxter, Deerfield, IL). Medications used, such as angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARB) and erythropoietin (EPO), were identified from CAPD patient prescriptions.

All baseline data was collected from patients ~ 3 months after starting CAPD treatment, and they were followed up via telephone communication with trained nurses, as well as quarterly analyses at CAPD treatment centers, until death, transfer to HD, kidney transplantation, transferal to another CAPD center, being lost to follow-up, or reaching the end of the follow-up period on December 31, 2017. Primary outcomes were defined as newly-onset CVE, while secondary outcomes included CVD and all-cause mortality.

Data analyses

The 1,222 CAPD patients were divided into 2 groups, based on the optimal RPR cut-off value of 0.084 identified via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis: low (RPR < 0.084, 710 patients) and high RPR (RPR ≥ 0.084, 512 patients). The 2 groups were compared, and stratification validated with X-TILE software. To assess the relationship between RPR with new CVE, CVD mortality, and all-cause mortality, restricted cubic spline (RCS), as well as Kaplan-Meier survival curve analyses, followed by log-rank tests, were carried out. Furthermore, to identify relationships between RPR and patient characteristics, uni- and multi-variate Cox logistic regression analyses were conducted; these analyses were adjusted for various variables, such as age, sex, co-morbidities, and laboratory parameters. Additionally, to assess the effects of other follow-up endpoint events on new CVE and all-cause mortality, competitive risk analyses were carried out. For all analyses, the random forest method was used to impute missing data, using R software (v4.3.1).

Statistical analysis

X-TILE and SPSS (v25.0) were used for statistical analyses. All continuous variables did not have a normal distribution, so they were presented as medians (25th -75th percentiles), while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between 2 groups were conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables, while for categorical variables, the χ2 test was used. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparisons of baseline characteristics between low and high RPR groups

For the 1,222 recruited CAPD patients, the mean follow-up duration was 35.5 months. During the follow-up period, 77 new CVE (6.2%), 104 CVD deaths (8.5%), and 212 all-cause deaths (17.3%) occurred. Furthermore, at the end of the follow-up period, 56 patients received kidney transplants, 128 received HD, 13 were transferred to other CAPD centers, and 14 were lost to follow-up.

Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of patients, stratified by RPR level. High RPR, compared to low RPR, had lower body weight, along with higher prevalence of cardiovascular comorbidities, and less frequent usage of cardioprotective medications, such as angiotensin receptor blockers and statins. In terms of hematological parameters, high RPR also had lower hemoglobin, red blood cell, and platelet counts, along with higher RDW and PDW, indicating alterations in erythropoiesis and platelet dynamics. Furthermore, high RPR had more pronounced inflammatory and metabolic profiles, including higher high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP), along with disturbances in mineral metabolism and lipid-related parameters. All these findings indicate that elevated RPR is associated with an adverse baseline phenotypical profile in CAPD patients, possibly reflecting heightened inflammation and hematologic dysregulation.

Comparison of CVE, CVD mortality, and all-cause mortality between low and high RPR groups

We then analyzed the 212 all-cause deaths, in which the leading cause was CVD, at 104 deaths; the remaining were caused by infections (23), unexplained gastrointestinal bleeding (5), other (60), or unknown (20) causes. The majority of these all-cause, as well as CVD deaths, were found in the low RPR group, at 122 (57.5%), and 56 (53.8%), respectively, while high RPR had 90 all-cause (42.5%) and 48 CVD deaths (46.2%; Table 2). No significant differences, though, were present between low and high RPR groups for all-cause and CVD mortalities (Table 2).

We further verified whether low RPR had fewer CVE than high RPR using Kaplan-Meier survival curve analysis. There, low RPR had significantly lower probabilities for new CVE, from ~ 5–60 months after the start of PD diagnosis; this is associated with a value of 10.715 from the log-rank test (Fig. 2B). This result is supported by multivariate Cox logistic regression analyses, in which under the unadjusted model, CVE was ~ 2.1 times more common in high RPR, compared to low RPR group. Discussion

In this study, we observed, based on Kaplan-Meier survival curve analysis, that higher RPR was correlated with greater risk for new CVE occurrences in PD patients. This was still present even after adjusting for baseline patient characteristics of sex, age, height, weight, co-morbidities (ex. hypertension, diabetes, CVD), treatments (ex. diuretics, ACEI, ARB, EPO, iron), as well as standard laboratory bio-markers (ex. total Kt/V, RRF, total cholesterol, triglycerides, serum albumin, creatinine, urea nitrogen, uric acid, FBG, ALT, AST, RBC, HGB, monocytes, lymphocytes, Cl-, P) in multivariate Cox logistic regression analyses. However, the relationship between RPR and new CVE was found to be a non-linear one under RCS analysis, in which RPR levels at ~ 0.06–0.15 were associated with higher CVE risk, while levels ~ 0-0.06 correlated with lower CVE risk. Nevertheless, these observations all illustrate that RPR levels could serve as a potential prognostic biomarker for CVE in CAPD patients. Furthermore, subgroup analyses, stratified for age, diabetes, and hypertension, found that the association between increased RPR with CVE occurrence was still present among different subgroups. This suggests that the prognostic value of RPR is not driven by a single dominant risk factor but may be applicable across clinically relevant patient subgroups. Additionally, Kaplan-Meier analyses identifying a significant association between RPR and CVE occurrence, but not for cardiovascular death, observed in the Kaplan–Meier analyses requires careful interpretation. Notably, in our cohort, cardiovascular deaths were not fewer than CVE events. Therefore, the lack of statistical significance for cardiovascular mortality cannot be attributed to insufficient event numbers. This study was based on a multicenter database with heterogeneous follow-up durations (minimum follow-up > 1 year). Among the enrolled patients, 697 had follow-up exceeding 2 years, whereas 525 had follow-up shorter than 2 years. Such inter-center variability in follow-up length may have influenced the temporal distribution of mortality events. Moreover, CVEs tend to represent earlier, more frequent cardiovascular disease events, while cardiovascular death is a downstream outcome influenced by multiple factors. As a result, a biomarker like RPR, which reflects the inflammatory and hematologic status of an individual, may be more sensitive for identifying patients with increased CVE occurrence than for cardiovascular disease mortality within the available follow-up period. Therefore, RPR could potentially serve as a simple, non-invasive, cost-effective tool for risk stratification in CAPD patients.

RPR was first introduced by Chen et al. as a novel index for predicting significant liver fibrosis and cirrhosis in chronic hepatitis B (CHB) patients, where it demonstrated superior predictive accuracy, compared to traditional methods [19]. In fact, it has greater sensitivity and predictive value, compared to RDW and platelets alone [19]. Therefore, RPR could serve as a potential simple, non-invasive, and cost-effective procedure to screen for liver disease, reducing the need for liver biopsies. Subsequent studies have demonstrated that RPR was able to predict prognoses for patients with septic acute kidney injury, severe pneumonia, or liver fibrosis in CHB [20–22]. RPR was also found to serve as a diagnostic indicator for CVD, such as by Tong et al., who identified it as an independent predictor for in-hospital mortality among critically ill acute MI patients, even after adjusting for confounding factors, such as age, weight, ethnicity, and other clinical variables [23], as well as Zhu et al., who showed that RPR accurately predicted CVE among HD hemodialysis patients with ESRD [24]. Moreover, RPR was noted to be able to predict poor prognoses in acute ischemic stroke patients 3 months post-mechanical thrombectomy [25], and was found by Tang et al. to possess a U-shaped relationship with 1-year mortality in heart failure patients, in which RPR both < 0.104 and > 0.104 was associated with increased mortality [26]. Additionally, RPR enhanced the predictive accuracy of clinical scoring systems, such as Sequential Organ Failure Assessment and Simplified Acute Physiology Score II, providing more robust risk stratification in heart failure management [26]. All of these studies, as well as the observation from García-Escobar et al. that elevated RDW was strongly correlated with increased mortality from cardiovascular diseases [27], indicated that RPR could serve as a diagnostic and prognostic marker for CVD, thereby aiding to enhance clinical decision making. In particular, the findings by Zhu et al. [24] and Tang et al. [26] were in line with our findings of a non-linear relationship between RPR and CVE in PD patients, and a similar U-shaped pattern, as that previously described for 1-year mortality, was present under RCS for RPR levels ~ 0-0.15, even after adjusting for various confounding factors (ex. sex, age, BMI, comorbidities, medications, total Kt/V, RRF, total cholesterol, triglycerides, albumin, creatinine, urea nitrogen, uric acid, fasting glucose, ALT, AST, RBC, monocytes, lymphocytes, electrolytes, etc.). The presence of such a relationship between RPR and CVE in PD patients, even after adjusting for age and hypertension as potential confounders, is particularly notable as age-related arterial stiffness and hypertension have been noted to contribute to heightened cardiovascular risk [28]. Therefore, RPR could serve as an effective method for assessing CVE onset in PD patients with different comorbidities, providing more accurate predictions and evaluations of their clinical statuses and prognoses.

It should be noted that PD patients have unique metabolic and inflammatory challenges [29], in that systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, both of which are exacerbated by factors, such as uremia and bioincompatible dialysis solutions, have been found to play critical roles in CVD development among this patient demographic [30, 31]. Consequently, RPR has significant clinical value, as it could serve as a comprehensive marker that reflects underlying inflammatory states and microvascular dysfunction. More specifically, elevated RPR levels, signaling increased inflammation and impaired platelet function, reflect the presence of a prothrombotic environment, potentially accelerating CVE onset [32]. As a result, incorporating RPR into clinical assessments could provide clinicians with a more robust tool to identify PD patients at higher risk for cardiovascular complications, enabling earlier and more targeted interventions. Interestingly, our observation that RPR was not significantly associated with residual diuresis, or LDL, which have all been linked to CVE occurrence, seems to suggest that RPR may reflect inflammatory and hematologic pathways distinct from residual renal function, lipid-related risk, or baseline cardiac neurohormonal stress. Therefore, RPR could provide complementary prognostic value, aside from traditional dialysis- and cardiometabolic-related markers.

Caution should be taken regarding the administration of erythropoietin-stimulating agents (ESAs), such as EPO, or iron supplements to CAPD patients, as they could serve as potential confounding effects on the putative association between RPR and CVE occurrence. Indeed, 69.6% (850/1222) of the CAPD patients were on EPO in this study. With respect to ESAs, this is owed to them being documented to affect RPR, via their effects on platelet and iron homeostasis [33]. More specifically, they are able to transiently increase RDW, particularly when iron supplies are marginal, as they promote erythropoiesis, resulting in more heterogeneous red blood cells being released from the marrow [34, 35]. ESAs are also linked to increased platelet counts in HD patients, likely stemming from higher thrombopoietin and iron restricted erythropoiesis [33]. Consequently, ESA administration could either lower RPR, owing to it increasing platelet counts, or increase RPR, which could be traced from increases in RDW under iron-deficiency conditions [33]. As for iron supplements, their administration, based on mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH) as an index, could possibly lower ESA dosages in ESKD patients. Indeed, MCH is often found to be low in CKD patients [36], and has been associated with impaired iron utilization and higher ESA doses. However, MCH primarily reflects changes in hemoglobin concentration and red blood cell counts and does not directly serve as an indicator of iron deficiency or ESA supplementation needs. Moreover, in ESKD patients, alterations in MCH and impaired iron utilization are frequently driven by renal anemia related to insufficient endogenous EPO production, differences in iron absorption and utilization, as well as inflammation, rather than absolute iron deficiency alone. It is thus not recommended to use MCH alone to guide iron supplementation or ESA dosing without concurrently assessing iron indices and clinical context. Aside from these associations between MCH with iron supplement or ESA administration, low MCH has been noted to potentially correlate with higher RDW, and thus, higher RPR [37], indicating that it could serve as a potential confounding factor regarding RPR and CVE occurrence. In light of these observations, we adjusted for EPO and iron levels in the multivariate Cox logistic regression analyses and found that RPR was still significantly associated with CVE occurrence, indicating that neither EPO nor iron supplementation did not have significant confounding effects. Nevertheless, future studies should still be conducted to further elucidate the associations between MCH, RPR, and subsequently, CVE occurrence [32]. There are a number of limitations in this study, one of which is that it was a retrospective study that only examined CAPD patients. The choice of CAPD patents was to minimize any heterogeneity related to dialysis modality. However, the results may not be fully reflective for other PD patients, such as those who underwent automated PD (APD), or for HD patients, as they substantially differ in dialysis techniques, hemodynamic stability, inflammatory burden, anticoagulation exposure, and cardiovascular risk profiles. Therefore, future studies involving multiple centers and prospective analyses should be conducted to identify the differences between CAPD and APD on cardiovascular risk, as well as directly comparing PD (CAPD, APD) and HD patients, in order to further examine whether differences in dialysis modalities affect the prognostic value of RPR. Another limitation was that RPR was derived from a single baseline laboratory assessment, which may not fully reflect long-term exposure. Furthermore, even though patients were generally under stable maintenance therapy, detailed information regarding recent EPO dose adjustments was unable to be obtained, nor were iron status parameters consistently available from all participating centers. Therefore, the possibility of transient hematologic fluctuations affecting RPR levels cannot be fully excluded. Moreover, owing to the dynamic nature of RDW and platelet counts, which are influenced by inflammation, nutritional status, infections, iron metabolism, and dialysis-related factors, temporal changes in RPR were unable to be captured. Future prospective studies, incorporating longitudinal RPR measurements, as well as accounting for EPO and iron status, should be conducted to evaluate its dynamic trajectory and potential role in monitoring cardiovascular risk progression over time. Additionally, the follow-up period of < 3 years may be considered to be on the short side to analyze cardiovascular outcomes, though it should be noted that CVEs in PD patients tend to occur relatively early after starting dialysis, due to pre-existing cardiovascular burden and accelerated atherosclerosis. Therefore, the follow-up period was sufficient to capture a meaningful number of incident CVEs, as reflected by the observed event rates. Nevertheless, a longer follow-up period may be helpful to fully evaluate cardiovascular mortality. Our observations also did not specifically examine the potential linkages that cardiac function parameters (ex. ejection fraction) and blood pressure indices (ex. pulse pressure, difference between systolic and diastolic blood pressures), may have on the relationship between RPR and CVE risk, even though previous studies have linked RDW to heightened mortality from various cardiovascular diseases [27, 38], suggesting that RPR could also be associated with cardiac dysfunction. Such analyses should thus be conducted in future studies, as they may provide more comprehensive insights by which RPR contributes to cardiovascular outcomes in PD patients. Lastly, future analyses should account for traditional chronic inflammation markers, such as hsCRP and albumin, as potential confounding influences on RDW and cardiovascular risk.

Conclusion

Higher RPR were found, under Kaplan-Meier, RCS, and multivariate Cox logistic regression analyses, to correlate with greater risk for new CVE occurrences in PD patients, even after adjusting for baseline patient characteristics of sex, age, height, weight, co-morbidities, and standard laboratory biomarkers. Our study suggests that clinicians should more closely monitor PD patients with higher RPR, as it potentially reflects heightened inflammation and platelet activation, which are possibly associated with CVE onset. This could be mitigated by anti-inflammatory and anti-platelet activation treatments. Therefore, RPR may serve as a simple, non-invasive, and cost-effective screening tool for new CVE in PD patients.
