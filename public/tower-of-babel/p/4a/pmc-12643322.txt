Poly-/Perfluoroalkyl Substance (PFAS) Immobilization Using a Novel Adsorptive Organoclay Compared with Colloidal-Activated Carbon in Soil under Dynamic Redox Conditions and the Response of Microbial Communities
Authors: Viraj Gunarathne, Marina Schauerte, Felizitas Boie, Xing Yang, Tatiane Medeiros Melo, Jörg Rinklebe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Poly-/perfluoroalkyl substance (PFAS) contamination is a significant global environmental concern and requires effective remediation solutions. Although it is often assumed that PFAS sorption is not redox sensitive, redox fluctuations can significantly modify the biogeochemical environment, influencing PFAS behavior indirectly. This study evaluated the effectiveness of soil amendments, a novel adsorptive organoclay (AOC) and a colloidal-activated carbon (CAC) on PFAS immobilization under dynamic redox conditions. PFAS-contaminated soil was treated with AOC and CAC. We created a slurry, set systematically five predefined redox windows (from −100 to +500 mV), and collected samples at each redox window. PFAS concentrations were measured in the dissolved phase (<0.45 μm), and phospholipid fatty acid (PLFA) was analyzed in the solid phase (>0.45 μm). The contaminated soil contained 13 PFAS compounds. CAC treatment drastically reduced the dissolved PFAS concentrations across all redox windows compared to the control and AOC treatment. Perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), perfluorooctanesulfonate (PFOS), perfluorononanoic acid (PFNA), and 6:2 fluorotelomer sulfonamide propyl betaine (6:2 FTAB) dominated in the dissolved phase of control and AOC treatment. The dissolved phase of CAC mainly contained shorter-chain perfluoropentanoic acid (PFPeA) and perfluorobutanoic acid (PFBA). The specific surface area (SSA) along with total carbon (TC) content of amendments are key factors in reducing PFAS concentration in the dissolved phase. Relatively similar PLFA profiles indicated no adverse effects on soil microbial community from either amendment compared to the control. This study highlights CAC as an effective amendment for PFAS immobilization even under changing environmental redox conditions.

Introduction

Per- and polyfluorinated alkyl substances (PFAS) are a fabricated class of chemical substances used in various industrial products due to their high persistence as well as their hydrophobic and lipophobic properties. Major sources of PFAS include aqueous film-forming foams (AFFF) used for firefighting activities, wastewater effluents, and industrial emissions. − PFAS, including perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), perfluorooctanesulfonate (PFOS), perfluorohexanesulfonate (PFHxS), and 6:2 fluorotelomer sulfonate (6:2 FTSA), which are extensively used in AFFF. More than 10,000 man-made compounds have been identified within the PFAS family according to the OECD definition of PFAS.

PFAS are persistent environmental contaminants found globally in soils and groundwater. Airborne PFAS emissions can lead to widespread soil and groundwater contamination, even in areas not directly downstream from sources. Furthermore, contaminated groundwater has the potential to spread and pollute adjacent wetlands, posing a threat to these sensitive ecosystems.

PFAS contamination in the environment could cause serious ecological consequences, affecting the health of animals, plants, and microorganisms. − Additionally, PFAS contamination of agricultural products and water sources poses a concern to human health and food security. Numerous studies demonstrated that PFAS have led to an increased disease of lung, liver, cardiovascular, thyroid, kidney, immune system, and reproductive disorders in animals and humans, while reducing growth, root membrane integrity, photosynthetic pigment content, and increasing oxidative stress in plants. − In the natural environment, PFAS are also observed to inhibit microbial growth, diversity, community structure, and metabolic processes depending on microbial species, and types and concentrations of PFAS. − PFAS can also induce or inhibit gene expression and lipid synthesis of bacteria and fungi while interfering with microbial nitrification and denitrification processes. −

The remediation of PFAS contamination in nature through chemical, physical, or biological methods is a great challenge. Difficulties for remediation occur due to complex molecular structure, high mobility in water and soil, and, extensive chemical, biological, and thermal stability of PFAS. , In soils, PFAS can be removed through abiotic degradation and/or immobilized through physical and chemical adsorption. Although abiotic degradation of PFAS using thermal energy or chemical agents has the potential to remediate solid media, they are associated with high costs, energy demands, and environmental constraints, making it not sustainable for many purposes. Therefore, the utilization of adsorption materials could be a sustainable and cost-effective approach to remediating PFAS-contaminated soil and groundwater employing remediation techniques such as adsorption barriers. Several recent studies have investigated a variety of adsorbent materials, as well as novel approaches and mechanistic insights, to improve the efficiency of PFAS immobilization. − Nevertheless, the majority of earlier research on PFAS immobilization has mostly been dependent on straightforward batch sorption tests that concentrate on individual adsorption events. In contrast, the current study was specifically developed to evaluate the performance of adsorption materials under more advanced dynamic redox settings, thus better simulating the varying environmental variables that occur in real soil systems.

This is the first time to compare the effectiveness of two adsorption materials, a novel adsorptive organoclay (AOC) and a colloidal-activated carbon (CAC), on PFAS removal under systematically changing redox conditions. Redox potential (Eh) can be a key factor in determining the mobilization and environmental fate of PFAS at the soil–water interface. Broader system-level changes can occur in redox-dynamic environments, such as changes in DOM composition, mineral dissolution, or microbial metabolism, which can affect the PFAS mobility as well. Additionally, for the first time, the mobilization of PFAS from contaminated soil is studied under systematically changed redox conditions in the presented manuscript. Redox potential changes in the environment are associated with flood-dry cycles, which can take long time spans ranging from months to years. We added both adsorption materials to the soil and mimicked changing environmental (Eh) conditions, which usually occur in the long-term in nature, associated with fluctuating water levels and the linked redox changes. This allows a prognosis concerning the mobilization of PFAS under changing environmental conditions.

Both AOC and CAC were specifically developed for the purpose of immobilizing PFAS as adsorption barriers in situ, which are established by the direct injection of colloidal particles into the soil and groundwater interface. Activated carbon-based adsorption materials such as CAC are already used to remove PFOS, PFOA, and other PFAS from aqueous media. , It revealed more than 90% efficiency toward the removal of PFOS. However, the removal of shorter-length PFAS with carboxylic head groups with activated carbon-based materials is challenging. Other contaminants, such as inorganic ions, further hinder the adsorption of shorter-length PFAS onto activated carbon. Therefore, novel adsorption materials or enhancements to existing adsorbents for the sustainable and effective remediation of soils contaminated with PFAS are urgently needed.

AOC is an engineered novel adsorptive material specially designed to remove PFAS in soil. It is a clay–polymer composite material comprised of low molecular weight (<100 kDa) polydiallyldimethylammonium chloride (pDADMAC) coated micrometer-sized sepiolite particles. Sepiolite is a geogenic clay mineral and is considered a nontoxic and low-cost source of adsorptive material, which exhibits a large specific surface area (SSA) and powerful adsorptive capabilities. , However, the negatively charged sepiolite particles are less effective concerning the adsorption of anionic compounds such as PFAS. Melo et al. indicated an increased PFAS removal efficiency with the modification of sepiolite with cationic polymer pDADMAC. Furthermore, pDADMAC is extensively used as a coagulant in water treatment plants and identified as an effective agent to remove PFAS. − In contrast to activated carbon, pDADMAC is considered to effectively absorb shorter-chain PFAS as well.

Additionally, there is no study available to determine the alterations in the microbial community structure in PFAS-contaminated soils under dynamic redox conditions. Changes in soil microbial community structure could be used as a viable indicator to assess ecotoxicological threats associated with soil amendments. , We hypothesized that the applied amendments and redox potential could alter microbial community structures in soils.

A better understanding of the role of redox potential in the interaction between PFAS and adsorption materials allows for better design and implementation of remediation strategies for PFAS-contaminated soils and groundwater. Therefore, in this study, an automated microcosm system (MC) was used to set predefined redox conditions, which has been proven to be a useful tool in elucidating the mobilization of different elements and their underlying biogeochemical processes. − Furthermore, redox experiments utilizing systematically changing redox potentials can be employed to elucidate the fate of PFAS and amendments in soils in an accelerated manner, thus providing the necessary information regarding the reliability of using soil amendments for long-term applications. Therefore, this study aims to quantify and assess the effectiveness of two amendments, i.e., AOC and CAC, on PFAS immobilization in a PFAS-contaminated soil under predefined redox conditions and to uncover changes in microbial community structure.

Materials and Methods

Soil Sampling and Preincubation

The PFAS-contaminated soil was collected from a former firefighting training site of an abundant military airport in Viersen, Germany (51°12′07.1″N, 6°07′18.0″E). At the sampling site, the soil was covered with tarpaulin sheets to prevent wind erosion and limit the spread of PFAS-contaminated soil particles. This covering also prevented plant growth at the sampling site. Soil samples were taken from the soil surface layer (0–20 cm) and pooled into an approximately 10 kg composite sample. Subsequently, the composite soil sample was transported to the Soil- and Groundwater-Management Laboratory, University of Wuppertal, Germany. The bulk soil was homogenized, air-dried, ground, and sieved <2 mm prior to use. This soil sample was divided into three parts (each part consisted of 2 kg of air-dried soil). One part without amendment served as control, and the other two parts were treated separately with AOC (a sepiolite-pDADMAC composite provided by Intrapore GmbH, Essen, Germany; commercially known as Fluorolock, formerly Intraplex A) and CAC (a colloidal-activated carbon provided by Intrapore GmbH, Essen, Germany; commercially known as Intraplex, formerly Intraplex B) at an 8 g kg–1 application dose. The amendment ratio of 8 g kg–1 was chosen to ensure adequate interaction between the adsorbents and PFAS under the experimental setup, allowing for a meaningful comparison of the two materials. Each soil sample was preincubated for 8 weeks at 65% of the water-holding capacity of the soil using ultrapure water. The 8-week preincubation period was used to allow the soil microbial population and amendments to stabilize in the soil. This technique helps to reduce microbial response variability during subsequent redox treatments. During preincubation, soils were kept at ambient oxic conditions, with no glucose or wheat straw added. The soil water content was maintained by adjusting two times per week while homogenization was carried out once a week by hand. Gravimetric measurements were used when adjusting the soil water content.

Characterization of Soil and Amendments

The air-dried soil was used to determine the PFAS content with LC-MS/MS (by Eurofins Environment West GmbH, Germany) and characterized using standard protocol, DIN 38414-14 (2011). The pH of PFAS-contaminated soil and soil-amendment mixtures was determined by extracting with 0.01 M CaCl2 and ultrapure water separately at a solid–liquid ratio of 1:5. The electric conductivity (EC) was measured in PFAS-contaminated soil and soil-amendment mixtures after extracting with ultrapure water at a solid/liquid ratio of 1:5. Microwave digestion of 0.6 g of sample (i.e., soil, amendments, or amendment treated soil) with aqua regia (3:1 hydrochloric and nitric acid) followed by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES, Ultima 2, Horiba Jovin Yvon, Unterhaching, Germany) was employed to determine the elemental concentrations. In addition, the soil was analyzed for total carbon content using a C/N analyzer (Multi N/C 2100, Analytik Jena, Germany).

The specific surface area (SSA) of untreated soil, amendments, and soil: amendment mixtures was calculated based on the method mentioned in Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) using N2-adsorption on Quantachrome Autosorb iQ apparatus (Boynton Beach, Florida). The method was performed according to Wang et al. The morphology and chemical composition of the amendments were determined with scanning electron microscopy (SEM) coupled with EDX (JSM-6460LV, JEOL USA Inc., MA). Additionally, the soil particle distribution was measured using an automated soil particle size analyzer (PARIO, METER, Germany) and the soil texture class was identified in accordance to the German soil texture classification (KA 6).

Experiment under Dynamic Redox Conditions

An automated biogeochemical microcosm (MC) system (see Supporting Method S1 for additional details) was utilized to facilitate water-saturated conditions of the soil and systematic controlled changes in the redox potential (Eh). In the current study, five independent MCs (replications) were used for each treatment (a total of 15 MCs). The glass chamber of each MC was filled with 210 g of preincubated soil and 1680 mL of tap water to maintain a 1:8 soil-to-water ratio. Additionally, 15 g of finely powdered wheat straw and 15 g of glucose were added to each microcosm to provide an organic substrate for soil microorganisms. The MCs were enclosed airtight. This specific equipment enabled us to create specific Eh windows. Each MC employed three specialized electrodes (Sensortechnik Meinsberg, Germany) to continuously monitor the Eh, pH, and temperature. The data logger (LogTrans 16GPRS, UIT, Dresden, Germany) coupled with MCs recorded the Eh, pH, and temperature data in 10 min intervals. The Eh values acquired with an Ag/AgCl electrode at ambient temperature were later standardized to the hydrogen electrode at 25 °C. ,

The soil slurry was collected from MCs at each predefined redox window ranging from −100 to +500 mV (Figure ) without interrupting mixing. However, at the initial sampling, the Eh were not controlled by the addition of N2 or O2. The redox windows used represent important oxidative and reductive states seen in natural soil settings, and the order used represents dynamic redox cycling to replicate natural oscillations. The actual redox potentials at each sampling point slightly deviated from the predefined redox windows (Table ). However, to ensure consistency and facilitate comparison across treatments, the predefined redox windows of +400, −100, +100, +300, and +500 are consistently used here. Approximately 750 mL of samples were taken from each MC over the course of the experiment’s five predefined redox windows. The total volume withdrawn was kept below 45% of the initial volume, which was factored into our mass balance and found to have no major effect on the system or the findings.

The collected dissolved phase (approximately 150 mL of the slurry from each MC) was centrifuged for 15 min at 5000 rpm (Heraeus Megafuge 16, Thermo Scientific, Germany), and filtered using a filtration apparatus with 0.45 μm Whatman cellulose nitrate Millipore filter papers inside an anaerobic workstation (Whitley A35 Anaerobic Workstation, Don Whitley Scientific, Shipley, U.K.). These filter papers are PFAS-free and have minimal sorptive interaction with PFAS chemicals; they were specifically chosen to avoid any interference or loss of PFAS due to adsorption during sample processing.

Inside the same anaerobic workstation, filtrates from MCs of the same treatment were mixed to obtain a composite sample aiming to reach sufficient volumes (ca. 500 mL), which are required to measure PFAS concentrations since the dissolved phase contained comparably low PFAS concentrations. The separated unstabilized dissolved phase was stored in PFAS-free PE plastic bottles at 4 °C until collection by Eurofins Environment West GmbH. The solid phase collected from the MCs of the same treatment was pooled and homogenized similarly to the dissolved phase. The dissolved phase was used to determine PFAS concentration and other chemical parameters (Section ). The subsamples for measuring the total Al, Fe, Mn, and S concentrations were acidified with 65% HNO3 (1 vol %) and stored at 4 °C, while solid samples were stored at −80 °C for phospholipid fatty acid (PLFA) analysis.

Chemical Analysis

Samples for PFAS analysis were analyzed by the accredited commercial company Eurofins Environment West GmbH, Germany, according to the standard DIN 38407-42 (2011) procedure to measure the 27 most frequently detected PFAS (Supporting Method S2). The concentration of metals in the dissolved fraction was analyzed using ICP-OES (Ultima 2, Horiba Jovin Yvon, Unterhaching, Germany), while ion chromatography (Eco IC, Metrohm, Switzerland) was used to measure the concentrations of anions (NO3 –, SO4 2–, and PO4 3–). Total dissolved carbon (TDC) and inorganic carbon (DIC) were determined by a C–N analyzer (Multi N/C 2100, Analytik Jena, Germany). Dissolved organic carbon (DOC) was calculated by subtracting DIC from TDC. The UV/vis spectrophotometry (DR 6000, HACH Lange, Germany) absorbance at 254 nm and DOC values were used to calculate the specific UV absorbance (SUVA254) in the solution. The Fe2+ concentration was estimated in the filtered liquids by employing colorimetric techniques coupled with UV/vis spectroscopic measurements (DR 6000, HACH Lange, Germany) (Supporting Method S3).

PLFA Analysis

Soil phospholipid fatty acids (PLFA) were evaluated following established extraction and analysis protocols , (Supporting Method S4), aiming to investigate the microbial community structure in the solid phase. Samples from the freeze-dried solid phase were used for the PLFA analysis. The polar lipids in the extracted solution were separated from glycolipids and neutral lipids using a silica-bonded phase separation column and trans-esterified into fatty acid methyl esters via slight alkaline methanolysis. Finally, the samples were analyzed for PLFA using a GC-MS (GC-MS-QP2010 ultra, Shimadzu, Japan) with a nonpolar DB-5MS column (length = 60 m, internal diameter = 0.250 mm, film = 0.25 μm).

The categorization of PLFA in the current study was based on their chemical fractions, following the nomenclature proposed by Böhme et al. (Table S1). Individual PLFA were summed to calculate the total PLFA concentration. Analytical Quality Control

Analytical-grade chemicals were used for all chemical analyses, which were conducted with replicates, except for the PFAS analysis. All extractions and chemical analyses related to PLFA analysis were also performed with replicates by using GC-MS grade chemicals. To maintain analytical quality control, experimental blanks and standard reference materials were used for all analytical procedures.

Results and Discussion

Soil and Amendment Properties

The studied soil has a medium loamy sand soil texture with a pH­(H2O) of 4.9, whereas both amendment treatments have a pH(H20) of around 4.3 (Table S2). The electrical conductivity and total carbon content of the soil were 145.4 S m–1 and 31 g of C (kg soil)−1, respectively (Table S2). Soil Fe and Al contents were 5916 and 4911 mg kg–1, respectively. The SSA of soil is 0.68 m2 g–1. The CAC has more than 13 times greater SSA than that of AOC (AOC - 97.89 m2 g–1, CAC - 1352.81 m2 g–1) (Table S3) which resulted in higher SSA in CAC-treated soil (2.68 m2 g–1) compared to the AOC-treated soil (0.68 m2 g–1) (Table S2). CAC contains 90% of TC, reflecting its’ activated carbon-based origin. In contrast, AOC contains greater content of Al, Fe, and S compared to CAC. The particle size of AOC is much higher compared to CAC (Figure S1). Therefore, a higher surface area could be expected from CAC. The larger particle size of AOC (Figure S1) and the presence of chlorine on the particle surfaces (Figure S2) indicate the successful surface coating of micrometer-sized sepiolite clay mineral particles with pDADMAC.

In the collected solid soil samples, 13 PFAS compounds were detected, namely, PFBA, PFPeA, PFHxA, PFHxS, PFHpA, PFOA, PFOS, PFNA, PFDA, PFUnA, 6:2 FTS, 8:2 FTS, and 6:2 FTAB. The concentrations of PFOS, 6:2 FTAB, PFNA, and 8:2 FTS (170, 78, 53, and 51.5 μg kg–1, respectively) are substantially higher than the concentrations of the other PFAS (Table ) and occupy more than 78% of the total PFAS concentration in soil.

PFAS Immobilization Efficiency of Amendments and Effect of Specific Surface Area

PFAS concentrations in the dissolved phase varied depending on the treatment and the Eh (Figure a). The CAC treatment showed the lowest total PFAS concentrations in the dissolved phase throughout the experiment, with values between 1000 and 2200 ng L–1, compared to the other treatments, with PFAS concentrations ranging from 5409 to 30,020 ng L–1. Therefore, CAC seems to have the highest PFAS immobilization efficiency. This might be explained primarily by the higher surface area of the amendment CAC compared to AOC (Table S1 and Figure S1) and second by its greater affinity for PFAS adsorption. The higher surface area of CAC resulted from its primary component of activated carbon with developed porosity. Furthermore, the presence of hydrophobic surfaces in activated carbon components, which regularly attract fluorinated carbon chains, results in its greater affinity toward PFAS. Previous studies already highlighted the excellent capability of activated carbon on the adsorption of relatively long-chain PFAS and PFAS with sulfonic head groups. −

(a) Dissolved PFAS concentration (ng L–1) in the different soil treatments and different redox potentials, and (b) the average relative mobilization of the dissolved PFAS in relation to soil adsorbed PFAS (plotted using the relationship of PFAS concentration present in the original soil to cumulative PFAS concentrations measured under all tested redox conditions, averaged across replicates; AOC: adsorptive organoclay, CAC: colloidal-activated carbon).

In contrast to CAC, AOC demonstrated limited effectiveness for PFAS removal (Figure a). However, prior studies indicated that either pDADMAC alone or engineered adsorptive materials containing pDADMAC adsorb PFAS effectively. ,, This discrepancy may be attributed to the water-soluble nature of pDADMAC. , The AOC-amended treatment had higher chloride concentrations in the dissolved phase throughout the experiment compared to the control and the soil amended with CAC (Figure S3). AOC is loaded with the polymer pDADMAC, which contains chloride (Figure S2). Therefore, it is likely that pDADMAC was released from the sepiolite particles and transferred to the dissolved phase. This could contribute to the lower effectiveness of AOC for the removal of PFAS from the dissolved phase. Nevertheless, AOC decreased the mobilization of PFHxS, PFHpA, PFOA, PFOS, PFNA, PFDA, PFUnA, and 8:2 FTS (Figure b). In contrast, the mobilization of PFBA, PFPeA, 6:2 FTS, and 6:2 FTAB in the dissolved phase of AOC treatment was higher compared to that in the control (Figure ). We hypothesize that PFAS were attached to pDADMAC in the dissolved phase, which could temporarily prevent them from being adsorbed by the soil particles. Our previous column study using AOC suggested that nonbound pDADMAC particles to sepiolite mineral surfaces can leach into aqueous solutions, as indicated by their presence in column effluents, causing ecotoxicological impacts on Daphnia magna. This would explain why the concentration of total dissolved PFAS was higher in the treatment with AOC during the experiment compared with the control (Figure a). However, to verify this hypothesis, deep geochemical evidence remains to be elucidated in future.

The impact of the specific surface area of amendments on PFAS mobilization warrants further discussion, as it likely plays a crucial role in determining the immobilization of PFAS. PFAS mobilization followed the trend: AOC > control > CAC, which inversely mirrored the specific surface area values of AOC < control < CAC (Tables S2 and S3). The significantly higher SSA of CAC compared to AOC and the control (Table S3 and Figure S1) likely contributed to its enhanced PFAS retention, thereby increasing immobilization. Conversely, the lower specific surface area of AOC compared to the control aligns with its lower PFAS immobilization efficiency. Even though comparing surface areas between materials with different compositions and adsorption mechanisms should be done with caution, these findings highlight the critical influence of the surface area for the sorbent of especially multicompound systems such as PFAS in soil systems.

Effect of Eh, Chain Length, and Headgroup on PFAS Mobilization

PFAS compositions in the dissolved phase of the current study differed by treatment and redox potential. On average, in terms of all treatments, 6:2 FTAB, PFNA, PFOS, and PFOA accounted for the largest mean proportion of dissolved PFAS in the control (71.0%) and AOC (78.3%) treatments (Figure a). Whereas PFBA and PFPeA accounted for the largest mean proportion in the CAC treatment (58.1%) (Figure S4). Here, the control showed a substantial decrease in 6:2 FTAB, PFNA, PFOS, and PFOA concentrations between +400 to +500 mV except at +100 mV. In contrast, the dissolved concentrations of 6:2 FTAB, PFNA, PFOS, and PFOA varied only slightly in the AOC-amended soil during the Eh +400, −100, +100, and +300 mV, respectively (Figure a). However, the concentrations of those PFAS decreased substantially at +500 mV. The PFBA and PFPeA concentrations in the CAC treatment increased up to +300 mV and decreased slightly at +500 mV (Figure S4).

The highest total PFAS concentration in the dissolved phase was observed in the initial sampling at +400 mV of the control (30,049 ng L–1) (Figure a). The concentration of PFAS in the control decreased gradually during the experiment (exception from −100 to +100 mV) and reached 5450 ng L–1 at +500 mV. PFAS concentrations in the AOC-amended soil ranged from 22,861 to 29,060 ng L–1, staying within a comparable order of magnitude for Eh conditions of +400, −100, +100, and +300 mV. Only the PFAS concentration at Eh +500 mV was lower (13,534 ng L–1).

In general, PFAS compounds can withstand elevated redox conditions, although several innovative and advanced oxidation and reduction techniques have proven efficient in treating PFAS in aqueous media. However, under the created redox conditions in this experiment, we would not expect a breakdown of PFAS molecules purely due to the redox potential. Under the redox conditions applied in our experiment, precursor transformation can occur, ultimately generating stable PFAS compounds. We hypothesize that the detection of short-chain PFAS, such as PFBA, with mobilization exceeding 100% (Figure a) may be attributed to these precursor transformations. However, our data set’s resolution is not enough to conclusively connect precursor transformation to particular redox circumstances. Furthermore, redox conditions are important in the PFAS immobilization process because adsorption materials such as activated carbon act as an electron acceptor and redox mediators.

In the control and AOC treatment, the dissolved concentrations of 6:2 FTAB, PFNA, PFOS, and PFOA were higher than those of short-chain PFAS (Figure a). Although long-chain PFAS are known to be less mobile. ,, However, PFOS (37.8%), 6:2 FTAB (17.3%), PFNA (11.8%), and 2:8 FTS (11.4%) showed the highest relative abundances in the sampled soil, which led to enhanced dissolved concentrations of these PFAS (Table ).

Additionally, it is likely that the head groups of the PFAS also influenced their mobilization. The relative mobilization of 6:2 FTAB and PFNA in the dissolved phase was higher than the PFOS concentrations in the control and the two treatments (Figure b), although the soil contained a much higher PFOS content (Table ). PFNA and PFOS have the same chain lengths but showed different mobilization. PFAS with a sulfonic headgroup, such as PFOS, show a higher affinity for adsorption sites than PFASs with a carboxylic headgroup, such as PFNA. ,, According to McCleaf et al., this can be attributed to the greater electrostatic effect of the sulfonic functional group compared to the carboxylic group. Crone et al. mentioned that sulfonates are considered more hydrophobic than carboxylates, which in turn leads to higher adsorption of these substances.

However, 6:2 FTAB, despite being the longest substance and having both carbonic and sulfonic groups, is detected at the highest concentration in the dissolved phase of control and AOC treatment in our experiment (Figure a). Possibly due to its polyfluorinated structure, 6:2 FTAB indicated poorer adsorption efficacy than fully fluorinated PFAS compounds. The degree of fluorination affects the hydrophobicity, which in turn leads to lower adsorption of polyfluorinated compounds compared to their perfluorinated counterparts. , For instance, Xiao et al. discovered in an experiment with granular activated carbon that polyfluorinated compound Keto-perfluorooctanesulfonate adsorbs less effectively than its perfluorinated counterpart, PFOS. Gellrich observed a comparable trend with HPFHpA–PFHpA and 6:2 FTS–PFOS in a sorption experiment.

Microbial Interactions with PFAS

The concentration of total PLFA increased in the solid phase of the control and the two treatments during the experiment from +400 to +500 mV (Figure ). PLFA mainly represents living microorganisms due to the fast degradation of PLFA after cell death. , Therefore, an increase in the total PLFA concentrations can be related to an increase in living microbial biomass. The increase in microbial biomass during the experiment can be explained by the growing microbial population during the duration of the experiment, with the increase of Eh supported by the initial addition of glucose and wheat straw as growth substrates. The increase in microbial growth can also be attributed to the higher proportion of aerobic microorganisms present in the PFAS-contaminated soil, whose growth is stimulated under more favorable conditions for them at higher Eh.

The control and AOC treatments exhibited a faster increase in total PLFA concentration with increasing Eh compared to the CAC treatment (Figure ). However, the control showed a decrease in total PLFA concentration at +500 mV. According to Wimpenny and Necklen, optimal aerobic growth occurred between +200 and +300 mV. Therefore, it seems that the growth of aerobic microorganisms was restrained in the control after +300 mV in our experiment. The treatment with CAC showed a lower total PLFA concentration at −100 mV compared to the initial Eh (+400 mV) and thereafter indicated an increase of total PLFA concentration with increasing Eh. The removal of PFAS from the dissolved phase and its subsequent adsorption onto solid particles by CAC may have temporarily restrained microbial growth on those surfaces, as the higher concentrations of PFAS adsorbed to the solid phase could have inhibited the activity of surface-attached microbial communities. This is likely to explain the slower increase in the microbial biomass reported. Xu et al. reported a significant reduction in soil respiration with PFAS contamination in the soil indicating the toxicity of PFAS on microorganisms. Activated carbon-based adsorption materials such as CAC can bind microbial growth-promoting inorganic and organic compounds due to their large surface area and thereby inhibit microbial growth.

Control and the two treatments showed an increase in the absolute concentration and relative abundance of the terminally branched unsaturated fatty acids (TBSAT) during the experiment (Figures and S5). TBSATs are biomarkers of Gram-positive bacteria (GPB) or, being more accurate, of Firmicutes. The phylum Firmicutes contains the species Bacillus subtilis, which was found to take up certain PFAS. The PFAS contamination in our studied soil could have promoted a high abundance of B. subtilis. Li et al. indicated accelerated growth rates of the members in the phylum Firmicutes by the addition of glucose in the early stage of their growth curve. Furthermore, lower environmental pH values are involved in the higher growth rate of Firmicutes. Therefore, the addition of glucose at the start of the redox experiment, coupled with the gradual decrease of pH over time (Figure ), may have also contributed to the increased growth of Firmicutes.

Furthermore, the PLFA concentrations were used to calculate stress ratios. The control and the two treatments showed similar changes in the MONO/SAT ratio, while AOC showed slightly lower CY/PRE, TRANS/CIS, and ANTEISO/ISO ratios compared to the control and the treatment with CAC (Figure ). The MONO/SAT and the CY/PRE ratio can be connected to substrate availability and the abundance of Gram-negative bacteria (GNB). − A decreasing MONO/SAT ratio suggests a reduction in GNB and/or an increase in Gram-positive bacteria (GPB) populations within the microbial community. Additionally, increases in the CY/PRE and TRANS/CIS ratios are indicative of a stressed GNB community, as these ratios are commonly associated with bacterial responses to environmental stressors such as nutrient limitation, toxicity, or changes in redox conditions. In the control and two treatments, the MONO/SAT ratio decreased from the initial to the last sampling, while the CY/PRE ratio increased during the experiment (Figure a,b). The MONO fatty acids are biomarkers for GNB, which feed on easily available substrates. Due to the addition of glucose and straw at the beginning of the experiment, substrates were available for the microbial community at the initial sampling, which supported the growth of GNB. However, flooding and low pH conditions can suppress GNB, while promoting the growth of anaerobic bacteria. , Many species of the phylum Firmicutes are anaerobic and fermentative organisms capable of rapid growth under anoxic conditions when glucose is available in the early stage of their growth curve, exhibiting traits characteristic of r-strategists. During the experiment, the growth of Firmicutes resulted in a decrease in the MONO/SAT ratio (Figure a), driven by their consumption of readily available substrates, as indicated by the decline in DOC concentrations (Figure S3). TRANS/CIS ratios >0.1 can be used as a microbial stress index, while an increase could be caused by contamination or starvation. , In this study, the TRANS/CIS ratio was >0.2 for the control and two treatments during the experiment, showing slightly lower TRANS/CIS ratios for the treatment with AOC (Figure c). We assume that AOC’s comparatively lower PFAS adsorption resulted in less PFAS load in the solid phase, reducing its impact on the microbial community. Furthermore, the ANTEISO/ISO ratio is an important predictor of microbial adaptability to environmental circumstances. Bacteria regulate this ratio to maintain membrane fluidity and stability in response to temperature changes and harmful chemicals. Our results suggest that the bacterial communities in the solid phase of CAC and AOC treatments exhibited positive adaptations to the evolving conditions within the MC over time (Figure d). Especially, the comparatively higher ANTEISO/ISO ratio of CAC treatment indicated better adaptability of microbial communities compared to the AOC treatment.

AOC may exhibit both beneficial and potentially concerning ecotoxicological effects. It was shown to increase soil aggregate stability, a positive indicator of soil health and structure (in this study, AOC was referred to as Intraplex A). However, the same study also reported increased earthworm lethality, raising concerns about its impact on the soil fauna. While no adverse effects on microbial biomass were observed based on the PLFA profile, it is important to note that microbial diversity was not explicitly measured, leaving room for potential undetected impacts on the microbial community structure.

Changes in Other Chemical Parameters

The Eh values ranged from +400 to +500 mV during the initial sampling period for the control and two treatments (Figure ). Then, the Eh values were controlled by the addition of O2 or N2, and subsamples were collected at approximately −100, +100, +300, and +500 mV. The pH values of the two amended soils were about 5.5 at the beginning of the experiment, whereas the pH of the control was about 6.5. During the experiment, pH values decreased continuously to about 4.0 in the control and two treatments. Previous studies indicated that Eh and pH are negatively correlated. , Therefore, acidic conditions are expected to be reached with increasing Eh.

The highest Fe concentration in the dissolved phase was reported at +300 mV in the AOC treatment (Figure S3). AOC contained 2846.2 mg kg–1 of Fe (Table S3), which is most likely the reason for the highest Fe concentration in this treatment. However, the control exhibited the highest Fe concentration in the dissolved phase at +400, −100, and +500 mV compared to those of the two treatments. Chaudhary et al. found a pronounced effect of Fe2+ for PFAS (i.e., PFOS, PFDA, PFOA, and GenX) adsorption under anaerobic conditions with mineral surfaces of goethite. However, our findings did not provide sufficient evidence to support this observation.

DOC concentration indicated a reducing trend in the control and the two treatments with time. One reason is the microbial consumption of carbon as a food source over time. The other reason is increasing Eh over time, which in turn oxidizes Fe to Fe­(hydro)­oxides, resulting in coprecipitation of DOC along with them. SUVA provides the ratio between aromatic and aliphatic compounds. The control exhibited a sharp decrease in SUVA at +100 mV followed by a significant increase until +500 mV. Similarly, AOC treatment showed a pronounced reduction of SUVA at −100 mV and an increase until +300 mV. Meanwhile, AOC treatment indicated a slight reduction and increment of aromatic carbon fraction at +100 and +300 mV, respectively. Similarly, the results of previous studies revealed the presence of depleted amounts of aromatic carbon fraction in reducing conditions, which were increased under oxidizing conditions. According to Li et al., SUVA has a positive correlation with the binding constant of humic compounds and PFAS, suggesting that high aromaticity enhances their interaction. In our study, the control exhibited an inverse relationship between SUVA and dissolved PFAS concentrations.

The dissolved PO4 3– and SO4 2– in samples taken from five redox windows showed a similar pattern for the control and the two treatments (Figure S3). Supposedly, the reduction of SO4 2– takes place by microbial activities below −50 mV. , None of our microcosms achieved a redox potential less than −150 mV, probably due to the physicochemical characteristics, mainly low organic carbon content of the studied soil. However, slightly increasing SO4 2– concentration with increasing Eh of the control and the two treatments could be explained by the oxidation environment liberating the SO4 2– into the dissolved phase. However, no clear interaction of PO4 3– and SO4 2– for PFAS adsorption was observed in our study. In contrast, the dissolved Cl– concentration in the AOC treatment under all redox conditions used was higher compared to the other two treatments (Figure S3). AOC was loaded with pDADMAC, which contains chloride (Figure S2). Therefore, we assume that the elevated concentration of Cl– in the AOC treatment resulted from the release of Cl– from the AOC particles. The diminished PFAS adsorption performance of AOC may be attributed to the increased solution ionic strength caused by the dissolution of the Cl– ions. Tang et al. demonstrated that increased ionic strength with NaCl lowers the electrostatic interaction between PFOS molecules and the mineral surface of goethite, resulting in decreased PFOS adsorption.

In summary, this study involved assessing the effectiveness of AOC and CAC on immobilization of PFAS in contaminated soil under changing redox potentials under flooded conditions. CAC treatment involved the lowest dissolved PFAS concentration during the whole experiment, indicating its efficiency and effectiveness in PFAS immobilization from the dissolved phase. CAC used in our advanced study under dynamic redox conditions demonstrated competitive effectiveness when compared to other adsorption materials that were previously investigated for PFAS immobilization using batch sorption and leaching studies (Table S4). The higher specific surface area and the greater affinity for attracting the fluorinated carbon chains of PFAS by activated carbon components are likely the reasons for the high PFAS immobilization efficiency resulting from CAC. However, relatively higher percentages of short-chain PFAS remained in the dissolved phase of CAC treatment, revealing its low affinity for immobilization of short-chain PFAS compounds. Nevertheless, the total PFAS concentration of the dissolved phase of AOC treatment in −100 to +500 mV exceeded the total dissolved concentrations in the corresponding Eh of the control.

One could assume that the affinity of AOC to adsorb PFAS may have diminished under the conditions applied in our redox experiment. We need to be aware that these kinds of experiments involving systematic changes of redox potential within a short period can also be considered forced experiments that mimic the fate of substances in natural soils in an accelerated manner. In contrast, the accelerated redox conditions did not result in adverse effects on the PFAS adsorption efficiency of CAC. Following the results from the redox experiment of this study, CAC will therefore retain its initial affinity to adsorb PFAS also under changing redox conditions, which emphasizes its capability to allow for a sustained immobilization of PFAS.

Furthermore, relatively matching PLFA profiles obtained for each sampling point of all treatments reflected negligible ecotoxicity of AOC and CAC amendments on soil microbiota. CAC temporarily showed retarding effects on the microbial growth. However, those effects became less prominent with the ongoing experiment duration and were finally absent at the end of the experiment. Therefore, the results in this study highlight the potential use of colloidal-activated carbon-based CAC for immobilization of dissolved PFAS under a wide range of redox conditions, as well as its promising use in in situ adsorption barriers.

Further study should focus on improving these CACs’ immobilization capacity for short-chain PFAS. Additionally, how different environmental parameters, such as ionic strength and competing contaminants, affect the performance of these adsorptive materials remains to be explored. Moreover, further investigation into the scalability and economic feasibility of using CAC in in situ adsorption barriers is recommended. Furthermore, developing hybrid materials that combine the benefits of activated carbon with other adsorptive materials, such as clay minerals, could help to progress PFAS remediation approaches.

In the context of testing the ecosafety of used adsorption materials, additional studies are needed to comprehensively evaluate their ecotoxicological footprint, particularly regarding microbial diversity and other nontarget soil organisms. Long-term field investigations are also suggested to examine their efficacy under natural soil conditions and interactions with various microbial communities.
