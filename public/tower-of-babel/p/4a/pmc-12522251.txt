Associations between four standard interventions as well as epinephrine use and sustained ROSC, 30-Day and one-year survival of patients in traumatic cardiac arrest: a multicentre, retrospective cohort study
Authors: Romana Erblich, Jens Meier, Tina Tomic Mahecic, Matthias Noitz, Stephan Kalb, Helmut Trimmel, Julian M Baumkirchner, Maximilian Havlicek, Wolfgang Voelckel, Luca Carenzo, Martin W Dünser
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground The evidence to support guideline-recommended interventions in traumatic cardiac arrest (TCA) is low.Methods This multicentre, retrospective study included 996 paediatric and adult patients with out-of-hospital TCA (01/2010-12/2019). Demographic, clinical, and pre-hospital data, binary delivery of five core interventions, and outcomes were collected. Crude (crosstabs) and adjusted (logistic regressions) odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (95%CI) were calculated to determine the associations between single interventions and outcomes.Results A sustained return-of-spontaneous-circulation (ROSC) was achieved in 341 subjects (34.2%). The 30-day and 1-year survival rates were 7.4 and 6.6%, respectively. The 95%CIs of the crude ORs for sustained ROSC were greater than one for all interventions. The 95%CIs of the crude ORs for 30-day and 1-year survival were greater than one for fluid infusion (20.5, 5.0-84.4), pelvic binder application (11.2, 3.3–37.7), as well as airway protection and ventilation with 100% oxygen (3.7, 1.5–9.5), but not for epinephrine use (1.4, 0.8–2.5) or bilateral chest decompression (1.0, 0.5-2.0). The 95%CIs of the adjusted ORs of fluid infusion (7.8, 4.7–13.5), application of a pelvic binder (8.9, 1.3–17.6), airway protection and ventilation with 100% oxygen (4.5, 2.5–8.7), as well as epinephrine use (4.0, 2.6–6.2) were greater than one for sustained ROSC. For 30-day and 1-year survival, the 95%CIs of the adjusted odds ratios of fluid infusion (30-day: 19.9, 4.1–359; 1-year: 16.8, 3.4–305) and pelvic binder application (30-day: 14.4, 2.3–90.8; 1-year: 19.8, 3.1–127), but not airway protection and ventilation with 100% oxygen (30-day: 1.9, 0.7–6.6; 1-year: 1.4, 0.5–5.1), epinephrine use (30-day: 1.0, 0.5–2.1; 1-year: 0.7, 0.3–1.5) or bilateral chest decompression (30-day: 1.1, 0.4–2.8; 1-year: 1.5, 0.6–3.9) were greater than one. The number of interventions correlated with the rates of sustained ROSC (Φ = 0.405; p < 0.001), 30-day (Φ = 0.168; p < 0.001), and 1-year (Φ = 0.169; p < 0.001) survival.Conclusions Our results suggest that fluid infusion, application of a pelvic binder, as well as airway protection and ventilation with 100% oxygen in TCA are associated with higher rates of sustained ROSC, 30-day and 1-year survival. A bundled approach of these interventions might be beneficial. The high 30-day and 1-year survival rates underscore the value and effectiveness of resuscitation efforts in TCA patients.Keywords: Traumatic cardiac arrest, Helicopter emergency medical service, Interventions, Return of spontaneous circulation, Survival

Background

The term traumatic cardiac arrest (TCA) summarizes a spectrum of pathophysiological conditions that are clinically indistinguishable from each other but present with the same phenotype of an unresponsive trauma patient without a palpable central pulse [1]. TCA typically occurs within minutes to a few hours after severe trauma [2], mainly affects young and healthy individuals [3], and is associated with dismal chances of survival. Systematic reviews reported mortality rates of patients with TCA ranging from 92.3% to 100% [1, 4]. Although TCA survivors show better neurological recovery than survivors of non-traumatic cardiac arrest [5], resuscitation of patients in TCA has often been considered futile. However, recent data reporting 30-day survival rates of 10.6% in TCA patients [3], along with a growing recognition that a relevant number of TCA patients may actually be in a low-output state rather than true circulatory arrest [1, 6], have shifted the focus on the importance of identifying and addressing reversible causes of TCA.

Acknowledging that hypovolaemia, hypoxia, and obstructive shock from either tension pneumothorax or pericardial tamponade are the most common reversible causes of TCA [3, 7–9], the European Resuscitation Council (ERC) recommended an algorithm in their 2021 guidelines, which timely and simultaneously addresses these central pathologies in all TCA patients, who do not present with injuries incompatible with life or an absence of signs of life >15 min [10]. The ERC algorithm includes the following four interventions, which are - in addition to chest compressions - recommended in every TCA patient: airway protection and ventilation with 100% oxygen, bilateral chest decompression, application of a pelvic binder, and transfusion of blood or infusion of fluids. Complementing these core interventions, external haemorrhage control should be performed in patients with catastrophic external bleeding. If certain conditions apply, additional invasive procedures such as resuscitative thoracotomy with manual aortic compression or resuscitative balloon occlusion of the aorta are recommended in the 2021 guidelines [10]. Despite being based on sound physiological rationale, the amount and quality of the scientific evidence to support the delivery of these core interventions in TCA is low.

In this study, we evaluated the associations between each of these four core interventions as well as the use of epinephrine and the rates of sustained return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), 30-day and 1-year survival in 996 paediatric and adult patients with TCA, who were managed by the largest physician-staffed helicopter emergency medical service (HEMS) in Austria during ten years.

Methods

This analysis was designed as a multicentre, retrospective, observational cohort study. It included data of patients treated by the teams of thirteen HEMS bases of the OEAMTC Air Rescue service (Christophorus Flugrettungsverein, OEAMTC, Vienna, Austria) during the time period from January 1, 2010 until December 31, 2019. This analysis was part of a larger research project aiming to investigate the characteristics, treatment, as well as short- and long-term outcomes of patients with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest managed by HEMS teams of the OEAMTC Air Rescue service. The research project was evaluated and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Federal State of Lower Austria (GS4-EK-4/703–2020). Due to the retrospective design of the study written informed consent was waived. The results of an analysis focusing on the outcomes of a selected group of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest patients have been published before [11]. The methodology and results of this analysis are presented in line with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [12].

Study setting

The OEAMTC Air Rescue service is a private organization contractually bound to provide nationwide HEMS in Austria. During the observation period, the OEAMTC Air Rescue service operated sixteen HEMS bases throughout the country. Fifteen bases operated from dawn to dusk, while one base was operational for 24 h. Three bases (Vienna, Salzburg, and Nenzing) were run in cooperation with local emergency medical services. Due to data protection regulations, medical records from these bases were not provided for this analysis. The HEMS teams consisted of a prehospital emergency physician (75% anaesthesiologists), a flight paramedic, and a pilot. The decision whether a physician-staffed rapid response car or a HEMS team was dispatched to a patient with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest was made by regional ambulance control centres. It generally depended on availability, accessibility, and the estimated time needed to reach the emergency scene. No protocol or standard operating procedures for managing patients with TCA existed within the HEMS service. During the study period, none of the HEMS units carried blood products but used fluids to restore circulating blood volume in patients with TCA.

Patient population

The medical records of the electronic database of the OEAMTC Air Rescue service were screened for primary missions responding to patients with a confirmed out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. All patients, who sustained a TCA and in whom resuscitation was initiated, were eligible for study inclusion, irrespective of age. TCA was defined as the clinical phenotype of an unresponsive patient without a palpable central pulse supposedly due a traumatic cause. Non-traumatic, external causes of cardiac arrest (e.g., drowning, hypothermia) were not considered as TCA. Similarly, trauma patients, in whom mission protocols implied that cardiac arrest resulted from a medical cause, were not classified has having a TCA. Patients with massive trauma incompatible with survival, no signs of life >15 min, and those in whom no resuscitation efforts were performed for other reasons, were excluded. Patients with TCA due to severe burn injuries were also excluded, as the pathophysiology of cardiac arrest due to burn injuries relevantly differs from that of cardiac arrest due to blunt or penetrating trauma [13].

Data collection

The following data were extracted from the electronic database: age, sex, type of accident, mechanism of injury, injury pattern (as determined by the HEMS physician using clinical acumen with or without point-of-care sonography), HEMS response time (time from dispatch alarm to arrival on scene), bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation, occurrence of cardiac arrest in the presence of an emergency medical service, initial electrocardiographic rhythm, type of airway protection (if any), ventilation with 100% oxygen, defibrillation, type of vascular access, infusion of fluids, administration of epinephrine, application of a pelvic binder, bilateral chest decompression, resuscitative thoracotomy, the immediate outcome of resuscitation efforts (ROSC vs. no sustained ROSC), time the HEMS team spent on scene, and the flight time to the hospital (in patients with a sustained ROSC). Since neither the doses of epinephrine nor the amounts of fluids infused were consistently documented in the database, all interventions delivered during TCA were only collected in a binary fashion (yes/no). The survival status of study patients with Austrian nationality at thirty days and one year after TCA was retrieved from the Austrian registry of deaths. The survival status of study patients of foreign nationalities could not be collected.

Definitions

Aiming to evaluate the outcome effects of interventions, which are delivered to all TCA patients, we defined the following four therapies as core interventions in line with the 2021 ERC guidelines [10]: (1) airway protection (with either a supraglottic airway device or an endotracheal tube) and ventilation with 100% oxygen; (2) infusion of fluids; (3) application of a pelvic binder; (4) bilateral chest decompression. Since epinephrine is commonly administered to TCA patients in Austria, we also included the use of epinephrine into the analysis. We did not include specific interventions to secure the airway or establish vascular access, as these were considered prerequisites for ventilation with 100% oxygen and infusion of fluids. Similarly, we did not consider external haemorrhage control, defibrillation, or resuscitative thoracotomy (an intervention only sporadically performed in the OEAMTC Air Rescue group) as core interventions, as indications to perform any of these interventions depend on the presence of either catastrophic external haemorrhage, a shockable electrocardiographic rhythm, or certain prerequisites (e.g., penetrating chest injury, expertise, equipment, environment, elapsed time). We defined sustained ROSC in line with the Utstein criteria as the restoration of palpable central pulses and an autonomous electrocardiographic rhythm, which lasted for at least 20 min after the cessation of cardiopulmonary resuscitation [14].

Study goals

The primary goal of this study was to determine the associations between each of the four core interventions as well as the use of epinephrine and the rates of sustained ROSC, 30-day, and 1-year survival in patients with TCA. The secondary study goal was to determine the associations between these interventions and sustained ROSC rates in TCA patients categorized by their initial electrocardiographic rhythm (pulseless electrical activity; asystole; shockable rhythm), three age groups (paediatric: <18 years; adults: 18-64.99 years; geriatric: ≥65 years), and injury pattern (multiple trauma; isolated, severe traumatic brain injury; asphyxia including isolated, severe chest trauma, hanging and strangulation).

Data processing

Only relevant sections of the medical records of the electronic database were transmitted by the OEAMTC Air Rescue service, strictly complying with both pseudo-anonymization and data minimization requirements. Following extraction of study variables from the electronic database, quality control checks were performed to identify syntax or entry errors. Wherever possible, these errors were rectified. No data imputation methods were applied in case of missing values.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed with the SPSS software package (SPSS 30.0.0; IBM, Armonk, New York). Descriptive methods were used to report the characteristics of the study population. Continuous variables are presented as median values with interquartile ranges, and categorical data as absolute numbers with percentages. Using crosstabs, crude odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals (95%CI) were calculated to determine the relationships between single interventions and the rates of sustained ROSC, 30-day and 1-year survival. Binary logistic regression models were built to determine adjusted odds ratios (with 95%CIs) of single interventions for sustained ROSC, 30-day, and 1-year survival. Covariates included age, sex, the type of accident, mechanism of injury, injury pattern, HEMS response time, bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation, occurrence of cardiac arrest in the presence of an emergency medical service, and the initial electrocardiographic rhythm. Chi-square tests and Phi-correlation coefficients were calculated to evaluate the relationships between the number of core interventions delivered during TCA (none, one, two, three or more) and the rates of sustained ROSC, 30-day, and 1-year survival. Only study patients with Austrian nationality were included into the analyses evaluating 30-day and 1-year survival. Given that multiple tests were performed, the significance level was adjusted and set at a p-value < 0.004. To evaluate the crude and adjusted effects of single interventions on the rates of sustained ROSC, 30-day, or 1-year survival, we determined the respective odds ratios with 95% CIs. We assumed statistical significance and a clinically relevant effect to be present, if the odds ratios and their 95% CIs were all greater than one.

Results

Of 9,018 patients with confirmed out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, 1,845 patients were classified as suffering from TCA. Nine-hundred-ninety-six of these TCA patients were finally included into the analysis (Fig. 1). Table 1 reports the characteristics of the study population. A sustained ROSC could be achieved in 341 subjects (34.2%). Eight-hundred-sixty-eight of 996 (87.1%) study patients were Austrian nationals. The 30-day and 1-year survival rate could be retrieved from the Austrian death registry in 847 (97.6%) of these patients. Sixty-three (7.4%) and 56 (6.6%) of these study patients were alive at 30 days and one year after the TCA, respectively (Table 2).

Discussion

In this multicentre, retrospective, observational cohort study, we found that fluid infusion, application of a pelvic binder, as well as airway protection and ventilation with 100% oxygen were associated with higher odds of sustained ROSC, 30-day and 1-year survival in 996 paediatric and adult patients with TCA. Significant correlations were observed between the number of interventions and the rates of sustained ROSC, 30-day and 1-year survival from TCA. The number of interventions were also correlated with the rate of sustained ROSC in most subgroups including patients categorized by their initial electrocardiographic rhythm, age, and injury pattern.

Our study population comprised almost 1,000 patients with TCA of all age groups. The majority of the subjects were adult males, who sustained blunt trauma from road traffic, work or household accidents. The very low rate of penetrating trauma mechanisms and the fact that 7.9% of TCAs occurred in alpine terrains are noteworthy differences compared to many previous studies evaluating TCA. The rates of sustained ROSC as well as short- and long-term survival of TCA patients in our study are high compared to other reports [1, 4]. The high ROSC and survival rates were observed across all subgroups in our cohort, remarkably including also TCA patients with asystole as the initial electrocardiographic rhythm and geriatric patients with TCA. These long-term survival rates emphasize the value and importance of resuscitation efforts in patients with TCA [1, 3–5]. It is, however, important to note that trauma patients, who experienced a cardiac arrest due to a presumed medical cause and typically have better chances of survival than patients with TCA [9], were excluded from the study. On the other hand, 437 patients with TCA were excluded as resuscitation was withheld for unknown reasons. This might have introduced a potential preselection bias by the possible exclusion of TCA patients with a low likelihood of survival and could have therefore driven the ROSC and survival rates of our cohort towards more favourable outcomes. In addition, the survival status at 30 days and one year after TCA could only be retrieved from Austrian nationals, who constituted 87.1% of the study cohort. Finally, we only reported binary survival status, as neither the causes of death nor the functional, neurological outcome after TCA were available in the databases accessed.

Since this was an uncontrolled study, our results only describe associations between therapeutic interventions and TCA outcomes instead of cause-and-effect relationships. The results of unadjusted and adjusted models demonstrated that the rates of sustained ROSC, 30-day and 1-year survival in TCA patients were significantly associated with three therapeutic interventions: fluid infusion, airway protection and ventilation with 100% oxygen, and application of a pelvic binder. The significant correlations between the number of interventions and the rates of sustained ROSC, 30-day, and 1-year survival suggest a possible bundle- or dose-response effect. This was most pronounced for the occurrence of sustained ROSC, largely irrespective of the initial electrocardiographic rhythm, age groups, and injury pattern. A resuscitation time bias might explain why survival rates at 30 days and one year after TCA were not higher in all study patients with TCA, who received three or more compared to two interventions. This bias is a unique challenge for observational cardiac arrest research and refers to the observation that an intervention in patients with cardiac arrest is more likely to occur the longer the cardiac arrest continues. Since longer resuscitation times are strongly associated with adverse outcomes, the resuscitation time bias typically shifts the results towards a harmful effect of higher numbers of interventions [15].

Our results support the core interventions in TCA as suggested by the 2021 guidelines for TCA of the European Resuscitation Council [10]. However, we found no significant association between bilateral chest decompression and sustained ROSC or survival. Several reasons may explain this observation. First, the rate of bilateral chest decompression in our study was low (18.8%). It is likely that not all patients, who might have benefited from bilateral chest decompression, had received the intervention. On the other hand, tension pneumothorax is a less common cause of TCA than hypovolaemia and hypoxaemia [3, 7–9], both conditions reversed by fluid infusion, pelvic binder application, airway protection and ventilation with 100% oxygen potentially explaining why significant associations with sustained ROSC and survival were found for these interventions but not for bilateral chest decompression. Based on our results, it appears plausible to prioritize fluid infusion, pelvic binder application, as well as airway protection and ventilation with 100% over bilateral chest decompression in future TCA algorithms in order to maximize the odds of survival in the majority TCA patients.

Only few studies have so far evaluated the effects of therapeutic interventions on TCA outcomes. While a large database analysis of 2,300 patients with TCA from North America reported that no advanced life support procedure was associated with increased odds of survival [16], other studies found that endotracheal intubation and ventilation with 100% oxygen as well as fluid resuscitation during TCA were associated with higher rates of sustained ROSC and survival to hospital admission [17–22]. Interestingly, three studies reported that chest decompression in TCA increased the probability of survival in TCA patients [9, 23, 24].

Apart from the four core interventions in TCA suggested by current guidelines [10], we evaluated the association between the use of epinephrine and TCA outcomes, as 59.4% of study subjects received this drug. While epinephrine use was significantly associated with sustained ROSC, survival rates at 30 days and one year after TCA were not associated with the use of epinephrine in TCA patients. These findings are in line with the results of earlier studies on the use of epinephrine in TCA [25, 26]. In contrast to some studies [27, 28], we did not identify a signal of harm associated with epinephrine in TCA.

Our study has both strengths and weaknesses. A key strength is its large sample size of almost 1,000 patients with confirmed TCA. By including data of the majority of bases (13 out of 16) of Austria’s largest HEMS provider, our study results consistently reflect the care and outcomes of TCA patients managed by HEMS in Austria and may be extrapolated to other Central European environments with similar HEMS systems. On the other hand, important limitations need to be considered when interpreting the findings of our study. First, this was a retrospective cohort study spanning over a period of ten years. According limitations, such as incomplete or missing data, potential data misclassifications, and time-related confounders, may have occurred and decrease the validity of the results. These limitations might especially apply to the subgroup analyses (e.g., subgroup of patients stratified by their initial ECG rhythm, which was unknown in 42.8% of study patients). Second, we extracted data on interventions in TCA patients only in a binary fashion. This is particularly relevant when interpreting the associations between fluid infusion and epinephrine use and patient outcomes, as the dosing of both interventions is likely to have an impact on ROSC and survival rates. Similarly, we could not identify the exact time points when interventions were delivered in relation to TCA. This might be specifically problematic when interpreting the results of pelvic binder use in TCA patients, as the pelvic binder may have been applied in some TCA patients only after ROSC had occurred. This could explain why the highly infrequent application of a pelvic binder (1.3% of all patients) in this study population was significantly associated with all outcome variables in our cohort. Finally, although the analysed sample size was large and the observed survival rates higher than in other cohorts, the low absolute number of survivors resulted in wide 95%CIs and thus a relevant uncertainty regarding the true estimates of our study endpoints.

Conclusions

The results of this multicentre, retrospective, observational cohort study support current guidelines and suggest that fluid infusion, application of a pelvic binder, as well as airway protection and ventilation with 100% oxygen in TCA may increase the likelihood of sustained ROSC, 30-day and 1-year survival. Positive correlations between the number of interventions in TCA and rates of sustained ROSC, 30-day and 1-year survival further indicate that a bundle-approach might be beneficial, irrespective of the initial electrocardiographic rhythm, age, and injury pattern. Thirty-day and 1-year survival rates of 7.4% and 6.6%, respectively, underscore the value and effectiveness of resuscitation efforts in patients with TCA.
