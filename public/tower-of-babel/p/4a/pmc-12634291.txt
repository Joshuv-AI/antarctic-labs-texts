Patient centered comparative evaluation of injectable platelet concentrate with or without micro-needling in the management of thin gingival phenotype: A randomized clinical trial
Authors: Anisha Singh, Chetan Chandra, Vivek Kumar Bains, Anil Kumar Jha, Ruchi Srivastava, Sunakshi Soi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo evaluate the clinical and patient-centered outcomes of Injectable Platelet Concentrate (IPC) and Microneedling (MN) in augmenting thin gingival phenotype (GP).Materials and methodsThe present study was a patient-centered, split-mouth, randomized clinical trial involving 26 participants with thin GP who were randomly treated with IPC + MN (Group 1) and IPC-MN (Group 2). Clinician oriented parameters such as Gingival Thickness (GT), Keratinized Tissue Width (KTW) and Probe Visibility (PV) in addition to Patient Reported Outcomes (PROMs) in form of Translucent Tissue Visibility Score (TTVS) for appearance of underlying hard tissue through thin gingiva were measured at baseline, 1 month, 2 months and 3 months. Overall Plaque Index (PI), Gingival Index (GI), Probing Pocket Depth (PPD), Gingival Bleeding Index (GBI), Specific PI (S-PI), Specific GI (S-GI), and Specific-GBI (S-GBI) were also assessed for additional information.ResultsThe study demonstrated a significant increase in GT for both treatment groups, with Group 1 (IPC + MN) showing greater augmentation compared to Group 2 (IPC-MN) (p < 0.005). Analysis of PROMs revealed that TTVS significantly decreased, with Group 1 (IPC + MN) showing a complete reduction in translucency at 3 months (0 %) compared to 38.5 % in Group 2 (IPC-MN) (p < 0.005). Overall, PI and GI increased slightly over time, but no significant differences were observed between the groups. S-GBI improved with increasing GT, indicating better tissue stability.ConclusionThe combination of IPC and MN significantly enhances GT and reduces gingival translucency, making it a promising approach for managing thin gingival phenotypes. However, the associated decrease in brushing comfort highlights the need for patient-specific considerations when selecting treatment modalities. Future studies should explore long-term outcomes and patient adaptation to increased gingival thickness.Keywords: Gingival phenotype, Platelet concentrate, Microneedling, Keratinized tissue, Gingival thickness

Introduction

Gingiva, the part of the oral mucosa that covers the alveolar processes of the jaw and surrounds the neck of the teeth in a collar-like fashion, plays a vital role in oral health by not only acting as a protective barrier against microbial invasion and mechanical trauma, but also providing signalling to the chemical, mechanical or thermal insult. The term gingival phenotype (GP) encompasses both gingival thickness (GT) and keratinized tissue width (KTW).1 GP is broadly classified as “scalloped and thin” or “flat and thick” gingiva according to Ochsenbien and Ross.2 Siebert and Lindhe3 categorized GB as “thick-flat” and “thin-scalloped”. According to Paolantonio et al.4 Aimetti et al.,5 Kan et al.6 and Egreja et al.,7 a GT greater than 1 mm is classified as thick, while a GT less than 1 mm is considered a thin tissue biotype. Although a 1 mm threshold used to distinguish thin and thick GPs represents a widely accepted reference value in the literature,4, 5, 6, 7 variations can occur depending on the measurement method (e.g., transgingival probing, ultrasonography, or CBCT) and operator calibration.

In the context of periodontal therapy, understanding the patient's GP is crucial for tailoring treatment plans. Several surgical techniques have been proposed to enhance GT and lead to favourable treatment outcomes. These methods include the use of subepithelial connective tissue grafts, acellular dermal matrix, chorion membrane, and guided tissue regeneration (GTR). However, challenges such as donor site morbidity, limited availability, high treatment costs, and longer operating times are some of the drawbacks associated with these techniques. The limitations of traditional surgical methods underscore the need for less-invasive, cost-effective, and efficient treatment modalities for managing thin GPs.

Platelet concentrates (PC) have been used in dentistry for over 30 years as a regenerative tool. These concentrates are capable of releasing supraphysiological levels of growth factors that promote tissue regeneration, sourced from the patient's own body. Liquid injectable platelet-rich fibrin, now better termed as injectable Platelet Concentrate (IPC), was developed by modifying spin centrifugation forces and by utilizing non-glass centrifugation tubes.8,9 The benefits of IPC, such as its high content of white blood cells,10 its ability to provide a slow and sustained release of growth factors, and its capacity to stimulate the expression of transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1), platelet derived growth factor (PDGF), vascular endothelial growth factors (VEGF) and collagen-1 mRNA, have been shown to contribute to cell proliferation, migration, and vascularization needed for tissue regeneration.11 This liquid formulation of platelet concentrate offers successful clinical applicability for the clinicians to readily apply the biomaterial alone or in combination with other biomaterials to promote bone regeneration.12, 13, 14, 15

Advances in regenerative medicine and biomaterials have paved the way for new approaches. For patients with thin GPs, minimally invasive surgical techniques (MIST) like percutaneous collagen induction therapy, also termed microneedling (MN), can help enhance tissue thickness and promote better healing outcomes. MN involves creating microinjuries that cause minimal superficial bleeding and initiate a wound-healing cascade.16,17 During MN, the tissue reacts as if it has been injured, prompting the body to stimulate its own collagen production to maintain tissue integrity due to the release of growth factors.17,18

When MN is combined with PCs, the growth factors are delivered more effectively into the gingival tissues, enhancing their regenerative potential. This combined approach has been shown to result in significant improvements in GT and overall periodontal health, making it a valuable tool in periodontal therapy for patients with thin GPs.17,19 Although both IPC and MN have been comparatively studied for their effects on soft tissue healing, these studies are intended to evaluate the surrogate findings.19,20 Ozsagir et al.,19 Adhikary et al.,21 Soundarajan et al.,22 Mhatre et al.,23 and Chetana et al.,24 evaluated the impact of MN along with IPC in gingival augmentation. However, none of these studies have considered Patient Reported Outcomes (PROMs) in their randomized clinical trial in addition to GT. While addressing this research gap in the literature, this is the first study to report the true endpoints, directly measuring patients’ perception of gingival transparency in showing the underlying hard-tissue contour. Thus, the aim of the present study was to compare the efficacy of IPC with or without MN in managing thin GPs, primarily focusing on changes in GT and true endpoint patient-reported outcomes (PROM) in terms of appearance score. The null hypothesis stated that there was no difference in improvement in GT and PROMs between the two comparators (IPC + MN and IPC alone). Thus, the present study evaluated minimally invasive treatment options that influence both tangible and intangible outcomes. The study also evaluated the correlation between the efficacy of IPC with and without MN in relation to gingival bleeding, as well as the correlation between GT and keratinized tissue width (KTW) with the severity of plaque accumulation.

Materials and methods

The present split-mouth, prospective, single-centered, placebo-effect, double-blinded (patient and observer) simple randomized comparative clinical study was conducted in the Department of Periodontology, between April 2023 and December 2024. The study design and protocol were performed in accordance with ethical principles laid down in the Helsinki Declaration 1998, revised 2013. After approval from the Institutional Research and Development Committee (SDC/IRDC/2022/MDS/14) and the Institutional Human Ethical Committee (SDC&H/IHEC/2022/MDS/14), all the participants were informed about the study, and written informed consent was obtained from the participants in their vernacular language. The study protocol was registered in Clinical Trial Registry India (CTRI) with the registration number CTR347517161. The results of the study were reported in accordance with CONsolidated Standards Of Reporting Trials (CONSORT flow chart, Fig. 1).

Participants

The participants reported to the outpatient department of Periodontology for the treatment of periodontal disease were recruited for the present clinical study based on the following inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Inclusion criteria

Participants from both the genders between 18 and 35 years of age; Systemically healthy participants (ASA-1) who were not on any short- or long-term medications that can interfere the results of present study; Participants who agreed to register in the present randomized clinical study after voluntarily signing the informed consent; Participants having thin GP based on probe visibility test given by Kan et al.25 in mandibular anterior region; Compliant participants with good oral hygiene who attended regular follow up visits; Oral health care providers who were concerned about their thin gingival phenotype and aspired for its correction.

Exclusion criteria

Participants who were former or current smokers and alcohol users (self-reported); Participants with parafunctional habits; Participants who were former or current consumers of tobacco in any form; Participants who were pregnant or lactating; Participants who underwent surgical or non-surgical periodontal treatment before, at operating site within the span of the last 6 months; Patients undergoing active orthodontic therapy.

Preparation of Injectable Platelet Concentrates (IPC)

The standardized protocol for preparation of IPC, as outlined by Miron et al.8 and others,26 was followed for its procurement. A single trained operator procured all the IPC samples for the study. After cleaning the skin with an alcohol swab (ASSURETM Medical Disposables 3 cm × 3 cm, saturated with 70 % Isopropyl Alcohol), 10 ml venous blood was drawn from the antecubital region of the participant using a 10 ml disposable syringe (ROMO JETR 10 ml RomsonsR REF SS-6084) with a 21G needle. The venous blood sample was immediately transferred into two sterile 5 ml polyethene terephthalate (PET) plastic PRF tubes (MaxxliteR 5 ml Plane Plastic PRF Tube with 2 cm diameter cap) for centrifugation, which allows the PRF to remain in liquid form for 15–20 min before clotting.8,27,28 As per the manufacturer specification (OPTIKON, India), tabletop centrifugal machine used for the present study was having highest RPM of 4000 with 5 points settings along with fixed-angle rotor radius of 7.5 cm. As per linear step model, each steps add about 1/5th of the total range. So, approximate RPM at setting 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 were 800, 1600, 2400, 3200 and 4000 respectively. For the present study, i-PRF was prepared at setting 1, giving approximate RPM of 800. The samples thus processed resulted in a Relative Centrifugal Force (RCF) of ≈54 g (as detailed in Table 1).26, 27, 28, 29, 30 After centrifugation, the blood was separated into two parts: liquid plasma on the top and concentrated red blood cells at the bottom. Injectable liquid rich in plasma concentrate was procured using two sterilized disposable insulin syringes of 2 ml (DISPOVAN Super fine 31G, 0.25 mm). A total of 1–1.5 ml of IPC was collected, which was subsequently used for treatment purposes (Fig. 2).

Intervention and comparison

In the present study, thin GPs were treated either with Injectable Platelet Concentrates with microneedling (IPC + MN) or Injectable Platelet Concentrates only (IPC-MN). The keratinized gingiva of each side of mandibular anterior teeth region from central incisors to canine were selected as the Treatment Allocation Sites (TAS1) i.e. from mesial line angle of right mandibular central incisor (41) to the distal line angle of right mandibular canine (43) and (TAS2) i.e. from mesial line angle of left mandibular central incisor (31) to the distal line angle of left mandibular canine (33). TAS1 and TAS2 were randomly allocated to one of the two interventions: Group 1 (IPC + MN) and Group 2 (IPC-MN). This split-mouth design minimized inter-subject variability by using each participant as their own control. Further, this strengthens the reliability of comparisons by reducing confounding variables related to individual patient differences. Proper randomization of treatment allocation to the left or right side was done to avoid bias.

On the appointed day at baseline, the operator received a randomly selected interventional envelope. Selected participants were given a pre-procedural rinse with Chlorhexidine mouthwash 0.2 % w/v (Rexidin Plus ICPA Health Products, Ltd), followed by extraoral disinfection using a cotton swab burnished with 5 % Povidone-Iodine Solution (IP HANDSHIELD P.V. 5 %). The operated sites were then anaesthetized using a topical anaesthetic spray 15 % w/w (Lidocaine Topical aerosol USP Nummit). The IPC collected in an insulin syringe was then injected into the attached gingiva covering the allocated area for TAS1 and TAS2. A minimal 0.5 ml IPC was injected per TAS site until visible clinical endpoints (i.e., gingival blanching and fullness) were achieved, ensuring that the biologic activity of the concentrate reached the required subepithelial plane.19,29,30

On experimental Group 1 (IPC + MN) sites, the microneedling (MN) was performed using a 28-gauge (0.35 mm) x 3 mm lancet needle (Sinodraw Soft) after the IPC treatment as explained above (Fig. 3, Fig. 4).19,21,23,24 The area of keratinized gingiva was measured using a thread, placed vertically and horizontally in millimetres. The needle was inserted vertically into the tissue surface until the hard tissue was felt. Micro-punctures were made on keratinized gingiva extending from the mesial line of the central incisor to the distal line angle of the canine. The decision to use an easily available and cost-effective lancet needle instead of an automated derma MN device was due to the non-availability of intra-oral adaptable micro-needling pens. Carefully calibrated micropunctures were made using cast-based puncture simulation. For each treatment site, an average of 2 cm square was targeted pertaining to TAS, with nine micropunctures administered per centimetre square, maintaining a gap of 30 s between successive micropunctures by the single trained, calibrated operator using an appropriate technique to minimize operator-dependent variability, which was standardized through preliminary pilot trials to achieve consistent capillary bleeding without excessive trauma.31

On experimental Group 1 (IPC + MN) sites, the IPC was injected.

On experimental Group 1 (IPC + MN) sites, the microneedling (MN) was performed using 28-gauge (0.35 mm) x 3 mm lancet needle (Sinodraw Soft) after the IPC treatment as explained above.

On the control Group 2 site (IPC-MN), a lancet needle (Sinodraw Soft) was just touched (placebo-microneedling or placebo-control) without actually piercing the attached gingiva to provide “placebo-effect” to the participant.31 Since both the experimental and control sites (TAS1 and TAS2) were anaesthetized using topical anaesthesia, the participant could not differentiate between the two comparative interventions. This manipulation check was performed during observer calibration priorly to clinical trial. The operated sites were then irrigated with saline solution, followed by placing sterile gauze over them for 5 min. Both interventions, i.e., IPC-MN and IPC + MN, were repeated on the 10th and 20th days following the baseline treatment at the allocated sites.

The participants were advised not to brush at the treated site on the day of treatment and to resume brushing the following day using a non-traumatic roll tooth brushing technique with an ultra-soft bristle toothbrush.

Randomization and allocation concealment

Randomization was applied in this study to eliminate the potential convenience bias of the pre-trained observer. If the observer had known the treatment site with the intervention given in advance, they might have unintentionally influenced the data. After the initial screening of 50 participants, 32 were finally enrolled for this randomized split-mouth placebo-effect control study based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. Enrolled participants fulfilling the inclusion and exclusion criteria were randomized in a 1:1 allocation ratio for two treatment modalities [Group 1 (IPC + MN) versus Group 2 (IPC-MN)] using a simple randomization method to eliminate site selection bias and variability of data. The randomization was performed according to the Sequentially Numbered Opaque Sealed Envelope (SNOSE) method. The investigator prepared 32 identical opaque envelopes, each containing a slip that specified the site and treatment rendered. Sixteen slips were written as TAS-1: IPC + MN and TAS-2: IPC-MN, while the remaining 16 slips were marked as TAS-1: IPC-MN and TAS-2: IPC + MN. Silver foils were also inserted in each of these envelopes to ensure that the researcher could not use the bright light source to see the treatment modality rendered to each patient. These envelopes were first jumbled and then sequentially numbered by a pre-trained and blinded observer (1, 2, 3, and so on until 32). These sequentially numbered envelopes were handed over to the investigator. Once the patient consented to participate, the investigator handed the sealed envelope to the operator, who then administered the required treatment at the specified site as indicated in the allocated slips.

Blinding

To prevent selection bias, the patient and pre-trained observer remained blinded to the allocation array, and hence they did not have access to the output of this allocation sequence before and after the intervention. Access to the output allocation sequence was restricted to the operator who revealed each successive trial participant's allocation only after that participant was accepted into the trial.

Methodology

For the present interventional study, 50 volunteers were initially screened. Based on the above-mentioned inclusion and exclusion criteria, 32 eligible participants initially underwent phase 1 therapy as part of preliminary initial treatment, as outlined in the CONSORT chart (Fig. 1).

Initial therapy

A preliminary phase I periodontal therapy was provided to the participants as per requirement, which included oral hygiene instructions (patient motivation and education), full mouth supragingival and subgingival scaling and root instrumentation with hand instruments (Hu-Friedy, Chicago, IL, USA and 1–14 Gracey Curettes, Hu-Friedy), and ultrasonic scalers under local anaesthesia if needed, in a single appointment (approximately 80–90 min).32 Temporary/permanent restorations as per requirements were done to reduce bacterial load.

Participants who underwent phase 1 therapy were recalled after 2 weeks and then after 4 weeks for evaluation and reinforcement of oral hygiene instructions. Baseline clinical parameters were recorded for all the enrolled participants. Compliant participants with full mouth Plaque Index (PI) score 0 to 1, Gingival Index (GI) score <1, Gingival Bleeding Index (GBI) score of less than 15 %, and GT of less than 1 mm calculated using endodontic spreader No.15 (0.15 mm) with rubber stopper at specific sites after Phase 1 therapy, were randomly assigned for one of the following interventions: Group 1: Injectable Platelet Concentrate with Microneedling, Group 1 (IPC + MN); and Group 2: Injectable Platelet Concentrate only, Group 2 (IPC-MN).

Observer calibration

A comparison standard treatment pilot study was conducted, including five individuals who were not participating in the study. For the calibration, the observer was trained to record periodontal indices along with GT and keratinized tissue width (KTW). This trained, blinded observer measured GT and KTW for the pilot study twice, with a 60-min interval between recordings, while periodontal indices were recorded at 24 h. To ensure inter-rater reliability and for reproducibility of the findings, intra-observer calibration was then calculated using the Intra-Class Correlation Coefficient (ICC) and Cohen's Kappa test. The trained blinded observer recorded the following parameters: Gingival Thickness (GT),33 Keratinized Tissue Width (KTW),33 Plaque Index (PI),34 Specific PI (S-PI),34 Gingival Index (GI),35 Specific GI (S-GI),35 Gingival Bleeding Index (GBI),36 and Specific GBI (S-GBI).36

The parameters recorded in five non-participating patients using the same methodology were reliable, with an Intra-category correlation coefficient (ICC) of >0.98 and similar ICCs≥ 90 % of periodontal indices.37,38 Since the ICC Values were close to 1 (e.g., 0.92, 0.90), they suggest excellent agreement for PI, GI, GBI, S-PI, S-GI, S-GBI, gingival thickness, and keratinized tissue at 24 h. The observer evaluated the differential pressure perception between true and sham MN to validate participant blinding. This was done using post-operative questionnaires to assess the effectiveness of topical anaesthesia at both treatment sites.

Gingival thickness (GT)

The gingival thickness was assessed by drawing an imaginary perpendicular line from the tip of the papilla to the mucogingival junction. The centre of this line was used to measure the thickness. A #15 endodontic spreader (0.15 mm) with a rubber stopper (Mani Flare Finger Spreader 25 mm Stainless Steel Tapered Dental Root Canal Files Size 15) was used to pierce the gingival tissue until the hard tissue was felt.33,39, 40, 41 The penetration depth between the tip of the spreader and the rubber stopper was measured with the help of a Digital Vernier Calliper (Themisto, India) (Fig. 5). GT was measured for each tooth of TAS1 and TAS2 and recorded after taking the average value.

The penetration depth between the tip of spreader and the rubber stopper.

Keratinized tissue width (KTW)

A Histochemical staining method was used to differentiate the mucogingival junction (MGJ). The MGJ was demarcated using Lugol's iodine solution (Fig. 6). A piece of cotton was held using a cotton plier. Lugol's Iodine Solution 2 % (Bio Balance 60 ml, 2.5 mg Iodine per drop) was burnished with a cotton pellet using gentle pressure on the patient's gingiva and alveolar mucosa until the demarcation between alveolar mucosa and mucogingival junction (MGJ) was perceived visually. The graduated periodontal probe (Hu-Friedy PCPUNC156 15 UNC Colour-Coded Probe, #6 Satin Steel Handle, 1–15) was then used to measure the KTW from the free gingival margin to the MGJ at the mid-facial region of TAS1 and TAS2 separately.33

The mucogingival junction was demarcated using Lugol's iodine solution.

Probe visibility test (PV)

The visibility of the probe (“visible” or “not visible”) through gingiva was evaluated using a standard periodontal probe (Hu-Friedy PCPUNC156 15 UNC Colour-Coded Probe, #6 Satin Steel Handle, 1–15) which was inserted 1 mm deep into the gingival sulcus of TAS1 and TAS2.6,42, 43, 44 All assessments were done in natural light without any method of magnification. The visibility was recorded as a dichotomous variable. No dental chair light was used to illuminate the oral cavity to avoid light scattering or interference with the gingival transparency.

Recording of clinician reported outcome and patient related outcome

The Patient Reported Outcome Measures (PROMS) were measured in the form of patient concern regarding the GP's appearance using the Translucent Tissue Visibility Score (TTVS). Patient-reported appearance was recorded using the TTVS score at TAS1 and TAS2, separately. It was measured by asking questions from the patient about the visibility (visible or not visible) of the underlying hard-tissue contour through translucent gingiva, at baseline, 1 month, 2 months and 3 months.

Translucent Tissue Visibility Score (TTVS)

Patient-reported appearance was recorded using the TTVS score at TAS1 and TAS2, separately. It was measured by asking questions from the patient about the visibility of the underlying hard-tissue contour (visible or not visible) through translucent gingiva, at baseline, 1 month, 2 months and 3 months.

Other clinical parameters

Other clinical parameters recorded were Plaque Index (PI),34 Gingival Index (GI),35 and Gingival Bleeding Index (GBI).36 Specific Plaque Index (S-PI) using PI score,34 was used to assess the amount of plaque accumulation on tooth surfaces of TAS1 and TAS2 to evaluate the oral hygiene status for the interventional site specifically. Evaluation of plaque was done on the teeth in TAS1 and TAS2 after drying. The surfaces examined were facial, disto-facial, mesio-facial and lingual using a mouth mirror and a dental explorer.35 Specific Gingival Index (S-GI) was used to assess the severity of inflammation, to evaluate the colour, consistency and bleeding on probing of the gingiva for TAS1 and TAS2. The tissues surrounding each tooth were divided into four gingival scoring units (disto-facial papilla, facial margin, mesio-facial papilla and lingual gingival margin). A blunt instrument such as periodontal pocket probe (Hu-Friedy PCPUNC156 15 UNC Colour-Coded Probe, #6 Satin Steel Handle, 1–15) was used to assess each of the four gingival units according to GI.34 Specific Gingival Bleeding Index (S-GBI) was measured as percentage of bleeding on probing number of sites present or absent by gentle probing of the gingival crevice with a periodontal probe (Hu-Friedy PCPUNC156 15 UNC Colour-Coded Probe, #6 Satin Steel Handle, 1–15), according to GBI.36 A positive score was given if the bleeding appeared within 10 s, which was further expressed as a percentage of the total number of gingival margins examined for TAS1 and TAS2.

Statistical analysis

The data for the present study were entered in Microsoft Excel 2007 and analyzed using the SPSS statistical software 23.0 Version. Descriptive statistics included the mean and the standard deviation. One-way ANOVA was used for inter-group comparisons, and paired t-tests were applied for intra-group comparisons across time intervals. Chi-Square tests were used for ordinal variables. The level of significance for the present study was fixed at 5 % (p < 0.005).

The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to investigate the distribution of the data, and Levene's test evaluated homogeneity of variance. If any assumption was violated, the equivalent non-parametric tests (Wilcoxon signed-rank test or Friedman test) were used. Post-hoc analysis following ANOVA identified specific group differences for GT, KTW, and PI across different time intervals. Pearson's correlation coefficients were also determined between two variables. To enhance interpretability and statistical transparency, 95 % confidence intervals were calculated for key parameters such as GT, KTW and %GT. Given that the analyses were hypothesis-driven and limited in number, no formal correction for multiple comparisons was applied.

Results

Fifty participants were initially screened for eligibility, of whom 32 participants underwent phase 1 therapy as part of preliminary treatment. Twelve participants did not meet the inclusion criteria, while 6 participants who declined to participate were excluded after initial screening. Among 32 enrolled participants, 4 participants did not complete all three sessions of the intervention, while 2 participants were lost to follow-up; hence, the data of 26 participants were finally analyzed for the present study. The participants were evenly distributed between genders (16 females and 10 males), representing 61.5 % females and 38.5 % males of the total population for each group. The mean age of the male participants was 30.10 years, while the mean age of the female participants was slightly lower at 23.13 years. The overall mean age of the participants was 25.8 years (SD 5.23). These values are presented for descriptive purposes only, and no inferential subgroup analysis was performed, as the study was not powered to detect gender-based differences. Throughout the study, no significant changes were observed in mean values of PI, GI and GBI, suggesting that the participants had fair to good oral hygiene (Table 2).

Group 1 (IPC + MN) led to a significant increase in GT from baseline to 3 months with a mean change of 52.74 % (SD = 10.23), with a 95 % confidence interval (CI) from 48.61 % to 56.87 % and p < 0.005. However, the increase in KTW over the 3-month period was statistically non-significant (mean difference = 0.48 mm, 95 % CI -0.21 to +0.30, p = 0.70). Group 2 (IPC-MN) also showed significant increased gingival thickness (GT) from baseline to 3 months, showing a mean change of 41.06 % (SD = 10.98), with a 95 % CI from 36.62 % to 45.50 %, p < 0.005. However, there was a non-significant mean increase in KTW over 3 months in both groups. Intergroup comparison of IPC + MN over IPC-MN showed a significant increase in GT from baseline to 3 months (mean difference = 0.262 mm, 95 % CI 0.126 to 0.398, p < 0.005). Conversely, the intergroup comparison of KTW was not significant (mean difference = 0.009 mm, 95 % CI -0.35 to 0.36, p = 0.96) (Table 3).

Discussion

The present study was conducted to evaluate the efficacy of IPC with and without MN in the gingival augmentation of the mandibular anterior region. The mandibular anterior region was deliberately chosen because it generally exhibits a thinner gingival phenotype compared to the maxillary arch, making it more susceptible to recession and thus an ideal site for evaluating augmentation outcomes. Studies such as Müller and Eger,1 and Kloukos et al.44 have documented significant anatomical variation in gingival thickness between arches, with mandibular incisors having the lowest mean values.

Both GT and KTW were evaluated as indicators of soft-tissue modifications. GT has been considered as an essential factor in determining the quality of life in terms of oral health assessment.1,7 Unlike previous reports19,21, 22, 23, 24 the present study utilized a standardized method for measuring GT by transgingival probing using No. 15 endodontic spreader (0.15 mm) with a rubber stopper, at the centre of an imaginary perpendicular line drawn from the tip of the papilla to the mucogingival junction. Standardised transgingival probing using an endodontic spreader provides a simple, chair-side and reproducible clinical method for assessing gingival thickness, with acceptable correlation to non-invasive ultrasound and CBCT measurements as reported in previous studies.1,39, 40, 41 It remains a cost-effective and readily available option, particularly in settings where advanced imaging facilities may not be accessible. However, in the current clinical scenario, its minimally invasive nature and inherent operator dependent variability are acknowledged limitations when compared with contemoprary non-invasive modalities. Similarly to previous reports,42, 43, 44 the visibility of the probe for evaluating gingival thickness was assessed using a standard periodontal probe (Hu-Friedy PCPUNC156 15 UNC Colour-Coded Probe, 6# Satin Steel Handle, 1–15). This method remains widely accepted, non-invasive clinical screening tool that has been validated in multiples studies for reproducible classification of GP in epidemiological and clinical settings.42, 43, 44 However, in contemporary practice, its operator-dependent subjectivity may limit its precision and broader applicability despite its simplicity and prior validation.45 Consistent with the earlier studies19,21 for the measurement of KTW, the MGJ was demarcated using histochemical staining.

Results of the study showed that there was a significant increase in the GT when treated with IPC with or without MN at baseline, 10th day, and 20th day. Both treatment approaches were effective in enhancing gingival thickness, but microneedling provided a significantly greater improvement, likely due to its stimulatory effect on collagen production and tissue remodelling.46,47 The selected time intervals were chosen based on biological principles of wound healing and tissue regeneration, particularly in the context of platelet concentrate activity and controlled gingival micro-injury, with the aim of enhancing tissue response and optimizing clinical outcomes. This further complies with the slow and sustained release of growth factors from IPC, typically extended over a period of 10 days.9,19,29,30 Further, it was also observed that there was a positive correlation between GT and improvement in PV, TTVS and a negative correlation with S-GBI. Thus, the null hypothesis stating that there was no significant difference between the effectiveness of Group 1 (IPC + MN) and Group 2 (IPC-MN) was rejected.

While a statistically significant increase was observed in GT, an increase in KTW was not significant. These findings suggested that the intervention primarily enhances soft-tissue thickness rather than keratinized tissue width. Limited change in KTW may be attributed to the biologic and anatomic stability of keratinized mucosa as well as short follow-up duration, which may not have allowed complete maturation of keratinized tissue. Thus, findings of the present study corroborate with previous literature,22,48, 49, 50, 51 suggesting GT responds more readily to regenerative and augmentation procedures, whereas KTW may require longer observation periods or alternative techniques for measurable modifications. Further, GT demonstrated a stronger association with periodontal phenotype compared with KTW and papillary height, which may explain its greater responsiveness to therapeutic modalities.22

Similar to Camirand and Doucet,52 the present study performed calibrated manual microneedling using a 0.35 mm (28-gauge) x 3 mm lancet needle. Unlike previous studies,19,21, 22, 23, 24 the present study standardized the area for manual microneedling by preparing a dental cast of the mandibular arch for each participant. The area of keratinized gingiva for MN was then measured using a thread, placed vertically and horizontally over the dental cast for the two treatment allocation sites (TAS).

A 15 % w/w topical anaesthetic spray (ICPA Nummit® Lidocaine Topical Aerosol USP) was administered to the treatment site before the interventions (IPC + MN and IPC-MN). This approach was chosen to prevent inaccuracies in gingival thickness measurements that could arise from the temporary deposition of the anaesthetic solution within the tissue. Additionally, the vasoconstrictive properties of the injectable anaesthetic solution, which could influence the distribution of IPC, have been minimized by using a topical spray.19 However, the potential anti-inflammatory effect of topical anaesthesia may modulate healing in both groups equally and transiently.53

Patient-centered outcome revealing appearance of translucent gingiva showing underlying hard tissue contour using Translucent Tissue Visibility Score (TTVS) decreased significantly in both groups, indicating improved gingival thickness. This TTVS was developed as a novice patient-centered clinical endpoint in the present study, aiming at visual translucency of the gingiva and visibility of the underlying hard tissue contours. As a first-time exploratory tool, TTVS employed in the present study serves as a simple, patient reported parameter designed to capture perception based outcomes in GP assessment. Although its binary structure may appear limited compared with more elaborate esthetic indices or visual analogue scales, it was intentionally selected for its clarity, feasibility and ease of understanding in a routine clinical setting to complement traditional clinician-reported outcomes with a subjectively meaningful patient-centered endpoint. TTVS may be considered a pilot exploratory tool, providing a basis for future refinement and standardization through large scale validation studies for broader clinical adoption.

The results of this study demonstrated a clear inverse relationship between GT and Specific Gingival Bleeding Index (S-GBI) in both treatment groups. The strong inverse relationship between GT and S-GBI emphasizes the clinical importance of soft tissue augmentation in periodontal therapy, not only for esthetic or volumetric gains, but also for improved inflammatory control. However, very high negative strong correlation should be interpreted carefully, as a small sample size in the present study can artificially inflate the correlation coefficient. Nevertheless, the direction of association supports the expected biological relationships. Gingival thickness plays a crucial role in treatment outcomes, as variations in tissue volume can influence blood supply to the underlying bone and its risk for resorption.54,55 Consequently, individuals with a thin GP are more prone to gingival and periodontal diseases.

While this study provides valuable insights into the effectiveness of MN as an adjunct to IPC, certain limitations were encountered. The exclusion of the maxillary gingiva may limit the generalizability of the findings, as GT and phenotype responses to intervention can vary significantly based on location. Another limitation was the use of manual lancet-based puncturing, which may not have been ruled out due to intra-operator-dependent variability. Also, the study compared IPC + MN and IPC alone. Still, it couldn't evaluate the MN-only group, which limits the ability to discern whether the observed results arise from MN alone or a synergistic interaction between MN and IPC. These findings are indicative rather than confirmation of the combined effects of MN and IPC, and henceforth, future studies incorporating an MN-only arm are recommended to delineate the independent and combined roles of these modalities. Another area of concern is that, despite keeping the study blind, the possibility of detection risk bias in the placebo-control group cannot be completely ruled out. Furthermore, the outcomes were evaluated over a shorter time with a limited sample size, which may not be sufficient to assess the long-term stability of KTW, but primarily reflects early soft-tissue healing and adaptation. Thus, current findings must be interpreted as suggestive of short-term outcomes, and extended longitudinal studies with large sample sizes are necessary to confirm the persistence and stability of these changes. The study also did not apply formal correction for multiple comparisons, which may influence the robustness of statistical inference; however, confidence intervals have been presented to aid the interpretation of key parameters. At last, the study relies on clinical measurements without histological confirmation of tissue changes, limiting the ability to analyze cellular-level improvements. Although histological healing occurs in 6 months, tissue maturation with clinical soft tissue healing takes place in 3 months, thus warranting the clinical significance of regenerative or biostimulatory therapy of IPC + MN and IPC-MN.17,46,56

Despite limitations, the present study provided many key observations. The study highlights the significance of regenerative and minimally invasive techniques in enhancing gingival augmentation, offering an alternative approach for managing thin gingival phenotypes. The application of biomaterials like IPC, combined with MN, represents an innovative, cost-effective, and patient-friendly biologically-driven approach to enhance GT, where surgical interventions may not be feasible. The present study also addresses the true endpoints considering PROMS, thus opening a new horizon for improving quality of life through TTVS. Suitable candidates with healthy periodontium and systemic conditions may benefit from the procedure. The present study provided a preliminary, proof-of-concept investigation intended to assess early tissue response and feasibility within ethical and logistical constraints. Future studies should confirm long-term stability and establish appropriate guidelines for case selection and maintenance.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of the present study, it can be concluded that both Injectable Platelet Concentrate with (IPC + MN) or without microneedling (IPC-MN) led to a significant increase in GT from baseline to 3 months. However, there was an insignificant mean increase in Keratinized Tissue Width (KTW) over 3 months. Patient-reported outcomes (PROMs) recorded in terms of Translucent Tissue Visibility (TTVS) were reduced significantly over time in both groups. GT showed a significant negative correlation with site-specific S-PI and S-GBI in both groups.
