Association between environmental endocrine disruptors and autoimmune diseases: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yuzhu Xu, Jie Xu, Shixu Li, Heng Sun, Chengxing Wang, Kai Pan, Jie Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryEnvironmental endocrine disruptors (EDCs) affect the immune system and influence the development of autoimmune diseases (ADs). However, a comprehensive summary of the relationship between EDCs and ADs has not been developed. Consequently, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of previous observational studies examining the association between exposure to EDCs and AD outcomes. We searched relevant literature published from January 2008 to the present, which ultimately included 19 studies. The synthesis of evidence demonstrated a positive association between AD risk and exposure to major EDC classes such as bisphenols (strongest association: OR = 2.38, 95% CI: 1.27–4.45), organochlorine pesticides, phthalates, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons. This trend was not observed for polychlorinated biphenyls. These findings position EDC exposure as a potential risk factor for ADs, yet the mechanistic pathways require elucidation. Future high-quality longitudinal and experimental studies are essential to confirm these relationships and explore the underlying biology.Subject areas: Health Science

Introduction

Autoimmune diseases (ADs) are a class of disorders in which the immune system malfunctions and attacks the body’s tissues. Common examples include systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), rheumatoid arthritis (RA), type 1 diabetes (T1DM), and Sjögren’s syndrome (SJS). The incidence and prevalence of ADs are increasing globally. For example, the incidence of SLE ranges from 1 to 5 per 100,000 people in Europe, 3 to 8 per 100,000 people in East Asia, and 5 to 9 per 100,000 people among Hispanic/Latin American populations.1 Furthermore, females are at a significantly higher risk of developing ADs compared with males2.2 These established epidemiological patterns highlight the importance of non-genetic factors in AD pathogenesis. Previous studies have suggested that 30% of AD risk is attributable to genetic susceptibility, while 70% is associated with environmental factors.3 Environmental elements such as air pollution, ultraviolet radiation, pesticides, and heavy metals, such as mercury,4 and endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) can contribute to disease pathogenesis through mechanisms such as inhibition of DNA methylation. These elements interact with genetic and hormonal factors in a dose-dependent manner to promote disease development.5

Environmental endocrine disruptors (EDCs) are exogenous chemicals that can interfere with the normal endocrine function of organisms. EDCs are hypothesized to contribute to ADs partly through disruption of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis.4,6,7 A previous study demonstrated that exposure to low concentrations of bisphenol A (BPA) could induce autoimmune responses in zebrafish embryos.8 Furthermore, exposure to BPA at thrice the tolerable daily intake (50 mg/kg) accelerates the spontaneous onset of insulitis and diabetes in non-obese diabetic (NOD) mice.9 Exposure to hexachlorocyclohexane and Dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane (DDT) significantly affects T-lymphocyte subpopulations and cytokine expression, which results in reduced levels of interleukin-2 (IL-2) and interferon-gamma (IFNγ).10 Furthermore, exposure to perfluorooctane sulfonate (PFOS) reduces IL-2 production in humans.11 Additionally, subchronic exposure to nonylphenol (NP) at the dose of 40 mg/kg/day for over 180 days can disrupt immune function in rats, which in turn leads to immunotoxicity and inflammatory effects, and alters the activity of transcription factors such as activator protein-1 (AP-1), nuclear factor of activated T cells (NFAT), and nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB), as well as inflammatory cytokine expression. Estrogen receptors play a critical role in this process.12 Thus, EDCs influence the immune system through mechanisms such as oxidative stress, systemic inflammation, and the upregulation of inflammatory factors, which leads to the onset or progression of ADs.

Despite a growing body of research on the mechanisms through which EDCs influence ADs, the findings across studies remain inconsistent. A previous study did not observe any statistically significant difference in urinary BPA levels between children with type 1 diabetes (T1DM) and control groups.13 Another study reported that while prolonged exposure to DDT promoted proteinuria in lupus-prone mice, it did not significantly exacerbate the progression of lupus autoimmunity. Furthermore, TCDD exposure has an inhibitory effect on the development of SLE.14 For further investigations of the association between EDCs and ADs, this study conducted a meta-analysis of published observational studies examining the relationship between EDC exposure and AD risk, to clarify the association between EDCs and ADs.

Results

Literature screening process and results

The initial search identified 2,916 articles. After removing 796 duplicates, 2,916 records were retained. Following title-based screening, 2,817 publications were excluded, and 99 articles underwent full-text review. Ultimately, 18 studies meeting the inclusion criteria were included for data extraction and analysis. (Figure 1).

Flow diagram of study selection

Basic characteristics and quality assessment of included studies

We included a total of 18 studies that examined various types of EDCs and their association with ADs, including RA, SLE, T1DM, and SJS. Quality assessment revealed an overall average score of 7.32 (≥6), which indicated moderate to high study quality. Fourteen studies (73.68%) were classified as high quality, while five (26.32%) were classified as moderate quality. Association between different types of EDCs and risk of ADs

Association between BPs and ADs

Four studies investigated the association between BPA exposure and ADs. Owing to high heterogeneity among these studies (I2 = 53.64% > 50%), a random-effects model was used for data synthesis. BPA exposure was positively associated with ADs (OR = 1.69, 95% CI: 1.09–2.61). However, only the association between BPA and type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) was of statistical significance (OR = 2.38, 95% CI: 1.27–4.45, Figure 2).

The forest plot of BPs and ADs

Association between OCPs and ADs

Six studies examined the relationship between OCP exposure and ADs. Owing to low heterogeneity (I2 = 33.61% < 50%), a fixed-effects model was used for data synthesis. The results indicated that OCP exposure increased the risk of ADs (OR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.06–1.46, Figure 3).

The forest plot of OCPs and ADs

Association between PAHs and ADs

Three studies assessed the relationship between polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) and ADs. Considering that no heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 0.00% < 50%), a fixed-effects model was applied for data synthesis. The results indicated a positive association between PAH exposure and RA (OR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.28–1.50, Figure 4).

The forest plot of PAHs and ADs

Association between PCBs and ADs

Four studies investigated the relationship between PCBs and ADs. Owing to high heterogeneity (I2 = 67.27% > 50%), a random-effects model was used for data synthesis. The findings revealed no substantial association between PCB exposure and the risk of any AD (OR = 1.03, 95% CI: 0.9–1.18, Figure 5).

The forest plot of PCBs and ADs

Association between per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFASs) and ADs

Three studies examined the association between PFAS exposure and ADs. Owing to high heterogeneity across the studies (I2 = 93.05% > 50%), a random-effects model was used for data synthesis. The results indicated an overall positive association between PFAS exposure and ADs (OR = 1.44, 95% CI: 1.01–2.06), with the most notable association observed in patients with SLE (OR = 4.56, 95% CI: 2.81–7.41, Figure 6).

The forest plot of PFAS and ADs

Association between PAEs and ADs

Three studies analyzed the association between phthalate (PAE) exposure and ADs. Owing to low heterogeneity (I2 = 32.47% < 50%), a fixed-effects model was used for data synthesis. The results indicated a positive association between PAEs and RA (OR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.07–1.21, Figure 7).

The forest plot of PAEs and ADs

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

In this study, the results of the meta-analyses for each type of EDC remained consistent when individual studies were sequentially excluded, which indicated stable results. The results of Egger’s and Begg’s tests revealed that only the PFAS group exhibited publication bias (p < 0.05). A funnel plot analysis revealed that the results of the studies examining the relationship between PFAS exposure and ADs were predominantly distributed to the left of the null line or outside the plot, which indicated that most of the studies reported negative findings. This observation indicated no substantial association between PFAS exposure and the risk of ADs. Publication bias was not detected in the other EDC categories (p > 0.05, see Table S2).

Discussion

This meta-analysis employed systematic search and rigorous selection methods to comprehensively analyze recent studies on the relationship between EDCs and ADs. This study is one of the first studies to systematically investigate this association. The study provides new insights and evidence regarding the environmental pathogenic factors of ADs. Our findings indicate a positive association between exposure to EDCs, such as BPs, OCPs, PAHs, PFAS, PAEs, and ADs. However, no significant association was observed for PCBs. These results contribute to the growing body of evidence on environmental risk factors for ADs and provide theoretical guidance for future research investigating the association between EDCs and ADs.

Exposure to BPA is associated with ADs such as SLE and Sjögren’s syndrome, but conflicting data and inconsistent results subvert the elucidation of the underlying mechanism.33 Our findings similarly reflect this inconsistency. BPA was positively associated with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) but not with RA or SLE. In vivo studies indicate that BPA exposure could reduce T cell populations and promote the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interferon-γ, IL-6, and TNF-α in the early stages of disease, thereby exacerbating the progression of type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM).34 Furthermore, it may directly impair pancreatic β cell function and disrupt immune homeostasis, facilitating the development of T1DM.35 At the molecular level, BPA acts as an endocrine disruptor by binding to estrogen receptors in pancreatic β cells, leading to aberrant insulin secretion and apoptosis through mechanisms involving the disruption of calcium signaling and the activation of oxidative stress.36 A substantial body of in vivo and in vitro evidence demonstrates that BPA could alter DNA methylation, histone modifications (e.g., H3K4me3, H3K27me3), and noncoding RNA expression, thereby inducing transgenerational epigenetic effects on key imprinted genes such as insulin-like growth factor-2 (Igf2) and critical metabolic pathways.37 These epigenetic modifications are among the central mechanisms through which BPA contributes to the pathogenesis of immune-mediated and metabolic diseases, including T1DM. These epigenetic modifications are likely among the central mechanisms through which BPA contributes to the pathogenesis of immune-mediated and metabolic diseases, including T1DM.

Notably, the subchronic exposure to both low and high doses of PCBs decreased the incidence of T1DM in mice, indicating a potential negative association.38 However, our results observed no significant association between PCBs and ADs, including T1DM. This discrepancy might have stemmed from interspecies variations, differences in exposure dosage, or critical developmental windows. In contrast, OCPs were positively associated with RA and SLE. Previous studies suggest that OCPs might disrupt T cell homeostasis, reduce levels of Th1 cytokines such as IL-2 and IFN-γ, and elevate autoantibody levels, as observed in patients with pemphigus.39 Furthermore, OCP exposure had been associated with enhanced autoantibody production and accelerated autoimmunity, which was potentially mediated through estrogen receptor interference, oxidative stress, and B cell overactivation.40 Despite limited evidence, these findings underscore the potential pathogenicity of OCPs in specific ADs. Future studies should clarify the molecular mechanisms of OCPs in RA/SLE and validate PCB and AD associations (particularly regarding T1DM) in larger, well-characterized cohorts to elucidate interspecies discordance.

The present study indicates that both PAHs and PAEs could increase the risk of RA. PAHs promote autoimmune responses by inducing sustained oxidative stress and systemic inflammation, activating key pro-inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB and MAPK, and disrupting immune homeostasis.41 Furthermore, different types or exposure doses of PAHs may exert differential effects in patients with RA.42 Among PAEs, dibutyl phthalate (DBP), as a representative compound, primarily triggers immune dysregulation—including Th1/Th2 imbalance, Th17 hyperactivation, and autoantibody production—through oxidative stress, thereby promoting autoimmune pathology.43 In Wu et al.’s study, the autoimmune pathology induced by DBP was modeled as chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis (CLT). This suggested that PAEs as a class could potentially operate through similar pathways (such as oxidative stress-induced Th17 activation) to promote RA pathogenesis.43 However, the precise mechanisms by which PAHs and PAEs contribute to RA pathogenesis are not fully understood. Also, the epidemiological evidence linking these compounds to other ADs remains scarce. Future research should prioritize elucidating the specific pathogenic mechanisms of PAHs and PAEs in RA, while also expanding epidemiological investigations and mixed-exposure assessments to include other autoimmune disorders.

The findings of this study on the association between PFAS and ADs contrast with the positive correlation between PFOS and RA reported by Steenland.44 This discrepancy might have stemmed from the differences in the types of PFAS examined and their diverse immunotoxic mechanisms. Previous studies indicate that PFAS compounds could elicit immunotoxicity through pathways such as PPAR-α activation, NF-κB inhibition, or mitochondrial apoptosis, impacting immune organs, cytokines, and cell survival.45 Variations in immunotoxic potency and mechanisms across PFAS types might explain the heterogeneity between this study, including multiple PFAS, and previous single-compound research. Thus, despite no significant overall association discovered between PFAS and RA in this meta-analysis, their established immunomodulatory effects warrant attention. Future studies combining exposure and mechanistic data are needed to clarify how specific PFAS may influence AD development via distinct pathways.

4-nonylphenol (4-NP), a widespread environmental contaminant, has attracted scholarly attention due to its close association with inflammatory responses.46 In vivo studies have demonstrated that 4-NP exposure can promote pulmonary inflammation and contribute to the mechanisms underlying asthma development.12 However, although no epidemiological studies have directly linked 4-NP to ADs, its potential to disrupt immune regulation is a concern. This is evidenced by its ability to cross the placental barrier and exhibit developmental immunotoxicity. For example, the prenatal exposure to 4-NP (200 mg/kg/day) significantly suppressed immune function in male offspring rats.47 In summary, given its dual role in inducing inflammation and potentially disrupting immune development, 4-NP warrants further investigation as a potential risk factor for ADs. According to the literature and findings from this study, recognizing the potential influence of environmental pollutants on ADs is crucial, particularly regarding the impact of EDCs on immune function and regulation in females.

Strengths and limitations

In conclusion, this study summarized and analyzed the inconsistent results from research conducted over the past 15 years exploring the association between EDCs and ADs. The study provides evidence of the role of environmental risk factors in the development of ADs and offers theoretical guidance for future research exploring the associations between EDCs and ADs. Furthermore, owing to the wide variety of EDCs, our analysis was limited to broad classifications, which hindered a more detailed examination of specific subcategories. At the same time, in view of the fact that most ADs themselves have some specific population distribution characteristics, this study failed to conduct an in-depth analysis of the potential differences in the risk of ADs among individuals of different genders and different age groups. In addition, the selection of EDCs exposure samples and the differences in measurement methods may also introduce bias, so the interpretation of the results should be cautious.

Conclusion

Our meta-analysis revealed a positive association between exposure to environmental endocrine disruptors—such as bisphenols (BPs), OCPs, PAHs, PFASs, and PAEs—and the risk of ADs. However, no association was observed between polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) and ADs. The underlying mechanisms through which EDCs influence the development of ADs remain unclear. This condition highlights the need for further high-quality research and experimental studies to verify these findings.

Method details

Study design

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. While a protocol was not previously registered, the research question, inclusion and exclusion criteria, data extraction items, and statistical analysis plan were pre-defined before initiating the literature search and data collection.

Literature search

We performed comprehensive literature searches in two electronic databases: PubMed, Web of Science and Embase, for relevant studies published between January 1, 2008 and December 31, 2023.The search strategy included the following key terms: “Environmental endocrine disruptors” or “EEDs”, “Bisphenol A” or “BPA”, “per and polyfluoroalkyl substances” or “PFAS”, “Phthalate” or “PAEs”, “Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons” or “PAHs”, combined with “Autoimmune Disorders” or “ADs”, “Systemic Lupus Erythematosus” or “SLE”, “Rheumatoid Arthritis” or “RA”, “Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus” or “T1DM”, “Sjögren’s syndrome” or “SJS”. (Details are in Table S3).

Study selection

The study selection process followed a two-phase approach. Initially, duplicate records were removed manually and using reference management software. Two researchers (Y.Z. Xu and J. Yu) independently screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved records against the pre-defined inclusion criteria. The full texts of potentially eligible studies were then reviewed independently by the same two researchers to determine final inclusion. Any disagreements during the selection process were resolved through discussion, and if consensus could not be reached, a third researcher (J. Xu) made the final decision.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion Criteria: i) The study must have explicitly investigated the association between EDCs and ADs with a specified sample size; ii) Only original research articles published in English were included; iii) Studies must meet the requirements for meta-analysis, specifically providing effect size estimates and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the relationship between EDCs and ADs.

Exclusion Criteria: i) Non-English literature, unpublished studies, dissertations, conference abstracts, commentaries, reviews, monographs, and case reports were excluded; ii) Studies were excluded if key methodological information was incomplete, specifically the lack of essential demographic data or an unclear/unspecified study design; iii) Articles unrelated to ADs or EDCs were excluded.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by two researchers (Y.Z. Xu and J. Yu) using a standardized data extraction form. The following data were extracted from each included study: first author, publication year, study location, type of endocrine disruptor, type of autoimmune disease, sample size, covariates adjusted in the analysis, and effect estimates (odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals). When studies reported multiple adjusted models, we extracted data from the model with the most comprehensive adjustment for potential confounders. For studies examining dose-response relationships using quartiles, we prioritized data comparing the highest versus the lowest exposure categories. Any discrepancies in data extraction were resolved through discussion or by consultation with a third researcher (J. Xu).

Quality assessment

The quality of included studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for observational studies. The NOS evaluates studies based on three domains: selection of study groups, comparability of groups, and ascertainment of exposure or outcome. Studies were scored on a 9-point scale, with scores of 7-9 considered high quality, 5-6 moderate quality, and 0-4 low quality.

Statistical analysis

Data collected from the literature were compiled into a database, and Stata 17.0 software (StataCorp LLC, USA) was used to conduct the meta-analysis. ORs and their 95% CIs were used to evaluate the relationship between EDCs exposure and ADs. Heterogeneity among studies was assessed both qualitatively and quantitatively via the chi-square test and the I2 statistic. For I2 < 50%, which indicated low heterogeneity, a fixed-effects model was used to calculate the combined effect size; otherwise, a random-effects model was applied. Sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the robustness of the combined results. Publication bias was evaluated via Egger’s and Begg’s tests combined with funnel plots.
