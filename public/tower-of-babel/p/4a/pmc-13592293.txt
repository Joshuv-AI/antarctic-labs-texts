Carbon‐Encapsulated Gemini Ionic Liquid as Advanced Bromine Hosts for High‐Performance Zn–Br2 Batteries
Authors: Vishwakarma Ravikumar Ramlal, Elayaperumal Sujithkrishnan, Langyuan Wu, Sakengali Kazhiyev, Subramanian Sowmya, Pradip Mamilwad, Sagiv Weintraub, Amey Nimkar, Zhou Yu, Daniel Sharon, Netanel Shpigel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTZinc–bromine (Zn–Br2) batteries represent a promising aqueous energy storage technology, yet their widespread deployment is hindered by the uncontrolled diffusion of molecular bromine from the cathode side, leading to severe self‐discharge and capacity fading. Here, we demonstrate an effective strategy to mitigate bromine crossover by employing gemini‐type bromine complexing agents (BCAs) impregnated into porous carbon hosts in their oily phase. This approach facilitates the in situ conversion of bromine into a water‐immiscible polybromide BCA phase, thereby confining the active species within the electrode structure. The presence of aromatic linkers in the gemini BCA molecules significantly enhance their retention in the carbon network, most likely through π–π stacking interactions with the carbon framework. Moreover, controlled discharge protocols enabled preferential nucleation of polybromide domains within the confined pore environment, further improving the self‐discharge stability of the system. As a result, the Zn–Br2 cells incorporating gemini‐type BCAs exhibited suppressed bromine crossover, enhanced coulombic efficiency, and superior cycling durability compared to conventional systems. This work provides mechanistic insights and practical guidelines for the rational design of molecular complexants and electrode architectures for next‐generation Zn–Br2 batteries.Keywords: bromine compounds, gemini ionic liquids, self‐discharge, zinc–bromine battery

Introduction

The growing and continuous consumption of fossil fuels has led to significant environmental issues, including pollution and climate change, making the development of renewable energy sources crucial for the future. As a result, the focus has shifted toward energy conversion, storage, and utilization, with an increasing emphasis on renewable energy storage. Among the emerging technologies, zinc–bromine batteries (ZBBs) have gained significant attention in recent years due to their low cost, high safety, impressive energy density, and long cycle life. In fact, compared to emerging battery chemistries such as nonaqueous Na‐ion batteries or rechargeable aqueous Zn batteries employing insertion cathodes (i.e., MnO2 or V2O5), Zn–Br2 systems are significantly more mature. The first commercial‐scale prototype was introduced in the mid‐1970s by Exxon in New Jersey, as part of the company's efforts to reduce its dependence on oil in response to the 1973 and 1979 energy crises. While the emergence of rechargeable lithium‐ion batteries in the early 1990s shifted much of the research and development focus away from zinc–bromine systems, several companies‐such as ZBB Energy Corp. (USA), RedFlow (Australia), and Primus Power (USA) continued to develop and commercialize Zn–Br2 batteries for stationary energy storage applications, targeting markets such as off‐grid systems, telecommunications backup, and renewable energy integration. These systems, however, were based on a flow configuration in which the electrolyte is continuously circulated between external storage tanks and the electrochemical cell, a design that, while enabling modularity and long‐duration operation, is associated with high costs and reduced volumetric and gravimetric energy densities ranging between 60–85 Wh/kg and 15–65 Wh/L [1]. To improve performance and reduce costs, recent efforts have turned to static (non‐flow) Zn–Br2 configurations. However, these face a key challenge: retaining oxidized species (Br2 and polybromides) within the cathode framework. In the absence of continuous electrolyte circulation, bromine tends to diffuse into the bulk electrolyte, leading to rapid self‐discharge (SD) even within an hour, due to crossover to the Zn anode. This limitation underscores the need for effective bromine confinement strategies to preserve capacity and enable practical application of static Zn–Br2 systems. Among the proposed approaches, such as tailoring the pore structure of the cathode [2, 3], development of selective membranes, or designing new cell architectures [4, 5, 6], the use of bromine complexing agents (BCAs) has emerged as one of the most effective strategies [7]. BCAs are by most aqueous soluble quaternary ammonium bromide salts, which transform into immiscible oily phases upon interacting with molecular bromine. This reaction forms stable bromine‐rich complexes that remain sequestered within the cathode region, effectively reducing bromine crossover, volatility, and self‐discharge. Furthermore, as Br2 has relatively low solubility in aqueous solutions (i.e., 0.22 M at 25 °C), its complexation by BCAs to form polybromide complexes allows for greater utilization of bromine while preventing the release of toxic Br2 gas. Given the highly corrosive nature of Br2, chemically stable and corrosion‐resistant host materials are required for its confinement. Among the various proposed hosting structures, including functional frameworks such as MOFs and COFs, as well as two‐dimensional materials [8, 9], carbon‐based electrodes remain the most attractive choice for the cathode due to their excellent chemical stability, high electrical conductivity, and well‐developed porous architecture. To maximize the ability of the BCA to capture oxidized species within the conductive host matrix, it is necessary to effectively encapsulate the BCA molecules inside the positive electrode. To date, this has been done either by vacuum impregnation of an aqueous BCA into the electrode structures [10, 11], or by physically mixing the solid BCA salts with a high amount of carbon to form a hydrogel electrode [12, 13]. Based on the reported data, the first method exhibits faster SD rates, with less than 80% capacity retention within several hours [10]. Over the time, the impregnated BCA gradually diffuses out of the solid matrix, further degrading the cathode's ability to effectively host the oxidized species. In contrast, the solid mixing method demonstrates remarkable SD stability, maintaining performance for over 72 h in some systems [13]. However, despite its superior SD suppression, the active mass fraction in electrodes prepared by this method typically remains below 50%, thereby significantly limiting the practical capacity of the electrode. Consequently, there is a pressing need to develop advanced complexing agents capable of storing a significant amount of bromine, while remaining effectively encapsulated within the electrode over extended resting periods.

In light of the above, we introduce here a new gemini‐type bromine complexing agent (BCA), namely 1,4‐Bis(1‐methyl‐4‐methylimidazolium)benzene dibromide (BMIB) featuring a dual quaternary ammonium centers. This molecule can effectively interact with the generated bromine species, facilitating their conversion into a immiscible phase. Unlike previous cathode fabrication strategies, BMIB was directly embedded into the carbon matrix from its oily phase, resulting in substantially improved cathode performance. As will be demonstrated, the combination of this novel BCA with the optimized fabrication process yields significantly improved Zn–Br2 battery performance, characterized by high energy density, enhanced long‐term stability, and reduced self‐discharge even after 12 h. Combining systematic electrochemical investigations with complementary spectroscopic and microscopic evaluations of both the as‐fabricated and cycled electrodes, alongside theoretical simulations, we provide new insights into the influence of BMIB on the electrochemical behavior of Zn–Br2 systems. These findings reveal how BMIB facilitates more stable bromine complexation within the electrode structure, suppresses parasitic reactions, and contributes to maintain electrode integrity during prolonged cycling.

Results and Discussion

Preparation and Characterization of the Complexant‐Impregnated Electrodes

The preparation route of the gemini [14] type BCA and its incorporation into the porous carbon electrodes are provided in Figure 1. As seen in Figure 1a, the targeted compound, BMIB, was prepared through a two‐step synthetic route: (i) N‐alkylation of imidazole with bromoethane in the presence of KOH in acetonitrile, yielding liquid 1‐ethylimidazole, as confirmed by NMR analysis (see Figures S1–S3). (ii) Nucleophilic substitution of the nitrogen atoms in the previously obtained structure by 1,4‐bis(bromoethyl)benzene. This results in the formation of the final bis‐imidazolium salt, as identified from the 1H and 13C‐NMR spectra presented in Figure 1c. The detailed synthesis procedure is provided in the Supporting Information section. The molecular composition was further confirmed by Electrospray Ionization Mass Spectrometry (ESI‐MS, Figure S4). Fourier‐transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was utilized to identify the functional groups present in the synthesized gemini ionic liquid (Figure 1b). Aromatic C─H stretching vibrations were observed at 3096 cm−1. The imidazole ring vibrations, associated with C═C and C─C stretching, were detected between 1642 and 1560 cm−1. Additionally, aromatic C═C stretching appeared at 1520 cm−1. The C─N stretching vibrations of the imidazolium rings were identified in the range of 1451 cm−1. The imidazolium ring stretching modes are evident from distinct absorptions in the region of 1550–1650 cm−1 (C═N stretching). A prominent band at 1158 cm−1 corresponds to in‐plane bending vibrations, while an out‐of‐plane bending vibration related to the imidazole ring was observed at 721 cm−1 (Table S1). These spectral features are consistent with previously reported data for imidazolium‐based ionic liquids [15, 16].

In search of a chemically resistant and electronically conductive host structure for Br2, we selected high‐surface‐area activated carbon (BP‐2000, Cabot) as the cathode material to eliminate the need for metallic current collectors, which suffer from poor corrosion resistance in bromine‐rich environments. The electrodes were fabricated as free‐standing films by mixing the activated carbon particles with 5 wt% PTFE binder. As shown in Figure S5, this process yielded flexible, binder‐integrated electrodes composed of aggregated carbon particles with primary sizes of around 50 nm, as presented in Figure 1d. Nitrogen adsorption–desorption analysis (Figure S7) was carried out to evaluate the surface area of BP‐2000 powder, free‐standing BP‐2000 electrodes, and BMIB@BP‐2000 electrodes. The BP‐2000 powder exhibited a typical type IV isotherm, characteristic of mesoporous carbon materials [17] and showed a high BET specific surface area of 1310.78 m2 g−1. The free‐standing BP‐2000 electrode displayed a reduced surface area of 350.89 m2 g−1, attributed to loss of accessible porosity due to the mixing with the Teflon binder. To ensure effective confinement of the bromine‐complexing agent (BCA) within the carbon matrix, the gemini ionic liquid BCA (BMIB) was loaded into the porous carbon by impregnation from its bromine‐containing oily phase. Specifically, a 1 M of BMIB was added into an aqueous solution of 2 M ZnBr2, followed by the addition of 1 M HBr and 1 M H2O2. In this reaction, bromide ions are oxidized by H2O2 to form elemental Br2, which immediately interacts with the dissolved BMIB to generate polybromide‐rich, immiscible organic phase, as presented in Figure 1d. Due to the hydrophobic nature of the BMIB‐based phase, soaking the activated carbon in this mixture leads to the selective adsorption of the polybromide complexes onto the carbon surface. Previous studies have demonstrated that hydrophobic ionic liquids exhibit enhanced adsorption onto activated carbon from aqueous solutions [18, 19, 20, 21]. As demonstrated elsewhere [22] that adsorption strongly depends on the ionic liquid hydrophobicity and molecular structure, where ionic liquids containing longer alkyl chains exhibited significantly higher adsorption capacities. This behavior is mainly attributed to dispersive interactions and the lower affinity of hydrophobic species toward the aqueous phase, which promotes their preferential partitioning into the porous activated carbon structure. In contrast, more hydrophilic ionic liquids exhibited substantially lower adsorption due to their stronger interactions with water molecules. The strong tendency of the hydrophobic bromine‐containing species to adsorb onto the porous carbon is clearly visualized by the optical images in Figure S6 and the accompanying video (see Supporting Information), which show the rapid uptake of the oily bromine‐rich phase from the aqueous solution into the carbon electrodes within only a few seconds. Consequently, the resulting composite material is enriched with bromine‐containing species, forming a stable, bromine‐loaded carbon matrix.

The presence of the BMIB phase in the carbon electrodes was confirmed by SEM‐EDS mapping, which showed a uniform distribution of Br and N (Figure 1e,f) across the electrode. As seen in Figure S7, a further decrease in surface area was observed for the complexant‐impregnated electrodes (77.81 m2 g−1), indicating significant pore filling/blockage by the immobilized BMIB. The formation of complex phases, both in the solution phase and within the electrodes, was further confirmed by Raman spectroscopy. As shown in Figure 1g, the spectrum of a pure 1 M ZnBr2 solution displays a single peak at approximately ∼184 cm−1, corresponding to the ZnBr3 − complex [23]. This peak diminished upon the addition of 1 M HBr, due to the reduced Zn2+ concentration. Subsequent addition of 1 M H2O2 generated Br2, which immediately reacted with Br− to form polybromide species specifically Br3 − and Br5 − exhibiting characteristic symmetric vibration peaks at ∼162 and ∼252 cm−1, respectively, along with asymmetric vibration peaks at ∼196 and ∼218 cm−1, in agreement with previously reported Raman spectra assignments for polybromide anions [24, 25]. Further addition of the BMIB phase led to increased intensity in the low‐frequency region (<150 cm−1), attributed to stretching and bending vibrational modes associated with intermolecular cation–anion interactions and hydrogen bonding in imidazolium‐based phases [26]. Finally, the Raman spectra of the BMIB‐encapsulated electrodes exhibited two distinct peaks corresponding to Br3 − and Br5 − polybromides. The sharper and more intense Br3 − peak suggests preferential encapsulation of Br3 − over higher‐order polybromides, likely due to size constraints imposed by the electrode pore structure. Overall, the spectra confirm that polybromide anions are the dominant species within the carbon matrix, rather than solvent molecules, indicating preferential interactions between the hydrophobic BMIB phase and the hosting electrode.

Electrochemical Performance of the BP2000@BMIB Electrodes

The electrochemical behavior of BP2000@BMIB electrodes was investigated in a T‐cell configuration, employing Zn foil as the counter electrode and 2 M ZnBr2 as the electrolyte. As shown in Figure 2a, the complexant‐encapsulated electrodes exhibit markedly different electrochemical performance compared to pristine BP2000 electrodes (without BMIB), highlighting the influence of BMIB encapsulation on electrode behavior. A direct consequence of incorporating Br2 − containing phases within the electrodes is the high initial open‐circuit potential of the BP2000@BMIB system, which reaches about 1.9 V. This potential corresponds to the Br2||Zn cell voltage (see cycle 1 in Figure 2a), whereas non‐impregnated electrodes exhibit only ∼1.1 V. Focusing on the initial cycles, the cell was operated at a current density of 1 mA cm− 2 with a specific capacity of 116 mAh g− 1 (calculated based on the total dry mass of the cathode, corresponding to 25 Ah L− 1 when normalized to the geometric volume of the electrode). Under these conditions, the cell exhibited a coulombic efficiency (C.E.) of 100%, indicating that only a fraction of the encapsulated Br2 was utilized during cycling. In contrast, the pristine electrode exhibited a C.E. of less than 74% (Figure 2d), resulting from poor bromine retention within its pore structure. With continued cycling under these conditions, the BP2000@BMIB electrodes exhibited a progressive decline in the reduction plateau, ultimately converging toward a lower cut‐off potential of ∼0.5 V, as shown in Figure S8. As noted in our previous studies, the continuous shift of the potential toward less positive values is attributed to the rearrangement and diffusion of water‐immiscible, bromine‐containing clusters from the narrow carbon slit pores into the bulk electrolyte solution [10]. During this cycling period, the C.E. remained stable at > 99 % as shown in Figure S9c. In sharp contrast, the electrode lacking the bromine‐complexing agent exhibited lower C.E. values of 95%–96% and, more critically, substantially larger voltage hysteresis, resulting in a reduced E.E. of only 77%–80% (Figure S9a,b). Moreover, spikes occasionally appeared in the charging profiles during random cycles (see, for example, the 50th cycle in Figure 2d). As will be demonstrated later, these spikes are associated with soft short‐circuiting of the cells, caused by the formation of Zn dendrites in the absence of a complexing agent. These results highlight the dual beneficial role of the impregnated BCA: (i) suppressing bromine migration, thereby enhancing the coulombic efficiency of the cathode, and (ii) improving the redox kinetics of the Br2/Br− couple. The latter is plausibly related to the ability of BMIB to interact with the carbon pore walls and therefore retains the generated bromine in close proximity to the electrified interfaces. In the absence of the complexing agent, bromine species are more likely to enter the pores along with solvating molecules, which screen direct bromine–carbon interactions and slow electron transfer.

Starting with an electrode that contains a large reservoir of Br2 naturally produces 100% C.E. when the cell is cycled at limited capacity. Obviously, such a situation prevents meaningful evaluation of the SD suppression capabilities of the BCA‐impregnated electrodes, since the excess bromine supply masks any potential losses to parasitic migration or side reactions. Therefore, to remove the excess Br2, a deep‐discharge step was applied according to the following protocol: First, the impregnated electrode was stabilized for 10 cycles (Figure S10). Subsequently, a galvanostatic discharge was performed at a current density of 0.50 A cm−2, with the lower cut‐off potential set to 0.50 V versus Zn/Zn2+ as the limiting potential.

The effective removal of excess complexing agent was confirmed by Raman analysis of the BMIB‐impregnated electrodes (Figure S11). Prior to the depletion step, strong characteristic bromide/polybromide bands were observed in the 150–300 cm− 1 region. These intense signals completely obscured the characteristic D and G bands of the carbon framework, indicating extensive filling of the porous electrode with the bromine‐containing phase. Following the deep‐discharge step, the bromide‐related bands disappeared entirely, while the carbon D and G bands became clearly visible. This observation confirms the effective depletion of bromine‐containing species from the pore volume and demonstrates that the majority of the BMIB‐rich phase was removed from the electrode. It is worth noting that during the deep discharge step BMIB molecules also diffuse out from the electrode; however, using elemental analysis (Table S2) one can see that a significant fraction of the nitrogen (about 60%), associated with the BCA (later confirmed by XPS), remained inside the electrode after this step.

Following the bromine depletion step, the electrode was subjected to 10 additional cycles within a restricted discharge window, with the lower cut‐off voltage set at 1.40 V. The cycling was performed at a constant current density of 1 mA cm−2 and a capacity of 116 mAh g−1. Under these conditions, a coulombic efficiency of 98.4%, was observed, indicating effective utilization of the available bromine inventory with minimal parasitic losses. Finally, the electrodes were charged to a capacity value of 116 mAh g−1 and then left at open circuit potential (OCP) for resting periods of 1, 5, and 12 h. The capacity retention was calculated as the ratio between the discharge capacity and the capacity of the preceding charge step.

After each rest period, the electrodes were re‐cycled 10 times under identical conditions, followed by an extended OCP hold to evaluate the effect of prolonged rest on SD behavior. The potential‐time profiles recorded during the self‐discharge protocol are shown in Figure S12. The corresponding capacity retention at the end of each self‐discharge sequence is presented in Figure 2c. As seen, full capacity retention was achieved for the BMIB encapsulated electrodes after 1 and 5 h rest periods. An extended period of 12 h at OCP resulted in 77% capacity retention. As seen, particularly for long duration at OCP, achieving maximal capacity retention after the resting period required a higher discharge voltage. This behavior is attributed to the rearrangement of the BMIB‐polybromide clusters within the porous host, followed by the diffusion of the complexed polybromide species out of the electrode structure, as will be discussed later. In sharp contrast, the pristine BP‐2000 electrodes tested under identical conditions exhibited poor capacity retention; even after just 1 h at OCP, only 90% of the initial charging capacity was recovered, followed by a pronounced potential drop to 0.5 V. Further extending the rest period to 5 h and 12 h resulted in capacity retentions of only 55% and 40%, respectively (Figure 2f). The fact that the BMIB‐loaded electrodes retained an improved self‐discharge stability even after deep discharge indicates that the complexant remains confined within the carbon pores. This behavior suggests strong interactions between the BMIB molecules and the carbon walls, likely mediated by π–π stacking, as discussed later. It is worth noting that prolonging the charging duration to accommodate higher Br2 storage was accompanied by reduced reversibility, as reflected by a decline in coulombic efficiency from 95.4% at discharge charging capacity of 133 mAh g−1 to about 93.3% at charging capacity of 197 mAh g−1 (see Figure S13). Considering that the capacities of both treated and untreated electrodes were essentially identical, it can be inferred that the dominant factor governing the storage capacity is the accessible surface area of the electrodes rather than the presence of the BCA alone. As previously demonstrated, impregnating the carbon with large amounts of BMIB leads to its gradual escape from the electrode pores during cycling or resting, likely due to weak confinement forces and partial solubility in the electrolyte. Consequently, only a fraction of the BMIB remains confined within the pores, likely corresponding to molecules that interact with the carbon surface via π–π stacking or other non‐covalent interactions. The extent of this residual fraction is dictated by the accessible surface area of the carbon, which in turn control its effectiveness in stabilizing Br2 through strong surface molecule interactions within the pore network. This concept is schematically shown in the inset in Figure 2c,f. The presence of a complexing agent layer (depicted as purple films) inside the carbon slits stabilizes the generated polybromides, thereby suppressing their migration. In contrast, in the absence of this layer, the polybromides readily diffuse out of the electrode.

The beneficial performance of the BP2000@BMIB electrodes is further highlighted when lower current densities were utilized. Figure 2g presents the C.E. and the E.E values of both modified (red colors) and unmodified cathodes (blue colors) at varied current densities. The obtained GCD profiles are presented in Figure S14. For the BMIB‐loaded electrodes, reducing the current density while maintaining the same capacity as at 1 mA cm−2 markedly improves voltage hysteresis, yielding exceptionally high energy efficiencies of up to 92.2% at 0.25 mA cm−2. This improved performance stems from the modified electrode's ability to effectively confine Br2 within its structure during extended discharge time, thereby sustaining high coulombic efficiency. We propose that the use of slow charging rates can mitigate the kinetic limitations associated with the denucleation of the poorly conductive oily‐phase aggregates, enabling more complete and efficient charge recovery. Conversely, the pristine electrodes at the lower current density exhibited a pronounced decline in both C.E. and E.E. 62% and 60%, respectively, owing to insufficient bromine confinement resulting in accelerated self‐discharge of the cathode. The long‐term performance of the modified carbons is presented in Figure 2h. As shown, at a current density of 1 mA cm−2, no capacity fading was observed over 500 cycles (after which the test was intentionally terminated), while a high average coulombic efficiency of 98.3% was maintained throughout the cycling period.

We assessed the influence of the BCA on Zn plating/stripping reversibility using graphite foil (Grafoil) as the substrate. Unlike metallic Zn, which contains an effectively infinite Zn reservoir, Grafoil enables accurate determination of coulombic efficiency while providing a chemically stable, non‐corrosive surface in the bromine‐rich electrolyte. For this comparison, two cells were assembled with either regular BP2000 or BP2000@ BMIB as the counter electrode, paired with a Grafoil working electrode. The variation of potential with time and the GCD profiles are shown in Figure S15a–d. The CE values for both systems are presented in (Figure S15d–e). As seen, substantially improved stability and CE of the Zn deposition/stripping cycles were obtained in the presence of BP2000@ BMIB cathode. This stable electrochemical response is attributed to BMIB's ability to suppress the shuttling of reactive Br2 and polybromide species toward the Zn anode, while providing favorable Zn morphology for stable cell operation.

In contrast, the system with the BP2000 cathode exhibited a highly fluctuating electroplating response, with sharp current spikes appearing near the end of the reduction step in the GCD profiles. Such events typically occur at the end of the Zn reduction step, when the concentration of bromine species released from the cathode reaches its maximum, intensifying local corrosion and dendritic growth. Consistent with this hypothesis, SEM images of the Zn anodes recovered after cycling (Figure S16) reveal substantial morphological differences between cells employing pristine BP‐2000 cathodes and those containing BMIB‐impregnated cathodes. In the former case, pronounced vertical growth of Zn crystallites is readily observed, indicating highly anisotropic Zn deposition. In contrast, Zn surfaces cycled against BMIB‐impregnated cathodes exhibit a significantly more porous and amorphous‐like morphology, without evident formation of vertically oriented crystalline structures.

Since the growth of vertically oriented Zn deposits is widely recognized as one of the primary causes of internal short‐circuiting in Zn‐based batteries [27, 28, 29, 30], these observations suggest that the presence of BMIB modifies the Zn deposition behavior and mitigates the formation of detrimental Zn structures. This effect may arise from the reduced availability of free bromine species at the Zn interface due to bromine complexation by BMIB, thereby suppressing corrosion‐induced localized Zn growth and improving the overall electrochemical stability of the cell.

In contrast, no detectable morphological changes were observed for either the pristine or the BMIB‐containing carbon cathodes, both of which exhibited similar surface appearances after cycling (Figure S17). This observation suggests that the residual BMIB retained within the electrode is distributed as a thin, highly dispersed layer on the carbon surface, consistent with the Raman, CHNS, and XPS analyses presented herein. Consequently, the retained BMIB does not significantly alter the morphology of the porous carbon framework or produce noticeable charging artifacts during SEM imaging.

This observation underscores the dual role of the BCA, not only in preventing self‐discharge at the cathode by retaining Br2/polybromide species, but also in protecting the Zn anode from capacity loss.

Influence of the Charging Protocol on Self‐Discharge Behavior

At first glance, the decision to set the cut‐off voltage of the discharge step (Br2 reduction) at 1.4 V may appear non‐conventional. While in this limited voltage, the CE remains consistently high at ∼98%–99%, one can observe that the discharge step was not fully completed. On the other hand, the first discharging step after each OCP test is always characterized by relatively low efficiency values, usually ranging between 70%–80% (see Figure S10). This temporary loss in efficiency does not originate from parasitic side reactions, but rather from kinetic constraints associated with the slow reduction of condensed Br2 phases. These species require higher overpotentials for reduction and therefore, remain trapped within the host structure in their oxidized form when the discharging potential is limited. As a result, stable reservoirs of Br2 are retained within the cathode, subsequently contributing to the apparent excess discharge capacity observed after extended rest periods.

In situ optical microscopy studies have previously shown that, in the presence of quaternary ammonium‐based ionic liquids, Br2 forms and continuously grows as oily‐phase nuclei during charging [31, 32, 33]. When the discharge voltage is restricted, the excess Br2 remains confined within the electrode pores, leading to the formation of large, viscous bromine‐rich droplets with limited diffusivity, effectively immobilized within the porous structure. Working in a limited reduction potential ensures a long residence time of Br2 within the cathode, while simultaneously maintaining a relatively high discharge voltage.

To validate this proposed mechanism, we have applied GCD in an extended discharge voltage window (up to 0.5 V) to ensure full extraction of the Br2. As in the previous experiment, a pre‐conditioning deep‐discharge step was applied to eliminate excess Br2 introduced during electrode fabrication. The variations in the voltage–time profiles of BMIB@BP2000 and pristine BP2000 electrodes during the SD experiments under unlimited voltage conditions are shown in Figure 3a,b. As can be clearly observed, the complexant‐containing carbon exhibits well‐defined flat voltage plateaus, whereas the pristine carbon electrodes display sloping voltage profiles that become more pronounced with increasing resting time. Such changes in the voltage profiles, although not directly correlated with the absolute amount of Br2 retained within the electrodes [10], indicate a significant depletion of Br2 from the electrode structure. Indeed, the discharge profiles of the BP2000 electrodes after different SD times presented in Figure 3d show poor capacity retention of 70%, 44%, and 30% after 1, 5, and 12 h, respectively. In contrast, significantly higher capacity retention of 88%, 81%, and 70% was obtained for the BMIB@BP2000 cathodes, as presented in (Figure 3c). Yet, these values are presumably lower than those achieved for the same electrodes cycled within a limited potential range.

A schematic illustration of the Br2 storage mechanism of the BCA‐impregnated electrodes under limited and unlimited potential ranges is provided in (Figure 3e,f). As explained, charging within a limited potential window induces the aggregation of polybromide‐containing, water‐immiscible phases with suppressed diffusion capability. In contrast, applying an unlimited discharge voltage leads to the formation of smaller, more mobile oily phases that readily diffuse out of the carbon structure.

Br2 Storage Mechanism of the BP2000@BMIB Electrodes

CHNS elemental analysis was conducted to assess the elemental composition of the samples. As shown in Table S2, the uncycled BP2000@BMIB electrodes exhibited a high nitrogen content (3.3 wt%), which decreased to 1.79 wt% after the initial deep discharge step. Surprisingly, a similar nitrogen amount was found after 200 cycles (BMIB‐200C). These results indicate that a significant fraction of the complexing agent remains confined within the electrode pores even after prolonged cycling. These findings are further supported by the X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis presented in Figure S18. The Br 3d spectrum of the BMIB‐impregnated electrode prior to cycling exhibits the characteristic Br 3d5/2 and Br 3d3/2 doublet at 68.3 and 69.3 eV, respectively, consistent with bromide/polybromide species [34]. The corresponding N 1s spectrum displays two principal components, denoted N1 and N2. The high‐binding‐energy component centered at ∼402 eV is assigned to the positively charged imidazolium nitrogen atoms of BMIB, whereas the lower‐binding‐energy component at ∼400 eV is attributed to chemically modified or partially decomposed imidazolium‐derived nitrogen species. After cycling, the intensities of both the Br 3d and N 1s signals increased, indicating an enrichment of BMIB‐derived species at the electrode surface [35]. In addition, new Br 3d components emerged at approximately 70.0 and 71.2 eV, which are attributed to covalently bound C–Br species [34, 36]. Concurrently, the N 1s spectrum developed an additional low‐binding‐energy component, suggesting the formation of further reduced imidazolium‐derived nitrogen species during electrochemical cycling. The XPS results, together with the elemental analysis, clearly indicate that BMIB remains associated with the electrode even after prolonged electrochemical cycling.

This observation supports our hypothesis that a portion of BMIB adheres to the pore walls‐ possibly through π–π stacking interactions (as will be discussed later), thereby maintaining its ability to complex the generated Br2 effectively. To elucidate the nature of BMIB interactions, both with polybromide species and with the carbonaceous electrode surface, DFT simulations were carried out. Figure 4a presents the optimized geometry and electrostatic potential (ESP), revealing that the imidazolium rings are strongly electron‐deficient (blue), whereas the two terminal alkyl tails exhibit only weakly electron‐deficient regions (orange to red). As shown, the positive charge is delocalized across the entire BMIB framework, making the cation an effective binding center for negatively charged polybromide anions through electrostatic attraction. The optimized BMIB–polybromide ionic clusters (Figure 4b–d) reveal that this charge delocalization enables multiple stable binding configurations. In these arrangements, the Br3 − anions are typically enclosed between the imidazolium rings, whose positive charges stabilize the polybromide ions via electrostatic interactions. As the size of the polybromide increases from Br3 − to Br5 −, the alkyl‐imidazolium “arms” of the BMIB molecule extend outward, likely due to steric hindrance imposed by the larger anions. In the mixed BMIB2+–Br3 −–Br5 − clusters (Figure 4d), the Br3 − ion remains closely associated with the BMIB core, while the Br5 − ion resides slightly farther away. These configurations are consistent with the Raman spectroscopic results, in which the sharper and more intense Br3 − peak signifies preferential stabilization and encapsulation of Br3 − over higher‐order polybromides. Focusing on the most energetically favorable configuration of the BMIB–2Br3 − cluster (Figure 4b1), we examined its interaction with a graphene surface. The optimized geometry shows that the benzene ring of the BMIB cation lies nearly parallel to the graphene plane, forming an angle of approximately 178.1°, determined by fitting two least‐squares planes and calculating the angle between their normal vectors (Figure 4e). The separation between the benzene ring and the graphene surface is about 3.22 Å, which falls within the characteristic range of π–π stacking interactions. The calculated binding energy of approximately –0.58 eV further indicates that the BMIB–2Br3 − complex adsorbs favorably onto the carbon surface. Charge density difference maps (Figure 4f,g) display alternating zones of charge accumulation (yellow) and depletion (cyan) between the aromatic rings of BMIB2+ and the graphene layer, signifying notable electron redistribution and orbital overlap typical of π–π interactions rather than ionic or covalent bonding. Collectively, the near‐parallel orientation, moderate binding energy, and interfacial charge rearrangement strongly support that BMIB2+ ions engage in π–π stacking with the graphene bilayer, which is consistent with the adsorption behavior observed experimentally on activated carbon.

Design Criteria for Effective Br2 Capturing Cathodes

To establish a more general framework for the development of high‐performance Br2 −capturing cathodes, it is essential to identify the fundamental requirements for both the BCA and the carbonaceous host material. As a first step, we examined the effect of electrode surface area on the electrochemical performance of the system. Following the fabrication procedure described above, we also prepared nonporous electrodes composed of Super P carbon particles. This carbon displays a particle size and overall morphology comparable to BP‐2000 (see SEM images in Figure 5a inset), yet is essentially nonporous, lacking the high internal surface area characteristic of activated carbons. As shown in Figure 5a, applying the same cycling protocol to the Super P electrodes led to a markedly lower coulombic efficiency of about 76% during continuous cycling, accompanied by severe capacity loss after only 1 h of rest in the charged state. These observations clearly emphasize the necessity of highly porous electrodes capable of effectively adsorbing and storing both polybromides and complexing agent molecules. We further explored the influence of molecular structure on the Br2 storage performance of the cathode. As a first step, BP2000 carbon was impregnated with 1‐ethyl‐3‐methylimidazolium bromide (EMIM‐Br). Serving as a structural analogue representing half of the gemini complex, EMIM‐Br provides a useful reference to disentangle the specific contribution of the gemini architecture. Furthermore, to elucidate the role of the benzene linker in the BMIB structure, we synthesized a BMIB analogue, namely, 1,2‐bis(3‐methylimidazolium‐1‐yl)ethane dibromide (BMIE), in which the benzene spacer is replaced by an ethyl group. It is worth mentioning that the similar to the previously studied BMIB, a Br2 − containing oily phase was also observed in BMIE solutions utilized in the electrode impregnation step, while EMIM‐Br remains insoluble in the HBR environment (see Figure S19). As depicted in Figure 5b, employing the “half‐BMIB” structure resulted in deteriorated cycling performance under both limited (brown lines) and unlimited voltage conditions, even worse than that of the Super‐P@BMIB electrodes.

GCD profiles obtained for (a) Super‐P@BMIB electrode. Inset: SEM image of the Super‐P electrode (b) BP2000@ EMIM (c) BP2000@ BMIE (d) BP2000@ BMIE discharge profile after 1h and 5 h, demosntrating the capacity retention.

The BMIE analogue exhibited a performance trend similar to that of the benzene‐based counterpart upon initial cycling (Figure 5c); however, it suffered from poor capacity retention, maintaining only ∼80% after 1 h at OCP and virtually no capacity after 5 h (Figure 5d).

To demonstrate the significance of the proposed fabrication approach, additional control experiments were performed. In the first, BMIB was impregnated into the porous carbon electrode in its water‐soluble form i.e., without the subsequent reaction with HBr and H2O2 that generates the hydrophobic bromine‐rich phase. In the second, the pre‐formed oily phase was added directly to the electrolyte rather than being confined within the electrode.

As shown in Figure S20a,c, electrodes impregnated with the soluble BMIB exhibited lower CE and energy efficiency (EE) compared with those containing the oily phase fractions. Moreover, these cells suffered from significantly faster self‐discharge, retaining less than 92% of their initial capacity after 5 h.

An even more pronounced deterioration was observed when the oily phase was simply dissolved in the electrolyte, with the cells retaining only about 37% of their initial capacity after 5 h. Collectively, these results emphasize that the superior performance of the proposed system arises not only from the presence of the bromine‐complexing agent itself, but also from its confinement within the porous electrode in the form of a hydrophobic bromine‐rich phase, which effectively suppresses active‐species crossover and self‐discharge.

Considering the obtained results, four key factors should be taken into account to achieve optimized Br2 storage performance. First, as noted above, a highly porous carbonaceous electrode is essential to accommodate large quantities of both polybromide species and BCA molecules. Second, the use of gemini‐type BCAs is advantageous, not only because of their two quaternary ammonium binding sites, but also because their large molecular dimensions facilitate size‐constrained confinement within the carbon pores. Third, maintaining the BCA in its hydrophobic bromine‐rich phase is crucial, as this significantly reduces its diffusion from the porous host into the bulk electrolyte. Finally, to further inhibit the escape of the complexing agent from the pore interior, the incorporation of an aromatic benzene ring is highly beneficial, as it can promote π–π interactions with the graphitic carbon framework, thereby enhancing molecular retention within the electrode.

Conclusions

A key challenge toward the practical implementation of static Zn–Br2 batteries is the mitigation of Br2 diffusion from the cathode side. The use of gemini‐type BCAs, impregnated in their oily form, enables effective encapsulation of the BCA within the carbon host. This approach allows efficient capture of bromine through its chemical conversion into a water‐immiscible polybromide–BCA phase, thereby suppressing diffusion into the aqueous electrolyte solution. Furthermore, the presence of benzene linkers was found to play a crucial role in stabilizing the complexant inside the pores, most likely via π–π stacking interactions with the carbon framework. By regulating the discharging voltage, preferential nucleation of polybromides within the confined carbon environment was maintained, further enhancing the self‐discharge stability of the system.

These findings highlight the importance of tailoring both molecular structure (e.g., aromatic linkers, alkyl chain length, symmetry of cationic heads) and host–guest interactions to achieve optimal confinement of the active polybromide phase. Such an approach bridges the gap between fundamental molecular design and practical device engineering, offering a pathway toward scalable, safe, and long‐lasting Zn–Br2 energy storage systems.
