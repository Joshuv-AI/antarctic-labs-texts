Preinfusion risk factors for the development of severe neurotoxicity following CART therapy in pediatric patients
Authors: Caroline Diorio, Regina M Myers, Yimei Li, Hongyan Liu, Samuel Belfer, Amanda DiNofia, Allison Barz Leahy, Haley Newman, Jillian Dolan, Zachary Martinez, Amira Elhachimi, Robert B Lindell, Cassidy Mullen, Alexander Li, John Kim, David T Teachey, Shannon L Maude, Stephan A Grupp, Jennifer L McGuire
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeurotoxicity is a common and potentially life‐threatening complication after chimeric antigen receptor T‐cell therapy (CART) for pediatric B‐cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B‐ALL). The only consistently demonstrated clinical risk factor for severe neurotoxicity in prior studies is high, preinfusion bone marrow disease burden (DB). To better prognosticate this syndrome, we sought to identify preinfusion clinical, laboratory, and imaging risk factors for the development of severe neurotoxicity, stratified by DB. We determined the incidence of severe neurotoxicity (defined as Grade ≥3 neurologic event[s]) in children and young adults treated with investigational anti‐CD19 or anti‐CD22 CART products across eight clinical trials or with commercial tisagenlecleucel. We comprehensively examined the association of putative clinical, laboratory, and imaging factors with the development of severe neurotoxicity. The occurrence of a seizure was included as a secondary outcome. Severe neurotoxicity was observed in 45/442 (10%) patients and was more frequent in the high DB (26/107, 24%) than the low DB (17/312, 5%) cohort. In the high DB cohort, history of a prior transient neurologic insult was associated with severe neurotoxicity (adjusted odds ratio, 5.73; 95% CI, 1.63, 21.7; P = 0.007). In the low DB cohort, no studied risk factors were robustly associated with severe neurotoxicity. Risk factors associated were different in the high and low DB cohorts. Patients with high DB and a previous history of transient neurologic events had a higher risk of severe neurotoxicity. Although the frequency of severe neurotoxicity was much lower in low DB patients, low DB patients accounted for 40% of severe neurotoxicity cases. Future studies should consider patients with high or low DB separately.

INTRODUCTION

Neurotoxicity and cytokine release syndrome (CRS) are the two most common immune‐related adverse events (AEs) associated with chimeric antigen receptor T‐cell therapy (CART) for B‐cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B‐ALL).1, 2, 3 Although multiple targeted therapies are effective for CRS, few therapies for neurotoxicity are effective, and the fundamental pathophysiology of this condition remains obscure. Neurotoxicity occurs on a spectrum of phenotype and severity from mild tremor and speech finding difficulties to severe manifestations such as seizure, coma, and cerebral edema. 2 Effective treatment of neurotoxicity has been limited in part by the heterogeneity of the clinical syndrome and by challenges associated with identifying high‐risk patients.

The only preinfusion, patient‐specific risk factor for neurotoxicity consistently demonstrated across clinical trials is bone marrow (BM) disease burden (DB). Patients with high DB are more likely to develop severe neurotoxicity and severe CRS than patients with low DB. 3 Multiple other putative risk factors for neurotoxicity have been identified through clinical practice including preinfusion neuroimaging abnormalities, history of neurologic insults such as methotrexate toxicity, and signs of preinfusion inflammation.3, 4, 5 However, the association of these factors with severe neurotoxicity has not yet been comprehensively evaluated.

We sought to identify clinical, laboratory, and neuroimaging preinfusion risk factors for severe neurotoxicity in a large cohort of children and young adults (CAYA) with B‐ALL. We hypothesized that previous neurologic toxicities or comorbidities would be associated with an increased risk for severe neurotoxicity. Given the known contribution of BM DB, we a priori stratified analyses by DB. In secondary analyses, we evaluated specific risk factors for seizure and explored preinfusion biomarkers for severe neurotoxicity.

METHODS

Study design and population

We conducted a retrospective review of CAYA who were treated with investigational CD19‐ or CD22‐directed 4‐1BB‐based CART on one of eight clinical trials (CTL019: NCT01626495, NCT02435849, NCT02228096, NCT02906371, and NCT04276870; humanized CART19: NCT02374333 and NCT03792633; CART22‐65s: NCT02650414),6, 7, 8, 9, 10 or with commercial tisagenlecleucel for B‐ALL at Children's Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP) between April 2012 and June 2023, who had full safety data available. Standard lymphodepletion with fludarabine (30 mg/m2/day × 4 days) and cyclophosphamide (500 mg/m2/day × 2 days) lymphodepleting chemotherapy was utilized in almost all patients (425/442, 96%). Split dosing was performed on three trials (NCT01626495, NCT02906371, and NCT02650414). Dosing was not based on preinfusion DB; rather, subsequent doses were held for patients who developed fever or other early signs of toxicity development.

Although there were minor differences in infusion criteria between trials, the following practice was consistent: patients were not infused if they had brain parenchymal disease, uncontrolled central nervous system (CNS) disease, high cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) white blood cells (WBCs), or uncontrolled neurotoxicity. This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of CHOP. Parents or their guardians provided written informed consent for treatment on each respective clinical trial.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the development of severe neurotoxicity, defined as at least one Grade 3 or higher neurologic AE based on the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), or any seizure within 8 weeks following infusion (Supplemental Methods). 2 , 11 CTCAE grading was used over American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy (ASTCT) consensus guidelines, because CTCAE grading was available for all trial participants. Secondary outcomes included the development of any seizure or cerebral edema.

All outcomes for patients treated on clinical trials were collated from prospectively obtained AE reports. For patients treated with commercial tisagenlecleucel, outcomes were abstracted retrospectively using the same methodology as the clinical trials.

Potential risk factors for neurotoxicity

We hypothesized that a history of a previous neurologic insult would predispose to severe neurotoxicity. We retrospectively abstracted the following variables by chart review: reported methotrexate neurotoxicity, seizure, posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES), intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), stroke, and presence of an indwelling medical device (such as Ommaya catheter or shunt). We categorized neurologic insults as “transient” (discrete events that resolved including methotrexate toxicity, seizure, and PRES) or “structural” (chronic static injuries including ICH, stroke, and medical device). For patients treated with prior blinatumomab or other CART products, we abstracted neurotoxicity history.

We a priori stratified the models based on BM DB because of the established association of this clinical factor with severe neurotoxicity. BM DB was defined as High if M3 marrow or ≥25% blasts or Low if M1/M2 marrow or <25% blasts. Other potential clinical risk factors abstracted and a priori included in our models were age and CNS status (CNS1: no evidence of disease; CNS2: <5/μL WBCs and blasts present; and CNS3: >5/μL WBCs and blasts) at infusion.

In addition, we abstracted laboratory data immediately pre‐lymphodepletion and on the day of CART infusion. All laboratory values were treated as continuous variables. For patients who had preinfusion magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain within 6 months of infusion, MRIs were examined and categorized as normal or abnormal by study neurologists (J.L.M., S.B.).

Serum protein assays

Serum proteins (N = 171 unique) were measured on an Olink Signature Q100 machine (Olink, Massachusetts, USA) using two panels (Immune Response, Immuno‐Oncology; Table S1).

Statistical analysis

Standard descriptive statistics were calculated for patient and disease characteristics, prior neurologic history, laboratory values, neuroimaging data, and neurotoxicity incidence. Baseline characteristics were compared by neurotoxicity group (no/mild neurotoxicity vs. severe neurotoxicity) using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test for continuous variables and the chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. Univariable logistic regression models were constructed for each risk factor and each outcome separately.

Multivariable logistic regression models for severe neurotoxicity and seizure were developed, with a priori inclusion of the following clinical variables: age, CNS status at infusion, history of transient neurologic insult, and history of structural neurologic insult. These variables were hypothesized to be associated with an increased risk of severe neurotoxicity based on clinical experience and previously published literature.11, 12, 13, 14 The model for severe neurotoxicity was a priori stratified by DB. The model for seizure was not stratified on DB due to the small sample size; instead, DB was included as a predictor. For severe neurotoxicity, a full multivariable model including DB stratum and its interactions with clinical predictors was also constructed to assess if the effect of each predictor was significantly different for the two DB strata. As a sensitivity analysis, we used the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator LASSO method for selecting variables into the multivariable model of severe neurotoxicity, including all the baseline clinical risk factors for screening. 15 The LASSO method was chosen to accommodate the small number of events and large number of predictors in our study. A 10‐fold cross‐validation procedure was used to optimize the tuning parameter (lambda) to select the optimal model, as implemented in the R package glmnet.

In an exploratory analysis, we expanded the primary models developed above to include additional clinical and laboratory variables. These additional variables were selected if they had a P‐value of <0.1 in a univariate model or a >10% difference between the severe and non‐severe groups in initial descriptive statistics.

In a subpopulation of patients with preinfusion neuroimaging data available, the association between neuroimaging findings and severe neurotoxicity was explored in univariable logistic models.

All statistical analyses were performed in R v4.4.0 and in SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). A two‐sided P‐value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Patients

We identified 442 patients (median age: 12 years, range 0−29) treated on clinical trials (n = 363) or with commercial tisagenlecleucel (n = 79; Table 1) during the study period. Most patients were at second or greater relapse. A higher proportion of patients with severe neurotoxicity had a history of radiation compared to the cohort with no/mild neurotoxicity (Table 1). Only 2/26 patients (4.4%) with Trisomy 21 developed severe neurotoxicity. DB information was available on 43/45 (95.6%) patients who developed severe neurotoxicity.

Incidence of neurotoxicity

Severe neurotoxicity occurred in 45/442 (10.2%, 95% confidence interval [CI] 7.5%–13.3%) patients (Figure 1A). Considering death as a competing event, the cumulative incidence was 10.2% (95% CI 7.6, 13.2). The median onset of severe neurotoxicity was 6 days (interquartile range [IQR] 5, 9). As expected, a higher incidence of severe neurotoxicity was observed in the high DB (26/107, 24.3%) than the low DB (17/312, 5.4%) cohort. Incidence of severe neurotoxicity increased with DB (M1: 15/278 [5.4%], M2: 2/17 [11.7%], and M3: 26/107 [24.2%]). Seizures (any grade) occurred in 21/442 (4.8%), and cerebral edema occurred in only 2 patients (0.4%). One episode of Grade 5 cerebral edema occurred in a patient with congenital heart disease, who was cannulated onto ECMO for severe CRS and subsequently deceased. The other patient had full resolution of symptoms. Full disease staging information was available on 20 patients with seizure; 10 (50%) patients had low DB. Any grade encephalopathy was observed in 144/442 (32.6%) patients and any grade neurotoxicity in 187/442 (42.3%; Figure 1A).

Demographic and clinical risk factors for severe neurotoxicity

We hypothesized that a history of transient or structural neurologic insults would predispose to severe neurotoxicity. Structural neurologic insult was not associated with severe neurotoxicity in either the low or high DB cohort (Figure 2A,B). Transient neurologic insult was associated with severe neurotoxicity in the high DB cohort (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 5.73; 95% CI, 1.63, 21.7; P = 0.007). CNS status was not significantly associated with severe neurotoxicity in either the low or high DB cohort. We evaluated the performance metrics of both models including Area Under the Curve, Sensitivity, and Specificity (Table S2). The high DB model was more sensitive (39%) and specific (94%) than the low DB model. In the sensitivity analysis using the LASSO method for variable selection, results were similar: no risk factor was significantly associated with severe neurotoxicity in the low DB cohort. In the high DB cohort, transient neurologic insult was significantly associated with severe neurotoxicity; Other race (compared to White) was also significant, but this has limited interpretability given the heterogeneity of patients in this race category. In an exploratory secondary analysis, each element of the composite transient neurologic injury variable was individually examined in a univariable model. The history of PRES was associated with an increased risk of severe neurotoxicity (Table S3). The sample size was insufficient to examine each element stratified by DB.

To examine whether inclusion of patients treated with CART22 influenced the results, we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding any patients treated with CART22. The results of the analysis did not change (Table S4). As a further exploratory analysis, in the CART19 patients alone, we examined whether the humanized or murine CART was associated with a higher risk of neurotoxicity. There was no statistically significant association of CART product type in the low DB (aOR, 0.83; 95% CI, 0.31, 2.30) or high DB cohort (aOR, 2.72; 95% CI, 0.85, 10.5).

Clinical laboratory risk factors for severe neurotoxicity

We examined the association of laboratory values immediately pre‐lymphodepletion (WBC count and absolute lymphocyte count [ALC]) or at infusion (complete blood count, lactate dehydrogenase [LDH], C‐reactive protein [CRP], and ferritin) with severe neurotoxicity, stratified by DB (Table S5). In the low DB cohort, WBC at infusion was inversely correlated with risk of severe neurotoxicity (unadjusted odds ratio [OR], 0.24; 95% CI, 0.06, 0.75; P = 0.029; Table S5). In the high DB cohort, no laboratory values predicted severe neurotoxicity. Distribution of laboratory values stratified by DB is presented in Table S6.

Imaging risk factors for severe neurotoxicity

Preinfusion brain MRIs were available for 226/442 patients (Table 2). Patients with MRIs were older than those without MRIs (median 10 vs. 13 years, P = 0.002), likely due to routine screening implemented in 2018 at our institution for all children able to tolerate a limited MRI without sedation. Notably, only 31% of patients with preinfusion MRIs had normal studies. Common findings included volume loss (46%) and probable treatment‐related T2/fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) hyperintensities (39%). The only preinfusion MRI finding significantly associated with severe neurotoxicity was T2/FLAIR hyperintensity in the brainstem (unadjusted OR, 8.54; 95% CI, 1.07, 55.2; P = 0.024). Preinfusion leptomeningeal or parenchymal enhancement was present in 10% more children who developed severe versus no/mild neurotoxicity but did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.169).

Exploratory multimodal models incorporating demographic, clinical, and laboratory findings

Multivariable models stratified by DB were next constructed to include demographic and clinical variables (age, CNS status at infusion, transient or structural neurologic toxicity, history of cranial radiation or TBI) and laboratory findings (infusion platelets and infusion WBC). Neuroimaging findings were not included in multimodal models because data were only available in a subset of patients who differed epidemiologically from the overall cohort. No risk factors were significantly associated with severe neurotoxicity in the low DB cohort (Table S7). A history of a transient neurologic insult was the only factor that remained significant in the high DB cohort.

Previous neurotoxicity is not associated with an increased risk of severe neurotoxicity

Of 144 patients with prior exposure to blinatumomab or alternative CART constructs, 26 (18.1%) experienced neurotoxicity with these previous treatments. Following treatment with the current CART product, 11/144 (7.6%) developed severe neurotoxicity. We did not observe a difference in current neurotoxicity incidence between patients with a previous history of neurotoxicity (2/26, 7.7%) versus without (9/118, 7.6%; unadjusted OR, 1.01; 95% CI, 0.15, 4.24; P = 0.99), although we may have been underpowered to detect a difference.

Risk factors for seizure

Thirty‐nine percent (174/442) of patients were on standing anti‐seizure medications (n = 23) or received prophylactic levetiracetam (n = 151) starting at infusion, at the discretion of the treating physician. Twenty‐one patients developed a post‐infusion seizure; 11/21 (52%) were on anti‐seizure medications. High DB was associated with increased seizure risk (aOR, 4.48; 95% CI, 1.65, 12.5; P = 0.003; Figure 3). Adolescents and young adults >15 years‐old had a lower risk of seizure in the non‐stratified multivariable model (aOR, 0.27; 95% CI, 0.07, 0.96; P = 0.047). A history of transient or structural neurologic insult was not associated with seizure. The model had an area under the curve of 0.72 with a sensitivity of 80% and specificity of 57% (Table S2).

Forest plot of potential preinfusion risk factors for the development of seizure. The risk factors in the models were selected based on clinical relevance and prior literature. CNS, central nervous system; OR, odds ratio.

As an exploratory analysis, we examined individual elements of composite exposures as risk factors for seizure (Table S8) and found that a history of methotrexate toxicity was associated with a higher risk of seizure (OR, 3.89; 95% CI, 1.20, 10.8; P = 0.013). Notably, a previous history of seizure was not associated with an increased risk of post‐infusion seizure (OR, 1.26; 95% CI, 0.35, 3.57; P = 0.686). Patients with a previous history of seizure were more likely to receive anti‐seizure medications at the time of infusion than those without (67/73, 92% vs. 107/369, 29%, P < 0.001).

Preinfusion biomarker discovery

As an exploratory analysis, we sought candidate serum preinfusion biomarkers for severe neurotoxicity, stratified by DB. Comparison between high and low DB patients is shown in Figure S2. PRDX5, an antioxidant peroxidase, was lower in patients with low DB who developed severe neurotoxicity (n = 15) compared to those who did not (n = 34; Figure 4A). Extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) was used to rank the top 20 features important for distinguishing patients with severe neurotoxicity from those with no/mild neurotoxicity (Figure 4B). CXCL12 and CCL23 were the top‐ranked proteins. In the high DB cohort, CD27 was higher in patients who developed severe neurotoxicity (n = 17) than in those who did not (n = 21; Figure 4C). CAIX and LAG3 were the top proteins in that cohort identified by XGBoost (Figure 4D).

DISCUSSION

We present the incidence and risk factors for severe neurotoxicity among 442 CAYA treated with CD19‐ or CD22‐directed, 4‐1BB‐based CART for B‐ALL. This is the largest cohort to date, allowing for a detailed examination of preinfusion clinical, laboratory, and neuroimaging risk factors, stratified by DB. In the low DB cohort, severe neurotoxicity occurred in 5% of patients, and no preinfusion clinical, laboratory, or neuroimaging risk factors robustly predicted the development of severe neurotoxicity. In the high DB cohort, severe neurotoxicity occurred in 24%, and a previous history of a transient neurologic insult was associated with a higher risk of severe neurotoxicity. We also demonstrate that a previous history of neurotoxicity after prior immunotherapies was not associated with a higher risk of severe neurotoxicity with subsequent CART.

Our results confirm the critical association between preinfusion DB and severe neurotoxicity. 11 In the high DB cohort, a history of a transient neurologic injury was associated with a higher risk of severe neurotoxicity. The presence of this association in the high DB cohort exclusively implies a potentially additive mechanism for neurotoxicity, whereby patients who previously experienced a neurologic insult and come into infusion with substantial disease may be predisposed to develop severe neurotoxicity. Additionally, patients with high DB are at high risk for severe CRS, which may further contribute to the pathophysiology of severe neurotoxicity. 16

We also demonstrate that 40% of severe neurotoxicity occurred in patients with low DB. Despite this frequency, we did not identify any preinfusion clinical, laboratory, or neuroimaging factors associated with severe neurotoxicity in the low DB cohort. While a higher WBC at infusion was associated with a lower risk of severe neurotoxicity in initial models, this factor was no longer significant when incorporated into the larger multimodal model. Factors in patients' clinical history like prior neurologic insults, CNS disease, or neuroimaging abnormalities that typically lead to caution in proceeding with CART may not be as clinically important in the low DB setting. These results suggest that for patients with concerning neurologic risk factors, the risk of severe neurotoxicity could be mitigated by effective cytoreduction to a low DB state before infusion.

In exploratory biomarker analysis, the serum proteome was different in the low versus high DB cohorts. However, of the 171 proteins examined, no protein was more highly associated with severe neurotoxicity in patients with low DB. These results imply that the mechanism of neurotoxicity in patients with low DB may be distinct from patients with high DB and that the risk of severe neurotoxicity should be considered differently in these two populations. The identification of proteins such as PRDX5, LAG3, and CAIX that are associated with a lower risk of severe neurotoxicity is an important area for future mechanistic studies. PRDX5 is an antioxidant peroxidase. 17 Lower levels of this peroxidase could predispose patients to a higher risk of toxicities mediated by reactive oxygen species. LAG3 is an inhibitory receptor expressed by exhausted T‐cells. 18 The role of serum LAG3 in B‐ALL patients has not been established. Higher expression of serum LAG3 in high DB patients who did not develop severe neurotoxicity may be indicative of a higher proportion of exhausted T‐cells in these patients. CAIX is a carbonic anhydrase that is produced by some tumors and may be a surrogate marker for DB. 19 Highly multiplexed studies are at risk of Type I errors, as they may fail to pick up low‐abundance proteins.

Interestingly, despite recent reports of an increase in neurotoxicity in CAYA with Trisomy 21 (T21) treated with the CD19/CD3 bispecific antibody blinatumomab, we saw no difference in severe neurotoxicity in patients with T21 (8%) compared to those without (10%, P = 1.0). 20 Notably, the two patients with T21 in our cohort who developed severe neurotoxicity had seizure. The mechanism by which blinatumomab increases seizure risk when CART does not is unclear and is an important area for future study.

We examined multiple laboratory parameters and found that, in contrast to previous reports, none robustly predicted severe neurotoxicity. Previous studies identified Endothelial Activation and Stress Index (EASIX, LDH × creatinine/platelets) scores to be predictive of neurotoxicity and CRS in adult21, 22, 23, 24 and pediatric patients, 25 particularly when combined with CRP (m‐EASIX; LDH × CRP/platelets) and ferritin. 21 , 24 , 26 However, we found no associations between the components of m‐EASIX scores and severe neurotoxicity after stratification by DB. This difference may be in part due to elements of the EASIX score, such as LDH, being associated with DB.

Notably, the presence of an abnormal MRI finding was not associated with an increased risk of severe neurotoxicity. These findings are consistent with emerging multisite data demonstrating a lack of association between preinfusion neuroimaging findings and post‐infusion MRI abnormalities in the setting of neurotoxicity. 27 This suggests that patients with common preinfusion neuroimaging abnormalities may be safely treated with CART. The absence of predictive features on preinfusion MRI does not negate the potential clinical utility of obtaining baseline neuroimaging in patients who subsequently develop severe neurotoxicity.

Our study has several limitations. First, neurotoxicity is a heterogeneous clinical syndrome that may have distinct underlying mechanisms and risk factors. We focused on severe neurotoxicity instead of mild or any neurotoxicity because of the high clinical relevance and the accuracy of ascertainment of this outcome. Current assessment of neurologic AEs on clinical trials based on CTCAE criteria may be insufficiently sensitive and specific to identify subtle neurologic sequelae of CART. Furthermore, in our cohort, retrospective grading of patients treated with commercial tisagenlecleucel may have led to underreporting of mild neurotoxicity, as mild neurotoxicity is more difficult to ascertain retrospectively. The more recently developed ASTCT criteria have similar limitations. Second, our cohort spans more than a decade of treatment in a rapidly evolving field. Clinical practice has changed over that period, most notably with a shift to treating patients with lower DB as access to CART broadened. At our institution, preinfusion neuroimaging in patients began in 2018, so preinfusion neuroimaging is rarely available for patients treated before then. Finally, neurologic exposure data were collected from retrospective chart review and may be particularly vulnerable to recall bias. Since 2018, most of our patients have undergone prospective screening neurologic assessments before infusion, partially mitigating this potential bias. Our report focused on pediatric patients with leukemia. These findings should be generalized to adult populations with caution as adults may have unique risk factors for severe neurotoxicity that are not present in pediatric patients.

Our results have important implications for risk stratification of pediatric patients receiving CART for B‐ALL beyond baseline DB. In patients with high DB, a previous history of a transient neurologic insult further increased the risk of severe neurotoxicity. Caution should be applied in this group of patients with increased consideration for preemptive strategies for treatment of neurotoxicity and pretreatment cytoreduction. In patients with low DB, severe neurotoxicity also occurs, albeit at a lower frequency. Risk factors for severe neurotoxicity in low DB patients remain obscure and are distinct from those in high DB patients.
