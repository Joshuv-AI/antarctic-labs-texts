Clinical outcomes after antipsychotic discontinuation in nursing home residents with dementia: a nationwide cohort study
Authors: Chun-Ting Yang, James M. Wilkins, Aaron S. Kesselheim, Dae Hyun Kim, Kueiyu Joshua Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAntipsychotic medications in people with dementia should be avoided or limited to treatment courses of fewer than 3–4 months. It is unknown whether discontinuation of long-term antipsychotic medications in this population can improve clinical outcomes. This study evaluated clinical outcomes associated with discontinuation of long-term antipsychotic medication use compared with continued use among individuals with dementia residing in nursing homes.MethodsThis cohort study used Medicare claims data (2013–2022) linked to Minimum Data Set clinical assessment data. The study population included nursing home residents aged ≥ 65 with dementia who had used antipsychotic medications for more than 90 days. Individuals who discontinued antipsychotic medications (gap ≥ 60 days) were matched to continuers using incidence density sampling based on time since first prescription of antipsychotic medications. Propensity score matching was applied to adjust for 86 covariates. Study outcomes included all-cause hospitalization, specific rehospitalization reasons, and all-cause mortality. Hazard ratios (HRs) were estimated using Cox proportional hazards model, and rate differences (RDs) per 1,000 person-years were estimated using generalized linear models.ResultsTwo thousand one hundred eleven propensity score-matched pairs were included and followed for a median of 298 days. Antipsychotic medication discontinuation versus continuation was associated with lower risk of all-cause hospitalization (HR [95% CI]: 0.83 [0.72, 0.96]; RD [95% CI]: -55.85 [-97.82, -13.88]) and hospitalization for pneumonia (HR: 0.51 [0.29, 0.90]; RD: -12.85 [-23.26, -2.44]) or urinary tract infections (HR: 0.47 [0.26, 0.83]; RD: -14.22 [-24.62, -3.83]); non-significantly lower risks of hospitalization for falls (HR: 0.88 [0.70, 1.11]; RD: -13.19 [-37.78, 11.41]), delirium (HR: 0.76 [0.57, 1.02]; RD: -19.26 [-39.18, 0.66]), stroke (HR: 0.95 [0.42, 2.16]; RD: -0.31 [-7.06, 6.44]), and all-cause mortality (HR: 0.89 [0.79, 1.02]; RD: -38.23 [-81.01, 4.54]). Subgroup analyses showed the greatest reduction in hospitalization risk was among individuals with moderate-to-severe dementia (HR: 0.78 [95% CI: 0.65, 0.93]; RD: -96.76 [-163.75, -29.77]).ConclusionsAmong nursing home residents with dementia, discontinuing long-term antipsychotic medication use was associated with lower risks of hospitalization, pneumonia, and urinary tract infections, suggesting clinical benefits in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13195-026-02134-0.

People with dementia often experience behavioral and psychological symptoms during the course of their illness. The occurrence and severity of these symptoms, such as agitation, aggression, psychosis, depression, and anxiety, can fluctuate over time and may not always align with the trajectory of cognitive decline. Such symptoms affect 32% to 68% of community-dwelling older adults with dementia [1, 2], with prevalence rising to as high as 92% among nursing home residents [3, 4]. Agitation and aggression are particularly common and are often managed with antipsychotic medications [1, 5]. However, the increased risk of serious adverse drug events, such as sedation, extrapyramidal symptoms, and stroke, may outweigh the modest effectiveness of antipsychotic medication use in this population [6]. Due to these safety concerns, clinical guidelines recommend that antipsychotic medication use in patients with dementia should be avoided [7] or at least restricted to fewer than 3–4 months [8–10].

Given the recommendations of clinical guidelines around antipsychotic medication use in patients with dementia, discontinuation of such drugs may result in important clinical benefits. But the largest randomized controlled trial to date examining antipsychotic medication discontinuation in long-term users with dementia, the Dementia Antipsychotic Withdrawal Trial (DART-AD), found no significant differences in cognitive and functional outcomes, or neuropsychiatric symptoms. Notably, this trial was underpowered, had a high dropout rate (38%), and approximately one-fourth of participants did not initiate the assigned treatment [11]. A systematic review including other smaller randomized trials assessing a similar question found insufficient evidence to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of antipsychotic medication discontinuation in this population due to small sample sizes and outcome heterogeneity [12]. A leading reason for such limited data is that enrolling patients with dementia in randomized trials has practical and ethical challenges, including recruitment barriers, high attrition, and limited generalizability to the more frail populations seen in routine clinical care [13, 14].

To understand the clinical implications of antipsychotic medication discontinuation in patients with dementia, we sought to overcome the inherent challenges of organizing clinical trials in this population by applying a target trial emulation framework [15, 16]. We used routinely collected data from a nationwide cohort of nursing home residents with dementia, a particularly vulnerable population frequently treated with long-term antipsychotic medications [9], to evaluate the patient-level impact of discontinuing versus continuing chronic antipsychotic medication use.

Methods

Database and study population

Medicare fee-for-service Part A (inpatient), B (outpatient), and D (prescription) data and the Minimum Data Set (MDS) were used in this study. Medicare is a federal program that provides health care coverage to individuals aged 65 and older, as well as those with disabilities. The dataset used for this study included all Medicare beneficiaries with dementia and a 16% random sample of those without a dementia diagnosis. The Minimum Data Set is a federally-mandated assessment by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) for residents in CMS-certified nursing homes. It collects standardized clinical assessment data on cognitive function, functional status, behavioral and psychological symptoms, and other clinical indicators from the care of nursing home residents. CMS-certified nursing facilities are required to complete these assessments at least quarterly, at admission and discharge, and following significant changes in clinical status.

We then emulated a hypothetical trial comparing discontinuation versus continuation of antipsychotic medications among patients with dementia living in nursing homes with long-term antipsychotic medication use (Supplementary Table 1). Key components of the trial design (i.e., eligibility, treatment strategies, assignment, follow-up, end points, causal contrast, and statistical analysis) and the approaches used to emulate each are described in Supplementary Table 1. In the emulation using observational data, we identified Medicare beneficiaries with dementia who had continuous antipsychotic medication use for more than 90 days between July 2013 and December 2022. Dementia was determined using a validated algorithm requiring a dementia diagnosis in at least two care settings, which has demonstrated a positive predictive value of 77% [17] and is among the highest reported for claims-based dementia algorithms [18, 19]. Continuous antipsychotic medication use was defined as having no prescription gap longer than 30 days between refills.

Next, we identified individuals who discontinued antipsychotic medications, defined as having a prescription gap of ≥ 60 days following the end of continuous exposure. The end of this 60-day gap was designated as the cohort entry date. This 60-day gap definition has a positive predictive value of 81% for discontinuation of atypical antipsychotic medications and 88% for discontinuation of haloperidol, based on discontinuation recorded in electronic health records [20]. Using an incidence density sampling approach, discontinuers were matched to continuers based on the duration from the first antipsychotic medication prescription to the cohort entry date (< 180, 180–239, 240–299, or ≥ 300 days). For each discontinuer, the matching pool consisted of individuals still using antipsychotic medications at the time the discontinuer stopped use. One continuer was randomly selected from this pool without replacement and assigned the same cohort entry date as the matched discontinuer.

Only long-term nursing home residents were further included in the study. We also excluded individuals with any history of psychiatric disorders, Parkinson’s disease and secondary parkinsonism, or end-of-life conditions (e.g., end-stage liver or renal disease, metastatic solid tumors, or receipt of hospice or palliative care) within the 180 days prior to the cohort entry date. We assumed that patients with dementia without other clinical indications for antipsychotic medication use were prescribed long-term antipsychotic medications primarily for the management of behavioral and psychological symptoms. Additionally, to avoid misclassification of discontinuers and continuers due to lack of medication information, those with hospital or skilled nursing facility stays within 60 days before the cohort entry date were excluded. The cohort selection flow is illustrated in Supplementary Figure 1, and detailed operational definitions are provided in Supplementary Method 1.

Study covariates and outcomes

We adjusted for 86 covariates measured during the 180-day period prior to and including the cohort entry date. These covariates included demographics, dementia-related characteristics, comorbidities, medication history, and use of health care resources. To capture overall comorbidity and frailty burden, we incorporated a validated comorbidity score predictive of mortality [21] and a claims-based frailty index validated against several clinical measures such as gait speed, grip strength, hospitalization, mortality, and dementia severity [22–27]. Moderate-to-severe dementia was defined as claims-based frailty index ≥ 0.28, an indicator that has been validated against Functional Assessment Staging Test stages 5–7 in Medicare beneficiaries with possible or probable dementia [26, 27]. Additionally, we assessed the most recent records from the Minimum Data Set within the 180-day baseline period to capture: (1) agitated behavioral (e.g., rejection of care or physical, verbal, or other behavioral symptoms) and psychotic (e.g., hallucinations or delusions) symptoms; (2) activities of daily living; (3) cognitive functioning levels based on daily activities (e.g., memory and decision-making abilities) measured by the Cognitive Functional Scale [28]; and (4) depression severity, assessed using the Total Severity Mood Score, which is calculated as the sum of individual item scores corresponding to specific symptoms of depression based on the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 [29]. These measures were derived from standardized MDS assessment instruments and reflect observed or reported symptoms and functional status within a 7–14 day lookback period at the time of assessment [30]. The median time (interquartile range [IQR]) from the most recent Minimum Data Set record to the cohort entry date was 51 days [24–80] for continuers and 54 days [25–79] for discontinuers. Detailed operational definitions are provided in Supplementary Method 2.

The primary outcome was all-cause hospitalization. Secondary outcomes included hospitalizations for delirium, pneumonia, urinary tract infections (UTIs), stroke, falls (including emergency department visits), as well as all-cause mortality (see validated outcome definitions in Supplementary Method 3). The study patients were followed from the cohort entry date until the occurrence of the study outcome, antipsychotic medication re-initiation for the discontinuers or discontinuation for the continuers, death (for non-fatal outcomes), disenrollment, maximum follow-up period of 365 days, or the end of the data availability.

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics between the two study groups were compared using standardized mean differences, with an absolute value of < 0.1 indicating adequate balance. 1:1 nearest neighbor PS matching, with a caliper of 1%, was applied to control for confounding. The probability of antipsychotic medication discontinuation versus continuation (i.e., PS) was estimated using a logistic regression model based on 86 baseline covariates (Supplementary Table 2).

The comparative effect of antipsychotic medication discontinuation versus continuation on study outcomes was estimated using the Cox proportional hazards model and presented as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The proportional hazards assumption was checked by the scaled Schoenfeld residuals. Rate differences per 1000 person-years (RDs) of study outcomes between study groups were estimated using a generalized linear regression model with normal distribution and identify link [31].

Several sensitivity analyses were performed. First, we included individual components of the claims-based frailty index in the PS model to enhance confounding control. Second, Fine and Gray model was applied to account for competing risk of death. Third, discontinuing antipsychotic medications in individuals with severe behavioral problems can be challenging [8–10]. To further reduce potential confounding by indication for continued antipsychotic medication use, we excluded individuals with severe behavioral problems, defined as having an Agitated and Reactive Behavior Scale ≥ 3 [32], calculated by summing the frequency scores of four Minimum Data Set items related to behavioral problems (physical aggression, verbal aggression, other behavioral symptoms, and rejection of care). Each item is scored from 0 to 3, resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 12. Fourth, hospitalizations and skilled nursing facility stays are common among older adults. However, individuals with recent hospital or skilled nursing facility stays were excluded from the main analysis, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. We removed this exclusion criterion in the sensitivity analysis, as the average length of these stays is less than 30 days [33, 34], making it unlikely that such admissions would account for the 60-day gap used to define antipsychotic medication discontinuation. Fifth, a 30-day and a 90-day prescription gap to define antipsychotic medication discontinuation, rather than the 60-day gap, was applied in the sensitivity analyses. Lastly, an intention-to-treat analysis without censoring patients who changed the study exposure status was conducted.

Subgroup analyses were performed based on age (< 85 vs. ≥85 years), sex (male vs. female), dementia severity (claims-based frailty index < 0.28 vs. ≥0.28), agitated behavioral symptoms and psychotic symptoms (absent vs. present), and cognitive impairment (intact or mild, moderate, and severe). PS estimation and matching were re-iterated within each subgroup. Heterogeneity between subgroups was assessed using Cochran’s Q-test, with p-values < 0.05 indicating significant heterogeneity.

All analyses were conducted using the Aetion Substantiate® (2025) with R (version 4.4.3) integration [35]. This study was approved by the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board (#2023P001111). Informed consent was deemed exempt as this study involved the use of secondary data, which were de-identified and did not involve direct interaction with participants. Our study followed the STROBE reporting guideline for observational studies.

Results

We identified 2,611 APM discontinuers (mean age [SD]: 84.37 [7.76], 36.1% male) and 2,623 continuers (mean age [SD]: 84.64 [7.56], 34.4% male) after the incidence density sampling (Supplementary Figure 2). Of the study cohort, 3,833 (73.2%) had exposure to antipsychotic medications for ≥ 180 days (Table 1). The comorbidity score (2.64 [2.35] vs. 2.75 [2.37]), claims-based frailty index (0.23 [0.08] vs. 0.23 [0.08]), history of comorbidities and comedication use were similar between discontinuers and continuers. For the clinical assessment data, the median time from the proximal Minimum Data Set assessment to the cohort entry date was 52 days (interquartile range: 26–78) for discontinuers and 50 days (IQR: 24–79) for continuers. Compared with continuers, discontinuers had a higher prevalence of disability in locomotion (66.6% vs. 60.7%), walking in a corridor (60.1% vs. 54.4%), and walking in a room (58.0% vs. 52.7%), and a lower prevalence of agitated behavioral symptoms (26.0% vs. 33.2%) and delusions (7.3% vs. 11.8%). After PS matching, no difference in baseline characteristics were observed based on the absolute standard mean difference values. Baseline characteristics are detailed in Supplementary Tables 2, and PS distributions pre- and post-matching are shown in Supplementary Figure 3.

Discussion

In a nationwide US cohort of nursing home residents with dementia, we found that discontinuing long-term antipsychotic medication use – consistent with clinical guidelines – was associated with lower risks of all-cause hospitalization, as well as hospitalization for pneumonia or urinary tract infection, compared to continued use. This study showed the potential of target trial emulation as a complementary tool that can be used to address inherent limitations in randomized trials and demonstrated some of the meaningful clinical advantages of policies encouraging broader de-prescribing of chronic antipsychotic medications among patients with dementia.

In a prior systematic review of randomized trials evaluating clinical outcomes following the discontinuation versus continuation of long-term antipsychotic medication use in patients with dementia, 10 trials were identified covering a total of 632 participants [12]. The review concluded that discontinuation may not impact overall neuropsychiatric symptoms, as measured by various clinical scales, but the quality of the evidence leading to this conclusion was low due to the randomized trials have a substantial risk of bias, small sample sizes, and limited data on adverse events and mortality. By leveraging a population-based database, our study included an around 25-times larger sample size (4,222 matched individuals versus 165 participants in the largest randomized trial [DART-AD]), allowing for the evaluation of clinically meaningful outcomes associated with antipsychotic medication discontinuation versus continuation and helping to address gaps in existing evidence. Lower rates of falls, delirium, pneumonia, and urinary tract infections, which are common adverse events associated with antipsychotic medication use [36–40], may help explain the reduced risk of all-cause hospitalization observed among antipsychotic medication discontinuers in our study. A similar pattern was reported in a previous study of post-discharge older adults, in which antipsychotic medication discontinuation was associated with a lower risk of rehospitalization and antipsychotic medication-associated adverse effects, compared with continuation [41].

We observed various clinical benefits of antipsychotic medication discontinuation, some of which were especially pronounced among the moderate-to-severe dementia subgroup: in particular, a 22% reduction in hospitalization risk, amounting to a staggering 97 hospitalizations per 1,000 person years. Individuals with advanced dementia, who often have a higher baseline risk of adverse outcomes and greater susceptibility to adverse drug events, may therefore derive important benefits from discontinuing antipsychotic medications.

By contrast, reduced risk of hospitalizations was not observed in the subgroup with agitated behavioral and psychotic symptoms. Previous studies have reported that individuals with more severe neuropsychiatric symptoms may experience behavioral benefits from continued antipsychotic medication use [11, 12, 42]. Ballard et al. reported that individuals with high baseline Neuropsychiatric Inventory scores (> 14) were more likely to develop pronounced behavioral problems following antipsychotic medication discontinuation [42]. Similarly, in the DART-AD trial, participants with high baseline Neuropsychiatric Inventory scores showed a mean Neuropsychiatric Inventory change of -16.9 points favoring antipsychotic medication continuation compared to discontinuation, although the interaction between baseline symptom severity (i.e., Neuropsychiatric Inventory ≤ 14 or > 14) and treatment group was not statistically significant [11]. These subgroup results therefore raise questions about the relative benefits and risks of discontinuing antipsychotic medications among individuals with agitated behavioral and psychotic symptoms. However, these findings based on the subgroup analyses were under-powered and should be viewed as exploratory. Further studies are warranted to corroborate these observations.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although this study emulated a hypothetical trial, it does not overcome limitations of data sources and lack of randomization. Despite that we incorporated clinical assessment variables from the Minimum Data Set (including agitated behavioral and psychotic symptoms, functional status, cognitive impairment, and depression severity), the severity of neuropsychiatric symptoms may not have been fully captured. The intention and reasons for antipsychotic medication discontinuation could not be measured from claims data. The lack of adjustment for these characteristics may lead to potential residual confounding. Second, although we made efforts to use MDS-derived measures in the analysis to adjust for residual confounding and to identify clinically meaningful patient subgroups, a key limitation is that these measures reflect symptom presence and functional status within a 7–14 day lookback period at the time of assessment. Therefore, these measures could only provide a time-limited snapshot of clinical status and may not fully capture fluctuating symptoms occurring outside the assessment window, even though assessments are repeated at quarterly intervals or following clinically significant changes. This may lead to underestimation of the longer-term prevalence of certain conditions. Third, while the gap-based approach used to define discontinuation of antipsychotic medications has been validated in Medicare claims, misclassification of study exposure remains possible. Also, with such gap-based definition, our study included only discontinuers and continuers who survived the specified gap period (i.e., 60 days). Therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to individuals who experienced withdrawal effects or other outcomes within the gap period.

Fourth, because behavioral and psychological symptoms are often under-detected or under-diagnosed in routine clinical practice [43, 44], we assumed that patients with dementia with no documented history of psychiatric disorders or other clinical indications received long-term antipsychotic medications primarily for managing behavioral and psychological symptoms. Fifth, dementia was identified using a validated claims-based algorithm (positive predictive value of 77%) rather than direct clinical assessment, which may introduce diagnostic misclassification. For example, some ICD codes included in the algorithm are non-specific and may reflect age-related cognitive or functional decline rather than a confirmed clinical diagnosis of dementia. Moreover, some individuals with clinically significant cognitive impairment may not have been captured. As a result, the study population primarily reflects nursing home residents with dementia documented in administrative claims, and findings may not be fully generalizable to individuals with undiagnosed or unrecorded dementia. In addition, we lacked reliable information on dementia subtypes, as there are no well-validated claims-based definitions beyond Alzheimer’s disease, and other etiologies (e.g., Lewy body, frontotemporal dementia) could not be consistently reported due to CMS cell size suppression requirements. Sixth, our findings may be limited to nursing home residents with dementia and long-term antipsychotic medication exposure, a population with an urgent need for deprescribing antipsychotic medications due to the known risks associated with prolonged antipsychotic medication use.

In addition, we excluded individuals with selected psychiatric conditions (depression, bipolar disorder, and schizophrenia) and Parkinson’s disease to reduce the likelihood that antipsychotic use reflected indications other than dementia-related behavioral and psychological symptoms and to improve internal validity by minimizing confounding by treatment indication. While this approach strengthened cohort specificity, it may limit generalizability to residents with comorbid psychiatric conditions as well as those with Parkinson’s disease or Parkinsonian syndrome. Lastly, the limited number of events for specific causes of hospitalization precluded subgroup analyses to evaluate whether the observed associations (e.g., pneumonia, UTI) differed across patient subgroups.

Conclusion

Discontinuation of antipsychotic medications was associated with reduced risks of hospitalization, as well as severe pneumonia and urinary tract infections, among patients with dementia living in nursing homes. These findings show that meaningful clinical benefits can result from re-evaluating long-term antipsychotic medication use regularly and encouraging efforts to deprescribe antipsychotic medications among patients with dementia.
