Health profession students’ anxiety, depression, and imposter syndrome in a rural versus urban setting
Authors: Edie L. Sperling, Kalyanii Kennedy, Targol Mehrazar, Madeleine Takahashi, Sophia Rezayee, Chadbourne Zitek, Emily Gresner, Mandilin G. Hudson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLimited research exists comparing healthcare students’ mental health among disciplines or between rural versus urban healthcare students. The researchers aimed to examine the prevalence and severity of anxiety, depression, and imposter syndrome among medical and physical therapy students in an urban versus a rural university.MethodsStudents enrolled in the Doctor of Osteopathic Medicine (DO) and the Doctor of Physical Therapy (DPT) programs in all class years at an urban campus in Pomona, California and rural campus in Lebanon, Oregon, were sent an anonymous survey containing the Generalized Anxiety Disorder scale, Patient Health Questionnaire-9, and Clance Imposter Scale.ResultsN = 316 participants responded, a powered sample at 80%. Mean scores indicated high levels of imposter syndrome and mild levels of anxiety and depression among participants. Urban DPT students had significantly higher anxiety than urban medical students (p = 0.02). Female (p = 0.05) and LGBTQ (p = 0.05) students also had significantly higher imposter syndrome scores than male or heterosexual students, respectively. Anxiety was lower among clinical students compared to preclinical (p = 0.005).ConclusionsImposter syndrome is high among urban and rural medical and physical therapy students. Interventions targeting imposter syndrome, particularly among women and those identifying as LGBTQ, may be beneficial to both medical and physical therapy students. Further research in a larger population is warranted.

Background

Anxiety and depression are higher among healthcare professionals than in any other profession, leading to high rates of career burnout and attrition [1–4]. More than 30% of health professionals report an intent to leave healthcare within a year, contributing to the growing healthcare shortage in the U.S [5–7]. Medical students and physical therapy (PT) students both demonstrate significantly elevated levels of stress, anxiety, depression, and burnout, and it is likely that these mental health challenges during schooling are the start of the ongoing challenges faced by professionals upon graduation [8–10]. Recent research finds 15–20% of medical and PT students are depressed and 20–47% have high anxiety [11, 12].

Imposter syndrome or phenomenon is the internalized belief that one is less intelligent, competent, or worthy than others who have achieved the same things [13]. Imposter syndrome is a psychological pattern of behavior characterized by a disconnect between one’s achievements and perceived level of competence, with a tendency to attribute success to external factors rather than intrinsic abilities [14, 15]. Imposter syndrome also typically comes with a fear of being “exposed” as incompetent [14, 15]. Imposter syndrome has been shown to contribute to poor mental health and is common among individuals in high-achieving fields, making those pursuing healthcare education particularly vulnerable [16].

Only previous study has assessed if differences exist between the prevalence of imposter syndrome in rural versus urban-living individuals, finding that young adults in rural areas had higher rates of imposter syndrome than those in urban areas [17]. The study hypothesized that individuals in rural areas had lower self-esteem and felt inferior because rural living could suggest lower social and economic status in society [17]. To our knowledge, there is no comparative research on the prevalence of imposter syndrome among health profession students by university setting.

Among practicing physicians, the prevalence of imposter syndrome has been assessed at 4–10%, but may be much higher among PTs, as high as 50% [14, 18]. Studies among medical students show a prevalence of imposter syndrome at 22–60% and support an association with female gender, anxiety, stress, and depression [15, 16]. Literature on imposter syndrome among PT students is scarce; one study found prevalence to be 74% among n = 593 PT students from across the US [19]; a dissertation reported 100% prevalence among n = 39 PT students in urban Arlington, Virginia [20].

The researchers are located at a large West Coast health sciences university with two campuses: an urban campus in Pomona, California, and a rural campus in Lebanon, Oregon. There are medical and PT programs at each location, making the researchers well positioned to initiate investigation into imposter syndrome among health profession students at an urban and rural setting. The medical and PT programs are the only two disciplines which have programs on both campuses, which is why they were chosen for this exploratory study. Programs have identical or very similar curriculum across campuses.

Our aims were to: (1) evaluate the prevalence and severity of imposter syndrome, anxiety, and depression in two Doctor of Physical Therapy (DPT) and two Doctor of Osteopathy (DO) programs, one of each on an urban and rural campus; (2) assess differences in anxiety, depression, and imposter syndrome between medical versus PT students, urban versus rural students, preclinical versus clinical students, and by demographics; and (3) evaluate the associations between anxiety, depression, and imposter syndrome.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to compare the prevalence and severity of imposter syndrome among graduate health profession students, and to assess it in an urban versus rural setting. Given the negative impact imposter syndrome has on mental health, identifying potential differences among health profession disciplines and settings may improve understanding of those most at risk so that prediction, prevention, and management of imposter syndrome can be better addressed.

Methods

Design and setting

A cross-sectional, descriptive, survey-based design was used to address the aims of this study. The Institutional Review Board of the Western University of Health Sciences approved this study (#2150379) and all participants gave their informed consent to participate. Surveys were distributed to all years of medical and physical therapy students at the university.

Sample size and power

A convenience sample was used, appropriate for an initial exploratory study. The total available population was N = ~ 1,682, including ~ 1,340 medical and ~ 342 physical therapy students. The minimum acceptable sample size was calculated at n = 313, or 18.5% of the target sample (α-level 5%, power 80%, precision d = 5%).

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria included students enrolled in the DO or DPT professional graduate programs at Western University of Health Sciences in any year of the program, at either campus, and age 18 or older. There were no specific exclusion criteria.

Data collection

Recruitment

Recruitment was done through university email. Reminder emails with the survey link were delivered twice more at weekly intervals.

Outcome measures

Outcome measures were compiled in a Qualtrics survey which took approximately five to ten minutes to complete and included demographic information, program, and year of enrollment. Qualtrics is a single sign-on, HIPAA-compliant, online survey program.

The Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale (GAD-7) is a screening tool and severity indicator for anxiety that uses a self-rated scale of 7 items [21]. GAD 7 scale scores range from 0 to 21, each item scored between 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day) [21]. The scale has generally high internal consistency (Cronbach α = 0.92) and a good test-retest reliability (intraclass correlation = 0.83) [21]. Scores between 5 and 10 are rated as a mild risk of anxiety, scores 10–15 as a moderate risk, and scores above 15 as a high risk [21].

The Patient Health Questionnaire 9-item scale (PHQ-9) is a self-administered questionnaire that uses the 9 DSM-IV criteria and has patients rank the criteria from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day) [22]. The PHQ-9 scale ranges from 0 to 27 [22]. Cronbach α is generally high (~ 0.86) [22]. Test-retest reliability is also high (~ 0.84) [23]. Cut-off scores are 5–9 (mild), 10–14 (moderate), 15–19 (moderately severe), and 20–27 (severe) regarding risk of depression [22].

The Clance Imposter Scale (CIS) is a 20-question self-administered assessment that uses a 5-point scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) with scores ranging from 0 to 100 [24]. Cronbach α is generally high [23]. Scores ≤ 40 indicate low imposter characteristics, a score 41–60 indicates moderate imposter experiences, 61–80 indicates frequent, high levels of imposter experiences, and any score greater than 80 indicates very intense imposter experiences that interfere most frequently and severely in a person’s life [24].

Data analysis

SPSS version 29.0.2 and R were used to assess the aim of examining the prevalence, severity, and relationship between perceived anxiety, depression, and imposter syndrome in medical and PT students [25]. Descriptive statistics were done for demographic variables and outcomes and compared to cut-off values in the literature. Data normality was assessed with histograms, and size of subgroups assessed to determine if the data was balanced. Independent t-tests were used for variables with two balanced categories (e.g., gender), and one-way ANOVAs for variables with three or more categories, with Bonferroni’s post-hoc tests adjusted for multiple comparisons. Mann-Whitney U and Kruskal-Wallis tests were used for unbalanced group comparisons with Bonferroni’s post-hoc tests adjusted for multiple comparisons. Pearson’s correlation statistics were used to assess the variables’ relationships. The psychometrics of each outcome measure in the current study were assessed using Cronbach’s alpha for reliability and Pearson’s correlation matrix and critical values for validity [26, 27].

N = 316 students completed the survey, n = 236 DO students and n = 80 DPT students, for an overall response rate of 18.8%. Three students were fifth-year DO students enrolled in a fellowship program and were not statistically different on any outcomes, so left in the analysis but not assessed by subgroup. Repeating students had significantly higher anxiety scores and so were removed prior to analysis. N = 21 responses were removed due to lacking at least one complete outcome measure. Sample size and power

The sample size (n = 316) met the target sample size of n = 313 needed for 80% power. A post-hoc power analysis revealed 100% power using the mean for the GAD-7 in this sample (M = 6.44, SD = 5.4) compared to the mean on the GAD-7 in the general population, M = 3.66, SD = 3.46) [28], with alpha level 0.05.

Medical versus physical therapy students

There were no significant differences between medical and physical therapy students’ means scores for anxiety, depression, or imposter syndrome with all four programs and all years combined. Significant differences were found in subgroup analyses.

Urban versus rural setting

Rural versus urban medical and physical therapy students had no differences in imposter syndrome or depression. Rural PT students had significantly lower anxiety than urban PT students (H(3) = 9.85, p = 0.02, pairwise = 2.78, p = 0.03), but there were no differences in depression or imposter syndrome.

Clinical versus preclinical students

Anxiety and depression differed significantly between preclinical (first- and second-year medical and PT) and clinical (third- and fourth-year medical students and third-year PT) students. Anxiety (H(3) = 13.00, p = 0.005) differed between preclinical and clinical students. Specifically, medical students in their clinical years had significantly lower anxiety than preclinical medical students (pairwise = 3.36, p = 0.005) and preclinical physical therapy students (pairwise = 2.76, p = 0.04). Depression (H(3) = 16.01, p = 0.001) differed between preclinical and clinical students; specifically, medical students in their clinical years had significantly lower depression than preclinical medical students (pairwise = 2.99, p = 0.02) and preclinical physical therapy students (pairwise = 3.61, p = 0.002).

Demographic differences

Imposter syndrome significantly differed by gender (H(4) = 8.58, p = 0.04) with female students reporting significantly higher imposter syndrome scores than male students (pairwise = 2.40, p = 0.05). Anxiety also significantly differed by gender (H(2) = 7.38, p = 0.03), with females reporting significantly higher anxiety than males (pairwise = 2.50, p = 0.04). Imposter syndrome also was significantly higher among students who identified as LGBTQ than as heterosexual (U(2) = 1.94, p = 0.05). Age, race, and ethnicity were not significantly associated with any outcome.

Psychometric analysis

Psychometric analysis was done on the outcome measures to determine their reliability and validity in this specific sample [27, 30–35]. Cronbach’s α for the PHQ-9 was 0.85, for the GAD-7 α = 0.92, and for the CIS α = 0.94. Evidence of validity was confirmed with Pearson correlations for all scales greater than the Pearson critical value (0.11).

Discussion

This study investigated the prevalence, severity, and relationship between anxiety, depression, and imposter syndrome among medical and PT students from an urban and a rural campus of a large health sciences university. Mean scores indicated high levels of imposter syndrome and mild levels of anxiety and depression among all students. The prevalence of imposter syndrome among U.S. adults has been estimated at 9–82% [36]; this sample’s prevalence of those with moderate, high, or severe imposter syndrome was 88.6% (39.1% moderate, 33.7% high, 15.8% severe), indicating significant burden. High levels of imposter syndrome are consistent with the literature on health profession students [11, 12], although this is just the third known study to quantify imposter syndrome among PT students, and the first study to compare students’ imposter syndrome across health disciplines. No differences were found between medical and PT students’ levels of imposter syndrome; both were high.

Rural and urban students reported no significant differences in levels of imposter syndrome or depression, but urban PT students’ anxiety levels were significantly higher than rural DPT students. As far as we know, this is the first study to evaluate imposter syndrome among health profession students from urban versus rural settings, and although research in the general population has found higher imposter syndrome among those living in a rural community [17], that is not the case here. Anxiety among an urban general population has been found to be higher than in rural counterparts, however, which is consistent with findings of this study [37]. As students come from across the country to attend health professions schooling, it may be that upbringing has more of an impact on imposter syndrome than where students attend graduate school. Anxiety may be more impacted that imposter syndrome by an urban or rural environment in this population. As there was no difference between preclinical versus clinical students’ imposter syndrome, imposter syndrome may also be stable across years of medical training, while anxiety decreased. This may suggest imposter syndrome is a more stable trait than anxiety, though there is limited research on the persistence of these variables in a health student population.

Female- and LGBTQ-identifying students had significantly higher imposter syndrome ratings than male- or straight-identifying students, respectively. This could be due to cultural and institutional marginalization which impacts females and LGBTQ students [38, 39]. Although females now outnumber males in the U.S. medical student population, research demonstrates that persistent stereotypes and discrimination still exist [38]. Imposter syndrome is consistently higher among women compared to men, particularly in medicine, and in historically marginalized groups such as LGBTQ individuals [38–41]. Imposter syndrome may have a direct correlation with belonging to a marginalized group [40].

Differences between preclinical medical students, who had high anxiety, and clinical medical students, who had lower anxiety, suggest that anxiety may decrease over time in medical school, or from preclinical to clinical years. Further investigation may be useful to discern if this is due to adaptation to academic responsibilities, differences in preclinical versus clinical duties, or if there are actions taken in the clinical years which could assist with reducing anxiety and depression earlier in training.

Implications and recommendations

Therapeutic interventions have been shown to be successful in addressing and reducing anxiety and depression among health profession students, including small-group cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and dialectic behavioral therapy (DBT) [42–44]. Research on educational interventions for imposter syndrome includes individual, group, and institutional interventions [45]. Models cited include Bowen’s Family Therapy and Gestalt psychology [45]. Specific interventional strategies to improve imposter syndrome included journaling and self-reflection, peer coaching, mindfulness instruction, building support communities of peers, identifying family of origin patterns, understanding imposter syndrome with a focus on empathy and vulnerability, challenging perfectionism, normalizing imposter syndrome in the university community, and addressing systemic biases and gender inequality [45, 46]. For medical students specifically, one study completed eight, 90-minute CBT sessions, which significantly improved expressive repression, mental health, self-esteem, and cognitive reappraisal among medical students with imposter syndrome [46]; another study suggested that one-on-one coaching was more effective than group training in reducing imposter syndrome, with both coaching and group training focusing on fostering a growth mindset [47].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. There is an overall small sample size and low response rate, and although student survey response rates are often low and analysis determined a powered sample, 18.8% response rate may not be representative [48]. As a cross-sectional design, this study does not capture changes in anxiety, depression, or imposter syndrome over time. This study was done at a single health sciences university across two campuses and therefore is not generalizable to other healthcare students or institutions. Instead, this study provides us with data with which to justify larger studies.

Conclusions

Urban and rural medical and PT students demonstrated equally high levels of imposter syndrome, and mild levels of anxiety and depression. Anxiety was significantly higher among urban PT students compared to rural PT students, and among preclinical students compared to clinical students. Imposter syndrome and anxiety were significantly higher among female students than male students, and imposter syndrome was significantly higher among LGBTQ-identifying students compared to heterosexual students. More research into measures of mental health and their association with imposter syndrome in a larger sample size and across more settings would be useful to illuminate potential differences between disciplines and settings. Longitudinal research to follow students over time and into professional practice is warranted to further our understanding of health profession students’ mental health struggles and optimal points for intervention. Interventional research aimed at reducing imposter syndrome may be beneficial, particularly for female and LGBTQ-identifying students.
