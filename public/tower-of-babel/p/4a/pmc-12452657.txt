Incompleteness of the circle of Willis affects sleep quality, cognitive function and inflammation in patients with primary hypertension
Authors: Ying Feng, Xueqi Yang, Yang Song, Yu Zhang, Tianning Zhang, Chenglong Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To investigate whether incomplete Circle of Willis (Incomplete CoW) affects neuropsychological outcomes in patients with primary hypertension, a cross-sectional study was conducted involving 150 patients diagnosed with primary hypertension, a population at increased risk for neurovascular compromise. Magnetic Resonance Angiography was used to classify patients into two groups: Complete CoW (n = 41) and Incomplete CoW (n = 85). Sleep quality was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and cognitive function was evaluated using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE). PSQI scores were significantly higher in the Incomplete CoW group compared to the Complete CoW group, indicating poorer sleep quality in patients with an Incomplete CoW. A significant negative correlation was found between PSQI and MMSE scores in the Incomplete CoW group, linking poor sleep quality with cognitive impairment. These findings suggest that the incompleteness of the CoW may contribute to neuropsychological impairments in patients with hypertension, potentially mediated by enhanced inflammatory responses.Data availabilityThe data used to support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon request.

Introduction

Hypertension has increasingly been recognized as an inducer of sleep and cognitive disorders [1], [2]. Hypertension-induced sleep disturbances often lead to dysfunction in the physical and psychological status [3], [4], seriously endangering human health. In particular, the relationship between insomnia and cognitive impairment has garnered attention [4]. Individuals with insomnia typically experience diminished attention and memory during the day, impacting their learning, work, and social activities, and consequently affecting their normal life and work routines [5]. Moreover, prolonged insomnia can increase the risk of cognitive disorders, positioning it as a crucial potential risk factor for such conditions.

Diagnostic biomarkers are important in identifying patients at risk for sleep and cognitive disorders among hypertensive patients. In previous studies, serum biomarkers, particularly inflammatory biomarkers, are increasingly recognized for their role in identifying sleep and cognitive disorders in hypertensive patients [6]. Hypertension is known to disrupt the blood-brain barrier and induce neuroinflammation [7]. Elevated levels of inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) are commonly found, suggesting an underlying inflammatory process that may contribute to neural damage [8], [9]. In conjugation with serum biomarkers, Imaging biomarkers have been pivotal in elucidating the neural mechanisms underlying sleep and cognitive disorders in hypertensive patients. The Circle of Willis (CoW) is a crucial arterial structure located at the base of the brain, forming a ring-like configuration that provides collateral blood flow between the anterior and posterior cerebral circulations [10]. This anastomotic network consists of the internal carotid arteries, the anterior cerebral arteries, the anterior communicating artery, the posterior cerebral arteries, and the posterior communicating arteries. The primary function of the CoW is to ensure a consistent and adequate blood supply to the brain, even if one part of the system experiences a blockage or stenosis. Variations in the anatomy of the CoW are common, with some individuals having a complete CoW, providing robust collateral circulation, while others have an incomplete CoW, which may result in reduced or less effective collateral blood flow. These anatomical differences can have significant clinical implications, particularly in conditions such as ischemic stroke [11], aneurysms [12], and other cerebrovascular disorders [11]. The integrity of the CoW is thus an important factor in maintaining cerebral perfusion and mitigating the risks associated with vascular insufficiencies. Additionally, an incomplete variation of the CoW (Incomplete CoW) has been linked with the incidence of chronic diseases. For example, it was observed that CoW variation was associated with cognitive impairment, suggesting it is a risk factor for cognitive decline [13].

Despite these insights, the impact of an incomplete CoW on the sleep quality and cognitive functions of patients with primary hypertension remains unclear. In this study, we set out to explore the correlation of the completeness of CoW to sleep quality and cognitive functions among patients with hypertension. Our study recruited patients with primary hypertension and assessed their CoW integrity using Magnetic Resonance Angiography (MRA) [14]. We conducted sleep quality and neurocognitive function tests, utilizing the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) [15] and the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) [16] to measure sleep and cognitive impairments, respectively. Our findings indicate that patients in the Incomplete CoW group exhibited poorer sleep quality and cognitive functions compared to the Complete CoW group. Notably, there was a significant negative correlation between PSQI scores and MMSE scores, suggesting a potential link between poor sleep quality and cognitive dysfunction in patients with an Incomplete CoW. Further, elevated levels of inflammatory markers IL-1β and MMP9 in the Incomplete CoW group suggest a heightened inflammatory response, potentially contributing to the observed impairments.

Methods

Subjects and study design

This cross-sectional study recruited 150 patients with diagnosed primary hypertension from a regional health facility. Patients were included based on criteria that excluded secondary causes of hypertension. The study's primary aim was to investigate the correlation between the structural integrity of CoW and its impact on sleep quality and cognitive functions. MRA was used to categorize patients into two groups: those with a Complete CoW and those with an Incomplete CoW. The study was approved by the Second Affiliated Hospital of Mudanjiang Medical University, and written consent was derived from the participants.

Participants were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (1) age ≥ 30 years, to exclude younger individuals with a higher likelihood of secondary hypertension; (2) a clinical diagnosis of primary hypertension, defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, measured on three separate occasions without the use of antihypertensive medication; (3) availability of complete cranial Magnetic Resonance Angiography (MRA) or Computed Tomography Angiography (CTA) data that allowed for clear visualization and assessment of the Circle of Willis (including the anterior and posterior communicating arteries); (4) availability of basic metabolic and clinical data including sex, body mass index (BMI), blood lipid profile, blood glucose levels, and medical history; and (5) availability of quantitative assessments of cognitive function and sleep quality, specifically the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) scores.

Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) presence of known secondary hypertension due to conditions such as renal artery stenosis, pheochromocytoma, primary aldosteronism, or chronic kidney disease; (2) a prior history of cerebrovascular events, including ischemic stroke, lacunar infarction, or cerebral hemorrhage, as well as the presence of intracranial vascular abnormalities such as aneurysms or moyamoya disease; (3) MRA or CTA scans with severe imaging artifacts that precluded accurate interpretation of CoW structure; (4) diagnosis of malignancy, end-stage heart failure, or severe hepatic or renal dysfunction (defined as estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] <30 ml/min); and (5) a family history of hereditary hypertension, or current pregnancy or lactation.

Magnetic resonance angiography (MRA)

MRA was utilized to assess the structural integrity of the CoW in all participants. The MRA scans were conducted using a 3 Tesla MRI scanner (MAGNETOM Prisma, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany), which offers high-resolution imaging of cerebral vasculature, and Syngo MR + Syngo.via software was used for image processing. Patients were positioned supine on the MRI table, and a head coil was used to enhance signal reception. A time-of-flight (TOF) MRA sequence was employed, which does not require contrast agents and relies on the flow of blood to generate images. This sequence was optimized with a slice thickness of 1 mm and a field of view covering the entire CoW. Imaging parameters included a repetition time (TR) of 25 ms, echo time (TE) of 3.5 ms, and flip angle of 20 degrees, ensuring detailed visualization of the blood vessels.

The completeness of the CoW was assessed based on established anatomical criteria. A Complete CoW was defined as the presence of the following components: (1) bilateral posterior communicating arteries (PComA) connecting the posterior cerebral arteries (PCA) to the internal carotid arteries, (2) an intact anterior communicating artery (AComA) linking the anterior cerebral arteries (ACA), and (3) continuity of flow across all major arterial segments. Any absence, hypoplasia (<1 mm diameter), or discontinuity in any of these vessels was classified as Incomplete CoW, in accordance with commonly used definitions in previous literature [17].

All MRA images were independently reviewed by two board-certified radiologists and one neurologist, each with over five years of experience in cerebrovascular imaging. Reviewers were blinded to all clinical and demographic data to avoid bias. In cases of disagreement, a consensus was reached through joint review. Inter-rater agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa to ensure consistency and reduce variability in classification.

PSQI assessment

Sleep quality was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), the most commonly used subjective questionnaire for evaluating sleep dysfunction in both clinical and non-clinical subjects. The PSQI assesses sleep quality and disorders over a period of more than one month, providing a comprehensive evaluation through a total global score ranging from 0 to 21. The PSQI comprises 19 items across seven domains: subjective sleep quality (assessing overall perception of sleep quality), sleep latency (measuring the time taken to fall asleep), sleep duration (evaluating total sleep per night), sleep efficiency (calculating the percentage of time spent asleep while in bed), sleep disturbances (identifying factors that disrupt sleep, such as waking up in the middle of the night or early morning), use of sleep medication (assessing the frequency and extent of sleep medication usage), and daytime dysfunction (evaluating the impact of poor sleep on daily functioning, including difficulties staying awake during daytime activities and maintaining enthusiasm). Each domain is scored on a scale from 0 to 3, with higher scores indicating greater dysfunction. The domain scores are summed to yield a global PSQI score, with higher global scores representing poorer sleep quality. This detailed assessment allows for a thorough examination of various aspects of sleep and their potential impact on the individual's daily life and overall well-being.

MMSE assessment

Cognitive function was evaluated using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), a widely utilized 30-point scale that assesses several cognitive domains, including orientation, attention, calculation, language, memory recall, and visuospatial performance. Scores were interpreted as follows: normal cognitive function was defined as a score of 25 or higher, mild cognitive impairment (MCI) was defined as a score ranging from 18 to 24, and severe cognitive impairment was defined as a score of 17 or lower. This classification allows for the identification and differentiation of various levels of cognitive impairment among the study participants.

Inflammatory markers assessment

Blood serum levels of inflammatory markers IL-1β and MMP9 were quantified using Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) with commercial ELISA kits (Human IL-1β ELISA kit, Abcam, Cat. No. ab108865; Human MMP9 ELISA Kit, Abcam, Cat. No. ab246539). ELISA assays were conducted by using the Varioskan LUX multifunctional ELISA reader, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA. The level of CRP was detected by a fully automated biochemical analyzer (Siemens, ADVIA Centaur XPT). Reference values for IL-1β, MMP9 and CRP were as follows: IL-1β ≤ 5 pg/ml, MMP9 ≤ 22.17 pg/ml, CRP < 5 mg/L. If these markers were significantly upregulated (P < 0.05) in hypertensive humans with Incomplete CoW compared with hypertensive humans with complete CoW, then inflammation was considered elevated.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical data analysis was performed by using the SPSS software (version 25, SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.0, GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA) software. The quantitative data in this study were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Descriptive statistics were employed to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics. Categorical variables were compared with χ2 tests or Fisher exact test. Group means for continuous variables were compared using t-test or Mann–Whitney U test. Correlation analyses were performed by using the Spearman r-test to explore the relationships between PSQI and MMSE scores and inflammatory markers. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, indicating that the results could be deemed reliable within a 95 % confidence interval.

Assessment of sleep quality of patients

Subsequently, we assessed the sleep quality of the patients using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), where a higher score indicates poorer sleep quality. Comparisons were made based on the completeness of the CoW. It was found that the PSQI scores were higher in the Incomplete CoW group (7.68 ± 4.21 scores) compared to the Complete CoW group (3.07 ± 2.43 scores, P < 0.0001), suggesting that patients with an Incomplete CoW have poorer sleep quality (Fig. 2). We next examined whether an incomplete CoW also affects the cognitive functions of patients with hypertension (Fig. 3). Thus, it can be inferred that patients with hypertension in the Incomplete CoW group may exhibit poorer sleep quality and cognitive function, suggesting a possible link between the two. Discussions

This study aimed to explore the relationship between the structural integrity of the CoW and its impact on sleep quality and cognitive function in patients with primary hypertension. Previously, techniques such as structural MRI and DTI were used to assess brain volume and white matter integrity, which revealed that hypertensive patients are characteristic of reductions in gray matter and compromised white matter tracts [18]. Compared to previous imaging biomarkers, CoW presents a unique advantage over in the study of sleep and cognitive disorders among hypertensive patients due to its fundamental role in maintaining cerebral perfusion and providing collateral circulation. Unlike other biomarkers that typically reflect downstream effects of cerebrovascular health, the CoW directly reflects the brain's ability to compensate for arterial blockages and maintain consistent blood flow [19]. Variations in the CoW's anatomy, such as incomplete formations, can lead to significant differences in the brain's resilience to ischemia and its capacity to sustain cognitive and sleep functions under hypertensive conditions. This direct anatomical feature offers a more immediate and structural insight into potential vulnerabilities, allowing for a more targeted assessment of cerebrovascular integrity and its impact on neuropsychological outcomes.

In the present study, participants were categorized into Complete and Incomplete CoW groups based on MRA findings to evaluate the overall influence of CoW completeness on sleep quality and cognitive function in patients with primary hypertension. This binary classification was intended to provide an initial, overarching understanding of the clinical relevance of CoW morphology. To address the heterogeneity within the Incomplete CoW group, we documented the distribution of specific missing or hypoplastic communicating arteries. These data are presented in Supplementary Table S1, providing detailed information on the anatomical variations observed in the 85 patients with an incomplete CoW. We found that patients with an Incomplete CoW have significantly poorer sleep quality, as evidenced by higher PSQI scores, and worse cognitive function, as indicated by lower MMSE scores, compared to those with a Complete CoW. Additionally, the significant negative correlation between PSQI and MMSE scores in the Incomplete CoW group suggests a possible link between poor sleep quality and cognitive impairment in this population. It has been shown that an incomplete CoW is more common than a complete configuration, both in the general population and among individuals with hypertension [20]. The prevalence of CoW incompleteness typically exceeds 50 %, which aligns with the distribution observed in our study (incomplete CoW in 85 out of 126 patients). Notably, the clinical significance of CoW variation may be amplified in hypertensive individuals, where reduced collateral capacity increases the risk of cerebrovascular events and impaired cerebral perfusion

Elevated levels of inflammatory markers IL-1β and MMP9 in the Incomplete CoW group further imply that chronic inflammation may play a role in these observed impairments. Our results are consistent with previous research demonstrating the adverse effects of hypertension-induced sleep disorders on cognitive function. Notably, our study represents one of the first studies that link these effects specifically to the structural variations in the CoW among hypertensive patients. Prior studies have highlighted the CoW's role in maintaining cerebral perfusion and mitigating ischemic risks, but its influence on sleep and cognitive outcomes has not been extensively explored. Our findings clarified that an Incomplete CoW could be a critical factor influencing these neuropsychological outcomes, supporting the potential use of CoW for identifying patients with high risks for sleep and cognitive disorders.

The observed associations may be explained by several mechanisms. An Incomplete CoW could lead to insufficient collateral circulation [21], [22], potentially resulting in cerebral hypoperfusion, which is known to affect both sleep and cognitive functions. Furthermore, the elevated inflammatory markers in the Incomplete CoW group suggest that inflammation could be a mediating factor. Chronic inflammation has been implicated in both sleep disturbances and cognitive decline, providing a plausible link between CoW morphology and these outcomes [23].

These findings have significant clinical implications. Assessing the integrity of the CoW using non-invasive imaging techniques like MRA could help identify hypertensive patients at higher risk of sleep and cognitive impairments. Early identification and intervention could mitigate these risks, potentially improving the quality of life and clinical outcomes for these patients.

Inflammation has been widely recognized as a critical mediator linking hypertension to both cognitive dysfunction and sleep disturbances. While general markers such as CRP and IL-6 have been extensively studied, this study specifically focused on IL-1β and MMP9 due to their central roles in neurovascular pathology. IL-1β is a key pro-inflammatory cytokine that disrupts the blood-brain barrier (BBB), promotes neuroinflammation, and has been implicated in both sleep regulation and cognitive impairment. MMP9, a proteolytic enzyme involved in extracellular matrix remodeling, is upregulated in response to hypertension-induced vascular stress and contributes to BBB degradation and neuroinflammatory cascades. Both biomarkers are known to be elevated in cerebrovascular and neurodegenerative conditions and may reflect early microvascular injury even before overt stroke or infarction occurs. Therefore, IL-1β and MMP9 were selected as mechanistically relevant inflammatory biomarkers to explore whether CoW incompleteness may mediate cognitive and sleep disturbances via inflammatory pathways in hypertensive individuals.

Despite providing these novel insights, this study has limitations. The cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of causality and longitudinal studies are needed to confirm the temporal relationships between CoW integrity, sleep quality, and cognitive function. Future studies should also consider other potential confounders, such as medication use and lifestyle factors, in more detail. While this study focused on the global impact of CoW incompleteness, we recognize that specific arterial abnormalities (e.g., absence of the anterior or posterior communicating arteries) may differentially influence cerebral perfusion and clinical outcomes. The current dataset does not stratify patients by the type or number of CoW anomalies, which is a limitation. We plan to explore the impact of specific vascular segments on sleep and cognitive outcomes in future analyses. Investigating the potential benefits of targeted interventions, such as anti-inflammatory treatments or therapies aimed at improving cerebral perfusion, could provide further insights and practical applications. Another limitation of the present study lies in the binary classification of the CoW as either complete or incomplete. While this approach aligns with commonly used definitions in prior neurovascular research, it may oversimplify the spectrum of anatomical variability that exists within the population. Variations such as hypoplasia, segmental absence, or unilateral incompleteness may have differing effects on cerebral perfusion and clinical outcomes, including sleep quality and cognitive function. Although the MRA-based classification was conducted independently by experienced radiologists and neurologists who were blinded to patient data, and consensus was reached in the event of discrepancies, we acknowledge that further stratification based on the location and severity of CoW variants would enhance the granularity of future analyses. Moreover, quantitative assessments of vessel diameter and collateral capacity may provide additional insight into functional cerebrovascular reserve, which a purely structural assessment cannot fully capture. Future studies should incorporate detailed subclassification of CoW variants to evaluate their distinct hemodynamic consequences and clinical significance in hypertensive populations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights the significant impact of CoW completeness on sleep quality and cognitive function in patients with primary hypertension. The findings suggest that an Incomplete CoW may contribute to these impairments, potentially through mechanisms involving cerebral hypoperfusion and chronic inflammation. These insights underscore the importance of considering vascular anatomy in the management of hypertensive patients and pave the way for future research to develop targeted interventions.
