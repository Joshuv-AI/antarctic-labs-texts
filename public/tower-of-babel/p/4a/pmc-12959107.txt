Introduction

Both Histoplasma capsulatum and Blastomyces dermatitidis are fungi that are endemic in North America, in common regions including the Ohio River Valley, Mississippi River Valley, and The Great Lakes region. Incidence rates of each fungus is difficult to quantify due to underreporting & underdiagnosis, however, in endemic regions rates of blastomycosis range from 0.41 to as high as 10.9 cases per 100,000. Histoplasma has a much higher prevalence with rates up to 100 cases per 100,000 population.1 Histoplasma, and to a lesser extent Blastomyces, can cause subclinical illness, but can also cause progressive disseminated diseases.2

Diagnosis is best made by visualization of yeast in smears, in tissue specimens, or by culture.1–3 Unlike Candida and Aspergillus, Histoplasma and Blastomyces do not colonize, therefore, identification of the fungi leads to correct diagnosis.1 Treatment recommendation includes itraconazole, but amphotericin B is used for severe cases.1

Here, we present a rare case of dual fungal infection in a 35-year-old immunocompromised female with advanced HIV, who presented with lower abdominal pain and was found to have concurrent disseminated histoplasmosis and intrapulmonary blastomycosis. This case highlights the importance of early recognition and management of dual fungal infections in immunocompromised patients.

Case presentation

A 35-year-old female with past medical history significant for human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and substance use disorder presented to the hospital for two-week history of lower abdominal pain, weakness, and unintentional weight loss of 40 pounds in the last five month. The patient states she has also been experiencing worsening dyspnea and subjective fevers. There were no night sweats, hemoptysis, sputum production, or productive cough. She had an occasional dry cough but otherwise had no other respiratory symptoms. The patient was diagnosed with HIV approximately eight years ago and has been noncompliant with antiretroviral therapy. She also admits to a 20-pack-year history as well as weekly intravenous methamphetamine use.

Her initial vital signs upon presentation showed respiratory rate of 24 breaths per minute, heart rate of 136 beats per minute, blood pressure 116/79 mmHg, temperature of 99.5 F, breathing 94%on roomair. On physical examination, she was noted to be unkept, thin, and appeared older than stated age with diffuse sarcopenia. She was in no acute distress but appeared uncomfortable. She also appeared to be hypovolemic with dry mucous membranes and increased skin turgor. Cardiac exam was notable for tachycardia, with regular rhythm and without additional or attenuated heart sounds. Pulmonary examination revealed diffuse inspiratory and expiratory wheezing, as well as diminished air flow across all lung fields.

Her abdomen was flat, soft without rigidity, guarding or rebound tenderness. No evidence of ascites or distention however was notable for moderate suprapubic tenderness as well as mild splenomegaly. Genitourinary and rectal exam were deferred as the patient refused both.

The patient’s initial labs showed a sodium level of 129 mEq/L (normal range 136–144), potassium of 3.2 mEq/L (normal range 3.6–5.1), lactic acid 2.1 mmol/L (normal range <2.1), magnesium 1.4 mg/dL (normal range 1.6–2.6), hemoglobin 10.0 g/dL (normal range 12.5–16). Urinalysis showed 1+ leukocytes, positive nitrites, 1+ blood, 2+ protein, 4+ urobilinogen, 22 RBC per HPF (normal range 0–5), 41 per HPF (normal rage 0–5). Urine drug screen was negative. She was found to have a viral load for HIV-1 of 680 and an undetectable CD4 count. Chest radiograph showed evidence of diffuse miliary patterns of lung fields bilaterally, predominantly in the mid-to-lower lungs (Fig. 1). This continued to progress as seen in Fig. 2. The extensive miliary pattern was further recognized on her chest CT (Fig. 3). Initially the miliary pattern on chest x-ray in our patient with AIDS was interpreted as tuberculosis and thus was started on rifampin/Isoniazid/pyrazinamide/ethambutol (RIPE) therapy.

The patient underwent further workup with bronchoscopy and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL). The BAL cultures were negative for Legionella, Mycobacteria, Coccidioides and showed no other bacteria. After four days, the BAL cultures returned with significant growth of both Histoplasma and Blastomyces. Urine cultures were positive for Escherichia coli. Blood test of the Blastomyces dermatitidis antigen was positive at 13.84 ng/mL (positive is above 0.2 ng/mL).

With RIPE therapy discontinued, she was initiated on amphotericin B 300 mg every 24 h for 14 days. Ten days into her hospital course she developed worsening cough, hypoxemia, hemoptysis, and hallucinations, suggestive of worsening sepsis from fungal pneumonia. Her condition continued to deteriorate, requiring intubation, ICU transfer, and vasopressor support. After several days of aggressive antifungal and antibiotic therapy, she was extubated but soon developed recurrent hemoptysis and hypotension, necessitating re-intubation. With the anticipated poor prognosis, family agreed to comfort measures, and our patient died soon after.

Discussion

Concurrent infections with Histoplasma capsulatum and Blastomyces dermatitidis are not well-documented together, making diagnosis difficult. The overlapping geographic distribution of these dimorphic fungi—primarily in the Ohio and Mississippi River valleys, the southeastern United States, and Great Lakes regions—further complicates identification. 1,2,4–8 While histoplasmosis is documented in northern Florida, blastomycosis is rare in the state, and our patient had no travel history to suggest exposure from endemic areas.9–11

Histoplasmosis is the most common endemic mycosis in the United States.1,2,5 Blastomycosis was initially reported as a dermatologic condition but later recognized as a systemic infection.1,5 Both are acquired through the inhalation of fungal spores, which convert into pathogenic yeast forms in the lungs.1,5 Exposure risks for histoplasmosis include disturbing environments rich in bird or bat guano, such as abandoned chicken coops, tree cutting, and spelunking in bat-inhabited caves while blastomycosis is associated with soil, decaying vegetation, bird excreta, and bodies of water.1,4,5

In healthy adults, acute symptomatic pulmonary histoplasmosis presents with nonspecific symptoms such as fever, chills, myalgias, dyspnea, and hypoxia.1,2,4 Chest imaging often reveals patchy pneumonia, with hilar and mediastinal lymphadenopathy. 4 A severe acute pulmonary infection can develop when an individual inhales a large quantity of H. capsulatum.4 Chest imaging often shows widespread reticulonodular infiltrates.4 Disseminated histoplasmosis primarily affects the skin and soft tissues, with hepatosplenomegaly, fever, and weight loss as hallmark features.1,4,12 Common skin findings include erythema nodosum and erythema multiform.4 Bone marrow involvement can lead to cytopenias, and less commonly, Addison’s disease, meningitis, or endocarditis may occur.1 While many individuals recover without treatment, severe cases require antifungal therapy to prevent respiratory failure and facilitate recovery.4

Blastomycosis typically manifests between 3 weeks and 3.5 months post-exposure.7 Its diagnosis is often delayed due to its ability to mimic bacterial pneumonia, tuberculosis, malignancy, lymphoma, amyloidosis, and sarcoidosis.1 If the fungal spores evade initial host defenses, they transition to yeast, proliferate in the lung parenchyma, and disseminate via the bloodstream.1 The disease spectrum includes asymptomatic infection, isolated pulmonary disease, disseminated disease (involving skin, bones, and CNS), and extrapulmonary disease.1–3 A pulmonary infiltrate is the most common clinical presentation.1 Acute pneumonia due to blastomycosis reveals fever, chills, and productive purulent cough, and hemoptysis.1 Chronic pneumonia presents two to six months before diagnosis with weight loss, night sweats, fever, cough, and chest pain.1 Cutaneous lesions often start as papulopustules before progressing into ulcerative plaques, differentiating them from the mucocutaneous involvement seen in histoplasmosis.1,7 Other extrapulmonary manifestations include osteomyelitis, genitourinary symptoms (prostatitis, epididymitis), meningitis, and epidural or cranial abscesses.1,8

A thorough patient history—including travel, occupational exposures, and environmental risk factors—is crucial for identifying potential fungal exposures.7 Culture and cytopathology are the gold standard for diagnosis,1–4,7 but obtaining adequate specimens often requires bronchoscopy. Antigen testing for histoplasmosis has an 83 % sensitivity for detecting pulmonary histoplasmosis, while blastomycosis antigen testing has a 76 % sensitivity.2 Cultures require extended incubation.8,13 Cross-reactivity between histoplasmosis and blastomycosis antigen tests is high, with over 60 % of positive Blastomyces urinary antigen assays cross-reacting with Histoplasma antigen assays.2 False-negative results may occur early in infection, particularly in antibody-based testing.2 Our patient’s cultures, obtained via bronchoalveolar lavage, confirmed concurrent infections.

Management of concurrent histoplasmosis and blastomycosis requires individualized treatment plans tailored to disease severity and patient immune status. In cases requiring supplemental oxygen or ventilatory support, early antifungal initiation is crucial.1 Itraconazole is the treatment of choice for mild to moderate histoplasmosis, while severe cases warrant initial therapy with liposomal amphotericin B followed by itraconazole.4,7,14 Ketoconazole can also be considered as an alternative treatment.1 Antifungals for mild cases of histoplasmosis is not necessary.1 Blastomycosis also responds well to these agents, making careful coordination essential to prevent drug interactions and manage overlapping regimens. Typically, with blastomycosis, the presence of pleural disease or extrapulmonary manifestations warrants treatment. 1 Both ketoconazole and itraconazole work on blastomycoses but ketoconazole has a less favorable side effect profile.1 Regular liver, renal, and hematologic function monitoring is critical during treatment, particularly in immunocompromised patients, to optimize outcomes and minimize complications.7

In reviewing the literature, seven cases of disseminated cutaneous histoplasmosis were reported in South Florida, two involving AIDS patients with CD4 counts <40 cells/mm3.10 Another case described a 61-year-old male with histoplasmosis endocarditis despite no known exposure to birds or bats.9 A separate report detailed a 15-year-old male in Florida diagnosed with blastomycosis, potentially linked to prior camping trips in endemic regions.11 His family recalled trips to the Minnesota/Canada border and North Carolina, two to three months and two to three weeks before symptom onset, which may explain the infection.11 Otherwise, literature on histoplasmosis and/or blastomycosis in Florida is scarce, likely due to underreporting and the region’s non-endemic status.

Conclusions/take home points

Dual endemic mycoses can co-occur in immunocompromised hosts, even in non-endemic areas. This case highlights the rare but possible concurrent pulmonary blastomycosis and disseminated histoplasmosis in a patient with AIDS and no known travel to endemic regions, emphasizing the need for a broad infectious differential in severely immunosuppressed patients.
