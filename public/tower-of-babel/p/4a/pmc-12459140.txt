Hematologic Variations in Children With Down Syndrome
Authors: Golda Grinberg, Gwen Sokoloff, Beverly Hay, Stefanie Lowas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hematologic abnormalities in children with Down Syndrome (DS) have previously been described, but with inconsistent conclusions. A retrospective single-institution cohort study was conducted to evaluate complete blood count (CBC) ranges in children with DS. Mean values for CBC results are presented by age and sex, as a clinical resource for the evaluation of DS children. In comparison to reference values, children with DS had higher mean corpuscular volume (MCV), lower absolute lymphocyte count, and a trend toward higher hemoglobin (Hgb) values. The presence of these findings across the cohort suggests that these are benign hematologic variations in DS.

Down syndrome (DS, or trisomy 21) occurs in about 1 in 700 births, resulting in about 6000 new cases in the United States every year.1 Children with DS may have health problems involving multiple systems, including the hematologic system.2,3 The risk of developing acute leukemia is 10 to 20-fold higher in children with DS compared with children without DS.2 Aside from hematologic malignancies, children with DS may have other, benign, hematologic variations. High mean corpuscular volume (MCV) throughout childhood has been noted in multiple studies.4–10 Lymphopenia has also been observed, while total lymphocyte counts in children with DS paralleled the otherwise expected decrease with age.6,10–14 Leukopenia, alterations in neutrophil and eosinophil counts, decreased platelet counts, and high hemoglobin (Hgb) values have each also been noted,6,10,14 but these findings have not been reliably replicated. Of the available publications on this subject, only 3 included more than 100 patients and only 2 were published after 2012. One study, from 2022, included a large cohort of children with DS, but it found most hematologic differences to only be present in variable age ranges in each sex.10 Furthermore, that study was performed at a high altitude (5000 feet above sea level), potentially impacting Hgb values and limiting the application of the findings to other locations.10,15

Due to the limitations noted above, standard ranges for hematologic values for patients with DS throughout childhood have not been well documented. This creates difficulty for health care providers when determining whether to refer these patients to hematology for abnormal complete blood count (CBC) results. Because of their leukemia risk, routine monitoring of CBC with differential is recommended for children with DS. This study seeks to define expected CBC ranges for these children, compared against a population-based database. These findings may guide physicians when evaluating hematologic values found in the DS population.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We performed a retrospective cohort study of children with DS 1 to 18 years of age, identified from a database of patients of an academic medical center’s DS clinic. The study was approved by the medical center’s Institutional Review Board. Cohort data were collected and deidentified by the institution’s Data Science Core. We excluded patients documented to have leukemia.

Data collection from the study cohort included values from CBCs, which had been drawn as part of routine medical care from January 01, 2008, to May 01, 2020. Variables for the analysis included Hgb, MCV, platelet count, white blood cell count (WBC), and absolute numbers of lymphocytes and neutrophils. Other collected data included sex and age at the time of lab draw. For patients who had multiple CBCs drawn in a given calendar year, only the first data point for each variable per year was included in the analysis. Thus, the same patient could have data points over multiple years, but not multiple data points in a single year. Mean, SE, and CI were calculated for each variable for each year of age. To mimic standard pediatric CBC reference ranges, we calculated the mean and SD for our cohort’s lab values for ages 1 to 3, 4 to 7, 8 to 12, and 13 to 18 years (separated by sex for the latter group). For this analysis, the first data point per year per patient for each variable was isolated and then the data were bracketed. As a result, a patient may have zero or multiple data points in each age bracket.

Cohort data were compared with reference data collected by the National Health and Nutrition Survey (NHANES) from 2009 to 2018, with synthesized sample sizes for each age based on the weighted frequency of the United States population.16 The NHANES population likely included children with DS, but their values would have had a negligible effect on the means, due to the DS prevalence of about 0.1%.1 Our cohort’s means were measured against the CIs of the means of the NHANES data. If our cohort’s mean for a variable was outside the NHANES CI, the 2 means were deemed to be statistically significantly different. In addition, we applied a weighted regression model and computed bootstrap estimates (based on 1000 or more simulation runs) to ensure statistical significance.

RESULTS

One hundred ninety-eight patients (98 female, 100 male) met the inclusion criteria for our study cohort. This included 8 to 41 patients per sex for each year of age at the time of the lab draw. (See Figure, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JPHO/A749, the total number of patient data points per sex and year of age.) The NHANES data set represented 74,843,001 children 1 to 18 years old. There were differences in MCV, absolute lymphocyte count, and hemoglobin values between the study cohort and the NHANES data set. The average MCV and absolute lymphocyte counts with 95% CIs are shown in Figures 1 and 2. MCV values in children with DS were significantly higher than the NHANES data. This remained true throughout childhood for both sexes. Absolute lymphocyte numbers were lower in children with DS 1 to 11 years of age in both sexes, with a similar trend seen in the older children. While there were higher hemoglobin values in preschool and school-age children with DS, this difference was not large enough or consistent enough to be statistically significant (Fig. 3). WBC, absolute neutrophil counts, and platelet counts did not significantly differ between the groups (see Figure, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JPHO/A750 for graphs of these variables).

DISCUSSION

Hematologic differences in children with DS were first noted several decades ago,4 but there are limited recent data characterizing these variations. The clinical need for additional information to guide hematologic decision-making for these patients prompted the initiation of this study. The current study included 198 children with DS and collected their CBC values through childhood (1 to 18 y). These values were compared with the NHANES data set, representing children without DS. This cohort had high MCV values at each year of childhood, reinforcing similar findings which had previously only been published by age brackets.4,6,8–10 The analysis noted higher Hgb in females and prepubertal males with DS at each year of childhood versus the comparison data. This became significant when the values were grouped into age brackets that combined sexes prepuberty. This supports the difference in Hgb between children with and without DS published in some studies.4,6,10 Parallel to the current study, there was a publication of CBCs in a DS cohort of 355 children 90 days to 18 years old, residing at a median altitude of over 5000 feet.10 That publication used 4 age brackets (90 d to <2 y, 2 to <6 y, 6 to <12 y, and 12 to 18 y), separated by sex, which could impact clinical application of the data. In addition, high elevation is likely to increase Hgb values.10,15 Therefore, the current study’s Hgb ranges are more applicable to populations at low to medium land elevations.

Lymphopenia has been previously documented in children with DS, though the degree of significance has varied.6,10–12 This study demonstrated significantly lower absolute lymphocyte counts in prepubertal children with DS compared with the NHANES data, while the lymphopenia was more significant in females than males post puberty. Our age bracket analysis demonstrated trends toward lower WBC and higher neutrophil and platelet counts in children with DS, though these are not clinically significant. The previous large DS cohort study found differing results, including lower WBC, neutrophil, and platelet counts in some age/sex groups but not others.10 These discrepancies highlight the need for additional research to document hematologic variations in children with DS.

For this study, whether cohort means fell within NHANES CIs was used to determine statistical significance. This method was used instead of P-values because the NHANES data set was too much larger than this cohort to allow for P-value calculation. When a weighted regression model was applied, the difference between the 2 population means was highly significant despite collapsing mean intervals. The regression estimate results might be prone to bias because of the difference in sample size. To overcome this, bootstrap estimates were computed. NHANES estimates compared with the DS cohort estimates were still significantly different for collapsing mean intervals (data not shown).

This study represents the second-largest cohort of DS children in over 35 years to have CBC values collected and published. The children with DS in this study had higher MCV, lower lymphocyte, and often higher Hgb values than the comparison group. This indicates that these are common, benign hematologic variations, though additional research in even larger cohorts is needed. These data can serve as a resource for clinicians when assessing children with DS whose CBC results fall outside of the general population’s reference ranges. In the absence of other signs, symptoms, or lab abnormalities, DS patients with CBC values concordant with those published here may not require additional hematologic evaluation.
