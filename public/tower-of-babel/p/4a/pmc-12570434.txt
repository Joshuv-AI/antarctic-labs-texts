Student motivations and identity in biomedical sciences: a social cognitive careers theory approach
Authors: Rachelle Singleton, Antonia Verstappen, Kimiora Henare, Monica Kam, Nishani Lim, Leslie Schwarcz, Clare Wall, Tanisha Jowsey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis research explores the identity and career aspirations of biomedical science students across disciplines such as cell biology, anatomy, physiology, pharmacology, and pathology. The study examines students’ motivations for choosing these programmes and perceptions of career preparation, with analysis by programme, ethnicity, and gender.MethodsUsing a longitudinal cross-sectional survey guided by Social Cognitive Career Theory, we gathered responses from 172 students in 2023 and 184 in 2024. The questionnaire included both closed and open-ended questions, which we analysed using descriptive and thematic methods.ResultsMost students (76.8%) enrolled due to strong interest in the field. Clinical, research, and industry roles were seen as desirable, while academia, allied health, regulatory affairs, and teaching were less appealing. Postgraduate study became a more prominent career goal for more students enrolled in their capstone courses in the third and final year of the programme (stage III) than for students enrolled in courses/papers taken in the second year of the programme (stage II). Most students (83.7%) felt their programme adequately prepared them for career success, citing labs, field trips, problem-based learning, and assignments as key contributors. However, 22% viewed creativity and entrepreneurship as irrelevant to their future work. Most (73.9%) were confident about career prospects, though grades and competitive entry were concerns.ConclusionsFindings are discussed in relation to Social Cognitive Career Theory, highlighting the importance of authentic work experiences, professional identity development, and clear career pathways for improving student engagement and retention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08098-3.

Background

Biomedical sciences—encompassing cell biology, anatomy, physiology, pharmacology, and pathology—equip students with versatile skills applicable to diverse careers. Graduates pursue roles in basic research, regulatory agencies, clinical trials, commercial enterprises, or transition into healthcare professions [1]. However, little is known about what motivates current biomedical science students or how they perceive the effectiveness of university programmes in preparing them for professional success. Do students aspire to research or entrepreneurship? Are they aiming for clinical careers and view biomedical science as a stepping stone? Understanding these motivations is essential, as they should inform how universities communicate programme offerings.

Research from Australia highlights gaps in student awareness of career pathways beyond research and medicine, and how their skills transfer to other fields [2]. Students seek career guidance, industry exposure, and networking opportunities to shape their professional identity [2, 3], which is linked to career satisfaction and success [4].

Previous studies investigating biomedical science students’ motivations and career intentions have largely focused on static snapshots of student aspirations, often without exploring how these vary across demographic groups or as students mature and advance from second year (stage II) to the third and final year (stage III) of their studies. For instance, Panateros et al. (2019) conducted a qualitative analysis of four open-ended questions to explore students’ motivations, intended professions, and perceived skill applicability [2]. While valuable, and an inspiration for this study, theirs did not include in-depth demographic data such as ethnicity, nor did it examine how aspirations might shift as students’ progress through their degree. Similarly, Demaria et al. (2018) surveyed final-year biomedical science students before and after a capstone employability unit, finding that most retained their initial aspiration to pursue medicine [5]. However, their study focused on perceptions of transferable skills and did not investigate longitudinal changes in career aspirations or include demographic breakdowns.

Our study builds on this prior work by applying a Social Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT) framework to examine not only students’ initial motivations and career aspirations, but also how these evolve between the second and third year of study in the degree programme. We incorporate detailed demographic data—including gender and ethnicity—and focus on a New Zealand cohort, offering insights from a context that has been underrepresented in the literature. Importantly, our study was conducted in the post-COVID-19 era, a period marked by significant shifts in higher education [6], healthcare workforce demands [7], and student perceptions of career stability and relevance. Findings from a British Science Association survey revealed a marked uplift in 14-to-18-year-olds who would consider working in a scientific field as a result of COVID-19 – with 37% of young people now more likely to consider a scientific career [8]. These contextual changes may have influenced both the skills students value and their long-term career goals, making it timely to revisit and extend earlier findings.

Preparing students for career success

In today’s competitive job market, graduate employability is increasingly tied to skills competency [9, 10]. Universities are expected to equip students with both discipline-specific and transferable skills that align with labour market demands. However, evidence suggests that many institutions fall short in this regard. A review of 42 Australian universities in 2018 found that only half had embedded activities within their curricula aimed at developing employable graduates in alignment with industry needs [11].

Despite the growing emphasis on employability, there remains a lack of clarity around which skills students themselves perceive as critical for career success—particularly in Science Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics (STEM) fields such as biomedical sciences. Prior studies have shown that students often prioritise transferable skills such as communication, critical thinking, and teamwork over discipline-specific competencies like laboratory techniques and scientific reasoning. For example, Demaria et al. found that fewer than 20% of final-year biomedical science students considered discipline-specific skills vital to their intended professions, even after completing an employability-focused unit [5]. Similarly, Panaretos et al. reported that second-year biomedical science students ranked communication and critical thinking as the most important skills for their future careers [2].

These findings underscore the importance of understanding students’ perceptions of skill relevance as they approach graduation. By investigating which skills students deem essential for career success, universities can better tailor their curricula to support graduate outcomes. This research question is therefore critical to informing curriculum design, enhancing employability, and ensuring that biomedical science graduates are adequately prepared for the workforce.

Identity and social cognitive careers theory

This study is grounded in Social Cognitive Careers Theory (SCCT), selected over other relevant frameworks such as Situated Learning Theory [12], Identity Theory [13], and Vygotskian social learning theories. SCCT [14, 15] builds on Bandura’s (1986) social cognition theory and explores how self-efficacy, outcome expectations, and goals influence career choices within sociocultural contexts.

Social identity, defined as the part of self-concept derived from group membership and its emotional significance [16], is central to SCCT. The theory posits that individuals develop career interests by engaging in related activities, which build confidence and reinforce outcome expectations. Lent et al. (1994) argue that interests (e.g., curiosity about viruses) guide choices (e.g., studying biomedical science), and performance influences identity (e.g., success in science reinforces a scientific identity). In biomedical science, identity forms through both disciplinary (e.g., pharmacology, physiology) and professional lenses.

Sociocultural factors such as ethnicity and gender also shape identity and career development. This study, conducted in New Zealand (NZ)—a country with significant ethnic diversity—considers these influences. As of the 2023 census, 19.5% of the population identified as Māori, 20.4% as Asian, and 8.8% as Pacific [17]. Women comprise 50% of the national population [18], but 56% of University of Auckland students and 60–64% of biomedical science students [19].

Despite this, Māori and Pacific student graduation rates remain insufficient to meet the demand for health research leaders [20, 21]. Barriers include cultural isolation, racial discrimination, and large class sizes [22–25]. Enrolment in biomedical science programmes by Māori and Pacific students remains low (3–8% Māori, 4–12% Pasifika from 2020 to 2022), with even fewer pursuing postgraduate study [19]. These challenges are echoed globally [26, 27], where Indigenous students face systemic barriers to university completion [28] and greater stress adapting to unfamiliar environments [29, 30]. SCCT’s attention to sociocultural influences makes it a valuable framework for understanding how Indigenous students navigate these barriers and align career goals with cultural identity.

In the New Zealand context, Māori as tangata whenua (a Māori term that translates to “people of the land”) and Pacific peoples hold unique cultural, historical, and social positions that warrant focused attention in educational research. Māori are the Indigenous people of Aotearoa New Zealand, and Te Tiriti o Waitangi (Treaty of Waitangi) obligations require institutions to actively support Māori advancement. Pacific peoples, while not Indigenous to New Zealand, are a significant and historically underserved population with strong ties to the region.

Gender also plays a role in career identity. Research shows female STEM students often report stronger career identity and commitment than males [31], highlighting the need for gender-responsive employability strategies. This may reflect women’s greater engagement in career exploration and learning mindset [31]. Gender-responsive employability practices, such as mentoring programmes and inclusive career services, can further support women’s career development in STEM. Cross-national studies further reveal that gender differences in STEM aspirations are shaped by sociocultural and educational contexts [32].

These findings have global curricular implications. As higher education institutions worldwide seek to prepare students for dynamic and uncertain professional landscapes, understanding which skills students perceive as relevant and desirable becomes critical [33]. By identifying the evolving aspirations and skill priorities of biomedical science students, educators and curriculum designers can better align programme content with students’ career trajectories and professional identities. This ensures that graduates are not only academically prepared but also equipped with the competencies they believe are essential for success in their chosen fields.

Methods

Drawing on Social Cognitive Careers Theory, we designed a mixed-method questionnaire and administered it to students with a cross-sectional approach. The questionnaire had closed and open-ended questions. We undertook descriptive analyses and thematic analyses.

Ethics approval

This study was approved by The University of Auckland Human Participants Ethics Committee on 31/03/2023 for three years. Reference number UAHPEC 25,752.

Study context

In 2023 and 2024, we administered the questionnaire to students enrolled in one or more biomedical science courses at a leading university in Auckland, New Zealand. The Bachelor of Science (BSc) in Biomedical Science, or equivalent BSc major; pharmacology, physiology, medical imaging, biological sciences, food science and nutrition, medicinal chemistry, psychology degree at the university serves primarily as a pathway into academic research programmes, as well as entry into clinical programmes (such as medicine, optometry and pharmacy). Students intending to apply for direct entry into clinical programmes commonly enrol into the Biomedical Science programme to complete an Overlapping Year 1 (OLY1). However, after their first year of study, many of those who do not enter clinical programmes remain in their BSc Biomedical Science or specific BSc discipline major e.g., pharmacology or physiology. The focus of this study was students who remained in their BSc Biomedical Science or specific BSc discipline major after OLY1.

Study design

The study is cross-sectional in that the study examined different samples (or a “cross-section”) of the student cohort at points in time. The questionnaire was distributed to students enrolled in courses/papers taken in the second year of the programme (stage II) or in their capstone courses in the third and final year of the programme (stage III), in 2023 and 2024 (multiphase). The survey was anonymous, and we did not track individual students between years (i.e., 2023 stage II students may have completed the survey again as stage III students in 2024). A cross-sectional study allowed us to collect data from a large pool of participants and compare differences between cohorts (e.g., stage II cohort versus stage III capstone). The aim was to recruit > 25% of each cohort; ~110 student participants per year, of ~ 426 students enrolled into the biomedical, physiology and pharmacology programmes each year.

Positionality and reflexivity statement

This research team comprises seven non-Indigenous scholars from colonised nations and one Māori scholar (KH). Seven members of our team are female. We acknowledge that our positionalities influenced how we shaped the research project and engaged with data. While the study centralises Māori identity and female gender, the theoretical frameworks employed—identity, social identity, and Social Cognitive Career Theory—are grounded in Western paradigms, which are not specific to gender. To ensure Indigenous and male perspectives were meaningfully integrated, KH was actively involved throughout the research process, contributing cultural insight and guiding interpretation. This collaborative approach responds to calls for more flexible publishing formats that allow Indigenous researchers to present work authentically, without conforming to Western norms [34]. We remain critically aware of the limitations of our positionality and have approached this work with cultural humility and a commitment to respectful engagement.

Questionnaire design

The questionnaire developed for this study explored students’ motivations for choosing their programme, career aspirations, valued skills, and perceptions of opportunities to demonstrate these skills during their degree. It also collected demographic data and self-reported Grade Point Average (GPA). The University of Auckland uses a nine-point GPA scale. A letter grade is awarded in a course based on a defined percentage scale (e.g., 70%−74% is a B), and each letter grade has a point value on the nine-point GPA scale (e.g. a B = 5).

Demographic items were adapted from the Faculty of Medical and Health Sciences’ Health Professional Students and Graduates tracking project [35, 36] and the 2023 New Zealand Census. Questions on university experience were modified from the 2023 Australian Student Experience Survey [37]. There were 39 questions in total. Most items used select response formats, with some open-ended components allowing for detailed, personalised input. Questions with open-ended components (e.g. “Not confident (please explain why)” or “Other (please specify)”) included questions 3, 5, 8–13, 15, 17–19, 20–21, 24–25, and 39 (Supplement 1). For select response formats a 4-point Likert scale was used to reduce ambiguity, and middle-score bias common in 5-point scales [38]. One questionnaire item used an eight-point ranking system, asking students to rank the eight stated career options in order of preference, from first preference to eighth preference.

The list of influencing reasons included in our questionnaire (question 20, Supplement 1) was informed by the work of Panaretos et al. (2019), who conducted an inductive analysis of open-ended responses from biomedical science students (n = 433) regarding their motivations for studying science. Their analysis yielded 231 distinct reasons, which were grouped into coded descriptions and presented in Table 1 of their publication [2]. Each code was accompanied by its frequency of occurrence (% of respondents), allowing identification of the most cited motivations. For our questionnaire, we selected the coded descriptions with the highest frequencies as predefined response options, ensuring relevance to our target population. Additionally, we included an open-ended response option: “Other (e.g., personal experience with illness), please specify”, to allow students to report influences not captured by the predefined list. This ensured space for personalised and nuanced input beyond the scope of the original coding framework.

To ensure validity and cultural relevance, the questionnaire was piloted with eight team members, including one Indigenous researcher and seven course leaders. Feedback from a structured debriefing informed revision, including clarifying wording, adding culturally relevant items, and improving coherence. Completion time ranged from 10 to 20 min. The questionnaire used in this study has not been previously published elsewhere (Supplement 1).

Questionnaire administration

An anonymous online questionnaire was administered via the university-licensed Qualtrics platform [39]. Participants accessed the anonymous online questionnaire via a secure link, which included a participant information sheet outlining the study’s purpose, procedures, and their rights. By starting the questionnaire, participants acknowledged that they had read and understood the information provided and agreed to take part in the study. This process was approved by The University of Auckland Human Participants Ethics Committee (Reference number UAHPEC 25752) as evidence of informed consent, and the requirement for separate signed consent forms was formally waived. Participation was entirely voluntary and anonymous.

Eligible participants were students enrolled in the second year of the programme (Stage II) or in capstone courses in the third and final year of the programme (Stage III) (approx. 400–430 students annually). With course leaders’ approval, students were briefed on the study’s aims and, where possible, its findings. The questionnaire was distributed via Canvas, the university’s learning management system, to enrolled students only.

To avoid conflicts of interest, invitations were delivered by individuals not involved in course delivery—such as research assistants (under confidentiality agreements), academic support staff for Māori and Pacific students, or student representatives. Recruitment was conducted through Canvas announcements and posters in study spaces. Participants required access to an internet-enabled device and a stable internet connection.

We distributed the questionnaire through targeted programmes and relevant courses to ensure broad Māori participation and share results to support tino rangatiratanga (Article II of Te Tiriti o Waitangi).

Data analysis

We undertook descriptive analysis of demographic, Likert scale and selected response style questions. For open-ended questions we applied a qualitative thematic analysis.

We undertook inductive thematic analysis, following Braun and Clarke’s reflexive approach to thematic analysis [40]. This involved reading the data closely, identifying emerging codes (such as skill type, setting (e.g. lab), type of activity (e.g. staining or microscopy or tests), aspiration/s, motivation, satisfaction, career, and so forth) and grouping codes into themes (such as Learning Environments Matter). Quotes are grouped under themes or subthemes that emerged during analysis.

For ethnicity questions we chose Level 1 prioritised ethnicity in line with guidance from the NZ Ministry of Health (which is consistent with Stats NZ recommendations) for the reporting of ethnicity, where the order of priority is (1) Māori, (2) Pacific, (3) Asian, (4) Middle Eastern, Latin American, and African (MELAA), (5) NZ European. A fine-grained prioritised ethnicity analysis was not conducted as this would have resulted in losing numbers from some of the level 2 ethnic classifications which fell below the reporting threshold of n > 3.

Due to the small number of Māori and Pacific respondents, statistical comparisons were not appropriate. Instead, we used descriptive analysis to highlight patterns and perspectives within these groups. While absolute numbers were low, response rates were proportionally high relative to enrolment, indicating strong engagement. We included these data to ensure Māori and Pacific voices were represented, consistent with equity-focused research principles that value inclusion even when sample sizes are limited.

Results

Participants

Three hundred and fifty-six students responded to this questionnaire. Most students who responded were enrolled in a biomedical science degree programme (n = 113, 32%). Other programmes reflected in the data include Clinical pathway (n = 10, 2.8%), Bachelor of Science (BSc) (Pharmacology) n = 19 (5.3%), BSc (Physiology) n = 29 (8.1%), BSc (Other) n = 35 (9.8%), Conjoint degree n = 49(13.8%) (Table 1). Of those who stated which stage of their degree programme they were in; most students were in year two (stage II) (68.1%). Only 28.5% were in year three (stage III) and < 4% responded as ‘other’ reflecting those currently enrolled into an equivalent mix of both stage II and III courses. Respondents’ age range was 21–23 (64.0%), 18–20 (23.6%), 24–26 (9.0%) and > 27 (3.4%) (Table 1).

Most respondents indicated that their current degree was their preferred programme of study (i.e., the study option they intended to enrol in from the outset) (83.0%, n = 215). Of the 17.0% (n = 44) of respondents who said their current degree was not their preferred programme of study, most open-ended responses identified the first preference was a clinical programme, 63.6% (n = 28) of these specifically mentioned a preference for the medical programme.

Prioritised ethnicity reporting shows that the respondents were: (1) Māori (2.7%) (2) Pacific (3.2%) (3) Asian (51.0%) (4) MELAA (5.6%) (5) NZ European (37.5%). NZ Euro and Other Euro were grouped so that the Other Euro participants could be reported (because Other Euro respondents fell below our reporting threshold due to prioritisation). The number of Māori and Pacific respondents in this study reflects their low enrolment in the Biomedical Science, Pharmacology, and Physiology programmes, rather than a lack of engagement with the survey itself. In 2023, approximately 10 Māori and 24 Pacific students were enrolled, with 7 respondents from each group—representing response rates of approximately 70% and 29%, respectively. In 2024, enrolments declined further to around 7 Māori and 12 Pacific students. While the number of Māori respondents in 2024 fell below the reporting threshold, 4 Pacific students responded, yielding a response rate of approximately 33%. These figures suggest that the survey achieved strong engagement from these groups relative to their enrolment, and the data, while limited in size, remain meaningful for highlighting patterns and informing equity-focused discussions.

More females (72.8%) responded to our questionnaire than males (26.0%). We offered a third gender option in the questionnaire; ‘another gender’, however the number of responses to this option did not meet our reporting threshold for year one. The percentage of female responders is higher than the overall proportion of females in the biomedical cohort, where female students typically comprise 60% of the cohort.

Student motivations for studying their current degree programme

Students rated how strongly certain reasons influenced their decision to study their chosen degree programme. A total of nine reasons were ranked by students (n = 264, Response Rate (RR) = 74.2%), with an option of ‘other’ for those not captured in the prescribed reasons provided. Most respondents ranked ‘studying in a field that really interests me’ (76.8%) as strongly influencing their decision (Fig. 1). Improving job prospects (44.3%), social or societal impact (38.9%) and ‘training for a specific job’ (36.6%) were also strong influences. Most respondents ranked ‘developing talents and creative abilities’ or ‘expectations of parents/family’ and ‘careers advice’ as somewhat influential. For ‘prior work experience’ the majority selected ‘did not influence my decision’ (81.3%).

Students selecting ‘other’ were offered an opportunity to provide their influences as an open-ended response. Personal, family experiences with illness were the most frequently mentioned ‘other’ influencing factor (n = 29). Other influencing factors mentioned included personal experiences (n = 10) and personal interest (n = 6).

Māori and Pacific student motivations concurred with all other ethnicities with all respondents ranking ‘studying in a field that really interests me’ as strongly (72.2%) or somewhat influencing their decision (27.8%). Student career aspirations

Many students (38.7%) intended to use their current degree programme as a pathway to clinical training (e.g. medicine), citing clinical training as their first preference for a future career (Fig. 2). Industry and postgraduate study were also popular first preferences, at 16.0% and 31.6%, respectively.

We acknowledge that the option ‘Postgraduate study’ in the survey may appear broad and open to interpretation. However, we believe that most respondents understood this option to refer specifically to higher qualifications pursued after completing an undergraduate Bachelor of Science programme in biomedical science or a closely related major—such as a taught Master’s or research-based PhD. This interpretation is supported by the fact that other options were clearly delineated in the survey, including ‘Clinical training e.g., Medicine, Nursing, Pharmacy, Dentistry, Veterinary’, ‘Academia e.g., research and/or teaching in university’, and ‘Teaching (at primary and secondary school level)’. These distinctions were intended to separate postgraduate study in a research or academic context from professional training in health, education, or industry settings (Supplement 1).

Careers in academia (research and/or teaching in university), allied health (e.g., Nutrition, Physical therapy), and regulatory affairs (e.g., advise government on drug safety, test water quality) were not as highly desired or sought after by the students compared to other career options. Teaching (at primary and secondary school level) was the least favoured career aspiration.

Female respondents’ career aspirations were largely aligned with those of their male counterparts. Māori and Pacific student motivations were like other ethnicities with many respondents intending to use their current degree programme as a pathway to clinical training (38.9% reporting a first preference).

To determine whether self-reported GPA aligned with career aspirations, GPA was broken down into high (GPA 8–9, A to A+) or low (2–3, C to C+) ranges, and compared to preferred pathway or profession following graduation, however there were no significant findings.

Preferred pathways

Just over half of the students who responded said their ideas about their preferred pathway or profession following graduation had changed from the previous year (51.6%). Female responses regarding whether their ideas about their preferred pathway or profession following graduation had changed from the previous year were largely aligned with those of their male counterparts; female (53.6%), male (43.1%).

Over a third of Māori and Pacific respondents changed their ideas about preferred pathway/profession since the year prior (44%). Differences between other ethnicities (Asian, MELAA, NZ European) in terms of preferred pathways were minimal, with the majority responding that their ideas about their preferred pathway or profession following graduation had changed from the previous year as for all ethnicities.

Clinical training (e.g. medicine) was ranked highly as a preferred future pathway by students in both stage II and stage III of the degree programme (Fig. 3), however postgraduate study became the top ranked anticipated pathway/profession for more stage III students compared to stage II (Fig. 3B).

While some differences in anticipated career pathways emerged between Stage II and Stage III students—such as a greater proportion of Stage III students identifying postgraduate study as their top-ranked future pathway (Fig. 3B)—there were also notable similarities. Across both stages, clinical training (e.g., medicine) remained a highly ranked aspiration. Furthermore, student interest in careers in academia (research and/or university teaching), allied health (e.g., nutrition, physical therapy), regulatory affairs (e.g., advising government on drug safety, testing water quality), and teaching at the primary or secondary school level was consistent between Stage II and Stage III cohorts (Fig. 3). These shared aspirations suggest a degree of continuity in professional goals as students’ progress through the programme.

Student confidence

Most students were confident that they would get accepted into their preferred pathway/profession following graduation from their current degree programme (73.9%). Of those who were not confident, most mentioned in open-ended responses that it was because their grades were not high enough (n = 33). Some mentioned they were lacking confidence because of the competitive entry of their preferred pathway (n = 9), or that they were uncertain about their preferred direction (n = 13).

Most Māori and Pacific student respondents reported feeling ‘somewhat confident’ (n = 9), with none indicating they were ‘very confident’. No Māori respondents provided written explanations for their confidence levels. All Māori respondents reported average self-identified GPAs, with no students falling into the high (GPA 8–9, A to A+) or low (GPA 2–3, C to C+) ranges.

Female student motivations and confidence levels regarding career progression were largely aligned with those of their male counterparts. Most respondents, regardless of gender, expressed confidence in gaining acceptance into their preferred professional pathways following graduation. Notably, female students with both high (GPA 8–9, A to A+) and low (GPA 2–3, C to C+) academic performance levels demonstrated similar career confidence to students of other genders.

Skills valued by students

Students of all ethnicities perceived ‘ability and willingness to learn’ as the most relevant skill for their intended profession, rating it “highly relevant” (84.8%) (Fig. 4). Analytical/critical thinking (83.6%) and communication (written and oral) (79.1%) were also deemed “highly relevant”. Creativity and entrepreneurship were the lowest ranked skills, with 21.8% of respondents ranking entrepreneurship as not relevant. Differences between stage II and stage III as well as between genders were minimal. The overall trend is the same.

Māori and Pacific students rated resilience as a more relevant skill for their intended profession, rating it “highly relevant” (82.4%), compared to all other ethnicities (78.1%). Differences between other ethnicities (Asian, MELAA, NZ European) in terms of skills prioritisation were minimal.

To explore whether Māori and Pacific students held different perceptions compared to other participants, we conducted chi-square tests of independence on relevant categorical variables. However, the analysis showed no statistically significant difference in resilience between Māori and Pacific students and all other combined ethnicities (p = 0.39).

Students’ perceptions of opportunities to demonstrate relevant workplace skills: qualitative results

In the open-ended survey questions students were asked to provide examples of authentic learning; opportunities where they were able to demonstrate relevant workplace skills during specific courses. The number of student responses was 75 and 76 in 2023 and 2024 respectively. Respondents wrote an average of 27–28 words per response.

Two themes were identified in the qualitative results: Learning Environments Matter and Three Workplace Skills. Learning Environments Matter concerned student views on the various mechanisms through which learning was facilitated (labs, tutorials, group work, field trips, and Problem-Based Learning (PBL) sessions). Three workplace skills concerned three categories of skills that students identified as important to their future workforce readiness. These themes were interconnected.

Learning environments matter

In both 2023 and 2024 responses, overwhelmingly, respondents saw labs as the central mechanism through which they learned and demonstrated workplace skills. Students preferred labs over other learning mechanisms. The skill development that respondents associated with labs include managing patient samples, technical skills (pipetting, sterilisation, dissection, histological readings etc.) centrifuging, staining, microscopy, diagnostics and report writing, teamwork and communication, emotional intelligence, creativity, problem-solving, resilience and adaptability, integrity; and computer literacy, using signal processing hardware and software, taking and interpreting Echocardiogram readings.

In addition to labs, respondents identified the learning environments as important for embedding their learning of skills needed for the workplace: tutorials, group work, fields trips (e.g. BIOSCI206 [Principles of Ecology]), problem-based learning (PBL) sessions (e.g. OPTOM216 [Introduction to Optometry]), and assignments that included real world scenarios. Respondents talked about the field trip skills in terms of teamwork, resilience, curiosity, problem-solving, and time-management. Respondents talked about tutorials and PBL in terms of synthesising information and learning diagnostics and recognition of physiological change. Respondents talked about the assignments in terms of critical thinking, creativity, and written communication skill building.

Students’ university experience

A high percentage of students strongly or somewhat agreed with the statement “I often find it stressful managing my studies with other commitments in my life” (89.1%) (Fig. 5). Most students were satisfied with their course selection (82.8%) and could see the link between their subjects and future career prospects (83.7%). A low percentage of students wanted to study at another university (23.9%) or change their current degree programme (20.1%). Female responses regarding university experience were largely aligned with those of their male counterparts. Differences between ethnicities (Māori, Pacific, Asian, MELAA, NZ European) in terms of university experience were minimal.

Discussion

This study explored the motivations and career aspirations of undergraduate biomedical science students, interpreted through Social Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT) to understand how identity and professionalism develop during study. Students’ motivations were primarily driven by intrinsic interest in the field, with many viewing their degree as a pathway to clinical training. Career aspirations evolved over time, with postgraduate study becoming increasingly preferred among students in later stage of the programme (Stage III). While students generally felt their programmes prepared them well for career success, they placed particular value on authentic learning experiences such as labs, field trips, and problem-based learning.

Our findings show that identity development is shaped by students’ positionality —particularly their ethnicity, gender, and stage of study. For example, Māori and Pacific students, though underrepresented, demonstrated strong engagement and placed greater emphasis on resilience as a key skill for career success. The predominance of Asian (51.0%) and female (72.6%) respondents also reflects broader demographic patterns in biomedical science enrolments, which influence how students perceive their professional identities and navigate career pathways. These insights underscore the importance of culturally responsive teaching and equity-focused support structures in shaping inclusive professional identities.

Student motivations and career aspirations

SCCT posits that personal goals, self-efficacy, and outcome expectations shape learning and identity. Our findings align with Zimmerman [1], showing that intrinsic interest is the primary motivator for students, alongside job prospects and societal impact—consistent with SCCT-based research [41]. Embedding career education early in the curriculum, such as showcasing diverse STEM roles and integrating career mapping activities, could help broaden students’ awareness and aspirations beyond clinical pathways.

Teaching at the primary and secondary levels was the least preferred career path, despite New Zealand’s STEM teacher shortage. In 2018, the Ministry of Education projected a need for 850 additional teachers by 2019 [42]. The lack of interest in teaching may reflect systemic issues such as low financial incentives, undervaluing of the profession, and limited visibility of teaching as a STEM career. These perceptions are shaped by institutional cultures that prioritise research and clinical careers [1]. Financial barriers also influence career choices. Postgraduate study and academic careers are often inaccessible due to cost and job insecurity [43]. Zhou and Shirazi et al., found that financial stability and prestige significantly shape STEM career aspirations [44]. Reframing teaching as a meaningful, stable STEM career could address both the educator shortage and students’ limited career awareness. This may require targeted efforts to elevate teaching’s status, foster a sense of belonging of STEM scientists in the teaching community, and create clearer pathways into education roles.

Evolving professional aspirations during undergraduate study

Initially, many students viewed biomedical science as a route to clinical training, reflecting broader trends and in agreement with previous studies from Australia [2, 45]. However, our study shows the evolution of student aspirations whereby Stage III, more students anticipated pursuing postgraduate research. This shift supports literature showing that career goals are shaped by academic exposure and reflection. For instance, Nguyen et al., found that medical students’ specialty preferences changed with clinical experience [46]. Ho et al., similarly, describe how educational and social contexts influence aspirational development, while warning against “aspirational constriction” from early career decisions [47]. Although the majority of biomedical science students in Australia intended to pursue a career in medicine (69%), research (34%) or a science profession (14%) [2], a recent study by Lee et al. [48] showed that alumni from 38 Australian universities found on LinkedIn were, in fact, employed in the research sector (20%), education (16%) and healthcare services (15%). These findings highlight the need for flexible curricula and career guidance that support evolving goals throughout students’ academic journeys. Curricular flexibility, including elective modules and exposure to varied research and industry roles, may support students in refining their goals as they progress through their degree.

Equipping students for career success: technical, technological, and soft skills

Students identified key competencies – willingness to learn, analytical thinking, and communication – as essential for academic and professional success. These findings align with prior research showing that biomedical science students often prioritise transferable skills over discipline-specific competencies [49, 50]. For example, Demaria et al. found that fewer than 20% of final-year students considered discipline-specific skills vital to their intended professions, even after completing an employability-focused unit [5]. Similarly, Panaretos et al. reported that communication and critical thinking were ranked highest by second-year students preparing for future careers [2].

In our study, students echoed these preferences, valuing learning activities that improved their ability to locate and apply information, and favouring authentic learning experiences such as labs, field trips, problem-based learning (PBL), and assignments. Critical thinking and communication, in particular, were seen as enhanced through PBL, which has proven effective in medical and biomedical education [50, 51]. Laboratory sessions were most valued for skill development, supporting research that emphasises the importance of hands-on learning for engagement and skill acquisition [52, 53]. Embedding interdisciplinary projects and innovation challenges into biomedical curricula could foster entrepreneurial thinking and better align education with emerging workforce needs.

Despite the emphasis on transferable skills, creativity and entrepreneurial thinking were not widely prioritised. Many students viewed these as unrelated to their career goals, reflecting broader trends in STEM education, where creativity and entrepreneurship – though vital for innovation – are often under-emphasised. Ritter et al. argue that traditional academic structures limit opportunities for creative development [54]. Programmes like NYU’s Biomedical Entrepreneurship Educational Program (BEEP) show that structured entrepreneurial training can boost student confidence and interest in innovation [55]. Yet, as Romero-Galisteo et al. note, students’ entrepreneurial intentions depend on perceived relevance and feasibility [56], suggesting that unless entrepreneurship is clearly linked to biomedical careers, it will remain undervalued.

The limited emphasis on creativity and entrepreneurship may stem from a perception of biomedical careers as technical and linear. Addressing this requires curriculum reforms that integrate creative problem-solving and entrepreneurial thinking in ways that resonate with students’ professional identities [57].

Ethnic differences in skill prioritisation: amplifying Māori and Pacific voices

Māori and Pacific students in this study placed higher importance on resilience than students of other ethnicities did, highlighting ethnic differences in skill prioritisation [58]. This aligns with literature documenting the challenges these students face in their first year of tertiary health-related study, including cultural isolation, racial discrimination, and large class sizes [22–25]. The difficulties (in predominantly colonial institutions) that students experience in their first year of tertiary study are further compounded by socio-economic factors and by being the first in their family to attend higher education [26, 27, 59]. Support strategies for these students necessitates a multi-pronged approach and may entail such strategies as prioritised mentorship programmes, inclusive curriculum design, and even more extensive curricula commitments such as culturally responsive pedagogy and decolonised curricula.

Despite low Indigenous representation in this study, it aimed to amplify Māori and Pacific voices to promote institutional responsiveness. Evidence shows that systemic racism and cultural safety issues persist in tertiary health programmes, including biomedical sciences [23, 60, 61]. Recognising and addressing these challenges is essential to supporting Indigenous students and fostering inclusive, equitable learning environments.

Gender similarities in career confidence

Most students expressed confidence in their career prospects, though concerns about grades and competitive entry were noted, reflecting broader trends where self-efficacy and academic performance shape career confidence [62, 63]. In this study, female students’ motivations and confidence levels were largely aligned with those of male students. Regardless of gender, respondents were optimistic about entering preferred professional pathways, and academic performance did not appear to influence gender-based differences in career outlook. STEM disciplines have undergone significant gender shifts over the past 50 years [61], with increasing female participation driven by equity initiatives. In our study, women comprised 72.6% of respondents, consistent with broader enrolment trends. While Ananthram et al. found that female STEM students report higher confidence in career identity and learning relevance [31], our findings suggest more nuanced gender dynamics within biomedical science. This underscores the importance of examining gender within specific disciplinary and institutional contexts. Across all genders, students prioritised authentic learning and skill acquisition – particularly technical, technological, and soft skills – as central to career success.

Identity development

Students’ evolving aspirations reflect a shift from viewing biomedical science as a pathway to clinical careers toward seeing it as a foundation for scientific identity. This transformation is marked by the acquisition of key skills and shaped by personal interests. For some Indigenous students, reconciling professional and cultural identity remains challenging, with added stressors and persistent negative experiences in tertiary health education [64, 65]. Ongoing support beyond admission and transition phases is needed, with inclusive teaching approaches that address the needs of Māori, Pacific, and socio-economically disadvantaged students [23, 66].

Alignment with National student experience benchmarks

To assess comparability, selected items adapted from the Australian Student Experience Survey [37] were analysed. Our findings closely aligned with national data: students reported high satisfaction with course relevance and career preparation, alongside stress from competing commitments. While factor analysis was not conducted, the consistency with validated instruments [67] supports the reliability and broader relevance of our results.

Limitations

This study excludes students who discontinued after their first year of study (Stage I), omitting perspectives of those who faced academic challenges leading to early attrition. The small number of Māori and Pacific respondents reflects their low enrolment in biomedical science, pharmacology, and physiology programmes. This is partly influenced by initiatives like Vision 20:20, which aim to increase access to competitive health programmes such as medicine, pharmacy, and optometry [68], which occurs after the first year of the biomedical science programme.

While Māori identity was central to this study, the theoretical frameworks – identity, social identity, and Social Cognitive Career Theory – are rooted in Western paradigms. To mitigate this, the Māori scholar (KH) on our team was involved throughout the research process, offering Indigenous perspectives and deeper insights. This approach responds to calls for more flexible publishing formats that allow Indigenous researchers to present work authentically, without conforming to Western norms [34].

Additionally, the study offers only a snapshot in time, which limits exploration of identity as a dynamic, evolving construct.

Conclusions

This study provides new insights into the motivations, career aspirations, and skill development priorities of biomedical science students, interpreted through Social Cognitive Career Theory. Students were primarily driven by intrinsic interest and career prospects, especially in clinical fields, though aspirations evolved toward postgraduate study by Stage III. Authentic learning experiences and skill acquisition were universally valued, while creativity and entrepreneurship remained under prioritised. The study also highlights low interest in teaching careers despite national shortages, and amplifies Māori and Pacific voices, who emphasised resilience in response to unique educational challenges. These findings have implications for curriculum design, career guidance, and equity-focused strategies in biomedical science education.
