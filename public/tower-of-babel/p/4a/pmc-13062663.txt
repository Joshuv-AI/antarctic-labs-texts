Pure single-incision laparoscopic cholecystectomy using an articulating instrument: the Ewha Method in a retrospective observational study
Authors: Yeongsoo Jo, Seog Ki Min
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeConventional multiport laparoscopic cholecystectomy (CMLC) is the gold standard for benign gallbladder disease. Single-incision laparoscopic cholecystectomy (SILC) offers superior cosmesis but has limited adoption due to technical challenges. We introduce the Ewha Method, a modified SILC technique using an articulating instrument to improve visualization, ergonomics, and safety.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 530 patients who underwent SILC between November 2023 and August 2024. Surgical outcomes were compared between senior (n = 338) and junior (n = 192) surgeons. Difficult surgery (DS) was defined as conversion to CMLC or operative time >60 minutes. Multivariable analysis identified risk factors for DS, and the learning curve was evaluated.ResultsThe junior surgeon group included more high-risk patients, with higher proportions of elderly (≥65 years, 42.2% vs. 12.7%), male (53.6% vs. 29.9%), comorbidities, preoperative percutaneous transhepatic gallbladder drainage (PTGBD; 30.7% vs. 0.0%), and gangrenous cholecystitis (7.3% vs. 0.3%). The conversion rate was higher for junior surgeons (10.4% vs. 0.3%), but the severe complication rate was similar (0.5% vs. 0.0%). No bile duct injuries or incisional hernias occurred. Independent predictors of DS included diabetes mellitus (hazard ratio [HR], 4.045), preoperative PTGBD (HR, 5.780), leukocytosis (HR, 7.457), impacted stones (HR, 7.913), and gangrenous cholecystitis (HR, 38.736). The learning curve plateaued after 38 cases in low-risk patients.ConclusionThe Ewha Method standardizes SILC, ensuring safety and efficiency with a short learning curve and minimal complications. It may serve as a viable alternative to CMLC, warranting further validation in diverse clinical settings.Keywords: Laparoscopy, Laparoscopic cholecystectomy, Minimally invasive surgical procedures, Single-incision, Single-incision laparoscopic surgery

INTRODUCTION

Since the introduction of laparoscopic cholecystectomy in 1985, it has become the gold standard for the surgical treatment of benign gallbladder disease [1]. Compared with open cholecystectomy, laparoscopic techniques offer multiple advantages, including reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stay, and improved cosmetic outcomes. With further advancements in techniques, the potential applications of laparoscopic cholecystectomy have expanded, and it is increasingly being used in patients with severe inflammatory changes and selected patients with gallbladder cancer. In line with the general trend towards minimizing surgical invasiveness, single-incision laparoscopic cholecystectomy (SILC) was first performed by Navarra et al. [2] in 1997.

Although SILC offers better cosmetic outcomes and a reduction in incision-related morbidity, its broader adoption has been limited by certain drawbacks, including its technical complexity, ergonomic challenges, and a steeper learning curve [3,4,5]. Various techniques have been developed to address these problems, including the use of an articulating instrument and modifications, such as multichannel access ports [6,7]. Despite these advancements, SILC is still not universally considered to be a standard procedure, largely due to the lack of well-established methods and residual concerns over patient safety, particularly in complex cases, such as patients with acute cholecystitis [8,9].

Numerous studies have compared the outcomes of SILC with those of conventional multiport laparoscopic cholecystectomy (CMLC) [10,11,12,13,14,15,16,17,18]. Although several studies have demonstrated comparable safety and efficacy of these 2 techniques, other studies reported higher complication rates for SILC, especially when it is performed by less experienced surgeons [9,19]. The complication rate, difficulty in achieving an adequate view of Calot’s triangle, and extended surgery times remain as significant barriers to widespread adoption of SILC [20,21,22]. Furthermore, the diverse surgical techniques used at different institutions and by individual surgeons, each with distinct advantages and disadvantages, pose a significant challenge to standardizing this procedure [23,24].

In the present study, we introduce and evaluate the advantages of a new SILC technique, which we have called the Ewha Method. This method uses an articulating instrument to improve visualization and ergonomics. Our primary goal was to demonstrate the safety and feasibility of this technique, focusing on shortening the learning curve and improving surgical efficiency. By using an articulating instrument, the Ewha Method seeks to mitigate the technical challenges associated with traditional SILC, even making it accessible to surgeons with moderate laparoscopic experience. The safety profile and shortened learning curve of the Ewha Method could make it a new standard for minimally invasive cholecystectomy. With the intention of standardizing SILC techniques, our ultimate goal is to use the Ewha Method to establish SILC as the standard surgical procedure for benign gallbladder disease, similar to how CMLC replaced open cholecystectomy.

METHODS

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Ewha Womans University Seoul Hospital (No. SEUMC 2025-06-036-002), and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Patient selection and study design

This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement.

We reviewed all data for patients who underwent SILC following the opening of Ewha Womans University Seoul Hospital in February 2019 through to August 2024 (1,242 patients). For this study, we only included patients who underwent SILC by one senior surgeon who had performed over 10,000 cholecystectomy procedures, including more than 1,000 cases of SILC, or by one junior surgeon who had performed over 300 cholecystectomy procedures but had no prior experience of SILC before the study period. Sixty-three patients who underwent concurrent surgeries involving collaboration with other departments were excluded.

To compare the surgical outcomes between the junior and senior surgeons, we analyzed data collected between November 2023 and August 2024. Additionally, to assess the learning curve encountered by the junior surgeon, we separately analyzed the data for all patients who underwent SILC by the junior surgeon between March 2023 and August 2024. This dataset was divided into patients who underwent SILC in the early phase (March 2023 to February 2024; the first year of SILC experience) or in the late phase (March 2024 to August 2024). Thus, although the overall study period spans from March 2023 to August 2024, the comparison groups differed according to the specific analysis.

We conducted a comprehensive review to analyze variables including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), use of antithrombotic agents, prior abdominal surgery, comorbidities, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status (PS) classification, preoperative percutaneous transhepatic gallbladder drainage (PTGBD) status, preoperative laboratory findings, histopathologic diagnoses, conversion to CMLC or open surgery, operation time, estimated blood loss (EBL), postoperative hospital stay, and the incidence of moderate or severe complications. Difficult surgery (DS) was defined as cases requiring conversion to CMLC or open surgery, or if the surgery exceeded the cutoff of 60 minutes. Other cases were classified as non-DS (NDS).

To identify risk factors for DS, we performed univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses. This included 338 patients who underwent surgery by 1 senior surgeon and 192 by 1 junior surgeon. For patients who underwent surgery by the junior surgeon, we compared the early phase group comprising 107 patients and the late phase group comprising 122 patients.

The Ewha Method for single-incision laparoscopic cholecystectomy

The Ewha Method for SILC begins with a transumbilical incision, approximately 1.5–2.5 cm long, to provide access to the abdominal cavity. A commercial four-channel port (Glove port, Nelis) is used to establish the working channels, allowing the instruments to be inserted. The surgical field is visualized using a 5 mm flexible camera (Endoeye flex HD 5 mm, Olympus), and tissues are manipulated using an articulating instrument (ArtiSential 5 Fenestrated Forceps, Livsmed) (Fig. 1). Compared with conventional laparoscopic instruments, an advantage of the articulating instrument is its length, which improves traction in the desired direction and means the technique is also applicable for patients with larger body frames. Additionally, it is relatively easy to bend the distal end of the instrument in the desired direction, providing significant assistance during procedures. The instruments are arranged with the left-hand articulating instrument in the 9 o’clock channel, the 5-mm flexible camera held by the assistant in the 6 o’clock channel, and the right-hand instruments, such as the dissector, right-angle Mixter forceps, scissors, cautery or energy device, and clips, inserted through the 3 o’clock channel. The 12 o’clock channel is reserved for larger tools, such as a retrieval bag, large clips, or a stapler when needed (Fig. 2).

The camera shaft is rotated towards the 9 o’clock direction, with the distal joint bent inward to optimize visualization of the surgical field. The left-hand articulating instrument is positioned to grasp the body or neck of the gallbladder, with the shaft rotated toward the 10–11 o’clock direction, and the distal joint is bent downward to stabilize the gallbladder. The right-hand instruments are inserted through the 3 o’clock channel, and the surgeon pronates their hand to prevent collisions with the camera or other instruments (Fig. 2).

Gallbladder dissection begins by using cautery or an energy device to separate the lateral aspect of the gallbladder from the liver bed, gradually progressing toward the neck while peeling away the peritoneum. The left side of the gallbladder neck is dissected in a similar fashion to expose the cystic duct, allowing the surgeon to visualize part of the hepatocystic triangle, also known as Calot’s triangle. The cystic duct is then ligated and divided. Dissection continues along the gallbladder plate to expose the remaining portion of the hepatocystic triangle, where the cystic artery is ligated and divided. The left-hand articulating instrument is then repositioned to grasp the infundibulum, elevating the gallbladder in the 10–11 o’clock direction from below. This allows the surgeon to continue dissection to separate the gallbladder completely from the liver bed, and ultimately complete the procedure (Fig. 3).

(A) The neck of the gallbladder was grasped and retracted in the 10 to 11 o’clock direction. (B) The peritoneum was V-dissected using a hook electrocautery and an active bipolar energy device. (C) The cystic duct was isolated using a standard dissector and a right-angle dissector. (D) The cystic duct was ligated with surgical clips. (E) The infundibulum of the gallbladder was grasped, and the gallbladder was carefully detached from the liver bed.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 22.0 for Windows (IBM Corp.) and R software ver. 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Continuous variables were compared using the Student t-test. Categorical variables were compared using a chi-square test, linear-by-linear association, or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. We used the cumulative sum control chart (CUSUM) analysis method to draw learning curves for operation time. We also performed univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses to identify independent prognostic factors for DS. In the multivariable analysis, we used all significant variables from the univariate analysis. In all tests, P < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

RESULTS

Patient demographics and preoperative characteristics

A total of 530 patients were included in this study, of which 192 were managed by the junior surgeon and 338 by the senior surgeon (Table 1). The mean age of the cohort was higher in the junior surgeon group, and 42.2% were aged ≥65 years compared with 12.7% in the senior surgeon group (P < 0.001). Males accounted for 53.6% and 29.9% of patients in the junior and senior groups, respectively (P < 0.001). The prevalence of obesity (BMI, ≥25 kg/m2 or 30 kg/m2) was not significantly different between the 2 groups. Antithrombotic agents were used in 10.9% of patients in the junior group vs. 4.1% in the senior group (P = 0.002). The rates of prior abdominal surgery of 28.6% and 33.1% in the junior and senior groups, respectively, were comparable (P = 0.285). Hypertension was observed in 42.7% and 21.0% of patients in the junior and senior groups, respectively (P < 0.001), and diabetes mellitus was present in 22.4% and 18.3% of patients, respectively (P < 0.001). In the junior vs. senior groups, history of heart disease was found in 7.8% vs. 3.6% of patients (P = 0.032), and cerebrovascular accidents were reported in 8.9% vs. 1.2% of patients, respectively (P < 0.001). The ASA PS classification tended to be higher in the junior group than in the senior group (P < 0.001). The rate of preoperative PTGBD placement was 30.7% in the junior group vs. 0.0% in the senior group (P < 0.001). Preoperative laboratory findings, including elevated WBC counts and abnormal liver function tests, were more frequent in the junior group (P < 0.05).

Surgical outcomes and pathologic diagnosis

The rate of conversion to CMLC was significantly greater in the junior group than in the senior group (10.4% vs. 0.3%, P < 0.001), but open conversion was not reported in either group (Table 3). The incidence of cholecystolithiasis was not significantly different between the 2 groups (65.1% vs. 72.2%, P = 0.088), but the incidence of impacted stones was significantly greater in the junior group (36.0% vs. 19.2%, P < 0.001). The mean operation time and postoperative hospital stay were longer in the junior group (49.90 vs. 42.19 minutes, P < 0.001; 2.77 vs. 2.21 days, P = 0.001; respectively). There was only one patient with a severe postoperative complication in the junior group, comprising percutaneous drainage due to complicated fluid collection in the operation site. There were no severe postoperative complications in the senior group. Acute cholecystitis was observed in 59.9% of patients in the junior group vs. 23.7% in the senior group (P < 0.001). Gangrenous cholecystitis was also more frequent in the junior group (7.3% vs. 0.3%, P < 0.001). By comparison, chronic cholecystitis was found in 64.6% of patients in the junior group compared with 89.6% in the senior group (P < 0.001). The presence of adenomyomatosis was reported in 13.5% of patients in the junior group vs. 26.6% in the senior group (P < 0.001).

Learning curves for single-incision laparoscopic cholecystectomy by junior surgeons

We performed CUSUM analysis of the operation time to evaluate the junior surgeon’s learning curve for SILC. This analysis excluded patients who underwent procedures involving cooperation with other departments. For the overall patient cohort, the target value for the CUSUM analysis was set as the mean operation time of 50 minutes. As shown in Fig. 4A, the operation times for all patients managed by the junior surgeon from March 2023 onward were plotted sequentially. The corresponding CUSUM curve (Fig. 4B) indicates that the learning curve was overcome after 80 patients.

Analysis of difficult surgery

Among the 530 patients, 87 were classified as DS and 443 as NDS (Table 5). Patients in the DS group were older, and 37.9% were aged ≥65 years compared with 20.5% in the NDS group (P < 0.001). The DS group comprised a greater proportion of males than the NDS group (52.9% vs. 35.7%, P = 0.003). Obesity (BMI ≥25 kg/m2) was significantly more prevalent in the DS group than in the NDS group (63.2% vs. 42.7%, P < 0.001). The DS group included higher proportions of patients with hypertension, diabetes mellitus, or heart disease than the NDS group (41.4% vs. 26.4%, P = 0.005; 34.5% vs. 11.5%, P < 0.001; 11.5% vs. 3.8%, P = 0.006; respectively). The ASA classification was also higher in the DS group (P = 0.001). The DS group included higher proportions of patients who underwent preoperative PTGBD (31.0% vs. 7.2%, P < 0.001). Leukocytosis was also more frequent in the DS group (21.8% vs. 3.6%, P < 0.001; respectively). The incidences of cholelithiasis (80.5% vs. 67.5%, P = 0.016), impacted stone (61.5% vs. 16.8%, P < 0.001), and acute cholecystitis (63.2 vs. 31.6%, P < 0.001) were greater in the DS group, whereas chronic cholecystitis (85.8% vs. 54.0%, P < 0.001), gallbladder polyp (22.3% vs. 8.0%, P = 0.002), and biliary intraepithelial neoplasia (5.0% vs. 0.0%, P = 0.018) were more frequent in the NDS group. The incidence of gallbladder cancer was not significantly different between the DS and NDS groups (P = 0.417).

Logistic regression analysis for predictors of difficult surgery

We performed logistic regression analysis to investigate which predictors are associated with DS (Table 6). The univariable analyses identified age ≥65 years (hazard ratio [HR], 2.364; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.447–3.861; P = 0.001), male sex (HR, 2.024; 95% CI, 1.273–3.218; P = 0.003), BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 (HR, 2.310; 95% CI, 1.437–3.713; P = 0.001), diabetes mellitus (HR, 4.045; 95% CI, 2.382–6.871; P < 0.001), ASA PS classification ≥III (HR, 3.163; 95% CI, 1.524–6.568; P = 0.002), preoperative PTGBD (HR, 5.780; 95% CI, 3.238–10.316; P < 0.001), preoperative leukocytosis (HR, 7.457; 95% CI, 3.657–15.206; P < 0.001), impacted cholecystolithiasis (HR, 7.913; 95% CI, 4.609–13.584; P < 0.001), acute cholecystitis (HR, 3.720; 95% CI, 2.303–6.009; P < 0.001), and gangrenous cholecystitis (HR, 38.736; 95% CI, 8.566–175.161; P < 0.001) as significant predictors of DS. In the multivariable analysis, diabetes mellitus (HR, 4.045; 95% CI, 2.382–6.871; P < 0.001), preoperative PTGBD (HR, 5.780; 95% CI, 3.238–10.316; P < 0.001), preoperative leukocytosis (HR, 7.457; 95% CI, 3.657–15.206; P < 0.001), impacted cholecystolithiasis (HR, 7.913; 95% CI, 4.609–13.584; P < 0.001), and gangrenous cholecystitis (HR, 38.736; 95% CI, 8.566–175.161; P < 0.001) were independent predictors for DS.

DISCUSSION

For single-incision laparoscopic procedures to gain broader clinical adoption, the techniques must be simplified, standardized, made safe, and be learnable. The Ewha Method, which incorporates an articulating instrument and a defined port configuration, represents an innovative response to the ergonomic and technical challenges traditionally associated with SILC. This study demonstrates the safety and feasibility of the Ewha Method and supports its role as a potentially standard approach for minimally invasive gallbladder surgery.

A key strength of this study lies in its evaluation of junior and senior surgeons using the same technique. The Ewha Method consistently delivered favorable surgical outcomes across experience levels. The CUSUM analysis offers a critical perspective on the learning trajectory encountered by junior surgeons. When we evaluated all cases of SILC, the learning curve plateaued after 80 patients. By comparison, in patients without risk factors for DS, proficiency was attained after only 38 patients. This clearly demonstrates that, in the absence of complicating factors, the Ewha Method facilitates a stable and predictable learning process. These findings strongly suggest that surgeons with limited prior experience of SILC can achieve surgical proficiency relatively early when performing the procedure on appropriately selected cases.

Nevertheless, the Ewha Method is a viable approach for patients with risk factors for DS. Although these cases had longer operative times and potentially greater technical difficulty, our experience suggests that, when the inflammatory processes are medically controlled preoperatively, SILC can be safely attempted. Based on the accumulated clinical experience, we believe that of the factors that contribute to procedural difficulty, the degree of fibrosis in Calot’s triangle is the most critical determinant. However, objective quantification of this fibrosis remains elusive, limiting its utility in prospective risk stratification.

In comparative analyses of the senior and junior surgeon groups, the rate of conversion, operation time, and EBL were greater in the junior group. Additionally, within the junior group, these outcomes were greater in the late phase than in the early phase. We attribute these differences to the higher proportion of risk factors for DS, such as preoperative PTGBD, acute cholecystitis, and older age, in the junior group, especially in the late phase. This interpretation is further supported by the CUSUM curve, which displayed a secondary upward inflection after the initial proficiency threshold, indicating the ongoing impact of patient complexity on surgical performance.

Despite these challenges, the CUSUM curves for the overall cohort and no-risk cohort demonstrated a subsequent decline after the peak, suggesting that with accumulating experience, even difficult cases can be managed effectively. This reinforces the idea that the Ewha Method is applicable across a broad spectrum of clinical scenarios, provided the surgeon is equipped with appropriate training and a strategy for selecting eligible cases.

Based on these findings, we propose a pragmatic approach. For patients without suspected gallbladder malignancy, SILC using the Ewha Method should be the initial surgical strategy. If the intraoperative findings reveal dense fibrosis in Calot’s triangle that may compromise the safety of dissection or increase the risk of bile duct injury, prompt conversion to CMLC is advisable. This strategy minimizes risk while preserving the benefits of minimal invasiveness for appropriate cases.

Concerns about SILC have historically centered around incisional hernia and bile duct injury, both of which were more common in previous studies. In our cohort, none of these complications were observed during a minimum follow-up period of 6 months. We attribute this favorable outcome to the combination of a relatively small incision (1.5–2.0 cm) and enhanced operative visibility made possible by the use of an articulating instrument. This evidence supports the safety of initially attempting the Ewha Method with a low threshold for conversion, without incurring additional procedural risk.

The present study has inherent limitations due to its retrospective design, which restricted the availability of certain intraoperative and ergonomic data. As a result, several aspects require cautious interpretation.

First, although the Critical View of Safety (CVS) is widely recommended to prevent bile duct injury, the structural characteristics of the Ewha Method do not always allow for complete establishment of CVS in the traditional sense. Instead, we consistently ensured safe dissection by exposing Calot’s triangle from the right side of the gallbladder and beginning dissection above the imaginary line from Rouviere’s sulcus. Rouviere’s sulcus has been increasingly recognized in recent studies as a reliable and reproducible landmark for safe laparoscopic cholecystectomy, and we believe its use was a key factor contributing to the absence of bile duct injuries in our series [25,26]. In cases with severe fibrosis or poor visualization, where the risk of bile duct injury was judged to be high, we converted to CMLC. Nevertheless, because intraoperative CVS was not systematically documented in the records, the exact rate of CVS achievement could not be determined.

Second, we defined DS retrospectively as procedures requiring more than 60 minutes of operative time or conversion to multiport laparoscopic cholecystectomy. This approach enabled us to identify risk factors associated with challenging cases, but it differs from objective intraoperative grading systems such as the Parkland scale. Because Parkland grades were not routinely recorded in operative notes, stratification by intraoperative severity was not possible. Future prospective studies that include systematic grading could clarify how Parkland severity correlates with DS in SILC.

Third, ergonomic evaluation could not be performed. Validated tools such as SURG-TLX (surgical task load index) were not administered during the study period, and it is not feasible to reconstruct accurate, case-specific ergonomic data retrospectively without introducing recall bias. While our experience suggests that articulating instruments help mitigate ergonomic challenges, prospective studies with standardized ergonomics assessments will be needed to provide objective evidence.

Finally, the generalizability of our findings is limited by the single-center, dual-surgeon nature of this study. Multicenter research involving a wider range of surgeons, as well as prospective trials directly comparing the Ewha Method with conventional CMLC in both low- and high-difficulty cases, will be essential to establish stronger evidence for its broader adoption.

In conclusion, the Ewha Method offers a practical and standardized technique for SILC, overcoming many of the limitations associated with conventional single-incision approaches. Its relatively short learning curve in low-risk patients and its adaptability to more complex cases with accumulating experience make it a strong candidate for widespread adoption. With further validation, the Ewha Method may help position SILC as the new standard in the surgical treatment of benign gallbladder disease.
