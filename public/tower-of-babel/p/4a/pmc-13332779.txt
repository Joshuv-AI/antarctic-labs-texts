Biomarkers for Pediatric Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome: A Systematic Review
Authors: Fuxiang Yang, Jiahuan Zou, Ling Zheng, Cheng Zeng, Hongbo Xu, Jun Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivePediatric acute respiratory distress syndrome (PARDS) is a common and severe complication in critically ill children, significantly impacting both short-term and long-term outcomes. Diagnosis currently relies on the Berlin definition; however, the applicability of existing diagnostic criteria across different clinical settings remains challenging, particularly in pediatric populations, hindering early recognition and clinical implementation. Biomarkers have demonstrated potential in PARDS diagnosis, prediction, and prognosis assessment, but a systematic synthesis of existing evidence remains lacking.MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, Web of Science, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Database, VIP Database, SinoMed, and Scopus from their inception to September 12, 2025. Study quality was assessed using the Revised Newcastle-Ottawa Scale and the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (QUADAS-2). Data extraction included study characteristics, biomarker names, research objectives, sample types, sampling time points, biomarker types, PARDS diagnostic criteria, and outcome measures.ResultsA total of 4889 publications were screened, with 9 studies of moderate to high quality (score >6) ultimately included, involving 759 critically ill children (median age range: 1.8–14.7 years; 641 [63.8%] male). Nine biomarkers were identified and categorized into four mechanisms: epithelial injury (CC16), stress response (Glypican-4, nucleosomes), inflammatory response (Gal-3BP, miR-424, mirR-21, HBP, PGRN), and endothelial injury (VWF). Six biomarkers demonstrated strong predictive and diagnostic capabilities (AUC > 0.80), while the remaining three showed moderate efficacy (AUC 0.60–0.80).ConclusionAlthough the nine biomarkers demonstrate potential in PARDS assessment, their clinical utility remains inconsistent. Future large-scale, multicenter prospective validation studies are needed. Combining advanced technologies to optimize existing biomarkers and explore new candidates will enhance the prediction, diagnosis, and management of PARDS.

Introduction

Pediatric acute respiratory distress syndrome (PARDS) is a major cause of acute hypoxemic respiratory failure in critically ill children and remains associated with substantial morbidity and mortality.1–6 Beyond short-term mortality, affected children are at risk for prolonged mechanical ventilation, longer PICU stay, multiple organ dysfunction, hospital readmission, and long-term functional sequelae.5,7,8 Early recognition and biologically informed risk stratification are therefore central to improving outcomes.

At present, PARDS is still diagnosed primarily using consensus-based clinical criteria. The PALICC framework improved pediatric specificity over adult definitions by incorporating oxygenation index (OI) and oxygen saturation index (OSI), but these criteria do not fully resolve important diagnostic limitations.9–12 Chest-imaging interpretation remains subjective and shows only modest interobserver reliability, while oxygenation-based indices are influenced by ventilator settings, recruitment maneuvers, and other therapeutic interventions.9,10,13 Consequently, bedside diagnosis may not adequately reflect the underlying biological heterogeneity of lung injury.

Recent work increasingly suggests that PARDS should be viewed not as a single uniform syndrome, but as a biologically heterogeneous condition with distinct inflammatory and tissue-injury endotypes.13–16 Contemporary reviews have emphasized that biomarker patterns reflecting epithelial injury, endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, coagulation disturbance, and damage-associated molecular signaling can help refine phenotyping and support precision-medicine approaches in PARDS.13,16–18 These studies reinforce the concept that a biomarker-informed framework may complement syndromic definitions and improve clinical classification.

Biomarkers are objective and quantifiable indicators of physiological and pathological processes. In PARDS, they have potential value across the clinical continuum, including early risk identification, diagnostic support, severity stratification, prognosis assessment, and mechanistic subphenotyping.13–18 However, the existing pediatric evidence is fragmented across relatively small studies with heterogeneous populations, specimens, sampling time points, analytical platforms, and outcome definitions, which makes translation into routine care difficult.

Multiple candidate biomarkers have already been explored in PARDS, including markers of epithelial injury (eg, CC16),19 inflammation (eg, miR-424, miR-21, HBP, PGRN, and Gal-3BP),20–24 stress response (eg, nucleosomes and Glypican-4),25,26 and endothelial injury (eg, VWF).27 Nevertheless, their reported performance, intended clinical use (prediction, diagnosis, or prognosis), and mechanistic interpretation vary substantially. Accordingly, this systematic review aimed to comprehensively synthesize and critically evaluate the current evidence on biomarkers for PARDS, with the goal of informing future validation studies and supporting eventual clinical translation.

Methods

Due to significant heterogeneity among the included studies, a meta-analysis was not performed. This systematic review strictly adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and was registered in the PROSPERO international registry for prospective systematic reviews (Registration Number: CRD420251101634). Two researchers (YFX and ZJH) independently performed literature searches, study screening, eligibility assessment, quality and risk of bias assessment, data extraction, and synthesis analysis. Any discrepancies encountered were resolved through joint discussion or consultation with the corresponding author (JY).

Study Inclusion Criteria

Inclusion Criteria

Studies meeting all of the following conditions will be included: (1) Study Population: Pediatric patients aged 28 days to 18 years diagnosed with ARDS according to international guidelines such as the Berlin criteria (excluding ARDS directly caused by factors like surgery, burns, or trauma); (2) Outcome Measures: Studies reporting performance metrics of biomarkers for diagnosis, prediction, or prognosis, such as area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, etc.; (3) Study Type: Observational studies (cohort studies, case-control studies, or cross-sectional studies) to evaluate associations between biomarkers and PARDS outcomes; Interventional studies (randomized controlled trials or non-randomized controlled trials) limited to those reporting baseline or longitudinal biomarker data related to PARDS prediction, diagnosis, or prognosis; (4) Publication Date: Published after January 1, 2000; (5) Language: Chinese or English literature.

Exclusion Criteria

Studies meeting any of the following criteria will be excluded: (1) Primary conclusions based on combinations of multiple biomarkers or clinical scores (eg, SOFA score); (2) Animal studies or in vitro research; (3) Non-original study types, including reviews, conference abstracts, commentaries, case reports, and methodological articles; (4) Duplicate publications: studies reporting on the same patient cohort and biomarker data without providing additional novel information; studies with significant overlap in study populations and core findings with previously published research by the same authors, unless the subsequent study provides new biomarker data or longer follow-up periods; editorials, commentaries, or letters to the editor that rewrite or discuss the original research paper but do not present new data; (5) Studies lacking baseline characteristics or key outcome data; (6) Literature for which full-text access was unavailable.

Search Strategy and Study Screening

We collaboratively developed a systematic and comprehensive search strategy to maximize coverage of all relevant studies within this topic. Searches were conducted across the following nine Chinese and English databases: CNKI, Wanfang Data, VIP Journal Database, SinoMed, PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus. The search period spanned from each database’s inception to September 12, 2025. All retrieved literature was imported into EndNote (version 20, Clarivate Analytics) reference management software for centralized organization. Literature screening occurred in two phases: First, two reviewers independently conducted an initial screening of potentially eligible studies based on titles and abstracts. Subsequently, full texts of articles retained after the initial screening were obtained and underwent detailed assessment against predefined inclusion criteria to determine final study inclusion. Detailed search strategies and results for each database are provided in Table S1.

Risk of Bias Assessment

Cohort studies and case-control studies were assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) (0–9 points), categorized as high quality (7–9 points), moderate quality (4–6 points), or low quality (0–3 points).28 Cross-sectional studies were assessed using the Healthcare Research and Quality Authority (HRQA) 11-item scale, assigning 1 point for “yes” and 0 points for “no” or “unclear”. Total scores were categorized as low quality (0–3 points), moderate quality (4–7 points), or high quality (8–11 points).29 Randomized controlled trials were assessed using the Revised Cochrane Risk of Bias Assessment Tool 2.0 (RoB 2.0);30 non-randomized controlled trials used the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Tool.31 Diagnostic studies were evaluated using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies 2 (QUADAS-2) tool within RevMan (version 5.3.5, Cochrane Collaboration) software.32

Data Extraction

Two researchers independently extracted data using a pre-designed standardized data extraction form. Extracted information included: first author, publication year, biomarker name, study objective, sampling time point, specimen type, biomarker category, detection method, PARDS diagnosis time point, PARDS diagnostic criteria, and key outcome measures (including but not limited to area under the curve (AUC), 95% confidence interval (CI), cutoff value, specificity, and sensitivity).

Data Synthesis and Analysis

After evaluating the characteristics of the included studies, we observed substantial heterogeneity across population profiles, experimental designs, risk of bias, and outcome measures. This heterogeneity, determined qualitatively based on study characteristics rather than statistical measures such as the I2 statistic, undermined the scientific validity of quantitative synthesis methods such as meta-analysis. Consequently, this study ultimately adopted a narrative synthesis approach to qualitatively integrate evidence and elucidate the potential associations between various biomarkers and the diagnosis, prediction, and prognosis of PARDS.

Statistical Analysis

Due to significant clinical and methodological heterogeneity among the included studies in terms of study populations, sampling time points, detection methods, and outcome measures, a meta-analysis was not performed. Instead, a descriptive synthesis approach was adopted to qualitatively summarize the results. Area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, cutoff values, and 95% confidence intervals (where available) were extracted from each study, presented in tables, and subjected to descriptive comparisons. Heterogeneity was assessed qualitatively by evaluating study design, patient characteristics, biomarker type, and diagnostic criteria; effect sizes were not quantitatively pooled. The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used to assess the quality of cohort/case-control studies, while QUADAS-2 evaluated diagnostic test quality. NOS scores are presented as medians. Data extraction and tabulation were performed using Microsoft Excel and Word (version 16.104.1). No statistical software was employed for inferential analysis.

Key Findings on PARDS-Associated Biomarkers

This systematic review identified nine biomarkers associated with PARDS, categorized by their potential mechanisms. CC16 correlates with epithelial injury,19 while Glypican-4 and nucleosomes are linked to stress responses.25,26 Gal-3BP, miR-424, miR-21, HBP, and PGRN are associated with inflammatory processes.21–24,33 VWF is closely linked to endothelial injury.27

Among the included studies, eight utilized blood samples,19,21,23–27,33 and one employed sputum/alveolar lavage fluid samples.22 Five studies primarily employed enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for biomarker detection,19,23,25–27 while others utilized real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction,21,33 immunofluorescence dry-well assay,22 and co-immunoprecipitation (CO-IP).24 Nine studies employed international guidelines for ARDS diagnosis: seven used the Berlin Definition,21,22,24–27,33 one adopted the AECC guidelines,19 and another utilized PALICC.23 Notably, two studies performed serial biomarker sampling at multiple time points,19,27 while the remaining studies collected a single sample within 24 hours of PICU admission.21–27,33

Most studies (77.8% of included studies) primarily focused on PARDS diagnosis and prognosis,19,21–24,26,27 with three studies emphasizing prognostic analysis and three studies emphasizing diagnostic analysis.19,21–24,27 All studies reported biomarker AUC values (0.760–0.942), sensitivity (0.706–0.942), and specificity (0.781–0.868) ranges. Among the evaluated biomarkers, PGRN, CC16, and miR-424 demonstrated robust diagnostic performance, with AUC values consistently exceeding 0.90.19,23,33 Within the predictive biomarker category, Glypican-4 and miR-424 demonstrated comparable discriminatory capabilities (AUC > 0.80),26,33 whereas Gal-3BP exhibited limited predictive value with AUC values ranging between 0.60 and 0.80.24 Interestingly, PGRN levels showed significant correlation with disease severity,23 whereas Glypican-4 levels and miR-424 correlated with PICU mortality.26,33 Detailed characteristics of PARDS-associated biomarkers are presented in Table 2 and Figure 3. Discussion

Key Findings

This study systematically reviewed biomarkers associated with PARDS prediction, diagnosis, and prognosis. A total of nine biomarkers were identified and categorized into four groups based on their mechanisms of action: epithelial injury (CC16), stress response (Glypican-4, nucleosomes), inflammatory response (Gal-3BP, miR-424, miR-21, HBP, PGRN), and endothelial injury (VWF). Among these, PGRN demonstrated superior diagnostic performance, while Gal-3BP exhibited the lowest diagnostic and prognostic utility and involved cumbersome detection procedures, necessitating further validation for its clinical applicability.

Potential Mechanisms and Performance of PARDS Biomarkers

Epithelial Injury Biomarkers

The core feature of epithelial injury is diffuse alveolar damage (DAD), characterized by massive death and sloughing of alveolar epithelial cells (particularly type I cells), leading to severe disruption of barrier function.34,35 Diseases such as PARDS and pulmonary inflammation compromise alveolar epithelial cell integrity, thereby promoting inflammatory cell infiltration and increased oxidative stress.36,37 CC16 is an anti-inflammatory immunomodulatory protein secreted by Clara cells in the respiratory tract, capable of mitigating airway injury by maintaining the integrity and function of airway epithelial cells.38,39 Furthermore, CC16 inhibits the activity of secretory phospholipase A2 (a key enzyme in inflammatory responses), thereby reducing the release of inflammatory mediators such as prostaglandins, IL-6, and leukotrienes, and ultimately suppressing the inflammatory cascade Multiple studies have demonstrated significantly elevated CC16 levels in pediatric patients with asthma, acute bronchiolitis, and severe adenovirus pneumonia compared to controls.40–43 In PARDS, serum CC16 serves as a robust diagnostic biomarker. At a cutoff level of 16.64 μg/L, it achieves optimal diagnostic performance for PARDS with an AUC of 0.925, sensitivity of 88.18%, and specificity of 86.83%.19

Stress Response Biomarkers

ARDS and sepsis, oxidative stress-induced cellular damage and inflammatory responses play a pivotal role in disease progression.44,45 Nucleosomes and Glypican-4, as key molecules, have been demonstrated to possess strong predictive capabilities in PARDS.25,26 Elevated circulating nucleosome plasma concentrations correlate closely with PARDS mortality.25,46 Higher plasma nucleosome levels in non-survivors are associated with non-pulmonary organ failure and subsequent worsening oxygenation, demonstrating independent predictive value for mortality in multivariate regression analysis.25 Research findings indicate that circulating nucleosomes exhibit an AUC of 0.82 (95% CI 0.66–0.97), demonstrating strong discriminatory ability in predicting PARDS mortality. Glypican-4 serves as a potent prognostic marker in patients with pneumonia complicated by ARDS, where elevated plasma levels correlate closely with patient outcomes.26 At a cutoff value of 4.11 μg/L, Glypican-4 exhibited an AUC of 0.92, sensitivity of 94.2%, and specificity of 88.3%. Clinically, plasma Glypican-4 levels positively correlated with 28-day mortality.26

Inflammatory Biomarkers

The role of the inflammatory response in ARDS is critical.47–49 As a protective physiological response, inflammation aims to eliminate pathogens and repair damaged tissues by activating the immune system and releasing numerous inflammatory mediators.50 However, excessive or persistent inflammatory responses can cause alveolar damage and increased vascular permeability, thereby worsening oxygenation impairment, leading to organ dysfunction, and triggering systemic inflammatory reactions.14,47 Gal-3BP, a glycoprotein, participates in regulating intercellular interactions and immune responses.51 Studies indicate that Gal-3BP levels are elevated in children with sepsis and ARDS compared to those with sepsis alone, with an AUC of 0.76.24

HBP, released by neutrophils, is a protein playing a crucial role in inflammatory responses.52,53 Particularly in pediatric patients with sepsis complicated by ARDS, elevated HBP levels positively correlate with disease severity and organ dysfunction.22 Studies indicate that HBP levels not only effectively predict ARDS onset but also correlate with inflammatory mediators (eg, IL-6 and TNF-α) in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, demonstrating its predictive value for early diagnosis and prognosis assessment in sepsis with ARDS. In pediatric patients with sepsis complicated by ARDS, the optimal cutoff value for HBP is 27.9 μg/L, with an AUC of 0.772, sensitivity of 70.6%, and specificity of 79.3%.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are endogenous, non-coding small RNA molecules approximately 19–25 nucleotides in length. They bind to the 3’-untranslated regions of target genes through imperfect complementary sequences, exerting biological functions by inhibiting protein translation.54–56 Through this mechanism, miRNAs suppress target gene expression, thereby playing crucial roles in critical protein synthesis, neural cell development, tumorigenesis, apoptosis, and inflammation-related diseases.57–59 In ARDS, identifying miRNAs associated with this syndrome not only aids in understanding its pathophysiological mechanisms but also helps identify potential therapeutic targets.60–62 Studies indicate that miRNA-424 is downregulated in sepsis with ARDS, where it modulates alveolar epithelial cell apoptosis and inflammatory processes via the nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) pathway, playing a role in ARDS pathogenesis.20 miRNA-424 demonstrated an AUC value of 0.845 in predicting sepsis-associated ARDS, with a sensitivity of 81.8% and specificity of 84%, and was also identified as an independent risk factor in pediatric patients with sepsis-associated ARDS.33 miRNA-21 is upregulated during the acute inflammatory phase of ARDS, enhancing pulmonary cell survival. However, its overexpression may prolong inflammatory responses and exacerbate tissue injury.63 When predicting PARDS, miRNA-21 demonstrated a cutoff value of 1.54, an AUC of 0.796, sensitivity of 78.57%, and specificity of 82.65%.21

PGRN is a multifunctional glycoprotein secreted by various cells, playing a key role in physiological processes such as cell growth, tissue repair, and inflammation regulation.64–66 We note that PGRN expression significantly increases in patients with ARDS.67 This is primarily because PGRN, as a crucial inflammatory regulator, exhibits levels closely correlated with the severity of systemic inflammatory responses. Consequently, it emerges as a potential biomarker capable of sensitively and specifically reflecting the pathological state of ARDS. PGRN demonstrates substantial application potential in the clinical diagnosis of ARDS. Research data strongly supports this: when distinguishing ARDS patients from other dyspneic patients using plasma PGRN levels, its diagnostic efficacy is exceptional. Specifically, with an optimal cutoff value of 138.862 ng/mL, the diagnostic area under the curve (AUC) reaches 0.901 (95% CI: 0.856–0.949), indicating extremely high overall accuracy. Concurrently, a sensitivity of 90.4% ensures an extremely low rate of missed diagnoses, while a specificity of 78.1% indicates strong discriminatory capability. These outstanding metrics collectively demonstrate that PGRN is a biomarker with significant clinical application potential, offering robust objective evidence for the early identification, condition assessment, and prognosis evaluation of ARDS.23

Biomarkers of Endothelial Injury

Endothelial injury also plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of ARDS. As the innermost layer of the vascular wall, disruption of endothelial cell function directly impacts pulmonary gas exchange capacity, leading to increased vascular permeability. This allows proteins and fluid to leak into the alveolar spaces, forming pulmonary edema and thereby exacerbating hypoxemia.68,69 With the onset of endothelial injury, von Willebrand factor (VWF)—a key biomarker of endothelial damage—is released in large quantities into the bloodstream, reflecting the degree of endothelial cell activation and injury. In patients with PARDS, elevated VWF levels correlate closely with the severity of endothelial injury and clinical outcomes such as multiple organ dysfunction, mechanical ventilation requirements, and mortality.27 Studies indicate that plasma VWF-Ag levels on Day 1 and Day 3 are significantly higher than in normal controls. The AUC for VWF-Ag on Day 1 was 0.942, with a sensitivity of 94.2% and specificity of 83.1%. The high AUC value on Day 1 indicates that VWF-Ag can reveal severe endothelial injury early in the disease course, aiding in the identification of patients at risk for rapid deterioration. It provides highly sensitive early reflection of disease severity and effectively distinguishes between survivors and non-survivors. On Day 3, the AUC was 0.934 with 93.4% sensitivity and 81.7% specificity. Although slightly lower than Day 1, this still demonstrates VWF-Ag’s importance in disease progression. Day 3 VWF-Ag measurement assists clinicians in evaluating treatment efficacy and assessing disease progression, providing crucial evidence for determining the need for more aggressive interventions. Thus, VWF not only aids in assessing endothelial injury severity but also serves as a prognostic biomarker for PARDS, helping clinicians judge disease severity and predict outcomes. Specifically, VWF measurements on Days 1 and 3 provide critical early warning signals for mortality and disease progression. This confers significant clinical utility to VWF, enabling early identification of critically ill patients, optimization of personalized treatment plans, and ultimately improving patient survival rates and therapeutic efficacy.27

Challenges and Recommendations

Currently, the diagnosis of PARDS often relies on clinical symptoms and imaging examinations. However, these methods frequently lack sufficient sensitivity and specificity, making it easy to confuse PARDS with other respiratory diseases. Therefore, relying solely on these traditional diagnostic approaches can lead to misdiagnosis or missed diagnosis. The diagnosis of PARDS continues to face significant challenges.1,11 Although biomarkers hold considerable potential in clinical practice to support PARDS prediction, diagnosis, and prognosis assessment, their widespread adoption remains hindered by numerous obstacles. Existing PARDS diagnostic criteria and biomarker applications are limited in scope, and their accuracy and reliability in pediatric populations have not been fully validated.70 Current diagnostic criteria, such as the Berlin criteria, provide a basic clinical framework but lack pediatric-specific adjustments, limiting their effectiveness in children.1,12 Physiological characteristics, immune responses, and differences between children and adults may prevent these criteria from accurately reflecting the complex disease course in practice.71–74

Another major challenge is the immaturity of biomarker clinical application. Although biomarkers hold high diagnostic potential, most research remains preliminary, and validation for PARDS-specific biomarkers is insufficient. Existing markers often fail to comprehensively reflect the disease’s complex pathophysiology, limiting diagnostic accuracy.

Furthermore, due to the diverse etiologies of PARDS and its close association with different clinical treatment regimens, biomarker efficacy varies significantly across patient populations.75 Factors such as age, immune status, and complications influence biomarker changes, adding complexity to their clinical application. Despite these diagnostic challenges, advancing understanding of disease mechanisms and emerging technologies hold promise for improving diagnostic accuracy and reliability through multimodal approaches integrating imaging, clinical symptoms, and biomarkers. Ongoing pediatric research will further enhance early diagnosis and precision treatment for PARDS. These findings are encouraging, but several limitations of this systematic review must be acknowledged when interpreting the results.

Limitations

This review has the following limitations. First, the final number of included studies is limited, and significant heterogeneity exists across multiple dimensions—including study populations, sampling time points, and detection methods—restricting the generalizability of the results. Second, the presence of bias risks in some included studies (eg, lack of blinding, post-hoc threshold determination) may affect the reliability of the evidence. Third, most biomarkers remain in the exploratory research phase, lacking standardized testing and external validation, which limits their immediate clinical applicability. Fourth, our literature search was restricted to studies published in English and Chinese, potentially introducing language bias. Finally, due to substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity, meta-analysis was not feasible, precluding quantitative synthesis of the evidence. Collectively, these limitations determine that the conclusions of this study cannot yet directly guide clinical practice; instead, they provide priority directions and a theoretical foundation for future large-scale validation studies.

Conclusion

This systematic review provides a comprehensive synthesis of PARDS biomarkers based on their pathophysiological mechanisms—epithelial injury (CC16), endothelial injury (VWF), inflammatory response (Gal-3BP, miR-424, miR-21, HBP, PGRN), and stress response (Glypican-4, nucleosomes). Our findings reveal varying clinical utility: PGRN, CC16, and VWF demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance (AUC > 0.90), while Glypican-4, nucleosomes, and miR-424 showed strong prognostic value for mortality risk stratification. This mechanism-based categorization offers deeper insights into PARDS heterogeneity and supports the paradigm shift toward precision medicine, where biomarker panels could guide patient stratification and targeted interventions.

However, generalizability is limited by substantial heterogeneity among included studies, predominance of single-center designs, geographic concentration (6/9 studies from China), and lack of standardized detection protocols. These limitations preclude meta-analysis and immediate clinical translation.

Future research should prioritize multicenter prospective validation of promising biomarkers (PGRN, CC16, VWF, Glypican-4, nucleosomes, miR-424), standardization of detection methods, exploration of multi-biomarker combinations, integration with clinical and imaging data via machine learning, and elucidation of underlying molecular mechanisms. Achieving these goals will advance PARDS toward early diagnosis, risk stratification, and personalized treatment, ultimately improving outcomes for critically ill children.
