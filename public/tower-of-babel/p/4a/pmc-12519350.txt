New Opportunities for Health and Resilience Measures for Suicide Prevention (NO HARMS): protocol to investigate suicidal behaviours using linked multisystem administrative data
Authors: Spencer Hensley, Warren Szewczyk, Daniel Casey, Alastair I Matheson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionSuicide is a leading cause of death in the USA, and its incidence has increased substantially in recent years. Observational studies have used large, linked databases to investigate risk factors for suicide, but data generally originate from a small number of similar sources. Prevention efforts may be strengthened by linking data from diverse sources across multiple sectors. The New Opportunities for Health and Resilience Measures for Suicide Prevention project aims to develop a resource for characterising key risk and protective factors for suicide outcomes by linking state-level death records with county-level data from 12 administrative sources, including medical, legal and social service records, among others.Methods and analysisAdministrative records of death, emergency medical services, emergency department syndromic surveillance, homelessness services, psychiatric crisis services, jail stays, domestic violence referrals and Medicaid/Medicare claims will be obtained through partnerships with source agencies. Death records are available for Washington State, while all other records are available for residents of King County, Washington. Temporal availability varies, but records generally cover the years 2014 to 2023. An iterative machine learning paradigm will be used to link individuals across different records, and events will be harmonised and deduplicated.Ethics and disseminationThis study has been approved by the Washington State Institutional Review Board, and data sharing agreements have been established with each contributing data source. Descriptive information will be disseminated via public data dashboards of suicide and non-fatal suicide behaviours. Analytic studies will be disseminated in peer-reviewed scientific journal publications.Keywords: Suicide & self-harm, PUBLIC HEALTH, Health Services, EPIDEMIOLOGY, MENTAL HEALTH

Background

Suicide mortality is a leading cause of death in the USA, with increasing incidences across age groups, gender and race in the past two decades.1 In 2019, more than 1% of the US adult population was estimated to have planned a suicide attempt, and 1.4 million adults attempted suicide.2 Suicide is multifactorial, and its risk factors and antecedents span a wide range of domains.3 Nevertheless, observational research on suicide has most often linked suicidal behaviours to a single additional administrative data source, such as hospital discharge data,4 incarceration data5 or mental health service data.6 When research has linked multiple sources, they typically fall under a single domain of service utilisation, such as linking multiple healthcare utilisation sources.7 8 For example, the Maryland Suicide Data Warehouse uses health service and diagnostic data from several electronic health records (EHR) systems linked to medical examiner records, and from those EHRs also extracts information about relevant life circumstances such as housing and legal issues, but does not contain primary cross-sector event records from non-healthcare settings.9

Some cross-sector data analyses have begun to add to the understanding of suicide risk7 10 and to inform policy initatives,11 but to our knowledge, no existing studies or databases have linked data sources across multiple relevant domains such as physical healthcare, criminal/legal involvement, homelessness service utilisation, behavioural healthcare and psychiatric crisis services for the purpose of investigating population-level suicide risk.

Linking individuals across diverse data sources may strengthen research by identifying more specific populations at elevated risk for suicide-related outcomes and opportunities for tailored interventions. For example, most suicide decedents are in contact with health services in the months prior to suicide death,12 and both screening in primary care and general practitioner training are promising suicide prevention strategies.13 14 However, the effect of providing healthcare providers with contextual information about suicide risk (eg, recent jail stays) is understudied.

The New Opportunities for Health and Resilience Measures for Suicide Prevention (NO HARMS) project aims to create a resource for characterising key risk and protective factors for suicide outcomes by linking data across 12 administrative sources in King County, Washington. These sources include, among others, death records, hospital-based syndromic surveillance of non-fatal suicide outcomes, emergency medical services (EMS), homelessness services, county and municipal jail stays, domestic violence (DV) prosecution records, and Medicaid and Medicare claims. The overall aims of NO HARMS are to:

Develop an efficient and accurate method for linking administrative records relevant to suicide risk in King County, Washington.

Harmonise and deduplicate relevant constructs across sources to create a unified data resource for analysis.

Conduct descriptive and inferential analyses of suicidal behaviour and key risk and protective factors.

Methods and analysis

This study will link 13 routinely collected administrative data sources from King County, Washington related to suicide, non-fatal suicidal behaviours, or important risk and protective factors for suicidal behaviours. Data Sources

Person-level identifiers will be extracted and used for linkage. Demographic and event-level information will be extracted, assigned participant study ID numbers from the linkage, processed to create analytic tables and used for analysis. Construct definitions will be developed in collaboration with stewards from each data source. Events will be deduplicated and assigned a unique study event ID, so the same event can be identified when captured across multiple sources.

Data constructs and sources

Suicide

Suicide records will originate from three sources: Washington State death certificates, the King County medical examiner’s office (MEO) and the National Violent Death Reporting System (NVDRS). Suicide will be identified in death certificate records using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) codes (X60-X84) and cause-of-death flags from the Washington State Department of Health and in MEO records using the MEO cause-of-death determination. Records from NVDRS will include all suicides, as well as data about the circumstances of death, and life circumstances preceding death, which are ascertained through a combination of death certificates, medical examiner and law enforcement reports using standardised instruction from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).15 While NVDRS typically includes all violent deaths, we will only receive data on suicide deaths for this study.

Non-fatal suicidal thoughts and behaviours

Non-fatal suicidal thoughts and behaviours include suicide ideation and attempt, as well as self-harm when suicidality cannot be determined from the source data. These events will originate from four sources: countywide EMS, the Washington State Department of Health’s Rapid Health Information NetwOrk (RHINO) programme for emergency department syndromic surveillance, and Medicaid and Medicare claims. Suicide attempts and suicidal ideation in EMS records will be identified using the primary and secondary impressions—both of which include ’suicide attempt’ and ’suicidal ideation' as prepopulated options—as well as the chief complaint field, using keyword matching (eg, ‘suicidal’ or ‘suicide’, excluding phrases like ‘not suicidal’). The RHINO syndromic surveillance system collects healthcare encounter data from participating hospitals, emergency departments and outpatient clinics in Washington State. Events extracted from RHINO will include emergency department presentations for any of four syndromes defined by the CDC National Syndromic Surveillance Program: Suicide Ideation v1, Suicide Attempt v1, Suicide Attempt v216 and Suicide Related v1.17 These syndromes are defined within RHINO based on a combination of admit reason and discharge diagnosis (when available) and chief complaint fields. Suicide ideation and self-harm events will be identified in Medicaid and Medicare claims from ICD-10-CM codes X71-X83, T36-T65 when sixth character is 2, T71 when sixth character is 2 and T14.91; see Hedegaard et al for complete list) which do not distinguish between suicidal and non-suicidal self-harm.18

Behavioural health service events

We will extract service records from the King County Behavioral Health and Recovery Division (BHRD), which provides or funds a range of behavioural health services to residents of King County, Washington, including nearly all Medicaid-covered services. We will collect authorisations for outpatient behavioural health treatment and associated treatment encounters, involuntary psychiatric hospitalisations and encounters with the psychiatric crisis response system. From Medicaid and Medicare claims, we will extract primary care and emergency department visits related to mental health or substance use, as defined by the Washington State Department of Health.19

Criminal-legal events

All admissions to and releases from county and municipal jails within King County will be included in the NO HARMS database. Cases referred to the King County Prosecuting Attorney’s Office related to DV will be extracted, including both victim and defendant identifiers, as well as all extreme risk protective orders, which are civil orders that prevent an individual from possessing firearms.

Homelessness services and homelessness

Service encounters in King County for people experiencing homelessness will be extracted from two sources: the Healthcare for the Homeless Network (HCHN), which provides medical care, dental care, behavioural healthcare and case management services to people experiencing homelessness, and from the Homeless Management Information System (HMIS), a database which includes housing, outreach services and encounters with coordinated entry, where people experiencing homelessness are assessed and triaged for housing resources.20 Using data from these programmes, BHRD and Medicaid, we will create a binary flag to indicate people who have experienced homelessness. A binary flag representing any experience of homelessness will be used instead of a time-varying indicator because the available data cannot distinguish when a person is not experiencing homelessness from when they are not using homelessness services while still experiencing homelessness. All individuals in HMIS or HCHN will be considered to have experienced homelessness. When residential arrangement data from BHRD indicate that a person is homeless or when address information from claims data includes text such as ‘homeless’ in an address field, we will consider the person to have experienced homelessness.

Health conditions and area indicators

Chronic physical and behavioural health conditions, as defined by the Chronic Conditions Data Warehouse, will be captured from Medicaid and Medicare claims and from BHRD.21 For area indicators, the residential address information of individuals in the database will be extracted from different sources depending on the outcome of interest. For deaths by suicide, death certificate data will be used, while for non-fatal outcomes, addresses will be drawn from claims data or RHINO. This address data will be used to link individuals to census-tract-level socioeconomic indicators from the American Community Survey, including the proportion of individuals experiencing poverty, unemployment, lack of health insurance and frequent mental distress.

Demographics

Demographic data will vary across sources in terms of the characteristics recorded (eg, sex assigned at birth vs gender identity) and the ascertainment method (eg, self-report in medical record vs an external determination in death certificates). We will extract race, sex/gender, Hispanic ethnicity, veteran status, sexual orientation, disability status and date of birth from all data sources for which they are available and harmonise disagreements. Race, ethnicity, veteran status, being lesbian, gay or bisexual, being transgender and disability status will be coded such that an individual classified in at least one data source with a particular identity characteristic (eg, veteran) will be classified as such in our database. Racial identity classification will use US Census categories and will be coded as non-mutually exclusive. Sex/gender and date of birth discrepancies will be resolved based on a hierarchy of data source quality as determined by a cross-sector integrated data stewardship committee, on which stewards from multiple data sources participate and provide guidance about the nuances and use of data from their system. When discrepancies occur within the highest quality source, we will record the most commonly occurring value, or when multiple values occur with the same frequency, will randomly select a value from the recorded values. There is variability in the recording of sex and gender, with some sources capturing sex assigned at birth, while others capture self-reported gender identity. Others are not specific and may capture a combination. Thus, the construct for the present study includes a combination of these distinct concepts.

We expect some demographic characteristics to vary in their completeness, particularly sexual orientation, veteran status, disability status and being transgender, such that these fields may only be appropriate for analyses on some subpopulations within the data resource. For example, while death certificates have high completeness and low misclassification of veteran status, the same may not be true for data from other settings (eg, jail stays).22 23

Identity Linkage

To reliably identify people across different records and agencies with potentially discrepant identifier data, we will conduct an iterative machine learning identity linkage. This approach has been shown to improve linkage accuracy and reduce biases compared with probabilistic methods.24 The following identifiers will be extracted from all data sources, as available: source-specific unique identifier, first name, last name, middle name or initial, date of birth, social security number, sex/gender and address history. The training data will be purposefully assembled to ensure minimum numbers of pairs by race/ethnicity, source system, predicted match scores and data completeness. The goal will be to create a robust training dataset that includes examples of common linkage pitfalls, like double-barrel last names or missing identifiers. Tests of inter-rater reliability will be conducted on the coding of training data. Given the large volume of expected identifiers, our blocking method will rely heavily on date of birth and social security number, though these fields will vary in quality and completeness across our sources, which may result in missed links. To maximise the linkage among individuals with our outcomes of interest, we will additionally apply a deterministic match among records with suicidal behaviour. Records where first and last names match exactly and who have a record for a suicidal behaviour occurring on the same date will be linked. This includes suicide death data, which will additionally be linked across and within sources using date of death.

Event deduplication and harmonisation

Data will be deduplicated within and across data sources. First, events will be classified, in collaboration with stewards of each data source, into twenty event types: suicide death, unintentional poisoning death, other death, suicide attempt, suicide ideation, self-harm, jail stay, involuntary psychiatric hospitalisation, non-suicide EMS responses, primary care treatment for mental health, substance use, or all other needs, ED presentations for mental health, substance use, or all other reasons, homelessness services, psychiatric crisis services, extreme risk protection orders or DV case referral to the King County Prosecuting Attorney’s Office (table 2).

All events will be assigned a study event ID. Events of the same type for the same individual will share an event ID if a subsequent event begins within 1 day of a prior event ending or the events overlap. This approach aligns with the Yale Definition for identifying emergency department visits in claims data,25 in which many claims may be generated for the same ED visit, and accounts for facility transfers in the context of continuous jail or involuntary hospitalisations.

Initial data exploration has indicated that the classification of suicidal behaviours can change across data sources. For example, a person may present to the emergency department for a suicide attempt and then be hospitalised for suicidal ideation. We will therefore assign all suicidal behaviours for the same individual the same event ID when they occur within 1 day of each other, regardless of the classification. Event IDs will be available for analysts to use for deduplication, but all events will be retained in the database to permit alternative deduplication schemes and sensitivity analyses depending on the analysis being conducted.

Data management

Data will be stored in a secure Azure SQL environment where data are encrypted at rest. Data will be stored in two schemas: a named schema, which will contain identifiers and whose access will be limited to only those conducting the linkage, and a general schema which will contain the remainder of the data and be available to analysts on the study team. Study team members who do not conduct analyses will have no data access. Identifiers and events will be refreshed monthly by the study team. Data sharing, data infrastructure work and preliminary analyses began in 2024, and we expect all data sources and final data refreshes to be available by October 2025. The identity linkage and data specific to this project will be retained until the end of the study period in October 2026.

Patient and public involvement

Due to the scale and complexity of the data sources represented in this project, it is not feasible to involve panels of individual patients. All data originate from publicly funded agencies and programmes operating in King County, Washington. Data stewards representing each source were closely involved in the planning and execution of data collection, linkage and de-duplication and will support the interpretation and dissemination of results. Public experts in suicide-related work based in King County or Washington State, including clinicians, advocacy professionals and researchers, will be invited to focus groups to review data dissemination products during development, providing input as to the appropriateness, clarity and utility of the products. They will be compensated for their participation.

Privacy, ethics and dissemination

This work has been approved by the Washington State Institutional Review Board (approval #2022–140; Project title: NO HARMS). Data sharing is governed by data sharing agreements with each source of administrative data and in agreement with all applicable federal and state laws, including the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), 42 CFR Part 2, federal law governing public housing data, federal law governing criminal justice system data, and assorted state laws and administrative codes specific to certain data sources. To protect privacy, counts of less than 10 will be suppressed in all public reporting unless granted a waiver by the Washington State Department of Health. All analysts will complete standard privacy and data management training provided by Public Health – Seattle & King County (PHSKC). Data will be accessed securely via PHSKC computer systems. Given the multidimensional nature of the data, analytic code and output will be reviewed in consultation with source data stewards and subject matter experts.

Descriptive analyses will be disseminated via two publicly available data dashboards developed as part of the project, one focused on fatal suicides in King County and one focused on non-fatal suicide behaviours. These dashboards will provide population-level counts and rates of suicide-related outcomes as well as information about service utilisation and other key antecedents of suicide. Inferential and causal studies will be disseminated via publication in peer-reviewed scientific journals.

Planned statistical analyses

We plan to conduct four primary analyses. First, we will conduct descriptive analyses of suicide deaths and non-fatal suicidal behaviours in King County to publish on a publicly available online dashboard. This will include rates of suicide deaths and attempts by demographics and geography, rates of suicide over time following key events such as jail admissions and crisis response service utilisation and patterns in service utilisation following attempts. These analyses will inform local stakeholders and decision-makers about the population-level burden of suicidal behaviour, key populations and periods of high risk and the current state of service among people who attempt.

Second, we will conduct a latent class analysis (LCA) to identify distinct classes of suicide decedents based on their life experiences and service use patterns prior to death. A recent LCA identified a five-class structure among suicide decedents using national data from the NVDRS between 2003 and 2020.26 Our LCA will include all suicide decedents in King County between 2015 and 2021 using local NVDRS data linked to service encounters and diagnoses to enhance existing NVDRS indicators and measure new constructs. We will describe the degree to which local patterns match national patterns and how service data impacts LCA class structure. We will then examine how decedent demographics and suicide methods vary by class membership.

Third, we will use time-to-event methods accounting for competing risks to quantify the risk for suicide death following involuntary psychiatric hospitalisation (IPH). We will use estimated monthly incidence rates and cumulative incidence of suicide death, death due to unintentional poisoning and overall mortality after last IPH for all individuals who have an IPH record in the database. We will use Cox proportional hazard models to estimate associations between the following risk factors and suicide death among all people released from IPH in King County: demographics, homelessness service receipt, jail admissions, involuntary order type, length of involuntary hospitalisation and number of previous involuntary hospitalisations.

Finally, we will use a case-control design to estimate the association between being a victim or defendant in a DV case referred to the King County prosecutor and subsequent suicidal behaviour. Cases will include all suicide decedents in King County while controls will be drawn from non-suicide deaths and matched (4:1 controls to cases) based on factors hypothesised to be associated with DV exposure and suicide-related outcomes27 28 such as: race, ethnicity, age group and sex/gender. We will avoid matching on diagnosed mental health conditions since they may mediate these relationships and introduce bias.

Discussion

The NO HARMS data source and research project will expand knowledge about suicide outcomes by leveraging multiple linked data sources relevant to suicide risk and preventive factors. We expect this resource will provide detailed insight into utilisation patterns across a range of healthcare services, community services and criminal system involvement for people who experience suicide outcomes. It will expand existing knowledge about populations at risk for suicide and, using LCA methods, identify subpopulations of suicide decedents who may benefit from targeted intervention.

Limitations

While strengthened by its inclusion of linked multi-system administrative data, there are several limitations of the database that warrant discussion. Erroneous or missed linkages may result in important selection biases.29 Using data that were not originally collected for research purposes introduces the potential for misclassification and other measurement biases. For example, ICD-10-CM codes in the emergency department for self-harm generally fail to distinguish between suicidal and non-suicidal self-harm.18 30 Relatedly, many important factors related to suicide risk are not captured in the database, such as social support, access to lethal means and adverse life events. Some important factors like behavioural health diagnoses and primary care utilisation are only available for the subset of individuals in the database covered by Medicaid and Medicare. Demographic variables vary across sources in whether they are self-reported or assigned (eg, on death certificates) and may capture related but distinct concepts such as sex and gender. Harmonising these data may blur important distinctions, and some misclassification is likely. Finally, individuals are only included in the database by virtue of having at least one administrative record in the available data sources, limiting the potential for constructing a general population comparison group.
