Bi-allelic deleterious variants in SNAPIN, which encodes a retrograde dynein adaptor, cause a prenatal-onset neurodevelopmental disorder
Authors: Hammad Yousaf, Maayke A de Koning, Kamal Khan, Kelly L Gilmore, Mariëtte JV Hoffer, Georgios Kellaris, Sophie Lanone, Maylis Dagouassat, Farid Ullah, Phebe N Adama van Scheltema, Delphine Heron, Yline Capri, Alma Kuechler, Bernd Schweiger, Monique C Haak, Boris Keren, Frederic Tran Mau Them, Cacha MPCD Peeters-Scholte, Frank J Kaiser, Tamara T Koopmann, Hailiang Mei, Binnaz Yalcin, Christel Depienne, Neeta L Vora, Gijs WE Santen, Erica E Davis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryFetal brain anomalies identified by prenatal ultrasound and/or magnetic resonance imaging represent a considerable healthcare burden with ∼1–2/1,000 live births. To identify the underlying etiology, trio prenatal exome sequencing or genome sequencing (ES/GS) has emerged as a comprehensive diagnostic paradigm with a reported diagnostic rate up to ∼32%. Here, we report five unrelated families with six affected individuals that presented neuroanatomical, craniofacial, and skeletal anomalies, all harboring rare, bi-allelic deleterious variants in SNAPIN, which encodes SNARE-associated protein. SNAPIN is a ubiquitously expressed component of the autophagy-lysosomal pathway that catalyzes retrograde axonal transport and synaptic transmission. To investigate the role of SNAPIN in brain development, we generated zebrafish gene ablation models, which recapitulated human-relevant disease phenotypes. Two independent, genetically stable snapin mutants exhibited pre-adulthood lethality, reduced overall length, disproportionately smaller head size, and altered brain morphology. Transcriptomic profiling of snapin mutant zebrafish heads revealed an early and progressive transcriptomic shift marked by autophagy activation with concomitant downregulation of structural and neurodevelopmental genes. Assessment of brain cellular ultrastructure with electron microscopy and light chain 3 (LC3)-II immunoblotting revealed retrograde vesicle transport defects, with an accumulation of late endosomes and autophagosomes. Together, these findings support bi-allelic pathogenic variants in SNAPIN as a likely cause for a severe neurodevelopmental syndrome and expand the growing list of autophagy-lysosome pathway regulators essential for human brain development.Keywords: prenatal sequencing, SNAPIN, SNARE-associated protein, zebrafish, autophagy, dynein adapter, transcriptomic profiling, neural crest development, LC3-II accumulation, retrograde transport

Introduction

Structural anomalies are a major cause of fetal and neonatal demise. They affect 3%–5% of pregnancies that are examined with prenatal ultrasonography.1,2 Among structural anomalies, fetal brain anomalies (FBAs) are highly prevalent. FBAs affect 1–2 per 1,000 live births and are associated with up to 6% of stillbirths.3,4 FBAs can be classified into six main categories that include neuronal migration defects, neural tube defects, disorders of the posterior fossa (such as Dandy-Walker malformation), ventricular anomalies, midline defects (such as corpus callosum agenesis and holoprosencephaly), and vascular anomalies (vein of Galen malformation).5 To identify the underlying etiology, prenatal exome sequencing or genome sequencing (ES/GS) has emerged with a 32% diagnostic incremental yield over karyotyping and chromosomal microarray (CMA) for all congenital central nervous system (CNS) anomalies.6 Because of elective termination of pregnancy (ToP) or in utero demise, less than 50% of affected individuals with CNS anomalies survive until birth,7 with limited postnatal treatment options for survivors.8

Various cellular processes are critical for brain development. These molecular paradigms include cellular proliferation, differentiation, and migration; synapse formation; cell adhesion; intracellular trafficking; neuronal signaling (e.g., WNT, mTOR, Sonic hedgehog, and RAS); and autophagy (reviewed elsewhere).9,10,11 Disruption of any of these pathways has been reported to lead to FBAs. As an example, pathogenic variants in L1CAM (MIM: 308840), which encodes a cell adhesion glycoprotein, are known to cause brain anomalies including macrocephaly, corpus callosum agenesis, and cerebellar hypoplasia resulting in neonatal death.12 Additionally, MKS1 (MIM: 609883) encodes a ciliary protein, and pathogenic variants in MKS1 have been reported to cause Meckel syndrome, hallmarked by a constellation of lethal phenotypes including Dandy-Walker malformation, cerebellar hypoplasia, and corpus callosum agenesis.13 Also, NDE1 (MIM: 609449) plays a role in neuronal migration through interkinetic nuclear migration, and pathogenic variants impairing its encoded protein are known to cause in-utero-onset lissencephaly.14 Not surprisingly, the developmental complexity of the CNS contributes to the extensive genetic heterogeneity underscoring FBAs, thus compounding further the diagnostic challenges of this vulnerable population.15

As part of our ongoing efforts to employ parent-fetus ES/GS to understand the genetic basis of FBA, we implicate a critical role for SNARE-associated protein (SNAPIN; MIM: 607007) in prenatal brain development. Here, we report six affected individuals in five unrelated families that display FBAs and other structural abnormalities associated with bi-allelic variants in SNAPIN. SNAPIN is ubiquitously expressed and has a significant role in intracellular trafficking events to ensure cellular homeostasis. It is a component of the SNARE complex, which is responsible for exocytosis.16,17 SNAPIN is also part of the BLOC-1 complex, which regulates lysosomal and endosomal biogenesis.18 Furthermore, SNAPIN plays an essential role in lysosomal autophagy by enhancing retrograde vesicle transport,19,20 linking late endosomes and the dynein motor.21 We identified three putative loss-of-function and two missense variants in our cohort that segregated with disease. Zebrafish models of snapin ablation recapitulate phenotypes of affected individuals and indicate a partial loss-of-function pathobiology of the identified missense variants. Transcriptomic analysis of snapin mutant zebrafish revealed enrichment of autophagy-related pathways. Together, these findings implicate SNAPIN as a critical regulator of neuronal homeostasis and prenatal brain development.

Material and methods

Ascertainment, ES or GS, and bioinformatic analysis

We identified six affected individuals from five unrelated families (Figure 1; Table 1). All sequencing procedures were performed in a diagnostic context, except for family A, where ES was part of a research protocol. This study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill and the IRB of the Leiden University Medical Center (LUMC). We obtained written informed consent from the research participants or their legal guardians and unaffected family members. GS/ES and bioinformatic analysis were performed according to institutional-specific protocols (supplemental methods), and the cohort was unified through the gene matching platform GeneMatcher.22

RNA sequencing of human fibroblasts (family B)

We extracted total RNA from cultured fibroblasts with and without cycloheximide treatment. The translation inhibitor cycloheximide was used to inhibit nonsense-mediated decay (NMD). Briefly, skin biopsy-derived fibroblasts were grown in AmnioMAX-C100 basal medium or AmnioMAX-II complete medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to standard procedures. For isolation of total RNA, cells were cultured to 100% confluency in 2 × 75 cm2 tissue culture flasks. One culture was treated with cycloheximide (100 μg/mL) for 4–6 h. RNA extraction was performed automatically using the QIAsymphony RNA Kit (Qiagen). cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer primers and the Superscript II RNase H Reverse Transcriptase kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed on an Illumina platform using the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit, generating 150 bp paired-end reads. After sequencing, STAR was used to align the sequencing reads to the human reference sequence (GRCh38/hg38); the data were de-multiplexed, and each sample’s sequence data were aggregated into a single Picard BAM file. The Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) was used to visualize the results.

Zebrafish husbandry

Zebrafish experiments were conducted according to protocols approved by the Northwestern University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Adult fish were maintained at 28.5°C in a 14:10 h light:dark cycle under standard conditions. We obtained embryos through natural mating of either wild-type (WT) NIH, transgenic -1.4col1a1:egfp,27 or snapin mutants. Embryos were grown in egg water (0.3 g/L NaCl, 75 mg/L CaSO4, 37.5 mg/L NaHCO3, and 0.003% methylene blue) at 28.5°C under standard conditions until phenotypic endpoints (2, 3, 10, 33, 62, and 70 days post-fertilization [dpf]).

CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing of snapin

To find the zebrafish ortholog of human SNAPIN (GenBank: NP_036569.1), we used the DIOPT Ortholog Finder and identified the single reciprocal ortholog, snapin (GRCz11: ENSDARG00000062382), encoding a protein with 89% similarity and 82% identity. We used CHOPCHOP to identify single guide (sg)RNA target sites in snapin. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, we used the GeneArt precision gRNA synthesis kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to synthesize sgRNA using synthetic oligos as a template (Table S1). We used a published non-targeting sequence (scrambled [SC]) as a negative control.28 The guide RNA (SCgRNA) was ordered from IDT using the original sequence reported in the referenced study (Table S1). We microinjected embryos at the 1-cell stage with 1 nL of the sgRNA cocktail (containing 100 pg/nL sgRNA alone or in combination with 200 pg/nL Cas9 protein [PNA Bio]) into the cell using the Picospritzer III injection system (Parker). sgRNA efficiency was assessed using heteroduplex analysis in founder (F0) mutants as described.29 At 2 dpf, we extracted genomic DNA (n = 1 uninjected control [UC]; n = 4 F0 mutants) and amplified the target region with flanking primers (Table S1). The PCR product was denatured and slowly reannealed, migrated on a 20% polyacrylamide gel (Thermo Fisher Scientific), ethidium bromide stained, and visualized on the Chemi-Doc imaging system (Bio-Rad). To estimate mosaicism, we gel-extracted PCR products (QIAquick Gel extraction kit [Qiagen]), cloned them into the TOPO-TA cloning vector (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and sequenced individual colonies (n = 16 clones/embryo) using BigDye terminator 3.1 chemistry (Applied Biosystems) on an ABI 3730 sequencer.

Adult snapin F0 mosaics were outcrossed with the WT NIH line to generate heterozygous mutants, and we characterized two frameshifting mutations in exon 3: ENSDART00000090200.6 c.171_195delTAGGATCAATGAACACCAGAAGGTG (p.Cys57Trpfs∗3) (referred to as 25bpdel) and c.191_199delinsCT (p.Lys64Thrfs∗2) (referred to as 9bpdel-2bpins). Adult heterozygous mutants harboring these variants were in-crossed to obtain an F2 generation, which was used for downstream experiments in the genetically stable mutant line.

DNA extraction from zebrafish

DNA was extracted from harvested larvae or caudal fin clips by proteinase K digestion (0.2 μg/μL) for 2 h at 55°C, followed by enzyme inactivation at 95°C. From fixed larvae, we extracted genomic DNA by incubating with lysis solution containing 25 mM NaOH and 0.2 mM EDTA at 95°C for 45 min; the reaction was allowed to cool down to 4°C before adding 40 mM Tris-HCl. The resulting solution was used as template for PCR.30,31

Morpholino-based snapin knockdown

To achieve transient knockdown of snapin, we designed and synthesized a splice-blocking morpholino (MO) targeting the exon 2, intron 2 splice donor site (GeneTools). To determine MO efficiency, we injected 1 nL of MO into the yolk of 1- to 4-cell-stage WT NIH embryos (3, 6, and 9 ng/nL). At 2 dpf, we used Trizol (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to extract total RNA from embryos pooled by injection condition (n = 20 embryos/pool). The QuantiTect Reverse Transcription kit (Qiagen) was used for cDNA synthesis following the manufacturer’s protocol, and the MO target site was PCR amplified with flanking primers. b-actin amplification was used to control for RNA integrity. We extracted the RT-PCR product using the QIAquick Gel extraction kit (Qiagen) followed by TOPA-TA cloning (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Sanger sequenced individual colonies from each condition using BigDye terminator 3.1 chemistry (Applied Biosystems) on an ABI 3730 sequencer.

In vivo complementation assays in zebrafish

We obtained a Gateway-compatible WT human SNAPIN open reading frame (ORF; GenBank: NM_012437.6) vector from Thermo Fisher Scientific (clone ID: IOH3724). Using LR Clonase II (Thermo Fisher Scientific)-mediated recombination, the SNAPIN ORF was transferred to a pCS2+ destination vector and sequence confirmed to check ORF integrity. We introduced missense SNAPIN variants (encoding p.Glu49Asp [c.147G>C] and p.Arg55Trp [c.163C>T]) in the pCS2+ plasmid with site-directed mutagenesis as described.32 WT and mutant plasmids were linearized using NotI, and we transcribed RNA with the mMessage mMachine SP6 Transcription kit (Ambion) per the manufacturer’s protocol. To determine the optimum RNA dose, we injected 9 ng MO with 50, 100, and 150 pg of WT RNA, followed by brain phenotyping. Co-injection of 9 ng MO with 100 pg WT RNA was utilized for downstream experiments.

RNA isolation from zebrafish larvae

snapin heterozygous mutants (25bpdel and 9bpdel-2bpins) were in-crossed, and the resultant larvae were decapitated at 10 dpf. Heads were kept individually in TriReagent provided with Direct Zol Miniprep Kit (Zymo research cat. no. R2051) at −80°C, while tails were used for genotyping. Heads from the same genotype were pooled (10–15/pool) and proceeded for RNA extraction according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For RT-qPCR, the QuantiTect Reverse Transcription kit (Qiagen) was used for cDNA synthesis following the manufacturer’s protocol. We used SYBR green chemistry (Applied Biosystems) on a QuantStudio 3 real-time PCR machine (Applied Biosystems). The 2−ΔΔCt method was used for estimating relative gene expression, and data were normalized to b-actin as a housekeeping gene. Each sample was run in triplicate. Primers used for RT and RT-qPCR are listed (Table S1).

LC3 immunoblotting

Zebrafish larval progeny from snapin heterozygous mutants (25bpdel and 9bpdel-2bpins) were reared under standard conditions. Heads were harvested individually in ice-cold radio-immunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (50 mM HEPES [pH 7.6], 50 mM NaCl, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS, and 1 mM PMSF [Millipore Sigma]) supplemented with 1× complete protease inhibitor cocktail (Millipore Sigma), while tails were processed for genotyping after proteinase K digestion. Genotype-matched heads were pooled, homogenized, and centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was separated, and the lysate was quantified using the BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). We loaded 30 μg of heat-denatured lysate in each well of a 12% Mini-PROTEAN TGX stain-free protein gel (Bio-Rad cat. no. 4568044) for 75 min at 110 V immersed in Tris-glycine buffer (25 mM Tris, 192 mM glycine, and 0.1% SDS [pH 8.3]). Following resolution, we used a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane to transfer proteins in Tris-glycine buffer (25 mM Tris and 192 mM glycine [pH 8.3]) for 1 h at 100 V. The membrane was cut at the expected band sizes, blocked for 1 h (5% non-fat milk for anti- light chain 3 (LC3)B antibody and 5% normal mouse serum [NMS] for anti-actin), and incubated overnight in primary antibodies, anti-LC3B antibody (Abcam cat. no. ab51520, 1:1,000 of 5% non-fat milk in phosphate-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20 [PBST]), and anti-actin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology cat. no. sc-1615, 1:3,000 of 5% NMS in PBST). The next morning, membranes were washed with PBST, followed by incubation with mouse anti-rabbit immunoglobulin (Ig)G-horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (Santa Cruz Biotechnology cat. no. sc-2357, 1:4,000 of 5% non-fat milk in PBST) for detecting LC3 and mouse anti-goat IgG-HRP (Santa Cruz Biotechnology cat. no. sc-2354, 1:4,000 of 5% NMS in PBST) for detecting actin, respectively. Blots were developed using SuperSignal West Pico PLUS chemiluminescent substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) as per protocol and visualized using a ChemiDoc XRS imaging system (Bio-Rad). Band intensity was measured by the mean gray value method using ImageJ software.

Bafilomycin A1 treatment

For autophagic flux experiments, we bathed larvae in the autophagosome-lysosome fusion inhibitor33 bafilomycin A1 (BafA1; Cell Signaling Technology cat. no. 502050494) dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) at a concentration of 40 nM for 16 h prior to decapitation for tissue harvest at 10 dpf. Tails were used for genotyping, and genotype-matched heads were pooled for protein extraction.

Transmission electron microscopy of zebrafish brains

To visualize cellular ultrastructure, we decapitated snapin 25bpdel mutants and WT siblings at 70 dpf and dissected the optic tectum region of the midbrain. Tissue was fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer (pH 7.35) and post-fixed with 2% osmium tetroxide in unbuffered aqueous solution, followed by a rinse with distilled water. After post-fixation, samples were en bloc stained with 3% uranyl acetate, rinsed with distilled water, dehydrated in ascending grades of ethanol, transitioned with propylene oxide, embedded in the resin mixture of the Embed 812 kit, and cured in a 60°C oven. Samples were sectioned on a Leica Ultracut UC6 ultramicrotome. 1-μm-thick sections were stained with Toluidine Blue O, and 70 nm sections were collected on 200 mesh copper grids; thin sections were stained with uranyl acetate and Reynolds lead citrate. We used an FEI Spirit G2 microscope fitted with an Eagle 4k HR 200 kV camera.

Visualization of larval brain structures

We performed whole-mount immunostaining as described.34,35 We anesthetized larvae at 3 dpf and fixed them overnight in Dent’s solution (80% methanol and 20% DMSO). Larvae were then rehydrated in gradually decreasing amounts of methanol in PBST, followed by bleaching with 9 mL PBST + 1 mL H2O2 + 0.05 g KOH for ∼10 min (or until cleared) and 3× washing with PBST. We permeabilized larvae with proteinase K treatment (10 μg/mL in PBST) for 10 min. Post-permeabilization, larvae were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 20 min, followed by blocking (10% fetal bovine serum in 1% bovine serum albumin [BSA] in PBST) and overnight incubation with primary antibody (anti-α-acetylated tubulin: T7451, mouse, Sigma-Aldrich, dilution 1:1,000). The next day, larvae were washed 2× with 1% BSA in PBST before and after 2 h incubation with Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific cat. no. A-11001, dilution 1:500) secondary antibody. Larvae were mounted on an agarose mold, and z-stacked dorsal fluorescent images were acquired with a Nikon AZ100 microscope equipped with a pco.Panda 4.2 M camera (pco.Panda sCMOS) and controlled by Nikon NIS Elements Software.

Whole-mount TUNEL assay

We counted apoptotic cells undergoing rapid DNA degradation by terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assay as described36,37,38,39,40 using the ApopTag red in situ apoptosis detection kit (Millipore Sigma) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, at 2 dpf, we dechorionated embryos, fixed them overnight in 4% PFA at 4°C, and then dehydrated them for >2 h in methanol at −20°C. Embryos were rehydrated gradually, followed by 10 min washing with PBST. Embryos were bleached, followed by proteinase K-mediated permeabilization for 10 min. After permeabilization, we washed embryos with PBST for 3 min and then post-fixed them for 20 min in 4% PFA at room temperature. Larvae were equilibrated for 1 h with equilibration buffer and incubated overnight at 37°C with terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT) enzyme. Embryos were washed with PBST before and after 2 h incubation with anti-digoxigenin-rhodamine (Millipore Sigma) at 37°C. Imaging was performed as described above.

Head size, body length, and craniofacial patterning

To measure the effect of snapin disruption on head size, body length, and craniofacial patterning in developing zebrafish, we utilized WT NIH or transgenic -1.4col1a1:egfp embryos.27 We anesthetized larvae at 3 dpf and imaged fluorescent signals with the Vertebrate Automated Screening Technology (VAST) Bioimager (Union Biometrica) coupled with an AXIO microscope (Zeiss) with a 10× objective lens. We acquired bright-field dorsal and lateral images of larvae using a Prosilica GX camera (Allied Vision Technologies) operated through VAST BioImager software v.1.2.6.7 with a 60% similarity threshold to our template image. A GFP-positive signal from the ventral aspect was captured using an Axiocam 503 mono camera (Zeiss) controlled with ZenPro software (Zeiss) as described.37,40,41,42 For the juvenile stage analysis, we anesthetized snapin 25bpdel mutants and WT zebrafish at 62 dpf and captured lateral bright-field images.

Zebrafish brain histology

snapin 25bpdel mutants and WT siblings at 70 dpf were decapitated and fixed in 4% PFA for 48 h, followed by decalcification in 0.5 M EDTA (pH 7.8) for 6 days at room temperature. EDTA was replenished twice in 6 days. After decalcification, samples proceeded for tissue processing using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Excelsior AS instrument, where samples were rehydrated in increasing concentrations of ethanol, cleared using xylene, and infiltrated with wax. Embedding and sectioning into 5 μm sections were done using a Thermo Fisher Scientific HISTOSTAR and Thermo Fisher Scientific HM325 manual microtome. Slides were stained on an automated Thermo Fisher Scientific Gemini AS Stainer. Briefly, slides were baked for 20 min at 63°C, followed by xylene treatment for clearing (3× for 3 min each), and rehydration in 95% ethanol for 1 min. Slides were rinsed in running tap water and stained with hematoxylin for 3 min. Slides were then dipped in differential media (for 3 s), bluing agent (for 1 min), and 95% ethanol (30 s). Slides were exposed to eosin for 2 min and 15 s, followed by 3× treatment with absolute ethanol and xylene (1 min each treatment) before covering sections with a coverslip. Imaging was done using a Hamamatsu NanoZoomer S210 digital slide scanner.

Image analysis

Images were analyzed with Fiji. To evaluate relevant brain structures (optic tecta, cerebellum, and intertectal neurons), we measured the averaged dorsal fluorescent signal outlining both optic tecta and the cerebellar area and counted the intertectal neurons joining both optic tecta at the midline (Figure 3A). We measured the head area as marked in larvae (Figure 3B). In juvenile fish, we measured the entire lateral head area by outlining the gills, mandible, and dorsal aspects of the head; we measured body length from the most anterior point of the head to the attachment of the caudal fin. The ceratohyal cartilage angle was measured on fluorescent ventral images as shown in Figure 3C. TUNEL images were used to quantify apoptotic cells in the head area, excluding the eyes (region of interest, Figure 3D), and were analyzed through image-based tool for counting nuclei (ICTN, UC Santa Barbara) plugin available in ImageJ (NIH). While analyzing, we kept the following parameters: width of 12, minimum distance of 10, and threshold of 1.0, detect dark peaks enabled after converting the images to 8-bit and inverting colors. For histology, we used NDPview2 to analyze stained sections.

Statistical analysis

For all statistical analyses, we used GraphPad Prism 9 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). All data were normalized to the UCs in each experiment. Comparisons of different experimental conditions and controls were done by one-way ANOVA followed by the Tukey’s multiple comparison test.

RNA-seq of zebrafish

Two independent RNA-seq experiments were performed to investigate the transcriptomic impact of snapin disruption. In the first experiment, embryos obtained from the in-crossing of heterozygous adults carrying the 9bpdel-2bpins snapin allele were genotyped at 3 dpf using the Zebrafish Embryo Genotyper (ZEG), an automated system for rapidly extracting DNA from live embryos and larvae. Genotype-confirmed animals were then raised to 33 dpf, at which point they were decapitated, and total RNA was extracted from individual heads (n = 3/genotype). In a second, independent experiment, heterozygous snapin adults were in-crossed, and larvae at 10 dpf were decapitated and genotyped; genotype-matched heads (20 per group) were pooled prior to total RNA extraction (n = 2 pools/genotype). For both experiments, the stranded mRNA-seq was conducted at the Northwestern University NUSeq Core Facility. Briefly, total RNA samples were evaluated for quality using RIN scores generated from an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100. RNA quantity was determined with a Qubit fluorometer. The Illumina Stranded mRNA Library Preparation Kit was used to prepare sequencing libraries from 100 ng of high-quality RNA samples (RNA integrity number [RIN] > 9.5). The kit procedure was performed without modifications. This procedure includes mRNA purification and fragmentation, cDNA synthesis, 3′ end adenylation, Illumina adaptor ligation, library PCR amplification, and validation. The Illumina HiSeq 4000 sequencer was used to sequence the libraries with the production of single-end, 50 bp reads at a depth of 20–25 M reads per sample. Bioinformatics analysis of the data was performed using standard pipelines (supplemental methods).

Results

Affected individuals harboring SNAPIN variants have overlapping features

Family A (Figure 1; Table 1) is a Pakistani-American family identified through an ongoing fetus-parent trio ES research study in the United States.43,44 The proband was identified in the first pregnancy of a healthy consanguineous couple. Ultrasound examination performed at 22 weeks of gestation detected ventriculomegaly, cerebellar hypoplasia, flexion contractures, bilateral clubfeet, and abnormal facial profile (Table S2). The pregnancy ended in intrauterine fetal demise (IUFD) at 26 weeks of gestational age. Karyotype and CMA were normal. Research-based trio ES on amniocyte-derived genomic DNA followed by agnostic variant filtering revealed candidates in 13 genes (Tables S3 and S4); we prioritized the sole bi-allelic nonsense change in SNAPIN (c.91G>T [GenBank: NM_012437.6] [p.Glu31∗]) that was validated by Sanger sequencing and confirmed to be inherited from each parent (Figure 1A; Tables 1 and S4).

Family B was identified in the Netherlands and had an unremarkable family history. Healthy consanguineous parents presented with their third pregnancy for a routine ultrasound scan; imaging at 19- and 22-week gestation showed cerebellar dysplasia, ventriculomegaly, and bilateral clubfeet (Figures 2A–2E; Tables 1 and S2) progressing into right-sided schizencephaly, a severely hypoplastic cerebellum, and a small thoracic circumference. Based on these findings, the parents decided on ToP at 23 weeks of gestation. Prenatal ES was performed on both parents and fetal DNA derived from amniotic fluid; we identified a homozygous variant impacting a canonical splice acceptor site in SNAPIN: c.144−1G>A (GenBank: NM_012437.6) (p.?) (Figures 1A and 1B; Tables 1 and S4). To investigate the consequences of the putative mRNA splice-altering variant, we performed RNA-seq on total RNA extracted from cultured fibroblasts originating from the fetus and observed exon 2 skipping and NMD of variant transcripts (Figure 1C).

Family C, a consanguineous French couple, had two consecutive affected pregnancies (Figure 1; Table 1). The first pregnancy featured ventriculomegaly, cerebellar hypoplasia, brainstem dysgenesis, corpus callosum agenesis, and lissencephaly. Ultrasound also identified a cleft palate, flexion contractures, skin edema, and bilateral clubfeet. A subsequent pregnancy showed overlapping clinical features in addition to ventriculomegaly (Table S2). ES was performed on genomic DNA from both parents and on amniotic fluid from both pregnancies; we identified a bi-allelic putative truncating variant in SNAPIN, c.112C>T (GenBank: NM_012437.6) (p.Gln38∗), that segregated with disease in the family (Figures 1A and 1B; Tables 1 and S4). The parents opted for elective ToP in both pregnancies. In addition, this couple had a previous ToP due to hydrocephaly, for which no genetic diagnostics were performed.

The proband in family D is an 8-year-old girl with a bi-allelic missense variant in SNAPIN, c.147G>C (GenBank: NM_012437.6) (p.Glu49Asp) (Figures 1A and 1B; Tables 1 and S4). At her last clinical assessment, she displayed significant encephalopathy, abnormal movements, severe microcephaly (−8 SD), and bilateral cataract with microphthalmia. A postnatal MRI showed hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, delayed myelination, and general atrophy. In addition to the SNAPIN variant, she also harbors a homozygous variant of uncertain significance (VUS) in RNPC3 (MIM: 618016): c.1388G>T (GenBank: NM_017619.4) (p.Gly463Val) (Table S3). Pathogenic variants in RNPC3 are known to cause isolated growth hormone deficiency.45,46 The available prenatal information was notable for intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) in the proband, so RNPC3 was suspected to contribute to this aspect of the clinical presentation. Both variants segregate with disease; notably, the proband has one healthy sibling who is heterozygous for the SNAPIN and RPNC3 variants.

The family E proband was born to healthy consanguineous parents (Figure 1A; Table 1). Her last clinical assessment was at 1 year of age, but she has a history of prenatal ultrasonography showing enlargement of the left lateral ventricle, an enlarged cisterna magna, cerebellar hypoplasia, and moderate ventriculomegaly. Brain MRI at birth confirmed the existence of a complex brain malformation including microcephaly with simplified gyral pattern, polymicrogyria, a thin corpus callosum with bilateral enlargement of lateral ventricles, pontocerebellar hypoplasia, and periventricular white matter abnormalities suggestive of mixed subcortical heterotopias (Figures 2F–2R). ES identified a homozygous SNAPIN variant, c.163C>T (GenBank: NM_012437.6) (p.Arg55Trp) (Figures 1A and 1B; Tables 1, S3, and S4). She has two reportedly healthy siblings; segregation analysis did not detect the homozygous SNAPIN variant in either of them. We noted that this variant has been reported in another affected individual with an overlapping clinical presentation by Karaca et al.26 (Table 1).

Taking these results together, we observed a constellation of brain and non-CNS anomalies across the cohort of six individuals segregating bi-allelic SNAPIN variants (Table 1). Notably, ultrasound imaging as early as 11- to 13-week gestation showed unremarkable fetal anatomy, but brain anomalies and clubbed feet were detected on 17- to 21-week scans (families A, B, and C; Figures 2A–2E; Table S2). Brain anomalies included ventriculomegaly (5/6), cerebellar hypoplasia or atrophy (5/6), complete or partial corpus callosum agenesis (4/5 reported and 1 unreported), and abnormal head size (3/5 reported and 1 unreported). The most prevalent additional anatomical features included bilateral clubfeet (4/6) and abnormal craniofacial morphology (4/5 reported and 1 unreported), such as micrognathia and/or retrognathia and cleft palate. Four affected individuals, all of whom harbored putative truncating or mRNA splice-altering variants, did not survive beyond the perinatal period due to IUFD (1 individual), elective ToP (2 individuals), or neonatal death (individual), while the two individuals with rare missense variants survived past infancy (Table 1).

The combination of a relatively specific clinical signature coincident with rare variants in SNAPIN in multiple unrelated families suggested that there was a common molecular etiology.47 All variants were rare in population databases; two were absent, and the others had a frequency of <0.0001 and were not present in homozygosity (gnomAD v.4.1.0; Table 1). The nonsynonymous variants in our cohort include putative protein-truncating variants (n = 2), a splice-site variant (n = 1), and missense variants (n = 2) localized to exons 1 and 2, affecting two of three predicted domains of SNAPIN (Figures 1B and 1D). All the variants were classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic according to the ACMG classification48 (Table S4). We observed evolutionary conservation of the amino acid residues altered by the two missense variants among vertebrates (Figure 1E). Additionally, mice lacking Snapin have been reported to show neonatal lethality caused by a reduction in cortical plates and intermediate zone cell density.49,50 Snapin−/− cortical neurons exhibited reduced viability in high-density culture due to lysosomal accumulation, impaired retrograde transport, and autolysosome clearance.21 Our overlapping clinical and genetic data, together with data from the Snapin mouse model, supported the candidacy of deleterious variants in SNAPIN as a likely cause for a prenatal-onset neurodevelopmental syndrome.

Disruption of snapin in zebrafish

To investigate the role of SNAPIN in early development and establish relevance to phenotypes observed in our human cohort, we generated zebrafish disease models. We used reciprocal BLAST to identify a single ortholog of human SNAPIN on zebrafish chromosome 19: snapin (GRCz11: ENSDARG00000062382; 89% similarity and 82% identity human vs. zebrafish protein). SNAPIN is ubiquitously expressed in humans (GTex Portal), while in zebrafish, multiple RNA-seq datasets report high expression of snapin from fertilization at least until 5 dpf.39,51 Similar to humans, zebrafish snapin expression does not appear to be spatially restricted, as indicated by RNA in situ hybridization in one cell to pectoral (pec)-fin stages with a predominant signal in the brain (Zebrafish Information Network [ZFIN]). To target snapin, we used CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing; we synthesized two sgRNAs targeting exon 3 (Figure S1A) and co-injected each independently with Cas9 protein in 1-cell-stage embryos. To test the efficiency of sgRNAs, injected embryos were grown until 2 dpf, and genomic DNA was extracted for heteroduplex analysis.29 We estimated the efficiency of both sgRNAs to be >90% in F0 mosaic mutants (Figures S1B and S1C). Since both sgRNAs were equally efficient and targeted a proximal region, all downstream experiments were performed with sgRNA1 (referred to as sgRNA hereafter).

snapin mosaic mutants display disease relevant phenotypes

We and others have shown that zebrafish are a robust model to investigate neuroanatomical abnormalities and to establish the relevance of a candidate gene to clinical outcomes.34,35,38,52,53,54 To study the impacts of snapin disruption, we injected WT embryos with sgRNA alone and in combination with Cas9 and performed immunostaining with anti-acetylated tubulin antibody to mark neuronal axon tracts. Consistent with the human phenotypes, we observed significant reduction in the optic tecta area (<16% vs. UC; p < 0.0001), cerebellum area (<13% vs. UC; p < 0.0001), and intertectal neuron count (<15% vs. UC; p < 0.0001), which are established proxies for microcephaly, cerebellar hypoplasia, and corpus callosum agenesis, respectively34,35,55 (Figure 3A and 3E–3G; Table S5). Additionally, we measured head size from lateral bright-field images of F0 mosaic mutants. In agreement with a reduced optic tectum size, we also observed a small head (p < 0.0001 vs. UCs) (Figures 3B and 3H; Table S5). Importantly, larvae co-injected with a SCsgRNA control with Cas9 were indistinguishable from UCs (Figures S1D–S1F).

Since we observed craniofacial and skeletal anomalies in our human cohort, we next evaluated anterior patterning in snapin F0 mosaic mutants using the -1.4col1a1:egfp27 transgenic line with fluorescent cartilage structures. At 3 dpf, we measured the ceratohyal cartilage angle in the pharyngeal skeleton from ventral fluorescent images. We detected a mild but statistically significant increase in ceratohyal cartilage angle in crispants compared to UCs (p < 0.01 vs. UCs) (Figures 3C and 3I; Table S5).

Prompted by the significant reduction in larval anterior structure size, reduced viability of Snapin-deficient cultured neurons,21 and increased cell death in Snapin−/− embryonic mouse brains,50 we wondered if apoptosis could account for these defects in F0 snapin mosaic mutants. We performed a whole-mount TUNEL assay on WT zebrafish larvae at 2 dpf that were injected with sgRNA alone or in combination with Cas9 protein and counted the number of TUNEL-positive cells within a normalized area of interest between the eyes on dorsal images. In larvae injected with sgRNA+Cas9, we observed a 2-fold increase in the number of TUNEL-positive cells compared to larvae that received sgRNA alone or UCs (p < 0.0001 vs. UCs or sgRNA alone) (Figures 3D and 3J; Table S5). We conclude that the reduced size of anterior neuroanatomical structures can be attributed at least partly to increased apoptosis.

Homozygous snapin mutants show pre-adulthood lethality and abnormal brain histology

To assess the developmental anomalies and overcome the genetic heterogeneity of F0 mosaicism, we established and characterized genetically stable snapin mutants. We injected WT embryos with sgRNA+Cas9 and reared them under standard conditions until adulthood. Adult founders were outcrossed with the WT NIH line, and the resulting F1 progeny were screened for frameshifting germline mutations. We characterized two mutations in exon 3: ENSDART00000090200.6, c.171_195delTAGGATCAATGAACACCAGAAGGTG (p. Cys57Trpfs∗3) (referred to as 25bpdel) and c.191_199delinsCT (p.Lys64Thrfs∗2) (referred to as 9bpdel-2bpins) (Figure S2A). To test if these variants impact mRNA splicing, we in-crossed F1 heterozygotes, pooled genotype-matched animals, and performed RT-PCR to monitor snapin. We did not observe any unusual splicing or activation of cryptic splice sites for either mutant allele (Figures S2B and S2C). RT-qPCR on both mutants using two primer pairs flanking the snapin mutation sites showed that the mutant transcript at least partially survives NMD (Figures S2D and S2E).

We in-crossed F1 heterozygotes to obtain isogenic F2 homozygous mutants and subjected them to anti-acetylated tubulin immunostaining (9bpdel-2bpins mutant) and head size measurement (25bpdel mutant) at 3 dpf. Phenotyping followed by genotyping of individual larvae revealed expected Mendelian ratios (25bpdel, 21 homozygous mutants:42 heterozygous:21 WT; Figure 4A; 9bpdel, 19 homozygous mutants:40 heterozygous:12 WT; Figure 4B), but we did not detect any significant morphometric differences between mutants and WT siblings (Figure S3; Table S5). We suspected that the maternal snapin transcript from heterozygous F1 females might compensate for the expected early neurodevelopmental phenotypes. We attempted to obtain homozygous mutant adult females by in-crossing heterozygous mutants and allowing progeny to grow in standard conditions. However, genotyping at 75 dpf showed skewing of expected Mendelian ratios such that we did not find any homozygous mutants for either variant. To investigate mutant survival longitudinally, we genotyped the F2 generation at regular intervals and found that all 9bpdel-2bpins homozygous mutants had died by 60 dpf, while 91% of the 25bpdel mutants had died by 60 dpf (Figures 4A and 4B).

To further assess morphological consequences in surviving juveniles, we imaged 62 dpf 25bpdel mutants and WT siblings and quantified body length and head size from lateral bright-field images. snapin mutants show autophagic stress marked by accumulation of autophagic vacuoles and increased LC3-II

SNAPIN is known to interact with dynein, a cytoskeletal motor protein that facilitates autophagosomal trafficking in neurons from distal axons to the soma. Here, mature lysosomes are localized, thus bringing lysosomes and autophagosomes in proximity to fuse and maintain efficient autophagy-lysosomal function.21,50 We hypothesized that an abnormal autophagic stress response might contribute to the lethality observed in snapin zebrafish mutants and that we would be able to detect membrane-bound autophagic structures. We examined intracellular ultrastructure in 25bpdel mutant brains because this line showed survival beyond larval stages but with severe gross morphological abnormalities at 70 dpf. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) of the optic tectum from homozygous mutants (n = 3) showed an abnormal accumulation of double-membrane-bound vacuolar structures enclosing both cellular debris with high electron density and organelles such as mitochondria (Figure 5A). These observations were consistent with previously reported models of disrupted autophagy.21,56,57,58

To confirm this observation, we probed an autophagy marker, microtubule-associated protein 1A/1B-LC3, in whole-protein lysates from zebrafish heads. LC3 is a soluble cytoplasmic protein that is lipidated with phosphatidylethanolamine, forming LC3-II before being recruited to the autophagosomal membrane.59,60,61 We performed immunoblotting of lysates harvested at 70 dpf and observed an increase in LC3-II in 25bpdel mutants compared to WT siblings (5.5-fold; Figure 5B). To establish whether this disruption in autophagy initiated during larval development, we evaluated pools of genotype-matched progeny at 3, 6, and 10 dpf by immunoblotting. We observed a similar increase in LC3-II in 25bpdel mutants compared to WT siblings, primarily at 10 dpf (1.5-fold; Figure 5B). Next, we asked whether similar pathognomonic changes could be detected in homozygous 9bpdel-2bpins mutants, and we observed a 1.6-fold increase in LC3-II in mutants compared to the WT at 10 dpf (Figure 5B). These data raised the question of whether the increase in LC3-II is due to abnormally augmented autophagy induction or reduced clearance of autophagosomes. We treated progeny of 9bpdel-2bpins mutants in-crossed with 40 nM of BafA1 (known to inhibit fusion of autophagosomes and lysosomes)33 or DMSO only for 16 h prior to harvesting for immunoblotting at 10 dpf. We observed that the treatment with BafA1 increased LC3-II in the WT compared to a statistically significant change in homozygous mutants (Figure 5C). This suggested that the increase in LC3-II is due to reduced clearance of the autophagosomes rather than increased induction of autophagy. Together, our TEM and biochemical data across two different snapin mutant lines support a progressive larval-onset accumulation of autophagic vacuoles in the brain.

Transient snapin suppression models mimic F0 crispant phenotypes

We sought to reproduce the F0 mosaic zebrafish mutant phenotypes in an independent system. We synthesized an MO targeting the exon 2, intron 2 splice donor site of snapin. We injected the MO in WT embryos and harvested total RNA at 2 dpf, followed by monitoring of mRNA splicing by RT-PCR; we confirmed exon 2 skipping in morphants compared to controls, resulting in a frameshift and putative premature truncation (Figure S5A). Next, we injected WT embryos with increasing doses of MO (3, 6, and 9 ng) and immunostained for acetylated tubulin at 3 dpf. Consistent with our F0 mutant data, we observed a dose-reliant reduction in cerebellar area (9 ng: 10% vs. UC; p < 0.001), optic tecta size (9 ng: 30% vs. UC; p < 0.0001), and intertectal neuron count (9 ng: 20% vs. UC; p < 0.0001) (Figures S5B and S5C; Table S5). We rescued the phenotype by co-injecting 9 ng MO with three increasing doses of WT human SNAPIN RNA (50, 100, and 150 pg) and observed significant amelioration for all conditions tested, with 100 pg WT RNA showing optimal rescue efficiency (p < 0.0001 vs. MO for all measurements) (Figure S6; Table S5). Further, we asked whether we could detect a head area reduction and broadened ceratohyal angle in transient gene knockdown models similar to the F0 mosaics. We recapitulated both phenotypes in morphants compared to UCs (9 ng: p < 0.0001 vs. UCs), which we rescued by co-injecting 100 pg human WT SNAPIN RNA (head area: p < 0.0001 vs. MO; ceratohyal cartilage angle: p < 0.01 vs. MO) (Figures 6A, 6B, 6D, and 6E; Table S5). Moreover, and comparable to the F0 data, we performed TUNEL staining and observed a >3.5-fold increase in the number of cells undergoing apoptosis in a defined anterior region of interest in morphants vs. UCs (p < 0.001); this was rescued by co-injection of human WT SNAPIN RNA (p < 0.0001 vs. MO) (Figures 6C and 6F; Table S5).

In vivo complementation assay suggests that missense variants identified in affected humans are pathogenic

Among the allelic series of SNAPIN variants identified in our cohort, there are two rare homozygous missense variants: p.Glu49Asp and p.Arg55Trp. The latter was reported previously by Karaca et al. in an affected pediatric individual with intellectual disability, sensorineural polyneuropathy, hypotonia, cerebellar hypoplasia, microcephaly, and cortical atrophy.26 The p.Arg55Trp change is classified as likely pathogenic in ClinVar.26 To test the pathogenicity of these SNAPIN variants, we performed in vivo complementation assays32,55,62 and compared the ability of WT vs. variant human SNAPIN RNA to rescue the aberrant neuroanatomical phenotypes in morphants. For both variants, we observed a partial rescue that was statistically improved from MO alone (p < 0.0001 vs. MO); however, variant RNA rescue was significantly worse than WT RNA rescue (p < 0.0001 for MO + variant vs. MO + WT RNA) for each optic tecta area, cerebellar area, and intertectal neuronal count (Figures 6G–6J; Table S5). Importantly, ectopic expression of SNAPIN WT or variant RNA alone was statistically indistinguishable from UCs for all three measurements of optic tecta, cerebellum, and intertectal neuronal count (Figure S7; Table S5). Taken together, our data strengthen the notion that SNAPIN p.Glu49Asp and p.Arg55Trp are hypomorphic, pathogenic variants impairing protein function.

Zebrafish snapin mutants show an altered expression profile

To investigate the transcriptomic consequences of snapin loss at later developmental stages, we conducted RNA-seq on 33 dpf 9bpdel-2bpins snapin homozygous mutants and WT siblings. Embryos were generated by in-crossing heterozygous adults, followed by genotyping at 3 dpf. Heads from three fish per genotype were harvested, and total RNA was extracted for bulk RNA-seq (Figure 7A). Principal-component analysis (PCA) showed the separation of datasets by genotype (Figure 7B). Differential gene expression analysis (FDR-corrected p < 0.05) identified 3,736 upregulated and 3,073 downregulated genes in snapin mutants compared to the WT (Table S6). Pathway analysis revealed enrichment of upregulated genes involved in ribosome biogenesis, metabolic processes, proteasomal degradation, and oxidative stress responses, including KEAP1-NFE2L2 signaling and MAPK signaling (Figure 7C; Table S7). These changes indicate the activation of proteostasis and autophagy pathways, possibly as compensatory responses to cellular stress. In contrast, downregulated pathways, particularly those associated with cytoskeleton organization, extracellular matrix remodeling, and cell cycle regulation (Figure 7C; Table S7), indicate potential defects in neuronal connectivity and neuromuscular function. Notably, disruptions in Rho GTPase signaling and immune response regulation underscore SNAPIN’s critical role in maintaining nervous system integrity,63 aligning these findings with neurodevelopmental defects observed in human SNAPIN-related disorders (Figure 7C; Table S7).

To capture early transcriptomic changes that precede overt neurodevelopmental phenotypes, we also performed RNA-seq at 10 dpf on the same snapin mutant allele (Figure S8). Heterozygous adults were in-crossed, and progeny were decapitated at 10 dpf for two biological replicates per genotype. RNA extraction from genotype-matched WT and homozygote heads was followed by bulk RNA-seq analysis (Figure S8A). Differential expression analysis (FDR-corrected p < 0.05) revealed 255 upregulated and 117 downregulated genes between homozygous mutant and WT transcriptomes (Table S8). Pathway enrichment analysis identified upregulation of genes related to apoptosis, lysosome, MAPK signaling, glycolysis, and the HSP90 chaperone cycle, highlighting early activation of cellular stress and metabolic pathways (Figure S8B; Table S9). Downregulated genes were enriched for type I hemidesmosome assembly, neural crest development, amide transport, and cholesterol metabolism (Figure S8B; Table S9).

To validate these early transcriptomic alterations, we performed RT-qPCR on 10 dpf snapin mutant and WT heads, confirming the upregulation of four autophagy-related genes and the downregulation of two neural crest-associated genes (Figures S8C and S8D). These findings indicate that despite the absence of pronounced morphological differences at 10 dpf (Figure S3), snapin mutants exhibit biologically relevant transcriptional disruptions that persist and evolve into later developmental stages. Together, these results highlight the dynamic transcriptional consequences of snapin loss and their potential impact on neurodevelopment, providing insight into the molecular mechanisms underlying the neuroanatomical defects observed in SNAPIN-related disorders.

Discussion

We report six affected individuals from five unrelated families with rare bi-allelic variants in SNAPIN, a gene previously implicated in synaptic vesicle trafficking and autophagy. Across the cohort, we observed a consistent and penetrant neurodevelopmental phenotype comprising ventriculomegaly, cerebellar hypoplasia, corpus callosum agenesis, abnormal head size, and abnormal craniofacial and skeletal features, including bilateral clubfeet (Table 1). Notably, affected individuals with putative truncating or splice-disrupting variants exhibited severe syndromic disease, which was detectable on ultrasound as early as 17-week gestation and did not survive the perinatal period. However, two individuals with rare homozygous hypomorphic missense variants survived into early childhood with milder, albeit still profound, neurodevelopmental impairment. This genotype-phenotype correlation, supported by the absence of homozygous loss-of-function variants in SNAPIN in large population databases (gnomAD v.4.1.0; accessed May 2025), implicates SNAPIN as causative for a recessive prenatal-onset neurodevelopmental syndrome. Identification of additional affected individuals with bi-allelic SNAPIN variants will be required to formalize more detailed genotype-phenotype correlations and to further refine the temporal dynamics of the phenotypes as they emerge during gestation.

To further validate the role of SNAPIN in human disease, we functionally modeled the disorder in zebrafish using multiple complementary approaches. CRISPR-Cas9-generated snapin mosaic mutants and genetically stable mutant lines exhibited features recapitulating the human phenotype, including reduced brain size, cerebellar and tectal hypoplasia, abnormal craniofacial development, increased apoptosis, and lethality prior to adulthood. These findings were consistent with prior observations in Snapin-null mice, which show early embryonic lethality (embryonic day [E]14.5), cortical thinning, and impaired autophagy.50

snapin zebrafish models allowed us to overcome embryonic lethality and visualize dynamic developmental consequences at later stages, including musculoskeletal abnormalities, scoliosis, reduced head size, and disrupted brain architecture (Figures 4 and S4). These data contrast with the lethality observed in Snapin-deficient mice. This disparity likely reflects differences in maternal mRNA buffering in zebrafish and species-specific thresholds of functional SNAPIN protein required during development. Such exemplars of species disparity have been reported previously; for example, SETD2 (MIM: 612778) encodes a catalyst of trimethylation at H3K36a, and constitutive knockout mice display lethality in the embryonic period,65 while setd2 mutant zebrafish are viable and fertile.66 While we were unable to compare protein levels directly across species due to a lack of a zebrafish-specific snapin antibody, our results demonstrate that zebrafish serve as a tractable system for dissecting the pathophysiological roles of SNAPIN in vivo.

Mechanistically, our data reinforce the emerging understanding of SNAPIN as a critical effector of retrograde axonal transport and autophagy. SNAPIN serves as a linker between dynein motor proteins and vesicular cargo, facilitating lysosome-autophagosome fusion.21 Consistent with this role, we observed an accumulation of double-membraned autophagic vacuoles and increased LC3-II levels in snapin mutant zebrafish, indicative of impaired autophagosome clearance rather than increased autophagic flux. These defects were already evident by 10 dpf and worsened over time, suggesting progressive impairment of autophagic capacity. The presence of markers such as LC3-II and the increased apoptosis in both mosaic and transient models point to autophagic stress as a contributing factor in disease pathology.

Intracellular cargo trafficking is vital for neuronal homeostasis, survival, and organization due to the highly polarized structure and metabolic demands of neurons. Retrograde transport, mediated by a single form of cytoplasmic dynein,67,68,69 contrasts with the anterograde system, which utilizes ∼40 kinesin motor proteins.70 Dynein’s functional versatility arises from its interaction with various adaptor proteins (e.g., dynactin, BICD2, and hook proteins).71,72 Disruption of this transport system has been linked to neurological disease, with pathogenic variants in ∼20 transport-related genes. Despite their ubiquitous expression, impairment of the encoded adaptor protein leads predominantly to neurological phenotypes.10 We and others have reported Mendelian associations for pathogenic variants in several retrograde transport components, including DYNC1I2 (MIM: 603331 and 618492),34 NDE1 (MIM: 614019 and 605013),10,73 DCTN1 (MIM: 601143), BICD2 (MIM: 609797), DYNC1H1 (MIM: 600112), and LIS1 (MIM: 607432),10 all implicated in neurodevelopmental disorders. We now add pathogenic variants in SNAPIN to this emerging class. Given its role in retrograde transport essential for neurotrophic signaling,49 we propose that SNAPIN dysfunction impairs synaptic plasticity and leads to autophagic vacuole accumulation, as shown by elevated LC3-II levels and vacuolar buildup (see Figures 5 and S9 and reports from others).49,50,74

Transcriptomic profiling of snapin mutant zebrafish at both early (10 dpf) and juvenile (33 dpf) stages revealed dynamic and progressive molecular dysregulation, shedding light on the temporal impact of SNAPIN deficiency. Early-stage (10 dpf) mutants exhibited upregulation of lysosomal and apoptosis-related genes, while later-stage (33 dpf) profiles showed increased expression of stress-response pathways, including MAPK signaling and the KEAP1-NFE2L2 oxidative stress axis. Across both stages, we observed consistent dysregulation of genes involved in autophagy, proteostasis, and cytoskeletal organization. Notably, 33 dpf mutants also displayed altered expression of genes related to mitochondrial function, energy metabolism (including amino acid, fatty acid, and ATP metabolism), and elevated mitophagy and proteasome activity, suggesting mitochondrial stress and potential energy deficits in anterior structures. Although canonical synaptic vesicle trafficking pathways were not significantly enriched, the upregulation of selective autophagy and proteostasis pathways may indicate impaired turnover of synaptic components.75 Together, these findings reinforce SNAPIN’s essential role in intracellular transport and clearance mechanisms during development and provide mechanistic insight into the neurodevelopmental defects associated with its deficiency. Future studies, such as electrophysiology or imaging of synaptic activity, will further validate these transcriptional differences.

We noted that our cohort with SNAPIN variants shows skeletal and craniofacial anomalies such as micrognathia, retrognathia, and clubfeet. Although the role of SNAPIN in skeletal development/maintenance remains largely elusive, the impairment of the autophagy pathway has been known to cause skeletal anomalies (reviewed elsewhere).76 For example, Danon disease (MIM: 300257), a skeletal myopathy caused by loss-of-function variants in LAMP2 (MIM: 309060), is caused by impaired autophagosome-lysosome fusion, a cellular defect observed in the context of SNAPIN deficiency. Individuals with Danon disease display skeletal and cardiomyopathy along with cognitive deficits.77,78 Our zebrafish model of snapin ablation shows similar musculoskeletal anomalies, including altered craniofacial patterning, scoliosis, and disrupted muscle histology, with RNA-seq data suggesting possible perturbations in neural crest cell development, a progenitor population contributing to both craniofacial and peripheral nervous system structures. While direct evidence for a role of SNAPIN in neural crest biology is limited, prior work has shown the role of Snapin in modulating exocytosis at low calcium concentrations in mouse chromaffin cells,79,80 a derivative of neural crest cells.81,82

In summary, our study identifies bi-allelic SNAPIN variants as the likely cause of a prenatal-onset neurodevelopmental syndrome characterized by severe cerebral and craniofacial malformations. Through comprehensive modeling in zebrafish and functional validation of missense variants, we establish SNAPIN as a critical regulator of neurodevelopment, operating at the intersection of axonal transport and autophagy. These findings not only expand the genetic landscape of retrograde transport disorders but also underscore the importance of intracellular trafficking in human brain and skeletal development. Further studies will be essential to clarify the full phenotypic spectrum, molecular mechanisms, and potential therapeutic avenues for SNAPIN-related disorders.
