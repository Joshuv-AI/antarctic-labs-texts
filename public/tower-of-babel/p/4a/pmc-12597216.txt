Rational Design and Antimycobacterial Evaluation of Aryl Sulfonamide–Linked Isoniazid Hydrazones Against Mycobacterium Tuberculosis
Authors: Mukanda Gedeon Kadima, Sahil Mishra, Gobind Kumar, Pule Seboletswe, Afsana Kajee, Ankit, Françoise Roquet‐Banères, Maëlle Foubert, Laurent Kremer, Rajshekhar Karpoormath, Parvesh Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite significant advancements in antituberculosis (TB) drug discovery, considerable scope remains for novel therapeutic development. Molecular hybridization represents a promising strategy for generating new anti‐TB agents. In this study, in silico molecular docking is employed to design novel isoniazid–sulfonamide hybrids connected via a hydrazone bridge, designated as series 7j–r and 8a–i. Docking analysis reveals that these compounds interact significantly with the active site of InhA, particularly engaging the catalytic triad residues Y158, F149, and K165, as well as the cofactor NAD. Subsequently, both series are synthesized and evaluated against Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Generally, compounds from both series (7 and 8) exhibit enhanced activity compared to their precursors. Notably, compound 8a demonstrated approximately twofold greater potency ( minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) = 0.156 µg mL−1) with respect to compound 7j (MIC = 0.313 µg mL−1). However, these compounds lose efficacy against INH‐resistant M. tuberculosis strains harboring katG mutations and remain ineffective against multidrug‐resistant and extensively drug‐resistant strains of M. tuberculosis. Encouragingly, the tested compounds exhibit little cytotoxicity against the THP‐1 human monocytic cell line at a concentration of 20 µg mL−1. Additionally, the structural stability studies using 1H NMR confirm the structural integrity of these compounds. Overall, these molecular hybrids are promising for further development as anti‐TB agents after relevant structural optimizations.Keywords: flexibility, hydrazones, isoniazid, molecular docking, molecular hybrids, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, rigidity, sulfonamides

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is a communicable disease that has affected humans for millennia despite significant medical advancements.[ 1 ] According to a 2024 report by the World Health Organization, 10 million people developed active TB, and 1.3 million died from the disease, making it one of the leading infectious causes of death globally.[ 2 ] One of the major contributors to this global health threat is the emergence of multidrug‐resistant TB (MDR‐TB), characterized by Mycobacterium tuberculosis strains such as M. tuberculosis H37Rv‐INHR and M. tuberculosis H37Rv‐RIFR that are resistant to the first‐line anti‐TB drugs isoniazid (INH) and rifampin (RIF).[ 3 ] This burden of TB resistance is the consequence of irrational use, overuse, misuse, and noncompliance with the existing antimycobacterial drug regimens.[ 4 ] As a result, TB caused by drug‐resistant M. tuberculosis does not respond to first‐line anti‐TB drugs, including INH and RIF.

Bacterial fatty acids play an indispensable role in their growth and survival because they serve as both an energy source and a vital component of the cell membrane. These fatty acids are synthesized de novo and are crucial for membrane fluidity and maintaining structural integrity.[ 5 ] The mycobacterial type II fatty acid synthase (FAS‐II) is a validated anti‐TB drug discovery target.[ 6 ] InhA is a key enzyme, catalyzing the reduction of enoyl‐ACP to acyl‐ACP, a critical step in mycolic acid production.[ 7 ] INH, a first‐line anti‐TB drug, is activated by the catalase‐peroxydase KatG into its active isonicotinoyl radical, which then exerts its inhibitory effect by forming an INH–NAD adduct and blocking the function of InhA (Figure 1 ).[ 8 ] However, the rapid and prolonged use of INH leads to the accumulation of mutations in KatG, resulting in an INH‐resistant form.

Mechanistic representation of the first and second line InhA inhibitors.

Researchers have devised strategies to counter this challenge by hybridizing two or more known pharmacophores with a powerful synergistic effect.[ 9 ] Hydrazone derivatives of INH,[ 10 ] in particular, have shown promise in developing alternatives to reference drugs. Their chemical, physical, and pharmacological properties have generated enough scientific interest, and various studies have been published in the literature (Figure 2 ). Also, the bacteriostatic properties of sulfonamide[ 11 ] groups constitute an essential asset in medical research and are considered the first choice in the hybridization approach. The simple structure of sulfonamide derivatives facilitates the design of analogs with many biological activities, such as properties that fight malaria, cancer, fungi, bacteria, and TB.[ 12 , 13 – 14 ] For example, sulfamethoxazole is a well‐known antibiotic used to treat bacterial infections caused by gram‐positive and gram‐negative bacteria. Also, sulfamethoxazole, when combined with trimethoprim as co‐trimoxazole, has shown good activity against M. tuberculosis, particularly in immunocompromised patients with minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values ranging from <1–9 µg mL−1 tested against clinical isolates isolated from HIV‐infected persons. [ 15 ]

Some representative compounds bearing INH and sulfonamide moieties with potent anti‐TB activity.

One of the important approaches in the drug discovery process is structure‐based drug design,[ 16 ] which uses computational methods such as molecular docking to predict the position of small molecules within a 3D representation of the enzyme. Structural tuning is a key strategy in developing and improving the therapeutic potential of designed molecules. Key changes in size and shape influence their ability to interact with specific receptors or proteins and circumvent resistance mechanisms developed to present medications.

Building on these insights, we aimed to develop more potent INH‐based small‐molecule inhibitors of M. tuberculosis through InhA inhibition. Recognizing that hydrophobic residues and NADH occupy the large cavity of the enzyme InhA, we employed molecular docking to design (Figure 3 ) substituted aryl sulfonamides linked to INH hydrazones 8 (a– i) along with their structurally tuned 7 (j– r) analogs as potential anti‐TB agents. These compounds were then synthesized, and structurally characterized using different spectroscopic techniques. Their anti‐TB activity was then evaluated against both INH‐susceptible and INH‐resistant M. tuberculosis strains harboring katG mutations. Further, the compounds were also evaluated against MDR and extremely DR (XDR) strains. Additionally, the cytotoxicity profile of the most potent compounds was assessed using human THP‐1 macrophages, and their structural stability was confirmed via NMR analysis.

Results and Discussion

Molecular Docking Studies

Computational approaches like molecular docking studies can explore enzyme crystal coordinates, analyse enzyme–substrate interactions, and build novel chemical entities. It is well established that the substrate binding site of the enzyme InhA is large and occupied by hydrophobic residues[ 17 ] like P99, F97, K165, K118, I202, A198, Y158, and F149, F97. Also, Y158, F149, and K165 amino acid residues play an essential role in orienting the nicotinamide ring of NADH toward the natural enoyl substrate, facilitating its reduction by NADH. Hence, based on the above notion, the hybridization of INH with a hydrophobic phenyl group tethered by hydrophobic sulfonamides in compounds 7 and 8 was planned. Nine representative compounds 8 (a– i) and 7 (j– r) having different connectivities were flexibly docked using an induced fit molecular docking algorithm (IFD) in the InhA active site (pdb id: 4TZK).

Compounds 7, which when docked in the enzyme InhA active site, oriented themselves in a wider U‐shaped geometry and showed hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions with the surrounding residues, including M98, A198, R43, M199, Y158, and NAD (Figure 4 ). For example, compounds 7j, 7k, 7l, 7n, 7o, and 7p with respective docking scores of −9.912, −7.12, −10.1, −8.9, −7.5, and 10.4 Kcal mol−1, interacted hydrophobically with F149, Y158, M199, and NAD, and through H‐bonding with A198, R43, M98, P99, Y158. Given the critical role of hydrogen bonding in governing the specificity and stability of ligand–receptor interactions, we aimed to design compounds capable of forming H‐bonds with multiple active‐site residues, particularly the key catalytic triad (Y158, K165, and F149). To achieve this, a new series of compounds (8) was strategically designed to adopt a horseshoe‐shaped conformation within the enzyme's active site for efficient interactions. As anticipated, the series 8 compounds exhibited significantly more H‐bonding interactions (Figure 5 ) than those of series 7. For example, the structurally optimized compound 8c (docking score: −10.45 kcal mol−1) exhibited five hydrogen bonds with key residues Tyr158, Phe149, Pro99, and the cofactor NAD, owing to its closer U‐shaped conformation within the InhA active site. In contrast, its structural analog 7l formed only two hydrogen bonds, limited to Met98 and NAD. Similarly, compound 8a formed hydrogen bonds with NAD, Met98, Phe149, and Pro156, surpassing its analog 7j, which interacted only with Pro99. Compounds 8b, 8d, and 8e also demonstrated enhanced hydrogen‐bonding interactions. Consequently, both series 7 and 8 were synthesized (Scheme 1 ) and subsequently tested for their anti‐TB activity against INH‐sensitive and INH‐resistant strains of M. tuberculosis.

Chemistry

The synthetic route to structurally tuned compounds 8 (a– i) and 7 (j– r) is depicted in Scheme 1. Initially, N‐sulfonylation of compounds 2 and 3 resulted in precursors 5 (j– r) and 4 (a– i), respectively. These precursors were then refluxed with INH in ethanol in the presence of a catalytic amount of acetic acid to give the final compounds 8 (a– i) and 7 (j– r).

The structures of the synthesized compounds were confirmed through NMR (1H and 13C), infrared (IR), and high‐resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) techniques. For instance, the HRMS of precursor compound 4f showed a sodium adduct peak at m/z = 312.056 [M + Na]+ while its 1HNMR spectrum showed two sharp singlets at δ 2.34 ppm and δ 2.54 ppm corresponding to two methyl groups and a broad singlet at δ 7.95 for sulfonamide proton (NH). The final compounds 8 (a– i) and 7 (j– r) were obtained as a mixture of E and Z isomers, as confirmed by their NMR (1H and 13C) analyses. For example, compounds 8f and 7p peaked at δ 10.90–11.00 for CONH, corresponding to the E isomer. Compound 8f also showed a mass adduct at m/z = 409.1344 (M + H)+ in HRMS.

The presence of the carbonyl and imine linkage in final compounds 8 (a– i) and 7 (j– r) was ascertained through their 13C spectra, which displayed characteristic signals in the ranges of δ 163.0–162.7 ppm for the carbonyl carbon and δ 148.8–147.1 ppm for the imine carbon. Similarly, the IR spectra of compounds 8 (a– i) and 7 (j– r) show characteristic sulfonyl stretching vibrations in the range of 1100–1400 cm−1, along with N—H and C=O stretching bands in 3400–3200 cm−1 and 1670–1650 cm−1, respectively, further supporting the assigned structures of these compounds. 1H NMR, 13C NMR, Fourier transform infrared (FTIR), and HRMS data of the final compounds are provided in the and the mutants resistant against Supporting Information.

Biological Studies

In Vitro Activity Against INH‐Susceptible M. tuberculosis

Compounds 4 (a– i), 5 (j– r), 8 (a– i), and 7 (j– r) were then evaluated for their in vitro anti‐TB activity against M. tuberculosis mc26230 in Middlebrook 7H9 medium supplemented with 10% OADC, 109 μM pantothenic acid, and 0.025% tyloxapol at 37 °C using INH as a reference drug. The MIC values for the final compounds (7j–r and 8a–i) are summarized in Table 1 , whereas those of the precursor compounds (4ai, 5j–r) are presented in the supplementary data (Table S1, Supporting Information). For the 4 (a– i) series, compounds 4a, 4b, 4d, and 4e exhibited an MIC value of 200 µg mL−1, while compounds 4f, 4g, 4h, and 4i showed an MIC value of >200 µg mL−1. Interestingly, compound 4c was the most active with a MIC value of 100 µg mL−1. Similarly, for the 5 (j– r) series, compounds 5j, 5n, 5o, and 5p were the least active, showing an MIC value of >200 µg mL−1, whereas compounds 5k and 5q exhibited an MIC value of 200 µg mL−1. However, a ≈2‐fold improvement in the anti‐TB activity for compounds 5l and 5m was observed (MIC = 100 µg mL−1). Surprisingly, among both precursor series (4 and 5) lacking the INH moiety, compound 5r with a para‐methoxy substitution emerged as the most active, exhibiting an MIC value of 1.563 µg mL−1.

The significance of the INH‐hydrazone linkage in the anti‐TB drug design is evident by comparing the MIC data of precursor compounds 4(a– i) and 5 (j– r) (Table S1, Supporting Information) with those of the final compounds 8(a– i) and 7(j– r). The MIC values indicate that INH‐hydrazone associated with substituted aryl sulfonamides 8(a– i) and 7(j– r) displayed better activity than their precursors. For example, compounds 8c and 7l displayed a multifold improved activity (MIC = 0.156 µg mL−1) compared to their INH‐free precursors 4c and 5l (MIC = 100 µg mL−1). Similarly, compounds 8e, 8f, 7n, and 7o (MIC = 0.313 µg mL−1 each) demonstrated markedly superior activity when compared to their precursors 4e, 4f, and 5n and 5o (MIC ≥ 200 µg mL−1).

Overall, the compounds 8 (a– i) and 7 (j– r) bearing INH‐hydrazone‐sulfonamide linkages in loop conformations interacted efficiently with the surrounding key residues and NADH present in the active site of the enzyme InhA, resulting in superior anti‐TB activity.

Structure–Activity Relationship (SAR) Analysis

A detailed analysis of the biological results indicates that the anti‐TB activity of the molecular hybrids is influenced by factors such as electron density, the hydrazone linkage, and the structural orientation around key residues and NADH in the active site of InhA.

In the case of the precursors 4 (a– i) and 5 (j– r), the presence of electron‐donating and electron‐withdrawing groups attached to the phenyl ring of aryl sulfonamides, regardless of their positioning, displayed moderate to low activity with MIC values ranging from >200 to 100 µg mL−1. For example, compounds 4b and 4e, 5k and 5n, having electron‐withdrawing bromo and nitro groups at the para position of the phenyl ring, respectively, displayed a MIC value of 200 µg mL−1. Their trisubstituted analogs bearing an OH group at ortho and chlorine atoms at meta positions (4d and 5m) also showed low to moderate activity (MIC = 100–200 µg mL−1). Surprisingly, the placement of the methoxy group at the para position offered the most active compound (5r) of this series with an MIC value of 1.563 µg mL−1.

A significant improvement in the anti‐TB activities of the final INH‐hydrazone hybrids 7 (j– r) and 8 (a– i) was observed compared to their precursors 5 (j– r) and 4 (a– i). This enhancement can probably be attributed to the presence of INH‐hydrazone linkage, which increased their hydrophobicity, enabling more efficient interactions with the hydrophobic amino acid residues in the enzyme's active site. In certain cases, compounds from series 8 exhibited improved MIC values compared to their structural analogs in series 7. For instance, compound 8b, bearing a para‐bromo substitution, displayed a ≈2‐fold improved activity (MIC = 0.156 µg mL−1) compared to its position isomer 7k (MIC = 0.313 µg mL−1). A similar improvement in MIC activity was observed for 8a (MIC = 0.156 µg mL−1) compared to 7j (MIC = 0.313 µg mL−1). However, a multifold improvement in the anti‐TB activity of compound 8d (MIC = 0.313 µg mL−1) compared to 7m (MIC = 1.563 µg mL−1) was observed. This enhancement, partly attributed to the ortho‐hydroxy and meta‐dichloro substitutions, suggests that compound 8d may be better accommodated within the enzyme's active site compared to compound 7m. Concurrently, compounds 8a, 8b, 8d, 7j, 7k, and 7m exhibited significantly higher activity than their respective precursors, 4a, 4b, 4d, 5j, 5k, and 5m, highlighting the importance of the hydrazone linkage and the INH moiety in enhancing their anti‐TB activity. Notably, compounds 8c and 5l, featuring a chloro substitution at the para position, showed similar MIC values of 0.156 µg mL−1, irrespective of their orientation within the enzyme's active site. However, these compounds were markedly superior to their precursors 4c and 5l (MICs = 100 µg mL−1). In both the final hybrids and their precursors, hydrazone linkage exerted a greater influence on anti‐TB activity than the presence of electron‐donating or electron‐withdrawing groups. This is evident in compounds with para‐nitro (8e and 7n), para‐methyl (8f and 7o), and para‐methoxy (8i and 7r) groups, all of which showed a multifold improvement in activity compared to their respective precursors in series 5 and 4. However, these compounds had a similar MIC of 0.313 µg mL−1 activity in either of the observed conformations.

Overall, although docking studies predicted para‐substituted aryl sulfonamides to be potentially superior inhibitors compared to their meta‐substituted analogs owing to their more folded conformations and favorable interactions with both NADH and key acidic residues in the InhA active site, experimental MIC data did not reveal a consistent trend favoring para‐substitution. Several meta‐substituted analogs, including compounds 7l, 7n, 7o, 7q, and 7r, exhibited comparable activity with MIC values of 0.313 μg mL−1. Conspicuously, compound 7p (MIC = 0.156 μg mL−1) demonstrated superior activity to its para‐substituted counterpart 8g (MIC = 0.313 μg mL−1). These findings suggest that the SAR for this series is multifactorial and not solely governed by conformational preferences.

Compounds 8e, 8f, and 8g exhibited potent activity against the INH‐susceptible M. tuberculosis mc26230 strain, with MIC values of 0.313 μg mL−1. However, they showed a marked loss of efficacy against the katG mutant strains, with MIC values exceeding 20 μg mL−1 corresponding to a >64‐fold increase in resistance. Similarly, the reference compound (INH) displayed strong activity against M. tuberculosis mc26230 (MIC = 0.03 μg mL−1), but its potency significantly declined against the katG mutants, with MIC values exceeding 50 μg mL−1, as expected.

Overall, the compounds were highly active against M. tuberculosis mc26230 but lost efficacy against the katG mutant strains, indicating that, similar to INH, their antimycobacterial activity depends on bioactivation by KatG.

In Vitro Activity Against MDR and XDR Strains of M. tuberculosis

The anti‐TB effectiveness of compounds 4a–i, 5j–r, 8a–i, and 7j–r was also evaluated through experiments conducted on MDR and XDR strains. MDR‐TB was conducted on RIF‐resistant and INH‐resistant clinical strains with mutations in katG and InhA, while XDR‐TB was done on a fluoroquinolone‐resistant clinical strain that had a mutation in GyrA and was characterized phenotypically as resistant to bedaquiline but susceptible to linezolid. As per the MIC data (see Supporting information), compounds were found to be ineffective against both the MDR and XDR strains, with MIC values in the range of 50–100 µg mL−1. In the case of precursors 4a–i and 5j–r, compounds 4a, 5j, 5q, and 5r showed MIC values of 50 μg mL−1 against the XDR strain, much lower than the reference drugs such as INH, Moxifloxacin, and RIF (Table S1, Supporting Information). The presence of hydrazone linkages slightly improved the biological activity in the final compounds 8a–i and 7j–r, where compounds 7n, 7o, 7p, 7q, 8g, 8h, and 7r displayed the MIC value of 50 µg mL−1 against XDR strains, irrespective of the presence of electron‐donating or electron‐withdrawing substituents on the aromatic ring and their orientation inside the enzyme active site.

In Vitro Cytotoxicity Studies

Cytotoxicity analyses of 8 (a– i) and 7 (j– r), including INH (control drug), were carried out in a cell viability assay using human THP‐1 macrophages incubated for 24 h or 72 h with increasing drug concentrations. At the highest concentration tested (20 μg mL−1, much higher than their respective MICs), most compounds had little effect on cell survival after 24 h or 72 h of exposure (Figure 7 ).

Stability Studies of Compound 7p

Solvent effects and the acidity or basicity of the medium can influence the stability of imine bonds. Such effects may lead to hydrolysis of the imine, reverting the compound to its precursor components and potentially resulting in misinterpretation of biological activity. To rule out the possibility that the observed anti‐TB activity was due to the INH moiety or hydrolysis products, we investigated the stability of the most active compound, 7p, in DMSO using 1H NMR spectroscopy.

For the stability study, 10 mg of compound 7p was dissolved in DMSO‐d6, and 1H NMR spectra were recorded at 0 (t0), 4 (t4), 8 (t8), and 24 (t24) h. As shown in the overlaid spectra in Figure S2, Supporting Information, no changes in chemical shifts or the appearance of new signals were observed over the 24 h, indicating that compound 7p remained chemically stable in DMSO. These results confirm the structural integrity of the compound during the assay period and support the reliability of the observed anti‐TB activity.

Conclusion

In this study, molecular docking revealed that para‐substituted aryl sulfonamides (8a–i) adopt a more folded, U‐shaped conformation within the InhA active site compared to their meta‐substituted counterparts (7j–r), forming a stronger hydrogen‐bond network with key amino acid residues and the cofactor NADH. These compounds were subsequently tested against wild‐type M. tuberculosis mc26230 and three katG mutant strains. Among them, compounds 8a, 8b, 7l, and 7p exhibited enhanced anti‐TB activity against the parental strain, comparable to INH, suggesting that conformational preference alone does not dictate activity. Other factors, such as electronic effects and solvation, may also play important roles. However, these compounds lost efficacy against katG mutants and were inactive against MDR and XDR clinical isolates, as well as the M. tuberculosis H37Rv strain. Notably, all tested compounds exhibited low cytotoxicity toward human macrophages. Overall, these molecular hybrids demonstrate promising anti‐TB potential and can be further structurally optimized to develop novel therapeutic agents with improved pharmacological profiles.

Experimental Section

Chemistry: Experimental

All the chemicals utilized to synthesize the molecular hybrids were purchased from Merck, Sigma‐Aldrich, and CRD, and were further used without any purification. Thin‐layer chromatography (TLC) 60 F254 silica gel plates were used to monitor the reaction progress. A Bruker Avance‐III spectrometer (600 MHz) was used to record 1H and 13C (150 MHz) NMR spectra of the compounds. The multiplicities are reported as follows: singlet (s), doublet (d), doublet of doublets (dd), multiplet (m), triplet (t), broad singlet (brs), and coupling constant (J) in Hz. IR spectra were obtained on Perkin Elmer Spectrum BX FT‐IR (Beaconsfield, UK) and reported in the range of 4000–400 cm−1 using an ATR accessory (Pike Technologies) with a diamond crystal. The high‐resolution mass analysis spectra were obtained using Waters Micromax LCT Premier TOF‐MS instrument using a 1–2 ppm sample concentration. Column chromatography was performed on silica gel (60–120 mesh).

Chemistry: General Procedure for the Synthesis of 4 (a–i) and 5 (j–r) (Procedure A)

4‐aminoacetophenone (2) or 3‐aminoacetophenone (3) (500.0 mg, 1.0 mmol) was mixed with benzene sulfonyl chloride substitutes (1) (1.0 mmol) in the presence of pyridine (1.5 mmol) in dichloromethane (DCM) (8 mL). The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for 3 to 4 h. After the completion of the reaction (through TLC), it was quenched with water and then extracted with ethyl acetate and washed using water three to four times, and finally dried over sodium sulfate. The organic layer was concentrated under vacuum, and the crude product was purified through column chromatography using hexane‐ethyl acetate as eluent to procure products 4 (a– i) and 5 (j–r).

Chemistry: General Procedure for the Synthesis of 8 (a–i) and 7 (j–r) (Procedure B)

A mixture of appropriate phenyl benzenesulfonamide substitutes 4 (a–i) and 5 (j–r) (200.0 mg, 1.0 mmol) and INH (6) (1.2 mmol) was refluxed with a few drops of acetic acid in 4 mL of ethanol for 4 h. After completion of the reaction (TLC), the reaction mixture was cooled, and the precipitated solid was filtered and washed with 10 mL of warm ethanol and 10 mL of water. The precipitates were left to dry to give compounds 8 (a–i) and 7 (j–r).

Biological Assays: Culture Conditions

The pantothenate‐auxotrophic M. tuberculosis mc26230 strain[ 18a ] and the mutants resistant against INH M. tuberculosis mc26230_R1, M. tuberculosis mc26230_R2, and M. tuberculosis mc26230_R3 were ranger[ 18b ] grown in Middlebrook 7H9 broth supple15mented with 10% OADC, 0.025% tyloxapol, and 109 μM pantothenic (complete 7H9) at 37 °C without agitation.

Biological Assays: Drug Susceptibility Testing

MIC was defined as the lowest concentration of compound inhibiting 99% of bacterial growth, at which no change in turbidity was observed, and performed in 7H9 broth supplemented with 10% OADC, 0.025% tyloxapol at 37 °C without agitation. For the experiment, 109 μM pantothenic acid was added to all experiments. MIC determination was done using the broth dilution method. Briefly, a log‐phase culture was diluted to an OD600 = 0.05 in 7H9 medium and deposited in 96‐well plates. Compounds were then directly added (in general, 2 μl well−1 of a 20 mg mL−1 or 10 mg mL−1 stock solution) to the first‐row wells containing 198 μL of bacteria. Serial twofold dilutions were then completed, starting from the first row. Plates were wrapped in parafilm and then placed in a 37 °C incubator and observed after 6 days. Control wells included DMSO as vehicle control, in which bacterial growth was not inhibited (as for untreated wells). INH was included as a reference drug.

Biological Assays: MDR and XDR Testing of Compounds

The effectiveness of the new compounds was evaluated in comparison with INH, moxifloxacin, and RIF through experiments conducted on M. tuberculosis H37Rv, as well as MDR and XDR strains, which are clinically isolated. The microplate alamar blue assay was utilized for the testing at[ 19 , 20 ] Inkosi Albert Luthuli Hospital in Durban, South Africa, in the Department of Microbiology. To minimize evaporation of the medium, 100 µL of sterile deionized water was added to the outer wells of each 96‐well plate. Subsequently, 50 μL of Middlebrook 7H9 broth was added to each well, and various diluted test compounds were prepared on the plate. The tested compounds had final concentrations ranging from 100 to 0.36 µg mL−1. The experiment involved the use of drugs such as INH, rifampicin, and moxifloxacin. Each well received a 50 μL inoculum supplement. The plates were sealed with parafilm and incubated at 37 °C for 7days. After the initial incubation period, 20 μL of resazurin dye solution was added to each well, followed by another overnight incubation. A blue color indicated no bacterial growth, while a pink color indicated bacterial growth. The MIC was determined as the transition from blue to pink for the test candidates or compounds.

Cytotoxicity Assay

Human THP‐1 monocytes were grown in RPMI medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Sigma‐Aldrich) and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cells were differentiated with 20 ng mL−1 phorbol myristate acetate in a 96‐well plate (2.104 cells well−1) for 48 h and exposed to decreasing concentrations (ranging from 100 μg mL to 0.78 μg mL−1) of the tested compounds. Following incubation (24 or 72 h), 10% v/v resazurin was added to each well and left to incubate for a few hours at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Data were measured using a fluorescent plate reader (excitation 540 nm, emission 590 nm). Results are the mean of two independent experiments done in duplicate.

Computational Studies: Molecular Docking

Molecular docking was conducted using the Schrödinger suite (2022‐1 release) with calculations based on the OPLS4 forcefield. Firstly, the structure of M. tuberculosis enoyl‐acyl carrier protein reductase (3; PDB ID: 4TZK) was obtained from the Protein Data Bank. This protein was then modified by adding missing hydrogens, side chains, and loops using Prime.[ 21 ] Epik[ 22 ] was used to generate tautomers and heteroatomic states at pH 7.0 ± 2.0 and delete water molecules that are 5 Å away from the binding cavity. The hydrogen network was then optimized and minimized to a root mean square deviation of 0.3 Å. Subsequently, the binding site was minimized using Prime. Compounds for docking were drawn, saved as ChemDraw files, imported into the workspace, and prepared using the LigPrep module to generate 3D models with the lowest energy conformation. The IFD module was employed to dock these compounds into the active site of InhA; PDB ID:4TZK.[ 21 ] This process involved defining the ligand box by selecting a box using the centroid of selected residues with dimensions under 20 Å, and performing conformational sampling within a 2.5 kcal mol−1 energy range. Ligands were docked with Glide, and poses were refined by adjusting side chain conformations using Prime. Poses with energies up to 30 kcal mol−1 were redocked, and the top 10 were selected for evaluation. The best pose for each protein–ligand complex was chosen based on docking scores, IFD scores, Glide e‐model, and Glide energy.
