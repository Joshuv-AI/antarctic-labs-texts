Anti-Müllerian hormone promotes minichromosome maintenance protein expression and creatine metabolism in primate preantral follicles under hypoxia
Authors: Fuhua Xu, Xiaoxiao Zhang, Shally Wolf, Jessica Stanley, Adam J. Krieg, Jing Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

In the postnatal ovary, primordial and preantral follicles are located in the ovarian cortex, an avascular region without ready access to the circulation [1, 2]. A state of physiological hypoxia appears to be critical for primordial follicles to remain quiescent, and therefore ovarian reserve is maintained [3, 4]. However, preantral follicle growth can also be restricted due to hypoxia. Under oxygen deprivation, granulosa cells (GCs) of preantral follicles express hypoxia-inducible factor-1 (HIF-1) protein which leads to cell cycle arrest [5, 6]. Evidence suggests that, to sustain GC proliferation, preantral follicles produce minichromosome maintenance proteins (MCMs). MCMs inhibit the function and enhance the degradation of HIF-1 A which prevents GC cell cycle inhibition and promotes follicle growth toward the antral stage [7–9]. Additionally, when adenosine triphosphate (ATP) generated by mitochondrial respiration is limited under hypoxic conditions, creatine metabolism is essential for energy production to support cell cycle progression of GCs in preantral follicles [10–12]. Although hypoxia decreases creatine uptake, creatine can be produced locally by GCs to sustain ATP generation [12–14]. The reaction of glycine and arginine gives rise to guanidinoacetate which is then converted to creatine. Phosphorylation of creatine forms phosphocreatine, an energy buffer in cells, along with creatinine as a byproduct. Thus, maintaining cell cycle progression and cellular energy homeostasis is a fundamental process to match the demand of preantral follicle development in a particular ovarian niche.

Anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) is a member of the transforming growth factor beta family produced by GCs of growing follicles in the postnatal ovary. AMH action during follicular development appears to be related to its stage-specific and growth-dependent expression. After primordial follicle activation, AMH expression increases in preantral follicles with levels correlated positively with follicle diameters, a pattern observed in the ovary of rodents [15, 16], domestic animals [17, 18], nonhuman primates [19, 20], and humans [21]. In addition, studies from nonhuman primates suggest that AMH expression in preantral follicles is heterogeneous in the ovary [19, 20]. When developed in vitro, AMH levels produced by growing follicles were higher than those of no-grow follicles without significant diameter increase in culture [20, 22]. Although AMH expression is always associated with growing follicles at the preantral stage, data on the regulatory role of AMH are inconsistent among species. The stimulatory effect of AMH on preantral follicle growth in vitro has been demonstrated in rats, goats, rhesus macaques, and humans via follicle or ovarian tissue culture [23–27]. Intraovarian infusion of AMH protein promoted GC proliferation in vivo in rhesus macaques [28]. However, data from mice suggest that AMH inhibits follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH)-dependent preantral follicle development to the antral stage [15]. Molecular and cellular events regulated by AMH in GCs of preantral follicles require further investigation to delineate the underlying mechanism, especially in the large animal and human ovary with a relatively thick and dense cortex region.

Individual nonhuman-primate follicle culture offers a useful in vitro model to study the direct actions of AMH on GCs during specific stages of follicular development precluding endocrine and paracrine effects. Direct tissue measurements of ovarian oxygen in juvenile rhesus macaques revealed an approximate concentration of 5% [29], suggesting that physiological hypoxic conditions can be mimicked in vitro by culturing macaque preantral follicles at 5% oxygen. Previous studies showed that this relatively low oxygen exposure enhanced the survival, growth, and AMH production of macaque follicles developed in vitro [22, 30]. Therefore, experiments were performed to validate culture conditions for studying oxygen-dependent cellular adaptation during preantral follicle growth in nonhuman primates. This in vitro model was used to determine AMH-regulated gene expression in promoting cell cycle progression and creatine metabolism in sustaining cellular energy production under hypoxia in GCs of preantral follicles.

Methods

Animal use

The general care and housing of rhesus macaques (Macaca mulatta) was provided by the Division of Comparative Medicine, Oregon National Primate Research Center (ONPRC), Oregon Health & Science University (OHSU), as previously described [22]. Briefly, macaques were pair-caged in a temperature-controlled (22 °C), light-regulated (12 L:12D) room. Diet consisted of monkey chow (Nestlé Purina PetCare, St. Louis, MI, USA) twice a day supplemented with fresh fruit or vegetables once a day and water ad libitum. Animals were treated according to the National Institutes of Health (NIH)’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Protocols were approved by the ONPRC Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Protocol number: IP00000401).

Physiological hypoxia in the ovary

Ovary pairs were collected from four adult female macaques (6–12 years old) at necropsy by the ONPRC Pathology Services Unit via the Tissue Distribution Program. Euthanasia was due to reasons unrelated to reproductive health [25]. To assess physiological hypoxia status of follicles, one ovary from each of the four animals was processed for immunohistochemistry, as previously described [20], to examine the expression of carbonic anhydrase IX (CA-IX) [31]. Briefly, ovaries were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde-phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at 4 °C overnight, dehydrated in ascending concentrations of ethanol, and embedded in paraffin. Five-micrometer sections were cut, deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through a graded series of ethanol and PBS, and incubated in 3% hydrogen peroxide/60% methanol. Adjacent sections were incubated at 4 °C overnight with mouse anti-human CA-IX antibody (1:1000, MA5-15737) and mouse IgG1 isotype control (negative control, MG100) from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Sections were then incubated with biotinylated anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody, processed using a VECTASTAIN Elite ABC-HRP Kit (PK-6102, Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, USA), and counterstained using hematoxylin. Visual assessment of staining intensity was performed using an Olympus BX50 microscope (Olympus Corporation of the Americas, Center Valley, PA, USA).

Hypoxia status in cultured follicles

To establish an in vitro model mimicking physiological hypoxia status, the other ovary from each of the four animals described above was used for follicle isolation and culture in a low oxygen environment as previously described [22, 26]. Briefly, preantral follicles (diameter 125–225 μm) were mechanically isolated and cultured individually in 200 µl media in 96-well ultra-low attachment plates (Corning, New York, USA) at 37 °C and 5% oxygen. Culture media contained alpha minimum essential medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 15 mIU/ml recombinant human FSH (Merck & Co., Rahway, NJ, USA), 6% (v/v) human serum protein supplement (CooperSurgical, Trumbull, CT, USA), 0.5 mg/ml bovine fetuin, 5 µg/ml insulin, 5 µg/ml transferrin, 5 ng/ml sodium selenite, and 10 µg/ml gentamicin (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA) [32]. Follicles from each of the four animals were randomly assigned to culture groups (4–6 follicles/animal/group) with either control media only or 1-hour incubation in 2 µM Image-iT Green Hypoxia Reagent (I14833, Thermo Fisher Scientific), a fluorescent reporter for cellular hypoxia [33], followed by control media culture. After 2 days of culture, bright-field and fluorescent images were captured using a Lumascope 620 microscope (Etaluma, Carlsbad, CA, USA) housed in a 2730B Hypoxic Glovebox chamber setting to 5% oxygen (Coy Laboratory Products, Grass Lake, MI, USA). Fluorescent images were also obtained using a Leica SP5 AOBS confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). The objective PL APO CS 63 × 1.3 GLY UV was used to collect Z-stack data sets with images taken every 0.5 μm [34].

Follicular AMH and creatine production under hypoxia

Six adult female macaques (6–10 years old) that exhibited regular menstrual cycles were assigned to the present study. Ovary pairs were collected via laparoscopic oophorectomy at early follicular phase, i.e., cycle day 1–4, as previously described [25]. To assess heterogeneous AMH expression and creatine production, preantral follicles (diameter 125–225 μm, 48 follicles/animal) were isolated from one ovary of each of the six animals for individual culture in control media at 5% oxygen as described above. Media (100 µl) was collected and replaced on culture day 2 and then every other day during 5 weeks of culture. Follicles were photographed on culture day 2, and then weekly to evaluate follicle survival and antrum formation. “Surviving” follicles, i.e., follicles surviving 5 weeks of culture, were categorized as “growing” or “no-grow” follicles based on whether or not antrum formation was observed at week 5, respectively [32]. Diameters on culture day 2 of surviving follicles were measured using Image J software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) [26]. Media samples of surviving follicles collected on culture day 2 (25 µl) were analyzed for AMH concentrations using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (AL-105, Ansh Labs, Webster, TX, USA) by the ONPRC Endocrine Technology Support Core [20]. The remaining media samples (75 µl) were pooled for no-grow and growing follicles of each animal to measure creatine metabolism components, including glycine, arginine, guanidinoacetate, creatine, phosphocreatine, and creatinine, using liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry [35].

AMH-regulated cell cycle progression and creatine production under hypoxia

To examine the direct action of AMH on GC proliferation and energy production under hypoxia, the other ovary from each of the six animals described above was used for additional follicle isolation and culture with or without AMH supplementation as previously described [36]. Briefly, individual preantral follicles (diameter 125–225 μm) were cultured at 5% oxygen as described above. Follicles from each of the six animals were randomly assigned to culture groups (48 follicles/animal/group) with either control media only or 100 ng/ml recombinant human AMH protein (1737-MS, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN) supplementation for 8 days [36]. Media (100 µl) was collected and replaced on culture day 2 and then every other day. Photographs were obtained on culture days 2 and 8, i.e., early week 2 when follicles were at the preantral stage, to evaluate follicle survival. Diameters on culture days 2 and 8 of surviving follicles were measured. Media samples of surviving follicles collected on culture day 8 were pooled for control and AMH-treated follicles of each animal for liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry analysis as described above.

Surviving follicles with oocyte surrounded by GCs from each animal were collected on culture day 8 and pooled for the control and AMH-treatment groups, respectively. Total RNA was extracted using an Absolutely RNA Nanoprep Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) for Illumina short read RNA sequencing by the OHSU Massively Parallel Sequencing Shared Resource as previously described [20]. Briefly, RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies) and libraries were constructed using a TruSeq RNA Library Preparation Kit v2 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Poly(A) + RNA was isolated using oligo-dT bound to magnetic beads. Fragmented RNA was converted to double-stranded complementary DNA using random hexamer. Overhanging nucleotides were removed and a single adenine was added to the 3′ end of each complementary DNA strand. Illumina adaptors with barcode sequences were ligated to the fragments. Resulting libraries were amplified using PCR. Unincorporated nucleotides and adaptor dimers were removed using an Agencourt AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). Libraries were applied for multiplexing and paired-end sequencing using a HiSeq 2000 sequencer (Illumina). Base call data were assembled into FASTQ files using CASAVA (Illumina).

RNA aliquots from the same samples were reverse-transcribed to complementary DNA using a GoScript Reverse Transcription System (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA) based on the manufacturer’s instruction. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using TaqMan Gene Expression Assays (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Applied Biosystems 7900HT Fast Real-time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.), as previously described [20], to validate RNA sequencing results for HIF1A, MCM2, MCM3, MCM5, and MCM7 (Assay ID: Rh00936379_m1, Rh02854731_m1, Rh01071627_m1, Rh01052149_g1, and Rh01097207_m1). Mitochondrial ribosomal protein S10 (MRPS10) served as internal control (Assay ID: Rh02983847_g1).

Statistical and bioinformatic analyses

Statistical analysis was performed using SAS software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Due to skewed distribution, a mixed model was used to analyze follicle survival rates with rank-based analysis applied. Two-sample t-test was used to analyze follicle diameters, media AMH and creatine metabolism component concentrations, and gene expression data from real-time PCR. Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05 and values are presented as mean ± SEM.

RNA sequencing data analyses were performed by the ONPRC Bioinformatics & Biostatistics Core. Quality of the raw sequencing files was evaluated using FastQC (Babraham Institute, Cambridge, UK) combined with MultiQC (Seqera Labs, Barcelona, Spain). Trimmomatic (Usadel Lab, Aachen, Germany) was used to remove remaining Illumina adapters. Reads were aligned to Ensembl’s Mmul10 along with its corresponding annotation e!98. STAR v2.7.3a was used to align reads to the genome [37], utilizing gene annotation file and calculating the number of reads aligned to each gene. RNA-SeQC and another round of MultiQC were utilized to ensure that alignments were of sufficient quality [38]. Gene-level raw counts were filtered to remove genes with extremely low counts according to the published guidelines [39], normalized using the trimmed mean of M-values method, and transformed to log-counts per million with associated observational precision weights using the voom method. Gene-wise linear models with primary variable treatment group, accounting for within subject correlation, were employed for differential gene expression analysis using Limma (The Walter and Eliza Hall Institute of Medical Research, Parkville, Victoria, Australia) with empirical Bayes moderation and false discovery rate adjustment. Data were further analyzed for biological pathways using the IPA Core Analysis (QIAGEN). The user dataset was used as the reference set with a significance value of P < 0.05 [40]. Data were deposited in the Sequence Read Archive (BioProject, accession number: PRJNA787917).

Results

Preantral follicles experience physiological hypoxia in the ovary

Specific immunostaining of CA-IX, a cellular indicator of hypoxia, concentrated in primordial follicles in the cortical region of the macaque ovary based on qualitative observations (Fig. 1A). CA-IX-positive staining was still evident as follicles progressed to the preantral stage, as shown in primary and secondary follicles. However, the intensity of CA-IX signal was diminished when follicles developed into the antral stage (Fig. 1A). In preantral follicles, extensive membrane and cytoplasmic CA-IX staining was identified in GCs, but not in theca or surrounding stroma cells (Fig. 1B). Oocyte cytoplasm also exhibited CA-IX immunostaining. CA-IX signal was absent in the negative control section (Fig. 1B inset).

Physiological hypoxia is mimicked in preantral follicle culture

Image-iT Green Hypoxia Reagent incubation did not generate cytotoxic effects on macaque follicles in culture. All follicles remained at the preantral stage with typical morphology, a centrally located oocyte surrounded by layers of GCs, without observable alterations on culture day 2 (Fig. 1C). When cultured at 5% oxygen, the interior GCs and oocyte were positive for hypoxia-indicating reagent staining (Fig. 1D). Fluorescent staining was absent in the outer layer cells, presumably theca and stroma cells (Fig. 1E). The z-stack imaging showed hypoxic signal in individual GCs and oocyte (Fig. 1F).

Growing follicles produces higher levels of AMH and creatine under hypoxia

A total of 79.1 ± 2.7% macaque follicles survived 5 weeks of culture in the control media, maintaining their three-dimensional architecture and intact basement membrane. While 21.9 ± 3.8% of surviving follicles remained at the preantral stage at culture week 5, 78.1 ± 3.5% exhibited antrum formation. On culture day 2, although the diameters of no-grow and growing follicles were comparable with each other, media AMH concentrations were higher (P < 0.05) in growing follicles than those of no-grow follicles (Fig. 2A). Culture media of growing follicles also contained higher (P < 0.05) concentrations of guanidinoacetate and creatine (Fig. 2B) than those of no-grow follicles. Media concentrations of glycine, arginine, and creatinine were comparable between no-grow and growing follicles on culture day 2 (scale intensity of glycine: 0.995 ± 0.015 versus 0.975 ± 0.021; arginine: 0.999 ± 0.017 versus 1.003 ± 0.018; creatinine: 0.819 ± 0.058 versus 0.846 ± 0.059).

AMH promotes cell cycle progression and creatine production under hypoxia

When macaque preantral follicles were cultured for 8 days, AMH supplementation did not alter follicle survival rates relative to those of the control group (73.0 ± 5.1% versus 70.5 ± 3.8%). All surviving follicles remained at the preantral stage on culture days 2 (Fig. 3A) and 8 (Fig. 3B). Although follicle diameters on culture day 2 were comparable between groups, AMH-treated follicles exhibited greater (P < 0.05) diameters on day 8 than those of the control follicles (Fig. 3C). The 8 days of AMH supplementation increased (P < 0.05) media concentrations of guanidinoacetate and creatine compared with those of the control culture (Fig. 3D). Although media concentrations of glycine and arginine were comparable between control and AMH-treated follicles (scale intensity of glycine: 1.061 ± 0.043 versus 1.025 ± 0.025; arginine: 1.020 ± 0.016 versus 1.001 ± 0.022), media from follicles cultured with AMH supplementation contained higher (P < 0.05) concentrations of creatinine than those of the controls (scale intensity: 1.083 ± 0.043 versus 1.011 ± 0.038).

Surviving follicles from each animal were pooled for the control and AMH-treatment groups, respectively, for gene expression analysis (32–37 follicles/animal/group). Out of 15,873 annotated macaque genes, the mRNA expression of MCM2-MCM10 was identified in all pooled follicle samples by RNA sequencing. MCM9 and MCM10 exhibited considerably low counts (< 50 counts per million). MCM8 and MCM9 were not identified as differentially expressed between control and AMH-treated follicles (P > 0.05). Changes in follicular expression of MCM4 and MCM6 after AMH treatment were minimal relative to those of controls (< 0.3 log2FoldChange). The expression of MCM2, MCM3, MCM5, and MCM7 was further validated by real-time PCR. Results indicated that the mRNA levels of MCM3 and MCM7 (major regulators of HIF-1 activity [7]) (Fig. 3E), as well as MCM2 and MCM5 (Fig. 3F), increased in follicles cultured with AMH supplementation compared with those of controls. Differential expression of HIF1A between control and AMH-treated follicles was not identified by either RNA sequencing (P > 0.05) or real-time PCR (HIF1A/MRPS10: 1.06 ± 0.43 versus 1.08 ± 0.15). Top canonical pathways identified by RNA sequencing included cell cycle control of chromosomal replication (P < 0.00005, 26.0% overlap), kinetochore metaphase signaling pathway (P < 0.005, 14.4% overlap), as well as DNA methylation and transcriptional repression signaling (P < 0.05, 21.7% overlap). Top molecular and cellular functions (P < 0.05) involved molecules associated with cell cycle (169 molecules), cellular assembly and organization (108 molecules), as well as DNA replication, recombination, and repair (96 molecules). RNA sequencing data were deposited in the Sequence Read Archive (BioProject, accession number: PRJNA787917).

Discussion

Previous research in nonhuman primates showed that stage-specific modulation of AMH, i.e., AMH supplementation during culture weeks 0–3 (preantral to early antral stage) followed by AMH depletion during culture weeks 4–5 (antral stage), promoted follicular development, typical cumulus cell expansion, and oocyte maturation in vitro [36]. The present study further investigated the underlying mechanism of AMH in promoting preantral follicle growth, i.e., preantral follicle development under hypoxic conditions. For the first time, cellular hypoxia was directly visualized in GCs and oocyte of formaldehyde-fixed follicles in ovary and live follicles in culture. Data support the heterogeneity of preantral follicle population present in the primate ovary in terms of their growth potential which correlates with their AMH production. The study provides the first evidence indicating the stimulatory effects of AMH on preantral follicle development potentially via promoting GC cell cycle progression and cellular energy production under hypoxia.

The hypoxic status of follicles developed in vivo was assessed by detecting CA-IX production. CA-IX is a canonical target of HIF-1 which is commonly used to detect tissue hypoxia [31]. The extensive levels of CA-IX produced by primordial follicles clearly demonstrated a state of physiological hypoxia which has been suggested to support follicle dormancy. Primordial follicle activation and significant loss was observed in xenotransplanted bovine ovarian tissue before revascularization took place [41]. This follicle “burn-out” was more evident in thin graft in which follicles were more likely exposed to oxygen diffused from the surrounding tissue. In women with normal menstrual cycles, vascularization in the superficial cortical stroma increased with age [3]. The elevated oxygen supply inversely correlated with the density of primordial follicles. The present study also showed that the hypoxic state appeared to persist in preantral follicles before transitioning into normoxia in antral follicles. Antral follicles reside in a highly vascularized medulla region of the ovary and were surrounded by capillary network. The increased ambient oxygen levels in this microenvironment and mitochondrial respiratory capacity in GCs are important for active GC proliferation, steroid hormone production, and oocyte development during antral follicle maturation [42, 43]. The different oxygenation status of preantral and antral follicles developed in vivo can be further evaluated in future studies by quantifying CA-IX signal using immunofluorescence staining or direct tissue measurement of oxygen concentration in the ovarian cortex and medulla.

To determine whether cellular hypoxia observed in vivo could be mimicked in vitro, macaque follicles cultured at 5% oxygen were treated with a live cell-permeable hypoxia-indicating reagent. The reagent fluoresces when cellular oxygen concentration is 5% or less without compromising cell viability [44]. The fluorescent signal detected in cultured preantral follicles indicated metabolic hypoxia, which was consistent with the observation of preantral follicles developed in vivo in the same animals. Using mathematical modelling, oxygen tension in the ovarian cortex was estimated to be between 2% and 8% in most mammalian species [45]. Compared with the typical 20% oxygen level for conventional cell or tissue culture, 5% oxygen supply appeared to better replicate the physiological hypoxic environment of the macaque ovarian cortex [29], which was shown to be beneficial to in vitro development of preantral follicles from nonhuman primates [22, 30]. Consistently, the survival and growth rates of mouse preantral follicles cultured at 5% oxygen were higher than those cultured at 20% oxygen [46]. In addition, when human ovarian cortical tissue fragments were cultured at 5% oxygen, preantral follicles exhibited improved viability and quality relative to those exposed to 20% oxygen [47]. The relatively low oxygen tension appeared to reduce oxidative stress and DNA damage in GCs of in vitro developed preantral follicles.

Although preantral follicles are similar in size and morphology, they have different growth potential. The heterogeneity of preantral follicle population can be demonstrated as distinctive growth patterns in culture. No-grow and growing follicles were consistently observed in cultured preantral follicles from various species, e.g., cattle, nonhuman primates, and humans, regardless of culture conditions [22, 32, 48]. The present study showed an increased AMH production by preantral follicles that would eventually grow to the antral stage, which is consistent with data obtained previously in macaque follicle culture [20, 22]. Notably, preantral follicles with enhanced growth potential also produced high levels of guanidinoacetate and creatine, suggesting an energy homeostasis maintained by creatine metabolism under hypoxia. When mouse preantral follicles were cultured at 5% oxygen, metabolic profiling indicated that almost no glucose was consumed with little pyruvate produced and, meanwhile, no lactate utilization was observed [46]. Consistently, if both aerobic respiration and anaerobic glycolysis were limited in cultured macaque follicles in the present study, phosphocreatine in GCs could break down to generate ATP for supporting cellular activity in growing follicles. Creatine, as a product, might contribute to the high concentrations in the culture media. The elevated media guanidinoacetate levels indicated increased creatine biosynthesis by growing follicles. Because glycine and arginine were present abundantly in the commercial base media, differences in amino acid consumption between no-grow and growing follicles were not significant when measuring their concentrations in the culture media. Nevertheless, this cellular adaptation for energy production was observed in mouse preantral follicles cultured at 5% oxygen [46]. Growing follicles exhibited increased utilization of arginine and glycine, but decreased utilization of branched-chain amino acids as substrates for mitochondrial oxidative metabolism [46]. The present data also indicated that the conversion of creatine into phosphocreatine appeared to be restricted since media concentrations of creatinine were similar between no-grow and growing follicles. The equilibrium point of this reversible reaction might not have been shifted to the right during the early stage of culture since the stock levels of phosphocreatine could be high in preantral follicles recently isolated from the ovary.

Because preantral follicles express ligand-specific receptor of AMH [20], AMH-regulated cellular events were further examined in cultured macaque follicles. AMH supplementation promoted preantal follicle growth, which was consistent with previous studies in macaques [25, 26]. Due to the lack of creatine uptake under hypoxia, intracellular availability of creatine for ATP generation relies on de novo creatine biosynthesis. Although media concentrations of glycine and arginine were not altered by AMH supplementation, follicular production of guanidinoacetate and creatinine was elevated upon AMH treatment which suggested active creatine biosynthesis in GCs and the subsequent conversion into phosphocreatine. The increased media concentrations of creatine could be a collective result from creatine biosynthesis and phosphocreatine breakdown. Since the compound phosphocreatine is not secreted by a cell, cellular levels of phosphocreatine can be assessed in GCs of cultured follicles in future studies. Nevertheless, enhanced AMH signaling appeared to boost cellular energy of GCs by sustaining or enhancing creatine metabolism in preantral follicles developed in an environment of low oxygen tension.

RNA sequencing was used to map and quantify the transcriptomes of preantral follicles developed in vitro. Pathways essential for DNA replication and chromosome segregation were altered by AMH supplementation, suggesting a positive contribution to cell cycle progression. Particularly, the expression of MCM2, MCM3, MCM5, and MCM7, factors critical for DNA replication initiation during the cell cycle, increased in follicles treated with AMH. Reduced mitochondrial respiration results in hypoxia-induced cell cycle arrest via HIF-1 action which is negatively regulated by MCMs [5, 6]. Prior reports in the literature have described direct interactions between HIF-1, MCM3, and MCM7. While MCM3 inhibits HIF-1 transactivation domain function, MCM7 enhances HIF-1 degradation [7]. In addition, MCM2 and MCM5 were shown to reduce HIF-1 transcriptional activity induced by hypoxia in cultured human colon cancer cells [7]. To date, data on expression and role of MCMs in the ovary, especially in GCs of preantral follicles, are limited. In a recent study, positive MCM2 immunostaining was detected in GCs of fixed human ovarian tissue [49]. An increased proportion of preantral follicles with MCM2-positive GCs were identified in patients with anovulatory polycystic ovary syndrome, which correlated with enhanced GC proliferation [9]. MCM5 protein was localized in GCs of mouse preantral follicles [50]. Inhibiting ubiquitination and degradation of MCM5 promoted GC proliferation in culture and preantral follicle growth to the antral stage in vivo [50]. Consistently, the present study suggested a stimulatory role of AMH in GC cell cycle progression under hypoxia potentially through inhibiting HIF-1 action by MCMs without affecting HIF1A transcription. Future studies are warranted to further delineate the molecular mechanism of AMH-regulated preantral follicle growth, including interaction between HIF-1 and MCMs, expression of HIF-1 target genes, and degradation of HIF-1 protein.

Conclusions

Findings from the present study indicate that endogenous AMH production is crucial for and reflects the potential of preantral follicle growth in a hypoxic environment. The stage-dependent AMH action in regulating follicular development is associated with oxygenation-controlled GC proliferation and energy metabolism. AMH may promote preantral follicle growth in the avascular ovarian cortex via MCM-facilitated cell cycle progression and creatine-mediated cellular energy production in GCs. Data provide insight into the mechanism of elevated expression of AMH during preantral-to-antral follicle transition, with a new concept of oxygen-dependent cellular adaptation during follicular development. Future studies can further investigate AMH effects on GC differentiation in late stage preantral follicles to establish cumulus cell population upon antrum formation, as well as the relationship between the oocyte and somatic support cells.
