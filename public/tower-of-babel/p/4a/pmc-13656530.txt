Burden and associated factors of surgical site infection following caesarean section in a Nigerian tertiary hospital: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Ayokunle Moses Olumodeji, Adekunle Stephen Bello, Taiwo Kuku-Kuye, Rasheed Adetunji Adesanya, Victoria Oluwabukola Ajayi, Medara Victoria Udoh, Ufuoma Oluwaseyi Olumodeji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSurgical site infection following caesarean section is a significant cause of maternal morbidity and mortality, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. Understanding the local burden and associated risk factors is crucial for developing effective prevention strategies. This study aimed to estimate the incidence and identify factors associated with surgical site infection post-caesarean section at a Nigerian tertiary hospital.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted reviewing records of all women who underwent caesarean section at Lagos State University Teaching Hospital from January 2018 to December 2022. Surgical site infection was diagnosed and classified using Centres for Disease Control and Prevention criteria within a 30-day post-operative period. Bivariate analysis and multivariable binary logistic regression were performed to identify factors associated with surgical site infection.ResultsAmong 491 women included in the study, 70 developed surgical site infection, giving a documented incidence of 14.3%. All participants were classified as overweight (53.4%) or obese (46.6%) no participants were underweight or of normal weight. All identified infections were superficial or deep incisional surgical site infection; no organ/space surgical site infection was recorded. On multivariable logistic regression, booking weight was the only independent factor associated with SSI, with higher booking weight associated with lower odds of infection (aOR 0.96, 95% CI 0.93–0.99; p = 0.009).ConclusionThe burden of post-caesarean surgical site infection in our setting is substantial, exceeding global averages. Lower maternal booking weight is an independent predictor of surgical site infection following caesarean section in this overweight and obese cohort. However, this observational finding does not establish a causal pathway, and booking weight in this population should not be interpreted as a proxy for malnutrition. The potential clinical utility of pre-pregnancy weight optimization or targeted perioperative interventions in this population remains to be determined and warrants investigation in prospective clinical trials.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Caesarean section (CS) is one of the most performed major surgical procedures worldwide, with global rates continuing to rise [1]. While CS is a life-saving intervention when medically indicated, it carries inherent surgical risks including surgical site infection (SSI) [2]. SSI following caesarean delivery is a significant contributor to maternal morbidity, prolonged hospital stays, increased healthcare costs, and in severe cases, maternal mortality [3, 4].

The global burden of post-caesarean SSI varies considerably across regions and healthcare settings. A comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis estimated the pooled global incidence of post-CS SSI at 5.63%, though this figure conceals substantial regional disparities [5]. The incidence is notably higher in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where rates ranging from 2% to 16% have been reported [6]. Africa bears a disproportionate burden, with pooled SSI rates estimated between 11% and 15.6% in sub-Saharan Africa [7, 8]. Studies from individual African countries report even wider variation: Ethiopia (11.0-12.32%), Kenya (19%), and Tanzania (10.9–34.1%) [9–11].

Within Nigeria, the burden of post-caesarean SSI remains a significant public health concern. Various Nigerian studies have reported incidence rates from 6.7% to 19.4%, depending on the study setting, population characteristics, and surveillance methods employed [12–14]. These figures exceed those reported from high-income countries, where rates typically range from 2% to 5% [15]. The economic implications are substantial, with SSI adding an estimated $3,000-$30,000 per case to healthcare costs in high-income settings [16]. Comparable estimates from Nigeria are scarce, highlighting the need for local studies evaluating the economic burden of post-caesarean SSI.

The Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) National Healthcare Safety Network (NHSN) provides standardized criteria for defining and classifying SSIs [17]. According to this classification, SSIs are categorized based on anatomical depth into three types: superficial incisional SSI (involving skin and subcutaneous tissue), deep incisional SSI (involving fascia and muscle layers), and organ/space SSI (involving any anatomy deeper than the fascial layer that was manipulated during surgery) [17, 18]. For caesarean sections, the surveillance period for SSI detection is 30 days post-operatively [19]. This standardized classification facilitates meaningful comparison of SSI rates across institutions and enables targeted prevention strategies.

Numerous risk factors for post-caesarean SSI have been identified in the literature. These can be broadly categorized into patient-related factors, obstetric factors, and surgical/operative factors [20–25]. Patient-related factors associated with post-caesarean SSI include obesity, diabetes, anaemia, malnutrition, and immunocompromised states [20–25]. These factors may influence postoperative recovery and wound healing, although their relative contributions vary across populations. Obstetric risk factors consistently associated with post-CS SSI include emergency caesarean section, prolonged duration of labour, premature rupture of membranes (PROM), prolonged rupture of membranes, chorioamnionitis, and multiple vaginal examinations [26–28]. Meta-analyses have reported odds ratios of 1.68 for PROM and 6.15 for chorioamnionitis [29]. Intra-operative factors such as prolonged surgical duration, excessive blood loss, type of incision, and surgical technique also influence SSI risk [30–34].

Although several studies have reported the burden and risk factors for post-caesarean SSI in Nigeria, the findings have been inconsistent across institutions owing to differences in patient populations, clinical practices, and surveillance methods [12–14]. Furthermore, there is limited recent facility-specific evidence from high-volume tertiary referral hospitals such as Lagos State University Teaching Hospital. Generating local epidemiological data is essential to inform context-specific infection prevention strategies and improve maternal surgical outcomes. Therefore, this study aimed to estimate the incidence of SSI following caesarean section at LASUTH and identify the socio-demographic, clinical, and obstetric factors associated with its occurrence.

Methods

Study design and setting

A retrospective cohort study was conducted reviewing medical records of women who underwent caesarean section at the Department of Obstetrics and Gynaecology, Lagos State University Teaching Hospital (LASUTH), Ikeja, Lagos, Nigeria. The study covered a five-year period from January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2022. LASUTH is a 750-bed state-owned tertiary academic medical centre serving as a major referral facility for Lagos State and neighbouring regions, managing both booked patients receiving antenatal care at the facility and unbooked emergency referrals, allowing evaluation of booking status as a potential risk factor for surgical site infection.

Study population

The study population comprised all women who underwent caesarean section during the study period. Inclusion criteria were all women who had caesarean section (elective or emergency) at LASUTH during the study period with complete medical records. Women with incomplete records regarding the primary outcome (presence or absence of SSI) or major predictor variables were excluded from analysis.

Infection prevention and control practices

All caesarean sections at the study site were performed following standardized infection prevention and control protocols. Pre-operative skin preparation involved three applications of Savlon followed by povidone-iodine antiseptic solution. Vaginal cleansing with Savlon was not routinely performed prior to surgery. All procedures were conducted under standard aseptic technique by either a senior registrar or consultant obstetrician and gynaecologist as the lead surgeon. Prophylactic antibiotic therapy was administered intravenously before skin incision and consisted of co-amoxiclav 600 mg every 8 h and metronidazole 500 mg every 8 h. Ceftriaxone was used instead of co-amoxiclav in cases of preterm delivery. Intravenous antibiotics were typically converted to oral formulations by post-operative day 2 once the patient was tolerating oral intake well, and oral therapy was continued for a minimum of 5 additional days. These infection prevention measures represent the institutional standard of care throughout the study period.

Outcome definition and classification

The exposure was caesarean delivery, and the primary outcome was surgical site infection (SSI) occurring within 30 days of caesarean delivery as documented in-patient hospital records or routine postnatal follow-up records. SSI was defined and classified according to the Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) National Healthcare Safety Network (NHSN) criteria [17, 18]. Based on the CDC/NHSN classification, SSIs were categorized as superficial incisional, deep incisional, or organ/space infections according to the anatomical depth of infection.

Data collection

A structured data abstraction proforma was developed specifically for this study based on the study objectives and routinely documented clinical variables in patient case files, theatre records, and postnatal clinic follow-up records. Although the proforma was not formally piloted, standardized data collection was undertaken by trained research assistants using the same abstraction tool to promote consistency. Postoperative SSI surveillance was based on inpatient records and routine postnatal clinic follow-up records up to 30 days after caesarean section. Women without documented 30-day outcome data or with incomplete records regarding SSI status were excluded from the analysis.

Data extracted from multiple sources were cross-checked for completeness and consistency. Where discrepancies were identified, the original medical records were reviewed, and the most complete and clinically consistent information was used. Variables with unresolved discrepancies or missing essential information were recorded as missing and handled accordingly during analysis. Completed abstraction forms were reviewed by the principal investigator for completeness and consistency before data entry.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the software, Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables with non-normal distribution were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR).

Bivariate analysis compared characteristics between women with and without SSI using Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Multivariable logistic regression was performed using complete-case analysis because missing values were present for several study variables. Variables with p < 0.20 in bivariate analysis or considered clinically important were included in the initial model. Binary variables were coded as 0 (reference) and 1 (exposure) prior to regression. Adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals were reported. The final multivariable model was assessed for goodness-of-fit and discrimination (c-statistic = 0.73), and multicollinearity was ruled out through the assessment of variance inflation factors. For statistical analysis, all confirmed surgical site infections were analysed as a single binary outcome (SSI present/absent). Stratification by SSI subtype was not performed because the retrospective dataset did not contain sufficiently consistent information to reliably classify all cases according to infection depth.

Due to sparse data and quasi-complete separation, chorioamnionitis and blood transfusion could not be reliably estimated in multivariable analysis. Neonatal Apgar scores were excluded as post-delivery variables not biologically plausible as predictors of maternal SSI [35].

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Health Research and Ethics Committee of Lagos State University Teaching Hospital (LREC/06/20/2649). As a retrospective records review, individual consent was waived. All data were anonymized to protect patient confidentiality. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki principles. This manuscript was prepared following the STROBE guidelines for cohort studies [36].

Results

Among the 491 women who underwent caesarean section during the study period, 70 developed features of surgical site infection, giving an SSI rate of 14.3% (Fig. 1). Table 1 compares the socio-demographic and clinical characteristics of women with and without surgical site infection. Most participants in both groups were aged 26–35 years, with similar median ages (31 years in both groups). Age category differed significantly between women with and without infection (p = 0.032). There were no significant differences between the groups with respect to religion, level of education, social class, diabetes, hypertension, sickle cell disease, retroviral status, hepatitis B surface antigen status, or body mass index. Overweight and obesity were common in both groups.

Parity, previous caesarean section, and booking status were similar between the two groups. A significant difference was observed in the type of caesarean section performed, with distributions of elective and emergency procedures differing between women with and without infection (p = 0.042). Labour status prior to caesarean section also differed significantly between groups (p = 0.002), while the median duration of labour was comparable. Gestational age at delivery did not differ significantly. Rupture of membranes before labour occurred more frequently among women with surgical site infection (p = 0.050), although the median duration of rupture of membranes was similar. Features of chorioamnionitis differed significantly between the two groups (p = 0.002). Subcutaneous layer closure was similar in both groups. Blood transfusion status differed significantly (p = 0.008). Median pre-operative and post-operative packed cell volumes were lower among women with surgical site infection, with statistically significant differences observed for both measures (p = 0.010 and p = 0.001, respectively) (Table 2).

As shown in Table 3, neonatal and post-delivery characteristics differed between women with and without surgical site infection. The status of the baby at birth showed a significant difference between groups (p = 0.007). Median Apgar score at 1 min was similar, while the median Apgar score at 5 min differed significantly (p = 0.002). Median booking weight was lower among women with surgical site infection (p = 0.014). Admission of the baby into the special care baby unit at birth differed between groups (p = 0.010). The median duration of hospital stay after surgery was comparable, Postnatal clinic attendance differed significantly between women with and without surgical site infection (p = 0.005).

In the multivariable binary logistic regression analysis (Table 4), age, parity, previous caesarean section, and type of caesarean section were not significantly associated with surgical site infection. booking status and rupture of membranes did not reach statistical significance as independent predictors of SSI (Table 4). Booking weight remained significantly associated with surgical site infection, with lower odds observed with increasing weight. Pre-operative packed cell volume and labour prior to caesarean section were not significantly associated with surgical site infection in the adjusted model.

When the analysis was restricted to booked patients, a significant association was found between the type of caesarean section and SSI. SSI occurred in 20.3% of elective procedures compared with 7.6% of emergency procedures. However, BMI category was not significantly associated with SSI within this subgroup (Table 5).

Discussion

This study examined the burden and factors associated with surgical site infection following caesarean section at a major Nigerian tertiary hospital over a five-year period. The documented incidence of SSI was 14.3%, and booking weight was the only variable independently associated with SSI in the multivariable analysis.

The SSI incidence observed in this study is higher than the pooled global estimate reported in recent meta-analyses and is consistent with the generally higher burden of post-caesarean SSI reported in low- and middle-income countries compared with high-income settings [5, 15, 37, 38]. Our finding also falls within the range reported from tertiary hospitals in Nigeria and is broadly comparable with estimates from sub-Saharan Africa [7, 8, 12–14, 39, 40]. However, direct comparison across studies should be interpreted cautiously because reported incidence may vary according to case-mix, the proportion of emergency procedures, surveillance methods, post-discharge follow-up, and local perioperative infection prevention practices.

The relatively high burden of SSI observed in our setting may reflect the realities of a busy tertiary referral centre managing large numbers of emergency and unbooked obstetric cases, many of whom present after prolonged labour or with pregnancy complications. These patients may have limited opportunity for optimal pre-operative preparation before surgery. In addition, high patient volume, resource constraints, and perioperative workflow pressures common in many referral hospitals in low-resource settings may also contribute to the observed burden [41, 42]. These contextual factors are important when interpreting the incidence observed in this cohort.

The most unexpected finding in this study was the inverse association between booking weight and SSI, with higher booking weight associated with lower odds of infection after adjustment. This finding contrasts with much of the published literature, in which obesity is commonly associated with increased postoperative wound complications. It should therefore be interpreted with caution. Importantly, all women in this cohort with recorded BMI were classified as either overweight or obese; no women were in the normal-weight or underweight categories. Consequently, lower booking weight in this study does not represent undernutrition in the conventional sense, but rather variation in weight within an already high-BMI obstetric population. Booking weight should not be interpreted as a direct proxy for nutritional status, adiposity, or causality. Instead, the observed association may reflect residual confounding, differences in maternal anthropometry or body composition, or other unmeasured clinical and contextual factors not captured in this retrospective analysis. This finding should therefore be regarded as hypothesis-generating and requires confirmation in prospective studies.

Although booking status was associated with SSI in the univariable analysis, it was not independently associated with infection after adjustment. This suggests that the apparent effect of booking status may have been explained by other maternal and obstetric characteristics. Nonetheless, the direction of the association is consistent with previous studies suggesting that access to antenatal care may influence maternal surgical outcomes [9, 10, 43, 44]. In our setting, unbooked patients often present as emergencies, frequently after prolonged labour or with obstetric complications, leaving less time for optimisation before surgery [45]. While this remains clinically plausible, our findings do not support booking status as an independent predictor of SSI in this cohort.

Rupture of membranes was also not independently associated with SSI in the adjusted model, despite its established association with postoperative infection in prior studies [26, 27, 29]. This discrepancy may reflect limited statistical power, residual confounding, or the relatively small number of women with this exposure in our cohort. Likewise, lower pre-operative and post-operative packed cell volumes were observed among women who developed SSI, but these variables did not remain independent predictors in the final adjusted model. These findings suggest that some observed bivariate associations may reflect interrelationships among maternal, obstetric, and perioperative factors rather than isolated causal effects.

Several variables frequently reported as risk factors for post-caesarean SSI, including diabetes, previous caesarean section, and emergency caesarean delivery, were not independently associated with SSI in this study. This may reflect limited power for relatively infrequent exposures, overlap between correlated obstetric variables, and the influence of unmeasured perioperative factors. Although these findings differ from some previous reports, they are not entirely inconsistent with studies in which such factors lost significance after multivariable adjustment [46–48].

No organ/space SSI was identified in this cohort; all documented infections were superficial or deep incisional. This may indicate that the most severe forms of post-caesarean SSI were uncommon in this setting, but under-ascertainment is also possible given the retrospective design and dependence on documented follow-up [49]. The predominance of superficial and deep incisional infections suggests that preventive efforts should continue to focus on perioperative skin preparation, surgical technique, antibiotic prophylaxis practices, and postoperative wound care [50].

This study has several strengths. The five-year study period provided a reasonably large cohort, and the evaluation of multiple socio-demographic, clinical, and obstetric variables permitted multivariable analysis of potential predictors. The use of CDC criteria for SSI classification improves comparability with other studies, and reporting was guided by STROBE recommendations [17, 18, 36].

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. The retrospective design makes the study vulnerable to information bias and reliance on the completeness and quality of medical records. As a single-centre study, the findings may not be generalisable to other settings. Some clinically relevant variables, including timing of antibiotic prophylaxis, duration of surgery, operative blood loss, surgeon experience, and adherence to institutional infection prevention protocols, were not consistently documented and could not be assessed. Chorioamnionitis could not be reliably included in the multivariable model because of sparse data. In addition, women who developed SSI after discharge and presented to other health facilities may not have been captured, which may have led to underestimation of the documented incidence.

An additional limitation was the presence of differential missing data across several variables, with documentation generally more complete among women who developed SSI than among those who did not. This pattern suggests that missingness may not have been random and may have introduced information bias or affected the estimated associations. The multivariable analysis was also restricted to women with complete data for included variables, which may have influenced the final model. Residual confounding therefore cannot be excluded.

Overall, this study demonstrates that SSI following caesarean section remains an important burden in this tertiary hospital setting. The inverse association observed between booking weight and SSI should be interpreted cautiously, particularly because it arose within a cohort in which all women were overweight or obese by recorded BMI. Further prospective studies are needed to clarify the role of maternal anthropometric and perioperative factors in post-caesarean SSI and to inform locally relevant prevention strategies.
