Intrahepatic Cholangiocarcinoma in Wilson’s Disease: 2 Case Reports
Authors: Joana Reis Aguiar, Helena Pessegueiro Miranda, Cláudia Pereira
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWilson’s disease is a rare autosomal recessive inherited disorder of human copper metabolism. The spectrum of clinical manifestations of liver disease ranges from asymptomatic to cirrhosis and acute liver failure. Unlike the increased frequency of hepatobiliary tumours in other chronic liver diseases, the incidence of these tumours in Wilson’s disease is an uncommon event, even in cirrhotic patients.Case PresentationWe describe two clinical cases of cirrhotic patients with Wilson disease diagnosed with cholangiocarcinoma.DiscussionThe occurrence of hepatobiliary malignancies in Wilson’s disease is rare, even in cirrhotic patients. The exact reasons for this low prevalence are unclear, with some conflicting data. Copper’s role in chromatin stabilization and angiogenesis, along with copper deficiency due to Wilson’s disease and its treatment, may reduce tumour growth. The current guidelines do not have specific directives regarding cholangiocarcinoma screening in patients with cirrhosis secondary to Wilson’s disease. Despite the lower incidence of malignancies in Wilson’s disease, vigilant monitoring is advised.

Introduction

Wilson’s disease (WD) is a rare autosomal recessive hereditary disorder of human copper metabolism. The underlying molecular defect is encoded by chromosome 13 and affects the ATPase ATP7B, which is involved in cellular copper transport, leading to decreased biliary excretion, decreased incorporation into ceruloplasmin and intracellular accumulation, especially in the liver and brain [1, 2]. This causes liver damage (with acute manifestations such as fulminant hepatitis or chronic manifestations such as cirrhosis) and compromise of the central nervous system (neurological manifestations such as an akinetic-rigid syndrome similar to Parkinson’s disease, pseudosclerosis dominated by tremor, ataxia, and a dystonic syndrome and/or psychiatric manifestations such as depression, neuroses, personality changes, or psychosis) [1, 3, 4].

Although the frequency of malignancy is generally increased in chronic liver disease, primary hepatobiliary tumours rarely develop in WD [5–7]. The cumulative incidence of liver malignancy in WD is only 1.2%, with cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) representing 0.5% [5, 8].

Case Presentation

Case 1

Woman, 61 years old, with ECOG 0 was diagnosed with WD at the age of 36 with hepatic involvement. The first clinical manifestation was decompensated cirrhosis with ascites. The patient was treated with penicillamine with good adherence. She had a progressive improvement leading to normal hepatocellular function (Child-Pugh A), the liver elastography 6 kPa, and no endoscopic signs of portal hypertension, maintaining neurological involvement (bilateral postural tremor) and ocular involvement (bilateral Kayser-Fleischer rings). She had a family history of a sister with WD, who died of acute liver failure.

During a routine abdominal ultrasound, a solid hypoechoic nodular lesion measuring 26 × 39 mm was identified in segment III. Subsequent abdominal computed tomography (CT) confirmed the presence of a nodular lesion measuring 37 mm with a slight bulging of the contour, without arterial enhancement or washout in the subsequent phases, with hyperdense areas in all phases (LIRADS-M). Additionally, another nodular lesion with similar characteristics measuring 17 mm was identified in segment II (LIRADS-M). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) confirmed the presence of both hepatic nodules, with predominant hyperintensity in T2, presenting millimetric areas of internal liquefaction suggestive of necrosis and restriction in diffusion-weighted imaging. In the dynamic study, they showed vascular enhancement, without secure washout in the subsequent phases, with generalized hypointensity in the hepatobiliary excretion phase (LIRADS-M). PET with FDG showed hypermetabolic hepatic masses in topography overlapping those already identified, without other lesions. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy and colonoscopy were normal. Blood tests were normal for alpha-fetoprotein, CA 125, CA 19.9, and CEA. Core biopsy of one of the tumoral lesions (segment III) showed an epithelial neoplasm forming nests, trabeculae, and glands, in a desmoplastic background. The neoplastic cells had eosinophilic cytoplasm, rounded nuclei with vesicular chromatin and a prominent nucleolus. They expressed CK7 and were CK20-, ER-, GATA3- and TTF1-negative. The patient underwent laparoscopic hepatectomy of the lateral segments of the left lobe (II and III) and cholecystectomy. The anatomopathological study of both lesions revealed moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma with morphological aspects compatible with CCA, with complementary immunohistochemical study showing immunoreactivity of neoplastic cells for CK19 and CK7, with no immunoreactivity for p63 and GATA3. Pathological staging (pTNM) was pT3N0M0. The non-tumoral liver showed portal and periportal fibrosis. Adjuvant treatment with capecitabine was initiated, with progression of disease. Palliative chemotherapy with cisplatin and gemcitabine was initiated, and the patient died approximately 1 year later.

Case 2

Man, 76-year-old, with ECOG 0 was diagnosed with WD at the age of 70, during the study of abnormal liver enzymes: serum ceruloplasmin was low 0.09 g/L (0.16–0.36) as serum copper was 4.4 µmol/L (8.8–17.5). The 24-h urinary copper excretion was 1.720 µmol/d (0.040–0.550). Initial liver biopsy revealed chronic hepatitis with morphological aspects compatible with WD: The rhodanine stain was positive, and the liver copper levels were elevated (1,108 microg/g dry weight). Portal and periportal fibrosis with septa was observed. He also had dyslipidaemia and hypertension, without diabetes mellitus or excess weight. Family history was negative for WD. Hepatic involvement progressed to cirrhosis (Child-Pugh A) with portal hypertension and oesophageal variceal bleeding 4 years after the diagnosis. Later, he presented ascitis and an episode of spontaneous bacterial peritonitis. No evidence of neurologic or ocular involvement was observed. He was treated with trientine with good compliance and tolerance. Five years after the diagnosis, during a screening abdominal ultrasound, a heterogeneous and hypoechoic nodular formation measuring 6.2 cm in diameter in segment IV was identified. An abdominal CT scan showed a solid nodular lesion with indistinct margins and infiltrative characteristics in segments II/III of the left lobe, measuring approximately 6.5 cm (LIRADS-M). Two small hypervascular foci were defined in the arterial phase within the matrix of the lesion, which was predominantly hypodense. Three months later, an abdominal MRI showed an increase in size of the lesion in the segments II/III, extending to segment IVa, which had a slight hypersignal on T2-weighted sequences and showed peripheral enhancement, without evidence of washout. This lesion measured approximately 9.5 cm in diameter and involved the bile ducts and vascular structures of the liver, without causing dilation of the bile ducts. Additionally, satellite nodules were observed in the left and right hepatic lobes, all of them with dimensions smaller than 15 mm and similar characteristics to those of the larger lesion. Biopsy of this lesion showed ductal adenocarcinoma suggestive of CCA. Alpha-fetoprotein and CA 19.9 were within normal range. Considering the rapid growth between the CT and MRI, with the appearance of new satellite lesions, it was decided in a multidisciplinary meeting that there was no indication for additional staging and that the patient would not be a candidate for targeted therapy, being referred for best supportive care.

Discussion

Although data are still scarce, the occurrence of hepatobiliary malignancies seems to be an uncommon event, in cirrhotic patients with WD [5–7]. The reasons for the low prevalence are not fully clear, and there are contradictory data [9, 10].

Copper in the cell nucleus at physiological concentrations appears to play a role in chromatin stabilization by directly binding to DNA [6, 8, 11, 12]. Additionally, as angiogenesis is a copper-dependent process, as copper is deposited in the liver and other tissues, the serum copper level decreases, resulting in less copper available to stimulate vascular endothelial growth factor expression [6]. On the other hand, high copper concentration is known to induce DNA damage by producing reactive oxygen species [13, 14]. Copper deficiency may also be a factor, since patients with WD are functionally copper deficient, as copper is not properly utilized. This may be further aggravated by medical therapy, to increase copper excretion. This copper deficiency may lead to decreased angiogenesis and reduced tumour growth [15]. For this reason, copper chelating therapies have been evaluated in various tumour entities, without significant adverse effects, but with only modest clinical benefit [16, 17].

Unlike hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), where a protective environment may exist, the more frequent occurrence of ICC in patients with WD can be traced to the discussion of copper-mediated carcinogenesis in chronic cholestatic diseases. Among chronic liver diseases, primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) is the only condition consistently associated with a high incidence of ICC. Ferenci and colleagues [18] demonstrated significantly elevated copper concentrations in the hepatocytes of PSC patients. This raises the possibility of similar effects in WD, due to either increased hepatocellular copper levels or altered copper compartmentalization. However, a notable distinction between tumour development in PSC and WD is the later onset of tumour diagnosis observed in WD patients [8].

Screening of CCA may be done during the screening for HCC, which is well stablished in most cirrhotic patients. European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) recommends the screening of CCA using 6-monthly interval ultrasounds in patients with cirrhosis [19], along with the screening of HCC [20–22]. However, in WD patients with cirrhosis the cost-effectiveness of screening for HCC is less well established. EASL and American Association for the Study of Liver Disease (AASLD) do not differentiate the recommendation of HCC surveillance according the aetiology of the cirrhosis [20, 21]; British Association for the Study of the Liver (BASL) mentions different expected incidences between different populations; however, it does not specify patients with Wilson’s cirrhosis [23]. Only AASLD mentions liver cancer specifically in patients with WD, stating that these patients with cirrhosis or regressed cirrhosis should undergo screening and surveillance for HCC, but the screening and surveillance for CCA are not indicated in WD [24].

In patients with WD, liver dysfunction and cirrhosis are common, which can also lead to elevated CA 19.9 levels independent of malignancy. This elevation is often related to the severity of liver fibrosis and cholestasis, which are common in WD, thereby complicating the interpretation of CA 19.9 as a marker for CCA diagnosis [25, 26].

These 2 cases highlight that, although hepatobiliary neoplastic disease may have a lower incidence in patients with WD, it can still occur. They underscore the importance of screening for hepatobiliary neoplasms in patients with cirrhosis due to WD. The occurrence of CCA in WD in our first patient, apparently with regressed cirrhosis, raises a new challenge about the indications of the screening. While the need for screening is still debated, ultrasonographic evaluation every 6 months could be considered, given its low cost, lack of radiation exposure, and easy accessibility. As with other chronic liver diseases, particularly in cirrhotic patients, CCA should also be considered a differential diagnosis in this population.
