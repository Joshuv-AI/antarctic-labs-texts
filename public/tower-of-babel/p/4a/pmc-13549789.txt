Traumatic Brain Injury and Risk of Intracranial Meningioma : A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Ahood Mahjari, Abdullah Alyazidi, Khaled El-Sayed, Abdullah Alqarni, Afrah A. Alharthi, Amjad Alshahrani, Nouf Al-Salaman, Amjad Majrashi, Fatimah Alqahtani, Shooq Al-Shahrani, Muhannad Al-Obaiyah, Renad Al-Obaiyah, Ziyad Aldossari, Abdulsalam Alfaifi, Mona Alfaifi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Meningiomas are the most common primary intracranial tumors, but the role of traumatic brain injury (TBI) as a risk factor remains unclear. This study aimed to systematically review and quantitatively synthesize the available observational evidence on the association between TBI and subsequent meningioma risk. We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies following PRISMA guidelines. PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane databases were searched through December 5, 2025. Studies comparing meningioma incidence in individuals with and without a history of TBI were included. Risk of bias was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa scale. Pooled odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using a random-effects models. Heterogeneity and sensitivity analyses were performed. In results, 10 observational studies (seven case-control and three cohort studies) were included in the systematic review, with eight contributing to the quantitative synthesis. The pooled analysis demonstrated a statistically significant association between TBI and meningioma (OR, 1.96; 95% CI, 1.31 to 2.95; p=0.0012) with moderate heterogeneity (I2=65.6%). Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the findings, although funnel plot asymmetry suggested potential publication bias. We concluded that although a statistical association between prior TBI and meningioma diagnosis was observed, the current evidence does not support a clear causal relationship. The pooled effect appears to be driven mainly by retrospective case-control studies, which are particularly vulnerable to recall and detection bias, whereas cohort studies provide less consistent support for a direct etiological link. Further large-scale prospective studies with long-term follow-up are needed to clarify this relationship.

INTRODUCTION

Meningiomas are the most common primary intracranial tumors, representing approximately 40.8% of all central nervous system tumors in the United States, with a higher incidence in females than in males, and the greatest sex disparity observed between the ages of 35 and 44 years [23]. Although the majority of meningiomas are typically benign, a small subset of cases can lead to life-threatening complications due to their impact on vital structures in the brain. While recent advances in imaging modalities have facilitated earlier detection and management of meningiomas, preventive strategies remain unclear [5]. Therefore, identifying additional risk factors is essential for guiding effective prophylactic strategies to prevent their occurrence. The most commonly known factors associated with meningioma development include genetic susceptibility, older age, previous cranial radiation exposure, female sex, and possibly hormonal or environmental contributors [11,24]. Moreover, epidemiological and clinical evidence indicate that individuals with traumatic brain injury (TBI) may have a higher risk of developing meningiomas compared to the general population [2,4]. The primary mechanism proposed to link head trauma to subsequent meningioma development involves trauma-induced activation of cellular oncogenes and inactivation of tumor-suppressor genes during tissue repair, as well as chronic inflammation and the presence of foreign material that may promote tumorigenesis [1,19]. Emerging evidence, including neutral findings reported by Kuan et al. [10], suggests that TBI does not increase the risk of meningioma. However, TBI was shown to be associated with increased incidence of meningioma in case-control studies [17,18,21]. These discrepancies may reflect important differences in study design, exposure assessment, latency definition, and susceptibility to bias. In particular, retrospective case-control studies are more prone to recall bias, whereas surveillance after TBI may increase incidental tumor detection and introduce detection bias. Therefore, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies to estimate the association between TBI and intracranial meningioma and to examine the extent to which methodological factors may explain heterogeneity across studies.

METHODS

This systematic review and meta-analysis were designed and executed following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [15], and the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis of Interventions [7], ensuring methodological rigor and transparent reporting.

Search strategy and study selection

We conducted a comprehensive search of major electronic databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane Central, for studies published from inception to December 5, 2025. Eligible studies had to compare the incidence of intracranial meningioma among individuals with TBI with that in a control group without any history of head trauma. A highly sensitive search strategy was developed using Boolean operators and a combination of MeSH terms and free-text keywords, including (“head trauma” OR “head injury” OR “traumatic brain injury” OR TBI OR “cranial trauma” OR “brain injury”) AND (meningioma OR “Intracranial tumor”). To ensure completeness, we also manually screened the reference lists of all eligible articles and relevant systematic reviews for additional studies. The full search strategies for each database are provided in Table 1. All retrieved records were imported into End-Note for duplicate removal. Following deduplication, the proposed studies were uploaded into the Rayyan screening platform for further evaluation [14]. Two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text assessment of potentially eligible studies based on predefined PICO (population, intervention, control, and outcomes) criteria. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer.

Eligibility criteria and outcomes

We included observational studies, including cohort, casecontrol, and registry-based studies, that compared individuals with a history of TBI with a control group without head trauma regarding the incidence of intracranial meningioma, without restriction on follow-up duration, enrollment period, or language. We excluded Mendelian randomization and other genetic causal inference studies, unpublished studies, case reports, duplicate publications, conference abstracts, and studies lacking comparable groups or extractable outcome data. The primary outcome was the diagnosis or incidence of intracranial meningioma during the longest available follow-up period.

Data extraction and management

Two independent reviewers extracted data in duplicate using a standardized extraction sheet. Extracted information included study design, follow-up duration, study period, risk estimates, inclusion criteria, and conclusions. Reviewers carefully examined the manuscript text, tables, figures, and supplementary materials to ensure all relevant data were captured.

Risk of bias

The risk of bias in included observational studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa scale (NOS) [22], which evaluates studies across three domains : selection of study groups (up to 4 stars), comparability of groups (up to 2 stars), and outcome (for cohort studies) or exposure (for case-control studies) assessment (up to 3 stars). Two reviewers independently scored each study, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Studies were classified based on their total NOS score as low risk of bias (7–9 stars), moderate risk of bias (4–6 stars), or high risk of bias (0–3 stars).

Statistical analysis and heterogeneity assessment

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Pooled effect estimates were calculated as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Reported ORs were converted to log ORs, and their corresponding CIs were transformed to standard errors (SEs) before statistical pooling. A random-effects model with the DerSimonian-Laird method was applied to combine the log ORs and SEs. Heterogeneity across studies was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and quantified with the I² statistic, with thresholds of 25%, 50%, and 75% indicating low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05. A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the influence of individual studies on the overall effect estimates and to identify potential sources of heterogeneity. Additionally, funnel plots were visually used to identify inconsistencies and potential publication bias among the studies.

RESULTS

Literature search

The systematic search of electronic databases identified 1686 records. After removing 274 duplicates, 1412 unique records were screened by title and abstract. Of these, 1384 records were excluded, leaving 28 articles for full-text assessment based on predefined PICO criteria. Finally, 10 observational studies that met the inclusion criteria were included in our systematic review [8-10,12,13,16-18,20,21], with eight of these studies included in the analysis [8,10,12,16-18,20,21]. The study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Characteristics and risk of bias assessment of the included studies

Ten observational studies examined the association between TBI or head trauma and the risk of meningioma. These included seven case-control studies and three cohort studies. Study settings varied across population-based registries, national health databases, hospital-based cohorts, and multicenter collaborations. Methodological quality was assessed using NOS. Among the case-control studies, scores ranged from 6 to 8, with five rated high quality and two moderate qualities. All three cohort studies scored 8 or 9, indicating high methodological quality overall (Fig. 2).

Primary outcome : meningioma incidence following TBI

A total of eight observational studies were included in the quantitative synthesis evaluating the association between TBI and the subsequent development of meningioma. Pooled effect estimates based on a random-effects model demonstrated that patients with a history of TBI demonstrated a significantly higher risk of meningioma compared with individuals without head trauma (OR, 1.96; 95% CI, 1.31 to 2.95; p=0.0012). There was moderate to substantial heterogeneity among the included studies (I2=65.6%; τ2=0.24; p=0.0049), suggesting variability in effect estimates across studies. The overall direction of effect consistently favored an increased risk in the TBI group (Fig. 3).

Sensitivity analysis (leave-one-out analysis)

A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the robustness of the pooled effect estimate. The results demonstrated that no single study had a disproportionate influence on the overall association between TBI and meningioma risk, ranging from 1.67 to 2.15. The direction and magnitude of the association were largely preserved across all iterations, indicating high stability of the main findings (Fig. 4).

Publication bias and small-study effects

Visual inspection of the funnel plot showed asymmetry around the pooled log odds ratio, indicating a potential publication bias (Fig. 5).

DISCUSSION

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first systematic review and meta-analysis focused exclusively on observational evidence evaluating the association between TBI and subsequent intracranial meningioma risk. The pooled analysis demonstrated a statistically significant association (OR, 1.96; 95% CI, 1.31 to 2.95), but substantial heterogeneity remained across studies. Importantly, this apparent association was driven predominantly by retrospective case-control studies, whereas cohort studies were less supportive of a strong etiological relationship. By restricting our analysis to clinical observational data, we sought to provide a coherent estimate of the real-world association between TBI and meningioma without introducing methodological heterogeneity from fundamentally different analytical frameworks.

The primary outcome—the development of meningioma— serves as the cornerstone of this analysis. Our pooled results demonstrate a statistically significant elevation in risk (OR, 1.96), which aligns with the trauma-tumor hypothesis that has persisted since the early observations of Cushing and Eisen-hardt [3]. This finding is heavily influenced by the robust body of case-control literature, specifically the works of Preston-Martin et al. [17,18,21] and Phillips et al. [16], which report odds ratios ranging from 1.5 to over 4.0. These studies suggested that reparative cellular proliferation following trauma, potentially alongside chronic inflammation, initiates neoplastic transformation [16,20].

However, the validity of this pooled estimate is substantially undermined when analyzing the source of the data. The results from cohort studies included in this review, such as those by Nygren et al. [13] and Kuan et al. [10], contradict the case-control data. Kuan et al. [10], utilizing the Taiwan National Health Insurance Research Database, reported a hazard ratio of 1.27 that was not statistically significant (p=0.514), effectively ruling out a strong causal link in a controlled registry setting. This discrepancy suggests that the pooled OR of 1.96 is likely inflated by recall bias—a phenomenon where patients with a brain tumor are more likely to selectively recall and report past head traumas than healthy controls [16,17].

A critical finding of this meta-analysis is the substantial heterogeneity observed across studies (I2=65.6%; p=0.0049). Our leave-one-out sensitivity analysis reveals that this heterogeneity is driven almost entirely by the study of Hu et al. [8], an extreme outlier with an OR of 16.36. The magnitude of this effect size markedly differs from Western cohorts and may reflect distinct environmental exposures or selection biases in that specific population [8]. When this outlier is removed, the statistical heterogeneity drops significantly, yet the conflicting narratives presented by cohort and case-control studies remain the most notable finding.

Regarding the temporal relationship, the data reveals a complex narrative driven by detection bias. Inskip et al. [9] demonstrated that the excess risk of brain tumors is highest in the first year following injury (Standardized incidence ratio, 3.38) and drops to near baseline thereafter. This strongly suggests that the association is often the result of incidental findings—head trauma precipitates imaging, which detects pre-existing, asymptomatic meningiomas [9]. Conversely, studies supporting causal interpretation rely on the existence of a prolonged latency period; Preston-Martin et al. [20] and Phillips et al. [16] found the highest risks for injuries occurring 15 to 24 years before diagnosis. However, the recent Mendelian randomization study by He et al. [6] provides a potent rebuttal to the causal hypothesis. By utilizing genetic variants as instrumental variables, they found no causal relationship between the genetic predisposition for TBI and meningioma risk (p>0.05), reinforcing the conclusion that observational associations are likely artifacts of bias rather than biology [6].

Limitations

A primary limitation is the high heterogeneity (I2=65.6%) observed in the pooled analysis, which is largely attributable to the variable definitions of head trauma across studies (e.g., selfreported bumps vs. hospitalized skull fractures). Additionally, the potential for selection bias in the included retrospective studies cannot be ignored, as noted in Preston-Martin et al. [17], methods relying on patient recall are inherently susceptible to memory distortion. Finally, while some studies controlled for radiation, the potential confounding role of diagnostic X-rays received during the evaluation of head trauma remains a complicating factor in isolating trauma as an independent risk factor [16,18].

CONCLUSION

This meta-analysis identified a statistical association between prior TBI and meningioma diagnosis; however, the finding appears to be driven largely by retrospective case-control studies and should be interpreted cautiously. Current observational evidence remains insufficient to establish a causal relationship, and cohort studies are less supportive of a strong association. Future research should prioritize large-scale prospective cohort studies to clarify the temporal and causal relationship between TBI and meningioma.
