Bimodal social capital associated with psychosocial profiles and brain network topology in a Japanese population
Authors: Chihiro Hosoda, Takuto Matsuhashi, Sebastian Meguro, Tzu Tien Chiu, Zhi Yi Zhou, Kenchi Hosokawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Social capital (SC) is a key determinant of well-being and social participation, yet the magnitude of its individual differences and their neurobiological bases remain poorly understood. Here we showed that social capital (SC) is bimodally distributed across the lifespan and that these modes align with distinct psychosocial and neurobiological profiles. In a nationwide cross‑sectional survey in Japan (N = 2,574; ages 4–87) comprising 731 parent–child dyads and 1,112 adults without children, SC showed a reproducible two‑mode pattern. LASSO regression identified consistent psychosocial predictors of higher SC, including perspective-taking, empathy, and extraversion. In children, SC was strongly associated with parental SC (r = 0.40) and positive parent–child relationships, while parental income and education showed little explanatory value. In an independent neuroimaging cohort (N = 108), higher SC was linked to a more efficient structural covariance network, including greater small-world topology, higher hub centrality, and regional differences in cortical thickness and myelination across social brain regions (e.g., angular and supramarginal gyri, cingulate cortex, ventromedial prefrontal cortex). These findings demonstrate that, in this Japanese sample, SC was bimodally distributed, and each mode was associated with distinct psychosocial and neurobiological profiles, indicating divergent developmental pathways of social connectedness with implications for health and society.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-60260-z.

Introduction

Social capital (SC) refers to the features of social relationships, in particular, interpersonal trust and norms of reciprocity, that enable people to cooperate for mutual benefit1. It encompasses the resources and support individuals can draw from their social networks, such as having others whom one can trust, rely on, and receive help from. Classic sociological perspectives have conceptualized SC either as an individual asset derived from one’s social connections2 or as a collective good arising from networks and shared norms3,4. Here, we adopt a broad definition stressing the quality of relationships, the trust that flows through social ties, rather than just the quantity of ties. It is important to distinguish social capital from mere social network size: having many acquaintances is not equivalent to enjoying high-quality, trust-rich relationships. Nonetheless, larger networks often co-occur with greater social capital, since actively maintained friendships can both reflect and reinforce trust and support3. Indeed, prior studies in social neuroscience commonly use network size as a proxy measure of social connectedness, yielding insights that likely extend to social capital5,6. Notably, individuals vary widely in their social capital, and these differences can have major implications for well-being and community engagement1. Understanding why some people develop much richer, more trust‐filled social connections than others is a fundamental question spanning sociology, psychology, and neuroscience.

Individual differences in social capital are thought to arise from a complex interplay of developmental, dispositional, and neurobiological factors. From a developmental perspective, the family environment and upbringing play a formative role. Parents serve as the primary agents of socialization, modeling patterns of social interaction for their children. As a result, a child’s social capital tends to partly mirror the social capital of their parents, an idea dating back to Coleman’s work on the intergenerational transmission of social advantage4,7. Empirical evidence supports this, for example, adolescents who grow up in warm, supportive family environments-where parents engage with them and demonstrate trust and reciprocity in relationships-are more likely to cultivate high generalized trust in adulthood8,9. Cross-national studies have similarly found that supportive parent–child relationships predict greater social trust later in life9. Notably, these familial influences appear to outweigh conventional socioeconomic factors: parents’ social connectedness and parenting style can be more important than parental income or education in shaping a child’s eventual social capital10.

In addition to family context, individual dispositions and social-cognitive abilities critically influence the breadth of one’s social capital. Personality research has long shown that certain traits predispose people to build larger and more supportive social networks. Foremost among these is extraversion-individuals who are more outgoing and sociable tend to initiate and maintain more relationships. Similarly, agreeableness, reflecting a cooperative and empathetic orientation, correlates with having more friends11,12. A longitudinal study tracking over 5,000 older adults for 14 years found that those higher in extraversion and agreeableness consistently maintained larger social networks12. These traits likely foster behaviors (such as approachability, positive effect, and willingness to spend time with others) that organically expand one’s social connections. Beyond broad personality factors, specific social-cognitive skills such as empathy and perspective-taking are also key13. People who are high in empathic capacity, especially the cognitive ability to understand others’ viewpoints, tend to develop stronger interpersonal bonds and more trusting, supportive relationships, thereby amassing greater social capital14. For instance, individuals adept at perspective-taking often have not only more diverse friends but also deeper bonds, as this skill helps them navigate social situations with sensitivity14. Deficits in these capacities can conversely constrain social capital: in clinical populations like autism spectrum disorder, where theory-of-mind and empathy are impaired, forming and maintaining social ties is notably difficult15,16. Such observations underscore that psychological traits and skills contribute substantially to individual differences in social capital, by affecting how people connect and build trust with others.

Beyond social experiences and personality traits, differences in brain structure and neural network organization, often referred to collectively as the “social brain”, also contribute to individual differences in social capital. The social brain hypothesis posits that primates evolved larger brains to manage complex social groups17. The neuroimaging studies have revealed that individuals with larger or more efficiently wired social-cognitive brain regions tend to maintain larger social networks. For example, structural MRI studies have shown that gray matter volume in regions implicated in social cognition (e.g., medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), temporoparietal junction (TPJ), anterior cingulate cortex (ACC)) correlates with the number of social relationships an individual can maintain5,18,19. Recent work has shown that aspects of social ties are reflected in distinct brain morphologies20. Notably, ventromedial prefrontal cortex volume is associated with both mentalizing competence and social network size6,21.

Complementing these structural findings, functional brain measures also correlate with individual differences in social network size. For instance, resting-state activity in social-cognitive regions (e.g., mPFC and the superior temporal sulcus (STS)) correlates with an individual’s social network size11. Moreover, graph-theoretical analyses show that human brain networks exhibit a “small-world” architecture (characterized by high local clustering and short path lengths) that supports efficient information integration22. Variations in such network efficiency may underlie individual differences in complex abilities, including social cognition. It has been proposed that brains with more optimally efficient “small-world” connectivity can more easily coordinate the multiple neural systems required for empathy, perspective-taking, and social interaction22. Conversely, neurodevelopmental conditions involving social impairments often show disruptions in brain connectivity; for example, autism is linked to reductions in global network efficiency, which may relate to difficulties in social interaction23,24.

In the present study, we examine how individual differences in social capital arise across age spans and social roles, parents, non-parent adults, and children, by jointly modelling family environment, personality traits, social-cognitive abilities, and brain structure. Our sample spans ages 4–87 and includes parent–child pairs, thereby capturing both developmental and familial contributions. This cross-generational, multilevel approach provides a novel, comprehensive perspective, allowing direct assessment of how neural, psychological, and familial factors together shape social capital.

Results

Bimodal distribution of social capital across generations

We analyzed social capital scores in 2,574 individuals, including 731 parent–child dyads and 1,112 adults without children (see Supplementary Tables 1–3 for demographics). Across all demographic groups (parents aged 26–68, non-parent adults aged 20–87, and children aged 4–17), social capital scores displayed a consistent bimodal distribution (Silverman’s multimodality test: at most two modes, p = 0.12; critical bandwidth = 1.24; Supplementary Table 4). To formally confirm the bimodal distribution, we applied Silverman’s multimodality test, which assesses whether the data are unimodal (single-peaked) or multimodal. This test indicated that social capital is not well fit by a single‐mode (unimodal) distribution, supporting the presence of two distinct modes in the population. Comparison between parents and adults without children

Because parents and adults without children represented distinct adult social-relational strata rather than age-matched comparison groups, we characterized demographic and SC differences between these groups descriptively. Parents were younger than adults without children (44.79 ± 6.72 vs. 52.13 ± 17.36 years; Welch’s t(1552.0) = − 12.73, p < 0.001), whereas sex distribution did not significantly differ between groups, χ2(1) = 0.74, p = 0.389. Mean SC was slightly higher in parents than in adults without children (18.30 ± 6.22 vs. 17.32 ± 6.78; mean difference = 0.98, 95% CI [0.38, 1.58]; Welch’s t(1653.1) = 3.20, p = 0.001; Cohen’s d = 0.15).

To examine whether this descriptive difference was robust to basic demographic adjustment, we fitted exploratory linear regression models. Parental status was associated with higher SC after adjustment for age and sex, β = 1.40, 95% CI [0.78, 2.02], p < 0.001, and after additional adjustment for household income and educational attainment, β = 1.20, 95% CI [0.55, 1.85], p < 0.001.

In both parents and adults without children, social capital was positively correlated with several psychosocial factors. These correlations were computed separately for each group as bivariate Spearman’s rank correlations between each participant’s continuous SC score and each outcome variable, with no covariates included. Notably, higher social capital was associated with greater subjective well-being (r = 0.31, p < 0.01 for parents; r = 0.42, p < 0.01 for adults without children), more time spent together with one’s parents during upbringing (r = 0.19, p < 0.01; r = 0.20, p < 0.01, respectively), and higher levels of interpersonal trust in childhood (r = 0.12, p < 0.01; r = 0.29, p < 0.01). Personality traits related to sociability and empathy also showed significant positive correlations with social capital: Extraversion (r = 0.24, p < 0.01; r = 0.27, p < 0.01), other-oriented emotional reactivity (r = 0.27, p < 0.01; r = 0.36, p < 0.01), and perspective-taking ability (r = 0.25, p < 0.01; r = 0.27, p < 0.01) were all higher in individuals with greater social capital (Fig. 1B, E, K, N; Supplementary Tables 5, 9; Supplementary Fig. 3). By contrast, social capital showed no significant correlation with household income (r = 0.03, p = 0.41 for parents; r = 0.04, p = 0.18 for adults without children) or with educational attainment (r = 0.06, p = 0.13 for parents; r = -0.01, p = 0.71 for adults without children), indicating that socioeconomic factors were generally weak predictors of SC. (Notably, very slight positive correlations between SC and SES were observed in certain sub-demographics, but these did not reach significance. Figure 1C, D, M; Supplementary Tables 5, 9; Supplementary Fig. 4).

To distinguish low- and high-SC individuals within the observed bimodal distribution, we identified the local minimum of the smoothed SC curve as the cutoff between the two groups. This data-driven threshold was 16 points and was applied to all subpopulations to classify participants into Low-SC and High-SC groups. Using this grouping, we then examined whether key background variables differed between low- and high-SC individuals. Importantly, this two-group categorization proved robust: repeating all analyses with slightly different cutoff values (e.g., 15 or 17 points) yielded consistent results. Socioeconomic status showed no significant effect on group membership: High- and Low-SC groups did not significantly differ in educational attainment (parents: t(531.6) = 0.42, p = 0.67; adults without children: t(1024.2) = –1.11, p = 0.27) or household income (parents: t(533.2) = 0.64, p = 0.52; adults without children: t(1029.2) = 1.13, p = 0.26) (Fig. 1, C, D, L, M).

To address whether current employment status could account for the observed SC pattern, we stratified participants into employed and non-employed groups and compared SC scores within eight subgroups (Supplementary Fig. 2; Supplementary Table 13).

In the parent sample, SC scores did not differ significantly between employed and non-employed participants in the full parent sample (employed: 18.27 ± 6.11, n = 611; non-employed: 18.47 ± 6.79, N = 120; t(159.0) = − 0.29, Cohen’s d = − 0.03, Bonferroni-corrected p = 1.000) or in any of the three child-age subgroups (4–6 y.o.: t(52.3) = − 1.04, d = − 0.20, p = 0.744; 7–10 y.o.: t(51.1) = − 1.14, d = − 0.21, p = 0.744; 11–17 y.o.: t(55.8) = 1.71, d = 0.29, p = 0.744). Effect sizes were negligible to small, and no consistent directional trend was observed across parent subgroups. In adults without children, non-employed participants scored modestly higher than employed participants in the full adult sample (employed: 16.87 ± 6.78, n = 763; non-employed: 18.31 ± 6.69, n = 349; t(683.5) = − 3.32, d = − 0.21, Bonferroni-corrected p = 0.008). This difference was small in magnitude and was not observed in any of the three age-stratified adult subgroups (young adults 20–39 years: t(68.6) = − 1.18, d = − 0.19, p = 1.000; middle adults 40–64 years: t(113.5) = − 0.02, d = − 0.002, p = 1.000; late adults ≥ 65 years: t(246.3) = 0.74, d = 0.08, p = 1.000). The overall adult-sample difference should be interpreted cautiously: non-employed adults were on average 14.2 years older than employed adults (61.87 ± 18.73 vs. 47.67 ± 14.69 years), and SC scores in the oldest age band were similarly high regardless of employment status (late adults: employed 19.74 ± 6.41 vs. non-employed 19.21 ± 6.45), suggesting that the overall adult difference reflects the age composition of the two groups rather than employment status per se. The bimodal-like SC distribution remained visually apparent in both employed and non-employed participants across all subgroups (Supplementary Fig. 2). These results suggest that current employment status did not exert a consistent association with SC across subgroups, and does not account for the observed bimodal SC distribution.

These results suggest that in our Japanese sample, social capital levels are not strongly influenced by socioeconomic factors such as education, income, or employment status. By contrast, clear differences emerged in relation to how one was raised during childhood and personality. In both parents and adults without children, individuals’ social capital levels were linked to how they perceived their childhood parenting. In particular, the High-SC group (parents and adults without children) reported significantly greater “time spent together” with parents in their youth (parents: t(533.0) = 2.24, p < 0.01; adults without children: t(1029.2) = 5.00, p < 0.01) (Fig. 1E, N). In addition, the High-SC group scored higher on certain personality traits. Notably, Extraversion was significantly higher in the high-SC group (parents: t(571.4) = 6.55, p < 0.01; adults without children: t(1063.6) = 7.92, p < 0.01), consistent with the idea that sociable, outgoing personalities cultivate richer social networks (Supplementary Tables 6, 10; Supplementary Figs. 3, 4). Subjective well-being was also significantly higher in the High-SC group in both parents (t(510.24) = 5.48, p < 0.001) and adults without children (t(973.43) = 11.29, p < 0.001). In adults without children, the High-SC group additionally showed significantly higher scores on Big Five Agreeableness (t(1004.18) = 5.16, p < 0.001), Conscientiousness (t(1033.36) = 3.16, p = 0.036), MES Other-Oriented Emotional Reactivity (t(956.17) = 8.45, p < 0.001), Perspective-Taking (t(991.55) = 6.70, p < 0.001), recalled parenting trust (t(997.30) = 7.49, p < 0.001), and parenting independence (t(1017.81) = 7.61, p < 0.001); these measures did not reach significance in the parent sample after Bonferroni correction. Conversely, no measures showed significantly higher scores in the Low-SC group among parents. In adults without children, the Low-SC group scored significantly higher on Big Five Neuroticism (t(1012.51) = − 5.13, p < 0.001), MES Self-Oriented Emotional Reactivity (t(1041.32) = − 3.18, p = 0.034), and recalled parental disinterest during upbringing (t(1013.97) = − 4.34, p < 0.001). All p values are Bonferroni-corrected. Full results are provided in Supplementary Tables 6 and 10. These findings indicate that adult SC was associated with recalled childhood relational experiences and personality/empathy-related traits. Because the upbringing variables were retrospectively reported, they should be interpreted as perceived childhood relational correlates of current SC rather than as direct evidence that these experiences causally shaped later SC or pre-parenthood social connectedness. Similar patterns were observed in both parents and adults without children, suggesting that these psychosocial correlates of SC were not confined to the current parenting context. Importantly, these trait differences by SC level were observed regardless of a parent’s status, indicating that the influence of personality on social capital transcends one’s parental status.

For the child participants, higher social capital was modest but significantly associated with greater subjective well-being (r = 0.29, p < 0.01) and higher life satisfaction (r = 0.24, p = 0.01). In contrast, age was not correlated with social capital among children (r = -0.02, p = 0.53) (Fig. 1G–I; Supplementary Tables 7, 8). At the group level, High-SC children showed significantly greater subjective well-being than Low-SC children (t(259.31) = 3.33, Bonferroni-corrected p = 0.01; Supplementary Table 8). No measured child-level variables showed significantly higher scores in the Low-SC group after correction. For dyadic parent-reported measures, parents of High-SC children reported a better relationship with their child than parents of Low-SC children (t(268.34) = 3.24, Bonferroni-corrected p = 0.01; Supplementary Table 12). Big Five personality traits and empathy measures were not administered directly to child participants; therefore, child-level group comparisons on these measures were not conducted. Full child-level and dyadic parent-reported group-comparison results are provided in Supplementary Tables 8 and 12. This suggests that even in childhood, social capital relates more to a child’s well-being and outlook than to their age or developmental stage.

LASSO regression analysis of social capital predictors

To identify the most influential predictors of social capital, we built separate LASSO regression models for each population: parents, adults without children, and children using the PyCaret library in Python. The features for these models included socioeconomic variables (household income and education), personality traits (Big Five dimensions), empathy measures (Multidimensional Empathy Scale subscales), retrospective parenting factors (six aspects of one’s upbringing), as well as age and sex. For the parent group, the factors with non-zero coefficients were Perspective Taking (β = 0.32), Extraversion (β = 0.27), Other-Oriented Emotional Reactivity (β = 0.20), Self-Oriented Emotional Reactivity (β = –0.12), and age (β = –0.10); all other predictors’ coefficients were reduced to zero. This parent model explained about 11% of the variance in parents’ social capital (R2 = 0.11, MAE = 4.54; Table 1). The LASSO model for adults without children retained a broader set of predictors compared to the parent model. The final model included Trust (β = 0.80), Encouragement of independence (β = 0.62), Other-Oriented Emotional Reactivity (β = 0.38), Extraversion (β = 0.38), Agreeableness (β = 0.29), Perspective Taking (β = 0.14), Household income (β = 0.08), age (β = 0.01), perceived parental interest (β = –0.13), and Self-Oriented Emotional Reactivity (β = –0.05). This model for adults without children explained about 26% of the variance in their social capital (R2 = 0.26, MAE = 4.95; Table 1).

It is notable that, as in the parent group, education level did not emerge as a predictor in the non-parent adult model. Household income did remain in the adult model, although it had a relatively small effect size compared to other variables. The LASSO regression results highlight that personality traits related to social engagement, empathy, and specific perceived parenting factors are more strongly associated with SC than socioeconomic factors, such as education and income, in both parents and adults without children.

Parental factors predicting children’s social capital (children model)

The features included in the child SC regression were parenting style dimensions (responsiveness and control), parents’ SC, household income, parents’ educational attainment, parents’ age and sex, and the child’s age and sex. The final LASSO regression model for children’s SC retained parental SC (β = 2.96), relationship with mother (β = 0.92), child’s sex (β = 0.29), parental responsiveness (β = 0.20), parental control (β = 0.14), parent’s age (β = 0.07), relationship with father (β = 0.05), parent’s sex (β = 0.04), and household income (β = 0.02). There were no negative factors retained in the final model, and it explained 12% of the total variance in SC (R2 = 0.12, MAE = 4.30) (Table 2). We also ran exploratory sex-stratified LASSO regressions, but both the boys-only and girls-only models had limited and unstable predictive power. The boys-only model retained different predictors (notably, it dropped parental SC) but showed no out-of-sample explanatory power (test R2 < 0). The girls-only model more closely resembled the full model (parental SC remained the dominant predictor) yet similarly failed to generalize (cross-validated R2 ~ 0.10; test R2 < 0). Thus, both sex-specific models are considered exploratory and not reliably predictive (Table 3). Parent–child social capital associations

A positive correlation was identified between parental and child SC (r = 0.40, p < 0.01, 95% CI [0.34, 0.46]). The parents of high-SC children had significantly higher SC themselves than parents of low-SC children (t(295.7) = 8.75, p < 0.01, d = 0.74) (Fig. 2A). Significant associations with parent–child relationships further supported the intergenerational continuity in SC. Moreover, the quality of the parent–child relationship was linked with the child’s SC. Children with a good relationship with their parents tended to have higher social capital (r = 0.26, p < 0.01, 95% CI [0.19, 0.32]), and the parents of high-SC children reported a better relationship with their child on average than those of low-SC children (t(268.3) = 3.24, p < 0.01, d = 0.29) (Fig. 2D). Parenting style also differed between the groups: high-SC children more often experienced parenting characterized by greater responsiveness to low-SC children (responsiveness: t(247.0) = 2.82, p < 0.01) (Fig. 2E). Additionally, parents of high-SC children differed in personality: for example, they scored significantly higher in Extraversion than parents of low-SC children (t(277.8) = 3.08, p < 0.01) (Supplementary Tables 11 and 12; Supplementary Fig. 5). By contrast, the child’s social capital was not significantly related to parental socioeconomic status. Neurostructural basis of social capital disparities

We next investigated the neurostructural network properties and neurostructural correlates of social capital in an independent sample of young adults (N = 108; mean age, 21.8 ± 2.1 years; 56 females and 52 males; Supplementary Table 14). The participants were divided into High-SC (N = 69) and Low-SC (N = 39) groups based on their SC scores, using the same Silverman’s test and local-minima approach to define a bimodal cutoff as in the survey (Supplementary Fig. 6, Supplementary Table 15).

Previous studies have suggested that “small-world” brain network architecture supports efficient information integration in social cognition25. Consistent with this idea, we examined whether global network efficiency differed by social capital level. The High-SC group showed significantly greater small-worldness in structural covariance networks (derived from cortical myelin data) compared to the Low-SC group (Fig. 3A; p = 0.03). Social functioning may depend on the hub that connects different neural subnetworks26,27. We therefore compared regional betweenness centrality (a measure of a region’s hub connectivity) between groups. The High-SC group showed significantly higher betweenness centrality than the Low-SC group (Fig. 3B; Supplementary Table 16). Notably, the most pronounced differences were observed in regions associated with social cognition and emotional regulation. Specifically, High-SC individuals demonstrated markedly greater centrality in frontal regions, including the anterior prefrontal cortex (areas 10d, 9a, 9 m, 9–46d), orbitofrontal cortex (10v, 11 l, s32, pOFC), and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (a24, d32)-all key “social brain” areas involved in integrating self- and other-related information28. Furthermore, High-SC individuals showed higher centrality in temporal regions involved in social and semantic cognition-including the superior temporal gyrus (STGa), anterior fusiform cortex (PHA3), and temporal pole (TGd)-as well as in parietal/temporoparietal regions implicated in attention and social inference (PGp, PGs, IP1, TPOJ2). Also, the centrality in the High-SC group was found in language-related inferior frontal regions (e.g., IFJa, IFSa, area 44), the insular cortex (FOP3, POI2, AAIC), and premotor areas (6v, 6a, 55b), suggesting enhanced coordination of sensorimotor and interoceptive processes29,30. To further characterize regional network organization, we additionally computed four nodal graph metrics—degree centrality, closeness centrality, clustering coefficient, and eigenvector centrality—for all 360 cortical regions of interest; full results are provided in Supplementary Table 16. Degree centrality reflects the number of direct connections of a region and therefore indexes its immediate connectedness. Closeness centrality reflects the extent to which a region can reach other regions through short network paths and therefore indexes topological accessibility. The clustering coefficient reflects the extent to which a region’s neighboring nodes are mutually interconnected and therefore indexes local network segregation. Eigenvector centrality reflects whether a region is connected to other highly central regions and therefore indexes embedding within influential network neighborhoods. These metrics were interpreted as complementary to betweenness centrality, which captures the extent to which a region lies on shortest paths between other regions and therefore indexes bridge-like hub function.

Because all nodal graph-metric comparisons were based on two-sided permutation tests, both High-SC > Low-SC and Low-SC > High-SC directions were examined. Of the five nodal graph metrics examined, betweenness centrality yielded the most widespread group differences. Degree centrality, closeness centrality, and clustering coefficient showed no significant group differences in either direction after FDR correction. Eigenvector centrality showed no significant High-SC > Low-SC effects, whereas Low-SC > High-SC effects were observed in the left frontopolar cortex (area a10p) and right primary somatosensory cortex (area 1). For betweenness centrality, six regions showed significantly greater values in the Low-SC group than in the High-SC group: left anterior agranular insular complex (L-AAIC; FDR-corrected p = 0.02), left parietal operculum area 4 (L-OP4; p = 0.02), right d23ab/posterior cingulate region (R-d23ab; p = 0.03), right primary visual cortex (R-V1; p = 0.01), right V4t visual cortex (R-V4t; p = 0.01), and left temporo-parieto-occipital junction area 3 (L-TPOJ3; p = 0.03). These reverse-direction findings are reported in Supplementary Table 16.

Cortical structure and myelination differences between high and low social capital

Finally, we compared cortical thickness and cortical myelin content between the High- and Low-SC groups, since these structural features of the “social brain” have been linked to social abilities. High-SC individuals showed significantly greater cortical thickness than Low-SC individuals in multiple regions, including the left inferior occipital gyrus, left lingual gyrus, left insula, posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), fusiform gyrus, supramarginal gyrus (SMG), angular gyrus (AnG), anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), and ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) (all p < 0.05, FWE-corrected; Fig. 3C; Supplementary Table 17). Myelin mapping further showed that the High-SC group had higher cortical myelin content than the Low-SC group in multiple areas-notably, bilateral angular gyrus (AnG), left posterior cingulate cortex, left superior frontal gyrus, left supramarginal gyrus, left superior temporal gyrus (STG), left inferior temporal gyrus (ITG), and left occipital gyrus (all p < 0.05, FWE-corrected; Fig. 3D; Supplementary Table 18). These structural differences provide convergent evidence of neuroanatomical distinctions associated with higher social capital, complementing the network-level findings above. We also examined the reverse Low-SC > High-SC direction for both vertex-wise cortical thickness and cortical myelination analyses using the same two-sided PALM/TFCE framework. No Low-SC > High-SC clusters survived TFCE-based FWE correction for either cortical thickness or cortical myelination. (Supplementary Tables 17 and 18).

Exploratory mediation analysis

No significant indirect effects were observed for any individual ROI model or for the five-ROI composite models. Thus, the measured social-cognitive traits did not statistically mediate the association between social-cognitive ROI myelination and SC in the present MRI sample.

Discussion

This study provides new insights into how social capital (SC) disparities emerge and persist across generations. In a large Japanese sample (N = 2,574), we observed that SC tends to cluster into two distinct levels, and this bimodal pattern was evident from childhood through older adulthood. Notably, our evidence indicates that intergenerational alignment of SC is more closely associated with relational and psychosocial factors (such as personal traits, parent–child relationship, and parental SC levels) than with conventional socioeconomic factors. In an independent neuroimaging subsample of young adults, we further demonstrated that high-SC individuals exhibit distinct brain characteristics, including more efficient “small-world” network organization and structural enhancements in key social-cognitive regions, suggesting a neurobiological basis for SC disparities. Together, these findings are consistent with divergent trajectories of social capital that are more closely associated with early social experiences and neural correlates than with material advantages.

The bimodal distribution of SC observed in our study aligns with prior observations that trust and social resources often bifurcate within populations rather than distributing uniformly31. Similar dual-peak patterns have been noted in organizational trust networks and interpersonal economic exchanges, implying that people tend to cluster into either high-trust or low-trust groups with few intermediates32. This divergence likely reflects path-dependent social trajectories. Early-life social experiences can send individuals down different paths that become self-reinforcing over time32. For instance, children raised in enriched relational environments-characterized by abundant trust, reciprocity, and social support-may develop strong interpersonal skills and confidence, propelling them into a high-SC trajectory later in life. Consistent with social learning theory, the present study suggests that social capital may be socialized across generations via modeled behaviors. Notably, one longitudinal study found that parents’ social capital predicted adolescents’ perceptions of parental sociability, which in turn predicted adolescents’ trust, reciprocity, and network size8. Thus, while social capital is not inherited genetically, a family environment rich in social connections can foster similarly high SC in children. In contrast, those lacking such supportive early environments may struggle to build social trust and remain on a low-SC pathway. Our analyses indicate that differences in SC were very slightly associated with income or education after accounting for other factors. Once an individual is set on a high- or low-SC path, feedback mechanisms likely stabilize these differences (for example, socially connected individuals gain more opportunities to connect, whereas socially isolated individuals face persistent isolation). Early-acquired social capital advantages or deficits, more so than inherited economic privilege, may underlie long-term disparities in social capital.

Interestingly, a similar bifurcation appears in Japan’s population-wide data on subjective well-being, hinting at broader social implications of our findings. National surveys consistently reveal a bimodal distribution of self-reported happiness in Japan, a pattern rarely observed in other countries, where responses typically cluster toward high well-being, yielding a unimodal distribution33,34. The parallel between Japan’s distinctive well-being distribution and the dual-peak distribution of social capital observed in our study may indicate a meaningful connection. Prior research indicates that social capital, including facets like community trust and mutual aid, is a significant determinant of individual well-being in Japan35. Moreover, culturally, Japanese conceptions of happiness emphasize social harmony and belonging more than personal achievement36. It is plausible, therefore, that one segment of the population, those embedded in strong, trust-rich social networks, enjoys very high well-being. In contrast, another segment with weaker social connections reports low well-being. Japan’s peculiar bimodal pattern of well-being may reflect an underlying divide between the well-connected and the less connected, paralleling the bifurcation in social capital. This linkage between social capital disparities and subjective well-being underscores the broader societal relevance of our findings.

Family social capital appears to be implicated in the intergenerational continuity of social capital4,10,23. Our research found that parents’ social capital (SC) levels were positively associated with their children’s SC. Our findings suggest that individuals who develop high social capital often originate from family environments rich in social capital themselves, characterized by parents who are socially engaged and who practice nurturing, responsive parenting. These findings support Coleman’s (1988) proposition that the social capital inherent in family relationships is crucial for a child’s development37. Our data revealed that factors such as parental responsiveness and encouragement of the child’s independence were significantly linked to higher social capital. In contrast, parental income or education had no significant effect. This suggests that children primarily acquire social capital through family socialisation—by becoming embedded in their parents’ social networks, observing norms of trust and reciprocity, and internalising relationship-building skills. Parents with richer social connections are more likely to expose their children to broader social circles and model positive social behaviours, thus facilitating social learning in the next generation. This idea aligns with prior evidence that personality traits such as extraversion lead to larger social networks in adulthood38. More extraverted, socially active parents may implicitly teach their children sociability by providing them with early opportunities to interact with others and by modelling trust-building in everyday life. In this way, social capital becomes self-perpetuating across generations through relational means. Even in the absence of economic privilege, a child who benefits from high family social capital, characterized by strong bonds of trust, open communication, and community participation, is better positioned to cultivate a robust social network in later life39.

The distinction between parents and adults without children should therefore be understood as a distinction between adult social-relational contexts rather than as a simple demographic contrast. In the parent sample, SC could be examined within a family/dyadic context, including parental SC, parent–child relationship quality, parenting style, and children’s SC. This design directly reflects family social capital theory, which conceptualizes social capital embedded in parent–child relationships as a relational resource that may support children’s development. By contrast, adults without children did not have corresponding parent–child dyadic variables in the present dataset. Their inclusion as a separate adult stratum allowed us to examine whether psychosocial correlates of SC, such as personality, empathy, and recalled childhood upbringing experiences, were also observed outside the current parenting context. Thus, the separation of these two adult strata was theory-driven and necessary for preserving the family-specific structure of the study. The results suggest that lower SC among parents is associated with less favorable retrospective childhood relational experiences, including less time spent with their own parents during upbringing. Together with similar findings in adults without children, these associations are consistent with possible life-course continuity in social connectedness. However, because the data are cross-sectional and based partly on retrospective reports, we cannot determine whether early parent–child relationships causally shaped the later capacity to build social relationships. Moreover, the similar bimodal SC pattern observed among adults without children indicates that low SC is not confined to the parenting context. Thus, the present findings cannot distinguish whether low SC among parents reflects social isolation emerging during child-rearing, pre-existing differences in social connectedness, selection into family roles, or a combination of these processes.

Beyond these social and developmental factors, our study provides novel evidence that neurobiological differences accompany and perhaps enable high levels of social capital. Individuals with high social capital (SC) showed significantly higher small-world index values in their structural brain networks than individuals with low SC. Small-world network architecture is characterized by an optimal balance between densely interconnected local clusters and short path lengths for global integration. It supports efficient communication across the brain22. The elevated small-worldness in high-SC brains implies that their neural networks may be more efficiently organised for processing complex information, which is essential for sophisticated social cognition. An efficiently wired brain can better coordinate the multiple specialised regions required for skills such as empathy, perspective-taking and social decision-making22. Consistent with this, clinical conditions involving social cognitive impairment, such as autism spectrum disorder, have been linked to disruptions in the small-world properties of brain networks23. Thus, possessing a brain network with high global efficiency could facilitate richer social interactions and greater adaptability, providing a neural advantage in building and sustaining social relationships. We also observed that high-SC individuals had higher regional betweenness centrality in numerous brain areas pivotal for social and emotional processing-including the prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), insula, and temporoparietal junction (TPJ). Betweenness centrality reflects the extent to which a given brain region acts as a hub connecting different neural communities. Greater centrality in these “social brain” hubs (e.g., medial/orbitofrontal prefrontal cortices, superior temporal gyrus, and inferior frontal regions) suggests that high-SC brains are organized to allow information to flow more readily through important social-cognitive nodes. In other words, the neural substrates for integrating cognitive and affective signals may be more developed in people with rich social capital. This could enable more seamless integration of social cues, for instance, linking perspective-taking with emotional empathy and even with language processing, to navigate complex interpersonal situations25,28. The high centrality of regions like the ACC in high-SC individuals is particularly intriguing given the ACC’s known role in monitoring social conflicts and guiding appropriate responses, which can facilitate smoother social interactions28. Likewise, heightened connectivity of the insula and inferior frontal cortex may reflect better integration of emotion with communication, potentially helping high-SC individuals express empathy and understanding more effectively. However, the observed network-level differences between the High-SC and Low-SC groups, including differences in small-world index and betweenness centrality, were moderate in magnitude and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Our study introduces a biological perspective to social capital research: just as some brains might be wired for exceptional memory or spatial skills, others appear optimally wired for social connectivity. The eigenvector centrality findings further indicate that the Low-SC group was not characterized by a simple reduction in all forms of nodal importance. In contrast to betweenness centrality, which emphasizes bridge-like nodes lying on shortest paths, eigenvector centrality emphasizes connections to other highly central nodes. The greater eigenvector centrality of the left frontopolar cortex and right primary somatosensory cortex in the Low-SC group may therefore reflect stronger embedding of a limited set of regions within influential structural covariance neighborhoods, rather than globally greater network efficiency. Taken together, the network findings suggest a qualitative topological difference between groups: High-SC individuals showed greater global small-world organization and widespread bridge-like centrality in social-cognitive and affective regions, whereas Low-SC individuals showed more focal eigenvector centrality increases. Given the modest MRI sample size and the group-level nature of structural covariance analysis, this interpretation remains exploratory.

At the level of individual brain networks, the high- and low-SC groups showed clear differences. High-SC participants had significantly greater cortical thickness in the angular gyrus, supramarginal gyrus, and TPJ regions central to Theory of Mind (mentalizing) and understanding others’ perspectives25,28. They also showed increased thickness in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) and dorsal ACC, which are crucial for empathy, valuation of others, and social decision-making25,28. Taken together with the greater small‑worldness and hub centrality observed in high‑SC group, these regional structural enhancements plausibly support a superior capacity for the social‑cognitive skills that underpin strong social capital22.In particular, structural augmentation of the temporoparietal junction and angular gyrus, core nodes for mentalizing, has been associated with more accurate inference of others’ thoughts and feelings. This competence promotes interpersonal trust and effective communication40–43. Likewise, ventromedial prefrontal cortex and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex may support emotion regulation and social valuation processes that facilitate prosocial behaviour. Convergent causal evidence comes from vmPFC lesion studies in humans and ACC lesions in non‑human primates showing decrements in prosocial motivation and vicarious reinforcement learning, respectively44–46. Higher T1w/T2w‑derived myelin content in high‑SC group across the temporoparietal, angular gyrus, posterior cingulate, and superior frontal gyrus suggests more reliable and efficient signal transmission within social‑cognitive circuits47,48. Myelin not only accelerates conduction but also tunes the timing of activity across distributed networks, enabling faster and more synchronous communication, an advantage for complex social tasks such as reading subtle cues and coordinating joint action49,50. Thus, the convergence of network organization, regional morphology, and myelin markers supports the interpretation that high‑SC group may possess neural traits better tuned for social information processing, offering a biological account of why some people more readily build and sustain extensive social networks6,18.

The exploratory mediation analysis further clarified the interpretation of the neuroimaging findings. Although the regions showing SC-related network differences overlapped with social-cognitive and empathy-related systems, the measured social-cognitive traits did not significantly mediate the association between ROI-level cortical myelination and SC. These findings suggest that the brain structural measures and measured social-cognitive traits should be interpreted as complementary correlates of SC rather than as evidence for a simple sequential pathway from brain structure to social cognition to SC. Social capital is a multidimensional construct that likely reflects multiple pathways, including personality, social motivation, family experiences, social opportunities, and broader network engagement.

Despite the strengths of this study, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes definitive conclusions about causality. We cannot determine whether enriched early social environments lead to the observed brain differences and higher SC, or whether children with certain predispositions (genetic or neural) tend to elicit more social stimulation from others and thereby develop higher SC. Longitudinal studies following families over time or intervention studies that experimentally enhance a child’s social environment would be invaluable for disentangling the directionality of these effects. Second, parents and adults without children were not age-matched, and direct comparisons between these two adult strata should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Although parents showed slightly higher SC than adults without children and this association remained after adjustment for age, sex, household income, and educational attainment, covariate adjustment cannot fully remove potential confounding by age, cohort, marital context, household composition, selection into parenthood, family formation, or other unmeasured life-course factors. The cross-sectional design also prevents us from determining whether becoming a parent increases SC, whether individuals with higher SC are more likely to enter parenthood, or whether both patterns reflect broader life-course processes. Longitudinal studies following adults before and after the transition to parenthood will be necessary to clarify how parenthood, aging, and changing social networks jointly shape SC. Third, our reliance on self-reported measures introduces the possibility of reporting biases. Future research should incorporate multi-informant assessments and more objective measures of social integration to validate and extend our findings. Fourth, the neuroimaging component was conducted on a relatively small sample of young adults, which may not capture neural differences present in other age groups. It would be fruitful to examine whether similar brain network characteristics predict social capital in adolescents or older adults, and whether cultural context modulates these neural correlates. Fifth, the employment-status sensitivity analysis should be interpreted with caution. Employment status was available only as a broad, dichotomous indicator and did not capture detailed occupational class, job type, working hours, job quality, unemployment duration, or retirement history. Furthermore, the non-employed group in the parent sample comprised entirely female participants (n = 120; 0% male), meaning that employment status and sex were substantially confounded within this subgroup, precluding independent evaluation of their respective effects on SC. Future longitudinal research should examine how specific life-stage transitions, including school-to-work entry, parenting leave, homemaking, unemployment, and retirement, shape the development and maintenance of social capital across the life course. Finally, although our overall sample was large and diverse in age, it was drawn exclusively from Japan. Therefore, the cross-cultural generalizability of the bimodal SC distribution itself remains unknown. Cultural factors, including norms around community engagement, interpersonal trust, reciprocity, and family interdependence, may influence both the expression and transmission of social capital. Thus, the observed two-mode pattern, as well as its psychosocial and neurobiological correlates, should not be assumed to generalize beyond the Japanese context. Cross-cultural studies using non-Japanese samples will be necessary to determine whether the bimodal SC pattern observed here is specific to Japan or reflects a broader feature of social capital.

Our findings contribute to an understanding of social capital as a multi-dimensional construct that is shaped by early familial relationships and embodied in the brain’s architecture. In contemporary Japan, concerns about social isolation among parents and declining community engagement are growing. Although the present cross-sectional design cannot resolve whether low parental SC reflects isolation emerging during child-rearing or pre-existing differences in social connectedness, the same bimodal pattern recurred across generations and outside the parenting context. Recognizing this pattern opens the door to preventive strategies: supporting individuals’ social connectedness in young adulthood (before they start families) might mitigate later isolation and its negative outcomes. More broadly, as low birth rates and aging populations change social structures, bolstering the social capital of caregivers and families becomes crucial for maintaining resilient communities. In conclusion, our findings illuminate how family social environments and neural factors together shape social capital disparities. Social capital, a key driver of well-being and social participation, appears to be cultivated or stifled by interpersonal relationships and corresponding neurobiological traits, rather than by economic resources alone. Recognizing the relational and neural influences on social networks can inform new interventions to help people develop and share social capital, thereby fostering healthier and more cohesive communities.

Materials and methods

Large-scale survey of SC

Data collection

We conducted a cross-sectional online survey in Japan from November 22, 2023 to January 9, 2024. Participants were recruited via NTT Com Research, Inc., and a total of 2,574 individuals completed all survey items. The sample comprised 731 parent–child dyads (731 parents and 731 children) and 1,112 adults without children. No participants were excluded from the analysis. The parent participants (n = 731, 320 female) had a mean age of 44.8 ± 6.7 years. The child participants (n = 731, 388 female) had a mean age of 10.5 ± 4.1 years. The adult participants without children (n = 1,112, 463 female) had a mean age of 52.1 ± 17.4 years.

Adults were stratified a priori according to parenting status. Parents were analyzed separately because they contributed parent–child dyadic data, including children’s SC, parent–child relationship measures, and parenting-style variables. These variables were not defined for adults without children. Adults without children were therefore analyzed as a separate adult stratum rather than as an age-matched control group for parents.

To address potential demographic differences between the two adult strata, we performed descriptive comparisons between parents and adults without children. Age and SC scores were compared using two-sided Welch’s t-tests, and sex distribution was compared using a chi-square test. We also fitted linear regression models with SC as the dependent variable and parental status as the independent variable, adjusting for age and sex. As an additional sensitivity analysis, we further adjusted for household income and educational background. All procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Tohoku University (Protocol 23A-01) and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from each adult participant prior to participation. For minors under 18 years old, consent was obtained from a parent or legal guardian. Participants were provided with a full explanation of the study’s purpose, methods, potential benefits, and possible risks, and they had opportunities to ask questions. They were also informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without penalty. To protect privacy, each participant’s data was anonymized using unique ID codes in place of personal identifiers.

Questionnaires assessing social capital and personality traits

We assessed social capital using four questionnaire items (two measuring trust and two measuring reciprocity) based on the framework of Kawachi1,51. In Kawachi’s original formulation, the questions refer broadly to “people” (including family members), but we modified the wording to focus on social capital outside the family. Specifically, following our previous study, we replaced the generic term “people” with “local and familiar people” or “friends” to ensure that respondents rated their external (non-familial) social capital. Participants answered each item on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 4 (“strongly agree”). Higher total scores indicated greater social capital. For younger children, a parent or guardian read and explained the questions to help the child respond, ensuring that even participants of early childhood age could provide meaningful answers.

Subjective well-being

We measured subjective well-being (SWB) in most participants using the Japanese version of the World Health Organization Subjective Well-Being Inventory (SUBI)52,53. The SUBI is a 15-item self-report instrument that evaluates an individual’s physical, mental, and social well-being. It comprises five subscales (confidence, transcendence, positive affect, expectation–achievement contingency, and negative affect), each derived from a set of three items. We administered the SUBI to the parent group, the adults-without-children group, and adolescents aged 11–17 years.

For younger children who could not easily complete the SUBI, we used age-appropriate scales. Specifically, children aged 4–6 and 7–10 years were assessed with the Child QoL scale, a well-being questionnaire derived from the KINDL instrument54. This child questionnaire asks about the frequency of various feelings in daily life (e.g., “How often have you felt healthy this week?”) and covers domains such as confidence, self-esteem, relationships (with family and friends), and recent emotions or experiences in preschool/school. There are two versions of the Child QoL scale: a 12-item version for ages 4–6 (responses on a 3-point scale: 1 = never, 2 = sometimes, 3 = always) and a 24-item version for ages 7–10 (responses on a 5-point scale from 1 = never to 5 = always). We chose these age-specific SWB measures to ensure developmental appropriateness and consistent interpretation of well-being across groups. For analysis, SWB scores were normalized within each age group so that scores would be comparable across different age ranges.

We also evaluated children’s overall life satisfaction using simple rating scales appropriate to each age group. For younger children (ages 4–6), life satisfaction was rated using a visual scale of five emoticon faces (from very sad to very happy), which we quantified on a 0–4-point scale. For older children and adolescents (ages 7–17), life satisfaction was rated on a numeric scale from 0 (“worst possible life”) to 10 (“best possible life”). The 11-point scale for older children is a standard measure used in international well-being surveys (e.g., OECD PISA and ISCWeB studies). As with SWB, we normalized life satisfaction scores within each age group to allow comparisons across different ages.

Parenting style and parent–child dyad relationship

Parents were surveyed on their parenting style using the Japanese adaptation of Baumrind’s Parenting Style Questionnaire55–57. This instrument contains 16 items that measure two dimensions of parenting behavior: responsiveness (also sometimes termed warmth or supportiveness) and control (discipline and supervision). Each dimension is evaluated by 8 items, and parents rate each statement on a 4-point scale (1 = “not at all,” 4 = “perfectly”). We computed separate total scores for responsiveness and for control to characterize each parent’s style. As a simple measure of the parent–child relationship quality, we asked each parent to rate their current relationship with their child on a single-item five-point scale. The item posed the question, “How would you describe your current relationship with your child?” with response options from 1 (“Very poor”) to 5 (“Very good”).

Personality characteristics

We next collected data on participants’ personality characteristics and related psychosocial factors to examine how individual disposition and childhood experiences might relate to social capital. Both parents and adults without children completed a battery of questionnaires including a Big Five personality inventory, a retrospective parenting experience questionnaire (six parenting factors, described below), and an empathy scale. These measures allowed us to capture each adult participant’s personality profile and their recalled relationship with their own parents.

The Big Five personality inventory provides scores on five fundamental dimensions of personality: Extraversion, Agreeableness, Conscientiousness, Neuroticism, and Openness. This broad measure of personality was included because certain traits (notably extraversion, neuroticism, and openness) have been linked to social network size and perceived social support in past research58.

Empathy was assessed using the Japanese Multidimensional Empathy Scale (MES)59, which was developed based on the multidimensional framework of the Interpersonal Reactivity Index60 to more explicitly distinguish self-oriented and other-oriented aspects of empathy. The MES consists of five subscales: self-oriented emotional reactivity, other-oriented emotional reactivity, susceptibility to others’ emotions, perspective-taking, and fantasy. In the present analyses, we focused on three subscales that were theoretically relevant to social capital and interpersonal connectedness: self-oriented emotional reactivity, other-oriented emotional reactivity, and perspective-taking.

Measures of upbringing (six parenting factors).

Childhood parenting experiences are known to shape an individual’s later relationships and social behaviours61,62. We therefore asked all adult participants (both parents and adults without children) to recall their own upbringing across six key dimensions of parental behavior63: time spent together (amount of quality time with parents), being scolded (frequency of strict reprimands), parental interest (perceived level of parental attention and interest), trust (felt trust towards parents), rule enforcement (strictness in enforcing rules), and encouragement of independence. Participants rated retrospective statements such as “I trusted my parents,” “My parents respected my opinions,” or “My parents scolded me when I didn’t do as I was told” on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). We computed a score for each of the six parenting factors for use in analysis.

In this paper, “parenting style” refers to the parents’ current behaviors (responsiveness, control) in analyses of children, whereas “upbringing” denotes adults’ recalled childhood experiences (e.g., time spent with parents, trust, encouragement of independence).

Sociodemographic data

We collected basic sociodemographic data on income and education. Income was reported in an anonymized categorical format with 12 brackets: from “ < ¥1 million” (category 1) in ¥1 million increments up to “ ≥ ¥15 million” (category 12). (For reference, ¥1 million is approximately USD 6,900.) Educational attainment was reported on a 6-level scale: 1 = junior high school, 2 = high school, 3 = vocational school, 4 = junior college or technical college, 5 = university, 6 = graduate school.

We also recorded participants’ current employment status. For the sensitivity analysis reported below, adult participants were classified into two groups: employed (those currently engaged in paid employment) and non-employed (those not currently engaged in paid employment). The non-employed category included homemakers, jobless individuals, and students. Because detailed occupational categories including occupational class, job type, working hours, job quality, unemployment duration, and retirement history, were not uniformly assessed across participants, this variable was treated as a broad indicator of current life-stage status rather than as a measure of occupation per se.

Data analysis

We used statistical tests to characterize the distribution of social capital and to examine group differences. First, Silverman’s multimodality test64 was applied separately to the parent, adults-without-children, and child subgroups to test whether their SC score distributions were unimodal or multimodal. We then identified the local minimum of each subgroup’s smoothed density curve (from a moving average) as the cutoff between Low-SC and High-SC groups, yielding a consistent threshold of approximately 16 points in all cases. To ensure robustness of the Low/High SC dichotomization, we repeated all group analyses across thresholds in 15,16,17 and additionally assigned groups using a two‑component Gaussian mixture model (GMM) with posterior probability > 0.5. All conclusions were unchanged. All statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS (v25). We checked the normality of variables with the Shapiro–Wilk test. We examined correlations using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients. For High-SC versus Low-SC group comparisons, p values were Bonferroni-corrected within the corresponding family of comparisons. Detailed demographic characteristics of each subgroup are provided in Supplementary Tables 2–4.

To characterize differences between the two adult strata, we performed descriptive comparisons between parents and adults without children. Age and SC scores were compared using two-sided Welch’s t-tests, and sex distribution was compared using a chi-square test. We further fitted linear regression models with SC score as the dependent variable and parental status as the independent variable. Parental status was coded as 1 for parents and 0 for adults without children. The primary adjusted model included age and sex as covariates. As an additional sensitivity model, we further adjusted for household income and educational attainment. These analyses were conducted to characterize group differences and address potential demographic imbalance; they were not used to infer a causal effect of parenthood.

Moreover, to examine whether current employment status could account for the observed SC distribution, we conducted an additional analysis stratifying parent and adult participants by employment status. Relative-frequency histograms of SC scores were generated separately for employed and non-employed participants in the full parent sample, parent subgroups stratified by child age (4–6, 7–10, and 11–17 years), the full adult-without-children sample, and adult subgroups stratified by age (young adults 20–39 years, middle adults 40–64 years, and late adults ≥ 65 years). We compared SC scores between employed and non-employed participants within each subgroup using two-sided Welch’s t-tests. P values were Bonferroni-corrected across the eight simultaneous employment-status comparisons. Cohen’s d was calculated as the mean SC score of employed participants minus that of non-employed participants, divided by the pooled standard deviation.

We selected Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression for predictive modeling because its L1-regularization performs automatic feature selection, which helps mitigate multicollinearity and improves interpretability by identifying the strongest predictors. While ridge regression (L2-regularization) yielded slightly lower prediction error in preliminary trials, we prioritized LASSO’s more interpretable sparse solutions over marginal performance gains. All models were implemented in Python (PyCaret) with tenfold cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning. For model development, each dataset was randomly split into a training set (90% of observations) and a hold-out test set (10%). We performed all model fitting and hyperparameter tuning (with tenfold cross-validation) on the training data and evaluated final model performance on the hold-out test data. We optimized model hyperparameters based on mean absolute error (MAE) instead of the default R2, because our residuals had a bimodal distribution that violated normality assumptions. MAE is a robust error metric that does not assume normally distributed residuals, making it a more appropriate optimization criterion in this case.

The predictor variables for the LASSO models included educational attainment, household income, Big Five personality trait scores, empathy (scores on the MES empathy subscales), retrospective parenting experience scores (the six parenting factors described above), and participant age and sex. The social capital score served as the outcome variable. This LASSO approach enabled automatic feature selection, helping to identify the most influential predictors for each group in an interpretable way.

Imaging experiment

Participants

This neuroimaging experiment included 108 healthy young adults (56 females, 52 males; mean age 21.8 ± 2.1 years) with no history of mental illness. All participants were right-handed and were recruited as volunteers. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant, and the imaging study was approved by the ethics committees of the University of Tokyo and Tohoku University (approval numbers 348–7 and 23A-01). Each participant’s age, sex, and social capital score were recorded (see Supplementary Table 14 for detailed demographics).

Assessment of social capital

Social capital in the imaging study was assessed in the same manner as in the large-scale survey. As expected, based on the survey results, the MRI sample’s SC scores also showed a bimodal distribution (Supplementary Fig. 6). Silverman’s multimodality test confirmed two distinct modes, mirroring the larger dataset. Using the score inflection point between the two distribution peaks, we divided the MRI participants into High-SC and Low-SC groups (see Supplementary Fig. 6 for the distribution and Supplementary Table 15 for multimodality testing results; demographics are in Table 14).

MRI acquisition

Structural MRI scans were acquired for each participant on a 3 Tesla Siemens Prisma scanner with a 64-channel head coil. We provided participants with standard instructions and hearing protection (MR-compatible earplugs) to minimize motion and noise. High-resolution T1-weighted images were obtained with a 3D MPRAGE sequence (TR = 1900 ms, TE = 2.98 ms, flip angle = 9°, field of view 176 × 260 × 260 mm). T2-weighted images were acquired with a 3D SPACE sequence (TR = 3200 ms, TE = 410 ms, flip angle = 120°, field of view 176 × 260 × 260 mm).

MRI preprocessing

Data preprocessing and analysis were conducted using the publicly available minimal preprocessing pipelines from the Human Connectome Project (HCP, version 4.3.0). These pipelines incorporate tools from Connectome Workbench command line utilities, FreeSurfer65, and FSL—FMRIB Software Library66 Map visualizations were generated using Connectome Workbench “wb_view” GUI (http://www.humanconnectome.org/software/connectome-workbench.html). Structural T1w and T2w images were preprocessed with HCP minimal pipeline (ver.4.3.0, https://github.com/Washington-University/HCPpipelines/tree/master) including the processing of PreFreeSurfer, FreeSurfer, PostFreeSurfer47. PreFreeSurfer carried out bias field correction and registration of the T2w image to the T1w image. FreeSurfer generated pial-gray matter (GM) and GM-white matter surfaces using additional information from the T2w image. PostFreeSurfer used a Multimodal Surface Matching algorithm67 (MSM) based on cortical curvature and myelination to space “164 k fs LR mesh”. Cortical thickness was measured as the distance between the GM and white matter. Since gyral crowns generally exhibit greater thickness than sulcal fundi, cortical thickness values were adjusted for folding-related biases by regressing out mean curvature from the data. Myelin maps were generated by computing the ratio of T1w/T2w image intensities, with residual bias field correction.

Graph-theoretical analysis

In this study, structural covariance networks (SCNs) were constructed using the HCP-MMP 1.0 atlas68, which parcellates the cerebral cortex into 360 distinct cortical regions of interest (ROI)69,70. SCNs represent patterns of interregional covariance in brain morphology and serve as a proxy for structural connectivity. Cortical myelin values were extracted for each ROI and adjusted for age, sex, and total brain volume via linear regression to control for potential confounds. Group-wise Pearson correlation matrices were then computed among all ROI pairs for the High-SC and Low-SC groups separately, yielding group-specific SCNs. To characterize the topological organization of the SCNs, we applied graph-theoretical analyses to quantify small-worldness and graph metrics. All computations were performed in R using the igraph package. Correlation matrices were thresholded across a range of network densities from 0.02 to 0.31 (in increments of 0.01), following previous studies66. The minimum threshold was set using the formula 2·log(N)/(N − 1) with N = 360. The maximum threshold was determined a posteriori as the highest density at which global network metrics could be stably computed in both groups. For each threshold, we binarized the correlation matrix to generate an unweighted, undirected adjacency matrix, retaining only edges with absolute correlation values exceeding that threshold. This procedure produced a series of binary graphs across different sparsity levels. The small-world index (SWI) was calculated at each density using the formula SWI (σ) = (C/C_rand) / (L/L_rand). Here, L and C are the average shortest path length and mean clustering coefficient of the empirical graph, and L_r and C_l are the corresponding values from an equivalent random graph and lattice graph, respectively. To capture regional network properties, we also calculated betweenness centrality for each node69. Betweenness centrality quantifies the extent to which a node lies on the shortest paths between other nodes in the network. To comprehensively characterize nodal network properties, we computed five standard centrality metrics for each of the 360 cortical regions of interest: betweenness centrality, degree centrality, closeness centrality, clustering coefficient, and eigenvector centrality.

Given the non-normal distribution of graph metrics, group differences in SWI and nodal metrics were assessed using non-parametric permutation tests. Mean differences between the High-SC and Low-SC groups were first calculated. For each permutation, participants were reassigned to pseudo-groups while preserving the original group sizes, structural covariance networks were reconstructed, and the corresponding network metrics were recalculated. Specifically, 1,000 permutations were used for SWI and 10,000 permutations were used for the nodal graph metrics. To account for variability across network densities, area-under-the-curve values were computed across the full density range for each metric.

Statistical inference for all five nodal graph metrics was based on two-sided permutation tests. Thus, both High-SC > Low-SC and Low-SC > High-SC directions were evaluated within the same permutation framework. P values for regional nodal metrics were corrected for multiple comparisons across the 360 ROIs using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate method, with FDR-corrected p < 0.05 considered significant.

Given the non-normal distribution of graph metrics71, group differences in SWI were assessed using non-parametric permutation tests, a method commonly employed in brain network studies. Mean differences between the High-SC and Low-SC groups were first calculated. Subsequently, permutation testing was conducted to evaluate the significance of group-level differences. Specifically, 1,000 random permutations were used for SWI and 10,000 permutations for betweenness centrality metrics, with subjects reassigned to pseudo-groups while maintaining the original group sizes. For each permutation, SCNs were reconstructed and the respective network metrics recalculated. To account for variability across thresholds, area under the curve (AUC) values were computed across the full range of densities for each metric. This process generated null distributions representing the hypothesis of no group difference. Group differences in all five nodal graph metrics were assessed using two-tailed asymmetric permutation tests (10,000 permutations). Two-tailed p values were computed as 2 × min(P_greater, P_less), where P_greater is the proportion of permuted AUC differences equal to or greater than the observed value, and P_less is the proportion equal to or smaller. P values were corrected for multiple comparisons across all 360 ROIs were considered significant at FDR-corrected p < 0.05.

Surface-based statistical analysis of cortical thickness and myelin

Group comparisons of cortical thickness and cortical myelination, indexed by the T1w/T2w ratio, were conducted using surface-based vertex-wise permutation testing with FSL PALM (version alpha119)72 and threshold-free cluster enhancement73 (TFCE). Group differences in cortical thickness and T1w/T2w ratio were assessed using vertex-wise GLMs with group as a fixed effect and age and sex as covariates. Statistical inference was based on two-sided permutation testing, and both High-SC > Low-SC and Low-SC > High-SC contrasts were examined. TFCE-based family-wise error correction was applied, with significance defined as FWE-corrected p < 0.05 based on 5,000 permutations.

Exploratory ROI-based mediation analysis

To examine whether measured social-cognitive traits statistically mediated the association between brain structure and SC, we conducted an exploratory ROI-based mediation analysis in the MRI sample.

Because structural covariance graph metrics were estimated at the group level, they were not used as individual-level predictors in the mediation models. Instead, we used individual-level cortical myelination values, indexed by the T1w/T2w ratio, from an exploratory, literature-informed social-cognitive ROI set. This ROI set comprised five right-hemisphere HCP-MMP 1.0 regions that showed significant High-SC > Low-SC betweenness centrality differences in the primary structural covariance analysis and are implicated in social cognition: right temporo-parieto-occipital junction area 2 (R-TPOJ2), right PGs (R-PGs), right anterior cingulate area 24 (R-a24), right medial area 9 (R-9m), and right anterior superior temporal gyrus (R-STGa).

MES Perspective-Taking and MES Other-Oriented Emotional Reactivity were examined as candidate mediators. Mediation models included cortical myelination as the predictor, the social-cognitive measure as the mediator, and continuous friendship-based SC as the outcome. Age and sex were included as covariates. All continuous variables were standardized before analysis. Indirect effects were evaluated using bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals with 5,000 iterations. Models were run separately for each ROI–mediator combination, as well as for a five-ROI composite brain metric created by averaging standardized cortical myelination values across the five ROIs. Because all variables were measured cross-sectionally, these analyses were interpreted as exploratory statistical mediation analyses rather than as tests of causal mediation.
