Examination of Peer Interactions During Cooperative and Competitive Board Games Among Children with Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder on and off Methylphenidate
Authors: Morgan L. Jusko, Brittany M. Merrill, Emily R. Sutton, Jennifer Sikov, Grant Edmondson, Timothy Hayes, Devon Tower, Amy Boeckel, Gregory A. Fabiano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Children with ADHD have significant difficulties with social skill application (e.g., waiting turn, interpreting social cues). Behavioral treatments to promote social skills, such as the Summer Treatment Program (STP), incorporate daily social skills training with systematic opportunities to apply skills as part of peer group interactions. Recreational periods are particularly well-suited for examining these behaviors, as social skills are essential during game play. The current study examined undesirable behaviors during board games and explored underlying factors (e.g., inhibitory control, sensitivity to reinforcement) that may contribute to undesirable behaviors during play. Twenty-eight children with ADHD participated in the current cross-over design study. At baseline, children completed a task of inhibitory control and their parents rated their child’s sensitivity to reinforcement. During the STP, children played competitive or cooperative board games, when taking either stimulant medication or placebo, in small groups of 3–4 children across 20 days. Undesirable behaviors were recorded during game play. Overall, medication use reduced rule violations, poor sportsmanship, and other behaviors. Only cooperative board games decreased teasing behavior. There was a reward x game interaction, such that those with higher reward sensitivity exhibited more poor sportsmanship during competitive games. Collectively, these findings suggest that stimulant medication attenuates undesirable peer interactions during board game play, cooperative board games may decrease teasing relative to competitive games, and the board game setting may be a potential avenue to better understand and address peer interactions in children with ADHD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10802-026-01442-1.

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by symptoms of inattention, impulsivity, and hyperactivity that cause impairment across multiple settings (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). ADHD symptoms are associated with significant peer relationship difficulties (Mikami, 2010; Nijmeijer et al., 2008; Pelham & Bender, 1982; Ros & Graziano, 2018; Spender et al., 2023), with more than half of children with ADHD classified as “rejected” by peers (Grygiel et al., 2014; Hoza, 2007; Hoza et al., 2005; Mikami & Hinshaw, 2003) and often disliked or less preferred by their classmates (Buhrmester et al., 1992; de Swart et al., 2025; Gardner & Gerdes, 2015; Hinshaw & Melnick, 1995; Hoza, 2007). Importantly, poor peer relationships persist and are linked with other psychiatric concerns in adulthood, including anxiety, depression (Nijmeijer et al., 2008; Powell et al., 2020) and substance use problems (Molina & Pelham, 2014). Given these long-term deleterious effects, a closer examination of social skills during common settings for peer interactions is warranted.

For children with ADHD, difficulties with peer relationships may be heavily influenced by the symptoms of the disorder. First, impulsive behaviors may disrupt the skills necessary for forming healthy peer relationships (Gardner & Gerdes, 2015; Wehmeier et al., 2010), as children with ADHD often intrude on others’ conversations and have difficulty sharing and waiting their turn, leading to peers to perceive them as annoying and exclude them from social groups. Second, children with ADHD may have difficulty with observing and attending to appropriate social cues (Cardillo et al., 2023; Hoza et al., 2000), which are essential to learning appropriate social skills. When children with ADHD are inattentive to these cues, they may struggle to interpret social feedback, ultimately hindering their ability to form and maintain meaningful peer relationships. Although children with ADHD may understand appropriate social behaviors (Barkley, 1997), they often fail to apply these skills consistently due to the interference of inattentive and hyperactive/impulsive symptoms of the disorder (i.e., a performance deficit as opposed to a knowledge deficit; Aduen et al., 2018).

Some evidence-based treatment programs provide children with ADHD the opportunity to learn and practice these skills in small-group settings. One particularly well-established and empirically supported intervention is the Summer Treatment Program (STP; Pelham & Hoza, 1996), a comprehensive, evidence-based behavioral program where children participate in a variety of activities within a same-aged peer group to reduce undesirable behaviors that often get in the way of forming meaningful relationships (e.g., interruption, teasing), and practice desirable behaviors that improve these relationships (e.g., helping, sharing) through a token economy. The STP has been shown to be an efficacious treatment for children with ADHD (Pelham & Fabiano, 2008), due to its integration of various social situations throughout the day that provide repeated opportunities to practice appropriate social behavior with immediate positive feedback, and children receive clear, immediate feedback and consequences for undesirable social behavior (i.e., contingency management; Pelham & Fabiano, 2008).

Recreational activity periods are one the most important times during the day when children are exposed to various social situations and can practice social skills. These activities involve children learning, practicing, or playing a game (e.g., soccer match) where they learn more about the organized game and implement various social skills during game play. Practicing recreational activities has been shown to improve sports skills and parent-report of peer relationships following treatment (O’Connor et al., 2014; Pelham et al., 1990). Within these organized recreational activities, children are also frequently exposed to situations, such as the other team scoring, that require them to use emotion regulation skills. Dealing with such challenging situations may improve their sportsmanship.

Not all recreational activities include sports, and some children have a preference for different activities. Board games are similar to sports in their rules, structure, and strategy, but they provide an alternative for children who do not have access to sports leagues or prefer non-athletic activities. There is a precedent for board games in clinical psychology; for instance they have served as a successful venue for evaluating peer-related social skills in children with high functioning autism (Lopata et al., 2022). There is only one study that examined peer interactons for children with ADHD during board games within the context of pharmacological treatment (Pelham et al., 2001). Within this study, children with ADHD were in small groups of four, each group supervised by a counselor who oversaw game play and encouraged competitive play (e.g., offering a small reward to the winner). When administered stimulant medication, children with ADHD demonstrated reductions in rule violations, disruptive behavior, and negative behavior frequency. This demonstrates that board game play offers an alternate, goal-oriented social setting within which to assess social behavior among children as well as demonstrating sensitivity of this observational paradigm to treatment effects. One limitation of this study was that only competitive board games were used.

Although there has been little work examining social interactions among children with ADHD within the context of board games, the existing literature including typically developing children generally suggests an improvement of prosocial behavior and enjoyment of the game while playing cooperative games (e.g., gaming, board games; Eriksson et al., 2021; Zan & Hildebrandt, 2003; Creighton & Szymkowiak, 2014; Marker & Staiano, 2015; Verheijen et al., 2019). However, in a study that focused primarily on competitive games in preschoolers, the authors’ argue that competitive games may be challenging for children, but also allow them to practice skills to manage coping with losing (Parlatan & Sığırtmaç, 2022). Notably, those children with low acceptance to losing had higher levels of aggressive behaviors when they lost the competitive games. For children with ADHD who have difficulties inhibiting their behavior during rewarding contexts (such as a winner-take-all board game), there may be greater likelihood of negative peer interactions.

During game play, children with ADHD often demonstrate more negative emotionality, decreased tolerance for frustration, and more negative affect (Gardner & Gerdes, 2015; Normand et al., 2011). Difficulties with emotional control during game play may be partially explained by difficulties with behavioral inhibition (Barkley, 1997). Behavioral inhibition, or poor effortful regulation of behavior (Barkley, 1997; Barkley, 2015), is thought to underlie several key executive functions (e.g., working memory, self-regulation of motivation). According to Barkley’s model, difficulties with inhibitory control, or the ability to inhibit a prepotent response, is the main overarching factor that contributes to deficits in the other domains of executive functioning (Barkley, 1997, 1999). Notably, there is a substantial evidence base to suggest that inhibitory control and play are directly related in samples of preschoolers without ADHD (Alamos et al., 2022; Trawick-Smith et al., 2025) and that better inhibitory control is thought to be related to better friendship quality in middle childhood (Zeytinoglu et al., 2023). Given the evidence to suggest that children with ADHD have difficulties with inhibitory control relative to typically developing peers (e.g., Barkley, 1997), examination of inhibitory control in the context of peer interactions among children with ADHD is vital. In studies that have examined parent-reported executive functioning with parent-report of their child’s social difficulties, no associations have emerged (Huang-Pollock et al., 2009; Kofler et al., 2011; Tamm et al., 2021). This may be because social interactions among peers are nuanced, and difficulties within peer interactions are more challenging to detect (e.g., Craig & Pepler, 1998). Despite this, only one study to date has incorporated observed peer interactions with children that have symptoms of ADHD and tasks of cognition (Huang-Pollock et al., 2009). Evidence from this study suggested partial mediation by executive functioning between social cues and verbal memory for a conversation. Although the integration of inhibitory control for understanding peer interactions during board games among children with ADHD is relatively novel (and exploratory), we expect that the ability to inhibit responses during game play would be associated with better peer interactions.

In addition to the role of inhibitory control in peer relationships, another component of behavioral inhibition is the self-regulation of motivation/arousal (Barkley, 1997). Specifically, reward sensitivity, particularly important within the context of games, may increase undesirable behavior during peer interactions. Although theoretically important to ADHD (Luman et al., 2010), research has only just started to incorporate this construct into treatment outcomes (Lynch et al., 2025; van Langen et al., 2021). To date, the findings suggest that greater parent-reported reward sensitivity is associated with more behavioral difficulties and less responsiveness to treatment (Lynch et al., 2025; van Langen et al., 2021). The current study adds on to this novel area of research by exploring the role of reward sensitivity during in vivo peer interactions.

The current double-blind, cross-over study aims to evaluate the behavior of children with ADHD during cooperative and competitive board games and examine the impact of stimulant treatment on behavior during board games. Furthermore, we aim to examine underlying factors that may impact behavior within the context of cooperative and competitive board games. We predict that: (a) there will be a direct effect of board game type, such that there will be fewer undesirable behaviors directed toward peers during cooperative board games; (b) there will be a direct effect of medication status, such that methylphenidate will largely improve undesirable behavior, consistent with past work (Pelham et al., 2001); and (c) there will be an interaction between medication status and board game type, such that cooperative board games will mitigate undesirable behavior during game play, especially when off methylphenidate. The second exploratory aim of the study is to examine the extent to which inhibitory control and sensitivity to reward interact with board game type. We predict that: (d) there will be a direct effect of inhibitory control, such that those with poorer inhibitory control will exhibit more undesirable behavior during board game play; (e) there will be an interaction between inhibitory control and board game type, such that cooperative board games will improve undesirable behavior, especially for those with poorer inhibitory control; (f) there will be a direct effect of reward sensitivity, such that those with higher reward sensitivity will exhibit more undesirable behavior during board game play; and (g) there will be an interaction between sensitivity to reward and board game type, such that cooperative board games will improve undesirable behavior, especially for those who have a stronger desire for a reward.

Method

Participants

Participants were 28 children (79% male, 21% female; M = 9.09 years, SD = 1.29; see Table 1) who were enrolled in the STP and a larger, federally funded trial investigating single and combined effects of ADHD interventions and accommodations in the STP classroom setting (WGC IRB #20211722). Participants were recruited for the larger trial via referrals from professionals (e.g., pediatricians, school personnel, mental health treatment providers), social media, and email distribution lists to caregivers. Informed consent and assent were provided for the current study. Participating children met DSM-5 criteria for ADHD (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). Evidence-based ADHD assessment included parent and teacher rating scales of symptoms (Disruptive Behavior Disorder Rating Scale; Pelham et al., 1992) and impairment (Impairment Rating Scale; Pelham et al., 2025b; Fabiano et al. 2006) as well as psychologist-conducted semi-structured and structured clinical interviews to ascertain onset, frequency, intensity and developmental inappropriateness of symptoms (Hartung et al., 2005; Pelham et al., 1992). ADHD, oppositional defiant disorder (ODD), and conduct disorder (CD) symptoms were considered present if either the teacher or parent endorsed them at clinically significant levels (Pelham et al., 2025b). Assessment data were independently reviewed by two Ph.D.-level psychologists to determine diagnosis. Any disagreements were resolved by a third Ph.D. reviewer.

Inclusion criteria for the larger study were (1) completing 1st – 6th grade, (2) Estimated IQ ≥ 70 (Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence, Second Edition, Full-Scale 2 [Wechsler, 2018]), (3) ADHD diagnosis, (4) willingness to utilize stimulant medication, and (5) ability to transport child to and from the STP. Exclusion criteria for the larger trial were (1) autism Level 2 or 3, (2) contraindictation for stimulant medications (e.g., prior adverse effects), and (3) history of suspension in the last year or school expulsion.

Procedure

Procedures for the study were approved by the university Institutional Review Board (IRB-24-0283). The investigation took place in the context of a STP (Fabiano et al., 2014; Pelham et al., 1998; Waschbusch et al., 2025). Each STP day lasted nine hours and was conducted Monday through Friday for eight weeks. Children were in age-based groups of 12–15 children with 5–6 paraprofessional counselors throughout each day. Children spent two hours per day in academic settings and the remainder in recreational activities (e.g., sports practice, sports games). During weeks 3–7 of the STP, children played board games during one of the 60-minute, daily recreational periods.

Board Games

Competitive and cooperative board games were alternated each day. Competitive games were defined as those that included advancing toward winning the game with a sole winner. Cooperative games were defined as those where players worked toward achieving a goal or goals, and all players could consider the gameplay a success if the goal(s) were met (see supplemental information for specific board game information). Board game designation (i.e., cooperative, competitive board game) was decided based upon use of these board games during past years at the STP, manufacturer designation of a “cooperative board game” (e.g., Outfoxed, Forbidden Island), and review of the objective of the game (e.g., sole winner vs. team-based winner). If a board game was not easily distinguished into one of these categories, the group discussed the categorization of the game.

Each board game period began with a pre-activity discussion where staff facilitated review of the board game period activity rules, examples of how to use the daily social skill during board games, and ways to earn and lose points during the board game period. Board games were placed at one of four tables in the room, with 4–5 chairs placed at each table. Each day, popsicle sticks with children’s names were randomly drawn to assign children to tables (i.e., assign to a subgroup to play the board game) to ensure assignment to the game and peers was random each day. Children then played the games for 45 min, and a counselor at each table facilitated game play and implemented the standard STP behavioral treatment and point system (e.g., prompted children to begin the game; reviewed game rules and procedures; praised positive behavior during gameplay). Following gameplay, children participated in a five-minute post-activity discussion to contribute examples of progress and problems that arose during game play, describe examples of social skill use or ways to earn points, and make suggestions for future gameplay periods. Each game period lasted exactly one hour. At the completion of the study, children selected their favorite board game, and it was purchased for them to provide compensation for participation.

Medication

As part of the larger study, children were enrolled in a double-blind, placebo controlled stimulant medication cross-over trial where they received alternating weeks (defined as five weekdays) of placebo or 0.3 mg/kg of methylphenidate (MPH) extended release (range of doses 10 to 30 mg; modal dose 20 mg). Cross-over order of administration was balanced such that ~ 50% of children in each age-based group received MPH and ~ 50% received placebo each week. Prior to arriving at the STP, the child was administered the placebo or medication in an opaque capsule by their caregiver, and administration was verified with the caregiver at drop-off daily.

Measures

Independent Variables

Inhibitory Control

The NIH Toolbox Flanker task was utilized as a measure of inhibitory control, given its developmental sensitivity, test-retest reliability, convergent validity, and ease of use for clinicians (Zelazo et al., 2013). The Flanker task has been used across the lifespan in clinical (e.g., Jones et al., 2022) samples. Within this task, children were presented with trials of either five fish (1st grade) or five arrows (2nd -5th grade). Children completed trials with two different trial types: congruent and incongruent trials. In the congruent trials, all arrows or fish pointed in the same direction and children selected the direction in which the “target” fish or arrow (i.e., the stimuli in the middle of the screen) pointed. During incongruent trials, the target fish or arrow pointed in the opposite direction from the other fish or arrows. When younger children in the study finished the trials with the fish stimuli, they were presented with the arrow trials. All children completed several practice trials prior to the start of the task to ensure understanding of the task. Children’s total raw score of the arrow trials was used as an indicator of inhibitory control. This value was mean-centered in subsequent analyses.

Sensitivity to Reward

The Sensitivity to Punishment and Sensitivity to Reward Questionnaire for children (SPSRQ-C) was utilized as a parent-report measure of sensitivity to reward (Colder et al., 2011; Colder & O’Connor, 2004; Luman et al., 2012). It has been validated in a sample of children aged 9 to 12 with strong convergent validity with tasks designed to measure reward sensitivity (Colder et al., 2011). In the current study, we utilized the Sensitivity to Reward subscale (combination Reward Responsivity, Drive, and Fun-Seeking), given the moderate (> 0.40) to large (> 0.60) correlations between the individual subscales; the theoretical overlap between reward responsivity, fun-seeking, and social drive with winning a board game; and consistency with the broader literature using this rating scale (Allen et al., 2018; Fosco et al., 2015; Vandeweghe et al., 2016). Parents responded on a 1 (Strongly disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree) scale. Chronbach’s alpha was adequate for the reward sensitivity subscale (alpha = 0.79). The reward sensitivity indicator was mean-centered in subsequent analyses.

STP Point System Dependent Variables

Frequency counts and other measures of behaviors that occurred in the board game period were collected daily across the medication/placebo and competitive/cooperative board game conditions. Behaviors were operationally defined in the STP manual (Pelham et al., 1998).

Six undesirable behaviors from the STP point system were isolated and analyzed as frequency-based dependent variables. Rule violations that occurred during board game activities (e.g., moving a game piece out of turn; using game materials inappropriately) were aggregated to create an overall count of rule violations. Poor sportsmanship behaviors (e.g., complaining; refusing to participate) were aggregated across the game period. Noncompliance in response to a staff member command was recorded in the board game period. Teasing towards other children during board game play (e.g., saying “Sorry!” sarcastically during Sorry!) was recorded to create an overall count of teasing across the game period. Interruptions that occurred during group discussions and board game activities were recorded. Finally, complaining during group discussions and board game activities was recorded1. Consistent with other studies (Pelham et al., 1998), the point system frequency counts were reliable. Throughout the study, a second observer watched the children assigned to a board game to collect inter-rater reliability data across the weeks of the study and the three groups of children. Intraclass correlations for frequency of rule violations (r = .85), poor sportsmanship (r = .79), noncompliance (r = .83), teasing (r = .82), interruption (r = .67), and complaining (r = .83) indicated correspondence between the frequency counts collected by the counselor and the independent observer. Standardized mean differences (Observer mean – counselor mean divided by pooled standard deviations) yielded modest differences for frequency of rule violations (d = 0.34), poor sportsmanship (d = 0.27), noncompliance (d = 0.11), teasing (d = 0.20), interruption (d = 0.28), and complaining (d = 0.24).

Data Analytic Plan

All children had complete data throughout the study with the exception of one child missing two days due to an illness. No missing data procedures were used, given that rates of missing data were low. The primary outcome across the two aims was the count of each undesirable behavior. Histograms and frequencies of each potential count outcome were examined (i.e., rule violations, poor sportsmanship, noncompliance, lying, verbal abuse, teasing, cursing, interruption, complaining). Means and standard deviations of each outcome with board game type and medication status are included in Table 2. There were insufficient instances of lying, verbal abuse toward adults, and cursing to include these outcomes in analyses. Therefore, rule violations, poor sportsmanship, noncompliance, teasing, interruption, and complaining were included as count outcomes in the analyses.

Histograms revealed over-dispersion and a high proportion of zeroes suggesting a negative binomial distribution (Hilbe, 2011). Multilevel negative binomial regression analyses were therefore utilized to accommodate the repeated measures data and non-normal count outcomes. Multilevel models included board game condition (Competitive vs. Cooperative), medication (MPH vs. Placebo), and their interaction as Level 1 predictors. Additionally, inhibitory control and sensitivity to reinforcement were included as Level 2 predictors and moderators of Level 1 independent variable effects in two separate sets of analyses. Data were brought into R Software (Version 2023.12.0) in long format. Analyses were conducted using the glmer.nb() function from the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015).

Discussion

Social behaviors and interactions present as a significant area of impairment among children with ADHD (Fabiano et al., 2006; Gardner & Gerdes, 2015; Hoza et al., 2005; Lee et al., 2021; Mikami & Normand, 2015). Past research has focused on the examination of peer interactions among children with ADHD in group situations (Hinshaw & Melnick, 1995; Hoza et al., 2005), improvements in social functioning with the use of stimulant medication (MTA Cooperative Group 1999; Pelham et al. 2005a, 2014), and improvements in peer interactions through sports-based activities (O’Connor et al., 2014; Pelham et al., 1990). The present study extends this work by examining social behaviors of children with ADHD within a full crossover design, in which each participant was observed both on and off medication across competitive and cooperative board games (Pelham et al., 2001). Furthermore, we examined exploratory moderators of the relationship between board game type and behavior.

Consistent with our hypotheses, there was a direct effect of stimulant medication, such that stimulant medication reduced the number of rule violations (72% fewer rule violations), poor sportsmanship (44% fewer poor sportsmanship), noncompliance (78% fewer noncompliance), and interruption (68% fewer interruptions), relative to placebo, consistent with prior work (Pelham et al., 2001). Prior studies have found the use stimulant medication improved undesirable behaviors among children with ADHD including self-control, noncompliance, aggression, and physical retaliation behaviors (Greenhill et al., 1999; Hai et al., 2022; Hinshaw, 1991; Hinshaw et al., 1989; Pelham et al., 2001, 2014). Interestingly, stimulant medication did not reduce teasing behavior. This finding is aligned with other studies that suggest medication does not result in an improvement of peer relationships (MTA Cooperative Group, 1999); the present study suggests one explanation for this result may be attributed to the lack of impact of medication on personally-directed, undesirable peer behaviors. Notably, there was no interaction between stimulant medication and board game type on rule violations, poor sportsmanship, and other undesirable behaviors (noncompliance, teasing, interruption, complaining).

Additionally, there was a direct effect of board game type, such that cooperative games reduced teasing behavior (106% fewer teasing behaviors). This is a novel finding within the peer interaction literature among samples with ADHD, and aligns with the broader literature that has reported children participating in cooperative games improved prosocial behaviors and decreased aggressive behaviors in comparison to competitive games (Greitemeyer & Cox, 2013; Jerabeck & Ferguson, 2013; Velez et al., 2014). The goals implicit in the cooperative games may have served as an antecedent to the promotion of prosocial behaviors (e.g., cooperation, problem-solving, communication) that would not typically be needed in a game where the goal was to compete to win independently. This study illustrates that the context in which children engage with peers may be as important of a consideration as the treatments chosen to address these peer-related impairments. There were no other direct effects of board game type on any other outcome.

While there was no statistically significant interactions between inhibitory control with board game type on any of the undesirable behavior outcomes, there was a marginal (p = .05) impact of inhibitory control with board game type on teasing behavior, such that those with poorer inhibitory control exhibited less teasing in the context of cooperative games. Notably, there was a statistically significant interaction of inhibitory control with medication status on teasing behavior, such that medication improved teasing behavior for those with better inhibitory control (49% reduction in teasing for those with better inhibitory control). This finding is interesting because others have found that stimulant medication improves other outcomes (e.g., classroom productivity) through improvements in inhibitory control (Hawk et al., 2018). While we did not examine inhibitory control as a mediator between stimulant medication and classroom productivity, our findings are in conflict with this work. It is possible that the differences in inhibitory control tasks as well as the timing of administration led to these differences. Alternatively, peer interactions during game play may pose unique challenges to those with better inhibitory control.

Finally, there was an interaction between reward sensitivity and game board type on poor sportsmanship, such that cooperative board games improved poor sportsmanship for children rated higher in reward sensitivity (151% reduction in poor sportsmanship for those with higher reward sensitivity). Notably, this finding is in contrast to van Langen et al. (2021) that found improvements in treatment outcomes for those with greater reward sensitivity; however, the current study utilized direct observations of behavior during peer interactions. In the current study, in the presence of a reward (i.e., during a competitive game in which there is one winner), children with higher levels of reward sensitivity struggled during peer interactions. It may be important, therefore, to incorporate games that leverage team-based rewards rather than a game in which there is a sole winner, depending on which social skills are most important to practice for each child.

Collectively, children with ADHD have been well-known to be highly disliked among peers (Hoza et al., 2005), exhibit behaviors that may make peers annoyed or avoidant (Pelham & Bender, 1982), and inconsistently apply social skills in peer settings (Mikami et al., 2017). Despite the importance of addressing these social impairments, many different approaches have been tried with equivocal results (Fabiano et al., 2021; Mikami et al., 2017). Indeed, teasing represents a particularly challenging behavior to treat in conventional intervention settings, as the child is removed from the social context where the behavior occurs (Mikami et al., 2017). The STP has long embedded treatment directly within social contexts commonly experienced by children with ADHD (Fabiano et al., 2014; Pelham et al., 1998). This paper extends this work by demonstrating additional types of social behaviors that can be effectively targeted, aligning with prior efforts that have directly addressed such behaviors within ecologically valid contexts (Cunningham et al., 1998).

Limitations

As with all research, the present study is not without limitations. While the STP setting promotes naturalistic behavior, the presence of highly trained staff, structured environment, and intensive positive behavior support system may limit generalizability of results to other settings where children play board games, such as community centers, schools, or homes. Specifically, counselor facilitation during board game play, the integration of the point system while playing board games (i.e., direct feedback on behavior during game play), and the daily pre- and post-board game discussion might attenuate these effects and limit implementation outside of the program. Additionally, the sample was comprised of predominantly White, elementary-school aged boys, highlighting the urgent call to increase the diversity within ADHD study samples to ensure reasonable generalizability of findings (Margherio et al., 2025; Merrill et al., 2024). The peer groups were also comprised entirely of children diagnosed with ADHD, which may not be generalizable to situations with less concentration of individuals with ADHD playing the game together. Furthermore, on the inhibitory control task, younger children (i.e., first graders) received more trials to ensure their understanding of the task relative to older children (i.e., second grade and above). Trials were used that were similar across grade level. This task has been utilized to examine inhibitory control capacity; however, it has limitations in terms of its applicability to real-world inhibitory control. In terms of the undesirable behaviors, the data provide evidence that there were elevations in teasing behavior within the context of competitive board games; however, it does not provide additional insight into whether the behavior emerged while children were winning or losing the board game or which game this may have emerged (e.g., multiple rounds of the game may have impacted undesirable peer behaviors). Finally, the counselors facilitated gameplay. Counselors were rigorously trained on the STP procedures for facilitating recreational activities and clinical superviors observed and met with groups daily throughout the summer to provide feedback. However, systematic fidelity data on counselor coaching during board games was not collected, resulting in an additional limitation of this study.

Importantly, only two interactions were significant in the current study, and board games were only significantly related to one undesirable behavior (albeit, an important peer-related outcome). Although several of our outcomes are trending (e.g., inhibitory control x board game on teasing; inhibitory control x board game x medication on teasing), the current study is likely underpowered to detect multilevel count outcome effects of undesirable behavior. Future work should incorporate a simulated power analysis to promote adequate sample size in detecting differences. Additionally, the exploratory analyses should be interpreted with caution given that this research area is relatively novel.

Future Directions

Finally, this study demonstrates that board games may be a reasonable setting for evaluating the effects of medication on the social behavior of children with ADHD. Approaches to evaluate effects of medication emphasize classroom behaviors and academic productivity, often through teacher ratings on checklists. To evaluate ADHD impairment in social settings, more intensive measurement approaches should be used (e.g., sociometric ratings; Erhardt & Hinshaw, 1994; Mikami et al., 2022), or field/gymnasium space for assessment in intensive treatment settings (Pelham et al., 1990, 2014). These approaches may not be feasible in many clinical settings. The board game settings may provide a reasonable middle ground to meaningfully assess social impairment in children, and provide socially valid assessment of medication, or through future studies, psychosocial treatment response. Future studies might explore board games as a venue for direct psychosocial intervention efforts, as well as continued assessment of peer interactions for children with ADHD. Early explorations of peer relationships in children with ADHD utilized similar small group activities (Pelham et al., 2001). Direct observation of peer interactions, as was used in the present study, may be especially critical as adults may miss nuances that enhance or impair peer interactions (Craig & Pepler, 1998). Given the reduction in challenging behaviors observed when using medication, and for the teasing behavior outcome during cooperative games, this represents a potential vehicle for promoting peer relationship skill application in children with ADHD.
