Regional differences, dynamic evolution, and driving factors of ecological resilience in China’s urban agglomerations
Authors: Xuesi Zhong, Rui Zheng, Wei Chen, Liqing Lv, Zijie Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
With the ongoing progress of urbanization, the urban ecosystem is becoming increasingly fragile, exacerbated further by the emergence of urban agglomerations. Based on the ‘Pressure-State-Response’ model and panel data covering the period from 2010 to 2021, the step-by-step vertical and horizontal tiered evaluation method is employed to calculate the scores of ecological resilience in China’s urban agglomerations. The Dagum’s Gini coefficient, global and local Moran’s index, unite strength and unite threshold, Markov chain, and geographic detector are utilized to reveal regional disparities, dynamic evolution, and driving factors of ecological resilience in China’s urban agglomerations. The results reveal several significant findings: (1) The ecological resilience in Chinese urban agglomerations exhibited a fluctuating yet upward trend. (2) Although disparities in the ecological resilience of China’s urban agglomerations are decreasing, differences between them remain significant. (3) Spatial correlation is evident in the ecological resilience of China’s urban agglomerations. High-high clusters are primarily found in the eastern and upgrading urban agglomerations, while low-low clusters are concentrated in the western and fostering urban agglomerations. (4) The number of cities exceeding the ecological resilience threshold is gradually increasing, with core cities playing a pivotal role. (5) The probability of transition in a city is affected by the ecological resilience states of adjacent cities. (6) Driving factors such as Internet penetration rate, informatization level, and innovation capacity have significant but heterogeneous effects on ecological resilience. This paper enlightens that spatial co-governance and zoning management of ecological resilience are crucial for achieving regional ecological security.

Introduction

The world is urbanizing at an accelerating rate, posing enormous challenges while presenting unparalleled opportunities for sustaining stability and improving the functionality of urban ecosystems. Urbanization fosters human well-being through economic structural transformations, job creation, infrastructure development, and abundant social goods production. However, as more natural ecological spaces are occupied and diverse resources are consumed, the conflict between humans and nature within cities intensifies1. This is accompanied by alterations to land cover, hydrological systems, and biodiversity2. In the process of urbanization, mega-cities and metropolitan areas are increasingly merging with adjacent cities to form a highly developed entity known as urban agglomeration. These urban agglomerations, which serve as vital contributors to economic growth, are characterized by a top-down structure, a high concentration of multiple production factors, compact spatial layouts3,4, and a robust interconnection between society, economy, and ecology5. Consequently, when black swan events like the COVID-19 pandemic and grey rhinoceros events such as the climate crisis occur, the ecosystems of urban agglomerations are more vulnerable than a single city and prone to spatial spillover effects that can trigger chain reactions6. Therefore, globally, urban agglomerations have emerged as crucial arenas for ecological governance7.

China, a prime illustration of the urbanization process in developing nations, is currently at a pivotal juncture of urbanization advancement, with its urban agglomerations steadily taking shape. Nonetheless, many of China’s urban agglomerations rely on traditional, extensive economic growth models that concentrate production factors8–10, resulting in resource depletion, environmental degradation, and potential vulnerabilities in their ecosystems11,12. Furthermore, the excessive concentration of functions and populations in core cities gives rise to “big city diseases”. At the same time, the regional ecological environment faces internal pressures and external threats, notably due to human-land conflicts13. As one of the countries with severe environmental pollution, precipitous ecological risks, and the greatest challenges in governance, China’s dilemma is mirrored in many developing countries worldwide. Traditional risk management approaches are no longer adequate to withstand this stage’s impending ecological and environmental hazards. Urban agglomerations, therefore, desperately need a newer and more effective prevention concept to guide their sustainable development.

The United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction has proposed “Ten Indicators for More Resilient Cities” and listed “Protecting urban ecosystems and natural barriers” as one of the key indicators. The concept of ecological resilience has proven to be an excellent analytical tool for determining the stability of urban ecosystems’ operational status14–16. In the “ecology of city” paradigm, ecologists emphasize studying cities from a complex systems perspective, viewing them as integrated complex ecosystems encompassing their biological, social, and economic components17. Research on ecological resilience follows two main trends. Firstly, ecological resilience is recognized as a crucial component of urban resilience, often analyzed in conjunction with other systems. This concept of resilience was initially articulated by Canadian ecologist Holling18. Resilience work expanded from adaptively managing ecosystems19,20, to adaptively governing complex social-ecological systems21, bringing in the role of institutions, organizations, networks, and agency in this context22,23. There are three typical models as the Baseline Resilience Index for Communities (BRIC), the Climate Disaster Resilience Index (CDRI), and the Driver-Pressure-State-Impact-Response (DPSIR) model. The BRIC, focusing on the infrastructure dimension, points out that medical capacity and recovery are indicators24. The CDRI incorporates ecosystem services, land use in natural terms, environmental policies, intensity of natural hazards, and frequency of natural hazards into its natural dimension25. The DPSIR model analyzes causal relationships between the components of the social system, ecological system, and economic system26,27. Secondly, research directly focuses on ecological resilience itself. A resilient ecosystem is able to minimize the impact of a disturbance and recover from the impact28. Ecological resilience in the urban system refers to the urban ecosystem’s ability to respond to crises, recover in a timely manner, and adapt quickly when affected by uncertainty and long-term pressure29. Under the framework of “Vulnerability-Sensitivity-Carrying Capacity,” vulnerability refers to the decline in ecosystem service capacity due to an inability to handle risks30, carrying capacity limits development intensity to preserve ecosystem integrity31,32, and sensitivity pertains to the probability of environmental issues arising from risk encounters33. By integrating various concepts, including those related to process and form, frameworks such as “Resistance-Adaptability-Recovery” and “Form-Density-Coordination” have been formed34. It is worth noting that no matter which perspective is chosen to explain the concept of ecological resilience, incorporating the key variable of human beings is essential35. However, there are some studies that just take landscape heterogeneity36,37, net primary productivity38, and natural disaster frequency39 as core indexes of the evaluation system of ecological resilience. This way may overlook the intricate interplay between human activities and the natural environment, which is vital for a comprehensive understanding and enhancement of ecological resilience.

Assessments of urban ecological environments and sustainable development have increasingly relied on ecological resilience as a pivotal metric, with research methods gradually transitioning from qualitative to quantitative approaches40,41. The most common combination of methods is the entropy weight method, Gini coefficient, and kernel density17,36. And there are other methods, such as scenario analysis42, index43, experimental methods44, threshold method45, geographic probabilistic approaches, and simulation model46, have been utilized by scholars to evaluate ecological resilience quantitatively. The main research objects of existing studies are single city47, economic belt48, and individual urban agglomerations49, with little attention paid to the urban agglomerations in a country. This makes it impossible to excavate the similarities and differences in the development of ecological resilience through horizontal comparison. Given the complexity and open-ended nature of cities, comprising multiple interacting systems, numerous factors have been identified by studies as influencing the development of ecological resilience. These factors can be broadly categorized into natural and human factors, encompassing economic development50, population agglomeration51, industrial structure52, soil and water conditions53, green infrastructure54, etc.

Previous studies have indeed provided valuable references for this paper, yet few have examined the ecological resilience development of China’s urban agglomerations from a holistic perspective. The development of urban agglomerations, however, exerts a dual influence on ecosystems. On the one hand, their formation may pose ecological risks. On the other hand, urban agglomerations serve as crucial material carriers for enhancing ecological resilience through regional cooperations and resource supplies. Recognizing that urban agglomerations represent the optimal trajectory for future urbanization system development, this paper, unlike prior research, specifically focuses on leveraging the spatial advantages of urban agglomerations to bolster ecological resilience. Firstly, in terms of conceptual exploration, we analyze the core essence of ecological resilience based on the PSR model. The PSR model is based on the notion of causality55, which is built upon the selection and measurement of indicators for three categories: indicators of anthropogenic pressures, of environmental states, and of societal responses. Then, we further discuss the applicability of PSR model within the context of urban agglomerations. Secondly, in terms of research subjects, this paper takes 19 urban agglomerations in China as its primary objects of research. Thirdly, in terms of methodology, the step-by-step vertical and horizontal tiered evaluation method effectively captures both the contemporaneous variations in ecological resilience systems across the “horizontal” dimension and their overall temporal trends in the “vertical” direction, thereby ensuring a comprehensive and unbiased evaluation. Additionally, the method of unite strength and unite threshold is utilized to quantify the dynamic integration process in urban agglomerations of varying development levels or different crystal structures at time points while assessing the pivotal role of core cities within each urban agglomeration. Furthermore, spatial Markov chain analysis is employed to discern spatial effects, and Geographic Detector is leveraged to decipher the factors driving ecological resilience development in China’s urban agglomerations.

Research framework

Fig. 1Research framework.

Research framework.

Considering the critical distinction between endogenous and exogenous driving forces within the system, the dual-layer research framework is shown in Fig. 1. In the field of urban research, urban ecosystems interact with social, economic, and technological systems, collectively forming the urban system56. Consequently, when evaluating the resilience of urban ecosystems, both the natural environment and human factors must be considered. Based on this, urban ecosystems can be divided into three subsystems: the natural backdrop, the resource environment, and the human support system57, and the concept of ecological resilience refers to the ability of urban ecosystems to withstand various pressures, manage them effectively to maintain their current state, and progressively enhance their functionality58. The “Pressure-State-Response” model is the most recent and widely accepted one for assessing ecosystem health59, and it also encapsulates the concept of ecological resilience. “Pressure” refers to the interference and stress faced by the city, “State” refers to the overall condition of the city’s current environment and social ecology, and “Response” refers to the system’s feedback to address the impacts of pressure, primarily reflecting human intervention60.

Specifically, stressors impacting urban ecosystems can be divided into sudden impacts and routine pressures. Sudden impacts stemming from unforeseen environmental events could be classified into natural risks and human risks. Natural risks include earthquakes, hail, freezing rain, epidemics, etc. The human risks, such as fires and hazardous material leaks, are often secondary to accidents and disasters. Conversely, routine pressures arise from objective risks linked to urban geospatial changes and subjective risks from social transformations. Objective risks include human-land interest adjustments like land requisitions during urbanization and pollutant accumulation during industrial development. Subjective risks involve psychological disparities between residents’ expectations of living environment quality and reality, differences in environmental awareness and participation, and intensified intergroup conflicts during pollution mitigation. Urban ecosystems have inherent self-purification mechanisms to cope with pressure related to the original natural foundation and ecological spaces post-establishment. However, these are insufficient, necessitating manual interventions like waste treatment facilities and green belts61. Green technology innovation improves energy efficiency, daily pollution control, and risk management in urban ecosystems62. Emergency rescue capabilities, like hospital occupancy rates, provide a reserve for the stability of urban ecosystems63. Environmental regulation can be further divided into formal and informal. The former mainly includes setting emission standards for pollutants, establishing emission charging or taxation systems, and emission trading systems. The latter mainly involves public awareness and negotiations between public organizations and pollution organizations. The intricate human-ecological relationship often sees the latter playing a passive role64, providing spatial and production factors for human activities. However, recognizing the ecological environment’s finite capacity to bear pressures is crucial, with boundaries controlling human activity intensity and resource adjustment degrees65.

As an important component of urban resilience56, ecological resilience will be influenced by various forces from other components. Organize existing literature and select the following driving forces for research. Firstly, Digital driving force. Digital technology plays a vital role in emission source locking, data analysis, real-time monitoring, and other aspects with the support of big data, cloud computing, and other technologies66,67. Secondly, innovation leading force. Increased scientific expenditure and Artificial Intelligence will significantly promote the incremental improvement of green innovation, which can improve energy utilization efficiency and production efficiency68. Thirdly, economic support force. Places with high levels of economic development have more power to optimize ecological resilience41. The increasing proportion of the tertiary sector in the economy, which is reflected in the industrial structure supererogation, will impact ecological resilience through emission reduction69,70. Fourthly, talent driving force. As the proportion of the higher educated population gradually increases, the voices of the people will be clearer: improve the environment and protect the ecology71,72. Fifthly, resource aggregation force. With the large-scale construction of transportation infrastructure in China, the openness of modern ecological resilience systems has gained prominence. The elements within these systems continuously flow and exchange with surrounding and even more distant regions through the transportation infrastructure. While this may lead to the dispersion of pollutants, it also brings in more high-quality elements essential for the development of ecological resilience73. Financial expenditure not only provides funds for green scientific research activities such as environmental remediation and carbon neutral technology tackling, but also provides ecological compensation. Ecological compensation includes the cost of protecting or restoring the ecological environment, internalizing the externalities of economic benefits through economic means, and investing in areas or objects with significant ecological value for protection74.

The development of ecological resilience is a long-term evolutionary process. Due to differences in resource endowments, economic development levels, and geographical locations, different cities display heterogeneity in the evolution path of ecological resilience in urban agglomerations. Grounded in the “Center-Periphery” theory, gradient development theory, and comparative advantage theory, the core city attracts various resources due to its robust sharing, matching, and learning capabilities. With the development of core cities, they tend to relocate outdated or polluting industries to peripheral cities, fostering a regional division of labor75. The core city avails of this chance to release more production factors into the service industry, emerging industries, infrastructure construction, etc., and the gap in the development level of ecological resilience between the core city and peripheral cities is further widening. Therefore, there is a phenomenon of differentiated development of ecological resilience within urban agglomerations. This phenomenon also occurs between urban agglomerations.

However, with the continuous improvement of economic agglomeration in core cities in recent years, on the one hand, the spatial differences in production costs have given rise to the demand for the diffusion of production factors from core cities to peripheral cities. On the other hand, the improvement of transportation and communication infrastructure has reduced the migration and exchange costs of industries between cities, promoted the deepening of spatial functional division in urban agglomerations, and helped to allocate industries between cities based on their comparative advantages. These changes can fully leverage the radiating function of the core city, thereby improving the mismatch of elements within the honest group, reducing resource waste, and enhancing urban operational efficiency. In addition, in the context of integrated development of urban agglomerations, the proximity of each city within the urban agglomeration will facilitate collaborative governance of ecological resilience. Local governments form strategic goals for common ecological governance through communication, cooperation, and resource sharing. This breaks the situation of “feudal town separatism”, reduces information asymmetry between cities, alleviates the dilemma of resource mismatch, and jointly manages the ecological environment. Thus, it promotes the enhancement of ecological resilience in the region.

Moran’s index

The Moran’s index is used to explore the spatial correlation of ecological resilience81. The global Moran’s index reflects the overall spatial clustering of a region, with a value range of [− 1,1]. If the value is greater than 0, it indicates a positive spatial correlation; If the value is less than 0, it indicates a spatial negative correlation; If the value is equal to 0, it indicates that the space exhibits randomness. The local Moran’s index can further test the spatial agglomeration of local regions, dividing the resilience level of grain production in each province into four regions: high-high (H-H) region, low-high (L-H) region, low-low (L-L) region, and high-low (H-L) region.

Measurement, regional differences and source decomposition of ecological resilience

Determine the ecological resilience development level by step-by-step vertical and horizontal tiered evaluation method

The calculation results are shown in Table 4. Due to space constraints, we report data biennially. It is noteworthy that our research interval is from 2010 to 2021, which poses a challenge to equal segmentation and makes it difficult to simultaneously consider both the first and last years. Therefore, we reported the situation for 2011, 2013, 2015, 2017, 2019, and 2021. From the perspective of temporal, the final measured values of ecological resilience in China’s urban agglomerations manifested a fluctuating yet upward trend during the observation period, indicating a continuous rise in this resilience level. However, in 2021, due to the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, the country’s focus shifted towards epidemic mitigation, leading to inadequate attention on ecological resilience and a consequent decline in its level. From the perspective of development levels, upgrading urban agglomerations generally have higher ecological resilience scores, indicating more effective urban ecological governance compared to developing and fostering urban agglomerations. This can be attributed to several factors. Firstly, these areas usually possess stronger economic strength, which enables them to have more advanced technological capabilities. This, in turn, allows for larger - scale investment in ecological restoration projects, such as wetland restoration and afforestation, and investment in state - of - the - art environmental monitoring and management systems, which enable more accurate assessment and prompt response to ecological changes. In contrast, developing and fostering urban agglomerations may be more focused on basic economic development and infrastructure construction, with relatively fewer resources allocated to ecological governance. Notably, the Pearl River Delta, as a hub of technological advancement and a pioneer in reforms, demonstrated exceptional performance in terms of ecological resilience. It has a high - tech - driven economy that promotes green innovation. From the perspective of geographical location, the eastern region, benefiting from superior natural endowments, solid economic foundations, and pronounced policy advantages, began reforming the field of ecological governance earlier. The early implementation of ecological - friendly policies in the east, such as the establishment of ecological compensation mechanisms, has encouraged local governments and enterprises to actively participate in ecological governance. In contrast, the central and western regions may face geographical limitations. Some inland areas in the central and western regions have relatively fragile ecological environments, such as arid and semi - arid regions with scarce water resources. These geographical characteristics pose challenges to ecological governance, and more effort and resources are required to improve the ecological resilience. Therefore, the urban agglomerations in the east have done better in terms of ecological resilience than those in the central and western regions. Focusing on the specific urban agglomerations, we can find that the Pearl River Delta, Shandong Peninsula, Yangtze River Delta, and Guangdong-Fujian-Zhejiang Coastal demonstrate consistently higher ecological resilience throughout the observation period. Geographically, they are situated either in the lower reaches of river basins or along coastal areas, a feature that inherently facilitates convenient transportation and economic integration. By contrast, Along the Yellow River in Ningxia, Lanzhou-Xining, Hohhot-Baotou-Ordos-Yulin, and Central Shaanxi Plain, which are located in the upper reaches of the basin, exhibit poor ecological resilience. This is primarily attributed to fragile Loess Plateau ecosystems, their monotonous industrial structures dominated by resource-intensive sectors, compounded by insufficient talent accumulation and technological innovation capabilities. Poor ecological resilience in Central Shanxi stems from coal industries, water scarcity, and inadequate environmental investment. In terms of the results of the traditional Markov chain (see Table 7), the probability values along the diagonals are all greater than those off the diagonals, indicating that the transition of ecological resilience types in China’s urban agglomerations is stable and the probability of maintaining the original state is relatively high. There is a phenomenon of “club convergence” in the ecological resilience of China’s urban agglomerations, and the types with low and high levels of ecological resilience have the highest probability of maintaining their original state in the next stage, at 69.3% and 67.76%, respectively. The probability of achieving “leapfrog” development in the transfer of ecological resilience types is relatively small, with less than a 10% chance of achieving cross-level transfers. In adjacent years, the probability of various types of ecological resilience transferring to adjacent low levels is higher than that of transferring to adjacent high levels. In terms of the results of the spatial Markov chain (see Table 8), spatial factors have a significant impact on the evolution of ecological resilience, and the probability of ecological resilience transferring in a certain city is influenced by the types of ecological resilience in adjacent cities. The ecological resilience of China’s urban agglomerations is synergistic with the types of regional ecological resilience. When the neighborhood type is low, the number of cities with low ecological resilience is significantly higher than that of other types of cities. When the neighborhood type is high, the number of cities with high ecological resilience in the time period is also significantly higher than that of other types of cities. In adjacent years, the probability of a city’s ecological resilience type shifting downwards is higher in areas with high ecological resilience than in areas with low ecological resilience. The reason may be that cities in China’s urban agglomerations with high ecological resilience optimize their industrial structure by transferring outdated production capacity to surrounding areas, thereby reducing pollutant emissions and improving urban ecological resilience. However, the surrounding areas need to bear more high emissions and high energy consumption from backward industries in a short period of time. Therefore, cities with high ecological resilience have negative spatial spillover effects on surrounding areas. However, when T = 3, this phenomenon has obviously improved, indicating that the overall level of ecological resilience of urban agglomerations will gradually improve over time.

Heterogeneity analysis

To delve into driving factors for urban agglomerations at varying stages, samples are categorized into three groups and analyzed using the factor detector. Due to space constraints, outcomes from 2017, 2019, and 2021 are selectively presented based on the principle of representative years (see Table 10). From an overall perspective, the significance of various driving factors has become evident, particularly for upgrading and developing urban agglomerations that exhibit higher levels of development. Conversely, in fostering urban agglomerations, the driving effects of these factors are less pronounced. Among the driving factors, the Internet penetration rate, level of informatization, innovation level, and economic development stand out as particularly important. From the perspective of heterogeneity, the most pronounced driving effects of upgrading urban agglomerations stem from economic development, Internet penetration rate, and innovation level. Benefiting from a robust economic foundation, a mature urban agglomeration structure, and widespread coverage of next-generation network infrastructure, these urban agglomerations are able to attract and concentrate a multitude of production and innovation factors.

Furthermore, the ecological resilience of their core city exerts a positive influence on the surrounding cities, enhancing the ecological resilience of the entire urban agglomeration. Developing urban agglomerations, which belong to relatively well-developed urban agglomerations in their respective regions, demonstrate significant driving effects from economic development, level of informatization, and innovation level. While traditional information infrastructure like 3G base stations is already in place, the proliferation of the Internet necessitates the deployment of new-generation network infrastructure. Consequently, the level of informatization has a more substantial impact on this type of urban agglomeration compared to the Internet penetration rate alone. Fostering urban agglomerations are mainly located in the western region of China and need to undertake industries transferred from the eastern region. Therefore, the development of ecological resilience is relatively backward. As the overall level of ecological resilience in China’s urban agglomerations continues to improve and optimize, these urban agglomerations are recognizing the need for transformation and are working towards strengthening their core competitiveness by adjusting industrial structures and fostering an environment conducive to innovation. The driving effect of optimizing industrial structures and enhancing innovation levels is gradually gaining prominence.

Discussion

The dual-layer framework adoption

Considering the critical distinction between endogenous and exogenous driving forces within the system, this study proposes a dual-layer framework that aligns with emerging global trends in multi-dimensional resilience modeling. In this design, ecological resilience is positioned as a distinct yet interconnected component of urban sustainability, aiming to ensure clarity in system-specific dynamics while acknowledging cross-system interdependencies. The “Pressure-State-Response” (PSR) model is employed to develop an evaluation index system for ecological resilience. It establishes pressure-related indicators from perspectives of “energy conservation” and “emission reduction,” explores state-related indicators focusing on maintaining ecosystem stability from angles of “resource endowment” and “human intervention,” and develops response-related indicators concerning “handling capacity” and “regulatory capacity.” This research confirms the broad applicability of the PSR model in ecological research. Some scholars use the DPSIR model or the “economy-ecology” resilience coupling coordination model to describe the causal relationship between the economy and the environment, incorporating the potential coupling of ecosystems with other systems into the evaluation index system for ecological resilience85–87. To emphasize the intrinsic characteristics of ecosystems, this research includes indicators with strong ecological attributes, such as green technology, in the evaluation index system for ecological resilience, while incorporating indicators with strong economic and social attributes, such as per capita GDP and human capital, into the driving factor system for ecological resilience.

Global and Chinese perspectives on urban resilience

In the global context, climate change remains a central focus in urban resilience research. For instance, Almulhim and Cobbinah (2024)88 reported that, over the past 30 years, Saudi Arabia has experienced 14 natural disasters linked to climate change, including flash floods and heatwaves, which collectively affected approximately 30,000 individuals and caused damages exceeding USD 450 million. These events highlight how climate-induced stresses, such as intensified heatwaves and erratic rainfall, exacerbate vulnerabilities in urban systems, particularly through aging infrastructure like outdated drainage networks that fail to withstand extreme weather, leading to cascading failures in water supply and transportation. In response, urban planning must prioritize ecological protection, such as expanding green infrastructure and restoring natural floodplains, to bolster resilience by enhancing ecosystems’ capacity to buffer climate shocks. Bush and Doyon (2019)89 emphasize that urban planning and resilience-building are imperative for protecting natural assets, repairing ecological damage, and rebalancing unsustainable development patterns through measures like land use efficiency improvement, spatial allocation optimization, and functional zoning. This aligns with global frameworks, such as the UN Sustainable Development Goals, which prioritize integrated environmental and social governance. Globally, the Rockefeller Foundation’s 100 Resilient Cities Programme defines resilience as the capacity of cities to “survive, adapt, and grow” across social, ecological, and technical systems. Rotterdam’s participation in this program exemplifies how integrating nature-based climate adaptation with social equity initiatives enhances systemic resilience90. Adam and Bevan (2005) proposed urban resilience frameworks encompassing social, economic, natural, institutional, and physical systems91. In contrast, Chinese resilience research, such as Zhao et al. (2022), has operationalized this complex framework through empirical studies, but pays attention to institution92. Zhao et al. (2022) further found that while southern China saw reduced low-resilience cities, institutional resilience, particularly in cross-departmental policy coordination and long-term ecological planning, remained a critical weakness.

Further focusing on urban agglomerations, research findings indicate that China’s urban agglomerations exhibit a fluctuating upward trend in ecological resilience, aligning with conclusions by Wang et al. (2023) and Yuan et al. (2024)93,94. Disparities among urban agglomerations are gradually narrowing, yet “low-level traps” and “high-level monopolies” persist. To assist cities with low ecological resilience in escaping these traps, adopting a holistic approach and strengthening interactions with surrounding cities is crucial, consistent with Li et al. (2023)17. Building on this foundation, this research focuses on the characteristics of urban agglomerations and further explores the path of strengthening their ecological resilience network. China’s urban agglomerations are characterized by complexity and uneven development, with significant differences in ecological background, resource endowment, and socio-economic conditions. Therefore, implementing policies according to local conditions is important. Additionally, the research finds that core cities play a pivotal role in urban agglomerations, and cities closer to the core tend to cross the threshold of ecological resilience earlier. Identifying the functions of each city within the urban agglomeration is crucial. This research aims to provide insights into utilizing urban agglomerations to form patterns of spatial co-governance and zoned management of ecological resilience, enhancing the network resilience of urban ecosystems. Jiang et al. (2024) examines the impact of the “regional integration” policy of urban agglomerations on urban resilience, pointing out core cities are influenced by “regional integration” policy, and their urban resilience is more robust95. This is a strategy distinct from Western bottom-up models. However, global gaps in resilience planning, as noted by Krishnan et al. (2023), apply universally: both Southern and Northern Hemisphere cities underimplement resilience concepts in long-term planning96. In the context of new technological advancements and digital economic development, this study also highlights the importance of innovative and digital driving forces in enhancing ecological resilience, alongside the critical role of government policy in shaping resilience outcomes.

Future research directions

There are aspects of this research that are less considered due to the unobtainable data. Critical indicators such as biodiversity and post-2021 pandemic-era data were absent. Biodiversity metrics are essential for capturing ecosystem complexity and resilience, while the COVID-19 pandemic’s impact on urban mobility, economic structures, and “green stimulus” policies, such as remote work-driven suburban sprawl or renewable energy transitions, remains unquantified. Moreover, the focus on macro-level drivers has overshadowed micro-mechanisms like community-level adaptation strategies or intra-urban green infrastructure interactions, which are pivotal for granular resilience analysis. To address these gaps, future research should prioritize integrating multidimensional data, including biodiversity indices and pandemic-era urban trends, into a holistic evaluation framework. Additionally, exploring how core cities in agglomerations can drive resilience through green technology spillover and policy coordination, while balancing macro-trends with micro-scale dynamics, will be crucial to enhancing the comprehensiveness and applicability of ecological resilience research.

Conclusions and policy recommendations

Conclusion

Firstly, the overall trend of ecological resilience development in urban agglomerations exhibits a fluctuating upward trajectory, accompanied by notable spatial imbalance characteristics. The Gini coefficient for the development level of ecological resilience in urban agglomerations shows a fluctuating downward trend, indicating that while disparities exist among urban agglomerations, these gaps are narrowing. The differences between urban agglomerations and trans-variation intensity contribute significantly to the Gini coefficient. There is a notable disparity in the development level of ecological resilience between eastern urban agglomerations and western urban agglomerations.

Secondly, urban agglomerations’ ecological resilience shows spatial correlation. High-high clusters are mainly in the eastern and upgrading agglomerations, while low-low clusters are in the western and fostering agglomerations. More cities exceed resilience thresholds, with core cities leading improvements. However, due to differences in development, location, and structure, resilience unity varies among agglomerations. The degree of ecological resilience unity in upgrading urban agglomerations, eastern urban agglomerations, and urban agglomerations with a monocentric crystal combination is particularly pronounced. Different core cities lead to varying degrees of unity.

Thirdly, China’s urban agglomerations have stable ecological resilience, with a low chance of significant change and a high tendency to maintain current levels, emphasizing the need for sustained efforts to enhance ecological resilience. Spatial factors significantly influence the evolution of ecological resilience in China’s urban agglomerations, with adjacent cities’ resilience types affecting each other’s transition probabilities. Cities with high ecological resilience tend to transfer outdated industries to neighbors, causing negative spillovers, but over time, the overall resilience of urban agglomerations improves.

Fourthly, driving factors such as the Internet penetration rate, level of informatization, innovation level, and economic development are significantly driving ecological resilience. Furthermore, urban agglomerations at different stages highlight the significance of the Internet penetration rate, informatization, innovation, and economic development as key drivers, especially for upgrading and developing agglomerations. Fostering urban agglomerations in western China, which are receiving industries from the east, are transforming to strengthen competitiveness through industrial restructuring and fostering innovation, with a growing focus on optimizing structures and enhancing innovation levels.

Policy recommendations

Firstly, Promote joint conservation of ecological resilience spaces and strengthen coordinated environmental governance. Address significant disparities among and within China’s urban agglomerations regarding development levels, resource allocation, and urban ecological resilience. Facilitate experience sharing on ecological governance, enhance regional policy coordination, and establish mechanisms for information sharing, conference consultations, joint law enforcement, and supervision. Clarify responsibility divisions to improve collaborative governance. Promote regional infrastructure interconnection, coordinated industrial development, and shared public services to support resilient urban ecosystems. Developed urban agglomerations should assist less developed ones, aiming to break free from the “resource curse” and build robust urban ecosystems.

Secondly, zoned management of ecological resilience should be implemented, and an ecological security framework established. Define functional zones at the city level within urban agglomerations, focusing on differentiated development to form a complementary and efficient urban ecological governance layout. Highlight cities with higher ecological resilience as pioneers in green development, leveraging their spatial spillover effects. Cities with lower resilience should focus on strengthening ecological space protection and utilizing positive spillovers from neighboring high-resilience cities to narrow development gaps and mitigate localized risks, preventing wider ecological resilience risks.

Thirdly, leverage technological advancements and digital transformation to enhance ecological resilience. Given the importance of factors like internet penetration, informatization, and innovation, integrate digital technologies into urban management—using big data and AI to monitor better and manage environmental resources. Foster a culture of innovation to develop new solutions that further boost ecological resilience. Recognize regional differences and tailor ecological resilience measures accordingly, ensuring each urban agglomeration considers its unique development context when implementing policies.
