Moving milk and shifting risk: A mixed methods assessment of food safety risks along informal dairy value chains in Kisumu, Kenya
Authors: Lilly Catherine Smith, Andrew Stringer, Kevin Omondi Owuor, Bryson Alberto Ndenga, Christabel Winter, Keli Nicole Gerken
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnimal sourced foods (ASF) are important for global food security and in mitigating the impact of undernutrition. Across Sub-Saharan Africa, demand and urbanization continue to increase, creating a greater divide between farm-level production and consumption. Food safety residues including antibiotics and aflatoxin residues often originate at the production site, and risk can shift as milk is transported. In 2022, milk samples (n = 190) were collected from eight milk vendors in Kisumu, Kenya and tested for betalactams and aflatoxins with cut off values of 300 parts per billion (ppb) and 200 parts per trillion (ppt), respectively. Data was collected on the origin and containers of each sample, and the eight vendors responded to an open-ended questionnaire regarding the structure and challenges in their milk business.Aflatoxin residues were detected in 7 % (13/190) of samples with no samples (0/190) testing positive for antibiotic residues. Overall, 80 % samples were collected from metal transportation containers, and no milk containers >20 l tested positive for aflatoxins. The origin location(s) of milk was not significantly associated with residues (p = 0.44). Vendor questionnaire data were summarized into three themes: 1) Physical properties of milk are understood to influence quality, 2) Fluctuating availability of milk impacts profits and is the main influence on choice of supplier, and 3) Trust and rapport among value chain actors are key to mitigating business challenges.Our results indicate that farm-level practices around antibiotic use is not posing a major milk safety risk to urban consumers. Use of large 50-l containers for transporting milk may be protective as risk of aflatoxin residues is diluted below key thresholds. We recommend supporting existing relationships and identifying drivers that build trust among informal value chain actors which could strengthen collaboration and allow actors to move towards a shared goal of reliable and safe milk available for urban consumers.Keywords: Aflatoxin residues, Food safety and security, Antibiotic residues, Informal milk value chain

Introduction

In the coming decades, rapid population growth and urbanization in Sub-Saharan Africa will exacerbate existing food security challenges in vulnerable countries as 70 % of the anticipated 9.8 billion population will reside in urban areas [1,2]. Urbanization leads to a concentrated demand for food which leads to outsourcing from the surrounding rural areas [3,4]. Safety must be viewed as important as quantity [5], yet assessing food safety risks in the urban setting requires an analysis of complex supply chains with various actors from the rural production to urban consumers [1]. Animal sourced foods (ASF) play an important role in food security by addressing the impact of malnutrition including stunting, wasting, and micronutrient deficiencies [6]. Milk, a nutritionally dense ASF, has profound health benefits, but has also been shown to have numerous contaminants, including aflatoxin residues [7,8]. Aflatoxin residues present in milk are the metabolite, M1 (AFM1), of the aflatoxin, B1 (AFB1), that animals ingest directly from feed contamination. Unlike AFB1, the aflatoxin M1 (AFM1) present in contaminated milk do not contribute substantially to liver cancer as highlighted in a recent risk assessment [9].

Understanding the degree to which milk products are unsafe, and the social and cultural context in which these risks are modulated is key to developing effective and acceptable food safety interventions.

The demand for milk in sub-Saharan Africa is expected to triple from that of 40 years earlier, an increase of 175 %, by 2050 [10]. In Kenya, the majority (60 %) is produced in the Rift Valley and Central regions, where there is abundant supply of grazing land, and milk is transported to cities where there is marketing opportunities associated with urban demand [4]. In addition, 70 % of all marketed milk flows through a complex, and difficult to trace informal, rather than formal commercialized, value chain [1]. Understanding the informal supply chain is critical for identifying food safety risks and locating where interventions would have the greatest impact on health outcomes [10].

The bulk of food safety research is focused on either production or consumption, neglecting the steps in between including processing, storage, and transportation, which are reported to contribute to 30–40 % of all economic value added in food value chains [11]. As urbanization and shifting land areas suitable for dairy production [12] contribute to longer and more complex livestock supply chains, researchers have recently advocated for holistic, interdisciplinary approaches to addressing the “missing middle” in livestock value chains [13]. The informal dairy market has historically been less regulated than the formal sector and is classically perceived as less safe [4,14]Hence, formalization is often pushed as a method to improve food security [4,14]. As initiatives within the informal system benefit a wider population, Vernooij and others present the two systems as a continuum of commercialization, intensification, and formalization to decrease the divisive narrative [15]. Indeed, those operating within the informal middle play a key role in addressing food insecurity issues and their viewpoints, challenges, and perspective should be included in any intervention aimed to improve food security, whilst protecting livelihoods and nutritional benefits of milk.

Here, we present results from testing milk for aflatoxins and antibiotic residues that was collected at urban milk vendors after being transported from the surrounding rural regions. We also present a thematic analysis of perceptions and opinions of milk vendors on what influences milk quality and safety and their relationships with other value chain actors.

Methods

Study overview

This cross-sectional study was embedded within a longitudinal study that collected pooled milk samples weekly from informal value chain vendors in Kisumu, western Kenya. Samples were screened for antibiotics (beta-lactam and tetracycline) and aflatoxin residues, and vendors responded to a semi-structured survey regarding their perception of safety influences and challenges among value chain actors.

Study site

Kisumu city of Kisumu County, Western Kenya, is located on the shores of Lake Victoria (0.0917° S, 34.7680° E). According to census data from 2019, Kisumu County has a population of 1,155,574 people with an average household size of 4.4 people. The land area covers 2085 sq. km with a population density of 554 people per square kilometer [16].

Ethics

This study operated within existing approvals in which we had permission to test milk for other food safety contaminants and interview vendors. The Kenya Medical Research Institute - Scientific Ethics Review Unit (KEMRI/SERU/CGHR/03–07-390/4293) was the primary institute responsible for the consent of research participants, ensuring ethical standards were met, and local authorities were engaged.

Study design

Collection of milk sample

Milk samples that had been transported from rural farms to urban Kisumu by couriers, were collected from eight vendor shops over six weeks in June and July 2022. Vendors were purposefully selected using key informants across different areas of the city, and their combined origin locations are displayed in Fig. 1.

Map of western Kenya study site where milk was collected from in urban Kisumu (green) and the surrounding counties where milk was sourced (red). (For interpretation of the references to colour in this figure legend, the reader is referred to the web version of this article.)

Milk samples (10 ml) were collected in 15 ml conical tubes each week, and we collected information on the supplier origin and specifics of the milk. Milk samples were transported on ice and held for a maximum of 12 h at 4 °C and tested within 48 h.

Testing milk samples for antibiotic residues and aflatoxins

A commercially available lateral flow assay “Rapid One Step Assay (ROSA)” was used to test for beta-lactam residues, tetracycline residues, and aflatoxins (Charm Sciences Inc., Lawrence, MA, USA). As per manufacture protocols, samples were tested, by pipetting 300 μl of a milk sample onto a testing strip placed in a Charm ROSA incubator set at 56 +/− 1 °C. The sensitivity and specificity for cut off values for aflatoxin, beta-lactams, and tetracyclines have been predetermined by the manufacturer, and detailed values for various antibiotics are available by request to Charm Sciences. In brief, the beta-lactam cut off value was 300 parts per billion (ppb) and aflatoxin test was 200 parts per trillion (ppt). For aflatoxins, the detection limit depends on the incubation time. A three-minute incubation corresponds to a 350 ppt level of detection and if the aflatoxin test incubation period is extended to eight minutes, this reflects a 200 ppt cutoff value. All results were read visually based on the relative darkness to lightness of the control and test lines on the strip, inconclusive tests were repeated. The cut off value of 200 ppt is between the cutoff value of 50 ppt allowed in the European Union (EU) and the United States Food and Drug Administration - Center for Veterinary Medicine (US FDA-CVM)’s cut off value of 500 ppt [17,18]. Additional information on these assays is available at https://www.charm.com/products/test-and-kits/antibiotic-tests/rosa-lateral-flow/.

Semi-structured interviews

To gain further insights into milk business and challenges, vendors were interviewed using an open-ended semi-structured interview. Each of the eight vendors orally completed a questionnaire (Appendix A) administered by the project team. Responses were recorded in English, and a thematic analysis was conducted using axial coding to form linkages across questions and participants.

Self-reporting diaries to identify routes used by milk transporters

To identify the specific routes that transporters were taking, we piloted use of a self-reported diary of stops along the route was proposed to transporters matched to our vendor participants. Ultimately this component of data collection was not successful as there had been recent arrest of transporters, and the sensitization efforts required were out of the scope of this project.

Statistical analysis

The dependent variable was each individual milk sample's binary result (positive/negative) for the laboratory assay, and independent variables included the specifics of the milk origin and container. Data were visualized and descriptive statistics were conducted for each independent variable, and Chi-Square tests were used to compute statistical relationships. Data was collated using Excel (Microsoft Excel version 16.86), and all analyses were conducted using R and R studio software (Version 2023.12.369).

Results

Over six weeks of sampling, 190 milk samples were collected from eight different vendors that sourced milk from 16 locations within nine counties (Fig. 2). We identified a 7 % (13/190) overall prevalence of aflatoxin detection at the threshold of 200 ppt when the test was incubated for eight minutes (Table 1). None of the 190 samples were positive for aflatoxin after a three-minute incubation which corresponds to a 350 ppt cut-off, and none of the milk samples tested positive for beta-lactam residues or tetracycline residues. As some of our vendor participants sourced from the same county, a schematic of milk flow is presented in Fig. 2. No statistically significant difference was identified in aflatoxin residue detection between origin locations (p = 0.44), and the specific administrative unit was only available for Kisumu County (Fig. 2). Most vendors sourced from a single location, yet 12.6 % (24/190) were mixed from multiple origin locations, and this was associated with aflatoxin detection (p = 0.04).

Validation of antibiotic assays

As no field samples tested positive for antibiotic residues, we validated the assay by testing samples from cows that had been treated by a local veterinarian within 48 h of sampling, and the person carrying out the test was blind to the antibiotic status of each sample. Three milk samples were only positive for the corresponding antibiotic the animal had been injected with (Received “Penistrep”: Positive for beta-lactams, negative tetracyclines; Received oxytetracycline: Positive for tetracycline, negative for beta-lactams, Received sulfamethoxazole and trimethoprim: Negative both antibiotic assays).

Thematic analysis of structured interviews with milk vendors

Eight semi-structured interviews were carried out with the milk vendors that we collected fresh milk for testing. Interviews lasted on average 10 min (range: 8–15 min). Three key themes relevant to food safety and challenges within milk business that may influence safety are summarized below (i-iii).

Physical properties of milk are understood to influence quality

When milk vendors were asked to describe what they considered important in the milk product they purchase to sell, most mentioned the word “quality” which was explained to primarily relate to the thickness. Thickness was assessed by visualizing the thickness of the milk, and three of the eight participants confirmed this with a lactometer. Participants also noted additives such as powder and margarine that make milk artificially thicker are added to account for watered down milk to increase volume, and additives impact the overall quality of milk. One participant suggested that milk safety could be improved by carefully choosing the people who supply and trusting transporters not to add water.

Freshness was also seen as a commonly reported criteria impacting milk quality. At least six of the eight participants mentioned milk spoiling or curdling before it is sold, which is sometimes out of their control, as a challenge to their milk business. Tasting was one way that freshness could be measured as they described they could sense if the milk had a normal cow milk taste, describing. “Fresh milk should smell like a cow; the smell is different than processed milk.”

Boiling was rarely noted to impact quality, and rather was said to be risky, potentially burning and ruining the product. Participants noted that safety gained from boiling was not necessarily linked to the foreseen quality of the milk product. Only three participants mentioned animal health but understood that farmers giving vaccines or treatments to cows could “lead to chemicals in the milk”.

Fluctuating availability of milk impacts profits and this is the main influence on choice of supplier

When participants were asked to describe challenges in milk business, they noted the inconsistency in available milk and transport time to impact their profits. Availability was dependent on demand and if there was a drought. Decreased volumes of available milk increased the price vendors must pay for the milk, and price was the primary reason customers selected their business over others. When vendors paid more for inputs, profits were lower, and this was evaluated daily as without refrigeration milk had to be sold the same day or made into sour milk products. One participant noted that the milk they can buy for the cheapest price is not necessarily the best quality, stating, “Nandi (distant origin location) milk is cheap, but the quality is not good.” Participants noted that if they had consistent electricity, equipment, and space, the pressure from fluctuating milk availability was mitigated.

“Give vendors support, like by giving them cooling equipment. It takes time to bring milk from Nandi”.

– Milk vendor in Kisumu city

Trust and rapport among value chain actors are key to mitigating business challenges

All vendors in this study used a middle-man transporter by vehicle, motorbike, and one used a bicycle rider. Two vendors outsourced from another collection center where multiple farmers combine milk in a larger cooling unit. One vendor aspired to eventually transport the milk themselves as challenges with middle-man transporters included tampering with the milk and government oversight of the transportation process. Participants thought transporter tampering was driven by the desire to stretch profits. Another vendor reported their trustworthy transporter refused to work after they were arrested by the Dairy Board, and others described tax on milk business to be out of scope for their business.

“The government is fighting and punishing instead of supporting. About once-a-year people get arrested”. – Milk vendor in Kisumu city.

Overall, trust and collaboration between the various stakeholders were important for mitigating business challenge, and buying from a trusted source increased their confidence in the quality. One participant described that in ten years of selling milk they have built “trust and rapport” with two consistent suppliers who know what they expect in the milk they buy. Most participants mentioned multiple stakeholders as sharing the responsibility for safe milk including farmers, suppliers, vendors, and the government regulatory board.

Discussion

We assessed food safety risks to urban milk consumers by utilizing a rapid diagnostic assay to investigate residues of antibiotics and aflatoxins that originate at the milk production stage. Detection prevalence and cutoff values have varied across other studies in Kenya. Milk from an informal urban settlement was screened for antibiotic residues with cut off at 50 ppb [19], and beta-lactam and tetracycline residues were 7.4 % and 3.2 %, respectively [20]. Milk from street vendors, vending machines, and markets in Eldoret identified that 24 % of samples had at least one antibiotic residue, no commercial samples tested positive [21].

In contrast to not detecting any antibiotic residues, we identified an aflatoxin M1 (AFM1) concentration above 200 ppt in 7 % (13/190) of samples, and no samples had a concentration above 350 ppt. Other studies have found higher percentages of milk contaminated with AFM1, but inconsistent cut off values make comparison challenging. In Nairobi, Kenya, 50 % of milk products exceeded the level allowed in the EU of 50 ppt, and 1 % exceeded the level allowed in the US of 500 ppt [22]. In a quantitative study around peri-urban areas of Nairobi, 99 % of samples were contaminated with a mean aflatoxin level of 84 ppt, and 64 % were over 50 ppt [23]. The aflatoxin test used in our study was predetermined by Charm Sciences and correspond to roughly midway between US FDA-CVM safe levels (500 ppt) and European Commission safe levels (50 ppt). A population-based understanding of the potential negative health consequences of low-level exposure to AFM1 over a lifetime is required to definitively determine acceptable levels, but it has been suggested that AFM1 does not contribute substantially to global liver cancer incidence [9] compared with the most potent aflatoxin (B1) that is produced when animals ingest the toxin directly from feed [18] (US-FDA, 2005). Thus, dumping milk contaminated at low levels may not benefit health and could exacerbate existing food insecurity [6,9].

The point of purchase and consumption provides an opportunity for overall value chain safety assessment, and programmes that recognize the complexity and importance of informal value chain systems that support underlying causes of reduced food safety are likely to have greater impact than those that blame or punish vendors for the outcome of contaminants. Our participants highlight that relationships are key to obtaining a fresh, reliable, and safe product. We were unable to trace the milk back to the origin by using self-reporting diaries, yet transporters play a key role in the value chain by facilitating farmers to get their milk to the locations where it is marketable. Potential additional support for these relationships include access to lactometers, so that transporters have accountability as vendors verify the absence of additives in the milk. Additionally, cooling equipment and reliable electricity could alleviate daily pressure to source milk when volumes are unreliable, and profits are impacted.

As an intermediate step to reducing aflatoxin residues, the impact of combining milk from multiple sources is worth considering as potential residues are diluted to cutoff values corresponding to health outcomes. No containers larger than 20-l carried milk that tested positive for aflatoxin, and container size was a significantly protective (p < 0.001). Further, encouraging use of 50-l metal cans for milk over plastic jugs has the added benefit of reduced total microbial contamination [24].

The results from this study may not be generalizable to other urban centers. Limitations of this work include non-random selection of vendors and the assays being optimized outside of Kenya where composition of milk could impact the test. Overall, the ROSA kits were feasible approach to identify residues with limited lab equipment availability, however, we caution against the use of rapid diagnostic assays alone to determine safety.

The Food Animal Organization (FAO) defines food insecurity as ‘a situation that exists when people lack secure access to sufficient amounts of safe and nutritious food for normal growth and development and an active and health life’ [25]. The informal market of milk is a significant and valued business, and stakeholder perspectives provide insight into how all dimensions of food security can be realized. Ultimately, urban centers will represent a large proportion of future demand for milk and producer-transport-vendor relationships will be key to providing adequate quantities of safe milk to growing and diversifying cities.

Conclusion

Our work demonstrates low levels of antibiotic residues in milk purchased from the informal value chain in urban centres even when applying standards set by high-income countries with formal milk systems. The consequences to human health of a 9 % aflatoxin AFM1 prevalence are difficult to quantify and will be influenced by the consumers age and overall health status. Nonetheless, we support the conclusions made by Turna and others that policy makers should consider the differential risk of AFM1 in milk compared to more-potent aflatoxins present in other foods, and this risk should be weighed with the important nutritional benefits of milk consumption [9].

Further assessment of potential interventions that can be adopted within the informal system to improve safety should be explored. For example, none of the 50-l metal cans we tested had aflatoxin residues, indicating that pooling of milk from multiple sources may reduce risk below harmful thresholds. Further, as the alternative plastic containers have been shown to have higher bacterial contamination, promotion of large metal containers may have a combined health benefit. A more enabling policy environment that embraces the informal value chain, rather than ostracizing stakeholders, is likely to foster greater trust building between value chain actors. Urban milk vendors are eager to provide a safe and quality product for their consumers and specifically requested more support to invest in safer milk products. To meet the demands of the future, policy makers must embrace the challenges and realities of urban milk vendors and work with all stakeholders to develop solutions that lead to safer milk for consumers.
