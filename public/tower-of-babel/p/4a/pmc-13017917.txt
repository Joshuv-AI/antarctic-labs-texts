Measuring patient experience of integrated care in multiple sclerosis: development and validation of the Integrated Care Experience Scale (ICES-MS)
Authors: Tommaso Manacorda, Paolo Bandiera, Piero Lamendola, Marco Salivetto, Federica Terzuoli, Mario A Battaglia, Michela Ponzio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeople with multiple sclerosis (MS) need to receive health and social care services from a diverse range of provider organizations, which carries risks such as disjointed care, discontinuities, and duplication. Many innovation programmes aim to provide better integrated and person-centred care (IPCC) for people with chronic conditions, including MS. Measuring patient experience is essential to evaluate interventions meant to shift the service model towards coordinated and personalized care. These transformations are central to global strategies for addressing the needs of ageing populations. AISM—Italian MS Association adapted an 8-item questionnaire, ICES-MS, from a set of questions originally designed for chronic patients in general. A total of 1602 persons with MS living in Italy completed the ICES-MS as a part of a broader survey that included other validated questionnaires on disability (Self-EDSS) and quality of life (EQ-5D-3L and EQ-VAS). Participants’ responses were also linked with data from a previous AISM survey on 169 Italian Clinical Centres. Structural, construct, criterion, and known-groups validity of ICES-MS were evaluated. The ICES-MS scale is a robust unidimensional measure of patient experience of IPCC in MS, with strong internal consistency and appropriate convergent validity with EQ-5D-3L and EQ-VAS. ICES-MS scores varied as expected by participants’ age and disability level. ICES-MS is a valid, succinct scale to measure patient experience of IPCC care in MS in Italy, and its original design suggests value in exploring its use in other chronic conditions and different countries.

Introduction

Integrated care in multiple sclerosis

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic neurodegenerative disease where an abnormal immune response compromises the central nervous system. MS is usually diagnosed between ages 30 and 40 and causes a broad range of symptoms that affect several body regions and vary greatly between patients and over time. These include vision loss, ataxia, tremors, bowel and urinary incontinence, pain, fatigue, cognitive issues, depression, and anxiety [1]. MS is progressive and many persons with MS (PwMS) develop disability over time, although disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) can slow its progression and delay disability [2]. An estimated 140 000 PwMS live in Italy in 2024 [3].

Most PwMS, including those with mild disability, require DMT, symptomatic treatment, rehabilitation, specialist care, diagnostic tests, psychological, and social support. These are provided in various settings by different professionals and organizations [4]. However, fragmentation and poor coordination often cause discontinuity and duplication in care delivery, hindering optimal MS management [5, 6].

Many countries have launched programmes to improve coordination and integration of care for people with long-term neurological conditions including MS, as integrated and person-centred care (IPCC) is central to global strategies meant to address chronic conditions [5, 7, 8]. Evaluating their impact is essential but often challenging, for several reasons [5, 9, 10].

Notably, there is no consensus on what exactly falls within the definition of IPCC, nor on how the concepts of integration and patient-centredness relate [7, 9]. Still, they are often considered interconnected and used together. Some authors view integrated care as service models centred on the priorities of patients, rather than those of provider organizations, to improve care effectiveness and system efficiency [11].

Patient insights are essential, as they have an unequalled standpoint to report whether care delivery was coordinated, centred on their needs and preferences, and enabled better management [12].

Many patient-reported experience measures (PREMs) have been developed to capture these insights [13], mostly targeting specific settings or single episodes of care [14]. Governments and providers use them to monitor and evaluate interventions, and evidence links positive patient experience, better clinical outcomes, and safety [15].

AISM’s research on integrated care in Italy

The Observatory on MS Policy at the Italian MS Association (AISM) regularly collects and analyses data on the needs of PwMS in Italy and on how the Italian health and social care system responds to them [6, 16, 17]. That includes regular surveys on PwMS and on MS Clinical Centres (MSCCs), the main providers of specialist care for MS in Italy, which are often responsible for ensuring a multidisciplinary approach. Results from this research are published yearly in the Italian MS Barometer, informing AISM’s institutional relations, policy, and advocacy activities [3].

Better IPCC is a common request of PwMS and an overarching goal in the Italian health and social care system [3, 5]. Integrated care pathways (ICPs) emerged as an essential component of the Italian strategy for chronic conditions, including MS, and spread widely across Italian regions in the past decade [18]. AISM was often involved by Regions and Local Health Authorities in the design of ICPs for MS [3].

Within its policy strategy, which includes evaluating the implementation and the impact of ICPs, AISM developed a PREM of IPCC, the Integrated Care Experience Scale for MS (ICES-MS), adapting an existing set of questions. ICES-MS was included in the 2023 edition of AISM’s survey on PwMS. This study aims to evaluate its validity (structural, construct, and known-groups) and reliability (internal consistency and scalability), and to assess its psychometric properties.

Methods

Participants

PwMS were recruited through AISM’s email lists and social media, and completed an online questionnaire (Computer Assisted Web Interview—CAWI). Participants were adults (>18 years) who reported being diagnosed with MS by a neurologist and provided informed consent.

The cross-sectional study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki guidelines for human subject research [19], and relevant Italian laws on research ethics and personal data processing.

Questionnaire

The questionnaire covered many life domains affected by MS, including work and social inclusion, with a substantial section on health and social care needs and service access. Alongside the ICES-MS, it included other validated scales: EQ-5D-3L for health-related quality of life (HRQoL), including the visual analogue scale EQ-VAS, and self-EDSS derived from the original EDSS (Expanded Disability Status Scale) and the Patient Determined Disease Steps (PDDS) scale [20, 21]. EQ-5D scores were converted into utilities using the Time-Trade-Off (TTO) method and the Italian tariff, attributing utility value between 0 = death and 1 = full health (negative values set to 0) in this analysis [22].

ICES-MS scope and development

The ICES-MS aims to capture the impact of IPCC on patients’ care journeys. It was designed to be brief and easy to complete, reducing risk of survey fatigue especially within longer questionnaires [23], and to be usable by service providers and Local Health Authorities for policy evaluation and quality improvement.

In designing ICES-MS, AISM adapted six questions originally developed by the Policy Innovation Research Unit (PIRU) at the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine (LSHTM). These questions were part of a patient and informal carer questionnaire devised for use in the national evaluation of the Integrated Care and Support Pioneer Programme (2015-22), funded by England’s NIHR Policy Research Programme, project PR-R10-1014-25001 [10]. They were based on a comprehensive literature review and included elements from the UK Care Quality Commission’s Adult Inpatient Surveys [24]. Designed for older people with chronic conditions, the questions were informed by the 2013 ‘I’ statements identified by the UK National Collaboration for Integrated Care and Support, which define what integrated care means from the patient’s perspective and the needs it should meet [12].

The six questions were translated into Italian following standard guidelines for cross-cultural adaptation of self-report Measures and adapted to MS [25]. After consultation with Italian MS experts, two more questions were added to capture personalization of care and training on how to navigate services.

ICES-MS consists of eight items, assessed on a four-point Likert scale from 0 (not at all) to 3 (very) (see Fig. 1 for item text). The total score ranges from 0 to 24, with higher scores indicating better IPCC.

Linking ICES-MS with AISM 2022 Survey on Italian MS clinical centres

Responses from PwMS were linked with data from 169 MSCCs (out of the existing 240) that participated in another AISM survey in 2022. Each PwMS was assigned binary variable indicating whether the MSCC providing their care had adopted a local ICP for MS. MSCCs reporting adoption were assumed to provide more IPCC [17].

Data analysis

Data were analysed using STATA 17 (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX, USA). Descriptive statistics summarized demographic, clinical, and item-level data. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test assessed normality of score distribution. Floor and ceiling effects were defined as the percentage of respondents scoring the minimum (0) or maximum (24) and considered present if more than 15% of participants fell in either category.

Validity

A psychometric evaluation was performed to assess structural validity, construct validity, criterion validity, and known group comparison and reliability of ICES-MS.

Structural validity

Structural validity assesses whether the items in a scale measure a single underlying construct, typically using factor analysis. It was studied using exploratory factor analysis (EFA). Factors were extracted by principal component factor analysis with varimax orthogonal rotation. Bartlett’s test of Sphericity (BTS) and a Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) test were conducted to confirm data suitability. BTS with P < .05 and a KMO value of .60 were suitable when running the EFA [26]. Kaiser’s criterion for factors with an eigenvalue ≥1 and the cut-off point criterion (i.e., inflection of the curve obtained by scree plot, unpublished data) suggested by Streiner were used for the components extraction, with factor loadings greater than 0.40 deemed significant [27, 28]. Model fit was tested with root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA; good if ≤0.08), comparative fit index (CFI; good if >0.95), and Tucker–Lewis index (TLI; good if >0.95). We report these statistics using the Satorra–Bentler adjustment since the distribution of the scale items was non-normal and, to improve the model fit, we considered the covariance of error terms [29].

Construct validity

Construct validity refers to the extent to which an instrument measures the theoretical construct it is intended to capture. It was assessed by testing hypotheses about expected relations between the ICES-MS and other measures (convergent and divergent validity). We expected health-related quality of life (HRQoL) to be associated with experience of better IPCC based on the known association between clinical outcomes and patient experience [15]. However, only a moderate association was anticipated, as HRQoL and experience of IPCC are conceptually related but distinct constructs. HRQoL reflects an individual’s health [20], while IPCC also incorporates relational and organizational aspects of care provision.

Spearman’s correlation coefficient (ρ) was used to assess all the hypothesized relations of the ICES-MS with EQ-5D-3L and with EQ-VAS. Correlation would be considered low if ρ < 0.30; moderate if ρ 0.30–0.59; and high if ρ > 0.60.

Criterion validity

Criterion validity is the extent to which an instrument’s score is related to an existing standard [30]. As no other standard scale to measure patient experience of IPCC was used, criterion validation was sought through data collected in 2022 from MSCCs and linked to 2023 survey on PwMS. The association between ICES-MS score and whether the MSCC that followed them adopted a local ICP was tested. Data reported by MSCCs show that adoption of local ICPs is associated with improved collaboration with other health and social care providers, as well as with a more holistic approach to care [17], supporting the assumption that centres adopting them would deliver more structured and coordinated IPCC. This comparison was assessed using the Mann–Whitney test.

Known-groups validity

Known-groups validity evaluates whether an instrument discriminates between groups expected to score differently. This was assessed by comparing the ICES-MS score for gender with the Mann–Witney test, and with the Kruskal–Wallis test for age and disability level. When group differences were observed, post hoc analyses (Bonferroni’s test) were applied.

Reliability

Reliability is the degree to which a scale consistently measures the concept or concepts of interest. The reliability of ICES-MS was assessed in terms of internal consistency and scalability.

Internal consistency

Internal consistency refers to the extent to which all items of a scale measure the same construct. It was estimated using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, average inter-item and item-total correlation. Statistically acceptable Cronbach’s alpha coefficient should be <0.7 and average inter-item and total-item correlations should be between 0.30 and 0.70 [28, 31].

Scalability

Scalability reflects the homogeneity of a set of items. Mokken scale was used to analyse underlying dimensions and the scalability of ICES-MS. Trace lines for all eight items were visually inspected, and the monotonicity assumption was assessed by calculating its criterion. A maximum value of the monotonicity criterion below 40 across all items was considered acceptable. Scalability of the ICES-MS items (Hi) and the total scale (H) were measured using Loevinger’s coefficient of scalability. Each of the eight items was required to have a Loevinger’s coefficient of scalability (Hi) of ≥0.30 and an overall scale scalability index (H) of ≥ .30 [32]. A scale is generally considered weak if 0.3 ≤ H < 0.4, moderate if 0.4 ≤ H < 0.5, and strong if H ≥ 0.5.

Test–retest reliability was not assessed, as the broader survey was not designed to include a second administration of ICES–MS within a timeframe short enough to minimize risk of true changes in patient experience. Additionally, logistical constraints and the risk of survey fatigue discouraged repeat measurement.

Results

Participant characteristics

Of the 2598 PwMS who started the survey, 1602 PwMS completed ICES-MS and were included in the analysis (Table 1). Gender distribution in the sample reflects the MS sex ratio [1], while the proportion of PwMS with moderate and severe disability (46.6%) is slightly lower than previous estimates in Italy [33].

All participants who started ICES-MS eventually completed all eight items, which indicates a high level of acceptability of the questions. Figure 1 shows response frequencies and the full item text. Validity

Validity of ICES-MS was assessed in terms of structural, convergent, criterion, and known-groups validity.

Structural validity

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) showed that ICES-MS measures only one construct. BTS with P < .001 and KMO of 0.913 confirmed the suitability of the data. EFA analysis of the eight ICES-MS items, after varimax orthogonal rotation, revealed only one factor with eigenvalues >1.0. This was confirmed by the scree plot showing a break after the first factor, suggesting that a one-factor model was the best, accounting for 59.7% of the total variance.

Construct validity

Concurrent and discriminant validity results, as indicated by the correlation coefficients between the ICES-MS and other measures, show that both hypotheses were confirmed (100%). ICES-MS showed a moderate, significant positive correlation with the utility score of the EQ-5D-3L (ρ = 0.33, P < .0001) and the EQ-VAS scale (ρ = 0.31, P < .0001). Item 6 (feeling confident about managing autonomously their MS) showed higher correlation with both scales (Table 2).

Criterion validity

Linkage with the 2022 MSCC survey was possible for 1261 participants to the PwMS survey. Figure 2 shows that, consistent with our hypothesis, the median value of ICES-MS was significantly higher for PwMS followed by MSCCs that adopted a local ICP for MS compared with PwMS in MSCCs without a local ICP (14.0 vs. 13.0, P = .0007) (Fig. 2). The strength of this association might be underestimated due to possible misclassification. Data were self-reported by MSCCs, and as ICPs are an innovative approach to care, reports of their adoption may have been influenced by social desirability. Additionally, some PwMS in MSCC that adopt ICP may have experienced care that is not fully aligned with the model, while some in other centres may have benefited from informal integration efforts.

Known-groups validity

ICES-MS discriminated between patients with different disability levels (self-EDSS), with significantly lower scores among PwMS with severe or moderate disability compared to those with mild disability (Bonferroni post hoc test). ICES-MS scores also varied by age, as younger PwMS (≤36 years) reported a significantly higher average score than older ones (≥56 years) (mean: 13.28 SD 5.52; median: 13 IQR 9-17 vs. mean: 12.03 SD 5.83; median: 12 IQR 8–16; P-values = .017. Bonferroni post hoc test: ≤36 years vs. ≥56 years, P = .006; mild vs. moderate, P < .0001 and mild vs. severe, P < .0001).

Reliability

Reliability was examined through internal consistency and scalability.

Internal consistency

Internal consistency of the overall scale was high (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.903) ranging from 0.884 (item 3) to 0.896 (item 6). The average inter-item correlation ranged between 0.522 (item 3) and 0.552 (item 6) (for overall scale = 0.538). Item-total correlations showed that all eight items were significantly correlated with the total, with values ranging from 0.726 (item 6) to 0.827 (item 3) (Table 2). These values meet published thresholds for satisfactory reliability.

Scalability

Visual inspection of item trace lines (not shown) and a maximum monotonicity criterion of −13 (item 1 and item 6) well below the threshold of 40, indicated that the assumption of monotonicity was met. ICES-MS shows strong scalability (H = 0.613). All items were also strongly scalable (H > 0.50) ranging from item 3 (H = 0.665) to item 1 (H = 0. 567).

Discussion

Results from this study indicate that ICES-MS is a robust unidimensional measure of IPCC for PwMS. The results of item-total correlation show that all items are highly correlated with the total score and the scalability analysis shows a strong homogeneity in items. ICES-MS measures a single robust construct, supporting the summation of scores as a valid overall measure of experience.

Comparison between ICES-MS and other PREMs

To our knowledge, ICES-MS is the first validated PREM scale that captures the experience of the entire MS patient journey while focusing solely on dimensions that matter to PwMS.

Unlike other PREMs of coordinated or integrated care that have been proposed and validated in the past, ICES-MS is not focused on any specific setting or provider. It captures the experience of the entire patient journey, not a single episode of care [14, 34]. ICES-MS is not informed by a specific care model (e.g. the Chronic Care Model) or conceptual framework of integrated care (e.g. the one proposed by Singer and colleagues) [35–37], hence it does not measure care delivery against it [37].

All PREMs potentially suitable for inclusion in AISM’s questionnaire that were identified from the literature included at least 20, and up to 56, items [14, 35–37]. The choice of developing ICES-MS was driven by the preference for a flexible and succinct tool, as a longer questionnaire would increase the risk of survey fatigue and dropout, that PwMS are particularly prone to [23].

Moreover, because the original PIRU questions were structured around the ‘I’ statements, and AISM’s adaptations and additions were consistent with that approach, ICES-MS is strongly focused on patient experience elements that matter for PwMS. These include: being listened to, being involved and informed about their care, being supported to be confident and independent in managing their needs (including in navigating services), and receiving coordinated and personalized care [12]. These elements are also key in AISM’s vision and mission [3].

What does ICES-MS measure?

The mechanisms underlying how IPCC is expected to achieve its intended outcomes are under debate [9, 36]. However, many authors agree that involving patients in designing their own care journey can activate and empower them, enabling collaboration and co-production of care with formal providers [8, 38]. This activation is a well-established determinant of better health and quality of life outcomes [39].

It is noteworthy that the ICES-MS item most strongly associated with EQ-5D-3L refers to the confidence in managing their own MS autonomously (item 6). As ICES-MS is unidimensional, this association suggests that the overall score likely captures experiencing a process in which patients are listened to and involved in care by providers, which leads to activation and care co-production.

The sensitivity of ICES-MS to self-EDSS, with lower average scores in people with more severe disability (and vice versa), is consistent with the hypothesis that ICES-MS measures a single construct that includes patients’ activation. Achieving activation in patients with more complex needs requires more and more structured IPCC [7], hence people with more complex needs are expected to achieve less activation than people with less complex ones when receiving the same amount of IPCC.

Applications of ICES-MS

ICES-MS is a valid measure of the experience of IPCC as reported by PwMS in Italy. High acceptability and brevity make it easy to use for evaluating the impact of policies and interventions aimed at providing IPCC for PwMS. Some Italian Local Health Authorities have started testing it for this purpose [3].

PIRU’s original questions were developed for people with any chronic conditions in England. AISM translated and adapted them to MS by essentially replacing ‘your health’ with ‘your MS’, maintaining consistency with the ‘I’ statements that originally informed them. Therefore, it is plausible that ICES-MS could be valid for other conditions or countries.

This study also suggests that ICES-MS captures patient involvement leading to activation and co-production, a mechanism widely acknowledged as key in the programme theory of interventions aimed at improving health outcomes by means of IPCC [9, 40].

Further adaptation and validation of ICES-MS in different contexts and with people with other chronic conditions are needed to establish its cross-disease and cross-national applicability. Its brevity and ease of use may facilitate its broader adoption to evaluate IPCC programmes, including for populations more prone to survey fatigue.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The survey was administered to PwMS already in touch with the MS association, which is known to be associated with lower participation from PwMS with severe symptoms. This limits the representativeness of the sample, as reflected in the EDSS distribution (43.3% with moderate or severe disability) compared to prevalence estimates (59.2%). However, those estimates are 8 years older than the survey, and the proportion of PwMS with less severe symptoms is increasing [4].

Due to convenience sampling, other unknown non-random factors may have generated selection bias, including the possible participation of patients more prone to activation. If that were the case, the generalizability of average ICES-MS scores would be affected, and possibly association among variables.

Only a single test administration of the ICES-MS was used in this study, so further testing of its reliability is required to examine the extent to which repeated measurements provide similar results.

The study design would have been stronger if convergent validity were assessed through comparison with other existing PREMs of IPCC. That was not feasible due to questionnaire length and the risk of survey fatigue in PwMS.
