A Review of the Application of Generic Preference-Based Instruments with Older People Across Care Settings
Authors: Kiri Lay, Jenny Cleland, Jyoti Khadka, Rachel Milte, Julie Ratcliffe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Global population ageing continues to place pressure on health and care systems. In Australia, the increasing integration of health and aged care systems is a rising policy approach to alleviating the burden on the health system and improving the quality of care for older people. Preference-based instruments (PBMs) have become increasingly important as a tool to enable quality assessment and economic evaluation of both health and aged care services. Given the changing policy context towards more integrated services and older peoples’ frequent movement across both health and aged care settings, it is important that PBMs are applicable across different care settings. This literature review aimed to assess the application of generic PBMs with older people across health and aged care settings. A systematic search of three databases for studies utilising PBMs with older people in health or aged care settings resulted in 204 articles included for review. The EQ-5D instruments were the most frequently utilised instrument across studies, followed by the Quality of Life-Aged Care Consumers (QOL-ACC). Most instruments were applied in either health or aged care settings, with few being used across both. The measurement of quality of life in older people across these settings using PBMs requires consideration of several issues particularly pertinent to this population, including the appropriateness, relevance and comprehensiveness of dimensions, the use of a suitable recall period and enabling self-report for people with cognitive impairment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40258-026-01060-4.

Key Points for Decision Makers

Introduction

Population ageing is placing increasing pressures on the health care system, resulting in poor quality care and negatively impacting the health and wellbeing of older people [1, 2]. Older people (≥ 65 years) are the fastest growing age cohort in Australia and in many other countries, and the proportion of individuals defined as the oldest old (≥ 80 years) living with frailty and dementia is rapidly rising [3]. The Australian health care system is predominantly occupied by older people, a situation that is mirrored internationally [4]. Older people currently account for only 16% of Australia’s population [3] but 42% of all hospitalisations and 49% of the total days spent in hospital [5].

Older aged care service users often live with chronic health conditions and cognitive impairment and are more likely to access emergency and acute health services [5, 6]. Additionally, older patients frequently transfer between health and aged care systems, particularly for acute health incidences where hospital stays are short, and patients may be discharged to either a different health care setting such as a rehabilitation or sub-acute care setting and/or an aged care setting [7]. In Australia, 10% of hospital stays for people aged 65+ result in an increase in aged care service post-hospitalisation, with more than 40% having some use of aged care services before hospitalisation [8]. In this context, the line between what represents aged care and health care is increasingly blurred with policy approaches fostering stronger integration and aiming to expand and adapt health and care services to provide flexible and multi-disciplinary care for older people [9]. In Australia, several care services traditionally offered by the health system are now also being provided by aged care providers in home and residential aged care settings including rehabilitation, restorative care and palliative care services [9, 10].

In recent years, the quality of life (QoL) and care experiences of older people in both health and aged care settings have become central elements of quality assessment processes in many countries [11]. This policy shift includes a recognition that person-centred QoL and care experience outcomes represent important considerations alongside traditional clinical indicators of care quality in the assessment and evaluation of care and outcomes for older people [12, 13]. In this context, preference-based measures (PBMs) that are designed to be applicable for both quality assessment and economic evaluation have become increasingly popular. PBMs of health and/or QoL include two main elements. First is the measurement of health and/or QoL via a descriptive system comprising several key health and/or QoL dimensions, with response levels reflecting varying degrees of impairment for each dimension (e.g. no problems to severe problems) [14]. Second is the valuation of health and/or QoL through the application of an accompanying preference-based scoring algorithm with preference weights attached to each possible combination of responses defined by the descriptive system [14].

Preference weights are typically based on general population preferences for each health and/or QoL dimension level described relevant to the country of interest. The preference-based scoring algorithm enables the estimation of a ‘utility score’, which is typically on the quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) scale (where 0 is equivalent to being dead and 1 is equivalent to full health), the key outcome in cost-utility analysis (CUA) for economic evaluation. Population preferences can also be used to generate utilities on different scales (for example, the capability scale for the Investigating Choice Experiments Capability [ICECAP] instruments); however, there are debated issues on methods for incorporating these utilities into CUA for economic evaluation [15]. CUA is the preferred method of economic evaluation recommended by many regulatory health funding bodies, including the Pharmaceutical Benefits Advisory Committee (PBAC) in Australia and the National Institute of Health and Care Excellence (NICE) in the United Kingdom (UK) [16, 17]. A previous systematic review to assess the PBMs most commonly applied in older populations in community aged care settings identified the EQ-5D instruments (EQ-5D-3L and EQ-5D-5L) as the most commonly applied [18], which may be due to these instruments being the recommended tools for economic evaluation by NICE.

There are unique challenges in assessing the QoL of older people using PBMs [19]. A review published in 20 by Cleland et al. explored the application of PBMs with older people across World Health Organization (WHO) disease classification groups. The authors outlined several key issues, including an over-reliance on proxy respondents, response shift effects and high levels of cognitive and vision impairment in older people as unique challenges in this population [20]. As the prevalence of older people accessing health and aged care services and the cost of providing care for older people continues to increase [21] and in the context of constrained health budgets [22], it is essential to accurately assess the cost-effectiveness of interventions for older people, to ensure the greatest possible QoL gains for this cohort. The collection of outcome data for an older person pertinent to a single health incident may occur across multiple settings, highlighting the utility of a consistent cross-sector outcome instrument that can be universally applied across care settings. There are key differences between older and younger adults in the preferences and relative importance of QoL dimensions [23]. Furthermore, generic measures may not include all the dimensions that may be important to older people. The question of which PBMs are most appropriate for measuring the QoL of older people across different health and care settings is crucial for reliable and consistent QoL measurement for this population group [23, 24].

The rapidly changing policy and practice landscape in models of care and care settings for older people, the expanded body of evidence and the recent development of further PBMs in the period since the Cleland review [20], necessitate an update to and extension of the Cleland review. While the Cleland review explored the application of PBMs in different disease groups, given recent movements in policy and practice in many countries towards the increasing integration of health and aged care services and the increasing frequency with which older people transition across health and aged care systems, the focus of this review is the application of PBMs in both health and aged care settings.

A comprehensive literature review was undertaken to determine the pattern of usage of PBMs with older people in health and aged care settings. The authors then highlight and critically examine methodological issues associated with the use of PBMs across diverse settings in this population.

The Cleland review identified 11 PBMs applicable to older people in health and aged care settings. These PBMs formed the foundations of this review. Two additional instruments that have been developed and applied since the Cleland review, the EQ-HWB and the Quality of Life-Aged Care Consumers (QOL-ACC), are also included. A brief description of the additional new instruments is provided in the following paragraphs; see the Cleland review [20] for a full description of the original 11 PBMs.

The QOL-ACC was developed in Australia and is designed to capture QoL dimensions relevant to older people, accessing both residential and community-based aged care services [24]. In contrast to most other generic PBMs, the QOL-ACC tool was developed using a ‘bottom-up’ approach where dimensions were developed from inception and then refined through focus groups and interviews with aged care service users and industry partners [24, 25]. The QOL-ACC has been extensively validated with older people in Australia utilising both home and residential aged care services [26–29]. It consists of six items corresponding with six dimensions, providing a conceptualisation of QoL that encompasses broader psychosocial elements of QoL [30]. The QOL-ACC is available in interviewer administered, self-complete and two different proxy perspective versions. The scoring algorithm for the QOL-ACC is based on the preferences of older people living in Australia [31]. In April 2023, the QOL-ACC was adopted as one of the key mandatory quality indicators in Australia’s National Aged Care Quality Indicator Program [32].

The EQ-HWB is intended to complement the existing EuroQoL instruments and was developed through a partnership between the University of Sheffield and the EuroQoL group to address the need for a greater focus on psychosocial aspects of wellbeing not covered by existing EuroQoL instruments [33]. The original long-form EQ-HWB has 25 items with 4-point scale response options and was designed for use with patients, social care users and carers [33]. The EQ-HWB-9 is a 9-item short version that was developed from the EQ-HWB to enable the generation of QALYs. A pilot scoring preference-based value set has been developed based on UK general population preferences [34]. Both the EQ-HWB and the EQ-HWB-9 are available to research collaborators as experimental versions but are not yet available to potential users of the instrument [35].

Literature Review

Search Strategy

The websites of identified PBMs were searched for articles published in the 5 years since the Cleland review [20]. Following this, a systematic search was conducted across three databases, SCOPUS, PubMed and PsycINFO, using an updated version of the search terms used in the Cleland review and including terms relating to the additional instruments. Search terms included the names of the identified instruments plus additional PBM-related terms and search terms relating to older people. The search was limited to articles published in English over the 5-year period from May 2019 to May 2025. This time period was chosen to avoid duplication of studies that were found in the Cleland review that was published in 2019. Articles were included if they described original research undertaken in tertiary health care or residential or community aged care settings, included one of the specified PBMs and included exclusively older adult participants (aged 65+). Theses, systematic reviews and conference abstracts were excluded, as were articles not published in the English language. A grey literature search was also completed using Google Scholar, with screening continuing until screening of 100 articles was completed with no further relevant results identified. Full translated searches for Google Scholar and SCOPUS are included in the electronic supplementary material (Appendix 1).

Screening and Extraction

Database results were uploaded to Covidence [36], and title and abstract screening was completed by two independent screeners (KL and JC) for the first 100 results. Following this initial double screening, agreement was calculated using Cohen’s Kappa (with a kappa of below 0.60 indicating inadequate agreement and above 0.80 representing strong agreement) [37], and the remaining results were screened by a single reviewer (KL) at the title and abstract stage in line with published screening recommendations [38]. All articles included for full text review were screened by two independent screeners (KL and JC). Any conflicts during screening were resolved via a discussion between the reviewers (JC, KL). Data extraction for all included articles was completed by KL using a custom data extraction template. The custom data extraction template included the aim of the study, experimental design, population of interest, PBM used, mode of administration (i.e. interviewer administered or self-complete) and the study setting. Data were organised according to study setting (acute health care, sub-acute health care or aged care) to facilitate appraisal, and analysed and described using a narrative synthesis approach.

Search Results

Website searching identified a total of 15 articles. Database searching produced 1316 articles, and a further 58 articles were identified through the Google Scholar search. Screening for duplicates removed 42 references, resulting in 1347 articles for title and abstract screening. Agreement between independent screeners after the first 100 results was estimated at 0.85, indicating near perfect agreement. At the title and abstract screening stage, 974 titles were excluded, and 373 articles were imported for full text screening. A further 169 articles were excluded at the full text screening stage, resulting in the inclusion of 204 articles for extraction and review. Comparison of Instruments

Measurement

A comparison of the included instruments’ measurement properties is presented in Table 1. There is a wide variation in the number of items in each instrument. While the instruments with the fewest items, the EQ-5D-5L and EQ-5D-3L, have five items covering five dimensions, instruments with more items may contain more than one item per dimension. For example, the Assessment of Quality of Life-8 Dimensions (AQoL-8D) has seven items in one dimension. In general, instruments with fewer items are applicable in a wider range of populations given the relative cognitive demands; this is especially true of older adult populations, who typically exhibit higher levels of cognitive impairment and dementia than younger adult populations. Though self-completion is considered the gold standard, interviewer-administered versions can help to facilitate data collection in populations where self-complete is not possible, for example, due to functional or literacy limitations. Proxy versions are available for all instruments, with most being formatted from the typical ‘proxy-patient’ perspective, that is, the proxy is asked to respond according to how they believe the patient/person of interest would respond. The QOL-ACC and both EQ-5D instruments have an additional proxy-proxy version available where the proxy is asked to report on their own perspective of the patient’s situation. The proxy-proxy perspective is the standard proxy version for the Health Utility Index Mark 2/3 (HUI2/3) and the AQoL instruments. Specific interviewer-administered versions are available for the EQ instruments, the Adult Social Care Outcomes Toolkit (ASCOT), the QOL-ACC and HUI instruments.

Descriptive Systems

The dimensions for all instruments, categorised into psychosocial or physical domains, are presented in Table 2, which elucidates the focus of the instruments (physical or psychosocial). The classifications are derived from definitions developed by Richardson et al. and subsequently adopted for use in the Cleland review [20, 39]. The ICECAP-O consists of solely psychosocial dimensions. Personal care is the most common physical dimension, with ten of the 12 instruments incorporating a domain related to personal care. Mobility, pain/discomfort and activities are also included in most (n = 9) instruments. Among the psychosocial domains, the emotional wellbeing domain is the most commonly included. Dimensions specific to mental health conditions, for example, depression and anxiety, are also included under the wider category of psychosocial domains. Activities as influenced by psychosocial health is the next most often included. Other psychosocial domains are included with a fairly consistent regularity, with the exception of enjoyment, family and intimacy, which were only included in one or two of the instruments. There is little variation in response scale levels, with all instruments providing between three (EQ-5D-3L) and six response scale options. Most instruments use a current or today recall period, with only the EQ-HWB (7 days), AQoL (1 week) and HUI (current, 1, 2 or 4 weeks, depending on research or clinical aims) instruments using longer recall periods. Valuation

Table 3 presents the valuation methods, populations and utility range for the instruments. Population preferences are, for nearly all instruments, developed using a general population sample. The exceptions to this are the QOL-ACC [30] and the ICECAP-O [40] instruments, which both derived preference weights based on samples of older people, as well as the AQoL-8D [41], which used both a general adult population sample and a mental health patient sample. The time trade-off (TTO) method was the most common method used to estimate preference weights; however, it is often used in combination with other methods, such as discrete choice experiments (DCEs), as with the QOL-ACC, and profile-based best–worse scaling (BWS), as for the ASCOT [42]. The ICECAP-O is the only instrument that has preference weights derived solely from BWS. The HUI2/3 are the only instruments to have preference weights derived from standard gamble (combined with visual analogue scales) [43]. The EuroQoL group have developed a standard valuation protocol known as EQ-VT, which combines composite time trade-off (cTTO) and DCEs [44]. cTTO combines standard TTO with lead time TTO to allow for the valuing of ‘worse than dead’ health states. Health states worse than death are possible for all instruments with the exception of the ICECAP-O, 15D and Assessment of Quality of Life – 6 Dimensions (AQoL-6D). The number of possible health states for each instrument varies widely, from 243 for the EQ-5D-3L to over 30 billion for the 15D. Application of PBMs Across Settings

The EQ-5D instruments were the most frequently used PBMs across all settings, with nearly 88% of all studies using either the EQ-5D-5L (n = 75, 37%), the EQ-5D-3L (n = 57, 28%), both (n = 3, 1.5%) or an unspecified version (n = 36, 18%). By comparison, other instruments were used considerably less, with the QOL-ACC (despite its very recent development and validation), the next most commonly utilised instrument, being used in 14 (7%) of the included studies. Mode of administration (i.e. interviewer, self-complete, proxy complete) was unclear in over half of studies (n = 113, 55%). Of those studies that did specify the mode of administration, self-complete was the most common form of administration, with only six studies (3%) relying solely on proxy completion. Participants were able to complete by either self-complete, defer to proxy responses or both in 12 studies.

There were clear differences in the frequency of instruments used across different health and aged care settings. Table 4 shows the frequency of each instrument by health or aged care setting. The EQ-5D instruments were used most often, with a total of 182 applications in total across both the 5L and 3L versions and including when the version was not specified. The majority (n = 94, 52%) of the studies utilising the EQ-5D were undertaken in acute inpatient care settings. By comparison, the EQ-5D was far less frequently utilised in aged care settings, although it was nevertheless the most frequently used instrument in this setting. By comparison, the ASCOT, EQ-HWB and QOL-ACC were used almost exclusively in aged care settings, with just one application of the QOL-ACC in a specialist outpatient clinic setting. The HUI instruments were solely applied in health care settings, and the 15-D was applied equally in both health and aged care settings. Discussion of Key Issues for the Application of PBMs in Older People

Considering the applicability of PBMs for older people across aged and health care settings requires exploration of several key issues. These are discussed in the following sections.

Comparability and Applicability Across Health Settings

The findings of this review with regards to the use of PBMs in health and care settings are not surprising. One would expect instruments widely used in economic evaluations of medications and interventions, such as the EQ-5D, to be utilised often in acute care settings. Additionally, the QOL-ACC and ASCOT were developed specifically for social care and aged care settings, and as such, this is where one would expect most of the studies using these instruments to be focused. However, given the increasing integration of health and aged care systems for older people, it is increasingly important for instruments to be applicable across multiple care settings. For example, the use and applicability of instruments may differ depending on the goals of the policymakers and the users of the instruments, as these may vary across different settings. Therefore, this review raises several methodological considerations in assessing the suitability of cross-sector QoL measurement with available PBMs.

The EQ-5D instruments were the most widely used in this review and were the only instrument to be utilised frequently across both health and aged care settings. However, there is growing evidence that the EQ-5D lacks reliability and responsiveness in older populations and has decreased acceptability with older people, both in clinical settings (as compared to younger adults) and in aged care [44–47]. Qualitative studies with older people have demonstrated content validity issues, with both the wording and the construction of some items found to be irrelevant and difficult for older people to understand and respond to [45, 46]. Additionally, the EQ-5D-3L and (to a lesser extent) the EQ-5D-5L have been found to have ceiling effects and sensitivity issues with older people, especially the middle and oldest old [48, 49]. It should be noted that the evidence is, however, inconclusive, and more research should be undertaken to determine the extent and impact of these potential validity issues. However, there is limited evidence for the utility and psychometric performance of aged care or care specific instruments such as the QOL-ACC and ASCOT in health care settings. A 2023 study by Engel et al., included in this review, employed a think-aloud protocol to compare the ASCOT, EQ-5D-5L, EQ-HWB-9 and QOL-ACC with an aged care cohort and found comprehension issues for all instruments; however, the QOL-ACC presented the fewest issues [45]. Van Leeuwen et al. compared the EQ-5D-3L, ASCOT and ICECAP-O in older people in Denmark and found frequent response issues for all three instruments [50]. Additionally, respondents felt that none of the instruments adequately covered aspects of their QoL, and relevance of the instruments was dependent on individual characteristics and situations [50].

The items included in descriptive systems are highly variable, as demonstrated in this review. This variance, combined with differences in measurement scales, contributes to the incomparability of health states based on measurement from different PBMs, i.e. utilities are different for the same health states [20, 51]. A lack of comparability in these instruments creates issues for decision makers when attempting to compare interventions using different PBMs [52]. The incomparability of PBMs highlights the importance of PBM selection, especially for economic evaluation.

There is evidence that the relative importance of dimensions differs between older and younger adults, with older people ranking independence, physical mobility and self-care relatively higher than younger adults [23]. Studies examining aspects of QoL important to older people consistently identify dimensions of independence or autonomy, physical independence and mobility, and social relationships [23, 52–55]. Typically, instruments designed to measure health-related QoL (HRQoL) for the QALY scale include primarily physical dimensions of QoL, consistent with more narrowly defined health status, and do not encompass these broader aspects of QoL that are important to older people. Interventions in geriatric medicine and aged care frequently include impacts that go beyond health status to provide broader wellbeing outcomes. In sub-acute care, for example, where an acute health incident can predicate a change in circumstances for the older person (i.e. resulting in admission to residential aged care or access to home care), these psychosocial elements, such as independence or autonomy, are intrinsically linked to health status [56]. In recognition of this broader focus, tools such as the ASCOT, ICECAP-O, QOL-ACC and EQ-HWB have been developed. The ASCOT does not include any health or emotional dimensions and is not recommended as a stand-alone PBM for health settings. The ICECAP-O is designed to measure capability rather than health status, and as such, its utility values are anchored on a 0–1 capability scale and, therefore, cannot generate QALYs. The EQ-HWB-9, while not developed with older people, is designed to encompass health and wellbeing to enable use in both health and aged care settings; however, there is only preliminary evidence for its validity with older people [45]. The QOL-ACC includes health and wellbeing domains and was developed and validated with an older person population in aged care settings. Work is currently underway to establish the validity of the QOL-ACC with older patients in health care [57]. This will facilitate comparability for quality assessment and economic evaluation by enabling the measurement and valuation of QoL with the QOL-ACC across both health and aged care settings. It should also be noted that the EuroQol group is in the process of developing and testing a suite of ‘bolt-ons’ that will enable additional domains to be combined with the core EQ-5D domains, to expand the measurement capabilities of the EQ-5D measures. This may facilitate inclusion of domains more specifically relevant to older populations [35].

Recall Periods

Evidence suggests that longer recall periods are more appropriate for stable or chronic conditions, while shorter recall periods are more suitable for use with people with acute conditions where more short-term change is expected [58]. This contributes to the complexity of consistent QoL measurement across settings for older people as different settings and patient/care groups are likely to have different levels of fluctuation, i.e. older people accessing long-term aged care are likely to be more stable than older people suffering an acute health incident or in sub-acute care. Further complicating this picture is the evidence that older people may deviate from the instructed recall period across items [46, 59]. While this apparent lack of attention to the stated recall period does not appear to impact on the psychometric validity of instruments, it may obscure effects in other contexts, such as randomised control trials, due to the often significantly smaller samples. The recall period of today utilised with the EQ-5D instruments may be inappropriate in an aged care setting, while a longer recall period may fail to capture change across acute health incidences. Most instruments in this review employ ‘current situation’ or ‘usual’, which may be an effective compromise, anchoring the respondent to their current situation more broadly without specifying an exact time frame; however, this could contribute to inconsistent ratings across respondents. The relative usefulness and relevance of different recall periods and the impact of recall periods on the reliability of outcome data are an underexplored area of outcome measurement research, and further evidence is needed to support the selection of the most appropriate recall period for older people in both health and aged care settings [60, 61].

Cognitive Impairment/Proxy Completion

The relatively high proportion of older people with cognitive impairment, particularly in aged care settings, highlights the importance of ensuring instruments used with these populations are easily able to be understood and completed – ideally without the use of a proxy respondent. Evidence suggests that instruments with a smaller number of dimensions/items are less cognitively taxing, with reduced missing data, and therefore may be more suitable for use with older people [62, 63]. Ideally, tools should be qualitatively tested for ease of completion, comprehensiveness and comprehensibility, including with cognitively impaired samples. However, content validity evidence with older people is limited for most tools, and the qualitative testing of instruments has suffered from a lack of methodological clarity, particularly when including a cognitively impaired sample [64].

While self-complete is considered to be the gold standard, proxy completion is often used when self-report is considered not possible, and there is a paucity of evidence for cognitive thresholds for self-completion of most PBMs, which may contribute to the preferencing of proxy measurement. The Cleland review recommended novel approaches to exploring a cognitive threshold for self-completion, and subsequently, two studies have used these methods to obtain preliminary threshold evidence for two of the tools included in this review, the QOL-ACC and the EQ-5D-5L [59, 65]. These studies employed a think-aloud approach combined with eye tracking to estimate the threshold, recommending that self-report be the preferred option for respondents with a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score of ≥ 24 for the EQ-5D and ≥ 18 for the QOL-ACC. These recommendations should be interpreted with caution, however; it is possible that self-complete is viable for people with lower scores. It may not be feasible to obtain cognitive impairment data for older people moving through different social and health care systems; however, where this information is available or when it is possible to estimate cognitive impairment, following guidelines on cognitive impairment thresholds could facilitate a greater reliance on self-completion. While there are dementia-specific PBMs available, such as the Dementia Quality of Life (DEMQOL) group of instruments, which can be reliably self-completed by people with more advanced cognitive impairment, these are disease-specific tools and thus were not included in this review. Furthermore, in a study by Engel et al., a cohort of people with mild dementia and their carers expressed no preference for dementia-specific instruments [52]. The ASCOT is available in easy read format, and work is currently underway to develop a communication accessible version of the QOL-ACC to promote self-completion for people with dementia and cognitive impairment.

Where a proxy version is employed, care should be taken in selecting both the proxy respondent and the proxy version (i.e. proxy–proxy or proxy–person). Most tools identified in this review have proxy-person versions available, with the exception of the HUI and AQoL instruments. There have been found to be significant gaps between proxy- and self-rating across PBMs [66, 67]. The proxy version used influences this gap, with more divergence when a proxy-proxy perspective is employed as compared to a proxy-person perspective [68]. It is recommended that a close family member or friend is used as the proxy respondent rather than staff or clinicians; however, there is evidence that clinician ratings of physical dimensions are more closely aligned with patient perspectives [69]. Overall, psychosocial dimensions appear to be less reliably reported by proxies than physical dimensions [66, 70]. Additionally, proxy responses diverge from self-report responses progressively with increasing age and with level of cognitive impairment [71]. The implications of these issues for proxy and self-assessment are the subject of ongoing research; however, they serve to highlight the complexities of proxy response with older people where PBMs with more psychosocial domains are applied and discrepancies between proxies and self-reports tend to increase with age and cognitive impairment.

Valuation

The question of who should value health states for economic evaluation has been subject to much discussion and is the focus of ongoing debate [72, 73]. The traditional approach has been to use valuations elicited from the general public (i.e. taxpayers) when providing economic evaluation within the context of publicly funded health systems, with the equity rationale that those funding the system should determine how outcomes are valued. However, it has also been posited that value sets should be obtained from the population of interest to provide a more accurate representation of QALY gains [73]. There is evidence that patients and the general population value health states differently and that patient groups may be more consistent in their stated preferences [74, 75]. Older people specifically have been shown to display recalibration effects where they have fundamentally different expectations of health as opposed to younger adults. This adaptation process, also found in disease and disability groups, has been employed as an argument both for and against the valuation of health states by the population of interest/patient population, and the debate is yet to reach resolution [76]. It should be noted, however, that there is little argument that the population of interest is more qualified to value the states that they themselves have experience of and, importantly for older people, the preferences for prioritisation of different dimensions are markedly different [23]. The valuations of both the QOL-ACC and the ICECAP-O instruments were completed with an older adult population, while all other instruments in this review obtained value sets from a general population or a mental health specific sample. There have been calls to produce a method to enable the simultaneous incorporation of patient (or population group) value alongside general population preferences; however, no such method is available to date [77].

Translation and Cultural Adaptation

It is also important to recognise that many PBMs have been developed in high-income countries, such as the EQ-5D measures that were developed in Europe and the QOL-ACC that was developed in Australia. This means that PBMs may not be generalisable to all global ageing populations if they have not been translated, culturally adapted and validated with those specific populations [78].

Limitations

While systematic searching was conducted and a comprehensive search was undertaken, resource limitations restricted searching to three databases and prevented a fully double-screened approach, which may have impacted on the comprehensiveness of included articles. Additionally, only articles involving older people exclusively were included, and some studies that stratified samples by age or included a slightly younger population may have provided insights that were not included in this review. Therefore, we are aware that this may have affected the results in unforeseen ways. It is also important to point out that the measurement challenges discussed in this review are not exhaustive and other important issues remain un-addressed. However, the list of generic PBMs is exhaustive, and the review provides an overview of the methodological challenges in measuring QoL for older people using the available generic PBMs. We also acknowledge that as this review was a literature review utilising systematic searching and not a systematic review, it was outside of scope to appraise the included studies. Therefore, the conclusions of the review should be tempered by potentially weak methodology of included studies and no assessment of risk of bias.

Conclusions

The changing policy and practice landscape in many countries towards stronger integration of services necessitates generic PBMs that can be used to consistently measure QoL across aged and health care settings. This review has highlighted several key challenges inherent to the measurement and valuation of the QoL of older people within and across health and aged care systems, including enabling accessible completion for older people with cognitive impairment and ensuring appropriate and relevant descriptive systems, item wording and recall periods. Future research should aim to validate generic PBMs identified as acceptable, valid and relevant for older people across a variety of care settings to directly facilitate empirical comparisons in quality assessment and economic evaluation across the care journeys of older people.
