Hidden risk factors for recurrence of jejunoileal Gastrointestinal stromal tumors with low NIH classification: implications for surveillance and adjuvant therapy
Authors: Xiangfei Sun, Yinwen Sun, Ran Xiong, Yuning Zhou, Xiaodong Gao, Yingyong Hou, Kuntang Shen, Ping Shu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights This study investigated very low-risk and low-risk jejunoileal GISTs exclusively after R0 resection.Recurrence occurred in 15.4% of patients, with an overall mortality of 8.9%.Independent RFS predictors identified: tumor size > 3.9 cm, mitotic index > 2/50 HPF, Ki-67 > 3.5%, jejunal location.Independent OS predictors identified: Ki-67 > 3.5%, tumor size > 3.9 cm, female sex.Refining NIH risk stratification is recommended to enhance postoperative surveillance and guide tailored patient management. Keywords: Gastrointestinal stromal tumors, Jejunum, Ileum, Recurrence, Risk assessment

Introduction

Gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) are the most common mesenchymal tumors of the GI tract, originating from interstitial cells of Cajal [1]. While GISTs can arise anywhere along the gastrointestinal (GI) tract, the small intestine accounts for approximately 20–30% of all cases [2]. Compared to gastric GISTs, small intestinal GISTs are biologically more aggressive and exhibit higher rates of recurrence and metastasis even when the tumor size and mitotic index are controlled [3].

To stratify the risk of recurrence after complete surgical resection, several classification systems, such as the modified National Institute of Health (NIH) consensus criteria and the Armed Forces Institute of Pathology (AFIP) system, have been developed by integrating tumor size, mitotic count, and anatomical location [4–6]. According to these systems, GISTs classified as “very low risk” or “low risk” generally have favorable clinical outcomes [7]. However, increasing evidence suggests that this risk categorization may underestimate the malignant potential of small intestinal GISTs, particularly those arising in the jejunum and ileum [8].

Indeed, retrospective series have revealed unexpected recurrence and metastasis in patients with ostensibly low-risk small intestinal GISTs, which raises questions about whether current risk criteria sufficiently account for anatomical and molecular heterogeneity [9–11]. Given that adjuvant imatinib is typically not recommended for very low/low-risk GISTs, missing high-risk characteristics in this subgroup could lead to inadequate postoperative management and delay in intervention. This study aimed to analyze the clinicopathological characteristics, explore the recurrence pattern of patients with very low- and low-risk small intestinal GISTs who underwent R0 resection, and identify potential predictors of recurrence and metastasis in this subset of patients, with the hope of providing a basis for developing postoperative surveillance strategies and selecting candidate patients who may benefit from more intensive follow-up or even adjuvant therapy despite their nominal low-risk classification.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

This study included 123 patients with primary small intestinal GIST who underwent surgical treatment in Zhongshan Hospital of Fudan University (Shanghai, China) between January 2011 and December 2021, including 17 patients in the very low-risk group and 106 in the low-risk group according to the modified NIH classification. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with histologically confirmed GIST located in the jejunum or ileum; (2) patients diagnosed with very low- or low-risk GISTs based on the modified NIH consensus criteria (tumor size, mitotic index, and site); (3) patients who had received no neoadjuvant or adjuvant imatinib therapy; and (4) patients with complete clinical and pathological records and regular follow-up data. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients who underwent non-R0 resection; (2) patients with synchronous metastases at diagnosis; (3) GISTs originating outside the small intestine; and (4) patients with no incomplete clinical data. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board of our institution, and informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Clinicopathological data collection

Demographic and clinical variables included age, sex, tumor location (jejunum vs. ileum), tumor size, mitotic index, growth pattern (exophytic, endophytic or mixed), resection margin status, presence of tumor rupture, and immunohistochemical results. Mutation data for KIT and PDGFRA were recorded when available. All resected specimens were reviewed by two experienced pathologists. Tumor size was measured as the largest diameter in gross pathology. Mitotic index was determined using the 2/50 high-power fields (HPF) standard. The highest mitotic count was 5/50 HPF with a median of 2/50 HPF, which made the classical 5/50 HPF threshold inadequate for stratification. Recurrence and metastasis were confirmed by radiological or pathological examination.

Mutational Analysis of KIT and PDGFRA

Genomic DNA was extracted from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor tissue. Mutational analysis of KIT (exons 9, 11, 13, and 17) and PDGFRA (exons 12, 14, and 18) was performed by Sanger sequencing, covering the most common mutational hotspots in GISTs. The assay sensitivity was approximately 15% mutant allele frequency (MAF), meaning that a mutation needed to comprise at least 15% of the total DNA in the sample to be reliably detected. This threshold was determined based on laboratory validation using serial dilutions of mutant DNA in a wild-type background and is consistent with widely accepted detection limits for Sanger sequencing. All tumor samples in this study had sufficient tumor cell content (> 50%), minimizing the risk of false negatives due to low MAF. Sequencing was performed in a certified pathology laboratory, with positive and negative controls included in each batch to ensure accuracy.

Follow-up and recurrence assessment

Postoperative follow-up and recurrence assessment were performed according to current National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guideline recommendations [12]. Recurrence was defined as radiologically or pathologically confirmed tumor regrowth or metastasis after R0 resection. A modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors 1.1 (RECIST 1.1) set of guidelines were used to evaluate how effectively tumors responded to treatments based on changes in tumor size observed on images obtained by contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and equivocal cases were further evaluated by positron emission tomography (PET)-CT or biopsy [13]. All 123 patients (100%) completed follow-up at intervals of every 3–6 months for the first 5 years, then every 6–12 months, including physical examination, lab tests, and imaging. Recurrence-free survival (RFS) was defined as the time from surgery to the first documented recurrence or last follow-up. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from surgery to the date of death or the last follow-up. Patients without events were censored at the time of last contact.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS (version 26.0) software (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, depending on whether or not data were normally distributed. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Receiver- operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to determine the optimal tumor size cut-off for predicting recurrence, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. Kaplan-Meier analysis was used to estimate RFS, and differences were assessed using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to identify independent risk factors for recurrence and prognosis. Variables with P < 0.1 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of patients

A total of 123 patients with very low- or low-risk jejunoileal GISTs were included in this study (Fig. 1). Among them, 17 (13.8%) were classified as having very low-risk GISTs and the remaining 106 (86.2%) as having low-risk GISTs according to the modified NIH risk classification criteria. The median age was 57 (24–92) years, with a moderate male predominance (60.2%). The median tumor size was 3.5 cm (interquartile range (IQR): 2.5–4.5 cm). The Ki-67 proliferation index ranged from 1% to 60%, with a median of 4.89%. Analysis on the KIT and PDGFRA mutational status was performed in 99 GIST cases in this series, among which 81 cases were found harboring KIT mutations, including 15 cases in exon 9 (18.5%), 59 cases in exon 11 (72.8%), 6 cases in exon 13 (7.4%), and one case in exon 17 (1.2%). No PDGFRA mutation was detected in any of the cases. The remaining 18 cases (18.2%) were found to have the wild type without KIT or PDGFRA mutations. Tumors were located in the jejunum in 76 patients (61.8%) and in the ileum in the remaining 47 patients. Among them, 19 patients (15.4%) developed tumor recurrence or distant metastasis, and 11 patients died due to disease progression. Compared to jejunal tumors, ileal GISTs showed a significantly lower recurrence rate (6.4% vs. 21.1%, P = 0.029), but had a higher proportion of tumors with Ki-67 > 3.5% (68.1% vs. 35.5%, P < 0.001) and mitotic count > 2/50 HPF (38.3% vs. 19.7%, P = 0.024). Recurrence and metastasis outcomes

Over a median follow-up period of 70 (2-125) months, 19 patients (15.4%) developed tumor recurrence or distant metastasis, including hepatic metastases in 10 patients, peritoneal dissemination in two patients, and recurrence near the surgical bed in seven patients. Compared to the non-recurrence group (n = 104), the recurrence group had significantly more tumors > 3.9 cm (78.9% vs. 34.6%, P = 0.001), higher mitotic index (> 2/50 HPF: 52.6% vs. 22.1%, P = 0.006), and increased Ki-67 index (> 3.5%: 73.7% vs. 43.3%, P = 0.015). Detailed comparisons are shown in Table 2. Notably, all 19 patients with recurrent progression were found in the low-risk group, indicating that even patients traditionally classified as low risk have a non-negligible risk of relapse. In our cohort, all procedures were performed using a laparoscopic approach and no tumor rupture occurred during surgery. Therefore, these factors were not included in the statistical analysis. No patients received adjuvant imatinib postoperatively, following current guideline recommendations.

ROC curve analysis was performed for both tumor size and Ki-67 proliferation index. Tumor size showed a significant ability to predict recurrence, with an optimal cut-off value of 3.9 cm. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis

A Cox regression model was used to determine factors affecting RFS and OS. Univariate analysis of RFS showed that jejunal location (hazard ratio (HR): 0.285; 95% CI 0.083–0.980; P = 0.046), tumor size > 3.9 cm (HR: 5.187; 95% CI 1.716–15.676; P = 0.004), mitotic count > 2/50 HPFs (HR: 2.929; 95% CI 1.189–7.218; P = 0.019), and Ki-67 > 3.5% (HR: 2.888; 95% CI 1.040–8.023; P = 0.042) were significantly associated with worse RFS. Multivariate analysis confirmed that jejunal location (HR: 0.175; 95% CI 0.048–0.636; P = 0.008), tumor size > 3.9 cm (HR: 3.828; 95% CI 1.242–11.795; P = 0.019), mitotic count > 2/50 HPFs (HR: 2.871; 95% CI 1.092–7.550; P = 0.033), and Ki-67 > 3.5% (HR: 3.078; 95% CI 1.035–9.156; P = 0.043) were independent risk factors associated with RFS (Fig. 4A).

Univariate analysis of OS showed that female sex (HR = 4.483, 95% CI 1.188–16.921, P = 0.027), tumor size > 3.9 cm (HR = 13.299, 95% CI 1.698–104.130, P = 0.014), and Ki-67 index > 3.5% (HR = 4.694, 95% CI 1.014–21.731, P = 0.048) were significantly associated with worse survival. In multivariate analysis of OS, female sex (HR = 5.850, 95% CI 1.380–24.801, P = 0.017), tumor size > 3.9 cm (HR = 12.824, 95% CI 1.618–101.626, P = 0.016), and Ki-67 index > 3.5% (HR = 8.352, 95% CI 1.619–43.096, P = 0.011) showed independent associations (Fig. 4B).

These findings highlight the prognostic value of tumor size, mitotic activity, proliferation index, and anatomical site even in very low- or low-risk jejunoileal GISTs, underscoring the need for refined risk stratification beyond the NIH classification criteria.

Survival analysis

During a median follow-up of 70 months, the 1-, 3-, 5-year RFS rates in all patients of this series were 99.2%, 95.7% and 90.2%, the 1-,3-,5-year OS rates in all patients of this series were 100.0%, 98.3% and 95.5% respectively, and no median RFS and OS were reached. Kaplan–Meier analysis showed significantly reduced RFS among patients with tumor size > 3.9 cm, mitotic index > 2/50 HPF, Ki-67 index > 3.5%, and jejunal tumors (all P < 0.05). Similarly, OS was significantly worse in patients with tumor size > 3.9 cm, Ki-67 > 3.5%, and female sex (Fig. 5A–I).

Nomogram construction and calibration

To provide individualized prognostic assessments, nomograms were constructed to predict the 3- and 5-year RFS and OS in patients with low- and very low-risk jejunoileal GISTs by incorporating independent prognostic variables identified by multivariate Cox regression analysis, including tumor size, mitotic index, Ki-67 index, tumor location, and sex. Each variable was assigned a specific score on the nomogram, and the total score was used to estimate the probability of RFS or OS at defined time points (Fig. 6A, C). The predictive performance of the nomograms was evaluated using calibration curves by comparing predicted probabilities with actual observed outcomes. As shown in Fig. 6B and D, the calibration plots for 3-and 5-year survival revealed excellent concordance between the predicted and observed outcomes, indicating the high reliability and discrimination ability of the models (Fig. 6B, D).

Discussion

This study revealed that a considerable proportion of patients with very low- or low-risk jejunoileal GISTs still experienced recurrence or metastasis after complete surgical resection, even in the absence of commonly recognized high-risk features. Our findings can supplement the existing risk stratification systems and underscore the need for more refined prognostic tools tailored to the biological behavior of jejunoileal GISTs [7, 14, 15].

Notably, all 19 cases of recurrence/metastasis occurred in the low-risk group, suggesting that a low-risk classification does not imply a benign clinical course. These patients had significantly larger tumors, higher mitotic counts, and increased Ki-67 indices, features that are not sufficiently emphasized in the NIH classification [5]. Previous studies have also reported unexpected recurrence in ostensibly low-risk jejunoileal GISTs, supporting our finding that risk of recurrence may be underestimated in this subgroup [16–18].

In this study, multivariate Cox regression analysis identified tumor size > 3.9 cm, mitotic count > 2/50 HPF, Ki-67 index > 3.5%, and jejunal location as independent predictors of worse RFS, which is consistent with earlier reports indicating that tumor size and proliferative activity are key determinants of prognosis even within low-risk categories [19, 20]. For OS, female sex, tumor size > 3.9 cm, and Ki-67 > 3.5% were also found to be associated with significantly worse outcomes, which highlights the need to take into account sex-specific biological differences [21]. Although the mutational status of KIT/PDGFRA was not available for all cases, comparative analyses showed no significant differences in clinicopathological features or survival between patients with and without mutation data (all P > 0.05). Thus, the missing data are unlikely to have materially influenced our overall findings or conclusions (Supplementary Table 1).

The prognostic value of the Ki-67 index in GISTs has been investigated in multiple studies, revealing that elevated Ki-67 index levels were associated with higher mitotic index and worse outcomes, particularly in small intestinal tumors [22–24]. However, the Ki-67 index is not incorporated in the current NIH or AFIP classification criteria, which may limit their predictive accuracy in certain anatomical subsets. Our ROC curve analysis determined an optimal cut-off of 3.5% for Ki-67, which indicated a good discriminatory performance (AUC = 0.697), and is consistent with previous studies suggesting that 3–5% is a biologically meaningful threshold [25].

Remarkably, jejunal GISTs in our series were not only more common than ileal tumors but also had a significantly higher risk of postoperative recurrence. Multivariate analysis confirmed ileal location as an independent protective factor (HR = 0.175; P = 0.008). The anatomical and microenvironmental differences between the jejunum and ileum, such as variations in blood supply, immune surveillance, or molecular subtypes, may contribute to this discrepancy [26–28]. First, previous studies have found that jejunal GISTs often harbor KIT exon 11 deletions and other mutations associated with higher proliferative activity and aggressive behavior, compared to ileal GISTs [26, 29]. Second, anatomical differences, such as richer vascular supply and longer segment length of the jejunum, may provide a microenvironment more conducive to tumor growth and possibly complicate complete microscopic clearance during surgery [30, 31]. Third, the local immune microenvironment and gastrointestinal motility differences between the jejunum and ileum may contribute to differential tumor progression [32]. These considerations suggest that tumor location reflects important biological variability rather than being a purely statistical observation, although further mechanistic studies are needed.

Besides tumor size, mitotic index, Ki-67 index, and location, our study also revealed a significant sex effect. Female sex was independently associated with worse OS. Although the biological mechanisms remain unclear, previous evidence suggests potential roles of sex hormones, genetic background, and immune response [33–36]. Our finding indicates that sex may further refine risk stratification, and female patients might warrant closer postoperative surveillance. To aid clinical decision-making, we constructed prognostic nomograms based on tumor size, mitotic index, Ki-67 index, sex, and tumor location, and the result revealed that they offered strong calibration and discrimination for predicting 3-year and 5-year RFS and OS. Previous studies have demonstrated the utility of nomograms for individualized risk prediction in GISTs [6–8], and our model further enhances it by integrating proliferative and anatomical variables that are often overlooked in categorical classification systems.

Established risk factors in the NIH classification criteria mainly include tumor size, mitotic index, and tumor location. However, our analysis identified additional parameters—such as Ki-67 index and patient sex—that may affect recurrence or survival but are not routinely taken into in current risk models. From a clinical perspective, our findings support more intensive postoperative surveillance for patients with low-risk jejunoileal GISTs harboring unfavorable features, particularly in cases of tumors > 3.9 cm, Ki-67 index > 3.5%, and mitotic count > 2/50 HPFs. We propose that these patients should undergo imaging every 3 months in the first 3 years, then every 6 months until year 5, followed by annual follow-up. In addition, these patients may also benefit from consideration of adjuvant imatinib therapy.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it is a single-center retrospective study, which may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability. Second, the cohort consisted entirely of Chinese patients, and external validation in multicenter or international cohorts is needed. Therefore, future prospective studies with larger and more diverse populations are required to confirm these findings and refine risk stratification for very low-risk and low-risk jejunoileal GISTs.

Conclusion

In summary, our study has demonstrated that recurrence is often unavoidable even in patients with low-risk GISTs, particularly those with tumors located in the jejunum, tumor size > 3.9 cm, mitotic count > 2/50 HPFs, and Ki-67 index > 3.5%. These findings underscore the need for refined, anatomy-specific risk stratification in jejunoileal GISTs. Integrating Ki-67 index and tumor location into prognostic models could help identify high-risk individuals within this subgroup, thus improving follow-up observation and the outcome of adjuvant therapy in such patients.
