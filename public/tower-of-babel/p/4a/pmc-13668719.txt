The role of the globus pallidus subregions in the schizophrenia spectrum continuum
Authors: Naohiro Okada, Kentaro Morita, Shimon Tonsho, Masahiro Kiyota, Eimu Shoji, Daisuke Koshiyama, Shinsuke Koike, Syudo Yamasaki, Atsushi Nishida, Shuntaro Ando, Noriaki Yahata, Kouhei Kamiya, Akira Kunimatsu, Osamu Abe, Sho Yagishita, Kiyoto Kasai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Within the cortico-basal ganglia network, the volume and functional connectivity (FC) of the globus pallidus (GP) are altered in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders, with GP enlargement being relatively specific to schizophrenia compared to other disorders. However, little is known about alterations in its two major segments, externus (GPe) and internus (GPi), in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders. In this study, we compared GPe/GPi volume and FC between patients with schizophrenia and controls (n > 100), and examined the associations of GPe/GPi volume and FC with subclinical psychotic experiences (SPEs) in early adolescents (n > 200). We found GPe-dominant enlargement and GP subregion-specific FC patterns (e.g., increased left GPe FC with the lateral occipitoparietal area and decreased left GPi FC with the precuneus) in schizophrenia. Additionally, we observed a positive association between GPe volume and SPEs in adolescence, as well as GP subregion-specific FC patterns (e.g., larger left GPe FC with the lingual gyrus) in adolescents with SPEs compared to those without. Future translational studies are needed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying such GP subregion-specific neurophysiological roles.

Introduction

Schizophrenia is a major psychiatric disorder, characterized by positive symptoms, negative symptoms, and cognitive impairments. Its onset often occurs during adolescence and young adulthood, affecting patients’ lives chronically. The dopamine hypothesis is one of the most robust theories regarding the neurophysiology of schizophrenia. Compatible with this hypothesis, considerable attention has been directed towards the disconnection between the cortices and the basal ganglia (i.e., the disconnected cortico-basal ganglia network) [1, 2]. Large-scale mega-analytic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies reported volumetric enlargement of basal ganglia, especially the globus pallidus (GP), in patients with schizophrenia [3–5]. This enlargement correlates positively with the dosage of antipsychotic medication and the duration of illness [6]. The enlargement of the globus pallidus has been reported even in first-episode schizophrenia (FES), although in a relatively small-scale study [7]. Additionally, we have found a marginally significant enlargement of the left GP volume in early adolescents with subclinical psychotic experiences (SPEs) compared to those without [8]. SPEs can be seen in the general population as a subthreshold phenotype, suggesting a continuum of psychosis from subclinical to clinical levels [9]. SPEs in early adolescence are a risk factor for developing schizophrenia later in life [10]. In sum, these findings suggest that enlarged GP volumes can be found across the stages of the schizophrenia spectrum. Moreover, according to our recent large-scale cross-disorder biotyping study, enlarged basal ganglia were found to constitute a brain structural biotype that is relatively specific to schizophrenia [5].

Previous functional MRI (fMRI) studies reported altered functional connectivity (FC) of the GP in patients with schizophrenia. The GP FC is decreased in schizophrenia compared to normal in the salience network, including the prefrontal-limbic cortices [11], the anterior cingulate cortex [12], and the insula [12, 13]. In addition, the GP FC is larger than normal in deteriorated schizophrenia in the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), precuneus, thalamus, and left superior temporal gyrus (STG) [13]. In patients with FES, interhemispheric GP FC [14], right GP – right thalamus FC [15], GP - supramarginal gyrus FC (only in patients with severe cognitive impairment) [16], and GP - raphe nucleus FC are smaller than normal [17], whereas right GP - right medio-dorsal thalamic nucleus FC is larger than normal [18].

The GP is anatomically divided into two major segments: the globus pallidus externus (GPe) and the globus pallidus internus (GPi), each playing independent roles in forming neural circuits with other brain regions. The GPi and GPe are the output nuclei of the basal ganglia circuit and receive input from the striatum within this circuit, with the GPi receiving input from the direct pathway and the GPe from the indirect pathway. The direct pathway goes from the striatum directly to the GPi and substantia nigra pars reticulata, while the indirect pathway passes through the GPe and the subthalamic nucleus before reaching the GPi and substantia nigra pars reticulata [19]. The direct pathway is excitatory for movement, whereas the indirect pathway inhibits movement. Additionally, the hyperdirect pathway involves direct input from the cerebral cortex to the subthalamic nucleus, which then projects to the GPi and substantia nigra pars reticulata [20]. Dysfunction in these neural circuits is implicated in movement disorders such as Parkinson’s disease [21] and Huntington’s disease [22]. Moreover, in both levodopa-induced dyskinesia and tardive dyskinesia, the volumes of presynaptic medium spiny neuron terminals and the postsynaptic somata in GPe neurons are increased, with these structural changes attributed to striatal overexpression of the vesicular gamma-aminobutyric acid transporter [23].

Recent research has revealed associations between the GPe/GPi and cognitive and mental functions, while it is already known that the GP has various roles not only in learning [24], attention [25], and emotions [26], but also in higher cognitive functions like inhibitory control [27], working memory [28], and goal determination, and action specification [29]. A study reported that deep brain stimulation (DBS) of the GPi is effective in improving symptoms of refractory Tourette syndrome [30]. According to a meta-analytic study, better outcomes can be obtained using GPi stimulation compared to thalamic stimulation [31]. Another study reported that DBS of the GPe for refractory Tourette syndrome has shown efficacy not only for tics but also for comorbidities such as depression and anxiety [32]. Therefore, it is conceivable that dysfunction in the GPe/GPi may be associated not only with movement disorders but also with psychiatric conditions and symptoms [33].

The GPe and GPi have different functions from each other as mentioned above, suggesting that they may play distinct roles in the neurophysiological substrates for the schizophrenia spectrum. A recent mouse study by Kang et al. reported that astrocyte activities in the GPe regulate action choice strategies in reward-seeking behaviors [34]. Notably, these behaviors are reduced in patients with schizophrenia [35]. Another mouse study by Farries et al. found that cell-specific forms of GPe value representation may influence the circuit mechanisms related to motivation and reinforcement learning [36]. These reports imply a potential link between GPe dysfunction and reinforcement learning deficits in schizophrenia. It is well established that dopamine activates direct pathway neurons through D1 receptors (D1Rs) and inhibits indirect pathway neurons through D2 receptors (D2Rs) [37]. Yagishita et al. reported that dopamine D1Rs detect a phasic burst of dopamine, promoting spine enlargement and encoding a reward-prediction error [38]. They also reported that while reward conditioning induces marked generalization in D1R-expressing neurons, this conditioning is refined by discrimination learning through transient reductions in dopamine concentration, called ‘dopamine dips,’ and spine enlargement in dopamine D2R-expressing neurons [39]. Dysregulated dopamine signaling caused by methamphetamine impairs discrimination learning and spine enlargement, and a D2R antagonist reverses these impairments [39], suggesting that impaired discrimination learning may lead to positive symptoms such as delusions.

It is thus crucial to elucidate, in a human study with a forward translational approach, whether structural or functional alterations are present in either the GPe, GPi, or both in schizophrenia, along with the imbalance of D1R/D2R (generalization/discrimination). However, to the best of our knowledge, no study has examined the GPe/GPi volumetric alterations and their association with clinical symptoms in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders in a reliable way. This is partly because, until recently, there was no automated image-processing method capable of accurately segmenting the GPe and GPi. Regarding functional research, Qi et al. reported larger-than-normal FC in patients with FES between the right GPi and several brain regions, including the right amygdala, right fusiform gyrus, right parahippocampal gyrus, right precentral gyrus, and bilateral postcentral gyrus [40]. In addition, Tarcijonas et al. revealed lower-than-normal left GPi FC with the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, caudate, and cerebellum in FES [41]. In contrast, an fMRI study by Fiore et al. reported increased blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) activation in the GPe in a high-reward condition compared to a low-reward condition and under risperidone administration compared to placebo, suggesting that action-state values are encoded in the GPe [42]. However, there have been few studies thus far investigating the FC between the GPe/GPi and other brain regions (i.e., cortico-basal ganglia networks specific to GPe or GPi, or shared with them) in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders.

In this context, we investigated the volume and FC alterations of the GPe/GPi and their association with clinical symptoms in patients with schizophrenia and high-risk adolescents (those with SPEs). Our hypothesis was that one or both of these GP subregions are altered in schizophrenia or high-risk groups and are associated with symptom severity. Adolescent participants aged about 11–12 years were included in this study. Children with psychotic symptoms at age 11 are at an increased risk of developing schizophrenia by age 38, with a relative risk of 7.2 compared to those without such symptoms [43]. Moreover, the risk of psychotic disorders at age 18 is greater in those with psychotic experiences at age 12 [44], suggesting that SPEs in early adolescence may indicate a later onset of psychotic disorders. Furthermore, early adolescents (ages 11–13 years) are more likely to have psychotic experiences compared to the general population and mid-adolescents (ages 13–16 years) [45, 46]. Thus, it is rational to investigate early adolescents with SPEs. In the volumetric analysis, the deep brain structures segmentation (DBSegment) software [47, 48], a state-of-the-art neuroimaging analysis method using deep learning techniques, was employed to segment the GPe and GPi more accurately than ever before. This study was expected to characterize the roles of the GPe and GPi as neurophysiological substrates in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders.

Results

Study 1: Schizophrenia cohort study

For the structural MRI (sMRI) analysis, regarding demographic characteristics, healthy controls (n = 63) were on average significantly older than patients with schizophrenia (n = 45) (t = 5.0, p = 2.5 × 10−6), and sex differences between the two groups were trend-level significant (χ2 = 3.2, p = 0.072) (Table 1a). The DBSegment was used to segment GPe and GPi from T1-weighted data, based on the Deep brain stimulation Intrinsic Template Atlas (DISTAL) atlas [49]. All segmentations passed visual quality control. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) revealed that the main effect of diagnosis on GP volumes was significant after controlling for age, sex, and intracranial volume (ICV) (F = 7.2, p = 8.4 × 10−3). The effects of diagnosis × subregion (externus/internus) interaction were significant (F = 8.1, p = 5.3 × 10−3) (Fig. 1a), while neither the diagnosis × hemisphere (left/right) interaction effects (F = 0.43, p = 0.52) nor the diagnosis × subregion × hemisphere interaction effects (F = 0.80, p = 0.37) were significant. Parameter estimation revealed that the left GPe (t = 2.5, p = 0.016, η² = 0.055, Cohen’s d = 0.48) and the right GPe (t = 3.0, p = 3.9 × 10−3, η² = 0.078, Cohen’s d = 0.58) were significantly larger in patients with schizophrenia than in healthy controls, while there was no significant difference in the volumes of the left GPi (t = 2.0, p = 0.052, η² = 0.036, Cohen’s d = 0.39) or the right GPi (t = 1.8, p = 0.075, η² = 0.030, Cohen’s d = 0.35) between the two groups (Fig. 1a). In patients with schizophrenia, none of the left/right GPe/GPi volumes were significantly associated with the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS) total score (Fig. 1b). However, exploratory analysis revealed significant (but uncorrected) positive associations of the right GPi volume with the BPRS positive symptoms subscale (t = 2.3, p = 0.025, n = 44) and the BPRS activation subscale (t = 2.3, p = 0.026, n = 44), while no other significant associations were found between any of the left/right GPe/GPi volumes and any of the BPRS subscales (Supplementary Fig. S1). Additionally, none of the left/right GPe/GPi volumes were significantly associated with daily doses of antipsychotics (Fig. 1c). For the resting-state fMRI (rsfMRI) analysis, regarding demographic characteristics, healthy controls (n = 60) were on average significantly older than patients with schizophrenia (n = 44) (t = 4.8, p = 6.7 × 10−6), and sex differences between the two groups were not significant (χ2 = 2.2, p = 0.14) (Table 1b). The CONN toolbox was used to preprocess and analyze rsfMRI data. Statistical significance was determined using a cluster-level false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected threshold of p < 0.05 and a voxel-level uncorrected threshold of p < 0.001. Using the DISTAL atlas, in patients with schizophrenia, after controlling for age and sex, the left GPe-seeded FC was significantly larger than normal in the left lateral occipital gyrus (peak Montreal Neurological Institute [MNI] coordinate [x, y, z] = [−58, −64, −10], cluster size = 106, cluster-level FDR-corrected p = 0.040) (Fig. 2a1, Fig. 2b1), and the left GPi-seeded FC was significantly smaller than normal in the precuneus (peak MNI coordinate [x, y, z] = [16, −72, 48], cluster size = 257, cluster-level FDR-corrected p = 0.00016; peak MNI coordinate [x, y, z] = [−14, −66, 44], cluster size = 165, cluster-level FDR-corrected p = 0.0021) (Fig. 2a2, Fig. 2b2). Additional analyses using the CIT168 atlas [50] and the Amsterdam Ultra-high field adult lifespan database (AHEAD) atlas [51] revealed that patients with schizophrenia exhibited significantly greater left GPe-seeded FC in the right angular gyrus and the right putamen (Fig. 2a4, Fig. 2a6, Fig. 2b4, Fig. 2b6), as well as significantly reduced left GPi-seeded FC in the precuneus (Fig. 2a5, Fig. 2a7, Fig. 2b5, Fig. 2b7). Because of discordant spatial patterns of the group differences in the left GPe-seeded FC across atlases, results obtained using a more liberal significance threshold are presented in Supplementary Fig. S2. The right GPe- or right GPi-seeded FC was not significantly different between patients with schizophrenia and healthy controls. These results are summarized in Fig. 2. Regarding clinical associations in patients with schizophrenia, analysis using the DISTAL atlas found that the right GPe-seeded FC was significantly negatively correlated with the BPRS score in the left middle temporal gyrus (peak MNI coordinate [x, y, z] = [−52, −60, 2], cluster size = 117, cluster-level FDR-corrected p = 0.0091) (Fig. 2a3, Fig. 2b3). This result was not replicated using the CIT168 atlas or the AHEAD atlas. Exploratory analysis revealed that, using the DISTAL atlas, the BPRS activation subscale was significantly positively correlated with the left GPe-seeded FC in the left Heschl’s gyrus (Peak MNI coordinate [x, y, z] = [−52, −12, 2], cluster size = 132, cluster-level FDR-corrected p = 0.015), the right superior temporal gyrus (peak MNI coordinate [x, y, z] = [58, −4, −4], cluster size = 105, cluster-level FDR-corrected p = 0.025), and the accumbens (peak MNI coordinate [x, y, z] = [−8, 4, −16], cluster size = 86, cluster-level FDR-corrected p = 0.040), and negatively correlated with the left GPe-seeded FC in the left cerebellum (peak MNI coordinate [x, y, z] = [−14, −68, −18], cluster size = 81, cluster-level FDR-corrected p = 0.040), while no other significant associations were found between any of the left/right GPe/GPi-seeded FC and any of the BPRS subscales (Supplementary Fig. S3). The results from additional analyses using the CIT168 atlas and the AHEAD atlas are also presented in Supplementary Fig. S3. Furthermore, no significant association was found in patients with schizophrenia between any of the left/right GPe/GPi-seeded FC and daily doses of antipsychotics (Fig. 2). Study 2: Adolescent cohort study

For the sMRI analysis, regarding demographic characteristics, SPE-negative (n = 139) and SPE-positive adolescents (n = 65) showed no significant differences in age (t = −0.6, p = 0.54) or sex (χ2 = 0.0, p = 0.95), while the SPE-negative group had a lower Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) total difficulties (TD) score than the SPE-positive group at a trend level (t = −1.8, p = 0.07) (Table 1c). The effects of age at the time of participation in the TTC on the SPE score (rho = −0.042, p = 0.55) or SDQ TD score were not significant (rho = −0.050, p = 0.47). The DBSegment was used to segment GPe and GPi from T1-weighted data, based on the DISTAL atlas. In 196 out of 204 subjects, segmentations passed visual quality control. In the remaining eight subjects, parts of the right GPe were mistakenly segmented as the left GPe. These mis-segmented regions were then correctly re-assigned to the right GPe (Supplementary Fig. S5). ANCOVA revealed that the main effect of SPE-groups on GP volumes was not significant after controlling for age, sex, ICV, and the SDQ TD score (F = 0.21, p = 0.65). Neither the SPE-group × subregion interaction (F = 0.000003, p = 1.0), the SPE-group × hemisphere interaction (F = 3.4, p = 0.65), nor the SPE-group × subregion × hemisphere interaction (F = 1.6, p = 0.20) had significant effects. Parameter estimation revealed that neither the left GPe (t = 0.74, p = 0.46, η² = 2.7 × 10−3, Cohen’s d = 0.11), the right GPe (t = −0.16, p = 0.88, η² = 1.2 × 10−4, Cohen’s d = −0.023), the left GPi (t = 1.0, p = 0.30, η² = 5.5 × 10−3, Cohen’s d = 0.16), nor the right GPi (t = 0.38, p = 0.70, η² = 7.5 × 10−4, Cohen’s d = 0.058) was significantly different in size between SPE-negative and SPE-positive adolescents (Fig. 3a). Additionally, in all subjects, the SPE score was significantly positively correlated with the left GPe volume (rho = 0.14, FDR-corrected p = 0.045) and the left GPi (rho = 0.12, FDR-corrected p = 0.0495) after controlling for age, sex, ICV, and the SDQ TD score, while it was not significantly associated with the right GPe (rho = 0.070, FDR-corrected p = 0.21) or the right GPi volumes (rho = 0.058, FDR-corrected p = 0.21) (Fig. 3b). Validation analysis revealed that the GP volumes calculated using the two methods (DBSegment and FreeSurfer) were significantly positively correlated on both sides (left: r = 0.54, p = 4.2 × 10−17; right: r = 0.76, p = 2.6 × 10−40) (Fig. 3c). Discussion

In the sMRI analysis of Study 1, we revealed the significant effects of the diagnosis × subregion interaction and found that the bilateral GPe (Cohen’s d = 0.48–0.58), but not the bilateral GPi (Cohen’s d = 0.35–0.39), was significantly larger in patients with schizophrenia than in healthy controls (Fig. 1a). In other words, the GPe enlargement was significantly more pronounced than the GPi enlargement in schizophrenia. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to reveal GPe-dominant enlargement in schizophrenia. The GPe is an output nucleus of the basal ganglia circuit and receives input from the striatum via the indirect pathway. The indirect pathway passes through the GPe and the subthalamic nucleus before reaching the GPi and the substantia nigra pars reticulata [19]. Thus, it is suggested that the indirect pathway is more affected than the direct pathway in patients with schizophrenia. In the striatum, discrimination learning refines reward conditioning through a dopamine dip detected by D2Rs, which induces spine enlargement in D2R-expressing neurons, while both discrimination learning and spine enlargement are impaired in a schizophrenia mouse model [39]. Considering that dopamine activates direct pathway neurons through D1Rs and inhibits indirect pathway neurons through D2Rs [37], and that the GPe lies downstream of the indirect pathway relative to the striatum [19], GPe-dominant enlargement may be a compensatory alteration for impaired discrimination learning and weakened inhibitory activity of the indirect pathway due to high dopamine occupancy of D2Rs. However, further studies are needed to elucidate why the GPe enlargement is more pronounced than the GPi enlargement in schizophrenia. Additionally, exploratory analysis revealed significant (but uncorrected) positive associations in patients with schizophrenia between the right GPi volume and the BPRS subscales of positive symptoms and activation (Supplementary Fig. S1). A rsfMRI study reported larger-than-normal right GPi FC in patients with FES [40]. While larger volumetric and FC in the right GPi may be related to psychotic symptoms, it is still unknown why and how the right GPi volume is related to such symptoms.

In the rsfMRI analysis of Study 1, we found that, in patients with schizophrenia compared to healthy subjects, the left GPe-seeded FC was significantly larger in the left lateral occipital gyrus (Fig. 2a1, Fig. 2b1). Recent rsfMRI studies have reported increased interaction of the lateral occipital gyrus with other regions [52] and reduced functional local efficiency in schizophrenia [53]. Additionally, a recent fMRI study showed stronger top-down inhibition from the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex to the lateral occipital gyrus in early psychosis [54]. These previous findings may be related to our results. While increased left GPe-left lateral occipital gyrus FC was demonstrated in schizophrenia using the DISTAL atlas, this was not replicated using the CIT168 atlas or the AHEAD atlas. Thus, this finding should be interpreted with caution. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S2, there were shared results across atlases using a liberal significance threshold (cluster-level FDR-corrected p < 0.05, voxel-level uncorrected p < 0.005). Thus, the application of a conservative significance threshold (cluster-level FDR-corrected p < 0.05, voxel-level uncorrected p < 0.001) may have led to the discordant spatial patterns. One possible reason for the FC differences across atlases is that each GP subregion is a relatively small structure. Using the CIT168 atlas and the AHEAD atlas, it was found that the left GPe-seeded FC was significantly larger in the right angular gyrus and the right putamen (Fig. 2a4, Fig. 2a6, Fig. 2b4, Fig. 2b6). The angular gyrus, like the lateral occipital gyrus, belongs to the lateral occipitoparietal area. Additionally, our finding of the increased GPe-angular gyrus FC in schizophrenia is in line with the study by Yasuda et al., that reported stronger-than-normal FC between the GP and the PCC/precuneus, belonging to the default mode network (DMN) [55], in patients with deteriorated schizophrenia [13]. A recent study from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) reported a negative correlation (an anti-correlation) between the GP and DMN in healthy subjects [56]. Thus, it is suggested that such a normal regulatory system may be impaired in patients with schizophrenia. A mouse study demonstrated that decreasing function of the striatopallidal pathway increases motivation by strengthening the initiation of goal-directed action [57]. Combining this with our finding of the larger-than-normal GPe-putamen FC in schizophrenia, we suggest that enhanced striatopallidal FC may serve as a neurophysiological basis for avolition in schizophrenia. We also found that the left GPi-seeded FC was significantly smaller in the precuneus in patients with schizophrenia compared to healthy subjects using all the three atlases (Fig. 2a2, Fig. 2a5, Fig. 2a7, Fig. 2b2, Fig. 2b5, Fig. 2b7). Considering the enhanced whole GP-DMN FC in schizophrenia found in a previous study [13], and the enhanced GPe-DMN FC and reduced GPi-DMN FC in schizophrenia found in this study, we suggest that the GPe and GPi may have differentiated network functions in the neurophysiology of schizophrenia. Wang et al. revealed that, in healthy subjects, oxytocin receptor (OXTR) gene rs2254298 A-allele carriers, vulnerable to developing various psychopathological conditions, had significantly lower FC between the PCC/precuneus and the GP compared to individuals with the GG genotype [58]. Montag et al. found that, in patients with schizophrenia, OXTR gene rs2254298 A-allele carriers showed significantly higher general psychopathology compared to those with the GG genotype [59]. Thus, the smaller left GPi-precuneus FC may serve as a neural basis for general symptoms, such as anxiety and depression, of schizophrenia. Exploratory analysis using the DISTAL atlas and the AHEAD atlas revealed that, in patients with schizophrenia, the BPRS activation subscale was positively correlated with the left GPe-seeded FC in the left Heschl’s gyrus (Supplementary Fig. S3). While little is known about the significance of the GP-Heschl’s gyrus FC, Shinn et al. reported that functional connectivity of the left Heschl’s gyrus plays a role in vulnerability to auditory hallucinations in schizophrenia [60], which is in line with our results.

In the sMRI analysis of Study 2, we found that, while there were no significant differences in the volume of any of the left/right GPe/i between SPE-negative and SPE-positive adolescents (Fig. 3a), the SPE score was significantly positively correlated with the left GPe volume in all subjects (Fig. 3b), and this association remained significant even when the analysis was restricted to subjects with successful segmentation. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to reveal a positive correlation between the GPe volume and psychotic signs in early adolescence. Using the same dataset, we previously found a marginally significant enlargement of the left GP volume in adolescents with SPEs compared to those without [8], which is consistent with the current finding. Additionally, Study 1 revealed GPe-dominant enlargement in schizophrenia (Fig. 1a), which is also consistent with the current finding. Considering both results, we suggest that GPe enlargement is present even in the very early stage of the schizophrenia spectrum with relatively elevated psychotic-like experiences, and that it becomes more prominent along with the clinical stages of the disorder. However, as a recent study reported an increase in GP volume in patients with FES after antipsychotic medication [61], it should be noted that not only the progression of the disorder but also antipsychotic medication may increase GPe volume. Collectively, our results are expected to deepen the understanding of GP subregional volumetric enlargement in the schizophrenia spectrum.

In the rsfMRI analysis of Study 2, we found that, in SPE-positive adolescents compared to SPE-negative ones, the left GPe-seeded FC was significantly larger in the lingual gyrus using all the three atlases (Fig. 4a1, Fig. 4a4, Fig. 4a5, Fig. 4b1, Fig. 4b4, Fig. 4b5). According to a meta-analytic study, the lingual gyrus is a region where hyperactivation is seen in schizophrenia [62]. Our results may represent the early stage of this phenomenon. In the rsfMRI analysis of Study 1, we found that, in patients with schizophrenia compared to healthy subjects, the left GPe-seeded FC was significantly larger in the right angular gyrus (Fig. 2a4, Fig. 2a6, Fig. 2b4, Fig. 2b6). The lingual gyrus and the right angular gyrus are both related to visuospatial processing [63, 64]. In addition, visuospatial memory impairments are associated with an increased risk of psychotic experiences, according to the Danish High Risk and Resilience Study, which included 449 children [65]. Visuospatial working memory is impaired in patients with schizophrenia [66]. Thus, visuospatial impairment may be related to psychotic symptoms through the increased GPe-lingual/angular gyri FC across the schizophrenia spectrum. However, further studies are necessary to reveal the detailed mechanisms. Moreover, in all subjects, the SPE score was significantly positively correlated with the right GPi-seeded FC in the right frontal pole using the DISTAL atlas and the AHEAD atlas (Fig. 4a3, Fig. 4a6, Fig. 4b3, Fig. 4b6). Qi et al. reported a positive correlation between severity of positive symptoms and FC between the right anterior GPi and several regions including the orbitofrontal gyrus in medication-naïve first episode schizophrenia [40]. This previous finding is in line with our current result despite differences in age groups.

Taken together, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically characterize the anatomical and functional substrates of the schizophrenia-spectrum continuum in the GPe and GPi, suggesting that each GP subregion has its own specificity. Genetic and socio-environmental factors influence the onset and chronicity of schizophrenia-spectrum disorders. However, it remains unclear whether and, if so, how these etiological factors differentially impact the volume and FC of each GP subregion in this process. We believe that, in the future, these questions are likely to be addressed through basic research using animal models of the disorder in reverse translational studies. Additionally, extended analysis revealed that no or only a small number of fiber streamlines were identified between any pairs of brain regions showing significant FC findings. This suggests that the observed FC alterations of GP subregions may not be fully explained by direct structural connectivity alone and that additional factors may contribute to these changes. Future studies are warranted to investigate the underlying biological mechanisms.

This study had several limitations. First, the similarity between the results of Study 1 and Study 2 should be carefully interpreted. As above-mentioned, psychotic experiences around the age of 12 increase the risks for developing schizophrenia later in life [10, 43, 44]. However, it is still unclear to what extent psychotic experiences in early adolescence can predict the onset of schizophrenia. Second, the quality of the DBSegment method has not yet been fully validated, as it is still very new. However, its agreement with FreeSurfer, a widely used and reliable tool for subcortical segmentation, is considered acceptable (Fig. 1d, Fig. 3c).

In conclusion, we observed more pronounced enlargement of the GPe compared to the GPi and identified altered FC specific to each of the GPe and GPi in patients with schizophrenia. Additionally, we found associations in early adolescents between the SPE score and the volume of the left GPe, as well as between the presence of SPE and increased FC of the GPe with the lingual gyrus. Future translational studies are needed to further elucidate the mechanisms underlying the specific roles of the GPe and GPi as neurophysiological substrates in the schizophrenia-spectrum continuum.

Methods

Study 1: Schizophrenia cohort study

Subject recruitment and clinical assessment

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee, Graduate School of Medicine, the University of Tokyo (Approval No. 3150). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Patients who met the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV) criteria for schizophrenia were recruited from the in- and outpatient clinics at the Department of Neuropsychiatry, the University of Tokyo Hospital. Healthy controls were recruited from the University of Tokyo and confirmed to have no current or past psychiatric disorders. The screening procedure for healthy controls was the same as that described in our previous study [67]. Experienced psychiatrists or psychologists administered clinical assessments. The exclusion criteria for controls included previous history of brain injury with unconsciousness for five minutes or more. The exclusion criteria for patients included (1) previous history of brain injury with unconsciousness for five minutes or more, (2) previous history of electroconvulsive therapy, (3) previous and present alcohol addiction, and (4) previous and present continuous substance use. For patients with schizophrenia, clinical assessment was performed using the BPRS [68], which includes five subscales (affect, positive symptoms, negative symptoms, resistance, and activation) according to Shafer’s study [69]. Additionally, information on daily doses of antipsychotics was collected at the time of participation.

MRI scanning and image quality check

MRI scanning was performed on a Discovery MR750w 3.0 T (GE Healthcare) using a 24-channel head coil. Each subject underwent an MRI examination, comprising rsfMRI and a T1 three-dimensional (3D) magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo sequence (3D-MPRAGE). T1-weighted structural MRI data were acquired using a spoiled gradient-recalled sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR), 7.7 ms; echo time (TE), 3.1 ms; flip angle, 11 degrees; voxel size, 1.2 × 1 × 1 mm3; field of view (FOV), 235 × 260 × 260 mm3; number of slices, 200. All T1-weighted images were visually checked to exclude the existence of insufficient brain coverage (FOV problem), low signal-to-noise ratios, artifacts, or gross anatomical anomalies that could influence image preprocessing. RsfMRI images were acquired using a gradient echo echo-planar imaging (EPI) pulse sequence for approximately 10 min with the following parameters: number of volumes, 240 plus four dummy scans; TR, 2500 ms; TE, 30 ms; flip angle, 80 degrees; voxel size, 3.3 × 3.3 × 3.2 mm3; slice thickness, 3.2 mm; slice gap: 0.8 mm, FOV, 212 × 212 × 160 mm3; number of slices, 40. During the rsfMRI scan, participants were asked to stay awake, not to focus their thoughts on anything as far as possible, and to keep their eyes on a fixation point at the center of the screen. In addition, B0 fieldmap images were obtained to allow distortion correction of the acquired EPI images with the following parameters: TR, 530 ms; TE short, 4.9 ms; TE long, 7.4 ms. Subjects with gross anatomical anomalies found in T1-weighted images were excluded from the rsfMRI analysis, while potential confounding effects including motion artifacts were removed in the image preprocessing procedures as described later.

MRI data preprocessing

T1-weighted images were preprocessed using the DBSegment software, a rapid deep learning approach to segment 30 deep brain structures from T1-weighted MRI [47, 48]. In DBSegment, the definitions of the GPe and GPi are based on the DISTAL atlas [49]. Accordingly, bilateral GPe and GPi were segmented from our T1-weighted data (Fig. 5a, b, Supplementary Fig. S7a-b). Subsequently, all segmentation images were visually inspected to exclude poorly preprocessed data. Segmentations with clear errors were corrected by reapplying the appropriate segmentation, and the corrected data were included in subsequent analyses. The volume of each of the left and right GP subregions was calculated using fslstats command (https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/fslwiki/Fslutils). Additionally, T1-weighted images were segmented into gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), and cerebrospinal fluids (CSF) using Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM) version 12 (Wellcome Trust Centre for Neuroimaging, London, UK), and ICV was calculated by summing up GM, WM, and CSF volumes. RsfMRI images, with dummy scans removed, were preprocessed using the CONN toolbox [71]. We used the default preprocessing pipeline for volume-based analyses (indirect normalization to MNI-space), including realignment & unwarp & distortion correction using fieldmap, slice-timing correction, identification of outlier scans, indirect segmentation & normalization, and smoothing. Conservative settings (95th percentiles in normative sample) were used to identify outlier scans, and smoothing was conducted with a 6-mm full-width half-maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel. Denoising was conducted with a linear regression of confounding effects, including the first five principal components of WM masks and CSF masks obtained from the anatomical component-based noise correction procedure (aCompCor), realignment parameters, outlier regressors, and a small trend at the very beginning of each scanning session. CONN default temporal band-pass filtering (0.008–0.09 Hz) and linear de-trending were applied. The DISTAL atlas [49] was used to create the GPe/GPi masks in the main analysis, while the CIT168 atlas [50] and the AHEAD atlas [51] were additionally used to account for potential atlas-dependent differences in seed-based FC. In the atlases, voxels with a computed probability of 0.5 or higher were classified as belonging to the region of interest (ROI), while those with a probability of less than 0.5 were classified as non-ROI. The BOLD time series of each voxel, as well as the average BOLD time series within each mask of the left and right GP subregions (ROIs), were processed. A spatial map of Fisher-transformed Pearson correlation coefficients between BOLD timeseries within each ROI (seed) and the BOLD timeseries of each individual voxel, namely a seed-based FC map, was created.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses, except where noted, were performed using SPSS 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) and R version 4.5.1. All significance p values were two-tailed with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. The variance between the groups being compared was examined for homogeneity. When the assumption of equal variance was violated, statistical methods robust to unequal variances (e.g., Welch’s t-test) were applied. It was confirmed that the data met the assumptions of the test. Prior to the main analysis, the demographic characteristics of each diagnostic group were examined using a t-test for continuous data and using a χ2 analysis for categorical data.

For the sMRI analysis, the effect of diagnosis on GP volumes was assessed using a three-way repeated measures ANCOVA test with one between-subject factor (diagnosis) and two within-subject factors (hemisphere and subregion) and with age, sex, and ICV as nuisance covariates. ANCOVA parameter estimation was used to assess the effect of diagnostic groups on each combination of hemisphere and subregion, including partial η² effect sizes. Cohen’s d effect sizes were then calculated from the partial η² sizes and the number of subjects. Next, multiple regression analysis was performed to examine whether, in patients with schizophrenia, each hemispheric and subregional GP volume was explained by the BPRS score (including its subscales, explored in an exploratory manner), controlling for age, sex, and ICV. In addition, we investigated whether it was explained by daily doses of antipsychotics. In supplementary analysis, the DBSegment method for assessing GP subregional volumes was validated by comparing the sum of GPe and GPi volumes calculated with DBSegment to GP volumes calculated with FreeSurfer. This comparison was conducted by examining Pearson correlation coefficients for each side in all subjects. It should be noted that FreeSurfer-segmented GP includes not only GPe and GPi but also the medial medullary lamina and possibly the ventral GP. It was hypothesized that the agreement between the two analysis methods does not differ significantly between patients and healthy subjects, as it depends on technical issues. Therefore, all subjects were included together in the analysis. Sensitivity analyses were planned in which cases with segmentation failures were excluded to assess the robustness of the significant findings. A priori power analysis focused on the main effect of diagnosis, which represents group differences in GP subregional volumes. Assuming a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.6), a total sample size of approximately 90 participants (45 per group) provides 80% power at a two-sided α level of 0.05, calculated using G*Power version 3.1.9.6 [72].

For the rsfMRI analysis, using CONN general linear model analysis, the between-diagnosis differences in FC maps seeded in each of the bilateral GPe/GPi masks were investigated, controlling for age and sex. Next, the association of each hemispheric and subregional GP-seeded FC map with the BPRS score (including its subscales, explored in an exploratory manner) was explored in patients with schizophrenia. In addition, its association with daily doses of antipsychotics was also explored. In CONN general linear model analyses, multiple comparisons were performed at the cluster level (cluster-level FDR-corrected p < 0.05, voxel-level uncorrected p < 0.001). In cases of discordant spatial patterns of GPe/GPi-seeded FC results across atlases, a more liberal significance threshold (cluster-level FDR-corrected p < 0.05, voxel-level uncorrected p < 0.005) was also applied for exploring the discordance in more detail. A priori power analysis for voxelwise analyses is not straightforward because their statistical power depends on spatially varying effect sizes and cluster-level inference. Accordingly, power analysis was conducted for the main effect of diagnosis on GP subregional FC, conceptualized as overall group differences in FC strength. Assuming a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.6), a total sample size of approximately 90 participants (45 per group) provides 80% power at a two-sided α level of 0.05, calculated using G*Power version 3.1.9.6 [72].

Extended analysis

While it remains unknown whether volumetric alterations and FC alterations of GP subregions are associated, we hypothesized that structural connectivity may underlie this relationship. Therefore, fiber connections between pairs of brain regions showing significant FC findings were examined using tractography-ready diffusion MRI datasets derived from the HCP Young Adult Cohort (https://brain.labsolver.org/mds/data-hcp/lifespan.html) and DSI Studio Hou version (http://dsi-studio.labsolver.org) [73].

Study 2: Adolescent cohort study

Subject recruitment

All protocols of this study were approved by the research ethics committees of the Graduate School of Medicine and Faculty of Medicine at the University of Tokyo (Approval Nos. 3150, 10057, and 10069), Tokyo Metropolitan Institute of Medical Science (Approval No. 12–35), and the Graduate University for Advanced Studies (SOKENDAI) (Approval No. 2012002). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Prior to participation, written informed assent was obtained from participants and written informed consent was obtained from their primary caregivers. The Tokyo Teen Cohort study (TTC) is a large-scale, prospective, population-based birth cohort study conducted in the Tokyo metropolitan area, with 3171 early adolescents participating [74]. The aim of the TTC was to investigate physiological and psychological development. The population-neuroscience study of the TTC (pn-TTC) is a longitudinal study designed to explore the neurobiological substrates of development during adolescence [75]. The target sample size of the pn-TTC study was around 300. Participants of the pn-TTC study were subsampled from the participant group of the TTC study. Specifically, TTC study subjects showing interest in the pn-TTC study were regarded as candidate participants, and some of them were enrolled in the pn-TTC study approximately one year after their participation in the TTC study. The basic attributes acquired in the TTC study were compared between participants and non-participants of the pn-TTC study to confirm there was no significant sampling bias in the pn-TTC subsample. No significant differences were found in adolescents’ age, sex, socioeconomic status, and intelligence quotient (p > 0.20). Thus, the pn-TTC subsample was regarded as being demographically and socioeconomically representative of the original TTC study population. This study was performed as part of the pn-TTC study.

At their first visit, participants entered a mock scanner to acclimatize to the MRI scanner environment and practice lying still during the scan. At their second visit, participants underwent MRI scanning. The following were the exclusion criteria for participation: (a) evident psychiatric or neurological disorders (e.g., autism spectrum disorder, attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder, Down syndrome, or epilepsy); (b) visual or auditory impairments (except for myopia); (c) endocrinological diseases, or diseases that might influence hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis function (e.g., diabetes mellitus, thyroid disease, or renal dysfunction), gonadotropic dysfunction, or adrenal dysfunction; (d) recent or long-term use of drugs that might affect the central nervous system (e.g., steroid hormones and antihistamines); (e) history of head trauma with loss of consciousness for five minutes or more; (f) metal implants or objects in the body (except titanium); and (g) unrest during the MRI practice session at the first visit.

Psychological evaluation

Psychological evaluation was performed at the time of the participation in the TTC study. The 7-item Adolescent Psychotic-like Symptom Screener (APSS) [76] is a self-administered questionnaire for screening SPEs in adolescents. Three response options—‘Yes, definitely,’ ‘Maybe,’ and ‘No, never’—are possible, scored as 1 point, 0.5 points, and 0 points, respectively. A total score, defined as the SPE score, is calculated by summing the scores for all items, ranging from 0 to 7. A total SPE score of 2 or more is considered SPE-positive, while a score below 2 is considered SPE-negative. The reliability of this cutoff point was validated by a previous study [76], and the APSS has been used in some previous neuroimaging studies. Additionally, the SPE score was used as a continuous variable in this study, as it represents the level of psychotic symptoms. It should be noted that the SPE scores are non-normally distributed [76].

The Japanese version of the SDQ [77] is a common parent-rated instrument for assessing psychopathology in children. The SDQ includes four problem factors: emotional symptoms, conduct problems, hyperactivity/inattention, and peer problems. Scores for each SDQ factor range 0 to 10. The higher the SDQ factor score is, the greater the child’s difficulty is. The SDQ TD score is calculated by summing four problem scales. Participants had already completed the SDQ at the time of participation in the TTC study. The SDQ TD score was used as a reference variable in this study, as there is an association between the SDQ TD score and psychotic symptoms in the adolescent population [45].

MRI scanning and image quality check

MRI scanning was performed on a Philips Achieva 3.0 T system (Philips Medical Systems, Best, The Netherlands) using an eight-channel receive head coil. Each subject underwent an MRI examination, comprising fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), rsfMRI, a T1 3D-MPRAGE, and MR angiography (MRA) sequences. Sagittal T1-weighted images were acquired with the following parameters: TR, 7.0 ms; TE, 3.2 ms; flip angle, 9 degrees; voxel size, 1 × 1 × 1 mm3; slice thickness, 1 mm; FOV, 200 × 256 × 256 mm3; number of slices, 200. All T1-weighted images were checked by visual inspection to exclude the existence of insufficient brain coverage (FOV problem), low signal-to-noise ratios, any artifacts (e.g., motion artifacts and magnetic susceptibility artifacts), or any abnormal organic anomalies identified in either T1-weighted, FLAIR, or MRA images (e.g., large arachnoid cysts, large cavum septum pellucidum, and large arterial aneurysms). The rsfMRI data were acquired using a gradient-echo EPI sequence for approximately 11 min with the following parameters: number of volumes, 250; TR, 2500 ms; TE, 30 ms; flip angle, 80 degrees; voxel size, 3.31 × 3.31 × 3.2 mm3; slice thickness, 3.2 mm; slice gap, 0.8 mm; FOV, 212 × 212 × 160 mm3; number of slices, 40. Participants were asked to stay awake, not to focus their thoughts on anything as far as possible, and to keep their eyes on a fixation point at the center of the screen during rsfMRI scanning. In addition, B0 fieldmap images were obtained to allow distortion correction of the acquired EPI images with the following parameters: TR, 488 ms; TE short, 4.9 ms; TE long, 7.4 ms. Subjects with gross anatomical anomalies found in T1-weighted images were excluded from the rsfMRI analysis, while potential confounding effects including motion artifacts were removed in the image preprocessing procedures as described later.

MRI data preprocessing

T1-weighted images were preprocessed using the DBSegment software, a rapid deep learning approach to segment 30 deep brain structures from T1-weighted MRI [47, 48]. In DBSegment, the definitions of the GPe and GPi are based on the DISTAL atlas [49]. Thus, bilateral GPe and GPi were segmented from our T1-weighted data (Fig. 5c, d, Supplementary Fig. S7c-d). Subsequently, all segmentation images were visually inspected to exclude poorly preprocessed data. Segmentations with clear errors were corrected by reapplying the appropriate segmentation, and the corrected data were included in subsequent analyses. The volume of each of the left and right GP subregions was calculated using fslstats command (https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/fslwiki/Fslutils). Additionally, T1-weighted images were segmented into GM, WM, and CSF using SPM version 12 (Wellcome Trust Centre for Neuroimaging, London, UK), and ICV was calculated by summing up GM, WM, and CSF volumes. For validation analysis, T1-weighted images were preprocessed using the FreeSurfer software suite version 5.3 (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu) [70].

RsfMRI images were preprocessed using the CONN toolbox [71]. We used the default preprocessing pipeline for volume-based analyses (indirect normalization to MNI-space), including realignment & unwarp & distortion correction using fieldmap, slice-timing correction, identification of outlier scans, indirect segmentation & normalization, and smoothing. Liberal settings (99th percentiles in normative sample) were employed to identify outlier scans as early adolescents are more likely than adults to experience in-scanner motion. Smoothing was conducted with a 6-mm FWHM Gaussian kernel. Denoising was conducted with a linear regression of confounding effects, including the first five principal components of WM masks and CSF masks obtained from the aCompCor, realignment parameters, outlier regressors, and a small trend at the very beginning of each scanning session. CONN default temporal band-pass filtering (0.008–0.09 Hz) and linear de-trending were applied. The DISTAL atlas [49] was used to create the GPe/GPi masks in the main analysis, while the CIT168 atlas [50] and the AHEAD atlas [51] were additionally used. In the atlases, voxels with a computed probability of 0.5 or higher were classified as belonging to the ROI, while those with a probability of less than 0.5 were classified as non-ROI. The BOLD time series of each voxel, as well as the average BOLD time series within each mask of the left and right GP subregions (ROIs), were processed. A spatial map of Fisher-transformed Pearson correlation coefficients between BOLD timeseries within each ROI (seed) and the BOLD timeseries of each individual voxel, namely a seed-based FC map, was created.

Subject inclusion and exclusion

The pn-TTC study is a birth cohort study in which the number of registered adolescents was fixed. Thus, the status of subject inclusion and exclusion in this study is described below.

Of the 301 early adolescents who participated in the first wave of the pn-TTC study, 272 [144 boys and 128 girls, mean age ± SD = 11.5 ± 0.7 years] underwent T1-weighted MRI scanning. However, 63 subjects (36 boys and 27 girls) were excluded after the identification of inadequate images in the quality control. Additionally, five adolescents (two boys and three girls) were excluded due to data missing in the SPE score. Finally, a total of 204 adolescents (106 boys and 98 girls, mean age ± SD = 11.6 ± 0.7 years) were included in the sMRI analysis. The demographic data for Study IIa are summarized in Table 1c. The mean interval duration between the participation in the TTC study and the MRI scanning was 17 months (ranging from 7 to 39 months).

Of the 301 early adolescents who participated in the first wave of the pn-TTC study, 257 (136 boys and 121 girls, mean age ± SD = 11.6 ± 0.7 years) underwent rsfMRI scanning. However, two subjects (one boy and one girl) who did not complete the T1-weighted imaging scanning needed in rsfMRI analysis and 16 subjects (six boys and 10 girls) who did not complete the fieldmap scanning needed in rsfMRI analysis were excluded. Additionally, twenty-three subjects (14 boys and 9 girls) with abnormal organic findings and five subjects (two boys and three girls) with missing SPE scores were excluded. Finally, a total of 211 early adolescents (113 boys and 98 girls, mean age ± SD = 11.6 ± 0.7 years) were analyzed in the rsfMRI analysis. The demographic data for Study IIb are summarized in Table 1d. The mean interval duration between the participation in the TTC study and the MRI scanning was 17 months (ranging from 7 to 39 months).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses, except where noted, were performed using SPSS 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) and R version 4.5.1. All significance p values, except where noted, were two-tailed with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. The variance between the groups being compared was examined for homogeneity. When the assumption of equal variance was violated, statistical methods robust to unequal variances (e.g., Welch’s t-test) were applied. It was confirmed that the data met the assumptions of the test. Prior to the main analysis, the demographic characteristics of the SPE-negative and SPE-positive groups (i.e., the SPE-groups) were compared using a t-test for continuous data and using a χ2 analysis for categorical data. Since the time interval between participation in the TTC study and the MRI scanning was not short (mean: 17 months), it was necessary to ensure that the age effects on the psychological evaluation results obtained in the TTC study were negligible. Thus, it was investigated using Spearman’s test whether the SDQ TD score and SPE score were associated with the age at the time of participation in the TTC study.

For the sMRI analysis, the effect of diagnosis on GP volumes was assessed using a three-way repeated measures ANCOVA test with one between-subject factor (SPE-group) and two within-subject factors (hemisphere and subregion) and with age, sex, ICV, and the SDQ TD score as nuisance covariates. ANCOVA parameter estimation was used to assess the effect of the SPE-group on each combination of hemisphere and subregion, including partial η² effect sizes. Cohen’s d effect sizes were then calculated from the partial η² sizes and the number of subjects. Additionally, non-parametric correlational coefficients between the SPE score and each hemispheric and subregional GP volume in all subjects were investigated controlling for age, sex, ICV, and the SDQ TD score. In our previous study, SPE-positive adolescents demonstrated a marginally significant increase in the left GP volume compared to SPE-negative adolescents [8]. Therefore, we hypothesized positive correlations between the SPE score and subregional volumes of the left GP, and these correlations were analyzed using one-tailed tests with FDR multiple-comparison correction. Additionally, in an exploratory analysis, associations between the SPE score and subregional volumes of the right GP were examined using FDR multiple-comparison correction. In supplementary analysis, the DBSegment method for assessing GP subregional volumes was validated by comparing the sum of GPe and GPi volumes calculated with DBSegment to GP volumes calculated with FreeSurfer. This comparison was conducted by examining Pearson correlation coefficients for each side in all subjects. It should be noted that FreeSurfer-segmented GP includes not only GPe and GPi but also the medial medullary lamina and possibly the ventral GP. Sensitivity analyses were planned in which cases with segmentation failures were excluded to assess the robustness of the significant findings. A priori power analysis focused on the main effect of the SPE-group, which represents group differences in GP subregional volumes. Considering that the ratio of SPE-negative to SPE-positive early adolescents is approximately 2:1 [76], a total sample of 102 participants (68 and 34) provides 80% power to detect a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.6) at a two-sided α level of 0.05, calculated using G*Power version 3.1.9.6 [72].

For the rsfMRI analysis, using CONN general linear model analysis, the between-group differences in FC maps seeded in each of the bilateral GPe/GPi masks were investigated, controlling for age, sex, and the SDQ TD score. Additionally, associations in all subjects between the SPE score and such FC maps were investigated controlling for age, sex, and the SDQ TD score. In CONN general linear model analyses, multiple comparisons were performed at the cluster level (cluster-level FDR-corrected p < 0.05, voxel-level uncorrected p < 0.001). In cases of discordant spatial patterns of GPe/GPi-seeded FC results across atlases, a more liberal significance threshold (cluster-level FDR-corrected p < 0.05, voxel-level uncorrected p < 0.005) was also applied for exploring the discordance in more detail. A priori power analysis for voxelwise analyses is not straightforward because statistical power depends on spatially varying effect sizes and cluster-level inference. Accordingly, we conducted power analysis for the main effect of the SPE-group on GP subregional FC, conceptualized as overall group differences in FC strength. Considering that the ratio of SPE-negative to SPE-positive early adolescents is approximately 2:1 [76], a total sample of 102 participants (68 and 34) provides 80% power to detect a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.6) at a two-sided α level of 0.05, calculated using G*Power version 3.1.9.6 [72].

Extended analysis

While it remains unknown whether volumetric alterations and FC alterations of GP subregions are associated, we hypothesized that structural connectivity may underlie this relationship. Therefore, fiber connections between pairs of brain regions showing significant FC findings were examined using tractography-ready diffusion MRI datasets derived from the HCP Young Adult Cohort and DSI Studio software [73].
