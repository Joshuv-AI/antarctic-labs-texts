Thoracic outlet syndrome in patients with clavicle fracture: Case report and national analysis
Authors: Randall R Rainwater, William Griffin, Tera A Scott, Regis L Renard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThoracic outlet syndrome (TOS) is a neurovascular condition caused by compression of the brachial plexus or subclavian vessels as they pass through the thoracic outlet. Since the clavicle border the thoracic outlet, injury to the clavicle and potentially post-traumatic callus formation, malunion, or scarring may precipitate compression and TOS. This study aims to determine the incidence and key predictors of TOS after clavicle fracture.MethodsWe present a representative case description illustrating the clinical course and diagnostic considerations of post-traumatic TOS. We also conducted a large-scale retrospective analysis using a national claims database to identify patients with clavicle fracture and evaluate the incidence and factors of TOS development.ResultsA 53-year-old woman developed TOS 10 months after undergoing surgical fixation of a displaced mid diaphyseal (Allman Tyle 1) clavicle fracture sustained in a motor vehicle accident. She presented with swelling and discoloration of the left upper extremity, where computed tomographic contrast imaging demonstrated five centimeter left subclavian vein thrombosis. The patient underwent hardware removal and first-rib resection, which resulted in complete resolution of symptoms. On database analysis, TOS most frequently occurs in middle-aged adults (40–64 years) and is more common following displaced clavicle fractures fixed with open reduction and internal fixation, as observed in this case. Comorbid conditions such as hypercoagulable states, obesity, and tobacco use along with displaced fracture pattern and congenital factors—including cervical ribs or cervical scoliosis—may further elevate risk.ConclusionsThoracic outlet syndrome should be considered in patients with history of recent clavicle fracture that develop symptoms such as paresthesia, swelling, or chronic pain of the ipsilateral upper extremity.Keywords: Thoracic outlet syndrome, Clavicle fracture, Neurovascular compression, Fracture displacement, Malunion, Subclavian vein thrombosis

Introduction

Thoracic outlet syndrome (TOS) is a condition characterized by compression of the neurovascular structures traversing the thoracic outlet, including the subclavian artery, subclavian vein, and brachial plexus. These structures pass through a confined space bordered by the clavicle and the first rib with a confined space of 12.6 ± 2.7 mm that decreases with shoulder abduction to 6.3 ± 3.3 mm and the vein diameter decreased from 16.1 ± 3.0 mm to 7.4 ± 2.6 mm with the arm abducted [1]. Compression of the brachial plexus represents the most common form of TOS, followed by venous and, less frequently, arterial involvement [2], [3]. TOS is often attributed to congenital abnormalities including anomalous fibromuscular bands, cervical rib and the anterior scalene musculature that reduce the thoracic outlet space [4]; trauma to the clavicle is an additional but less frequently recognized cause of TOS [5]. Several case reports have described TOS secondary to malunion of displaced clavicle fractures, where altered anatomy leads to narrowing of the thoracic outlet [6], [7], [8]. More recent reports have identified excessive callus formation, long screws, or prominent fixation plates as potential contributors to posttraumatic TOS [9], [10].

Although clavicle fractures are very common, the subset of patients who potentially develop TOS remains unknown and might be clinically meaningful [11]. Current literature inadequately defines which patient factors or fracture patterns might predispose to TOS. Existing evidence is primarily derived from isolated case studies, which limits broader understanding of incidence and risk [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]. The true frequency of post-fracture TOS is largely unknown, and individual institutions rarely see enough cases to perform meaningful statistical analyses. Moreover, follow-up across long periods, which is often necessary for symptoms to develop, can be inconsistent within single-center cohorts.

The present study employs a large, database-driven approach to identify patients with prior clavicle fractures and evaluate subsequent TOS incidence. In addition to this population-level analysis, we also present a detailed institutional case study that illustrates the clinical course, diagnostic challenges, and anatomic factors contributing to posttraumatic TOS. Together, these complementary perspectives address a critical gap in the literature and may improve recognition of posttraumatic TOS after clavicle fracture, enhancing clinical decision-making regarding differential diagnosis and surveillance strategies. By quantifying the incidence and determinants of TOS following clavicle fracture and contextualizing these findings with a case from our institution, this study aims to identify patient populations most susceptible to neurovascular complications. These insights may help refine postoperative surveillance, inform surgical technique, and ultimately reduce long-term morbidity associated with post-traumatic thoracic outlet compression.

Case report

A 53-year-old woman presented to our institution with left shoulder pain and swelling 11 months following open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) of a displaced mid-diaphyseal (Allman Type 1) left clavicle fracture sustained in a motor vehicle accident. She also sustained fifth and sixth right rib fractures with no other recorded injuries. ORIF was performed approximately two weeks after the initial injury using AO compression-style plate fixation by an outside facility. Her medical history was notable for a 12.5 pack-year smoking history and BMI of 28, without a significant coagulation or vasculopathic history. Review of the medical record demonstrated no prior anticoagulation use aside from baby aspirin and no history of stroke or thromboembolic disease.

Postoperative recovery was initially uncomplicated with anticipated fracture healing. Approximately 10 months after ORIF, the patient developed clavicular tenderness, ipsilateral upper extremity swelling, heaviness, and dark discoloration of the left upper extremity concerning for thoracic outlet syndrome (TOS). Duplex ultrasonography and CT venography demonstrated a five-centimeter thrombus within the left subclavian vein, establishing the diagnosis of venous TOS in conjunction with her clinical symptoms. The patient subsequently underwent hardware removal and first-rib resection via transaxillary approach. The indication for decompression was to relieve compression of the subclavian vessels and adjacent neural structures. Anticoagulation alone was not sufficient due to presence of large thrombus and 3-dimensional reconstruction showing severe impingement within the thoracic outlet. Intraoperatively, scar tissue surrounding the subclavian vein was excised. Fig. 1 demonstrates thoracic outlet impingement at presentation with venous thrombosis (Fig. 1A–C) and the unobstructed thoracic outlet following hardware removal and first-rib resection (Fig. 1D–F). Following plate removal and rib resection, the patient was started on apixaban 5 mg twice daily along with physical therapy. At four-month follow-up, the patient demonstrated complete clinical resolution of symptoms without recurrent thrombosis or residual paresthesia and was transitioned to aspirin for thromboprophylaxis.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study utilized the PearlDiver Mariner170 database (PearlDiver Technologies, Colorado Springs, CO) to identify patients with a diagnosis of clavicle fracture, (ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes; Supplemental File 1). Patients were stratified based on the diagnosis of thoracic outlet syndrome within 2 years after clavicle fracture. All included patients were required to have continuous medical record activity for a minimum of 2 years following clavicle fracture. Demographic and clinical variables collected included age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), geographic region, insurance type (commercial or Medicare), and comorbidities. All data was de-identified and compliant with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), and institutional review board (IRB) approval was exempt.

Patients were stratified into age groups (pediatric: <18 years, young adult: 18–39 years, older adult: 40–64 years, elderly: ≥65 years). The incidence of TOS within two years of clavicle fracture was calculated for each age group, and odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were determined relative to the elderly cohort. TOS subtype (neurogenic, venous, or arterial) was identified using diagnosis codes and analyzed across age groups to evaluate differences in distribution (Supplemental File 1).

Patients with clavicle fractures were stratified by fracture pattern (displaced vs. nondisplaced) and treatment modality (nonoperative, closed reduction, ORIF) using ICD-9/ICD-10 and CPT codes (Supplemental File 1). Patients were assigned to mutually exclusive groups based on the highest level of intervention received during the acute post-fracture period (ORIF > closed reduction > nonoperative). Within each fracture subgroup, the 2-year incidence of thoracic outlet syndrome (TOS) was calculated by treatment type. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were generated using nonoperative management as the reference, with additional pairwise comparisons performed between closed reduction and ORIF.

A multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of thoracic outlet syndrome (TOS) within two years of clavicle fracture. Variables included in the model were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and included demographic factors, comorbidities, fracture characteristics (displacement, malunion), and treatment modality (closed reduction, ORIF). All covariates were entered simultaneously into the model. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to assess the independent association of each variable with TOS development.

Comparisons between groups were performed using Chi-square tests for categorical variables and Student's t-tests for continuous variables of age and CCI, and logistic regression for multivariate analysis of independent predictors of TOS. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, and odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.

Results

Within the national database, approximately 700,000 patients were identified with a clavicle fracture. Of these, 6316 (0.90%) patients were diagnosed with thoracic outlet syndrome (TOS) within two years of fracture, while 693,096 did not develop TOS (Table 1). The TOS cohort was significantly older, with a mean age of 54.6 years compared to 36.1 years in the non-TOS group (p < 0.0001). Age distribution analysis demonstrated that adults aged 40 years and older comprised the majority of TOS cases (76%). Although pediatric patients accounted for the largest proportion of clavicle fractures overall, they represented only a small fraction of TOS cases. There was no significant difference in sex distribution between cohorts (female: 41.7% vs. 41.1%; p = 0.37). However, patients who developed TOS demonstrated a significantly greater comorbidity burden, with a higher mean Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) (2.17 vs. 0.80; p < 0.0001). Rates of obesity (29.3% vs. 16.6%), tobacco use (53.2% vs. 27.8%), and diabetes (32.1% vs. 15.3%) were all significantly higher among TOS patients (all p < 0.0001). Additionally, cardiovascular and vascular comorbidities—including atherosclerosis, cerebrovascular disease, and peripheral vascular disease—were markedly more prevalent in the TOS cohort, with peripheral vascular disease demonstrating the strongest association (OR 5.88, p < 0.0001). A prior history of venous thromboembolism was also significantly more common among TOS patients (7.0% vs. 1.1%; OR 7.14, p < 0.0001). We analyzed the interval between clavicle fracture and development of thoracic outlet syndrome (TOS). Across all fractures, the mean time to TOS diagnosis was 293 days (median 153 days; IQR 29–448 days). Among displaced fractures, the mean time was 281 days (median 206 days; IQR 39–474 days). Among non-displaced fractures, the mean time was also 287 days (median 209 days; IQR 49–477 days). Fracture displacement was not associated with a significant difference in time to TOS development (p = 0.66).

When stratified by age, pediatric patients had a markedly lower risk of developing TOS compared to elderly patients (OR 0.09), while young adults also demonstrated reduced risk (OR 0.71) (Table 2). In contrast, older adults had a slightly increased risk relative to the elderly cohort (OR 1.11). The type of thoracic outlet syndrome varied significantly across age groups. Neurogenic TOS predominated in pediatric (71.5%) and young adult (57.8%) populations, whereas arterial TOS became increasingly common with age, accounting for 23.8% of cases in older adults and 52.9% in elderly patients. Venous TOS remained less frequent across all age groups.

Fracture management strategy was significantly associated with TOS development (Table 3). Across all fracture patterns, operative fixation was associated with the highest incidence of TOS. Patients undergoing open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) had a significantly increased risk compared to nonoperative management (1.32% vs. 0.87%; OR 1.54 [1.42–1.67], p < 0.0001), while closed reduction demonstrated a smaller but still significant increase in risk (0.96%; OR 1.12 [1.03–1.21], p = 0.01). Direct comparison revealed higher odds of TOS following ORIF compared to closed reduction (OR 1.38 [1.23–1.54], p < 0.0001). When stratified by fracture displacement, important differences emerged. Among displaced fractures, both closed reduction and ORIF were associated with increased odds of TOS relative to surveillance (closed: OR 1.37, p = 0.001; ORIF: OR 1.30, p = 0.017), with no significant difference between operative strategies (p = 0.79). In contrast, among nondisplaced fractures, ORIF was associated with a markedly higher risk of TOS (1.40%; OR 2.50 [1.82–3.55], p < 0.0001), exceeding that observed with closed reduction (0.73%; OR 1.32 [1.02–1.72], p = 0.042) when compared to surveillance. Direct comparison demonstrated significantly lower odds of TOS with closed reduction compared to ORIF in nondisplaced fractures (OR 0.52 [0.35–0.78], p = 0.001).

Multivariate analysis identified several independent predictors of TOS following clavicle fracture (Table 4). The strongest positive predictors included peripheral vascular disease (OR 6.91), presence of a cervical rib (OR 4.18), and prior venous thromboembolism (OR 3.42) (all p < 0.0001). Additional significant predictors included clavicle fracture malunion (OR 2.27), cervical scoliosis (OR 2.08), kyphosis (OR 1.75), and cerebrovascular disease (OR 1.55). A personal history of healed traumatic fracture and multiple cardiometabolic comorbidities—including coronary atherosclerosis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, hypertension, tobacco use, and obesity—were also independently associated with increased risk (all p < 0.0001). Open reduction and internal fixation remained independent predictors of TOS (OR 1.11, p = 0.02), whereas closed reduction, age, and several metabolic variables were not independently associated with TOS development.

Discussion

In this large, database-driven study, we examined the incidence and risk factors for thoracic outlet syndrome following clavicle fracture, contextualized by a representative case at our institution. Our patient, a 53-year-old woman, developed venous TOS 10 months after ORIF of a displaced clavicle fracture requiring symptomatic hardware removal and first-rib resection. Within four months of hardware removal and resection, our patient made a full clinical recovery. This case highlights the clinical relevance of posttraumatic TOS and illustrates how prior clavicle fracture and individual anatomy can precipitate symptomatic neurovascular compression. Consistent with this case, our database analysis demonstrated that TOS patients were older, had higher comorbidity burden, and exhibited increased prevalence of comorbidities, but sex was not associated with risk.

Clavicle fracture prevalence has a bimodal distribution in young active adults and older adults [12], [13]. As seen with our patient and national data, TOS incidence is highest in patients aged 40 to 64 years old. The higher incidence of TOS observed in aging patients with greater comorbidity burden likely reflects the contribution of pre-existing degenerative changes, altered posture, and chronic scapulothoracic dysfunction. In this setting, underlying thoracic outlet narrowing or subclinical anatomic predisposition may be unmasked by traumatic injury or age-related changes in shoulder girdle mechanics. As seen in our patient, older adults are more prone to excessive callus formation, granulation tissue, and hypertrophic nonunion after fractures [14]. In older patients with decreased bone quality, the risk of secondary displacement of fracture segment may also impinge the thoracic outlet leading to delayed onset of symptoms [15]. Our patient did have a high energy injury mechanism causing clavicle fracture which is a significant contributor to TOS, along with concomitant upper extremity and thoracic injuries as seen in some reports [16], [17]. The type of TOS is an important consideration, as pediatric patients typically develop neurogenic TOS, whereas older adults have a higher prevalence of vascular TOS [2]. Furthermore, given that the middle 50% of patients develop TOS between approximately 1 and 15 months following clavicle fracture, clinical follow-up should extend through this interval to ensure delayed onset cases are not missed.

Overall, TOS following clavicle fracture differed by treatment strategy and fracture displacement (Table 3) supporting that abnormal post-fracture anatomy and mechanical narrowing of the thoracic outlet contributes to symptomatic neurovascular compression. The clavicle's proximity to the first rib and the neurovascular bundle makes it a critical site for potential compression when fracture healing is distorted [15]. Fracture displacement, comminution, and shortening are well-recognized indications for operative management to improve fixation stability, cosmesis, and functional outcomes [18], [19], [20], [21], but these same fracture characteristics and surgical interventions may also predispose patients to progressive anatomic distortion. Introducing hardware for surgical fixation inevitably alters native anatomy and the geometry of the thoracic outlet since plate and screw placement can directly endanger the underlying subclavian vessels and brachial plexus, especially when screws are excessively long or positioned medially [22]. Cadaveric and anatomical studies show that the medial screw holes are closest to neurovascular structures, and that patient positioning during fixation can further influence this risk [23], [24], [25]. In addition, surgical intervention and the initial trauma may promote local scarring and fibrosis, further narrowing the thoracic outlet over time [26], [27]. While greater degrees of displacement and surgical intervention may correlate with both the severity of outlet compression and the timing at which TOS becomes clinically apparent, careful attention to postoperative clinical course and early symptom recognition remains essential for optimizing management strategies.

Since diagnosis of TOS relies on clinical suspicion, the timing of symptom onset is an important consideration in post-traumatic TOS. In both our national cohort study and our patient case, TOS developed nearly a year after the index clavicle fracture, emphasizing that neurovascular compromise may evolve gradually as callus formation, malunion, or hardware position alters the thoracic outlet dimensions over time. Case reports and small series similarly describe a delayed presentation, with most patients becoming symptomatic within the first year, though later presentations have been documented when progressive deformity or remodeling narrows the costoclavicular space [15], [28], [29]. While plate fixation is typically left in place unless symptomatic, prominent hardware or plate proximity to the subclavian vein may contribute to venous compression or venous trauma, like our patient developing subclavian deep vein thrombosis [9]. Thus, hardware removal should be considered when symptoms emerge or when postoperative imaging suggests encroachment on the thoracic outlet. Surgical management of post-traumatic TOS is tailored to the underlying mechanism and may include hardware removal, first-rib resection, venolysis, or decompression of scar tissue and callus [30]. In cases with significant fracture malunion, osteotomy and revision fixation may be necessary to restore clavicular length and alignment. Restoring clavicular anatomy and stability is crucial to prevent recurrence of TOS symptoms [27]. Together, these considerations underscore the importance of long-term surveillance and individualized management in patients with clavicle fractures at risk for delayed TOS.

This study has several limitations. The case report findings are limited to a single patient at our institution which produces inherent limitations to generatability of the case. Analysis is based on a large administrative database, which may be subject to coding inaccuracies or missing clinical details, such as fracture displacement severity, exact surgical technique, or precise timing of TOS onset. While multivariate analysis identified age, displaced fracture, ORIF, and structural spine abnormalities as significant predictors, other factors such as soft tissue injury, patient activity level, and post-operative rehabilitation were not available. Capturing of concomitant upper extremity injury and injury mechanism is limited and strictly correlative within the national database. The impact of surgical repair requires further clarification. Certain patient populations may confound results; excluding patients with cervical rib, scapular, or other upper extremity injuries in future analyses could improve specificity. The relatively low prevalence of TOS in pediatric patients limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions about risk in younger populations.

Future research should focus on prospective studies that capture fracture characteristics, surgical details, and post-fracture functional outcomes in patients who develop TOS. Incorporating imaging, rehabilitation data, and detailed follow-up will allow more precise identification of patients at highest risk for TOS and could inform guidelines for surveillance, early intervention, and surgical decision-making. Additionally, age-specific differences in TOS subtype should be considered in both research and clinical practice, as younger patients more commonly develop neurogenic TOS, whereas older patients demonstrate a higher prevalence of arterial and vascular forms. Clinicians evaluating patients after clavicle fracture with upper extremity pain, swelling, or neurovascular compromise should maintain a high index of suspicion for posttraumatic TOS, particularly in patients with displaced fractures and those treated with open reduction and internal fixation, while tailoring evaluation to the TOS subtype most likely for the patient's age group.
