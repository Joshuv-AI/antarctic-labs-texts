Aging and cancer: current understandings and future perspectives
Authors: Qiucheng Li, Yau-Tuen Chan, Junyu Wu, Zhuofeng Jiang, Pengde Lu, Lin Xu, Zixin Feng, Hongchao Yuan, Yibin Feng, Ning Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Deciphering the mechanistic links between aging and cancer remains a central challenge in biomedical research. This review begins by presenting recent epidemiological data on cancer incidence across age groups, underscoring the complex and paradoxical interplay between aging and cancer. A systematic analysis follows four core signaling pathways that underpin both aging and cancer: (i) nutrient sensing and energy metabolism pathways, (ii) stress response and genome maintenance pathways, (iii) inflammatory and microenvironmental pathways, and (iv) sirtuin-mediated epigenetic and transcriptional modulation pathways. Each pathway is analyzed regarding its distinct and overlapping roles in aging and cancer, with exploration of strategies for comodulating these shared mechanisms to simultaneously mitigate both conditions. Established and emerging druggable targets within these pathways are highlighted, offering new perspectives for future therapeutic development. Current integrative strategies to achieve concurrent antiaging and anticancer effects include targeting cellular nutrient sensing and metabolism, enhancing genomic integrity, suppressing chronic inflammation, clearing senescent cells, and modulating epigenetic programs. Finally, the impact of aging on cancer therapy, particularly immunotherapy, is discussed, along with a review of representative aging-related pathways and genes and their roles in modulating immunotherapy efficacy. Analysis of agents targeting each pathway spanning preclinical studies, clinical trials, and clinically approved drugs reveals disparities in the research progress between anti-aging and anticancer applications. Consequently, a promising future strategy for drug discovery lies in repurposing well-established anticancer agents for antiaging interventions, as well as translating insights from aging research into novel antitumor therapies, thereby achieving synergistic benefits in both fields.

Introduction

Currently, the worldwide life expectancy of human beings is increasing with the development of life science and self-health consciousness. Meanwhile, the population growth rate has been continuously declining in recent decades. According to the statistical estimation of the World Health Organization, adults aged 60 years or older in the world will double to 2.1 billion by 2050, accounting for ~22% of the total population. A great challenge that the aging population brings about is the disease burden. Cancer, the worldwide primary cause of death, led to the deaths of 9.7 million people in 2022 globally.1 As the incidence of cancer is closely correlated with age in most cases,2 it is publicly considered a common disease of aging. Adults aged 65 years and older are estimated to make up 70% of all cancer cases by 2030.3 According to the published statistics updated by the American Cancer Society,2 the cancer morbidity and mortality of both males and females in 2025 are estimated to increase dramatically with aging (Fig. 1a, b). A steep elevation in the probability of developing cancer or dying from cancer was observed upon aging no less than 65 years. However, compared to people within the age range of 65–84 years, people aged no less than 85 years (85+) showed a relatively decreased probability of cancer development or death (Fig. 1c, d). In terms of the probability of developing a specific cancer in both sexes, breast, prostate, lung and bronchus, colorectum, and uterine corpus cancer ranked as the top five cancer types, while in terms of the probability of dying of a specific cancer in both sexes, lung and bronchus, breast, prostate, colorectum, and pancreas cancer ranked as the top five cancer types (Fig. 1e, f). For the vast majority of cancer types (n = 18 out of 23), the probability of developing cancer gradually increased with age from 0 to 84 years. Notably, 65–84 years was the age group with the highest risk of developing cancer. Intriguingly, the risk of the 85+ years group for all cancer types dropped slightly compared to that of the 65–84 years group (Fig. 1e), which suggests that the interplay between aging and carcinogenesis is considerably more intricate than a unidirectional causality wherein aging solely drives oncogenesis. Moreover, the age-dependent risk of dying from cancer was observed in most cancer types (n = 19 out of 23), where people aged no less than 65 years (65–84 and 85+ years group) showed a distinctively high probability of dying from cancer (Fig. 1f). Unlike the age-dependent probability of cancer incidence, individuals aged 85+ years diagnosed with breast cancer, prostate cancer, colorectal cancer, leukemia, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, bladder cancer, myeloma, melanoma of the skin, or stomach cancer exhibited a sustained or even elevated mortality risk compared to those aged 65–84 years. This suggests that the likelihood of cancer-related death demonstrates a stronger positive association with advanced aging. Therefore, deciphering the mechanistic links between aging and cancer has been a longstanding focus in biomedical research. It is known that the actual relationship between aging and cancer is far more intricate because neither aging nor cancer progression can be illustrated by a single mechanism or defined with a single feature. In 2023, Lopez-Otin et al. summarized twelve hallmarks of aging overlapping with ten hallmarks of cancer to different extents in different patterns.4 With the ongoing advancement in aging-cancer research, the aging and cancer paradox has been continuously enriched in recent years. In regards to aging-cancer consistency, key biological manifestations of aging include genomic instability and epigenetic alterations,5 mitochondrial dysfunction,6 gut dysbiosis,7 protein homeostasis impairment,8 and inflammation developing with advancing age (termed inflammaging),9 which have been reported to facilitate cancer development. Mechanistically, genomic instability and epigenetic reprogramming are highly prevalent in carcinogenesis. In terms of genomic instability, gain-of-function mutations of proto-oncogenes (e.g., KRAS,10 BRAF,11 EGFR12 and ERBB213), loss-of-function mutations of tumor suppressor genes (e.g., TP53,14 PTEN15 and APC16), and defects in DNA damage repair genes (e.g., BRCA1/2,17 MSH2 and MLH118) have been reported to drive tumorigenesis in a number of cancer types. For instance, TP53 is a crucial tumor suppressor gene that is considered the guardian of the genome,19 the somatic mutations of which are the most common genetic alteration in human cancers that can be observed in approximately half of human cancer cases.20 TP53 encodes tumor protein 53 (p53), which is a transcription factor that assures human longevity by triggering transient or permanent cell cycle arrest in response to various kinds of cellular stress, e.g., DNA damage, oncogenic signaling, and oxidative stress.21 Richardson reported a significant correlation between TP53 mutation and the aging-related rapid rise in the global cancer incidence of 25 cancer sites,22 which indicates that mutant p53 favors cancer development in the aged population. In terms of epigenetic reprogramming, global DNA hypomethylation, focal DNA hypermethylation, various histone modifications (i.e., methylation, acetylation, deacetylation, phosphorylation, and ubiquitination), chromatin remodeling dysregulation, and noncoding RNA dysregulation are confirmed carcinogenic epigenetic alterations. For instance, histone H3 lysine 4 trimethylation (H3K4me3) has emerged as a critical regulator of the tumor immune microenvironment. Accumulating evidence suggests that H3K4me3-mediated transcriptional activation upregulates the cancer cell expression of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), a key immune checkpoint molecule that facilitates tumor immune evasion through suppression of T-cell-mediated antitumor responses.23 Meanwhile, according to the latest finding reported by de Lima Camillo et al., the level of H3K4me3 correlates with age and can serve as an accurate epigenetic predictor of aging.24 This finding aligns with the “epigenetic clock” theory originally proposed by Horvath in the early 2010s, which proposed DNA methylation patterns to be a biomarker of biological aging.25 Intriguingly, progressive telomere shortening, an aging hallmark, has been reported to increase cancer incidence via genome instability in both telomerase-deficient zebrafish and mouse models.26 Mechanistically, telomere shortening leads to a loss of protection at chromosome ends, triggering catastrophic events such as breakage-fusion-bridge cycles, which cause genome instability.27 Moreover, in humans with ulcerative colitis28 and cirrhosis,29 premature telomere shortening in colonocytes and hepatocytes has been reported to induce genome instability and corresponding cancer development. In 2000, Franceschi et al. proposed a new term, “inflammaging”, which is defined as chronic and low-grade inflammation during aging and is correlated with age-related diseases, including various cancers.30 Inflammaging is attributed to various upstream events triggered by aging, e.g., senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP),31 gut dysbiosis,32 mitochondria dysfunction and oxidative stress,33 and immunosenescence.34 In addition to inflammaging, these upstream events are common in cancer progression. Physiologically, as people age, the gut microbiota shifts toward lower diversity and fewer beneficial gut bacteria but more proinflammatory species, all of which lead to gut dysbiosis and degrade gut barrier integrity.35 Currently, the gut dysbiosis-cancer axis has emerged as a focal point of current biomedical research. Abundant evidence supports that gut dysbiosis favors cancer development by impairing the gut barrier, producing carcinogenic metabolites, inducing chronic inflammation, and hindering the proper functioning of the immune system,36 which might be implicated in ~20% of cancer incidence.37 Mitochondria, namely, cellular energy factories, play critical roles in cellular metabolism. The accumulation of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) mutations and mitochondrial dysfunction have been recognized as distinct hallmarks of aging.38 During aging, elevated mtDNA mutations and impaired protein homeostasis synergistically induce excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, resulting in oxidative stress and mitochondrial autophagy impairment, eventually perpetuating a vicious cycle of mitochondrial dysfunction.39 Consistently, this vicious cycle can further amplify the carcinogenic signal. For instance, the transcription factor hypoxia inducible factor-1 alpha (HIF-1α) is induced in response to oxidative stress, further assisting metabolic reprogramming of cancer cells and promoting tumorigenesis.40 In terms of the immune system, a clinical study recruiting n = 363 healthy adults reported that the aged population showed a significant age-related difference in both adaptive and innate immune cell subsets from younger counterparts with a reduction in CD8+ T cells.41 Immunosenescence, which is the aging of the immune system with hallmarks including the senescence of immune cells, particularly T cells, impaired immune response, inflammaging, and metabolic alterations, has been demonstrated to facilitate tumor escape from immunosurveillance.34 In summary, aging hallmarks synergistically promoting tumorigenesis are classified as elements of aging-cancer consistency (Fig. 2).

Regarding aging-cancer antagonism, characteristic biological manifestations of aging-induced progressive telomere shortening,42 stem cell exhaustion,43 macroautophagy disability,44 and cell senescence45 have been demonstrated to antagonize cancer growth. Over 60 years ago, Leonard Hayflick reported for the first time that normal human cells undergo a limited number of cell divisions followed by entering cell senescence in vitro.46 The concept of the ‘Hayflick limit’ is widely regarded as a cornerstone of modern aging biology and provides a basis for understanding the mechanistic role of telomeres and telomerase in cellular aging.47 It has been proposed that shortened telomeres along with the inactivation of telomere-elongating telomerase constitute an evolutionarily conserved mechanism that protects normal cells from malignant transformation.48 Evidently, this aging hallmark is completely antagonistic to the replicative immortality of cancer cells. Stem cells are a population of undifferentiated cells characterized by their remarkable self-renewal capacity and the ability to differentiate into one or more specialized cell types. It is widely acknowledged that during organismal aging, stem cells undergo progressive functional decline and impaired regenerative potential, a state defined as stem cell exhaustion.49 With advances in biogerontology, in addition to pluripotent stem cell transplantation,50 partial cellular reprogramming has emerged as a promising strategy to counteract aging by resetting epigenetic aging clocks through the modulation of key transcription factors such as Yamanaka factors and associated signaling pathways.51 However, aging-induced stem cell exhaustion restricts cancer progression by impairing the phenotypic plasticity of cancer stem cells, thereby limiting their metastatic potential. Macroautophagy, a term that was first reported in the 1960s following the discovery of lysosomes, represents a catabolic process crucial for maintaining cellular homeostasis, including the degradation of unnecessary components and recycling of cellular contents.52 It is known that physical aging involves a decline in autophagy. This reduction creates a positive feedback loop, further exacerbating aging and the progression of age-related diseases.53 However, in cancer cells, this normally beneficial process is hijacked as a survival mechanism to avoid cell death and facilitate cell adaptation to environmental stress, which mainly occurs in late-stage tumorigenesis that promotes metastatic progression.54

Notably, in addition to the consistent and antagonistic effects of aging and cancer, the paradoxical relationship between aging and cancer converges on two aging hallmarks: telomere shortening and cell senescence, which exert dual opposing effects on tumorigenesis. As depicted above, telomere shortening, which is an aging hallmark, has been demonstrated to trigger genomic instability, a well-established hallmark of cancer. In contrast, shortened telomeres and inactivated telomerase safeguard normal cells from malignant transformation through an evolutionarily conserved mechanism. This process directly counteracts the replicative immortality that defines cancer cells. In terms of cell senescence, on the one hand, normal somatic cell senescence leads to SASP, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, immunosenescence and eventually the inflammaging status, which collectively create a favorable microenvironment for cancer progression. Senolytics, a group of medications selectively eliminating senescent cells via senolysis, have been suggested as a potential adjuvant anticancer therapy.55 On the other hand, this aging hallmark conflicts with the extraordinary replicative immortality of cancer cells (Fig. 2). Taken together, the relationships between aging and cancer are paradoxical, including both consistency and antagonism, which may partially explain the epidemiological observations that a certain number of cancer types showed an age-dependent probability of cancer incidence in people aged no more than 85 years and a slight reduction in probability in people aged over 85 years (Fig. 1).

Based on our observations of epidemiological statistics of aging and cancer as well as the paradoxical relationships between aging and cancer, in this review, we will cover the most aspects of research progress concerning 4 classifications of signaling pathways, including (i) nutrient-sensing and energy metabolism pathways; (ii) stress response and genome maintenance pathways; (iii) inflammatory and microenvironmental signaling pathways; and (iv) sirtuin-mediated epigenetic and transcriptional modulation pathways, their associated regulatory mechanisms, and clinical implications of aging and cancer in biology and disease.

Nutrient-sensing and energy metabolism pathways

In this section, we will mainly review two core pathways related to nutrient sensing and energy metabolism: the insulin/IGF-1/PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and the AMPK pathway (Fig. 4). The insulin/IGF-1/PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is activated under nutrient-rich conditions, promoting anabolic processes such as cell growth, proliferation, and protein synthesis. Hyperactivation of this pathway is usually associated with cancer progression and aging acceleration. The AMPK pathway is triggered during an energy crisis (e.g., low ATP levels, high AMP levels or nutrient deprivation), inhibiting mTOR activity and inducing catabolic processes such as autophagy. AMPK activation usually extends lifespan and suppresses tumorigenesis by maintaining energy balance and reducing oxidative stress. Insulin/IGF-1/PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway

Insulin/IGF-1/PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and aging

The insulin/insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) signaling (IIS) pathway, a highly conserved nutrient-sensing mechanism, was the first signaling pathway demonstrated to regulate both aging processes and susceptibility to age-related diseases in model organisms.56 The IIS pathway is highly conserved across most metazoan species, with core components and functions maintained from invertebrates to vertebrates.57 In 1990, Johnson reported for the first time that in Caenorhabditis elegans (C. elegans), a mutation in the age-1 gene significantly promoted the longevity of this model organism.58 The age-1 gene encodes a protein ortholog to the catalytic subunit of phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K), which is a key enzyme involved in the IIS signaling pathway in humans.59 Moreover, in 2003, Holzenberger et al. established a heterozygous knockout Igf1r(+/−) mouse model and demonstrated that, compared to their wild-type counterparts, Igf1r(+/−) mice showed significantly prolonged survival with better resistance to oxidative stress.60 Through distinct experimental approaches, both investigations consistently showed that perturbations in IGF-1/PI3K signaling substantially prolonged organismal lifespan. AKT, also known as protein kinase B (PKB), is an indirect downstream signal of PI3K that can be directly activated upon the phosphorylation of 3-phosphoinositide-dependent protein kinase-1 (PDK1).61 Subsequently, two downstream signals are modulated by AKT: one is the inhibition of forkhead box O transcription factor (FOXO) through AKT-mediated direct phosphorylation,62 and the other is the indirect activation of mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) via AKT-mediated phosphorylation and inactivation of tuberous sclerosis complex 2 (TSC2) and subsequent disrupted interaction of TSC1 and TSC263 (Fig. 4). In terms of FOXO, it has been widely recognized as a key class of transcription factors that promote longevity, so-called ‘long live FOXO’.62 To date, no therapeutic approaches have been developed to specifically target FOXO proteins. Current strategies focus on indirectly modulating FOXO activity through pharmacological agents such as PI3K/AKT inhibitors, adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activators, and sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) activators. For instance, Fan et al. reported that a dual inhibitor of PI3K/AKT and mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) selected from high-throughput screening of 156 drugs could trigger the apoptosis of senescent cells.64 Over one decade ago, Martin-Montalvo et al. reported that metformin can increase the longevity of mice by stimulating AMPK to reduce oxidative stress and chronic inflammation.65 Currently, the AMPK-SIRT1-FOXO pathway has been targeted to reduce oxidative stress and potentiate mitochondrial function.66 In addition to FOXO, mTORC1, the other downstream signal of the IIS pathway, serves as a central regulator of the mTOR signaling network, promoting cell survival, cell growth and cell cycle progression.67 Rapamycin was initially approved by the FDA in 1999 as an immunosuppressant for treating posttransplantation autoimmunity, which specifically suppresses the activity of mTORC1 in mTOR signaling and eventually disrupts cell proliferation and cell cycle progression of T cells while promoting immunosuppressive Treg development from naïve T cells.68,69 In 2009, Harrison et al. reported that rapamycin led to a 9% (male) and 14% (female) increase in maximal lifespan in mice at the age of 90% mortality by targeting mTOR, which was the first demonstration that mTOR signaling inhibition can regulate the lifespan of mammals with later-in-life drug administration.70 Since then, scientists have been devoted to studying the effects and underlying mechanisms of mTOR inhibitors in lifespan extension. Mannick & Lamming68 summarized the reasonable mechanisms of the geroprotective effects of mTOR inhibitors from several aspects, including (i) modulating protein translation to guarantee accurate synthesis and proper functions of proteins; (ii) increasing autophagy71; (iii) inhibiting cancer cell proliferation and impairing cancer stem cell functions72; (iv) benefiting normal stem cells; (v) avoiding hyperactive mTOR signaling in old age, which causes senescent cell accumulation and provokes SASP73; and (vi) alleviating immunosenescence by improving immune functions.74 In terms of the improvement in immune functions in old age by mTOR inhibition, short-term (6 weeks) administration of rapamycin to 26-month-old aged mice has been demonstrated to extend lifespan by rejuvenating hematopoietic stem cell function, boosting the generation of naïve lymphocytes, and enhancing the protective effects of vaccination against influenza virus.75 Based on this preclinical finding, Mannick et al. reported for the first time that mTOR inhibition can benefit immune functions in the aging human organ system,76 manifesting as an enhanced response to influenza vaccination and a decreased percentage of exhausted PD-1+ T cells in human peripheral blood.77 Low-dose administration of an oral mTOR inhibitor, RTB101, has been proven to be safe, well tolerated, and upregulates T1IFN-mediated immune responses to viruses in patients aged 65 years or above.78 In conclusion, when rapamycin or its analogs act as an anti-aging agent, they can improve the immunosuppressive environment of the aging host by boosting immunity, which sheds light on the dual benefits of mTOR inhibitors in longevity and overcoming immunotherapy resistance in the aged population.

Insulin/IGF-1/PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and cancer

Dysregulation of the IIS pathway has been widely implicated in tumorigenesis through its effects on cell proliferation, survival, and metabolic reprogramming. Gene mutations or copy number variations of IGF1R encoding insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF-1R) have been observed in various types of human cancer, which usually cause overexpression and hyperactivation of IGF-1R79 and eventually poor prognosis of cancer patients. For instance, activated IGF-1R was detected in over half of all breast cancer cases.80 In a cohort of 346 head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) patients, elevated IGF-1R expression was significantly associated with worse overall survival (OS) and disease-specific survival.81 Likewise, PI3K hyperactivation, phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) loss-of-function mutations and AKT gain-of-function mutations are well-established drivers of both aggressive disease progression and therapeutic resistance across multiple cancer types.67,82 For instance, PIK3CA is a proto-oncogene encoding the p110α catalytic subunit of PI3K, the mutations of which have been detected in approximately one-third to one-half of patients diagnosed with hormone receptor-positive (HR+) and human epidermal growth factor receptor-2 negative (HER2-) advanced breast cancer.83 In 2019, a phase III prospective clinical trial reported that the coadministration of alpelisib, a potent PI3Kα inhibitor, with fulvestrant, a selective estrogen receptor (ER) degrader, was effective in prolonging progression-free survival (PFS) in PIK3CA-mutated, HR+, HER2- advanced breast cancer.83 Recently, Eberlein et al. demonstrated that the combination of capivasertib, a first-in-class, oral pan-AKT inhibitor, and docetaxel, a potent chemotherapy agent, showed superior efficacy compared to monotherapy using preclinical prostate cancer models both in vitro and in vivo.84 Moreover, this study elucidated how capivasertib overcomes docetaxel resistance by suppressing AKT/glycogen synthase kinase 3β-driven survival in persister cells, providing a rationale for this combination in PTEN-deficient and wild-type PTEN prostate cancers.84 To date, numerous studies have reported the oncogenic pathway mediated by mTORC1, and therefore, mTOR inhibitors have entered clinical use in recent years. One representative mTOR inhibitor is temsirolimus, which is an analog of rapamycin referred to as “rapalog”. This therapeutic agent was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2007 for the treatment of advanced renal cell carcinoma.85 Mechanistically, temsirolimus not only inhibits cancer progression by arresting the cell cycle in the G1 phase and subsequently impairing cell growth and proliferation but also indirectly constrains tumor angiogenesis by decreasing the level of HIF-1α and the production of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF).86 Moreover, the anticancer effects of combining mTOR inhibitors with various kinds of immunotherapies have been investigated in several preclinical studies or are under exploration in clinical trials (to be reviewed in detail in Section “MTOR and immunotherapy”). In summary, substantial evidence has demonstrated that the oncogenic features of the IIS pathway and core signals involving IGF-1R, PI3K, PTEN, AKT and mTORC1 are promising anticancer therapeutic targets, and correlated in-depth investigations are still ongoing.

Therapeutic potential of the insulin/IGF-1/PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway in modulating aging and cancer

As discussed above, the signaling cascades mediated by the insulin/IGF-1/PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway exhibit synergistic effects in modulating both aging and cancer. This convergence of biological functions strongly supports the therapeutic potential of targeting this pathway with pharmacological agents capable of exerting dual anticancer and antiaging benefits. Some clinical trials show great interest in the potential anti-aging benefits of rapamycin and rapalogs, which have not yet received official approval for use in anti-aging interventions. For instance, a clinical trial investigating the effect of rapamycin in ovarian aging (NCT05836025, Phase II) sponsored by Columbia University is currently active (ClinicalTrials.gov, 2025). Another clinical trial focusing on the impacts of the mTOR inhibitor sirolimus on aged human muscle (NCT05414292) is now recruiting (ClinicalTrials.gov, 2025). Recently, Lee et al. systematically reviewed human studies on the effect of rapamycin and its analogs on aging.87 Studies have demonstrated their effectiveness in improving the immune response, cardiovascular function, and skin health in healthy individuals and those with aging-associated disorders.87 In 2023, Hedges et al. reported for the first time that alpelisib, a small-molecule PI3Kα inhibitor, prolonged the average lifespans of middle-aged mice by ~10% with manifestations of healthier aging, including improved body strength and coordination.88 In addition to the potential applications of clinically approved anticancer medications in antiaging, some candidates have exerted both antiaging and anticancer effects in a preclinical stage. For example, quercetin, a plant-derived flavonoid, has been demonstrated to inhibit the insulin/IGF-1/PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway to eliminate senescent cells and reduce SASP and oxidative stress both in vitro and in vivo.89 In 2018, Xu et al. reported for the first time that the administration of dasatinib, a second-generation tyrosine kinase inhibitor primarily applied in treating leukemia, and quercetin (D&Q), which was initially named the “senolytic cocktail”, could inhibit SASP and reduce pro-inflammatory factors, thereby improving healthspan and extending lifespan in aged murine models.90 Current records on ClinicalTrials.gov indicate that five clinical trials investigating the D&Q cocktail have been completed, with nine additional studies currently active or recruiting. This substantial research activity underscores the significant interest in this drug combination as a promising intervention for anti-aging and age-related disease research. Meanwhile, substantial evidence from in vitro and in vivo experiments supported the anticancer effects of quercetin by inducing apoptosis and constraining metastasis and chemosensitization.91 However, the clinical translation of quercetin through oral administration is significantly limited by its poor bioavailability, prompting recent research efforts to develop advanced nanodelivery systems for bioavailability enhancement.92

AMPK pathway

AMPK pathway and aging

The AMPK pathway serves as a critical cellular energy sensor that is activated under conditions of high energy demand. When the AMP/ATP ratio increases, AMPK becomes activated and phosphorylates downstream signaling molecules, ultimately reprogramming cellular metabolism that shifts from energy-consuming anabolic processes to energy-generating catabolic pathways and acts as a guardian of mitochondrial health93 (Fig. 4). In the 2000s, Greer et al. demonstrated that dietary restriction in C. elegans activates the AMPK-FOXO pathway, resulting in a significant lifespan extension of over 30% in wild-type worms,94 suggesting the potential anti-aging role of the AMPK pathway in mammals. In recent decades, scientists have been working on determining the in-depth mechanisms of the AMPK pathway in prolonging the human lifespan. Current evidence demonstrates that AMPK serves as a master regulator in attenuating aging processes through (i) orchestrating autophagic clearance of senescent cells,95 (ii) maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis, (iii) suppressing inflammaging, and (iv) modulating cellular energy metabolism. Specifically, in terms of AMPK-mediated autophagy, the AMPK/nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+)/SIRT1/peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) gamma coactivator-1 alpha (PGC-1α)/FOXO cascade96 and AMPK/Unc-51 like autophagy activating kinase 1 (ULK-1) axis are two main pathways97 (Fig. 4). In 2009, Canto et al. pioneeringly demonstrated that AMPK activation in skeletal muscle promotes mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation, leading to an increased NAD+/NADH ratio and subsequent activation of the NAD+-dependent deacetylase SIRT1.96 Moreover, this landmark study proposed for the first time that PGC-1α activity is dually regulated by AMPK through direct phosphorylation and SIRT1-mediated deacetylation, synergistically enhancing its downstream transcriptional control of autophagy.96 Meanwhile, this study provided key mechanistic insights that underpinned subsequent research on the AMPK-mediated metabolic and anti-aging effects of metformin and resveratrol. In terms of the mitochondrial homeostasis modulated by the AMPK pathway, in addition to the NAD+/SIRT1/PGC-1α/FOXO axis-induced mitigation of mitochondrial oxidative stress via ROS scavenging and mitophagy,98 AMPK-mediated induction of p53 signaling, AMPK-mediated inhibition of nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB), mTORC1, and HIF-1α signaling are directly or indirectly associated with mitochondrial health protection. Moreover, these AMPK-initiated signaling pathways mentioned above are more or less correlated with inflammaging and energy metabolism modulation. For instance, in response to metabolic stress, activated p53 upregulates downstream targets that modulate cellular metabolism and survival, including (i) TP53-induced glycolysis and apoptosis regulator (TIGAR), which suppresses glycolysis to limit ROS generation and apoptosis99; (ii) synthesis of cytochrome c oxidase 2 (SCO2), which enhances mitochondrial respiration and oxygen consumption100; and (iii) damage-regulated autophagy modulator (DRAM), which promotes pro-longevity macroautophagy.101,102 Overall, the AMPK pathway is a well-established anti-aging target, and its activators targeting signals such as AMPK,103 SIRT1104 and NAD+105 have been shown to extend healthspan or lifespan in preclinical models. Metformin, an anti-type 2 diabetic drug approved by the FDA for over 30 years that exerts its primary pharmacological effects of suppressing hepatic gluconeogenesis and enhancing peripheral insulin sensitivity,106 has been recognized as a brake of aging clocks for its geroprotective capacity in primate animal models, exhibiting an ~6-year attenuation in brain aging parameters.107,108 Recently, a preclinical study using the primate Microcebus murinus to investigate the geroprotective effects of resveratrol demonstrated that the compound improved cognitive and motor performance in middle-aged subjects.109 A clinical trial recruiting female adults aged 35–40 years investigating the effect of resveratrol on oocyte quality (NCT06235294, ClinicalTrials.gov, 2025) is currently active. Moreover, NAD+ precursors, e.g., nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) and nicotinamide riboside (NR), have been applied in clinical trials to reveal their potential geroprotective benefits. To date, a clinical trial investigating the efficacy and safety of NMN as an anti-aging supplement in healthy candidates aged 40–65 years has been completed (NCT04823260, ClinicalTrials.gov, 2021). The study reported that NMN supplementation enhanced physical endurance and general health markers among healthy adult participants.110 Another clinical trial evaluating the disease-delaying effect of NR supplementation in early Parkinson’s disease (NCT03568968, ClinicalTrials.gov, 2025) is currently active. These findings highlight the potential of the AMPK pathway as a key therapeutic avenue for anti-aging interventions.

AMPK pathway and cancer

While initially identified as a tumor-suppressive pathway, emerging research has uncovered the dual roles of AMPK in malignant transformation, where it can exert either antitumorigenic or protumorigenic effects depending on specific cellular and microenvironmental conditions. In 1998, Hemminki et al. provided the first evidence linking a protein-truncating germline mutation of LKB1, a gene encoding serine-threonine kinase liver kinase B1, to Peutz‒Jeghers syndrome, a cancer predisposition syndrome, which demonstrated the tumor-suppressive role of LKB1 in cancer development.111 Later, in the 2000s, Shaw et al. proposed the tumor-suppressive LKB1/AMPK/mTOR axis, as LKB1 was confirmed to be an upstream dominant regulator of AMPK112 and a negative regulator of oncogenic mTOR signaling via phosphorylation.113 AMPK suppresses mTOR signaling in both indirect and direct manners, as shown in Fig. 4. In an indirect manner, AMPK phosphorylates TSC2, which acts as a GTPase-activating protein (GAP) for Rheb, mediating the conversion of Rheb from a GTP-bound active state to a GDP-bound inactive state. The inactivation of Rheb directly blocks the activation of the mTOR pathway.113,114 In terms of the direct manner, AMPK phosphorylates Raptor, which is an mTOR binding partner and essential component of mTORC1, followed by the inhibition of mTORC1 activity and prohibited downstream signals.115 ULK1, a key kinase for autophagy initiation, forms a tripartite regulatory axis with AMPK and mTORC1, and its activity is dynamically modulated in response to cellular energy status.116 Under nutrient-deficient conditions, AMPK becomes activated, directly activates ULK1 and alleviates mTORC1-mediated suppression of ULK1, thereby inducing autophagy for cancer prevention. Conversely, nutrient sufficiency activates mTORC1, which then inhibits autophagy via ULK1 repression, eventually increasing the risk of tumorigenesis.116 In addition to the LKB1/AMPK/mTOR axis, AMPK also triggers tumor-suppressive downstream signals by inhibiting the NF-κB pathway and HIF-1α pathway (Fig. 4). The well-known “Warburg effect” discovered in the 1920s depicts the phenomenon in cancer cells that preferentially utilizes glycolysis for ATP production even under aerobic conditions instead of oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) in mitochondria, which is a cancer cell metabolic reprogramming to facilitate rapid tumor proliferation.117 Faubert et al. reported that suppression of AMPK pathway activity is capable of driving HIF-1α stabilization in the absence of hypoxia, resulting in an enhanced Warburg effect,118 which means that activated AMPK negatively regulates HIF-1α and a series of oncogenic events induced by HIF-1α, e.g., angiogenesis119 and cancer cell proliferation.120 Moreover, the p53 protein, encoded by the critical tumor suppressor gene TP53,19 has been shown to induce G1/S phase cell cycle arrest upon AMPK-mediated phosphorylation in response to glucose deficiency.121 However, prolonged AMPK-dependent activation of p53 can ultimately drive cells into senescence121 (Fig. 4). Collectively, the evidence summarized above supports the antitumorigenic roles of the AMPK pathway. However, under some specific conditions, the AMPK pathway also demonstrates tumor-promoting effects. For instance, Park et al. reported that 40% of human prostate cancer specimens showed highly activated AMPK, and small interfering (si)RNA-mediated AMPK gene knockout and exogenous treatment with the AMPK inhibitor compound C suppressed cancer cell proliferation and triggered cancer apoptosis.122 More recently, Eichner et al. demonstrated that AMPK exhibits protumorigenic functions in Kras-driven non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) by promoting tumor growth through Tfe3-mediated lysosomal gene expression, as genetic deletion of AMPK significantly reduced tumor burden in KrasG12D and KrasG12D p53f/f mouse models without phenocopying the tumor-suppressive effects of LKB1 loss, suggesting that the AMPK-Tfe3-lysosome axis is a metabolic vulnerability in NSCLC.123 These findings highlight a context-dependent oncogenic role of AMPK that contrasts with its canonical tumor-suppressive functions. Currently, there are no officially approved clinical anticancer medications directly targeting AMPK, and research on small-molecule AMPK inhibitors, i.e., compound C, and AMPK activators, i.e., MT 63-78,124 remains at the preclinical stage. Therefore, developing precision therapeutic strategies targeting AMPK could represent a promising direction for future research efforts.

Therapeutic potential of the AMPK pathway in modulating aging and cancer

Current evidence suggests that AMPK-targeted strategies have shown clearer mechanistic insights in anti-aging research than in oncology, where context-dependent effects and dual roles in tumor progression complicate therapeutic development. Therefore, we will focus on the findings of potential anticancer effects of preclinically validated antiaging medications, including (i) metformin, (ii) resveratrol and (iii) NAD+ precursors. Clinically, metformin remains the most widely prescribed first-line oral antidiabetic agent.125 Although its precise pharmacological mechanisms in glycemic control are not fully elucidated, a well-established mode of action involves the inhibition of mitochondrial complex I in the electron transport chain (ETC). This inhibition reduces hepatic ATP production, leading to an increased AMP/ATP ratio and subsequent activation of AMPK, ultimately suppressing hepatic gluconeogenesis.125 As mentioned above, the promising geroprotective effects of metformin observed in preclinical models stem from its pleiotropic mechanisms involving mitochondrial homeostasis regulation, AMPK-mTOR pathway activation, downstream autophagy stimulation, and concomitant reduction in aging-related inflammatory markers.126 Intriguingly, the aging-related pathways targeted by metformin exhibit significant overlap with cancer-associated pathways, offering mechanistic insights into its anti-neoplastic activity. Indeed, in recent years, preclinical and clinical findings have reported the anticancer effects of metformin as an anticancer agent or adjuvant to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy and immunotherapy.127 For instance, low-dose metformin treatment adjuvant to chemotherapy significantly prolonged the OS of patients diagnosed with ovarian cancer.128 Serageldin et al. reported that metformin greatly alleviated the toxicity induced by chemotherapy in nondiabetic breast cancer patients while maintaining chemotherapeutic efficacy against tumor cells in a randomized controlled trial.129 The chemoprotective effects of metformin observed in this trial include neurotoxicity reduction, mucosal protection, fatigue mitigation, cardioprotection, and hepatic protection.129 However, according to the findings of a phase II randomized controlled trial (NCT01210911, ClinicalTrials.gov, 2021), the addition of a conventional antidiabetic dose of metformin to the gemcitabine plus erlotinib regimen demonstrated no survival benefit in patients with advanced pancreatic adenocarcinoma.130 Therefore, future investigations into the potential of metformin in anticancer applications should systematically evaluate factors including dose‒response relationships, cancer type-specific and stage-dependent effects, metabolic comorbidities in patient populations, etc. Resveratrol, a well-known plant-derived polyphenol abundant in grape skins and red wine,131 exhibits pleiotropic effects in preclinical models, ranging from geroprotective to potential anticancer properties by targeting the AMPK pathway. Over a decade ago, Price et al. first established that resveratrol enhances mitochondrial function through activation of the AMPK/NAD+/SIRT1 signaling axis.132 Recently, Xia et al. reported the underlying mechanisms of resveratrol in anti-photoaging, including autophagy activation, reduced ROS production, reduced apoptosis and decreased inflammatory responses via AMPK-modulated downstream pathways.133 Similar to metformin, the anti-aging mechanisms of resveratrol share certain commonalities with tumor-suppressive pathways. A number of preclinical studies have reported remarkable anticancer effects of resveratrol in various cancer types, including melanoma,134 breast cancer,135 prostate cancer,136 colorectal cancer,137 pancreatic cancer,138 and lung cancer.139 Moreover, recently resveratrol has been reported to potentiate anti-PD1 treatment against pancreatic cancer by altering the gut microbiome and downstream metabolites, which indirectly promotes the infiltration of CD8+ T cells in the tumor microenvironment.140 However, the low bioavailability of resveratrol in humans remains a key obstacle hindering the translation of preclinical findings into therapeutic benefits.141 Therefore, scientists have been making efforts to improve the bioavailability of resveratrol in recent years, such as encapsulating it with nanoparticles142 or phospholipid complexes143 and discovering resveratrol derivatives.144 NMN and NR, both of which are NAD+ precursors with established antiaging effects in preclinical and clinical studies, have dual impacts on tumorigenesis depending on cellular conditions, the tumor microenvironment and different types or stages of cancer. For instance, Jiang et al. revealed that NAD+-mediated suppression of triple-negative breast cancer metastasis occurs via the SIRT1/p66Shc signaling pathway.145 In contrast, Zhou et al. reported that nuclear factor I B (NFIB) can upregulate the expression of nicotinamide phosphoribosyl transferase (NAMPT), which is the rate-limiting enzyme in the NAD+ salvage synthesis pathway, by suppressing the binding of microRNA (miR)-182-5p to its target site on NAMPT, thereby increasing NAD+ levels and driving colorectal cancer (CRC) cell proliferation.146 Considering the dual roles of NAD+ in both promoting cellular health and potentially supporting tumor growth, supplementation with NAD+ precursors requires careful consideration of individual cancer risk factors, dosage control, and medical supervision, particularly for high-risk populations or cancer survivors.

Stress response and genome maintenance pathways

As reviewed above, caloric restriction and metabolic reprogramming exhibit well-established geroprotective and tumor-suppressive properties. Nevertheless, cellular homeostasis faces persistent threats from progressive DNA damage accumulation, which results from diverse exogenous sources, e.g., smoking, ultraviolet (UV) exposure, ionizing radiation, and chemotherapeutic agents, and endogenous processes, e.g., oxidative stress, telomere attrition, oncogenic signaling cascades, and tumor-associated hypoxia (Fig. 5). These challenges are mainly modulated by stress responses and genome maintenance mechanisms. Therefore, the functional decline of the associated system not only represents a core hallmark of aging but also serves as a key driver of cancer development. Here, we mainly focus on 3 parts of the stress response and genome maintenance pathways: (i) the DNA damage response (DDR) pathway; (ii) the cell cycle regulation pathway; and (iii) the apoptosis pathway and the functions of key signals involved, i.e., ATM, ATR, p53, Bcl-2 and PARP in aging modulation and the existing or potential drug targets, i.e., Bcl-2, PARP, CDK4/6, ATM, ATR, CHK1, MDM2, p53, and WEE1 in cancer therapy (Fig. 5).Fig. 5Schematic of aging- and cancer-related stress responses and genome maintenance cascades. Stress response and genome maintenance pathways and aging

ATM/ATR and aging

In 2005, Lombard et al. summarized the relationship between aging and genome stability and found that DNA damage accumulates with aging, while defects in DNA repair can generate phenotypes of premature aging.147 As depicted in Fig. 5, the DDR is predominantly initiated by two major triggers: DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) and DNA replication stress, which activate distinct signaling pathways mediated by ATM and ATR, respectively. ATM is a gene encoding ataxia-telangiectasia mutated protein kinase, a member of the phosphatidylinositol-3-kinase-related protein kinase (PIKK) family, which functions as a master regulator in DNA damage response (DDR) checkpoint pathways.148 ATM kinase in eukaryotic cells can be rapidly switched to the active status upon sensing DNA DSBs and then coordinate various cellular physiology processes, including cell cycle arrest and DNA repair, by phosphorylating downstream effectors and transducers, including p53148,149 (Fig. 5). As its name implies, a lack of functional ATM kinase due to mutations in the ATM gene is the underlying cause of ataxia-telangiectasia. The characteristics of this early-onset disease include premature aging, neurodegeneration, chronic inflammation, immune dysfunction and cancer predisposition.150 In a premature aging mouse model with defective DDR and downregulated Atm kinase expression, the lifespan of mice with progeria has been demonstrated to be elongated by activating Atm with chloroquine, an effective antimalaria medication, through the Atm-Sirt6 axis.151 The longevity protein sirtuin 6, encoded by the Sirt6 gene, has been observed to be prevented from ubiquitination and degradation via Atm-mediated phosphorylation.151 Furthermore, in both murine and human cells, ATM kinase has been confirmed to be responsible for recruiting telomerase, an enzyme in charge of length maintenance of telomeres, which indicates that inhibiting ATM activity leads to progressive telomere shortening, a classic aging hallmark.152 In contrast, Kang et al. revealed a novel mechanism of the ATM-mediated aging process.153 Attenuated ATM kinase activity has been shown to facilitate the restoration of lysosomal acidification-dependent autophagy and clearance of dysfunctional mitochondria to recover mitochondrial function in both normal153 and accelerated-aging cells,154 which means that inhibiting ATM kinase activity can ameliorate cell senescence, a process of irreversible cell cycle arrest. Similarly, in terms of ATR, Ruzankina et al. discovered that at the adult stage, Atr knockout mice exhibited early-onset aging phenotypes, including hair graying, thymic involution, osteoporosis, and tissue-specific stem and progenitor cell exhaustion.155 Moreover, ATR has been demonstrated to play an essential role in telomere homeostasis in human cells.156 However, Feringa et al. demonstrated that persistent ATR activation is the key driver of cellular senescence.157 Mechanistically, the accumulation of DNA repair intermediates, which is a hallmark of inefficient repair, amplifies ATR signaling. This sustained ATR activation then initiates the senescence program during the G2 phase, leading to cell cycle exit and ultimately preserving genome stability.157 Therefore, as key guardians of genomic stability, the current research reveals that both ATM and ATR exhibit paradoxical roles in the aging process.

p53 and aging

TP53 is a crucial tumor suppressor gene that is considered the guardian of the genome,19 the somatic mutations of which are the most common genetic alteration in human cancers that can be observed in approximately half of human cancer cases.20 TP53 encodes tumor protein 53 (p53), which is a transcription regulator that assures human longevity by triggering transient or permanent cell cycle arrest in response to various kinds of cellular stress, e.g., DNA damage, oncogenic signaling, and oxidative stress158 (Fig. 5). Richardson demonstrated a significant correlation between TP53 mutations and the age-related rapid increase in global cancer incidence across 25 cancer sites,22 which indicates that mutant p53 favors cancer development in the aged population. While TP53 is well established as a tumor suppressor gene (as will be discussed in the following section), its role as an aging-related gene remains less clear. It has been suggested that in p53+/m mice with enhanced p53 activity, p53-mediated cell cycle arrest, while preventing damaged cells from proliferating, may also deplete hematopoietic stem cells with proliferative potential. This depletion can impair the structure and function of renewable tissues, ultimately contributing to aging.159,160 Furthermore, based on studies demonstrating accelerated-aging in mice with aberrant p53 activation caused either by heterozygous TP53 mutation161 or N-terminal truncation of p53,162 Wu and Prives proposed that normal aging of organisms relies on the adequate regulation of p53 by its negative regulator, mouse double minute 2 homolog (MDM2)163 (Fig. 5). Therefore, mutant p53 overactivation can accelerate aging. In terms of immune regulation, to some extent, p53 is considered an anti-aging modulator. For instance, in mice with p53 deficiency, the accumulation of T cells in the memory phenotype was sped up in a spontaneous manner, and the capacity of T-cell proliferation was limited, which ultimately resulted in the aging of the immune system, namely, immunosenescence.164 Moreover, in NSCLC, PD-1/PD-L1-driven T-cell exhaustion can be alleviated by stabilized and activated p53, owing to its role in miR-34-dependent PD-L1 downregulation.165 Accumulating evidence indicates that p53 transcriptionally regulates genes encoding critical cytokines166 and chemokines,167 all of which play essential roles in recruiting and activating immune cells during antitumor immunity. In addition, p53 has been found to play a key role in repressing the polarization of tumor-promoting M2 macrophages.168 Based on the findings above, maintaining the normal activity of p53 via proper regulation is suggested to be essential for avoiding aging phenotype formation and can also protect the organism from immunosenescence.

Bcl-2 and aging

Bcl-2 is the founding member of the Bcl-2 family of proteins. This family of proteins shares one or more Bcl-2 homology (BH) domains and exerts either antiapoptotic (e.g., Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL) or proapoptotic (e.g., Bax, Bak, Noxa, and Puma) effects. BCL2 is an oncogene encoding the antiapoptotic protein Bcl-2, which was initially observed to be overexpressed due to chromosomal translocation in B-cell lymphoma, indicating its strong relationship with cancer development (as will be discussed in the following section).169 Abundant research has reported that the dysregulation of apoptosis can contribute to the aging process by affecting cellular senescence and tissue homeostasis, eventually leading to age-related diseases. As an antiapoptotic gene, BCL2 has been reported to correlate with lifespan extension in animal models back in the last century,170 which indicates its potential role in promoting longevity by suppressing neuronal apoptosis. However, a review published by Martin et al. in 2023 unveiled the multifaceted roles of Bcl-2 family proteins in senescence, including not only serving as a target for eliminating senescent cells (by using BH3 mimetics to block Bcl-2 antiapoptotic proteins) but also being modulated to alleviate SASP (by promoting proapoptotic Bax/Bak-mediated mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP)).171

PARP and aging

MOMP triggers the irreversible release of cytochrome C and other apoptogenic factors from the mitochondrial intermembrane space into the cytosol, leading to irreversible caspase activation in most cases.172 These effector caspases target poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP), a key DNA repair enzyme, cleaving it to inactivate its repair activity, thereby ensuring efficient apoptotic cell death. Caspase-mediated PARP cleavage is a hallmark of apoptosis and serves as a critical biochemical marker for programmed cell death.173 Intriguingly, PARP exhibits complex and multifaceted functions in aging regulation. Olaparib, a PARP inhibitor (PARPi), has been demonstrated to not only exacerbate cellular senescence by amplifying DNA damage, upregulating senescence markers (e.g., SA-β-gal and p21), and inducing irreversible cell cycle arrest, particularly in replicatively senescent macrophages but also trigger mixed apoptotic and necrotic cell death at high doses.174 This finding highlights potential risks in elderly patients with preexisting senescent cell accumulation. In contrast, in 2011, Bai et al. reported for the first time that gene knockout or PJ34-mediated pharmacological inhibition of PARP1, a major NAD+-consuming enzyme, significantly increases intracellular NAD+ levels, particularly in brown adipose tissue and skeletal muscle, thereby activating the NAD+-dependent deacetylase SIRT1.175 In their study, PARP1-deficient mice exhibited improved metabolic phenotypes, and these effects were markedly attenuated upon SIRT1 knockout, confirming that SIRT1 serves as the central mediator of PARPi-mediated metabolic regulation.175 Therefore, chronic PARP1 activation may contribute to aging by depleting cellular NAD+ pools, thereby impairing NAD+-dependent longevity pathways. However, transient PARP1 activity is essential for maintaining genomic stability, demonstrating context-dependent effects. Recently, Guo et al. reported that PARP1 knockout Drosophila models showed increased AMPK signaling, accelerated mitochondrial turnover and improved muscle functional capacity.176 Overall, the roles of PARPis in aging modulation present an apparent contradiction in the current understanding.

Stress response and genome maintenance pathways and cancer

FDA-approved anticancer therapies targeting Bcl-2, PARP, and CDK4/6

While targeting stress response and genome maintenance pathways (e.g., ATM, ATR, p53, Bcl-2 and PARP) for anti-aging interventions remains largely confined to preclinical studies in cellular and animal models, the therapeutic exploitation of these same pathways in oncology has achieved significant clinical translation. For instance, venetoclax, the first small-molecule Bcl-2 selective inhibitor, has been approved by the FDA for the treatment of chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and some subtypes of lymphoma.177 The pharmacological mechanism of venetoclax is promoting the programmed cell death of cancer cells by antagonizing antiapoptotic Bcl-2.177 Moreover, venetoclax has been reported to increase the production of ROS by preventing the formation of respiratory chain supercomplexes to potentiate effector T-cell functions.178 Since 2014, PARPis, including olaparib,179 rucaparib,180 and niraparib,181 have been progressively approved by the FDA mainly for treating ovarian cancer with BRCA mutations or homologous recombination deficiency (HRD). PARP1 serves as a master regulator of single-strand break (SSB) repair, coordinating the recruitment of downstream repair factors to sites of DNA damage (Fig. 5). The synthetic lethality between BRCA mutations and PARPi arises from the simultaneous disruption of two critical DNA repair pathways: (i) BRCA1/2 loss abolishing homologous recombination (HR) and (ii) PARPi blocking base excision repair and trapping PARP-DNA complexes, leading to catastrophic genomic instability in cancer cells while sparing normal cells with intact HR.182,183

It is known that the Rb-E2 transcription factor (E2F) pathway plays a central role in mediating cell cycle arrest in response to stress signals. Activated cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors p21 (inhibiting cyclin E-CDK2) and p16 (blocking cyclin D-CDK4/6) prevent the phosphorylation of Rb, keeping it in its active, hypophosphorylated state. Active Rb binds and inactivates E2F transcription factors, suppressing the expression of cell cycle-promoting genes and inducing arrest at the G1/S checkpoint. If the stress is transient and resolved (e.g., through DNA repair), Rb becomes phosphorylated by restored CDK activity, releasing E2F and allowing cell cycle re-entry, which is called reversible cell cycle arrest. However, under persistent stress (e.g., irreparable DNA damage or oncogene activation), sustained p16/p21 expression locks Rb in its active form, leading to permanent E2F suppression and irreversible cell cycle arrest, thereby preventing aberrant proliferation184 (Fig. 5). Building on this mechanistic framework, CDK4/6 inhibitors, including palbociclib, ribociclib, and abemaciclib, have been approved by the FDA for HR+/HER2- breast cancer in the past decade for their targeted suppression of Rb phosphorylation, which effectively blocks the G1 to S phase transition in the cell cycle.185 Moreover, CDK2 inhibitor candidates, e.g., INCB123667186 (NCT05238922, ClinicalTrials.gov, 2025) and BLU-222187 (NCT05252416, ClinicalTrials.gov, 2025), have been tested in several ongoing phase I/II clinical trials and exhibited promising anticancer effects in certain types of tumors according to preliminary data.

Emerging therapies targeting ATM, ATR, CHK1, MDM2, p53, and WEE1

Beyond the FDA-approved Bcl-2, PARP and CDK4/6 targeted therapies, multiple promising drug candidates targeting ATM, ATR, CHK1, MDM2, p53 and Wee1 kinase (WEE1) have demonstrated significant antitumor efficacy in phase I to III clinical trials. For instance, the ATM inhibitor AZD0156 demonstrated preclinical efficacy in enhancing the anticancer activity of olaparib across lung, gastric, and breast cancer cell lines in vitro and in two patient-derived triple-negative breast cancer xenograft models in vivo.188 Monotherapy or combination therapy involving AZD0156 has advanced to a phase I clinical trial in patients with advanced cancer, although the results remain pending release (NCT02588105, ClinicalTrials.gov, 2022). A recent phase II umbrella study demonstrated promising clinical activity of ceralasertib, an ATR inhibitor, combined with durvalumab, an anti-PD-L1 monoclonal antibody, in patients with immunotherapy-refractory advanced NSCLC, potentially mediated through restoration of antitumor immunity (NCT03334617, ClinicalTrials.gov, 2025).189 Particularly noteworthy was the enhanced treatment response observed in patients harboring ATM alterations, with this subgroup achieving an objective response rate of 26.1% compared to 13.9% in the overall study population.189 Moreover, prexasertib, a CHK1 inhibitor targeting the ATR-CHK1 DDR pathway, exhibited promising clinical activity in a phase II trial recruiting 49 patients with high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma with resistance to platinum treatment,190 further underscoring its potential application, particularly in tumors with underlying DNA repair deficiencies or TP53 mutations where CHK1 inhibition may exert synthetic lethal effects.191,192 Recently, Wang et al. conducted a comprehensive review of preclinical and clinical studies targeting MDM2, confirming that MDM2 inhibition remains a promising therapeutic strategy.193 For example, a phase I clinical trial demonstrated that the novel MDM2 inhibitor ALRN-6924 exhibited a safe profile and preliminary antitumor activity in patients with wild-type TP53 solid tumors or lymphomas (NCT02264613, ClinicalTrials.gov, 2020).194 Regarded as the guardian of the genome, TP53 performs crucial tumor-suppressing functions.19 It is known that the stabilization and activation of wild-type p53 are essential for its tumor-suppressive effects. However, ~50% of human cancers harbor somatic TP53 mutations.20 Therefore, compounds capable of restoring wild-type p53 function in mutant p53-expressing tumors are potential anticancer agents.195 The mutant p53-targeting compound APR-246 exhibits this capability.196 Recently, in a phase I study, the combination of APR-246 and pembrolizumab demonstrated favorable tolerability in 37 patients with advanced solid tumors, achieving an objective response rate of 10.3%.196 Clinical activity was observed in patients with gastric/gastroesophageal junction tumors and bladder cancer patients without prior anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy, as well as in NSCLC patients previously treated with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors196 (NCT04383938, ClinicalTrials.gov, 2025). WEE1, a conserved serine/threonine kinase, maintains genomic integrity by phosphorylating cyclin-dependent kinase 1 (CDK1) to inhibit cyclin B-CDK1 complex activation. This mechanism prevents unscheduled mitotic onset, enforcing the G2/M checkpoint in response to replication stress or DNA damage197 (Fig. 5). Similar to PARPis, WEE1 inhibitors exert their anticancer effects primarily through synthetic lethality. By disrupting the G2/M checkpoint, WEE1 inhibition forces cells with endogenous DNA damage (e.g., due to TP53 mutations or HR deficiency) to prematurely enter mitosis, thereby amplifying genomic instability and triggering mitotic catastrophe.197 Multiple WEE1-targeted inhibitors are currently being evaluated in clinical trials across different cancer types. For instance, AZD1775 (adavosertib) monotherapy has demonstrated antitumor activity in treatment-refractory solid tumors with CCNE1 amplification, with particularly encouraging responses observed in epithelial ovarian cancer in a phase II trial198 (NCT03253679, ClinicalTrials.gov, 2023). In addition, a phase I trial investigating the tumor-suppressive effect of ZN-c3, a next-generation WEE1 inhibitor, combined with chemotherapy or bevacizumab in treating gynecologic malignancies, including ovarian, peritoneal, or fallopian tube cancer, is now recruiting patients (NCT04516447, ClinicalTrials.gov, 2025).

Stress response and genome maintenance pathways in modulating aging and cancer

Clearly, the stress response and genome maintenance pathways offer a promising avenue for repurposing anticancer drugs for antiaging applications. As summarized in Table 1, current drug development efforts targeting this pathway have been predominantly focused on oncology, with comparatively limited progress in aging-related applications. Therefore, the primary focus of this pathway regarding aging and cancer modulation lies in two key directions: (i) repurposing established anticancer drugs for antiaging applications and (ii) developing strategies to mitigate anticancer therapy-induced cellular senescence. In terms of direction (i), for instance, venetoclax, the first small-molecule Bcl-2 selective inhibitor, has emerged as a senolytic candidate for its pro-apoptotic effect, which could alleviate aging by eliminating senescent cells or attenuating inflammation.199 It is known that venetoclax has been approved by the FDA for leukemia treatment.177 Nevertheless, its clinical utility is limited by severe side effects, particularly neutropenia and thrombocytopenia, due to unintended toxicity to neutrophils and platelets.200 Undoubtedly, both cancer therapeutics and senolytic interventions face a shared dilemma of how to achieve highly selective cell killing. Regarding direction (ii), the clinically approved PARPi olaparib, which induces synthetic lethality in BRCA-mutated or HRD tumor cells, has been shown to promote cellular senescence through DNA damage accumulation, senescence marker upregulation, and irreversible cell cycle arrest.174 This dual activity raises important considerations about its potential pro-aging effects during cancer treatment. Therefore, a phase I clinical trial using a combination of olaparib with navitoclax, an inhibitor of Bcl-2/Bcl-XL, in patients with high-grade serous carcinoma and triple-negative breast cancer is currently active (NCT05358639, ClinicalTrials.gov, 2025). The rationale of this clinical trial is that cancer cells exposed to PARPi establish a prosurvival senescent phenotype, which could survive dependent on antiapoptotic proteins.201 The combination of navitoclax and olaparib exhibits synergistic efficacy, with navitoclax targeting antiapoptotic proteins and acting as a senolytic agent to clear senesce cells and enhance the anticancer effects of olaparib.201 In conclusion, given the paradoxical effects of targeting key components in this pathway (e.g., ATM, ATR, p53, Bcl-2, and PARP) on aging, where the same target may exert opposing biological outcomes, further preclinical studies are urgently needed to identify reliable molecular targets for aging modulation. Inflammatory and microenvironmental signaling pathways

As the central hub of inflammatory signaling, the NF-κB family comprises five transcription factors (p50, p52, p65 (RelA), RelB, and c-Rel) that form both heterodimers (e.g., p50/p65 (RelA)) and homodimers (e.g., p50/p50), which differentially regulate gene expression through canonical or noncanonical pathways.202 These proteins are constitutively expressed in the cytoplasm of all cell types, where they function as master regulators of inflammation, carcinogenesis, and innate and adaptive immune responses.202 The concept of “inflammaging” was first proposed by Franceschi et al. in 2000 to characterize chronic low-grade inflammation during aging that predisposes patients to various age-related malignancies.30 In 2008, NF-κB signaling was described as a culprit of inflammaging,203 which indicates that NF-κB signaling, inflammation and aging are closely interrelated. In addition, extensive studies have reported the roles of NF-κB signaling in cancer by modulating autonomous functions of both cancer and immune cells and crosstalk and interactions between cancer and immune cells.204 Serving as the core effector of canonical NF-κB signaling, the p50/p65 (RelA) dimer critically contributes to inflammaging via sustained proinflammatory responses and simultaneously facilitates oncogenesis by enhancing cancer cell survival, metastatic progression, and therapeutic resistance (Fig. 6). Here is an overview of current molecular biological mechanisms of NF-κB-centered inflammation-associated signaling pathways shared in modulating both aging and cancer. As depicted in Fig. 6, it is activated when extracellular or intracellular stimuli trigger the IκB kinase (IKK) complex consisting of catalytic subunits IKKα/IKKβ and the regulatory scaffold IKKγ (NEMO), which phosphorylates IκB to release sequestered NF-κB dimers for nuclear translocation and triggers IκB degradation by the proteasome.205 The NF-κB pathway can be initiated via extracellular and intracellular triggers. The upstream stimulating extracellular signals include T-cell receptor (TCR) activation by antigen-presenting cells, tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα) (via tumor necrosis factor receptor (TNFR)), pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) (lipopolysaccharide (LPS), dsRNA, etc.) (via toll-like receptor (TLR)), interleukin (IL)-1α/β and IL-18 (via IL-1R and IL-18R, respectively),205 whereas the upstream intracellular signals include ROS and DDR-triggered ATM signaling.206,207 As transcription factors, the NF-κB complex mediates the transcription of genes encoding SASP-related cytokines (TNFα, IL-1α, IL-6, etc.), pro-IL-1β/18, nod-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3), suppressor of mothers against decapentaplegic 7 (SMAD7), etc. These proteins are involved in the positive feedback of the NF-κB signaling pathway directly or indirectly. Moreover, IL-6 (signaling through the IL-6R/glycoprotein 130 receptor complex) and transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) (acting via the TGF-β receptor (TβR) II/I receptor complex) engage in crosstalk with the NF-κB pathway by activating their respective receptor systems and downstream signaling cascades. IL-6 activates the Janus kinase (JAK)/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) pathway,208 subsequently (i) increases the production of IL-6/8, BCL-2, PD-L1, VEGF and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) to promote SASP, antiapoptosis, immune invasion, angiogenesis, and cancer metastasis, (ii) upregulates the gene expression of p21 and p16 to arrest the cell cycle, and (iii) promotes mitochondrial STAT3 phosphorylation.209 TGF-β is secreted in response to inflammation, hypoxia, ROS, etc., and triggers the SMAD2/3/SMAD4/p21/p15 pathway to induce irreversible cell cycle arrest. However, as the downstream signal of the NF-κB pathway, SMAD7 suppresses TGF-β/SMAD2/3 signaling,210 which means it could alleviate irreversible cell cycle arrest (Fig. 6). Overall, inflammatory and microenvironmental signaling pathways comprise a complex network of molecular signals featuring multiple positive and negative feedback loops and crosstalk with other pathways. Here, we mainly focus on 4 parts: (i) NF-κB signaling; (ii) the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway; (iii) the IL-6/JAK/STAT3 signaling axis; and (iv) the TGF-β/SMAD pathway, and review their functions in modulating aging and cancer.

Inflammatory and microenvironmental signaling pathways and aging

NF-κB signaling pathway and aging

A number of mouse models with genetic modifications have been established to confirm the relationship between NF-κB signaling and aging. Tichy et al. established a mouse model with constitutive activation of IKKβ in muscle stem cells (MSCs) and found that hyperactivation of NF-κB signaling induced early telomere shortening in MSCs, which was not dependent on cell replication, suggesting that targeting NF-κB signaling could retard the aging process by enhancing the regeneration capacity of MSCs.211 In another study, researchers adopted the Acan CreERT2/+; R26 Ikk2ca/Ikk2ca IKKβ gain-of-function mouse model to specifically activate the IKKβ/NF-κB signaling pathway in adult chondrocytes, which was sufficient to mimic an osteoarthritis-like phenotype. This finding established that dysregulated activation of IKKβ/NF-κB signaling in chondrocytes is a key driver in the onset of age-related osteoarthritis.212 Recently, scientists identified NFKBMARL‑1 through cross‑species transcriptomic comparison and CRISPR screening as an evolutionarily conserved long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) markedly upregulated during aging.213 Subsequent mechanistic investigations revealed that upon initial NF-κB activation, NFKBMARL‑1 binds and recruits the additional transcription factor RELA to the promoters of target genes such as NFKBIZ, thereby establishing a NF-κB/NFKBMARL‑1/NFKBIZ feed‑forward loop that persistently amplifies NF-κB signaling activity.213 Therefore, this lncRNA not only serves as an aging biomarker but also acts as a key regulator that directly drives and reinforces NF-κB signaling, thereby promoting chronic inflammation and the aging process.213 Moreover, substantial evidence indicates that proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNFα, IL-1α, IL-6) secreted by cells with hyperactivated NF-κB signaling, a hallmark of the SASP, actively drive the aging process.214 Intriguingly, Ryu et al. used targeted gene sequencing in 450 centenarians, individuals who had reached the age of 100 years or older, and 500 controls and identified cognition-related genetic variants associated with exceptional longevity.215 They found that the noncoding variants in NFKBIA, which encodes IκBα (a repressor of NF-κB dimers), ranked among the top three functional longevity-associated variants in ~700 candidate genes.215 Currently, the IKK complex, SASP, IL-1α/β/IL-1R, and IκB have emerged as promising targets for anti-aging interventions (Fig. 6). In terms of the IKK complex, an innovative small-molecule compound, SR12343, capable of inhibiting NF-κB activation was recently identified, which functions by specifically disrupting the IKKβ-NEMO association.216 It not only mitigated senescence in both mouse and human cells in vitro, as evidenced by decreased SA-β-gal activity but also exerted anti-aging effects in vivo across two accelerated-aging mouse models and a naturally aged 2-year-old wild-type model. These effects included downregulation of senescence-associated biomarkers, SASP suppression, and muscle fiber hypertrophy.216 Regarding SASP, targeting IL-6R with tocilizumab, a neutralizing antibody, has been used as an indirect strategy to counteract the detrimental effects of IL-6, a major SASP component, in premature aging models.217 In addition, although senolytics (e.g., D&Q90 and navitoclax201) do not directly target NF-κB signaling components, they effectively eliminate the cellular source of SASP by selectively clearing senescent cells. This intervention consequently delays aging processes, mediated in part through interrupting the SASP-driven positive feedback loop that sustains NF-κB activation.218 Furthermore, some drugs targeting the IL-1 pathway, including recombinant human IL-1R antagonist (anakinra), soluble IL-1R (rilonacept), neutralizing antibody targeting IL-1α (bermekimab) or IL-1β (canakinumab), have exhibited anti-inflammatory effects and potential in prolonging human healthspan and lifespan.219 Anakinra, an FDA-approved drug issued for treating rheumatoid arthritis, has recently been demonstrated to rejuvenate hematopoietic stem cells by alleviating bone marrow inflammation in aged mice, therefore extending the healthspan of the hematopoietic system.220 Moreover, the proteasome regulating IκB degradation has become a focus for anti-aging drug development. Sulforaphane, a diet-derived isothiocyanate enriched in broccoli and related crucifers, has been reported to decrease vascular inflammation by inactivating TNFα/TNFR-induced downstream NF-κB signaling as well as inhibiting IκB degradation and eventually reducing the risk of vascular diseases in mice.221 Notably, a clinical trial investigating the effect of topical administration of sulforaphane for skin aging interventions is now active (NCT03730649, ClinicalTrials.gov, 2025). In summary, the NF-κB signaling pathway is critically involved in aging processes, primarily through its role in inflammaging. This connection highlights its potential as a target for innovative anti-aging interventions, warranting further in-depth investigation.

NLRP3 inflammasome pathway and aging

The NLRP3 inflammasome pathway requires NF-κB-mediated transcriptional priming for its activation, while its effector cytokines (IL-1β and IL-18) reinforce NF-κB signaling through their respective receptors, establishing a pro-inflammatory feedback loop.222 Specifically, following NF-κB priming, diverse danger signals (e.g., ROS and extracellular ATP) trigger the assembly of the NLRP3 inflammasome complex, which facilitates the autocatalytic activation of caspase-1, which then proteolytically processes pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 into their bioactive forms223 (Fig. 6). Several targets, e.g., NLRP3, caspase-1, IL-1β and IL-18, in this pathway have been investigated in anti-aging interventions. In terms of NLRP3 inhibitors, tranilast, a clinically approved anti-allergic drug with additional anti-inflammatory effects inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation by disrupting the mammalian NIMA-related kinase 7 (NEK7) interaction,224,225 has been confirmed to exhibit significant preventive and therapeutic efficacy in mouse models of NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated human pathologies, e.g., gouty arthritis and type 2 diabetes.226 Moreover, small molecule NLRP3 inhibitors, such as MCC950227 and CY-09,228 have achieved healthspan extension in mouse models, which are an 18-month-old mouse model and triple-transgenic Alzheimer’s disease (AD) mouse model, respectively. All of these preclinical findings suggest the potential therapeutic value of NLRP3 inhibitors in anti-aging interventions. Among caspase-1 inhibitors, both the synthetic compound VX-765229 and the endogenous metabolite β-hydroxybutyrate (β-HB)230 have demonstrated significant efficacy in mitigating aging-associated pathologies, particularly via neuroprotective mechanisms, across multiple animal models. Mechanistically, administration of VX-765 for one month prior to symptom onset could prevent the development of memory impairments in transgenic J20 mice with AD while also ameliorating age-associated cognitive decline in wild-type controls.229 In a rat model of distal middle cerebral artery occlusion, VX-765 significantly mitigated secondary thalamic damage and β-amyloid deposition by suppressing neuroinflammation, ultimately improving sensory and cognitive functional recovery.231 As the primary physiological source of β-HB, the ketogenic diet characterized by high fat and very low carbohydrate intake has been reported to prolong the healthspan and lifespan of adult mice.232 β-HB, one of the predominant ketone bodies circulating in human blood, has been extensively reviewed by Han et al. for its multifaceted therapeutic mechanisms, including not only NLRP3 inflammasome suppression but also histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibition, G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) modulation, and epigenetic regulation, demonstrating the therapeutic potential of β-HB in promoting longevity, suppressing tumors, protecting neuronal function, and improving metabolic homeostasis.233 Furthermore, similar to the IL-1β neutralizing antibody canakinumab, IL-18 signaling can be antagonized by its specific inhibitor, IL-18 binding protein (IL-18BP). As early as 2003, Gangemi et al. reported that centenarians exhibited elevated levels of IL-18BP, a natural antagonist of IL-18, suggesting its protective role in vascular health and indicating its potential as a biomarker for therapeutic response.234 Tadekinig alfa, a high-affinity recombinant IL-18BP with solid clinical evidence, shows therapeutic potential for inflammation-associated conditions such as aging and cancer.235

IL-6/JAK/STAT3 signaling axis and aging

As depicted in Fig. 6, NF-κB acts upstream to promote IL-6 expression, which then activates JAK/STAT3 signaling via receptor binding, linking these pathways in inflammatory and immune responses. The IL-6/JAK/STAT3 signaling axis primarily modulates cellular processes through three key mechanisms: (i) nuclear transcriptional regulation (e.g., proinflammatory and survival gene expression); (ii) cell cycle arrest (e.g., via p21 and p16 regulation); and (iii) mitochondrial dynamics. The IL-6/JAK/STAT3 signaling axis has consistently attracted attention in antiaging research due to its key roles in mediating proinflammatory responses and promoting cellular senescence. In addition to the IL-6R neutralizing antibody tocilizumab mentioned above, JAK inhibitors and STAT3 inhibitors have been investigated in preclinical trials for their anti-aging effects. For instance, in preclinical studies using a progeria mouse model, the FDA-approved JAK1/2 inhibitor ruxolitinib was repurposed to mitigate aging-related phenotypes.236 Moreover, AG490, a JAK2 inhibitor, has been reported to prevent stress-induced senescence of umbilical cord-derived mesenchymal stem cells (UC-MSCs) in both cell and mouse models, thereby preserving the antifibrotic therapeutic potential of UC-MSCs.237 Intriguingly, natural bioactive compounds (e.g., resveratrol and curcumin) exhibit antiaging potential by modulating STAT3 signaling. In addition to its AMPK-dependent mechanisms discussed above, resveratrol concurrently inhibits STAT3 signaling to achieve complementary therapeutic effects. Resveratrol has been reported to ameliorate inflammation by inhibiting JAK2/STAT3 signaling in glioblastoma cells and intestinal epithelial cells in vitro.238,239 Similarly, curcumin, a bioactive component of turmeric, has been reported to modulate neuroinflammatory responses by suppressing proinflammatory activation of microglia through regulation of the JAK2/STAT3 signaling axis in vitro.240 In terms of mitochondrial STAT3 (mtSTAT3) regulation in aging modulation, Zukowski et al. reported that inhibiting mtSTAT3 with the mitochondrion-targeted curcuminoid Mtcur-1 reduced age-related mitochondrial branching, complex II activity, and oxidative phosphorylation in CD4+ T cells from older adults while also lowering proinflammatory cytokine production from T helper 17 cells.241 These results suggest that targeting mitoSTAT3 could mitigate T-cell dysfunction during aging by restoring mitochondrial dynamics and reducing inflammation.241

TGF-β/SMAD pathway and aging

As early as 1993, Zhou et al. demonstrated elevated secretion of both TGF-β and IL-6 in spleen cells isolated from aged mice, suggesting these cytokines as potential mediators of immunosenescence.242 In aged mice, TGF-β depletion decreased effector CD8+ T-a apoptosis and restored their polyfunctional capacity.243 Sustained TGF-β signaling activation induces irreversible cell cycle arrest and subsequent cellular senescence through transcriptional upregulation of cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors p15 and p21, ultimately blocking cell cycle progression244 (Fig. 6). Research on the TGF-β/SMAD pathway in the field of aging has increased significantly in recent years, establishing it as a core regulatory mechanism in cellular senescence, tissue fibrosis, and age-related diseases.245 In a mouse model of Duchenne muscular dystrophy, bone-targeted TGF-β antibody (PCT-011) treatment demonstrated beneficial effects on bone development, suggesting its potential therapeutic value in mitigating age-related bone fragility.246 SMAD7 is a crucial negative regulator of the TGF-β/SMAD pathway, functioning primarily by inhibiting the activation of downstream SMAD2/3.247 Using a Smad7 knockout mouse model, a recent study revealed that loss of Smad7 exacerbates TGF-β-induced fibrosis and inflammation, thereby accelerating salivary gland aging and dysfunction.248 This finding underscores the protective role of SMAD7 in attenuating age-related decline.248 Despite compelling preclinical evidence supporting SMAD7 as a promising target, no therapeutic strategies currently exist to directly activate SMAD7 for anti-aging applications. Additionally, verteporfin has been reported to effectively disrupt the interaction between SMAD2/3 and SMAD4, thereby inhibiting TGF-β signal transduction. This mechanism ultimately suppresses aging-related tissue fibrosis and tumorigenesis, which suggests that SMAD2/3 and SMAD4 could be potential antiaging therapeutic targets.249

Overall, inflammation-associated pathways with NF-κB signaling serving as the central regulatory axis are highly complex and play a critical role in the aging process. Although numerous anti-aging targets related to these pathways have been discussed above, current research on therapeutic interventions remains largely confined to preclinical studies. This underscores both the promising potential of targeting this core signaling pillar and the need for further extensive investigation.

Inflammatory and microenvironmental signaling pathways and cancer

Given that several anti-inflammatory drugs are well established in cancer care, the following review of this core signaling pillar and cancer will focus on key inflammatory pathway targets that have entered clinical practice or clinical trials. With regard to NF-κB signaling, bortezomib, the first FDA-approved proteasome inhibitor for multiple myeloma, exerts part of its antineoplastic effects through a pharmacological mechanism that targets the NF-κB signaling pathway. This involves inhibiting proteasome-mediated IκB degradation, thereby suppressing NF-κB activation and its proinflammatory and prosurvival outputs.250 Additionally, bortezomib modulates several events involved in the stress response and genome maintenance pathways (Fig. 5), including stabilization of the tumor suppressor p53,251 upregulation of proapoptotic NOXA,252 and activation of caspase cascades,253 which collectively enhance its antitumor efficacy. In terms of the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway, a landmark analysis of the CANTOS trial (NCT01327846, ClinicalTrials.gov, 2019) revealed that targeted anti-inflammatory therapy with canakinumab, an IL-1β inhibitor, led to a marked decrease in fatal lung cancer cases and overall lung cancer incidence. This finding, derived from a large, randomized, placebo-controlled phase III study, validates the NLRP3 inflammasome and its downstream effector IL-1β as critical mediators of cancer progression and promising therapeutic targets for cancer interception and treatment.254,255 Regarding the IL-6/JAK/STAT3 signaling axis, siltuximab is an FDA-approved monoclonal antibody that targets and inhibits IL-6 for the treatment of multicentric Castleman’s disease, a disease characterized by a severe systemic inflammatory syndrome.256 However, in various clinical trials investigating its antitumor efficacy, siltuximab monotherapy demonstrated limited clinical activity, prompting a strategic shift toward exploring its role in combination therapies.257 Moreover, unlike direct anti-neoplastic agents, tocilizumab, a humanized monoclonal antibody antagonist of IL-6R, was approved by the FDA in 2017 as a critical adjuvant therapy for mitigating cytokine release syndrome, a severe and potentially life-threatening inflammatory complication arising from novel immunotherapies such as chimeric antigen receptor-T-cell (CAR-T) therapy.258 Furthermore, ruxolitinib, a pioneering JAK inhibitor and the first FDA-approved agent for intermediate- and high-risk myelofibrosis, represents a milestone in cancer therapy by modulating the tumor microenvironment through suppression of systemic inflammation in this rare and lethal myeloproliferative neoplasm.259 Lahiri et al. employed a triple-negative breast cancer cell model and demonstrated that mtSTAT3 promotes antioxidant gene expression by sustaining complex I activity and NAD+ levels, which inhibits ROS accumulation and ultimately enhances cancer cell survival and metastatic ability.260 Furthermore, employing both in vitro cancer cell lines and in vivo mouse xenograft models, Genini et al. demonstrated that the STAT3 inhibitor OPB-51602 targets mtSTAT3, inducing mitochondrial dysfunction and eliciting a synthetic lethality effect under glucose-deprived tumor microenvironment conditions, ultimately leading to the elimination of tumor-initiating cells and suppression of tumor progression.261 For the TGF-β/SMAD pathway, no drugs targeting this pathway have yet been approved for cancer treatment. However, two investigational agents, fresolimumab, a pan-TGF-β neutralizing antibody, and galunisertib, a small-molecule TβRI kinase inhibitor, have shown promising results in clinical trials. For instance, a phase II clinical trial (NCT01401062, ClinicalTrials.gov, 2019) investigating the effects of the combination of fresolimumab and radiotherapy in treating metastatic breast cancer reported that patients receiving a higher dose of fresolimumab (10 mg/kg) exhibited a significantly improved median OS compared to those receiving a lower dose (1 mg/kg).262 This survival benefit was associated with enhanced systemic immune responses, including an increase in peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) counts and a notable expansion of the CD8+ central memory T-cell pool.262 The anticancer efficacy of galunisertib has been assessed in several clinical trials involving patients with various cancer types in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs),263 sorafenib,264 or gemcitabine265 in recent years.266 Prolonged OS was observed with the galunisertib-sorafenib combination compared to sorafenib alone in patients with advanced HCC264 (NCT01246986, ClinicalTrials.gov, 2021) as well as with the galunisertib-gemcitabine combination compared to gemcitabine alone in patients with unresectable pancreatic cancer265 (NCT01373164, ClinicalTrials.gov, 2018).

Inflammatory and microenvironmental signaling pathways in modulating aging and cancer

As illustrated in Fig. 6, while the outputs of inflammatory and microenvironmental signaling pathways uniformly accelerate aging, their impact on cancer progression is heterogeneous. These pathways exert oncogenic effects primarily by fostering a protumorigenic microenvironment, largely mediated through the SASP. Furthermore, transcriptional upregulation of antiapoptotic BCL-2, immunosuppressive PD-L1, proangiogenic VEGF, and extracellular matrix-degrading MMPs further supports tumor progression. In addition, mtSTAT3 is implicated in the metabolic reprogramming of mitochondria, which contributes to cancer cell survival. Simultaneously, these pathways exert tumor-suppressive effects by inducing irreversible cell cycle arrest in cancer cells through upregulation of p15, p16, and p21. However, the subsequent accumulation of senescent cells exhibiting the SASP can transform the microenvironment into a pro-oncogenic state, ultimately becoming detrimental.267 In summary, among the four pillars, inflammatory and microenvironmental signaling pathways exhibit a relatively higher degree of consistency in regulating both aging and cancer. Targeting these pathways may therefore represent a promising strategy for dual-purpose intervention in aging and cancer.

Sirtuin-mediated epigenetic and transcriptional modulation pathways

According to a recent review by Wu et al.268 the Sir2 family comprises seven mammalian homologs, known as SIRT1 to SIRT7. As depicted in Fig. 7, sirtuins are all NAD+-dependent deacetylases, which means that their enzymatic activity is fundamentally coupled to cellular metabolic status, as they require NAD+ as an essential cofactor.269 Therefore, the cellular levels of NAD⁺ are the primary determinant of sirtuin activity. NAD⁺ is not consumed from the diet directly but is biosynthesized de novo from its precursor molecules, including nicotinic acid (NA), nicotinamide (NAM), nicotinamide riboside (NR), nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN), and tryptophan.270 Here, we use caloric restriction as an example, as caloric restriction is a powerful intervention that delays the onset of age-related diseases and promotes longevity.271 In response to a declining ATP pool and a rising AMP/ATP ratio, AMPK becomes activated (Section “Nutrient-sensing and energy metabolism pathways”). SIRT1-mediated modulation in regulating aging and cancer

Sirtuins are a class of proteins widely recognized for their link to longevity, with SIRT1 being the most extensively studied and best-characterized member.273 The anti-aging effects of SIRT1 are mediated through distinct pathways involving both epigenetic and transcriptional modulation (Fig. 7). In terms of SIRT1-mediated epigenetic modulation, the enzyme deacetylates histones at multiple sites. Among these, the deacetylation of promoter-associated H3K9 and H4K16, along with the resulting transcriptional repression, has been particularly well characterized.274,275 In 2004, Vaquero et al. provided for the first time direct molecular evidence that SIRT1-induced deacetylation of both core (H4K16, H3K9) and linker (H1K26) histones is a causative mechanism driving chromatin compaction and subsequent gene silencing.276 For instance, Wan et al. reported that in both senescent human umbilical vein endothelial cells and aged mouse aortas, overexpression of SIRT1 significantly reduced plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1) expression.277 This downregulation coincided with reductions in SA-β-gal activity in vitro and arterial stiffness in vivo, as well as an improvement in endothelial function in vivo.277 Mechanistic studies further revealed that SIRT1 directly binds to the PAI-1 promoter region, where it epigenetically suppresses PAI-1 transcription by decreasing the acetylation level of H4K16.277 Taken together, these findings demonstrate that SIRT1 exerts protective effects against vascular endothelial senescence by transcriptionally inhibiting PAI-1 expression through H4K16 deacetylation-mediated epigenetic regulation.277 In addition, by adopting the human hepatocyte line HL-7702 and an isoniazid-induced injury model, Zhang et al. demonstrated that SIRT1 activation by SRT1720 ameliorates hepatocyte damage. The protective mechanism involves SIRT1-mediated deacetylation of histone H3K9 at the IL-6 promoter, which suppresses IL-6 overexpression and subsequent inflammation,278 which further supports the anti-aging effect of SIRT1. However, the SIRT1-mediated epigenetic modulation of cancer is heterogeneous. For instance, Wang et al. identified the SIRT1/miR-1185-1/CD24 regulatory axis both in vitro and in vivo in which SIRT1 epigenetically represses miR-1185-1 transcription through H3K9 deacetylation, which in turn relieves translational suppression of CD24, a cancer stem cell marker,279 and ultimately promotes colorectal cancer stemness and tumorigenesis, which provides a potential epigenetic target for the treatment of colorectal cancer.280 In contrast, a study utilizing models of diabetic retinopathy, including retinal endothelial cells, diabetic mice, and human donor retinal microvasculature, demonstrated that the inhibition of SIRT1 induced by oxidative stress in hyperglycemia leads to hyperacetylation of the p65 subunit of NF-κB, enhancing its binding to the matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) promoter, which subsequently activates MMP-9 expression and activity.281 The resulting mitochondrial damage and apoptosis contribute to the pathogenesis of diabetic retinopathy. Crucially, pharmacological activation of SIRT1 with resveratrol prevented this pathological cascade.281 This finding provides an implication for cancer therapy by highlighting SIRT1 as a central node linking oxidative stress to key pro-oncogenic factors such as NF-κB and MMP-9. This suggests that SIRT1 agonists (e.g., resveratrol) could represent a novel therapeutic strategy for cancer by modulating the tumor microenvironment to suppress metastasis and angiogenesis.

Regarding SIRT1-mediated transcriptional modulation, as a deacetylase, SIRT1 activates or represses transcriptional regulators through deacetylation, thereby exerting both positive and negative control over gene expression. Numerous studies have established that SIRT1 activates transcription factors such as FOXO and PGC-1α while inhibiting key regulators, including p65 (a central component of the NF-κB pathway) and p53 (a critical transcription factor encoded by the tumor suppressor gene TP53). Furthermore, its regulatory effect on HIF-1α appears to be context dependent, exhibiting both activating and inhibitory functions under different physiological conditions. Through these actions, SIRT1 indirectly modulates its downstream signaling pathways, exerting an overall inhibitory effect on aging but a context-dependent influence on cancer development (Fig. 7). Brunet et al. demonstrated that in mammalian cells, SIRT1 binds to and deacetylates the transcription factor FOXO3 under oxidative stress. This deacetylation event differentially modulates FOXO3 activity: it augments stress resistance and promotes cell cycle arrest through upregulation of p27 expression while simultaneously suppressing FOXO3-mediated apoptosis. These findings imply that SIRT1 activation may foster longevity by redirecting cellular responses toward survival and repair mechanisms, thereby enhancing resilience to age-related stresses. In the context of cancer, the role of SIRT1 appears dualistic: it may contribute to cancer prevention by inhibiting the proliferation of damaged cells, yet it could also support tumor survival and confer therapy resistance in established cancers by attenuating apoptotic pathways.282 The regulatory role of the SIRT1/FOXO pathway in autophagy and mitophagy exhibits divergent, context-dependent functions in normal versus cancer cells. Wu et al. demonstrated that the activation of the SIRT1/FOXO pathway enhances autophagy, as indicated by an elevated LC3-II/I ratio, which reduces the secretion of pro-thrombotic factors in an endothelial cell model.283 Likewise, berberine has been demonstrated to activate the SIRT1/FOXO3/BNIP3 signaling pathway, thereby enhancing cellular autophagy and mitophagy, which are processes essential for clearing damaged mitochondria accumulated in renal tubules under hyperglycemic conditions. Ultimately, this mechanism exerts a protective effect on renal cells.284 In contrast, in HCC cells and patient-derived xenograft models, the novel CDK9 inhibitor Oroxylin A was found to suppress the SIRT1/FOXO3-mediated mitophagy pathway by inducing SIRT1 dephosphorylation and inactivation, promoting FOXO3 protein degradation, and subsequently inhibiting BNIP3 transcription and the PTEN-induced putative kinase 1 (PINK1)/Parkin pathway, thereby effectively blocking mitophagy initiation, disrupting mitochondrial homeostasis, and ultimately significantly inhibiting tumor proliferation and overcoming drug resistance.285 Similarly, Wei et al. reported that in endometrial cancer (EC), SIRT1 interacts with and deacetylates FOXO3, enhancing its transcriptional activity and promoting BNIP3 expression and PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy. This pathway drives tumor progression and hormone resistance both in vitro and in vivo, identifying SIRT1 as a key upstream regulator and potential therapeutic target in EC.286 As mentioned in Section “Nutrient-sensing and energy metabolism pathways”, PGC-1α is a well-established master regulator that coordinates mitochondrial biogenesis and antioxidant defense.287 Its activity is principally activated through deacetylation by SIRT1.288 However, the actual roles of PGC-1α in regulating tumorigenesis are complicated and heterogeneous. In intestinal epithelial cells, PGC-1α has been observed to promote mitochondrial respiration while simultaneously suppressing antioxidant capacity, leading to the accumulation of ROS and triggering apoptosis, thereby serving as a tumor-suppressive mechanism to prevent carcinogenesis.288 However, extensive research has established that PGC-1α often plays a tumor-promoting role in cancer. While the Warburg effect highlights glycolysis in cancer progression, emerging evidence indicates that malignant cells can exploit PGC-1α to augment mitochondrial OXPHOS, thereby shifting their metabolic dependency away from glycolysis.289 An in vitro study utilizing a panel of human cancer cell lines demonstrated that in cancer cells with wild-type p53, the SIRT1/PGC-1α/nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) signaling axis exhibits pro-oncogenic functions.290 The antidiabetic drug metformin can activate p53 and induce miR-34a expression, thereby suppressing this axis and ultimately sensitizing cancer cells to oxidative stress and to apoptosis induced by TRAIL, a cytokine that selectively triggers programmed cell death in cancer cells.290 Moreover, by deacetylating the p65 subunit, SIRT1 effectively inhibits the NF-κB signaling pathway and its inflammatory responses. This established action highlights its promise as a molecular target for anti-inflammatory therapy.291 Furthermore, SIRT1 deacetylates and inhibits p53 in an NAD+-dependent manner, thereby downregulating downstream effectors such as p21 and proapoptotic factors (e.g., BAX and BCL2) to modulate cell fate with the consequence of antiaging and tumor-promoting effects.292 Regarding the dual effects of SIRT1 deacetylation on HIF-1α, for instance, under aging or hypoxic conditions, decreased SIRT1 expression and activity has been reported to lead to enhanced acetylation of HIF-1α at Lys709, which upregulates profibrotic and proapoptotic genes, ultimately resulting in tubulointerstitial damage characterized by fibrosis and apoptosis,293 which means that pharmacological activation of SIRT1 to inhibit HIF-1α activity is proposed as a potential therapeutic strategy for mitigating age-related renal injury. However, Joo et al. demonstrated that under hypoxia, SIRT1 binds to and deacetylates HIF-1α, thereby preventing its proteasomal degradation. This stabilization enhances the expression of pro-invasive target genes (e.g., VEGF, GLUT1, MMP2), promoting cancer cell invasion294 (Fig. 7).

Other sirtuin-mediated modulations in regulating aging and cancer

As depicted in Fig. 7, SIRT2 is localized in both the cell cytoplasm and nucleus.295 Recently, Pun et al. reviewed the SIRT2-mediated deacetylation of BubR1, systematically elaborating a central IRT2/BubR1 signaling axis and revealing how this pathway serves as a molecular bridge connecting aging and cancer by maintaining genomic stability.296 Furthermore, in addition to stabilizing BubR1, SIRT2 contributes to the suppression of aneuploidy by deacetylating α-tubulin, thereby ensuring proper microtubule dynamics and accurate chromosome segregation during mitosis.297 In terms of nuclear SIRT2, Serrano et al. demonstrated that SIRT2 is a central regulator in maintaining genomic stability. The mechanism involves the deacetylation of H4K16Ac and the direct regulation of the activity of PR-Set7, a nucleosome-specific methyltransferase, and chromatin localization, thereby ensuring the proper deposition of H4K20me1. Loss of SIRT2 function leads to widespread epigenetic dysregulation, accumulation of DNA damage, and chromosomal abnormalities, ultimately significantly elevating cancer risk.298

SIRT3 is a sirtuin that primarily localizes to the mitochondria and functions as a major mitochondrial deacetylase, modulating mitochondrial biogenesis.299 Due to their shared roles in regulating mitochondrial-related processes, SIRT3 signaling exhibits crosstalk with SIRT1 signaling (Fig. 7). SIRT1 deacetylates FOXO transcription factors, which subsequently activate the expression of superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2) and catalase (CAT) genes.300,301 Moreover, the activity of SOD2 can be positively regulated by SIRT3.302 The deacetylation of SOD2 significantly boosts its antioxidant activity, leading to more effective protection of mitochondria against oxidative damage.302 Similar to the context-dependent dual roles of the SIRT1/PGC1α axis in tumorigenesis, the effects of SIRT3-mediated mitochondrial biogenesis on cancer are heterogeneous. While it is generally beneficial for longevity, its impact on cancer development depends on whether it occurs in normal or cancerous cells.303

SIRT6 is a histone deacetylase with known targets at lysine 9 and lysine 56 on histone H3 (H3K9 and H3K56).304 Kawahara et al. confirmed that SIRT6 represses NF-κB signaling by deacetylating histone H3K9 at the promoters of NF-κB target genes. Haploinsufficiency of RelA rescues the premature aging phenotypes in Sirt6-deficient mice, suggesting that hyperactivation of NF-κB signaling may represent a key mechanism underlying aging.305 In 2008, Michishita et al. established for the first time that SIRT6-mediated H3K9 deacetylation is crucial for telomere maintenance and protection against premature aging.306 Moreover, SIRT6 is termed a DSB sensor due to its sensitivity to DNA repair, which is important for longevity and cancer prevention.307 Zhong et al. demonstrated that SIRT6 inhibits glycolytic gene expression by deacetylating H3K9 and interacting with HIF-1α to suppress its transcriptional activity.308 This action maintains the flow of glucose into the mitochondrial TCA cycle under normal nutrient conditions.308 This study implies that SIRT6 is a central switch governing cellular metabolic modes that suppress tumorigenesis.

As the sole sirtuin family member predominantly localized to the nucleolus, SIRT7 positively regulates RNA Polymerase I (Pol I) transcription,309,310 which is crucial for alleviating nucleolar stress and has been implicated in preventing premature aging.311 SIRT7 is an NAD⁺-dependent histone deacetylase that specifically targets acetylated lysine 18 on histone H3 (H3K18Ac). Barber et al. reported that by interacting with the oncogenic transcription factor ELK4, a member of the ETS family, SIRT7 binds to the promoter regions of specific target genes, most of which are associated with tumor suppressor functions. It deacetylates H3K18Ac at these sites, resulting in transcriptional repression of these genes.312 Clinically, hypoacetylation of H3K18Ac is closely linked to aggressive tumor phenotypes and poor patient prognosis312 (Fig. 7). In contrast, Ianni et al. recently revealed that in response to UV stress, studies in both cultured mammalian cells and mouse models have demonstrated that SIRT7-mediated deacetylation of nucleophosmin (NPM), a nucleolar protein released from the nucleolus in response to stress stimuli, is essential for stabilizing the tumor suppressor p53, ultimately promoting cell cycle arrest and DNA repair to suppress tumorigenesis.313

In summary, as reviewed above, the role of sirtuin proteins in anti-aging is well established. However, their functions in cancer are highly heterogeneous and context-dependent and influenced by factors such as cell type (e.g., cancerous vs. normal somatic cells), tissue of origin, and cancer-specific characteristics. Therefore, prior to considering sirtuin-based anti-aging applications, it is essential to precisely evaluate and define their roles within specific physiological or pathological settings.

Drug targets in the sirtuin-mediated pathway

As illustrated in Fig. 7, NAD⁺ is the essential molecular initiator of all sirtuin-mediated pathways. Consequently, NAD⁺ supplementation has been extensively studied in numerous preclinical and clinical trials aimed at activating sirtuins for anti-aging intervention or anticancer purposes. To date, common NAD+ supplementations, including NR, NMN, and the combination of NR with pterostilbene (PT, a polyphenol found in blueberries) (NRPT), represent the primary NAD⁺-boosting interventions that have entered clinical evaluation.314 In 2023, Damgaard, M.V. and J.T. Treebak evaluated 25 studies on oral NR supplementation in humans and concluded that, albeit showing few clinically relevant effects, its anti-inflammatory properties position NR as a promising therapeutic candidate for a range of severe inflammation-related diseases.315 As recently updated by Song et al., of the 10 published clinical trials, NMN has been shown to be safe in the short term, significantly increase blood NAD⁺ levels, and demonstrate potential antiaging effects in areas such as muscle function, insulin sensitivity, and telomere length; however, more large-scale, long-term trials are needed to confirm its overall antiaging efficacy and safety.316 In terms of NRPT, the first human clinical trial on NRPT, reported by Dellinger et al., in 2017 demonstrated that it is safe and effectively boosts NAD⁺ levels in older adults in a sustained, dose-dependent manner.317 However, more recently, a clinical trial involving 32 elderly individuals found that although safe, daily supplementation with NRPT (1000 mg NR + 200 mg PT) did not improve muscle stem cell function or muscle regeneration recovery after injury. (NCT03754842) (ClinicalTrials.gov, 2020).318 In addition to NAD+ precursor supplementation, resveratrol, which has been reviewed as an AMPK pathway activator and a JAK2/STAT3 pathway inhibitor exhibiting both anticancer and antiaging effects (Table 1), has also been reported to protect vascular health from oxidative stress as a SIRT1 agonist in preclinical studies. However, due to their low oral bioavailability, scientists are actively seeking resveratrol-like compounds as more viable SIRT1 activators.319 Moreover, as the classic AMPK pathway activator, metformin has been demonstrated to indirectly activate sirtuin pathways (Table 1). Furthermore, NAMPT, the rate-limiting enzyme in the NAD⁺ salvage pathway, has been demonstrated to be an antiaging target in preclinical studies.320 Gong et al. identified a novel senescence-associated miRNA, miR-146a, which negatively regulates the NAD⁺/SIRT pathway by directly targeting NAMPT, while AMPK activators such as metformin counteract this process by suppressing miR-146a expression through the IKK/NF-κB axis, thereby restoring NAD⁺ production and offering a new therapeutic strategy against aging.320 In conclusion, drug discovery targeting sirtuin pathways represents a vibrant and promising field in the areas of antiaging and anticancer therapeutics. However, most related investigations remain at the preclinical stage. At present, maintaining a healthy lifestyle, such as adhering to a balanced diet and engaging in moderate exercise, remains the most evidence-based and safest approach to delaying aging and preventing disease.321–323

Impact of aging on cancer therapies

To date, cancer therapies have developed into the following main categories: (i) surgical operations, (ii) chemotherapy, and (iii) radiotherapy, which are the most common tools for cancer treatment324; (iv) targeted therapy, which utilizes small molecule drugs or monoclonal antibodies to target, control, and attack cancer cells accurately so that normal cells can be protected from attack as much as possible325; (v) stem cell and bone marrow transplants, which are treatment options for cancers affecting the blood and lymphatic system326; (vi) endocrine therapy, which is adopted for treating hormone-sensitive cancers327; (vii) thermal ablation mainly including cryoablation (extreme hypothermia) and radiofrequency ablation (extreme hyperthermia); and (viii) photodynamic therapy,328 both of which are cancer therapeutic procedures with minimal invasion, and (ix) immunotherapy, which will be reviewed later in detail. Due to the wide application of cancer therapies (i)–(iii) in elderly patients, there have been abundant reliable clinical data about the treatment outcomes of these three cancer therapies in elderly individuals. In one multicenter study of over 8000 participants, elevated cancer surgery-associated mortality and the possibility of complication development were observed in patients aged 75 years or older compared to younger patients329; aged cancer patients were found to be more susceptible to chemotherapy accompanying side effects, and corresponding findings have been reviewed elsewhere.330 In terms of radiotherapy, the effects of aging on radiotherapy have been controversial, as some studies reported that there was no age limit for radiotherapy,331 while others showed that age was a factor that increased the vulnerability of patients to radiotherapy toxicity.332 Daste et al. reviewed the data from pivotal clinical trials of people from different age subgroups receiving targeted cancer therapies and found that people aged 65 years or older shared similarities with their younger counterparts in therapy tolerance and efficacy; however, a relatively higher incidence rate of adverse events in some specific targeted therapies was observed in the aged group.333 Cupit-Link et al. reviewed the relationships between hematopoietic stem cell transplants and aging and concluded that this cancer therapy accelerated premature aging.334 Meanwhile, the elderly who had received stem cell transplants were reported to be more susceptible to infections because of immunosenescence during long-term survival.334 Normally, endocrine therapy is a treatment option for hormone receptor-positive breast cancer. Elderly patients with hormone receptor-positive breast cancer were found to benefit equally from the treatment as young patients.335 Moreover, primary endocrine therapy has been proposed to be an alternative to primary surgery for breast cancer treatment in frail elderly individuals.336 Due to the advantages of minimally invasive operations, thermal ablation has been suggested to be an alternative to invasive surgical operations in the elderly with breast cancer,337 whereas photodynamic therapy has been confirmed to be effective as salvage therapy after the failure of radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy in aged patients with esophageal cancer.338 Overall, for some cancer therapies, chronological age is considered to be a factor that may correlate with a higher incidence of cancer treatment-related side effects and adverse events. Therefore, comprehensive geriatric assessment has been acknowledged to be essential for minimizing the side effects and adverse events of cancer treatment regimens in the aged population.339 The other therapies with specific advantages were found to be superior to the common strategies in treating elderly patients but limited to some specific types of cancer.

Aging and immunotherapies

The efficacy of immunotherapy, a revolutionary pillar of oncological treatment, appears to be uniquely and paradoxically influenced by the process of aging. Since the approval of the first immunotherapy, interleukin-2 immunotherapy, by the FDA in the late 1990s,340 immunotherapy, a type of novel cancer therapy taking advantage of the immune system to suppress cancer development, has greatly improved cancer treatment prospects and progressively developed into five subtypes: (i) immune system modulators including various immunomodulatory cytokines, (ii) anticancer vaccines, (iii) ICIs including anti-programmed cell death-1 (anti-PD-1), anti-programmed cell death-ligand 1 (anti-PD-L1) and anti-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein-4 (anti-CTLA-4), (iv) T-cell transfer therapy including tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte therapy and CAR-T-cell therapy, and (v) monoclonal antibodies. Immunotherapy has initiated a revolution in the field of oncological treatment due to its promising effectiveness in extending the survival time of patients with fatal cancers.341 However, current immunotherapies have been reported to benefit only a limited number of patients. Resistance to immunotherapy manifests as patients showing no response at the beginning of treatment (i.e., primary resistance) or gradually developing immunotherapy resistance during the treatment process (i.e., acquired resistance).341 In recent years, scientists have been devoted to exploring the complicated mechanisms underlying immunotherapy resistance, among which the diminished antitumor T-cell immune response due to the decreased antitumor T-cell number and/or quality is a convincing driving factor.342 Therefore, the efficacy of immunotherapy in the aging population has drawn the attention of researchers because there is abundant evidence indicating that T-cell immunity gradually declines with age,343 manifesting a defective T-cell repertoire in cell number and diversity,344 impaired T-cell priming345 and signaling,346 etc., whereas the immunosuppressive network is activated as aging progresses with the involvement of immunosuppressive regulatory T cells (Tregs), MDSCs and macrophages.347 However, what is intriguing is that scientists have obtained ambiguous conclusions regarding the effects of aging on cancer immunotherapy from limited available data. In a comparative study of young and aged mice with triple-negative breast cancer, age was observed to worsen immune dysfunction and limit ICI treatment efficacy, which was probably attributed to the failure to activate innate immunity caused by downregulated interferon signaling and antigen presentation in aged mice.348 In another animal study of mice bearing orthotopic melanoma, in terms of tumor growth inhibition, aged mice responded comparably to anti-PD-1 treatment but showed less response to anti-CTLA-4 and even no response to anti-PD-L1 compared to young mice.343 In addition, the ability of all three ICIs to trigger T-cell activation in aged mice was found to be impaired.343 Surprisingly, most clinical studies have reported that aging has no influence or even positive effects on immunotherapy outcomes. Betof et al. reported that anti-PD-1 and anti-PD-L1 treatment could be safely tolerated and achieve comparable outcomes irrespective of age after their clinical retrospective research of patients with metastatic melanoma (n = 254) treated with anti-PD-1/PD-L1 agents.349 In a comparative study with a sample size of 538 melanoma patients who had received anti-PD-1 treatment, patients aged 62 or older were found to have a greater benefit from anti-PD-1 than their younger counterparts, which could be attributed to the comparatively lower proportion of FOXP3+ Tregs in the aged group.350 Similarly, in another retrospective study, the survival of patients with metastatic melanoma who had received immunotherapy (n = 2930) was demonstrated to be significantly associated with age, and patients aged 60 or older responded better to immunotherapy.351 Furthermore, a meta-analysis of over 10,000 patients from n = 19 clinical trials confirmed the age-dependent magnitude of response to ICIs, which means that aging brings more benefits to cancer patients in response to ICIs.352 However, due to the underrepresentation of aged cancer patients in most clinical trials, which could make the analysis lack statistical power, and the possible inconsistency in statistical analysis approaches among different studies, further research is required to evaluate immunotherapy efficacy in the aged population compared with their younger counterparts.353

Impact of aging on immunotherapies: underlying mechanisms of genetic and signaling pathway regulation

Current findings on the effects of aging on responses to immunotherapy concluded from limited clinical trials to some extent conflicts with speculations. Therefore, research findings in recent years on how aging affects the immunotherapy response via the dysregulation of aging-related genes and signaling pathways deserve to be reviewed and discussed. As a term describing the impact of aging on immunity,354 immunosenescence is a prominent factor that hinders cancer patients from benefiting from immunotherapy. For instance, in the bloodstream of patients with advanced NSCLC, circulating T cells with a senescent immune phenotype have been reported to correlate with reduced benefit from anti-PD-1/PD-L1.355 The senescence of responder T cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME), which can be induced by Tregs and malignant tumor cells in both human356 and mouse357 systems, has been demonstrated to be reversed by blocking DNA damage and/or MAPK signaling to enhance the antitumor efficacy of anti-PD-L1 in melanoma mouse models.357 In an editorial published in 2009, the authors highlighted the involvement of IGF-1 signaling, p53 signaling, and inflammatory pathways in the effects of aging on cancer immunity and immunotherapy.358 Recently, Zhang et al. analyzed publicly available multiomics data from over 12,000 human tumor samples from 33 different cancers to identify immunosenescence pathways by enriching 90 reliable immunosenescence-related genes.359 Among their reconstructed pancancer immunosenescence pathways, immunosenescence-related genes, including ATM, TP53, MTOR, BCL2, IGF1R, JAK2, STAT3, TNF, etc., with relatively high copy number variations and/or somatic mutations, are involved in more than one immunosenescence pathway.359 These genes/signaling pathways show substantial overlap with those central to the four pillars of aging and cancer. In the following sections, we will delve into the intricate roles of selected key genes and pathways, including IGF-1/IGF-1R signaling, MTOR, ATM, TP53, BCL2, NF-κB, and STAT3 signaling, examining how their definition as aging-related factors, their complex involvement in immune modulation, and their emerging applications in therapeutic strategies collectively shape the paradoxical response to immunotherapy in the aged population.

IGF-1/IGF-1R signaling and immunotherapy

In recent years, IGF-1R has been considered an emerging immunomodulator that regulates immune cell functions to improve immunotherapy.360 IGF-1 and IGF-1R have been confirmed as potential prognostic biomarkers of malignant cancer by analyzing IGF-1/IGF-1R expression and its effect on clinical prognosis among 33 types of malignancies; therefore, anti-IGF-1R therapy has been suggested to be adjuvant to immunotherapy.361 Moreover, Ma et al. reported that a higher frequency of IGF-1R mutations (e.g., gain-of-function alterations detected in >10% of samples in the cohort) is correlated with better clinical outcomes of immunotherapy in melanoma, which means that IGF-1R is also considered a predictive biomarker for immunotherapy-treated melanoma patients.362 Mechanistically, IGF-1/IGF-1R signaling induces immunosuppression via the STAT3 pathway, aided by SMAD3 and NF-κB crosstalk. This manifests as increased secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines (e.g., IL-10, TGF-β), elevated expression of proliferation-limiting enzymes (e.g., arginase-1) and immune checkpoints (e.g., PD-L1), ultimately potentiating immunosuppressive cells such as Tregs, regulatory NK cells (NKregs), regulatory dendritic cells (DCregs), MDSCs, and M2 macrophages.363 In summary, interfering with IGF-1/IGF-1R signaling is a beneficial strategy for delaying senescence and enhancing immunotherapy efficacy.

MTOR and immunotherapy

To date, the anticancer effects of combining mTOR inhibitors with various kinds of immunotherapies, including anticancer vaccines, ICIs, and T-cell transfer therapy, have been investigated in several preclinical studies or are under exploration in clinical trials.364 The enhanced antitumor effects of anticancer vaccinations combined with mTOR inhibitors have been confirmed in most preclinical studies utilizing different animal models. For instance, anticancer vaccination combined with temsirolimus, a novel derivative of rapamycin, significantly decreased tumor growth compared to monotherapies in melanoma and lung epithelial tumor mouse models, which was attributed to the increased proportion of tumor-specific CD8+ T cells in the TME.365 The blockade of Tregs further improved the combination therapeutic outcome, probably due to the increase in immunosuppressive Tregs induced by temsirolimus.365 In terms of immunotherapies targeting immune checkpoints, it has been demonstrated that the antitumor effects of anti-CTLA-4, anti-PD-L1 or anti-PD-1 therapy can be synergistically potentiated by vistusertib, an mTORC1/2 dual kinase inhibitor, in colorectal carcinoma mouse models.366 Combined therapy was found to lower the proportion of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells with the exhaustion marker PD-1 expression but upregulate the frequency of T helper 1 cells in tumors compared to monotherapy.366 In terms of T-cell transfer therapy, ex vivo treatment with rapamycin was demonstrated to enhance the cytotoxicity and tumor-suppressing effects of γδ T cells in a melanoma mouse model as a consequence of the upregulation of activating receptor natural killer group 2 member D (NKG2D) and TNF-α via the signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT)-5 pathways.367

ATM and immunotherapy

Canonically, ATM oversees sensing DSBs and coordinating DNA repair; therefore, inhibiting ATM has been a strategy of sensitizing cancer cells to ionizing radiation in radiotherapy.368 In addition to radiotherapy, ATM inhibition has been proposed to enhance immunotherapy via different mechanisms. Approximately one decade ago, ATM kinase deficiency in Drosophila was reported to increase the gene expression of the innate immune response,369 while ATM-deficient mice with unrepaired DSBs were found to induce Type I interferons (T1IFNs) via stimulator of interferon genes (STING)-dependent cytosolic DNA sensing pathways to enhance innate immunity against bacterial and viral infection.370 In recent years, in human and mouse pancreatic cancer models, ATM inhibition has been proven to enhance the efficacy of anti-PD-L1 by increasing the expression of MHCI antigen and PD-L1, increasing the number and functions of T cells via T1IFN-mediated innate immune signaling independent of cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)/STING.371 Moreover, another study using several human and mouse cancer cell lines revealed that the cytosolic presence of double-stranded DNA (dsDNA), which is mainly attributed to cytoplasmic leakage of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) caused by downregulated mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM) due to ATM deficiency, could trigger a T1IFN-mediated innate immune response via the cGAS/STING pathway in ATM-deficient tumor cells.372 This study suggests that ATM mutation could be a biomarker for a good response to ICIs and implies the great potential of ATM inhibitors as innate immune response activators and their coadministration with ICIs to enhance immunotherapy.372 Recently, Yu et al. reported that nanoparticles delivering ATM small interfering RNA (siRNA) could modulate the immunosuppressive TME and activate cytotoxic T cells via the cGAS/STING pathway to improve anti-PD1 efficacy in triple-negative breast cancer.373 Accordingly, the roles of ATM in restraining innate immune responses and promoting cell senescence of effector T cells emphasize the potential of ATM inhibitors in improving immunotherapy efficacy.

TP53 and immunotherapy

Considering that loss-of-function mutation of TP53 may result in immunosuppression and immunotherapy resistance, Xiao et al. engineered p53 mRNA nanoparticles specifically targeting hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) to reverse immunosuppression in TP53-deficient HCC mouse models and combined them with anti-PD-1 to potentiate treatment efficacy.374 Some distinguished features of immune TME reprogramming, including activation of cytotoxic T cells and natural killer (NK) cells, favorable polarization of tumor-suppressing M1 macrophages, increased antitumor cytokines and decreased immunosuppressive cytokines, were displayed in this combination therapy.374 Similarly, in TP53-deficient glioblastoma mouse models, tumor growth inhibition, immune response enhancement, and tumor immunogenicity increase, especially the elevated tumor cell surface expression of PD-L1, which has been suggested to reflect therapeutic response to anti-PD-1,375 was observed in combination treatment of a nanocomplex encapsulating p53 plasmid DNA with anti-PD-1 compared to anti-PD-1 blockade alone.376 In human lung adenocarcinoma, TP53 mutation has been suggested to serve as a potential predictor of a good response to checkpoint inhibitors targeting PD-L1 because missense mutations in TP53 correlate positively with elevated PD-L1 expression.377,378 In contrast, on the basis of sequencing data of metastatic solid tumors from 1770 patients, Kim et al. reported that the TP53 R175H mutation, which was the most frequent single-nucleotide variant mutation of TP53 in patients recruited, could predict a poor response to immunotherapy.379 The TP53 R175H mutation was more tumorigenic than the wild type, manifesting downregulated immune-related pathways and reduced expression of immune cells and PD-1 on cytotoxic T cells in the TME.379

BCL2 and immunotherapy

When investigating the correlations between BCL2 and immunotherapy, due to the FDA-approved use of venetoclax in treating certain leukemias and lymphomas, scientists have mainly focused on the anticancer effects of the combination administration of venetoclax and immunotherapy. Lee et al. proposed that venetoclax could be administered to patients with AML receiving adoptive T-cell transfer for its role in potentiating effector T-cell function.178 Kohlhapp et al. demonstrated that tumor-bearing mice coadministered venetoclax and anti-PD-1/PD-L1 showed restrained tumor growth compared to the ICI-treated group and an elevated proportion of intratumoral CD8+PD-1+ T effector memory cells.380 Liu et al. proposed an indirect anticancer effect of venetoclax with the aid of dendritic cells (DCs) in vivo, depicted as the imgration and antigen presentation capacity of conventional type-1 DCs being potentiated by the activation of the cGAS/STING pathway and the subsequent classical type-1 interferon pathway when BCL2 is genetically or pharmacollogically inhibited, which eventually improves the immunosurveillance capacity of T cells and enhances ICI therapeutic efficacy.381 Liu et al. found elevated expression of BCL2 in the T cells of patients diagnosed with CLL (n = 266).382 Moreover, they reported that the expression of BCL-2 in CD8 + T cells was correlated with the exhausted phenotype (CD8+PD-1+Tim-3+), and impaired cell cytotoxicity manifested as low expression of granzyme B and perforin,382 which was consistent with the previous finding reported by Mathew et al. in 2018 that venetoclax can reduce the proportion of Tregs and exhausted T cells to alleviate immunosuppression.383 Taken together, these results indicate that BCL2 inhibitors have great potential to be coadministered with immunotherapy products to potentiate the antitumor effects in two ways: one is to promote the apoptosis of senescent cells, and the other is to improve immunosurveillance.

NF-κB signaling and immunotherapy

It is known that the modulation of NF-κB signaling regulates both innate and adaptive immunity.384 To date, there has been a considerable amount of research investigating the roles of NF-κB signaling in different kinds of innate (i.e., DCs, NK cells, macrophages, MDSCs) and adaptive (i.e., B cells, effector and regulatory T cells) immune cells.385 However, whether to define NF-κB signaling in regulating cancer immunity as an ally or enemy is also inconclusive, as the same immune cell subgroup can exert both tumor-suppressing and tumor-promoting roles. Based on these observations, scientists have investigated the effects of both NF-κB enhancers or inhibitors on cancer immunity and other immunotherapies on tumor suppression. Evaristo et al. established an innovative mouse model with constitutive activation of IKKβ, which can be restricted to only CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, and then monitored tumor growth following B16. SIY melanoma cell inoculation.386 The constitutive activation of IKKβ was found to result in CD8 + T-cell-dependent tumor growth control, and this finding was further confirmed in preestablished tumor-bearing mice receiving adoptive transfer of engineered T cells with active IKKβ.386 TNFα-induced protein 3, which is encoded by TNFAIP3, can mitigate excessive inflammatory responses by inhibiting NF-κB signaling. In another similar mouse model with specific TNFAIP3 knockout in peripheral T cells, engineered mice showed potentiated antitumor ability, and elevated secretion of IL-2 and IFN-γ was confirmed from T cells isolated from engineered mice.387 Both in vivo studies highlight the potential of precise NF-κB signaling activation in T cells via gene editing for enhancing the immunotherapy efficacy of adoptive T-cell transfer. Nevertheless, compared to NF-κB signaling enhancers, more efforts are devoted to developing anticancer NF-κB inhibitors. One representative agent is curcumin, a plant-based NF-κB inhibitor, which has been confirmed in various tumor-bearing mouse models to exert antitumor effects with elevated tumor-suppressing effector immune cells, including CD8+ T cells, NK cells and M1 macrophages, and constrained immunosuppressive cells, including Tregs and MDSCs.388–390 In recent years, with the emergence of convincing evidence, the combination of NF-κB inhibitors with immunotherapies has been viewed as a promising advancement in cancer treatment. In animal studies, curcumin was found to inhibit COP9 signalosome 5 (CSN5) activated by RelA, a family member of NF-κB, to facilitate PD-L1 degradation in tumor cells to enhance immunosurveillance, which eventually potentiated anti-CTLA4 therapy in breast cancer.391 Bortezomib, an approved proteasome inhibitor that can effectively suppress NF-κB activation,250 has been coadministered with immunotherapies in preclinical and clinical studies to improve immunotherapy effectiveness in multiple myeloma. In preclinical studies, bortezomib combined with either IL-12392 or oncolytic reovirus393 was reported to augment immune activity and thereby anticancer effects. In a phase 3 clinical trial, triple therapy with bortezomib, lenalidomide and dexamethasone combined with autologous stem cell transplant was associated with improved progression-free survival of adults with multiple myeloma.394 Therefore, targeting NF-κB signaling could be a multipurpose strategy in antiaging and potentiating immunotherapy.

STAT3 and immunotherapy

Numerous studies have reported conspicuous antitumor effects by targeting hyperactivated STAT3 in cancers, as many oncogenic signaling pathways converge at STAT3.395 It is worth noting that not only cancer cells but also immune cells in the TME present hyperactivation of STAT3 because cancer cells with aberrantly activated STAT3 conduct stimulatory crosstalk with TME immune cells by increasing the secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines such as IL-6, IL-10 and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF).396 Immunosuppressive immune cells such as Tregs and tumor-promoting M2 macrophages with hyperactivated STAT3 potentiate immune evasion, whereas the antitumor cytolytic effect of tumor-defending cells such as cytotoxic T cells, neutrophils and NK cells is restrained by elevated STAT3 expression.395 In addition, STAT3 has been confirmed to increase the expression of immune checkpoint proteins, such as increasing CTLA-4 expression in both tumor-associated nonmalignant B cells in a mouse model397 and human Tregs in the presence of IL-10398, and participating in PD-L1 upregulation initiated by histone deacetylases in osteosarcoma cell lines.399 For decades, a number of STAT3 inhibitors have been studied in preclinical cancer models or successfully proceeded to clinical trials, which are mainly classified as STAT3 direct or indirect inhibitors.400 For instance, in BRAFV600E-mutant melanoma resistant to vemurafenib, APTSTAT3-9R, a peptide STAT3 inhibitor with cell permeability, combined with anti-PD-1 therapy has been reported to potentiate tumor growth suppression by facilitating the infiltration of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and impeding the infiltration of immunosuppressive cells.401 In a clinical trial of patients with unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma, bevacizumab, a monoclonal VEGF inhibitor, combined with atezolizumab, an anti-PD-L1 antibody (n = 336), was demonstrated to be significantly effective in prolonging survival compared to patients treated with sorafenib.402

Conclusion and perspective

In this review, four core signaling pathways implicated in both aging and cancer are systemically reviewed, including (i) nutrient-sensing and energy metabolism pathways, including insulin/IGF-1/PI3K/AKT/mTOR and AMPK signaling; (ii) stress response and genome maintenance pathways, covering DNA damage response, cell cycle regulation, and apoptosis; (iii) inflammatory and microenvironmental signaling pathways, such as NF-κB signaling, NLRP3 inflammasome pathway, IL-6/JAK/STAT3 signaling axis, and TGF-β/SMAD pathway; and (iv) sirtuin-mediated epigenetic and transcriptional regulation pathways. Key druggable targets across these pathways, both established and emerging, are highlighted to facilitate and inspire future therapeutic development.

Specifically, in the realm of nutrient-sensing and energy metabolism pathways, mTORC1 and AMPK are among the most prominent targets for antiaging and anticancer interventions. Regarding drugs inhibiting mTORC1, one representative is rapamycin. It is clinically known as sirolimus, which is the founding member and a widely used pharmacological agent for inhibiting the mTORC1 complex.87 Rapamycin and its analogs are widely used in clinical oncology for cancer treatment. Given their well-established mechanism of mTORC1 inhibition, they have been extensively employed in preclinical studies to explore anti-aging interventions.87 Although inhibition of mTORC1 in these models has demonstrated multiple geroprotective effects,68–78 none of these drugs have yet received official approval for the specific indication of treating or delaying human aging. Among pharmacological activators of AMPK, metformin and resveratrol have been extensively studied as representative compounds of interest for antiaging and anticancer applications. Metformin is a first-line medication for type 2 diabetes and a central candidate in drug repurposing strategies for both antiaging and anticancer research. There have been data available supporting its geroprotective107,108 and tumor-suppressive127,128 roles, albeit these promising preclinical and clinical findings have not yet been translated into established clinical applications for either aging or cancer treatment. Resveratrol is a plant-derived polyphenol and is currently marketed solely as a dietary supplement.131 Intriguingly, it has shown great potential in healthspan extension132,133 and antitumor therapy134–140 via abundant preclinical studies. However, to date, these observed benefits have not been substantiated by large-scale human clinical trials. With respect to stress response and genome maintenance pathways, as evidenced by the substantial literature, targeting key components in pathways related to aging (e.g., ATM,151–154 ATR,155–157 p53,159–168 Bcl-2,170,171 and PARP174–176) has yielded paradoxical effects, which means that modulating the same target can lead to opposing biological outcomes. Therefore, further preclinical studies are urgently needed to identify reliable molecular targets in this pathway for aging modulation. Compared to the limited and paradoxical findings regarding anti-aging interventions, some targets in this pathway have already been approved by the FDA for anticancer therapy (e.g., Bcl-2, PARP, CDK4/6), and others (e.g., ATM, ATR, CHK1, MDM2, p53, WEE1) are considered emerging for clinical use in tumor suppression. Concerning the inflammatory and microenvironmental signaling pathways, it contains the most abundant molecular targets implicated in dual anti-aging and anticancer processes among the four pathways reviewed, as evidenced by numerous findings of in vitro and in vivo preclinical studies. Although preclinical evidence for both functions is robust, the clinical trial data supporting their tumor-suppressive effects are substantially more extensive than those exploring their geroprotective potential. Within the sirtuin-mediated epigenetic and transcriptional regulation pathways, drug targets are predominantly located upstream of sirtuins, i.e., the AMPK pathway, NAMPT, NAD+, and sirtuins themselves. Consequently, there is considerable overlap with targets central to nutrient-sensing and energy metabolism pathways. However, in addition to metformin and resveratrol, the translational potential of other drugs is constrained by evidence limited to specific models or emerging from pioneering but unconfirmed studies, which require more in-depth investigations.

Of note, the distinct and overlapping roles of each pathway in aging and cancer are comprehensively reviewed, along with existing and potential strategies for their coordinated modulation to simultaneously ameliorate both conditions. Current integrated approaches for dual antiaging and anticancer benefits include interventions targeting cellular nutrient sensing and metabolism, reinforcement of genomic integrity, suppression of chronic inflammation, elimination of senescent cells, and modulation of epigenetic programs. As indicated in Table 1, a significant disparity exists between the advancement of anti-aging and anticancer research. For instance, within the stress response and genome maintenance pathway, anticancer therapeutics are considerably more developed, with numerous agents having progressed to clinical trials or obtained FDA approval. In contrast, most anti-aging interventions targeting this pathway remain confined to cellular and animal studies. Consequently, a promising translational strategy involves repurposing approved anticancer drugs for aging-related conditions, as well as leveraging insights from aging biology to inform novel cancer treatments, thereby promoting synergistic benefits across both fields. Moreover, within the sirtuin-mediated epigenetic and transcriptional regulation pathway, evidence supporting the roles of sirtuins in longevity is much more established than their functions in tumorigenesis. It is noteworthy that sirtuins either promote or inhibit cancer progression depending on factors such as sirtuin isoform, cell type, tissue origin, and tumor-specific characteristics. Therefore, before advancing sirtuin-based anti-aging therapies, it is crucial to thoroughly elucidate their actions within specific physiological and pathological contexts. Finally, as an additional point to the above discussion, the influence of aging on cancer treatment, especially immunotherapy, is also examined, with a focus on aging-associated pathways and genes such as IGF-1/IGF-1R, MTOR, ATM, TP53, BCL2, NF-κB and STAT3 signaling and their roles in shaping immunotherapeutic efficacy.

Deciphering the mechanistic connections between aging and cancer continues to be a pivotal challenge in biomedical science. As the global population ages and life expectancy extends, the demand for effective anti-aging interventions is growing rapidly. Concurrently, scientists have intensified their efforts to develop strategies for cancer risk reduction and identify highly efficient therapeutics with minimal side effects in response to the rising global incidence of the disease. These parallel trends underscore the critical need to target aging and cancer synergistically, making dual-purpose pharmacological strategies a major focus of future research.
