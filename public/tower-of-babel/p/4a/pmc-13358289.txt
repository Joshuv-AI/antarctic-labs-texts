Lipoprotein lipase deficiency presenting with gastrointestinal bleeding and compensated shock
Authors: Joseph Kates Rose, Jeffrey Do, Vesa Basha, Andrea Evans
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLipoprotein lipase (LPL) deficiency is an autosomal recessive disorder causing hypertriglyceridemia. Presentations are heterogenous and there are no standardized acute treatment protocols. Long-term management consists of strict dietary control. We report a case of LPL deficiency in an infant presenting with gastrointestinal bleeding and compensated shock, with a more severe presentation than previously published cases. Preliminary diagnosis was based on lipemic blood appearance. Intensive care resuscitation was required. Triglyceride levels peaked above 200 times the upper limit of normal. Insulin infusion provided no benefit. A whole-blood exchange transfusion led to a sustained reduction in triglyceride levels. Diagnosis was confirmed by genetic testing. Despite the magnitude of hypertriglyceridemia, there were minimal end-organ sequelae. The necessity of invasive management of severe hypertriglyceridemia is not established. This case highlights the range of presentations in inherited metabolic disease, and the limited evidence for acute management strategies.

Introduction

Lipoprotein lipase (LPL) is the rate-limiting enzyme facilitating hydrolysis of triglycerides from chylomicrons and very-low-density lipoproteins, which transport primarily long-chain triglycerides [1]. Lipoprotein lipase deficiency (LPLD) is an autosomal recessive disorder consisting of deficient enzymatic activity resulting in hyperchylomicronemia and hypertriglyceridemia. Overall prevalence is estimated at 1 per million [2, 3]. Approximately 25% of patients present in their first year. Presentations are heterogeneous, with previously documented features including seizures, irritability, pallor, gastrointestinal bleeding (GIB), and abscesses [4, 5]. The pathophysiological mechanism for these symptoms is not clear [5]. Proposed mechanisms for GIB include increased blood viscosity impairing oxygen delivery and the inflammation secondary to hypertriglyceridemia causing colitis [5, 6]. Physical examination can be normal or may reveal findings of eruptive xanthomata, hepatosplenomegaly, or lipemia retinalis [4, 7]. Lactescent blood on phlebotomy is pathognomonic. Potential sequelae include acute pancreatitis and end-organ fat deposition [8, 9]. There is no standardized acute treatment guideline [7]. Therapeutic modalities aimed at rapidly decreasing hypertriglyceridemia include insulin infusions, heparin infusions, hemofiltration, plasmapheresis, and exchange transfusion [4, 7]. Long-term management requires dietary restriction of long-chain fatty acids (LCFA). We report a case of an infant with LPLD presenting with acute-on-chronic GIB and compensated shock, notable for its clinical severity, degree of hypertriglyceridemia, and complications obtaining laboratory investigations.

Case presentation

A 5-week-old infant presented to the emergency department (ED) with 2 weeks of melena and blood-streaked stools and new lethargy and pallor. She was born at term to a gravida 2, abortus 1 mother following an uncomplicated pregnancy. Parents were nonconsanguineous, of French-Canadian background, and had no known history of inherited disease, diabetes, or hyperlipidemia. The infant was exclusively breastfed. She was assessed for GIB 1 week prior to ED presentation and diagnosed with cow's milk protein allergy. Despite maternal dairy elimination diet, GIB progressed. On presentation to ED, the infant was critically unwell with heart rate 165 beats per minute, blood pressure 110/73 mmHg, respiratory rate 90 breaths per minute, temperature 36.6 °C, and oxygen saturation 96% on room air. She was ashen colored with delayed capillary refill and poor perfusion. Remaining physical examination was noncontributory.

Diagnostic assessment

Initial assessment was challenging, as bloodwork investigations were unable to be reported, with blood draws described as pink and frothy (see Fig. 1). Hemoglobin and blood glucose tests returned unreportable. Abdominal x-ray was normal. A pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) was contacted for advice and transfer. Empiric management began and further investigations were completed following transfer. Abdominal ultrasound and Meckel scan were normal. Endoscopy was considered too high-risk. A lipid panel from the transferring site eventually returned with triglycerides 15752 mg/dL (SI: 178 mmol/L) (reference range <99.2 mg/dL [SI: <1.12 mmol/L]) and cholesterol 1121.4 mg/dL (SI: 29 mmol/L) (reference range <200 mg/dL [SI: <5.17 mmol/L]). In the PICU, the infant’s lipase was 36 U/L (SI: 9.44 μkat/L) (reference range, 11-30 U/L [SI: 0.18-0.50 μkat/L]) and total bilirubin was 1.4 mg/dL (SI: 24 µmol/L) (reference range, ≤0.99 mg/dL [≤17 µmol/L]). See Table 1 for triglyceride and lipid results during the intensive care period. Initial lactate, C-reactive protein, creatinine, urea, magnesium, phosphate, ionized calcium, albumin, alkaline phosphatase, gamma glutamyl transferase, thyroid stimulating hormone, free thyroxine, and respiratory and stool viral multiplexes were normal. Meanwhile, initial international normalized ratio, prothrombin time, fibrinogen, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, and ammonia were unable to be reported. Given the blood's lipemic appearance and the lipid results, familial chylomicronemia syndrome (FCS) was suspected and confirmatory genetic testing was sent.

The early assessment period was notable for laboratory challenges. Results were delayed several hours, and investigations were frequently reported as uninterpretable. Laboratory personnel noted that the lipemia was preventing the automated biochemical assays from providing accurate results. Protocolized manual dilutions of up to 100 times allowed prioritized investigations to be reported consistently and punctually.

Treatment

Given compensated shock, GIB, and unsuccessful attempts at intravenous access, intraosseous lines were placed and fluid resuscitation initiated. She received 60 mL/kg total of crystalloids and packed red blood cell (PRBC) transfusions. An empiric 5 mL/kg 10% dextrose bolus was provided for unmeasurable blood glucose, followed by 10% dextrose in normal saline infusion at maintenance rate. Empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics were given. She developed respiratory distress and briefly required continuous positive airway pressure ventilation. After significant delay, investigations returned with post-transfusion hemoglobin 5.3 g/dL (SI: 53 g/L) (reference range 9.0-12.5 g/dL [SI: 90-125 g/L]). Electrolytes, liver enzymes, and renal function were normal. She received further PRBC transfusions for anemia and hypotension and arrived at the PICU.

In the PICU she was made nil per os (NPO) with a nasogastric tube to low-intermittent suction, started on intravenous pantoprazole, and received intramuscular vitamin K empirically for GIB. As FCS was suspected, she remained NPO to prevent further dietary fat exposure. Concurrent intravenous insulin and dextrose infusions were initiated in an attempt to reduce her hypertriglyceridemia. As blood glucose results remained intermittently unobtainable, insulin (Novolin) infusion started at only 0.02 units/kg/hour and dextrose was infused at elevated glucose infusion rates (GIR). Maximum insulin infusion was 0.1 units/kg/hour with maximum GIR 26.7 mg/kg/min. Insulin was discontinued after 41 hours with no significant improvement in triglycerides (see Fig. 2). As hypertriglyceridemia remained over 100 times the upper limit of normal, invasive management was pursued. Hemofiltration and plasmapheresis were technically unfeasible due to patient weight. A manual whole-blood exchange transfusion was completed under mechanical ventilation to an exchange volume of 100 mL/kg. Following exchange transfusion, triglyceride levels dropped substantially (see Fig. 2). Post-procedural care was complicated by line-associated thrombi, treated with heparin infusion, and elevations in serum lipase. While lipase peaked at 565 U/L (SI: 9.44 μkat/L), there was never evidence of abdominal pain or pancreatitis findings on abdominal ultrasound. Thus, she did not meet diagnostic criteria for acute pancreatitis. Five days after admission enteral nutrition was restarted with a low-LCFA formula (Monogen®, Nutricia), gradually increasing to a target of 120 kcal/kg/day.

Outcome and follow-up

Following reintroduction of feeds, she remained well without recurrence of severe hypertriglyceridemia. She was assessed for end-organ sequelae of extreme lipemia. Magnetic resonance spectroscopy found an elevated lipid peak within the basal ganglia but no macroscopic fat deposition throughout the brain parenchyma. Fundoscopy found lipemia retinalis [9]. Abdominal ultrasounds did not find intra-abdominal fatty deposition. Genetic sequencing revealed a homozygous c.701C->T missense substitution in the LPL gene, which has been previously described and is likely pathogenic. She was discharged on a low-LCFA formula (Monogen®, Nutricia), feeding to a minimum volume of 140 mL/kg/day. Strict dietary lipid control consisted of a maximum of 1.47 g/kg/day of LCFA or 10% of total daily calories. All fats contributed 30% of daily calories. At long-term follow-up she is growing well and achieving expected milestones in all developmental domains. Triglyceride levels remain stable within 3 times the upper limit of normal. Trials of increasing dietary LCFAs have not been tolerated due to increasing serum triglycerides.

Discussion

This case describes a severe presentation of hypertriglyceridemia in an infant with LPLD and an unprecedented peak triglyceride level. LPLD is the most common cause of FCS with an overall prevalence of 1 per million but up to 200 per million in Quebec, due to the founder effect [1, 10]. Following unsuccessful attempts at pharmacological management, triglycerides were successfully lowered with a whole-blood exchange transfusion. Reductions were sustained via fasting and the use of low-LCFA formula. Despite the magnitude of hypertriglyceridemia, no pathological end-organ effects were noted, and the child has normal growth and development on follow-up. Triglyceride levels remain below 885 mg/dL (SI: 10 mmol/L), the threshold where the risk of pancreatitis is thought to increase [2].

Following ingestion, dietary fats are absorbed in the small intestine. LCFAs are esterified into triglycerides and packaged into chylomicrons and very-low-density lipoproteins [1]. Medium- and short-chain fatty acids, meanwhile, are transported through the portal system directly to the liver. The processing of LCFAs utilizes multiple proteins, including apolipoproteins, fatty acid transporters, and fatty acid binding proteins. LPL, with cofactor apolipoprotein C-II, catabolizes chylomicrons to generate free triglycerides and chylomicron remnants [2]. FCS, also called primary chylomicronemia or type 1 hyperlipidemia, is caused by pathological genetic variants in this metabolic pathway. Chylomicronemia causes increased blood viscosity and inflammation, possibly leading to GIB via ischemic necrosis or colitis [5, 6]. Interestingly, LPLD does not appear to cause predisposition to cardiovascular disease beyond the independent risk factor of hypertriglyceridemia, suggesting a limited role of chylomicrons in heart disease [11, 12]. The LPL variant in this case has been previously reported. It is thought to cause a pathogenic loss-of-function and is 1 of 2 common forms of LPLD in the Quebecois population [13].

Treatment for FCS includes pharmacological, nonpharmacological, and dietary interventions. Dietary management requires strict regulation of LCFA intake. As this case demonstrates, dietary restriction alone can be sufficient to maintain long-term near normalization of triglycerides. In the hypertriglyceridemic state, interventions are pursued to prevent pancreatitis and end-organ fat deposition. Some evidence exists for insulin infusions, heparin infusions, and combined insulin and heparin infusions [8]. These treatments are thought to increase endogenous enzymatic activity and are thus only beneficial in individuals with residual LPL function. Invasive interventions include plasmapheresis, hemofiltration, and whole-blood exchange [8]. A randomized clinical trial in adults with hypertriglyceridemic pancreatitis found hemofiltration to be faster than combined heparin and insulin at reducing triglyceride levels, although clinical outcomes were generally equal [14]. In the pediatric population, the minimum blood volumes required for hemofiltration or plasmapheresis can be prohibitive. Manual whole-blood exchange has been demonstrated to be effective in published cases [7]. Apolipoprotein C-III (APOC) inhibitors represent novel treatment options for FCS, with promising results in recent clinical trials, although experience in the pediatric population remains limited [15]. Given the paucity of clinical trial evidence, indications and thresholds for invasive intervention as primary prevention for the sequelae of hypertriglyceridemia remain unclear.

Unreliable laboratory investigations created challenges in this case's management, with automated tests unable to process severely lipemic samples. Increasingly, clinical laboratories are turning to automated procedures to process samples [16]. Automated investigations cannot always return utilizable results in extreme cases. As the number of biochemical personnel reduces, each operator has a greater number of tests to process. In this context, it will become increasingly important for clinicians to consider the reliability of reported results, maintaining an index of suspicion for possible confounding factors. When results are extraordinary or do not fit the clinical context, clinicians should review with the laboratory and consider requesting tests be manually reviewed or repeated.
