Effects of training modalities on physiology, hematology, and performance in elite Ethiopian distance male athletes
Authors: Nigatu Worku, Zeru Bekele Tola, Aschenaki Taddese
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the physiological, hematological, and performance adaptations resulting from sequential blocks of endurance (EnT), speed (SpT), and strength (StT) training in elite Ethiopian middle- and long-distance male runners. Using a quasi-experimental repeated-measures design with a fixed order (EnT → StT → SpT), twenty male athletes (23.7 ± 3.2 years; 3000–10000 m events) completed three 6-week training blocks. Physiological (estimated VO₂max, resting heart rate, running economy), hematological (hemoglobin, hematocrit, Red blood cell count (RBC), White blood cell count (WBC)), and performance measures (400 m speed, 5000 m time trial, 12-minute Cooper test) were assessed pre- and post-intervention using paired T-tests and repeated-measures ANOVA.ResultsThe endurance training block produced significant improvements in aerobic and hematological parameters, including estimated VO₂max (+ 6.20 ml·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹), resting heart rate (− 3.80 bpm), and hematocrit (+ 4.35%) (all p < 0.001). Subsequent strength and speed training blocks were associated with gains in running economy (StT: −4.2% oxygen cost, p < 0.01; SpT: −1.8%, p < 0.05) and 400 m speed (StT: −1.21 s, SpT: −1.00 s; both p < 0.001). All training modalities contributed to improved 5000 m times (EnT: −18 s, p < 0.01; SpT: −9 s, p < 0.01; StT: −6 s; p < 0.05). The sequential training approach resulted in complementary improvements across aerobic, anaerobic, and neuromuscular domains.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-026-07798-3.

Introduction

Middle- and long-distance running performance is influenced by a range of physiological determinants, notably VO₂max, running economy (RE), lactate threshold, and hematological variables such as hemoglobin mass (Hb) and blood volume [1]. Elite athletes and their coaches employ various training modalities to optimize these parameters; however, it is still unclear how distinct endurance, strength, and speed blocks differentially influence VO₂max, hematology, RE, and race performance in elite altitude-adapted athletes [2, 3].

Traditional distance running programs have emphasized high-volume endurance training to enhance aerobic capacity and cardiovascular efficiency [4]. In recent years, training paradigms have evolved to include strength and speed components within periodized frameworks [5]. Strength training (StT) is hypothesized to improve RE by enhancing neuromuscular coordination and force production [6], while speed training (SpT) may augment anaerobic capacity and fast-twitch muscle fiber recruitment, thereby improving racing tactics and late-race surges [7, 8].

Ethiopian athletes have achieved sustained international success in middle- and long-distance events, a phenomenon often attributed to a combination of genetic predisposition, high altitude training environments, and unique cultural practices [1, 9, 10]. These athletes commonly reside and train at elevations between 2000 and 3500 m, where chronic hypoxic exposure stimulates hematological adaptations such as increased erythropoiesis and oxygen-carrying capacity [11, 12]. Modern research continues to refine our understanding of the dose-response relationship between altitude exposure and hematological gains, while also highlighting significant inter-individual variability in adaptive responses [13].

However, direct comparative studies of sequential, distinct training blocks in elite altitude-adapted athletes are lacking. Although the theoretical basis for multi-modal training benefits is well supported [14, 15], empirical evidence comparing the relative efficacy of endurance, strength, and speed training blocks in elite distance athletes remains limited, particularly in this unique population [16, 17]. Recent systematic reviews have called for more targeted research on integrated periodization strategies in elite cohorts [18, 19].

Given the influence of altitude on training adaptations and the increasing use of integrated periodization strategies, there is a critical need to understand how specific training modalities affect key performance determinants in elite Ethiopian athletes. Investigating these effects could yield novel insights for evidence-based coaching and training optimization in this highly successful population [5, 10, 20].

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to investigate the distinct and comparative adaptations by implementing sequential six-week training blocks focused on endurance, strength, and speed in elite male Ethiopian middle- and long-distance athletes. We hypothesized that: (1) the EnT block would produce the greatest improvements in VO₂max and hematological parameters; (2) the StT block would result in the largest gains in RE and 400 m speed; and (3) the SpT block would lead to the greatest enhancement in anaerobic capacity.

Methods

Participants

Twenty male elite Ethiopian middle- and long-distance athletes (mean age: 23.7 ± 3.2 years) participated in this quasi-experimental study. All athletes were nationally or internationally competitive, with personal best times for 5000 m ranging from 13:10 to 13:45 min. Athletes were recruited from professional athletics teams in Addis Ababa, train in mid- to high-altitude environments (2100–3500 m) of Addis Ababa and the Oromiya region, Ethiopia. Twelve athletes specialized in the 5000–10,000 m events, and eight specialized in the 1500–3000 m events. Inclusion criteria required athletes to be: (1) competing at national or international level, (2) training consistently for at least three years, and (3) free from injury in the previous three months. The study received ethical approval from the Addis Ababa University College of Natural Sciences Research Ethical Committee, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Study design

A quasi-experimental, repeated-measures design was employed. Due to logistical constraints of working with elite professional training groups, a randomized crossover design was not feasible. All athletes therefore completed three sequential 6-week training blocks in a fixed order: Endurance (EnT), followed by Strength (StT), then by Speed (SpT). This order was selected to reflect a common periodization model progressing from general aerobic conditioning to specific speed and power development. Pre- and post-intervention testing was conducted for each block. Two-week washout periods of normal mixed training were implemented between interventions to minimize carryover effects, though the potential for incomplete washout represents a significant study limitation discussed below. All training and testing occurred at altitude (2400–2700 m above sea level), consistent with the athletes’ normal training environment.

Experimental protocol and timeline

The study began with a 4-week period of standardized pre-intervention training to establish baseline consistency among participants. This period consisted of ~ 100–110 km per week, with ~ 80% easy running (< 70% VO₂max), ~ 15% moderate running (70–85% VO₂max), and ~ 5% strides or short hills. Initial pre-tests for physiological and hematological parameters were conducted. The EnT intervention followed for 6 weeks, with post-tests administered immediately upon completion. After a 2-week normal training washout period (similar volume and intensity to the pre-intervention phase), pre-tests were repeated, and the StT intervention began, lasting 6 weeks and concluding with post-tests. Following another 2-week normal training period and subsequent pre-tests, the SpT intervention was implemented for 6 weeks, followed by its respective post-tests. This within-subject design ensured that all participants experienced each training modality in the same sequence.

Training modality protocols

Each 6-week intervention followed a structured weekly schedule with three hard, one moderate, two easy sessions, and one rest day. Training load was monitored using session-RPE (sRPE) and heart rate (HR) data (Polar Team Pro, Polar Electro Oy, Finland). Weekly training characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Endurance training (EnT)

Each session included 15 min of dynamic mobilization, followed by continuous runs at 70–85% of estimated VO₂max (guided by HR zones). Workouts comprised 60-minute continuous runs or segmented runs (2 × 30 or 3 × 20 min) at a constant pace, ending with a 3 km cool-down and 10 min of stretching. Progression followed the overload principle, starting at 80% of weekly volume (120 km) and increasing by 10% weekly to full load by week 6.

Strength training (StT)

Conducted thrice weekly, sessions included a 20-minute warm-up, dynamic mobilization, and circuit training of six sets of eight bodyweight exercises (1–2 min work, 1 min rest; 3 min between sets), finishing with 3 × 200 m hill runs. Load increased weekly by 10% toward 80% of maximum repetition, ending with a 10-minute cool-down.

Speed training (SpT)

Sessions began with a 5 km warm-up and 20 min of mobilization, followed by long intervals (1000–1600 m, 95–100% VO₂max) or short intervals (200–800 m, 105–110% VO₂max), totaling 6–8% of weekly volume. Weekly training included three interval sessions, three 13 km easy runs with 5 × 150–200 m strides, and four 6 km recovery runs, with one rest day.

Outcome measures

Physiological parameters

Estimated VO₂max was assessed indirectly through the 12-minute Cooper test, with distance covered converted to estimated VO₂max using standardized equations. logistical constraints of field-based testing at altitude (2400–2700 m) and the unavailability of portable metabolic carts in this setting. We acknowledge that indirect VO₂max estimation represents a significant limitation for an elite population where direct measurement would be preferable for precision.

Resting Heart Rate (RHR) was measured in the morning after waking, following standardized procedures. Athletes remained in a supine position for 10 min before measurement.

Running Economy (RE) was evaluated by measuring oxygen consumption during submaximal running at a standardized velocity of 16 km·h⁻¹. RE was expressed as ml·kg⁻¹·km⁻¹, and the relative change (%) in oxygen cost was used for analysis.

Hematological parameters

Blood samples were collected at baseline and after the six-week training intervention. Blood samples (10 ml) were collected in the morning following a 12-hour overnight fast and 48 h of rest from intense training. Athletes were instructed to maintain normal hydration. Parameters measured included: hemoglobin concentration (Hb, g·dL⁻¹), Hematocrit (Hct, %), red blood cell count (RBC, million·µL⁻¹), and white blood cell count (WBC, thousand·µL⁻¹). Analyses were conducted using a Sysmex XE-2100 automated hematology analyzer.

Performance parameters

400 m speed test was conducted on a standard 400 m track in the morning (12:00–1:00 PM) under cool conditions (10–15 °C), with athletes wearing racing spikes. 12-minute Cooper Test was used to determine distance covered in 12 min on a synthetic track, serving as both a performance measure and for VO₂max estimation. 5000 m Time Trial assessment was conducted on a standard track at altitude (2700 m above sea level) in the morning (1:00–2:00PM) under standardized weather conditions, with athletes wearing racing spikes.

Statistical analysis

Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Pre-post changes within each training block were analyzed using paired t-tests. Primary outcomes were defined a priori as VO₂max and 5000 m time trial performance. All other outcomes were considered secondary. To account for multiple comparisons across parameters, the false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied within each training block. To compare the effect of training modality on the magnitude of change for each parameter, a one-way repeated measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA) was conducted on the change scores (Δ = Post – Pre) with the within-subjects factor being ‘Modality’ (three levels: Endurance, Speed, Strength). Where Mauchly’s test indicated a violation of sphericity, Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied. Partial eta squared (ηp²) was calculated as the effect size for RM-ANOVA main effects. In case of a significant main effect, Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise post-hoc tests were conducted to identify differences between specific modalities. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were calculated to quantify the magnitude of changes, with values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 representing small, medium, and large effects, respectively. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (Version 28, IBM Corp.).

Results

Endurance training

Results during the EnT block showed significant improvements in aerobic and hematological parameters (Table 2). VO₂max increased from 71.92 ± 1.32 ml·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹ to 78.13 ± 1.88 ml·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹ (mean Δ: +6.20 ml·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹, 95% CI: 5.41, 6.99; p < 0.001; Cohen’s d = 1.47), reflecting a large effect size (Table 3). RHR decreased from 56.60 ± 3.41 bpm to 52.80 ± 3.58 bpm (Δ: −3.80 bpm, 95% CI: −4.82, −2.78; p < 0.001). Hematological adaptations included increases in Hb from 15.06 ± 1.42 g·dL⁻¹ to 15.56 ± 1.29 g·dL⁻¹ (Δ: +0.50 g·dL⁻¹, P < 0.05), Hct from 42.50 ± 3.38% to 46.85 ± 3.19% (Δ: +4.35%, 95% CI: 3.68, 5.02; p < 0.001; d = 1.32), and RBC from 5.08 ± 0.31 to 5.50 ± 0.35 million·µL⁻¹ (Δ: +0.42, 95% CI: 0.35, 0.49; p < 0.001; d = 1.29). Performance metrics improved significantly in the Cooper test (Δ: +236 ± 51 m, p < 0.001), and the 5000 m time trial (Δ: −18 ± 6 s, 95% CI: −21, −15; p < 0.01). The 400 m speed test showed a non-significant reduction (Δ: −0.45 ± 0.32 s, p = 0.072; d = 0.45), (Tables 2 and 3).

Strength training

Implementation of the StT block was associated with enhanced neuromuscular efficiency and anaerobic performance (Table 3). RE improved by 4.2 ± 1.8% (Δ: −4.2%, 95% CI: −5.1, −3.3; p < 0.01; d = 0.96), the largest gain among modalities. The 400 m speed improved by − 1.21 ± 0.45 s (Δ: −1.21 s, 95% CI: −1.43, −0.99; p < 0.001; d = 0.91), and the 5000 m time trial decreased by 6 ± 3 s (Δ: −6 s, 95% CI: −7.8, −4.2; p < 0.05; d = 0.58), (Table 3). No significant changes occurred in estimated VO₂max (78.34 ± 2.23 ml·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹ vs. 79.44 ± 4.64 ml·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹, p = 0.286; d = 0.45). Hematocrit increased slightly (47.00% to 47.67%, p < 0.05), but Hb and RBC count remained unchanged (Table 2).

Speed training

The SpT intervention was associated with significant improvements in anaerobic capacity (Table 2). The 400 m speed decreased from 57.00 ± 2.1 s to 56.00 ± 2.3 s (Δ: −1.00 s, 95% CI: −1.32, −0.68; p < 0.001; d = 0.86), while the 5000 m time trial improved by 9 ± 4 s (Δ: −9 s, 95% CI: −11, −7; p < 0.01; d = 0.67, Table 3). A marginal estimated VO₂max increase occurred (75.0 ± 2.3 ml/kg/min to 75.7 ± 2.4 ml/kg/min) (Δ: +0.7, p < 0.05; d = 0.30) (Table 2). The Cooper test increased by 40 ± 15 m (Δ: +40 m, p < 0.05). Hematological parameters showed no significant changes during the SpT intervention.

Training load

Mean weekly training load (sRTE, arbitrary units) was significantly different between blocks (F₂,₃₈ = 34.2, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.64), confirming distinct physiological stimuli: EnT (2850 ± 210 AU), StT (1950 ± 180 AU), SpT (2650 ± 230 AU).

Comparative effects between training modalities

RM-ANOVA on change scores revealed significant main effects of training modality for estimated VO₂max (F₂,₃₈=24.87, p < 0.001, ηp²=0.57). Post-hoc Bonferroni comparisons revealed that the magnitude of improvement in VO₂max was significantly greater following the EnT block compared to both the SpT (p < 0.001) and StT (p < 0.001) blocks. A significant main effect of modality was found for Hct (F₂,₃₈=18.30, p < 0.001, ηp²=0.49). Similarly, a significant main effect of modality was found for the change in 400 m speed (F₂,₃₈=15.42, p < 0.001, ηp²=0.45). Post-hoc tests showed improvements in 400 m speed were greater after the StT and SpT blocks compared to the EnT block (both p < 0.001), with no significant difference between StT and SpT (p = 0.42). Significant effects of training modality were found for 5000 m time trial performance (F₂,₃₈=12.2, p < 0.001, ηp²=0.39), and for RE (F₂,₃₈=10.3, p < 0.01, ηp²=0.35). For RE, post-hoc tests indicated the improvement was greater after the StT block compared to both the EnT (p < 0.05) and SpT (p < 0.01) blocks (Table 4).

The EnT block also showed significantly larger increases in hematological parameters (Hb, Hct, RBC count) compared to the other modalities (all p < 0.01). In summary, the EnT block produced the largest effect sizes for estimated VO₂max (d = 1.47), Hct (d = 1.32), and RBC count (d = 1.29). All modalities showed moderate to large effect sizes for 5000 m performance: EnT (d = 0.79), SpT (d = 0.67), and StT (d = 0.58), (Supplementary material). The results indicate the greatest adaptations for aerobic parameters occurred with EnT, while StT and SpT produced the largest effects on anaerobic and neuromuscular measures.

Discussion

This study evaluated the effects of three distinct, sequentially administered training modalities—endurance, strength, and speed training—on key physiological, hematological, and performance outcomes in elite Ethiopian male middle- and long-distance athletes. The findings demonstrate that each block was associated with unique, yet complementary adaptations, reinforcing the potential value of a periodized, multi-modal approach to athletic development in elite endurance sport. Importantly, the results must be interpreted within the context of the sequential design, where observed effects represent the impact of each modality delivered after preceding training blocks.

Endurance training adaptations

The endurance training block, delivered first in the sequence, produced substantial improvements in estimated VO₂max and hematological parameters. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that high-volume aerobic training, particularly in hypoxic environments, enhances oxygen transport capacity by stimulating erythropoiesis [7, 13, 21, 22].

The magnitude of hematological changes observed in this study warrants comparison with established altitude training literature. The 4.35% increase in Hct is remarkably similar to the 4–6% increases reported in classic “live high-train low” studies with sea-level athletes [2–5, 11, 23, 24]. However, it is important to note that our athletes are chronic altitude residents, not sea-level natives. The observed increase in hemoglobin concentration (~ 0.5 g·dL⁻¹) and VO₂max (~ 6.2 mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹) over six weeks represents an 8.6% improvement. While this exceeds the 2–5% typically reported in meta-analyses of elite sea-level athletes [25, 26], it is plausible within this specific context. First, The pre-intervention baseline followed a 4-week standardization period that may have included an untapered or slightly detrained state, potentially inflating the observed gain. Second, studies specifically in Ethiopian cohorts have reported similar latent plasticity when structured overload is applied [23]. Third, the combination of vigorous aerobic training at altitude may produce a synergistic erythropoietic stimulus not easily achieved in isolation [13]. These changes align with, and in some cases exceed, those reported in sea-level athletes undergoing similar duration training, potentially underscoring an enhanced erythropoietic sensitivity or synergistic effect of training stress combined with chronic hypoxia [11, 21, 27].

Strength training adaptations

The strength block was primarily associated with enhanced RE (4.2% improvement, d = 0.96) and short-distance performance (400 m speed: − 1.21 s), with minimal effects on estimated VO₂max or hematological markers. This effect size (d = 0.96) sits at The upper boundary of the 2–4% range reported in a recent meta-analysis of strength training in endurance runners [28]. We attribute this to the specific nature of our intervention, which combined heavy circuit training (6 × 8 exercises) with high-intensity hill sprints (3 × 200 m, 10% grade). This hybrid model provides a potent stimulus for both neuromuscular coordination (via resistance) and explosive power (via hill sprinting), which may be more effective for improving RE than isolated heavy strength work [6, 29]. The mechanisms likely involve enhanced neuromuscular coordination, improved force production characteristics, and potentially favorable changes in muscle architecture and stiffness properties, consistent with established physiological models [14, 29, 30]. Recent research further highlights the role of neural adaptations to resistance training, including motor unit recruitment and firing frequency, which are critical for enhancing RE [31].

Speed training adaptations

The speed block significantly improved anaerobic performance and 400 m speed, with a smaller but significant improvement in estimated VO₂max. These findings challenge the traditional dichotomy between aerobic and anaerobic training adaptations and support emerging literature suggesting that high-intensity sprint interval training can result in both central (cardiac output) and peripheral (muscle oxidative capacity) adaptations [32–34]. The concurrent improvement in both anaerobic capacity and aerobic performance parameters during SpT likely reflects the high metabolic demands of the interval protocols used, which stress both the phosphocreatine and aerobic energy systems. This dual adaptation is particularly relevant for middle-distance events where both systems contribute substantially to performance [8, 35]. Furthermore, recent systematic reviews confirm a dose-response relationship between interval training and VO₂max improvements even in well-trained athletes, reinforcing the observed aerobic benefits of the SpT block [25]. In our data, VO₂max gains during the SpT block were markedly smaller than during the EnT block, suggesting the primary value of this block for elite athletes lies in anaerobic and tactical adaptations rather than further large aerobic gains.

Importantly, the StT block was delivered after EnT blocks, so these RE gains likely reflect cumulative adaptations (EnT + StT) rather than the effect of StT alone. This sequence effect has important implications for periodization, suggesting that StT may be optimally positioned later in EnT phases to refine economy after a foundation of aerobic adaptations has been established. The choice between unilateral or bilateral resistance training is a contemporary debate, with meta-analyses suggesting benefits for endurance and strength measures, which could further refine speed protocols [36].

Implications for periodization models

This pattern of adaptations is consistent with the traditional model of “endurance base → strength refinement → speed development” as a viable periodization approach. Although the sequential, non-randomized design limits causal inference, it provides practical insight into an effective training framework [5, 18, 19]. The endurance-first phase maximized oxygen transport adaptations, supporting the high metabolic demands of subsequent SpT. The greatest improvements in running economy and anaerobic power occurred during StT, suggesting neuromuscular and anaerobic capacities develop best after a solid aerobic base. This sequence may allow newly acquired power to be expressed within a fatigue-resistant, aerobically supported framework, contributing to 5000 m performance gains across phases [37, 38]. Maintained hematological parameters during StT and SpT also indicate that altitude exposure preserves erythropoietic adaptations despite reduced endurance volume, aligning with recent altitude studies [27, 39].

Study limitations

The primary limitation was the sequential, non-randomized design. The fixed order introduced potential order effects, as carryover from earlier phases may have influenced later adaptations [18, 19]. The 2-week washout periods, though practical, are physiologically insufficient for complete hematological recovery given the ~ 120-day lifespan of erythrocytes. Consequently, the adaptations observed during the StT and SpT blocks almost certainly reflect cumulative training history rather than the isolated effect of that specific modality. This is a major constraint of the fixed-order design and limits our ability to claim modality-specific causality for later blocks. The lack of a control group or a comparator period without structured intervention limits our ability to account for confounding factors like maturation or seasonal variation. Furthermore, test-retest reliability data for key performance tests (Cooper test, 400 m, RE) in this specific population were not collected, which adds uncertainty to the interpretation of the magnitude of change. Future studies should adopt randomized crossover or parallel designs with longer washouts (8–12 weeks) to isolate modality-specific effects, though such designs are difficult in elite athletes [33, 40, 41]. Indirect VO₂max estimation, while field-appropriate, limits precision compared to direct testing. The small, homogeneous sample of elite male Ethiopian athletes restricts generalizability but provides valuable insight into high-altitude adaptation [9, 23, 24]. Direct blood volume measurement, rather than relying on hematological markers alone, would further clarify VO₂max changes, as unmeasured plasma volume shifts may have contributed to the observed hematology results [42].

Practical implications

Endurance training is likely to remain the foundation of distance running preparation for developing aerobic capacity and hematological function [4, 12, 21]. Substantial improvements observed indicate that even elite, altitude-adapted athletes can achieve notable aerobic gains through structured endurance phases [25, 26]. Implementing StT after aerobic development may be an effective strategy to improve RE and power while preserving fitness qualities [14, 43], suggesting optimal placement during pre-competitive or competitive periods. Running-specific strength work within concurrent models reinforces this integration [44]. The SpT phase appears well-suited to enhance anaerobic capacity while maintaining aerobic markers [8, 34, 35], supporting competition phases. The complementary adaptations associated with all modalities support multi-modal, periodized training programs. Coaches should consider integrating endurance, strength, and speed systematically, while adapting these ideas to individual athletes and local constraints, rather than treating them as competing methods [5, 18, 22].

Conclusions

Sequential endurance, speed, and strength blocks show distinct yet complementary adaptations in elite Ethiopian distance athletes. Delivered in this order, they were associated with optimized aerobic, neuromuscular, and anaerobic capacities, promoting comprehensive performance gains. The improvements in native athletes adapted to high altitude highlight continued trainability and support the strategic integration of diverse training stimuli within periodized plans. However, the sequential design necessitates cautious interpretation, as adaptations reflect cumulative sequence effects rather than isolated modalities. Randomized or parallel-group studies are needed to confirm causal effects and the optimal order of training modalities.
