Discharge Barriers and Length of Stay Following Congenital Heart Surgery: A Mixed‐Methods Approach
Authors: Zofia Zdanowicz, Christina Laternser, Colin J. Crilly, Kristin Kan, Amy S. Lay, Joyce L. Woo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to identify granular discharge barriers after congenital heart surgery and compare the contributions of discharge barriers versus clinical factors to postoperative length of stay (LOS) variation among infants with severe congenital heart defects.MethodsThis was a retrospective cohort study of patients who underwent initial cardiac surgery at ≤12 months, from 2015 to 2021, at a high‐volume congenital heart defect surgical center. The outcome was postoperative LOS. The exposure was discharge barrier category, a 7‐level categorical variable determined a priori using a mixed‐methods approach. Covariates included surgical/anatomic complexity, postoperative complications, sociodemographics, and comorbidities. Associations between discharge barrier categories and postoperative LOS were estimated with log‐linear models. A Shorrocks‐Shapley decomposition estimated each covariate's contribution to outcome variation.ResultsA total of 752 infants met the inclusion criteria; 24.3% experienced a discharge barrier. The most common discharge barrier category was medical equipment delivery or prior authorization delays (52/752, 6.9%). Median postoperative LOS for those without and with barriers was 8 (interquartile range, 5–15) and 26 (interquartile range, 14–52) days, respectively (P<0.001). In a multivariable analysis, 4 of the 6 barrier categories were associated with higher postoperative LOS when compared with patients without barriers (31%–242%, P=0.000–0.030). Surgical complexity and discharge barriers accounted for similar amounts of variation in postoperative LOS (22.2% [95% CI, 17.3%–26.8%] and 21.2% [95% CI, 14.9%–27.0%], respectively).ConclusionsDischarge barriers are as influential as surgical and anatomic complexity in explaining variation in postoperative LOS among infants undergoing congenital heart surgery. Future research and clinical efforts to mitigate modifiable discharge barriers may yield improvements in postoperative LOS.

Congenital heart defects (CHDs) are the most common and resource‐intensive birth defects, 1 affecting ≈1 in 100 live births. 2 Among those who require cardiac surgery, severe CHDs account for ≈15% to 25%, 3 an annual economic burden of ≈$75 billion, and a lifetime burden of $3 trillion. 4 Specifically, prolonged length of stay (LOS) is a well‐established proxy for both economic and clinical burden, given its consistent association with higher inpatient costs 5 , 6 and worse long‐term developmental outcomes 7 , 8 among children with CHDs.

Several studies have further demonstrated that clinical factors, especially surgical and/or anatomic complexity, are significant and consistent predictors of LOS. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 Yet, surgical/anatomic complexity is not modifiable, and large administrative and registry data lack the granularity to identify the modifiable factors that contribute to variation in LOS. 13 This highlights a clear knowledge gap: how can clinical care be modified to minimize postoperative LOS for patients following congenital heart surgery?

To address this knowledge gap, we aimed to (1) identify discrete discharge barriers using a mixed‐methods analysis of granular electronic medical record data, (2) estimate associations between discharge barriers and postoperative LOS, and (3) compare the relative contribution of discharge barriers and each covariate to variation in LOS. For the third aim, we hypothesized that discharge barriers were less contributory to postoperative LOS compared with clinical factors such as surgical/anatomic complexity. We investigated these aims among infants who received cardiac surgery for severe CHDs in the first year of life as a case example of a pediatric cohort with 1 of the highest degrees of resource use. 1

METHODS

Study Design and Data Source

This is a mixed‐methods cross‐sectional analysis of retrospective data obtained from the Ann & Robert H. Lurie Children's Hospital of Chicago, a high‐volume pediatric cardiac surgical center. 14 Infants with CHDs who received their first cardiac surgery at ≤12 months of age between January 1, 2015, and December 31, 2021, were included in the study. 15 , 16 We chose 12 months as the age cutoff to define severe CHD, in accordance with the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's definition. 3 The year 2021 was the upper bound of the inclusion period to maximize data accuracy; at the time of data acquisition, data accuracy after 2021 may have been substantially affected by post–COVID‐related disruptions and changes to hospital operation and discharge planning. Patients were excluded if they were diagnosed with connective tissue diseases leading to cardiac disease (eg, aortic root dilation) or acquired cardiac conditions (eg, endocarditis), or if their surgery did not require cardiopulmonary bypass (eg, ligation of patent ductus arteriosus), as these procedures do not reflect severe CHDs.

Data were obtained from the Society of Thoracic Surgeons Congenital Heart Surgery Database (STS‐CHSD) at Ann & Robert H. Lurie Children's Hospital of Chicago. The STS‐CHSD is the largest congenital heart surgery clinical registry in the world. It captures >90% of all US congenital heart operations, with detailed information on patient demographics, hospital, surgeon, 110 anatomically specific cardiac diagnoses, 187 procedures and procedural subtypes, 22 comorbid conditions and preoperative risk factors, as well as postoperative complications. 17 In addition, more granular clinical variables that were not available in the STS‐CHSD were collected manually from patients' electronic health records. Because of the presence of protected health information in the data set, data will not be shared or made available on request.

Outcome Variable

The outcome was postoperative LOS, in days, starting from date of surgery to date of discharge. Postoperative LOS, rather than total LOS, was chosen as the outcome to avoid confounding from preoperative events. 18

Exposure Variable: Mixed‐Methods Determination of Discharge Barrier Groups and Categories

The exposure variable was discharge barrier categories, which were defined a priori using a mixed‐methods approach: first, barriers to discharge were manually extracted from the electronic health record (Z.Z.) as excerpts from any clinical provider note (eg, physician, social worker, nurse, or case manager) that indicated a delay in discharge as perceived by the provider. Example excerpts included “identifying a Department of Child and Family Services–appointed guardian” or “insurance approval of enalapril solution.” Next, each excerpt was codified into 13 groups based on clinical consensus (Z.Z., J.W., and A.L.): delay because of parental preference, need for supervised parental overnight stay, availability of home nursing, long‐term acute care center bed availability, unexpected clinical instability, delayed medical equipment delivery, delayed medical equipment teaching, prior authorization, delay because of interpreter assistance, lack of transportation, involvement of the Department of Child and Family Services, lack of home resources (eg, utility assistance), and other.

Finally, the 13 groups were collapsed into 6 discharge barrier categories to achieve adequate cell size for analysis and to account for the fact that some patients experienced multiple discharge barriers. If a patient experienced multiple discharge barriers, his/her clinical documentation of the discharge process was rereviewed by 3 clinical cardiologists (J.W., Z.Z., and A.L.), and a single, predominant discharge barrier was assigned. Three categorizations were separately performed (Z.Z., J.W., and A.L.), then each was tested using ordinary least squares regression (C.L.). The final categorization was determined first based on the magnitude/rank of each of the coefficients for the 13 groups, followed by discussion and consensus by all coauthors. The final 6 discharge barrier categories were as follows: home nursing or long‐term acute care center bed availability, parental overnight stay, delay because of medical equipment delivery or prior authorization approval, delay because of parental preference or equipment teaching, delay because of social determinants of health (ie, transport need, utility assistance, or involvement of the Department of Child and Family Services), and unexpected clinical instability or other.

Additional Covariates

Additional covariates included clinical factors, sociodemographic variables, and comorbidities. Clinical factors included CHD surgical complexity measured by the Society of Thoracic Surgeons–European Association for Cardio‐Thoracic Surgery (STAT) category, a previously validated 19 , 20 5‐level categorical variable within the STS‐CHSD, where STAT 1 is defined as the lowest surgical mortality risk, and STAT 5 is defined as the highest surgical mortality risk; poor weight gain; postoperative complications; and discharge equipment needs (eg, oxygen or nasogastric tube). A patient was identified as having poor weight gain if postoperatively he/she was documented to have “poor weight gain,” “slow weight gain,” “inadequate weight gain,” or “failure to thrive” at least once. Postoperative complications included any complication listed in the STS‐CHSD, which includes, but is not limited to, arrhythmia, respiratory failure, and acute renal failure. Although each patient's respiratory needs were noted at discharge, his/her respiratory support need before his/her surgery, such as length of ventilatory support before the operation, was not noted. Sociodemographic variables included race, ethnicity, payor (commercial/military/Tricare versus public), and transportation aid received (parking only versus transportation only versus both). Military/Tricare insurances were grouped with commercial insurance given that their LOSs were similar to commercial rather than public insurance. Comorbidities included presence of a genetic syndrome, nonchromosomal syndrome, or extracardiac structural abnormality.

Statistical Analysis

Standard descriptive statistics were used to analyze each descriptive variable. Bivariate comparisons were performed using χ2 or Wilcoxon rank‐sum test.

A log‐transformed ordinary least squares regression model with robust SEs was used to estimate the adjusted associations between discharge barrier categories and postoperative LOS. The model included discharge barrier categories, STAT category, and additional covariates (postoperative complications, equipment needs at discharge, poor postoperative weight gain, chromosomal/noncardiac/genetic anomaly, race and ethnicity, insurance type, parking or transportation aid received, and a COVID‐19 indicator variable).

Notably, weight, operative duration, residential distance from the hospital, residential rurality, spoken language, and number of adults in the family unit were collected but excluded from the model either because of collinearity or lack of explanatory power. This was also found to be true for sex, age at surgery, and gestational age. There was some overlap between the discharge barrier categories and the remaining covariates; for instance, patients with discharge barriers related to delays in equipment delivery also had discharge equipment needs. However, testing between the covariates and the exposure variable did not suggest collinearity (variance inflation factor <2). To provide estimates that may be more clinically meaningful, we report differences in postoperative LOS in 2 ways: (1) differences in predicted mean LOS in days (ie, accounting for bias in prediction using a Duan estimator 21 and assuming averages of other covariates) and (2) percentage difference in median LOS (ie, log‐transformed regression coefficient). Because we analyzed postoperative LOS on a log scale, the estimated differences between groups depend on input values of the other covariates within the model. Therefore, differences in predicted mean postoperative LOS should be interpreted as relative, rather than absolute, differences.

Results were compared using the Shorrocks‐Shapley decomposition, 22 a postestimation that provides an additive decomposition of the relative contribution of each covariate (or groups of covariates) to the explained variation (R 2) in the model. We used the Stata command shapley2 to calculate the Shapley decomposition. 23

Sensitivity Analyses

We performed 3 sensitivity analyses. First, to determine how the Shorrocks‐Shapley decomposition might change based on the specification of the exposure variable, we tested a binary indicator for perceived discharge barriers, rather than a 6‐level categorical variable. Second, to test whether the 6 discharge barrier categories were too broad, we ran a model with the 13 discharge barrier groups individually and simultaneously. Third, to determine if results were significantly different based on the specification of the outcome variable, we replaced postoperative LOS with total LOS (number of days between admission and discharge). Fourth, to determine if results differed between high‐ versus low‐complexity surgical cases, we repeated the regression and Shorrocks‐Shapley analysis, stratifying by low (STAT category 1–2) and high (STAT category 3–5) cases. All analyses were conducted using Stata18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX). The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Ann & Robert H. Lurie Children's Hospital of Chicago (number 2022‐5367). Informed consent from the patients and their caregivers was waived.

RESULTS

Cohort Characteristics

In total, 752 patients met inclusion criteria. Of these, 24.3% (n=183) experienced at least 1 discharge barrier, and 8.5% (n=64) experienced >1 discharge barrier. The most prevalent discharge barrier categories were medical equipment delivery or prior authorization (6.9%, n=52) and discharge delay because of supervised overnight stays (6.5%, n=49). The least prevalent discharge barrier category was social determinants (1.9%, n=14; Table 1). The median postoperative LOS for the whole cohort was 11 (interquartile range, 6–21) days and was ≈3 times longer among those with versus without discharge barriers (8 [interquartile range, 5–15] days versus 26 [interquartile range, 14–52 days]; P<0.001; Table 2).

Adjusted Associations of Postoperative LOS

When compared with those without discharge barriers, those with delays because of home nursing or bed availability had a 33‐day longer predicted postoperative LOS (95% CI, 14–51 days) (ie, an adjusted 242% longer LOS [95% CI, 127%–415%]); those who required a supervised overnight parental stay had a 12‐day longer mean postoperative LOS (95% CI, 6–18 days) (ie, an adjusted 87% longer LOS [95% CI, 46%–139%]); those with unexpected clinical instability/other discharge barriers had a 10‐day longer mean postoperative LOS (95% CI, 4–17 days) (ie, an adjusted 78% longer LOS [95% CI, 34%–135%]); and those with delays because of medical equipment delivery and insurance prior authorization had a 4‐day longer mean postoperative LOS (95% CI, 0–8 days) (ie, an adjusted 31% longer LOS [95% CI, 3%–66%]) (Table 3).

Associations between equipment teaching, parental preference for extended stay, social determinants, and postoperative LOS did not reach statistical significance. Although other clinical factors, such as STAT category, postoperative complications, equipment needs at discharge, chromosomal, noncardiac or genetic anomaly, and poor postoperative weight gain were associated with longer postoperative LOS, associations between race and ethnicity, insurance type, and postoperative LOS did not reach statistical significance in the adjusted model (Table 3).

Contributors to Variation in Postoperative LOS

The Shorrocks‐Shapley decomposition estimated that discharge barrier category and STAT category accounted for similar, and collectively the highest, amounts of explained variation in postoperative LOS (21.2% [95% CI, 14.9%–27.0%] and 22.2% [95% CI, 17.3%–26.8%], respectively). Sociodemographic factors accounted for the least amount of explained variation, 6.9% (95% CI, 5.3%–10.8%; Table 3).

Sensitivity Analyses

Sensitivity analyses 1 and 2 yielded similar results to the main model (Tables S1 and S2). In sensitivity analysis 3, regression coefficients for each discharge barrier category were higher than in the main model, suggesting that postoperative LOS yielded more conservative estimates compared with total LOS (Table S3). In sensitivity analysis 4, stratification of the regression into low and high surgical complexity populations yielded an even greater contribution of discharge barriers to postoperative LOS relative to STAT category (Table S4).

DISCUSSION

In this analysis, we demonstrate that discharge barriers have a significant influence on variation in postoperative LOS for infants requiring congenital heart surgery. “Anatomy is destiny” is a common adage to describe the drivers of CHD outcomes, 24 , 25 , 26 yet we find that discharge barriers explain as much variation in postoperative LOS as STAT category, a measure of surgical or anatomic complexity. Although STAT category is often not modifiable, many discharge barriers might be: supervised overnight stays could be scheduled further in advance of planned discharge if the need for one is identified early in the patient's clinical course. Proactive identification of patient caregivers could prevent delays in the completion of discharge education, especially if certain caregivers are only available for teaching in brief intervals or teaching can only occur during daytime hours on a weekday. Health care providers can prescribe home medications or order home supplies once they are indicated rather than waiting until the patient is near discharge, thereby mitigating delays because of prior authorizations or logistics of supply delivery, such as delivering supplies before a weekend. This highlights a clear direction for future research: the associations between patient‐level factors and discharge barriers. Nonetheless, approximately one‐quarter of this cohort experienced at least 1 discharge barrier, which suggests that future interventions that reduce or remove these barriers to discharge could reduce postoperative LOS.

Similar to previous studies, our analysis found clinical contributors to LOS, including postoperative complications, equipment needs at discharge, and poor postoperative weight gain. Foote et al used clinical registry data from the National Pediatric Cardiology Quality Improvement Collaborative database to demonstrate that among patients following stage 1 single‐ventricle palliation, those surgical centers with the lowest LOS were more likely to care for patients with fewer additional cardiac operations and who were discharged home with nasogastric rather than surgical feeding tubes. 27 Using the same clinical registry, Baker‐Smith et al similarly found that among patients who underwent stage 2 single‐ventricle palliation, those with the longest LOS were more likely to have a cardiac reoperation, have a postoperative complication, or need a nonoral method of nutrition. 28

Other studies have leveraged administrative, rather than clinical registry, data, such as the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project Kids Inpatient Database and the Pediatric Health Information Systems database, to demonstrate that social determinants, such as race and individual and neighborhood‐level socioeconomic status, are associated with increased LOS. 29 , 30 Yet, our analysis did not detect differences in LOS based on these social determinants. We suspect that this is because of the use of more clinically granular variables that are not available in administrative data sets (ie, discharge barriers). In other words, discharge barriers are a reflection of social determinants: patients of lower socioeconomic status may have longer postoperative LOS because they experience more discharge barriers, and therefore a model that includes discharge barriers may no longer demonstrate statistical significance for socioeconomic factors.

The impacts of reducing discharge barriers for the population with CHD is an area that warrants significantly more study. Madsen et al also used highly granular, single‐center data of patients in a cardiac care unit (ie, patients who did and did not require cardiac surgery) and identified similar discharge barriers to those identified in our study: caregiver unavailability for discharge teaching, picking up medications, or transportation home. 31 Although they did not investigate the time necessary to achieve discharge readiness, they found that the time after discharge readiness was achieved (ie, discharge within 2 hours of achieving discharge readiness) was only associated with a modest improvement in LOS. Based on these findings and our analysis, we hypothesize that (1) mitigating discharge barriers is more impactful for the surgical than the nonsurgical patient population, and (2) the time to achieve discharge readiness has a greater impact on LOS than the time to discharge after a patient is deemed ready. Together, our analysis and the analysis of Madsen et al highlight the immense potential for an understudied area of CHD research pertaining to discharge barriers. Such research could span evidence‐based interventions (eg, identification of surgery‐specific discharge barriers and development of resultant quality improvement initiatives), to the implementation of quality improvement initiatives with quantitative analysis on the impact on local outcomes, and, finally, to prospective dissemination studies across surgical centers.

Limitations

A key strength of this analysis was the leverage of highly granular data to better understand LOS; however, this also introduces the limitation of reliance on the electronic health record documentation of discharge barriers. It is possible that other patients with shorter LOSs had discharge barriers present that we were unable to capture, an example of exposure misclassification, or that our discharge barrier categories mask heterogeneity following adjustment with covariates. To mitigate these limitations of the exposure variable, we adjusted for social determinants of health and clinical factors that could confound the relationship between discharge barriers and LOS. This retrospective study is also limited to a single cardiac center, with the impact of discharge barriers potentially being unique in our setting compared with others. Although other studies using national clinical registry data that did not contain discharge variables as a variable estimated similar clinical associations with LOS, 18 , 27 , 28 the observational nature of this study still limits the generalizability and the ability for causal inference. Future prospective studies may provide stronger evidence for quality improvement initiatives that lead to significant changes in postoperative outcomes. Finally, this study contained data from 2015 to 2021. These data may not account for post–COVID‐19 changes in operations and their influence on discharge barriers.

CONCLUSIONS

Discharge barriers are as influential as surgical and anatomic complexity in explaining variation in postoperative LOS among infants undergoing congenital heart surgery. Although surgical and anatomic complexity is often not modifiable, many discharge barriers are. Therefore, future research should aim to understand the causal effects of mitigating discharge barriers on postsurgical outcomes, which will inform the development and implementation of clinical interventions to mitigate discharge barriers.
