Optimum recombination rates for genetic gains in simulated recurrent selection in empirical maize populations
Authors: Chandrappa Anilkumar, Rex Bernardo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMeiotic recombination creates new allelic combinations, but it also disrupts favorable parental haplotypes. Our objective was to assess if optimum recombination rates exist in elite maize (Zea mays L.) populations undergoing simulated short‐term and long‐term recurrent selection. Genomewide marker effects were calculated for eight populations genotyped with 3072 single nucleotide polymorphism markers and phenotyped in up to 18 environments for yield, moisture, test weight, and plant and ear height. The recombination rate was altered by multiplying the sizes of linkage maps by 0.25–10. The regression of genetic gain on the number of crossovers was often curvilinear, and an optimum number of crossovers (COOpt) was found in nearly 90% of the trait–population combinations. The COOpt was higher in long‐term than in short‐term selection, and all linear regression coefficients that were significant (p ≤ 0.05) and negative in short‐term selection became positive or nonsignificant in long‐term selection. This result indicated that increased recombination initially disrupted favorable parental haplotypes but later facilitated the accumulation of favorable haplotypes. The variation in COOpt was greater among populations than among traits within the same population, and COOpt also increased as the size of the linkage map increased. Overall, our results showed that a higher recombination rate is not always beneficial and that each trait–population combination has an optimum recombination rate that maximizes genetic gain. The practical application of these results awaits the development of methods to both suppress and boost genomewide recombination rates.

INTRODUCTION

Cultivar development depends on generating allele combinations that are not found in any of the parents. Such nonparental combinations of alleles arise from chromosome shuffling and crossing over, which together create variation among the progenies in a cross. Meiotic recombination breaks linkages between loci, and the amount of genetic variation released after meiosis depends on the rate of recombination, the location of recombinations in the genome, and the linkage phase between loci. Suppose alleles A and B are favorable. Crossing over increases the genetic variance if loci A and B are linked in repulsion phase (Ab/aB in the F1 between homozygous parents) (Weir et al., 1980). In contrast, crossing over is detrimental if the favorable alleles have already been accumulated in one parent and, consequently, the loci are linked in coupling phase (AB/ab in the F1).

Recombination is a highly regulated process (Blary & Jenczewski, 2019). The rate of natural recombination is low, with one to four recombinations per chromosome typically occurring per meiotic generation in plants (Crismani & Mercier, 2012; Epstein et al., 2023). Continuous artificial selection, which constrains allele shuffling in domesticated crops, further reduces the rate of recombination (Moyers et al., 2018). As such, efforts are being made to overcome the low rate of recombination by manipulating genes and cofactors associated with recombination and by genome editing to induce recombination via double‐stranded breaks (Serra et al., 2018; Underwood et al., 2018).

Differential recombination rates have been observed across different maize (Zea mays L.) germplasm (Bauer et al., 2013). More than 80 genes involved in the regulation of meiotic recombination in plants have been identified in model species including Arabidopsis thaliana, maize, and rice (Oryza sativa L.) (Mercier et al., 2015). Mutations in orthologs of recq4 in rice, tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), and pea (Pisum sativum) increase the recombination rate by threefold (Mieulet et al., 2018). An increased copy number of pro‐crossover factor HEI10 in A. thaliana leads to a fivefold increase in the recombination rate per chromosome arm (Serra et al., 2018). Advances in DNA cleavage and joining techniques help overcome the low frequency of natural recombinations (Bogdanove & Voytas, 2011; Sadhu et al., 2016; Sarno et al., 2017). These approaches can be helpful in crop improvement by improving the precision of gene mapping and increasing the genetic variation that can be exploited in subsequent selection (Presting, 2018; Rey et al., 2018; Sadhu et al., 2016).

Several simulation studies have been conducted to explore the effects of increased recombination rates on genetic gain in plants and livestock (Battagin et al., 2016; Gonen et al., 2017; Taagen et al., 2022; Tourrette et al., 2019). In a simulation study in Brassica and rice, Tourrette et al. (2019) reported that higher recombination rates increased the selection response by up to 30% after 20 cycles of selection. In contrast, Taagen et al. (2022) found that increased recombination rates did not lead to larger selection responses after six cycles of simulated selection in wheat (Triticum aestivum L.). In such simulation studies, the effects of the quantitative trait loci (QTLs) had an assumed statistical distribution, and different proportions of repulsion and coupling linkage phases were modeled (Battagin et al., 2016; Taagen et al., 2022; Tourrette et al., 2019).

A meaningful complement to the Tourrette et al. (2019) and Taagen et al. (2022) simulation studies would involve empirical estimates of genomewide marker effects, which would result in a closer representation of the selection responses that can be achieved if recombination rates can be altered in different breeding populations. We surmise that the genetic gains would reflect a balance between (1) the undesired disruption of favorable linkage blocks already present in each parent versus (2) the desired accumulation of favorable allelic combinations absent in the parents. We then postulate that in a given biparental population, there would be an optimum recombination rate that depends on (1) the extent to which favorable allelic combinations are already present in the parents and (2) the length of the selection process, with the opportunity to accumulate favorable allelic combinations being greater with long‐term selection than with short‐term selection. Our objective in this study was to assess if optimum recombination rates exist for yield and other agronomic traits in elite maize populations undergoing short‐term and long‐term recurrent selection.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Germplasm and phenotypic and single nucleotide polymorphism data

The germplasm and empirical data used in this study were from the AgReliant Genetics commercial breeding program and were previously described by Krchov et al. (2015). In brief, doubled haploids from four maize biparental populations (A/B, C/B, C/D, and E/F) were crossed to two inbred testers (T1, T2, T3, or T4). The parental inbreds belonged to the Iowa Stiff Stalk Synthetic heterotic group. Eight testcross populations were evaluated in up to 18 location–year combinations in the US Corn Belt from 2008 to 2012. Grain yield (Mg ha−1 at 130 g H2O kg−1), grain moisture (g H2O kg−1), test weight (kg hL−1), plant height (cm), and ear height (cm) were recorded from each trial. A total of 35 trait–population combinations were present, with not all traits being recorded in all populations. Least‐squares estimates of testcross means across environments were used as the phenotypic data in further analysis.

The doubled haploids in each biparental population were genotyped with 3072 single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) markers using the Illumina iScan platform. The SNP marker data were filtered separately for each population to exclude monomorphic markers, markers with more than 10% missing values, and markers with more than 5% heterozygosity. After filtering, the number of SNP markers in each biparental cross was 621 in A/B, 461 in C/B, 1122 in C/D, and 508 in E/F.

Linkage maps were created for each population using QTL IciMapping software (Meng et al., 2015), and the linkage map lengths were 1626 cM in A/B, 1903 cM in C/B, 2339 cM in C/D, and 1739 cM in E/F. The recombination rate across the genome was modified by constricting or expanding the linkage map distances between adjacent markers by a factor of 0.25, 0.5, 2, 5, 8, and 10. Our initial simulations showed that crossover interference as modeled in AlphaSimR software (Gaynor et al., 2021; see Section 2.2) led to a limited number of recombinations even with a 10‐fold expansion of the linkage map. Crossover interference was therefore prevented in AlphaSimR to maximize the number of recombinations per chromosome. Genomewide marker effects for each trait were calculated by ridge regression‐best linear unbiased prediction (RR‐BLUP), estimated using RRBLUP2 software (Bernardo, 2017).

Simulated recurrent selection

We used AlphaSimR software to simulate recurrent selection (Gaynor et al., 2021). For each population, the F1 was simulated by combining haplotypes from each of the two homozygous parents. An F2 population, which was considered as Cycle 0, was simulated according to the SNP data and the linkage map (original or modified). We simulated short‐term recurrent selection with three cycles and long‐term recurrent selection with 20 cycles. In each cycle, the trait values of plants (on a testcross basis) were calculated as the sum of the RR‐BLUP effects of SNP alleles found in each plant. The SNP effects were assumed constant across cycles of selection. In short‐term recurrent selection, the best five plants in each cycle were intermated by chain‐crossing (1 × 2, 2 × 3, 3 × 4, 4 × 5, and 5 × 1) to form the next cycle. This select‐and‐intermate procedure was repeated until Cycle 3. Doubled haploids were induced at the end of selection (i.e., from the Cycle 3 plants) and their trait values were calculated. The simulations were repeated 100 times, with 5000 plants in each cycle.

The same select‐and‐intermate procedure was used in long‐term recurrent selection until Cycle 20, with doubled haploids being produced in Cycle 20. The number of plants selected in each cycle was 20, according to the guideline that the number of plants selected in each cycle should be roughly equal to the number of cycles that recurrent selection is performed (Bernardo et al., 2006). The simulations were also performed with a relaxed selection intensity of 100 plants (out of 5000) selected in each cycle of long‐term recurrent selection as well as with 500 plants (instead of 5000) in each cycle.

Genetic gains were expressed as the mean of each cycle minus the mean of Cycle 0. The genetic gains were always in the desired direction (higher yield and test weight, and lower moisture, plant height, and ear height). To facilitate the presentation and interpretation of the results, the genetic gains were expressed as absolute values. The number of crossovers within each chromosome was recorded from the Cycle 0 population in each repeat of a simulation experiment. The mean number of crossovers (across repeats) in Cycle 0 was then used to represent the number of recombinations per chromosome for a given level of expansion of the linkage map.

For each trait, the effect of recombination rate on genetic gain was first assessed by quadratic regression of the absolute genetic gain on the mean number of recombinations in Cycle 0. The quadratic regression model included both a linear regression coefficient and a quadratic regression coefficient. When the quadratic regression coefficient was not significant (p = 0.05), the relationship between genetic gain and number of recombinations was re‐analyzed by simple linear regression. When the quadratic or linear regression coefficient was significant (p = 0.05), the optimum number of crossovers per chromosome to maximize genetic gain (denoted by COOpt) was estimated as the point on the fitted line or curve at which the predicted gain was highest, within the observed range of the mean number of recombinations.

RESULTS

Number of crossovers

The number of crossovers per chromosome increased as the map length increased. However, the increase in recombination rate per chromosome was disproportional to the factor used to increase the map length. Among the simulated Cycle 0 plants across all populations, the mean number of crossovers per chromosome (without interference) for each fold of constriction or expansion of the linkage map (in parentheses) was 3.8 (quarter), 5.1 (half), 6.0 (original), 7.4 (twofold), 10.2 (fivefold), 12.2 (eightfold), and 13.2 (10‐fold). The populations differed in the mean number of crossovers per chromosome in Cycle 0 (Figure 1). A 10‐fold increase in the size of the linkage map led to a mean of 5.8 crossovers per chromosome in the E/F biparental cross and 24 crossovers per chromosome in the C/D biparental cross. Shrinkage of the map length by 0.25 led to a mean of 2.8 crossovers per chromosome in E/F and 7.6 crossovers in C/D.

Long‐term genetic gains and further simulations

In long‐term recurrent selection, the recombination rate had a significant effect on genetic gain in 30 out of the 35 trait–population combinations (Table 1). A total of 20 trait–populations had both a significant linear effect and a significant quadratic effect. Only two trait–population combinations had a significant linear effect and a nonsignificant quadratic effect, whereas eight had a significant quadratic effect and a nonsignificant linear effect.

All of the significant linear effects were positive, and all of the significant quadratic effects were negative, except for moisture in the E/F (T4) population, for which the linear effect was negative and the quadratic effect was positive (Table 1). The linear and quadratic effects were significant for each of the measured traits in the C/B (T1), C/B (T2), and E/F (T1) populations. The number of populations in which the recombination rate did not have a significant quadratic effect was two for yield, zero for moisture, two for test weight, one for plant height, and two for ear height.

A relaxed selection intensity did not alter the trends of genetic gains in the populations but maintained slow and consistent gains across cycles (results not shown). Likewise, reducing the number of plants in each cycle from 5000 to 500 did not change the overall results (results not shown).

Optimum number of crossovers

In short‐term selection, an optimum number of crossovers (COOpt) was present for 32 out of the 35 trait–population combinations (Figure 1). Out of the 32 values of COOpt, 14 were based on a significant negative linear regression coefficient, with the quadratic regression coefficient being nonsignificant (Figure 1; Table 1). In other words, the COOpt in these 14 trait–population combinations (mostly in A/B [T2] and C/D [T1]) represented the smallest number of crossovers per chromosome observed in the population (Figure 1). In long‐term selection, COOpt values were present for 30 out of the 35 trait–population combinations. None of the COOpt values in long‐term selection represented the smallest number of crossovers observed in the population (Figure 1).

The COOpt was higher in long‐term selection than in short‐term selection (Figure 1). Differences in the COOpt for different traits were usually small. In contrast, COOpt varied substantially among populations (Figure 1) with COOpt being higher in the C/D (T1) and C/D (T3) populations than in the six other populations that involved the A/B, C/B, and E/F biparental crosses.

DISCUSSION

There were three main results in this study. First, an optimum number of crossovers (COOpt) for maximizing genetic gains was present in about 90% of the trait–population combinations. Second, COOpt was higher in long‐term selection than in short‐term selection. Third, the variation in COOpt was greater among populations than among traits within the same population. Overall, the results underscored a needed balance between breaking favorable haplotypes already present in the parental inbreds (Stapley et al., 2017) versus accumulating favorable allelic combinations.

In a recent study with the same populations considered herein, crossing over had a negative impact on the amount of genetic variance for all five traits (Bernardo, 2025). The maximum proportion of the genetic variance attributable to crossing over was 28% (for plant height in E/F [T1]), with the genetic variance usually being higher without crossing over than with crossing over. These prior results were attributed to favorable allelic combinations in the parental inbreds (i.e., coupling linkages). Such an accumulation of favorable parental haplotypes was also evidenced by the distribution and sums of genomewide marker effects in parental homologues. For example, for test weight on chromosome 1 of population C/D (T1), 119 (out of 165) of the favorable SNP alleles were contributed by the first parent, and only 46 were contributed by the second parent. The sum of marker effects was 8.33 kg hL−1 for the homolog inherited from the first parent and −8.33 kg hL−1 for the homolog inherited from the second parent. The genetic gain for test weight on chromosome 1 of population C/D (T1) is then a function of the extent to which linkages are broken among the 119 favorable SNP alleles (as proxies for the underlying QTLs) already found in the first parent, versus the extent to which selection across several cycles accumulates new combinations of favorable alleles on the chromosome.

In short‐term selection, there could be a limited opportunity to recover or assemble favorable allele combinations (Allard, 1960). Given that the maize populations in this study had a preponderance of coupling linkages in the parents (Bernardo, 2025), the lower COOpt in short‐term selection (three cycles in this study) facilitated the preservation of parental allelic combinations. In contrast, the higher COOpt in long‐term selection (20 cycles in this study) facilitated the selection of new allelic combinations, including the recovery of favorable parental haplotypes that had been broken. This difference in the dynamics of short‐term versus long‐term selection (Jannink, 2010) was underscored by all of the negative linear regression coefficients in short‐term selection becoming positive or nonsignificant in long‐term selection (Table 1). A negative linear regression coefficient in short‐term selection and a nonsignificant linear regression coefficient in long‐term selection likely represented an intermediate stage of this negative‐to‐positive conversion. Linear regression coefficients that were positive in short‐term selection remained positive or were nonsignificant in long‐term selection, but they never became negative.

We surmise that differences in COOpt among populations were due in part to variation in how favorable alleles were distributed between the two parents. The size of the linkage map, however, was likely a stronger determinant of COOpt given that the testcross populations were all elite. The COOpt was consistently greater in testcrosses of the C/D biparental population, which had the largest linkage map (2339 cM), than in testcrosses of the three other biparental populations, which had smaller linkage maps (1626–1903 cM).

The minimal variation in COOpt across traits may have been due to prior multi‐trait selection when the parental inbreds were developed. Previous results with the same populations showed that crossing over had a negative impact on the genetic variance for all five traits, which in turn implied a preponderance of coupling linkages for all five traits (Bernardo, 2025). Selection for all five traits in this study is routinely done by maize breeders (Bernardo, 1991; King et al., 2024). From a breeding perspective, the minimal variation in COOpt for different traits within the same population is advantageous for multiple‐trait improvement.

Some of the COOpt values in short‐term selection may have been overestimated. Specifically, 14 of the COOpt values were equal to the lowest observed number of recombinations, with these instances corresponding to the trait–population combinations for which the linear regression coefficient was significant (p ≤ 0.05) and negative and the quadratic regression coefficient was nonsignificant (Table 1). It was possible that the gains in short‐term selection may have been higher if fewer crossovers per chromosome were simulated, and the minimum number of recombinations (Figure 1) exceeded the one obligatory crossover needed for proper disjunction of paired chromosomes during meiosis (Wang et al., 2015). The minimum number of crossovers would have been lower if interference was assumed in the AlphaSimR software (Gaynor et al., 2021), but we found in initial simulation experiments that doing so severely limited the maximum number of crossovers per chromosome. We considered the option of assuming no interference to sample lower recombination rates and assuming interference to sample higher recombination rates, but we decided against this procedure because the effects of recombination rate would have been confounded with the effects of interference between linked loci. Nevertheless, our inability to sample lower recombination rates did not affect the general conclusions in this study. On the other extreme, a 10‐fold expansion of the linkage map did not lead to a 10‐fold increase in the number of detectable recombination events, because an even number of crossovers between adjacent markers cannot be detected from the marker data.

Two previous simulation studies in plants have given conflicting results on the genetic gains with modulated recombination. Taagen et al. (2022) used twofold and 20‐fold levels of map expansion in wheat and found that increased recombination during six cycles of genomewide selection did not increase genetic gains. In contrast, Tourrette et al. (2019) found that higher recombination rates increased the expected gain from 20 cycles of selection in Brassica and rice. The results from the current study help reconcile the findings of Taagen et al. (2022), with six cycles of selection being in the shorter term, and the findings of Tourrette et al. (2019) for long‐term selection. The observed variation in COOpt in the current study also helps explain the divergent results in these two previous studies.

Maize germplasm shows natural variation in recombination frequency (Bauer et al., 2013). The choice of breeding germplasm, however, will continue to be based on performance for different traits rather than differences in natural recombination frequencies. The only method currently available to maize breeders to reliably manipulate recombination frequencies is multiple generations of random mating (Dudley, 1993; Dudley et al., 2004). Breeders, however, would rather devote additional generations to performing selection rather than to conducting random mating (Bernardo, 2020). Manipulation of environmental conditions, such as temperature and available water, may be a means to alter recombination frequencies (Stapley et al., 2017; Stevison et al., 2017; Yao & Kovalchuk, 2011). Biotechnology approaches to control recombination frequency may become practical in the future (Blary & Jenczewski, 2019; Mercier et al., 2015; Parée et al., 2024; Serra et al., 2018).
