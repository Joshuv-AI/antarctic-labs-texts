Cross-generational transcriptomic effects of alcohol use disorder in third generation offspring
Authors: Shirley Y. Hill, Gregg E. Homanics, Sean Farris, Pedro L. Baldoni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Alcohol use disorder (AUD) is a highly prevalent condition that is globally associated with increased morbidity and mortality. Transmission of AUD across generations is well known. Adverse health effects of alcohol misuse in persons with AUD are also well known. The effect of alcohol misuse in previous generations on gene expression in offspring potentially leading to increased disease burden are largely unknown. We conducted a study of familial use of alcohol contrasting individuals without parents or grandparents with AUD with individuals with either a parent with AUD or both a parent and grandparent with AUD. The study controlled for important sources of variation by including individuals with samples banked in early adolescence before substantial use of alcohol and other substances occurred. Mothers of the participants were queried about their prenatal use of alcohol and other substances. We found a large number of highly significant differences in gene expression between controls and those with an AUD parent or AUD parent and grandparent, allowing us to conclude that preconception use of high levels of alcohol is associated with differences in gene expression in offspring blood. Pathway enrichment analyses revealed a statistical over representation in those involving the immune system and cancer development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52597-2.

Introduction

Alcohol use disorder (AUD) is a highly prevalent condition affecting 29.5 million individuals ages 12 and older in the US1. Globally alcohol misuse was the leading cause of death and disability in 2016 among people ages 15–492. Risk for development of AUD across generations is increased with the presence of parental AUD3 making this a major societal problem. Parental AUD is associated with increased frequency of psychopathology in offspring4,5 along with physical health problems including greater mortality by adulthood6. We have reported that offspring of AUD parents from families with a high density of members with AUD have increased use of psychoactive substances5. A Swedish population-based sample of over 2.4 million individuals registered for the presence of AUD and their adult offspring has provided rates and causes of mortality. A three-fold increase in drug-related mortality was seen. Increased hazard rates were also observed for medical causes. The increased mortality due to medical causes may be linked to changes in pathways influenced by epigenetic changes from parents to their offspring.

Based on a meta-analysis of twin and adoption studies7, the heritability of AUD was found to be 0.49 (95% confidence interval (CI) 0.43–0.53] with the proportion of shared environmental variance estimated at 0.10 (95% CI 0.03–0.16). Because AUD susceptibility is driven by both environmental and genetic variation7, recent investigations have begun to include epigenetic hypotheses. The effects of alcohol use in human brain tissue have been investigated showing hundreds of changes in messenger RNAs (mRNAs) and non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs)8. Although post-mortem changes in gene expression taken from brain tissue provides important clues about how expression may differ in those who develop AUD, these studies may confound cause and consequence because consumption of alcohol can be expected to change the transcriptome. Accordingly, our goal was to identify changes in the transcriptome that preceed development of AUD or other SUD with the long-term goal of providing biomarkers of risk that can be utilized in early identification and intervention. Use of post-mortem brain samples may not be necessary if accessible tissue offers a valid reflection of transcriptomic changes in the brain. Transcriptomic studies using peripheral tissues have documented the potential for human investigations by showing a good correspondence between brain and blood expression9. Using blood samples from those at especially elevated risk for developing SUD, assessing them before SUD or significant quantities of use are present while controlling for important variation (prenatal) offers a unique window on possible cross-generational effects.

Building on published results showing epigenetic effects in banked samples from young participants without significant alcohol or drug exposures, we reported that parental and grandparental AUD was associated with changes in methylation of an oncogene (HRAS) and a tumor suppressor gene (TP53)10. In the absence of significant personal use of alcohol and other substances and controlling for maternal prenatal alcohol use, our results suggest that preconception use of alcohol produced epigenetic effects that were inherited across generations. We hypothesized that expression changes in multiple genes could be influenced by family history of alcohol use disorder through preconception epigenetic effects. If such effects were to be seen, we reasoned that these might present a host of health related susceptibilities. Moreover, the increased incidence of mental and physical problems reported in offspring with parental AUD might be due not only to the direct effects of the offspring’s use of alcohol and other substances, but also due to parental preconception effects on multiple pathways that may affect health.

Methods

Overview of the family study

A family study involving collection of clinical data for three generations of individuals provided the resource for this investigation. Our goal was to determine if transgenerational epigenetic effects could be revealed. Transgenerational epigenetic inheritance occurs when a parent experiences an epigenetic event that undergoes intergenerational inheritance without further exposure in subsequent generations. Establishing transgenerational epigenetic inheritance in humans is quite challenging because it is critical that information about subsequent exposures in offspring are known. Because our hypothesis was that parental preconception alcohol use could result in epigenetic changes in offspring, knowledge of prenatal use of substances was needed. Also, knowledge of the postnatal environment is important. The quality of the childhood environment was globally assessed using the parental socioeconomic status (SES). Additionally, use of substances by the offspring prior to obtaining biological samples is critical for adequate testing of our hypothesis. This was available from clinical assessment of the offspring during childhood.

Our study used total RNA sequencing from banked human samples from 3rd generation individuals studied during childhood, adolescence and young adulthood with either a parent (mother or father) with an AUD, those with both a parent and grandparent with AUD, or neither. Extensive clinical information from a longitudinal follow-up of a third generation of offspring from the family study and their relatives offered the opportunity to control relevant variables needed to uncover the existence of cross-generational effects.

1st and 2nd generation clinical evaluation

The first generation individuals we studied represents the grandparents of the 3rd generation for whom longitudinal follow-up occurred. The second generation included probands who were first identified in treatment for alcohol use disorder. Assessment of the 1st and 2nd generation was done in person using structured psychiatric interviews that provided DSM diagnoses supplemented by family history report. Information was obtained concerning the highest level of education and an occupational history for each of the 1st and 2nd generation individuals. The socioeconomic status (SES) of each participant was determined using standardized methods that utilize education and occupation (Table 1). The SES of the parents was utilized to assess the childhood milieu that the 3rd generation participant experienced.

2nd generation mothers

Mothers who accompanied their children to the laboratory visit provided an opportunity for obtaining in-person systematic interviews concerning their use of alcohol and other substances during pregnancy (Table 2). A broad range of use was present among the entire cohort. However, the present analysis was based on inclusion of samples collected from offspring with no or minimal use of substances during pregnancy. The selection strategy was successful in that among those women reporting any drinking during pregnancy the median consumption was 49.5 drinks over the entire pregnancy or 1.2 drinks per week.

3rd generation substance exposure

Information from the longitudinal follow-up during childhood, adolescence and young adulthood that included the date of assessment allowed for determination of alcohol, cigarette and drug use and type prior to the blood draw using the recorded date of the collection (Tables 3 and 4). Participants were queried about whether they had ever used alcohol, cigarettes or drugs and if so, the type of drug. We analyzed the reported frequency of any use before sample collection to determine if the groups differed significantly from each other. While there was no significant difference for any use of alcohol or cigarettes, marihuana use was significantly different across groups with Group 3 participants (parent and grandparent with AUD) showing a higher frequency of any marihuana use. However, when frequency of use of each substance over the past year prior to the blood draw was calculated no significant differences in frequency of use by familial risk group was seen.

3rd generation clinical evaluation

All 3rd generation participants received an age-appropriate clinical evaluation. Each child/adolescent (ages 8–18) and his/her parent were separately administered the Kiddie version of the Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia (K-SADS)11 by trained Master’s level clinical interviewers at each annual follow-up with DSM criteria applied. A second blind interview by an advanced level resident in psychiatry provided a consensus diagnosis for all axis I diagnoses. Follow-up beyond childhood included biennial assessment using the Composite International Diagnostic Interview (CIDI)12 during young adulthood starting at age 19. The CIDI assessment provided diagnoses for anxiety, depression and substance use disorders. The participants’ use of alcohol and drugs at each follow-up visit was utilized in the determination of exposure prior to blood collection.

Ethical review and informed consent

The study was approved by the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board. All methods used were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations consistent with the policies and procedures outlined by the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board. All participants provided written informed consent. Children provided assent with parental informed consent.

Sample selection

The present report is based on a subset of 71 samples from an existing collection of 441 samples banked during the longitudinal follow-up of offspring from families selected to either be from control families or those from families with multiplex AUD background. Our database was queried to identify samples meeting the familial group requirement, and to select those with minimal or no history of maternal use of substances during pregnancy. The 71 individuals with cryogenically preserved samples had been followed to a median age of 28 years. Samples were chosen to include half biological male and half female, and matched for age and socioeconomic status by group (see Table 1). Ethnicity was predominantly Caucasian (N = 66, or 93%) with 7% African-American (N = 5). The average age of the 71 participants was 13.9 ± 4.5 years when the blood draw was performed. The cryogenically maintained samples had been maintained an average of 23.9 ± 3.4 years (median = 24.0 years) prior to sequencing. The overall SES score for the sample was 41.1 ± 12.3 which places the sample within level II of the Hollingshead four factor measure of SES13, a level characterized by individuals who are owners of medium size businesses or employed in minor professional and technical occupations. The four-factor index was based on the occupation and education of parents at the time of the first visit in the longitudinal follow-up.

The 71 samples were processed with total RNAseq analysis which included three groups: Group 1 - Controls (N = 27), Group 2 - Parent positive for alcohol use disorder (AUD) (N = 23) and Group 3 (N = 21) - Parent positive for AUD and Grandparent positive for AUD. The Control Group participants did not have a parent or grandparent with AUD based on interview with them and supplemented with family history information from multiple relatives.

Samples were identified that had been stored in a cryogenic freezer in the Hill lab and delivered to the Health Sciences Research Core (HSRC). High quality analysis resulted in samples having 26–65 million paired-end reads. Our initial analysis has been directed at comparison of Group 1 Controls with Group 2 (Parent AUD) and Group 1 Controls with Group 3 (Parent and Grandparent AUD). Additionally, we combined Groups 2 and 3 comparing them to the Group 1 Controls.

Sample collection, lymphocyte isolation, sample preparation and storage

Whole blood samples from venous phlebotomy were obtained between 9 am and noon. An aliquot of 20 ml blood was mixed with 20 ml Gibco™ Phosphate Buffered Saline. Lymphocytes were isolated using Corning™ Lymphocyte Separation Medium. Following centrifuging, lymphocytes were removed and washed and Gibco Recovery Cell Culture Freezing Medium added with DMSO (10%) to cells, with cells then resuspended and stored at temperatures between − 135 C and − 140 C throughout storage.

RNA extraction, library generation, and sequencing

Extraction of RNA and library generation was performed by the University of Pittsburgh Health Sciences Sequencing Core (HSSC), Rangos Research Center (Children’s Hospital of Pittsburgh). Sequencing was completed by the Universisty of Pittsburgh Medical Center (UPMC) Genome Center, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania.

Total RNA was extracted using the RNEasy Micro Plus extraction kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD), following manufacturer’s instructions including the use of DNA elimination columns. rRNA depletion was completed on the samples using the Illumina Ribo-Zero Plus Depletion kit following manufacturers instructions. RNA was assessed for quality using an Agilent TapeStation 4150. RNA concentration was quantified on a Qubit FLEX fluorometer (Invitrogen). Libraries were generated with the Illumina Stranded Total Library Prep kit, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 22 ng of input RNA was used for each sample. Following adapter ligation, 14 cycles of indexing PCR were completed using IDT for Illumina RNA UD Indexes. Library quantification and assessment was done using a Qubit FLEX fluorometer and an Agilent TapeStation 4150. Libraries were normalized and pooled to 10 nM by calculating the concentration based off the fragment size (base pairs) and the concentration (ng/µl) of the libraries. Prior to sequencing, the library pool was quantified using quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) on a LightCycler 480 (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, Indiana) using the KAPA qPCR quantification kit (Kapa Biosystems, Wilmington, MA). Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000, using a S2 200 flow cell, with read lengths of 2 × 101 bp and a target average of ~ 47 million reads per sample.

Quality control measures assessed in sequenced samples

Prior to library preparation the quality of the samples to be sequenced were determined using the Agilent TapeStation. The RNA Integrity Number (RIN) was calculated for each of the 71 samples (mean = 5.13 ± 2.0) While it is generally preferable to utilize samples with RIN values above 6, lower values are considered acceptable where statistical analysis adjusts for the influence of RIN variability14. Moreover, an ANOVA analysis of RIN values by familial risk group found no significant differences in RIN values (F = 0.97, df = 2, 68, NS).

RNA concentrations determined with a Qubit FLEX fluorometer (Invitrogen) were calculated for each of the 71 samples. The combined sample was slightly below the often recommended value of 100–200 ng / µL (mean = 84.51 ng / µL ± 19.80 SE). This value though somewhat lower than the conventional value was considered acceptable in view of non-significant difference in RIN scores across groups. Also, individual RIN values were included in our analyses to control for any possible variation in sample quality.

The analysis utilized total RNAseq which included some ribosomal reads. Analysis of gene expression between groups was completed following the statistical removal of the ribosomal reads. The mean percentage of ribosomal reads was 28.94 ± 10.81 (SD). The percentage of ribosomal reads did not vary between groups (F = 0.26, df = 2,68, NS).

Genome alignment and read counting

Data were collected as paired-end FASTQ files and subjected to genome alignment. The samples were aligned to the human primary assembly reference genome GRCh38 using the align program from the R package Rsubread V 2.18.015. The reference index included sequences of the human ribosomal DNA complete repeating unit (GeneBank U13389.1) and human 5 S DNA (GeneBank X12811.1). Strand-specific gene level read counting was performed with the program featureCounts from the Rsubread package compared against the human transcriptome annotation Gencode v46. The annotation included sequences present in the ribosomal DNA and the 5 S DNA. Intronic reads from total RNA were included in the gene-level counting.

Groups compared for expression analysis

Differential expression analysis was performed with the R package edgeR v6.3 with the voom-lmFit option that implements empirical sample quality weights16. From our original set of 73 samples processed with RNAseq, two samples with a substantially higher percentage of mitochondrial reads (16% and 20%) were removed from the differential analysis. Annotated human genes that had an associated Entrez ID in the Gencode annotation and that were sufficiently expressed were retained for statistical analyses. Specifically, genes with a minimum read count of 15 across all samples and with a minimum count of 10 in at least the smallest sample size were retained. Gene level differential expression analyses contrasted Group 1 Controls with Group 2 individuals with a parent with AUD and contrasted Group 1 Controls with Group 3 individuals with both a parent and grandparent with AUD. Additionally, a combined analysis of data from participants in Groups 2 and Group 3 were evaluated in comparison to data from Group 1 Controls.

Statistical analyses

Results were analyzed comparing familial risk groups with relevant covariates included in the analyses. The first stage analysis utilized age of the participant at blood draw, sex of the participant, any maternal use of alcohol or other substances and whether the parent with AUD was male or female. Following the determination that age at blood draw and maternal use of alcohol and other substance during pregnancy was not significant, analyses proceeded with sex of the participant, and sex of the parent with AUD.

Pathway analyses

DGEs were entered into KEGG and GO pathway programs to uncover prominent pathways associated with any AUD family history versus Controls.

Results

Overview of statistical plan

Three statistical comparisons were made in which the Control Group (Group 1) was compared to those with a parent with AUD (Group 2), with a group with both a parent and grandparent with AUD (Group 3), and one in which the Control Group was compared to a combined group (Groups 2 and 3) of participants all with a parent with AUD with some (Group 3) also having a grandparent with AUD.

DEG results

Differential expression values for both comparisons (Group 1 versus Group 2 and Group 1 versus Group 3) showed significant FDR corrected17 values. In the comparison between Group 1 versus Group 2, we found 731 protein coding genes and long non-coding RNA (LncRNA) transcripts that differed by p < 0.05 (Supplementary Table 1). Comparison of Groups 1 and 3 in which two generations were affected with AUD revealed 122 genes and long non-coding ribonucleaic acid (LncRNA) transcripts that differed by p < 0.05 (Supplementary Table 2). An analysis performed for the combined AUD family history groups (Group 2 and Group 3) evaluated with respect to the Control Group (Group 1) revealed 853 genes and Lnc RNA variants with FDR corrected values p < 0.05 with 12 < 0.01 (Supplementary Table 3).

Global analysis

The global analysis was conducted comparing the combined groups with a family history of AUD (Groups 2 and Groups 3) and Group 1 Controls. The mean-difference plot showing the proportion of genes that were down and up regulated in this global comparison is seen in Fig. 1 which also provides the log-fold changes on the vertical axis. A heat map showing the log-expression mean centered standardized values for all differentially expressed genes is seen in Figs. 2. The set of highlighted genes in Fig. 1 is the same set of genes shown on the vertical axis of the heat map in Fig. 2. From among the 853 genes and LncRNA variants, with an adjusted p value of < 0.05, we have chosen the top signals with an FDR17 corrected value of 8.42 E-05 or less to briefly discuss the function of these signals. These include NCOA1, ARHGAP15, DDX17, DOCK2, OSBPL8, RPL11, DOCK8, ZMIZ2 and NLRP1.

Function in top genes from global analysis

The following highlights the function of those genes showing highly significant differences between the combined AUD groups (Groups 2 and 3) versus Controls (Group 1). Gene function was determined by consulting many resources including GeneCards and the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). Description of gene function included here is based on a summary of the most comprehensive and recent data available from these resources. Original sources were consulted and included following the guidance of these resources.

The NCOA1 gene known as Nuclear Receptor Coactivator 1 is a protein coding gene that acts as a transcriptional coactivator for nuclear hormone receptors such as estrogen and thyroid hormones. In its role forming coactivator complexes, it is involved in chromatin remodeling. It has histone methyltransferase activity rendering it capable of modifying histones and making the DNA more accessible for transcription. Recent evidence shows its involvement in several cancers18,19.

The ARHGAP15 gene (Rho GTPase Activating Protein 15 gene) provides instructions for making a protein that regulates GTPases. The gene is involved in an array of biological processes including immune cell function, nervous system development and cancers. Reduced expression of ARHGAP15 is associated with increased proliferation and invasion of cancer cells. Changes in expression have been useful in identifying the presence of occult lymph node malignancy in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC)20 and as a prognostic biomarker for pancreatic adenocarcinoma21.

The DDX17 gene (DEAD-box helicase gene) is involved in various cellular processes including microRNA maturation, splicing and translation and neuronal differentiation. DDX17 has been linked to both neurodevelopmental disorders22 and tumorigenesis23.

The DOCK2 gene (Dedicator of Cytokines 2 gene) encodes a protein that has an important role in cell migration, adhesion and immune function. Loss of DOCK2 increases tumorigeneses through immune dysregulation24. DOCK2 deficiency results in changes in both the quantity and the proliferation rate of T cells25.

The OSBP gene (Oxysterol Binding Protein Like 8 gene) plays an important role in lipid transport and is involved in a number of cellular processes. OSBPL8 and its overexpression has been linked to liver cancer26.

The RPL11 gene (Ribosomal Protein 11) is of interest as a potential regulator of many biological processes and diseases because of it is a structural component of a large 60 S ribosomal subunit that is responsible for building new proteins from messenger RNA (mRNA)27. It is of particular interest in many cancers because of its modulatory capacity in influencing the tumor suppressor P5327,28.

The DOCK8 gene (Dedicator of Cytokines 8 gene) has been found to harbor copy number variants associated with neurodevelopmental disorders29. Deficiency in the DOCK8 through intronic mutations capable of altering gene expression have been identified and associated with immunodeficiency30. Additionally, DOCK8 mutations may contribute to cytokine storm syndromes that lead to hyperinflammatory states leading to multiorgan failure and death31.

The zinc finger proteins (ZMIZ proteins) are involved in transcriptional regulation with ZMIZ1 (Zinc FingerMIZ-Type 1) and ZMIZ2 (Zinc FingerMIZ-Type 2) acting as coregulators. The ZMIZ1 is recognized as a causative gene in neurodevelopmental disorder with dysmorphic factors and distal skeletal anomalies (NEDDFSA), a rare disorder that is phenotypically quite variable32. ZMZI2 has been associated with triple receptor negative breast cancer33.

NLRP1 gene (Nucleotide binding domain and leucine-rich repeat containing 1 gene) was one of the first inflammasomes described and has recently gained significant attention as a major factor in immunity and disease34. It has been considered to be the predominant inflammasome in human barrier cells with suggested importance as a key therapeutic target for a variety of inflammatory diseases34.

Pathway analyses

Two different pathway analyses were undertaken to provide a comprehensive assessment of the pathways that might be affected by cross-generational familial effects of AUD. The KEGG35 and GO36 pathway analyses were utilized with input from the DEGs showing significant differential expression in the global DEG comparison. Each strategy provides both advantages and limitations as recently reviewed in efforts to provide a compendium of human gene functions for analyzing pathways37. Accordingly, utilization of multiple pathway analyses can be instructive.

Reproducibility of results: confirmation of cross-generational effects

The design of the study that included two unique groups of participants with a parent with AUD, one with parent only (AUD1), and one with a parent and grandparent as well (AUD2), provided an opportunity for comparison of the two groups with an AUD parent. If the parental preconception effect is reliable, the same transcripts should show significant differences between AUD1 and Controls and AUD2 and Controls. Using the list of 731 transcripts with an FDR corrected p value of < 0.05, obtained for the AUD1 group versus Controls, and the 122 transcripts from the comparison between the AUD2 group versus Controls at p < 0.05, agreement was found for 105 of the 122 or 86.6% indicating good internal replication of the results. Moreover, all of the top signals obtained (p < 0.1 × 10-5) in the global comparison in which the combined AUD1 and AUD2 sample was compared with Controls were among the concordant signals.

Family history of AUD and offspring adult AUD/SUD

Based on results of the larger study, we expected that the third generation offspring with parental history of AUD would have an earlier onset to develop SUD during adolescence and young adulthood38. Additionally, we expected that those with both a parent and grandparent might have an even earlier onset than those with just a parent with AUD. To address this, we calculated the age of onset from repeated longitudinal assessments and conducted a survival analysis to determine if the three groups that differed by familial risk for SUD would differ in the frequency of having an SUD diagnosis and the age of onset of the disorder. A Kaplan-Meier survival analysis of the three subgroups targeted for expression analysis revealed significant differences in SUD onset (Fig. 3). These results suggest that any transcriptomic changes revealed by familial risk status may have relevance to the etiology of AUD and other substance use disorders.

Discussion

The present results have important implications for mental and physical health related effects of alcohol. Our analyses suggest that the increased health problems and mortality by adulthood seen in offspring from families in which a parent has AUD6, or both a parent and grandparent with AUD as included in our study, may be influenced by preconception effects of this ancestral use. Increased risk for developing a substance use disorder conferred by having a family history of AUD may increase morbidity and mortality in offspring due to increased use of alcohol and other psychoactive substances. If cross generational epigenetic effects occur involving downregulation of immune pathways in families with a family history of AUD, the effects of having a substance use disorder may be amplified leading to greater disease burden and suggesting a “two hit” model of disease susceptibility. Although direct evidence of this is currently lacking, this working hypothesis, if found to be correct, may be useful by providing increased surveillance for targeted disease prevention. Cross-generational effects of preconception use of alcohol appear to provide epigenetic alteration in offspring increasing susceptibility to alterations in immune functioning and disease burden. This cross generational susceptibility is often coupled with adverse health consequences resulting from excessive use of alcohol and other psychoactive drugs brought about by familial transmission of substance use disorders. This combined vulnerability may place offspring from families with AUD at increased risk for aberrant immune functioning and elevated rates of many physical disorders. Pathway analyses revealed a number of disorders in which downregulation occurred that are associated with immune functioning. The KEGG pathway Viral Life Cycle-HIV-1 is one example of the potential for alcohol use having the potential to alter immune functioning that might be amplified by epigenetic susceptibility as a result of the individual’s family history of AUD.

The present findings may have the potential to inform early identification of those at highest risk for for development of substance use disorders. This could be especially useful as a prevention strategy in families at elevated risk for developing substance use disorders through better understanding of the specific transcriptomic changes associated with familial risk for addictive disorders. Identification could hasten the development of appropriate interventions.

The survival analysis for SUD by familial risk group performed for the participants in this report illustrates the profound effect that familial background has on the likelihood of developing a SUD. Our hypothesis is that familial risk places an increased risk for development of SUD but also results in differential risk for physical illness brought about by preconception effects of alcohol that alter pathways involved in disease risk. The data was acquired as a follow up on a unique study showing that preconception effects of AUD in parents and grandparents was influential in altering methylation of an oncogene and tumor suppressor gene. The current results include multiple genes and LncRNAs associated with cancer development showing differential expression in those with a family history of AUD in comparison to those from Control families. Overall, the results presented here suggest that many health consequences that individuals with a substance use disorder experience may not only be the result of their own use of alcohol and other psychoactive substances but influenced by the previous generations lifestyle that may have included excessive use of alcohol, potentially creating a two-hit model of disease susceptibility. Knowledge of cross-generational transcript status in offspring from multigenerational AUD background could offer valuable information for targeted prevention.

Strengths and limitations

There are many strengths that can be noted from our results. First, the participants were from families with either multigenerational families with multiple cases of AUD or were from Control families screened extensively for absence of AUD through multiple interviews with family members4. Second, blood samples were obtained when the 3rd generation offspring had not developed SUD by the time samples were collected. The longitudinal data collection enabled us to know the participants history of substance use prior to sample collection. Presumably their use of substances was not in sufficient quantity to produce major effects on the transcriptome based on their self-report and that of the parent who accompanied them to the laboratory. Third, the offspring were followed longitudinally so that substance use disorder outcome could be determined by young adulthood. Finally, it should be noted that parents and grandparents were evaluated with comprehensive psychiatric interviews that allowed for determining the presence or absence of AUD and other SUDs and psychiatric disorders. Mothers of the offspring for whom samples were obtained had been queried about their prenatal use of substances so that this variable could be controlled. This information has enabled us to conclude that preconception exposure is the most plausible explanation for the transcriptomic differences seen across the family groups.

An important strength of our experimental design is the use of samples from living participants. While post-mortem analyses of participants known to have AUD before death has revealed hundreds of changes in mRNA and LncRNA8, a recent report comparing post-mortem brain samples with living tissue obtained from participants undergoing deep brain stimulation (DBS) has shown low correlations between the two sets of samples39. Expression levels differed significantly in 80% of the samples tested. Although use of blood samples offers tissue from living donors, the degree to which blood samples represent expression in the brain needs to be considered.

Variation in expression between blood and brain though a continuing concern appears to be quite acceptable based on a recent report from the Living Brain Project40. The investigators obtained samples from individuals undergoing deep brain stimulation to obtain brain tissue from frontal cortex to perform bulk RNA sequencing for comparison with blood samples finding 70.5% of the 24,973 genes tested were expressed in both. Correlations were modest but much stronger where expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) values were concordant indicating that shared genetic control of gene expression is a factor in determining whether blood and brain samples show agreement. Although correlations varied across genes, subsets showed significantly higher paired brain-blood gene expression in fresh tissue from the living donors. Among those showing higher correlations were those in immune pathways.

Limitations of the current study include the relatively modest sample size though clearly of adequate size for RNAseq analysis to detect statistically significant differences between Controls and those with familial AUD history. Another potential limitation is the RIN values of the banked samples that were somewhat lower than ideal. In order to bank samples in childhood before extensive personal use of substances was present with subsequent follow-up to young adulthood required the preservation of samples across many years. Importantly, the mean RIN values did not vary across the three family history groups. In spite of any biological limitations of the samples, the number of significant differences with reasonably robust significance suggests that our results are likely to be replicated in larger samples. Also, internal replication was present in the comparison of the two groups with parental AUD, one with and one without a grandparent with AUD. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that at present independent replication is not available.

Although one strength of the study is the longitudinal follow-up that all of participants were enrolled in during childhood, adolescence and young adulthood, we can only determine their substance use disorder outcome by young adulthood at a median age of 28 years41, precluding determination of actual health consequences in middle and late adulthood. Although our expectation is that the significant effects seen for pathways involving immune functioning and development of diseases could mean that increased rates of adverse health consequences will be seen in the groups having a parent or both a parent and grandparent relative to Controls, we could not demonstrate this.

An additional limitation to our conclusion that the findings are the result of preconception alcohol exposure is the potential for prenatal alcohol use, cigarettes or other drug exposures to have influenced our results. This concern is tempered by the fact that mothers of these participants were each queried in person regarding their use of alcohol and other substances on the occasion of bringing the child into our laboratory for assessment. This information included quantitative estimates of alcohol, cigarettes and drug use for those using any substance during pregnancy and the number of mothers who abstained completely. It should be noted that greater than 70% of the entire sample reported no use of alcohol, cigarettes or other drugs. As expected, the Control Group mothers were completely abstinent during pregnancy, while mothers from Groups 2 and 3 with family history of alcohol use disorder did report some use. Previously we have reported that women with familial history of AUD are more likely to use substances during pregnancy than controls42. However, first stage analysis of differential gene expression (DEG) found no significant effect of maternal use of substances during pregnancy as a factor in DEG results in this study. This may be the result of the careful selection process for inclusion of samples with the aim of including only those with the least prenatal use. Although we cannot characterize the sample as completely without exposure, the careful methods used to determine the mothers’ use of substances during pregnancy suggests that we were able to capture this information in a meaningful way allowing us to conclude that the effects seen were most likely due to preconception exposures.

Future directions

Future follow-up is planned to determine substance use in middle age in these participants whose samples were collected while in childhood through early adolescence. This follow-up will provide important information on the trajectories of use from childhood, adolescence and young adulthood into the middle years. This planned follow up will enable the collection of new blood samples to determine if cross generational effects currently observed in the transcriptome are impacted further by substance use by middle age. This follow-up in middle age can also provide new information regarding the long-term development of psychiatric disorders reported for this cohort in young adulthood43.

Previously, we have found evidence for cross-generational effect in groups of laboratory mice44 exposed either to chronic ethanol or maintained unexposed as control mice which was followed by breeding of the mice and studying their offspring. This can offer control for factors that cannot be completely controlled in human investigations. Subtle differences in the diet of offspring from families with familial AUD could impact interpretation of findings. Finally, stress levels in families characterized by parental or multigenerational substance use disorders are conditions that appear to have an impact41. The role of potential childhood adversity on gene and LncRNA expression and their impact on development of substance use disorders and overall health is currently incompletely explored. The present results may provide an impetus for further investigation of this potentially important phenomenon.
