Extensive fecal cast production and growth of Vibrionaceae in heat-stressed Atlantic salmon post-smolts
Authors: John P. Bowman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Atlantic salmon (Salmo salar) are known to reduce or cease voluntary feeding and show altered digesta consistency under heat stress. This study was performed to determine what bacterial species occur and to characterize bacterial taxa and quantify changes in gut microbial abundance under heat stress. Atlantic salmon (all female, 1.5 kg average weight) in seawater tanks at 15 °C were fed for 2 to 4 weeks. Tank temperatures were increased to a 19 °C “warm phase” until voluntary feeding abated at which point tank temperatures were cooled to 15 °C for 4 weeks. At the end of each temperature phase the fish were stripped of feces and microbiome profiles were determined using 16 S rRNA V1-V3 metabarcoding. The tank experiment was repeated three times in successive years. Abundances of bacteria were determined using qPCR. Vibrionaceae comprised most reads after the warm phase completed. The prominent levels of Vibrionaceae were accompanied by a large predominance of cast (sloughed intestinal mucosa) containing fecal samples. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) estimated fecal Vibrionaceae cell populations increased 1.9–3.4 log units/g after the warm phase. This population then decreased by 0.3–1.1 log units/g by the end of the 15 °C recovery phase. The gut Vibrionaceae and non-Vibrionaceae compositions in the separate trials were different each time. The results indicated heat stress induced inappetence corresponds to increased cast production accompanied by predominance of Vibrionaceae. The predominance of different bacteria in each trial could be partly due to the different initial abundances of taxa in the inputted smolt. Vibrionaceae colonizing the Atlantic salmon gut should be the focus of studies on the microbiology of thermally induced inappetence and dysbiosis in Atlantic salmon.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26485-0.

Introduction

Farmed Atlantic salmon prefer water temperatures between 13 to 16°C1. The temperature limits and associated physiological impacts depend on the life stage2 and is affected to a lesser degree by the genetic background3. Heat stress can significantly impact farmed salmon health, productivity and their subsequent marketability4. The main signs of heat stress in Atlantic salmon and other salmonid species are lethargic behavior and reduced or no voluntary feeding5. This eventually results in substantial size and condition variation within cohorts. Lack of feeding may also lead to inconsistent flesh color due to uneven deposition of dietary astaxanthin into white muscle6. Heat stress and inappetence could be partly due to impacts on renal, liver and osmoregulatory functions. During prolonged and hot summers farmed Atlantic salmon also noticeably excrete pale yellow fecal material. This material primarily is made up of sloughed intestinal mucosal layer material that is referred to as “pseudofeces” or fecal casts7. To date these issues are managed by the salmon industry alongside other summer-related risks including amoebic gill disease8, toxic algal blooms9, sea lice infestation10, and reduction in oxygen availability11.

Fish have a gut microbiome that reflects their environment12 and is also affected by DNA present in their diet including feed pellets13. In addition, substantial numbers of bacteria present in water imbibed by the fish passively transit through the gut14. Furthermore, salmon smolt, when transferred to sea pens, physiologically adapt to the marine environment while at the same time their gut microbiota dynamically changes15–17. Diet may also influence the community composition of farmed Atlantic salmon though predominant bacterial species emerge given time despite even application of “extreme” diets17. These bacteria could be autochthonous (colonizer) species. A bacterial species believed to be vertically inherited in wild and farmed Atlantic salmon is “Candidatus Mycoplasma salmoniarum”18. This species has a very reduced genome. Whether it is a commensal or mutualistic is unknown. It has been detected in Atlantic salmon gut microbiomes world-wide but is particularly predominant in Atlantic salmon growing in high latitude locations19. Under more temperate conditions (sea surface temperatures of 10–20°C) typical of Tasmanian waters (43°S 147°E), the gut bacteria in farmed salmon include a predominance of Vibrionaceae (Aliivibrio, Vibrio, Photobacterium)20. Aliivibrio are usually the most abundant and species of this genus have been suspected to be more predominant in fish expressing casts or producing feces with a watery composition that may contain cast material7,17. The connection of casts with the presence of Vibrionaceae, to dysbiosis and other negative health impacts in Atlantic salmon20 remains unclear. Nevertheless, Vibrionaceae are of interest to salmon health due to their potential to cause disease22–24 and also a capability to act beneficially25. In this respect, it could be useful to understand whether colonizing Vibrionaceae are linked to cast production and whether they respond predictably to summer temperatures when dysbiosis is more likely26.

In this study the first aim was to determine the effect of temperature on the Atlantic salmon gut microbiome. This involved assessment of the species composition in digesta samples using 16S rRNA gene metabarcoding and an assessment of fecal consistency of samples from fish reared in seawater tanks initially set at 15°C. For the experiments tank water temperature was raised to a range (18.5–19°C) that progressively results in reduction of voluntary feeding. The temperature used for heat stress was lower than extremes that have occurred near where the experiments took place27 but potentially could eventually happen more frequently in the near future at temperate latitudes. Secondly, the experiment was repeated three times in successive years to determine the reproducibility of microbiome responses. The final goal was to measure directly bacterial populations levels using qPCR existing in Atlantic salmon reared under the temperature conditions of the experiment thus establishing the degree of bacterial proliferation that occurs.

Materials and methods

Research design

All animal care and experimental procedures in this study were authorized by the University of Tasmania Animal Ethics Committees under permits A0015452, A0016275, and A0017573, and experiments performed were compliant with the Australian Code for the Care and Use of Animals for Scientific Purposes, 8th edition, 2013. Experimentation was performed in in accordance with all ARRIVE guidelines.

Tank temperature experiments were performed at the Experimental Aquaculture Facility (EAF) of the University of Tasmania, Taroona, Hobart in 2016 through to the end of 2018. The EAF houses a multi-tank recirculating aquaculture system. In this experiment Atlantic salmon that were transferred from a marine farm, were all female, and with an average weight of 1.5 kg were exposed to staged temperature exposures as described in Fig. 1. The initial sample group held at 15 °C represented the baseline for the experiment. No fish were held isothermally at this temperature for the duration of the full experiment due to restricted tank access. However, the temperature-based experiments were repeated fully three times, in successive years (2016, 2017, 2018; from March-April to September). For sampling at the end of each of the temperature phases fish were obtained from multiple tanks. The tanks used were filled with 1 μm filtered seawater from the local Derwent Estuary at a daily exchange rate of 10% volume. Water quality was maintained using a combination of protein skimmers and a series of treatment steps including < 40 μm drum filtration, ammonia removal via nitrification-based biofilters, and UV light irradiation. The tank operating conditions used were similar to that previously described28. For the experiment smolt were initially transferred to a 13,000 L seawater tank and fed until they reached an average weight of approximately 1.5 kg. These fish were then evenly distributed between twelve 7000 L tanks in the common RAS system with a stocking density of 50–100 fish per tank and photoperiod set at 12:12 light: dark hours (illumination at 150 ± 50 µmol). The tanks were maintained at 15 °C for 25 to 28 d at which point fish were randomly chosen for sampling. Tank temperatures were then increased at 0.1 °C per day over 7 weeks to reach on average 18.5–19.0 °C and maintained at this temperature for approximately 4 months (100 to 125 d dependent on the trial) at which point fish ceased feeding. Tank temperatures were then reduced to 15 °C and the fish sampled after 2 to 4 weeks. Dissolved oxygen was maintained at 100 ± 2% saturation, water salinity at 35 psu and pH at 7.8 ± 0.1. Feeds used had 40–45% protein, 25–30% lipid, 14% carbohydrate, 8% ash, 8% moisture, and gross energy of 23–24 MJ/kg. Fish were hand-fed a post-smolt commercial diet continually throughout to maintain satiation.

Sampling procedures

Fish were sampled randomly from across multiple tanks by using a dip-net. For the initial time point 10, 10, and 24 fish were sampled in the three trials, respectively. After the warm water phase typically 6 fish were sampled from each tank resulting in 60, 42 and 60 fish being sampled in the three trials, respectively. In the recovery 15 °C phase 25, 36, and 12 fish were sampled in each of the three trials, respectively. The sample numbers vary due to availability fish for fecal sampling. These sample sizes conform to or exceed previous Atlantic salmon gut microbiome studies7,14,21. Captured fish were transferred into aerated tubs containing the isoeugenol-based anesthetic Aqui-S at 17 mg/L (AquiS, New Zealand). All fish capture fish were sampled only once, and no fish were rejected. For fecal stripping, ventral surfaces of anaesthetized fish were wiped with ethanol to minimize contamination, and samples were obtained by gently pinching and massaging the fish from the midline above the pelvic fins down towards the vent; 0.5 to 1 g of digesta was aseptically collected and placed into a sterile 15 ml tube. The tubes were snap frozen in a dewar containing liquid nitrogen or placed on ice. Fish were returned to tanks upon recovery from anesthesia. Frozen tubes were transported back to the laboratory and stored at −80 °C until DNA was extracted. The consistency of the fecal samples and presence of fecal casts was assessed visually using a previously defined method17. Photographs of samples matching each fecal score category is indicated in this article. In this respect fecal scores were visually scored 1 to 5 according to the following categorical scheme: 1 - firm digesta, no casts; 2 – digesta with mid-range water content, no casts; 3 – digesta watery, no casts; 3.5 - digesta watery and containing pale yellow or white cast material; 4 – only cast material obtained; 5 – empty gut, with only fluid with some mucus obtained. All samples underwent DNA analysis, however samples that had a score of 5 were not further analyzed (Table S1). Fish from the experiment were returned to marine farm pens.

DNA extraction

Total genomic DNA from 0.25 g fecal samples were extracted using the QIAamp DNA Stool Kit (Qiagen, Crawley, United Kingdom) following the manufacturer’s specifications. DNA extracts were stored at −20 °C before use. The concentration of DNA in extracts was estimated using a Nanodrop 8000 spectrophotometry for the purposes of DNA sequencing. For quantitative PCR (qPCR) fluorimetry was applied using the Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

MiSeq Illumina-based 16 S rRNA gene sequencing

All DNA amplifications and sequencing were conducted by using the Illumina MiSeq platform and 300 bp paired end sequencing chemistry. Amplicons were prepared using bacterial 16S V1-V3 rRNA gene primers V1-27F (5’-AGA GTT TGA TCM TGG CTC AG-3’)29 and V3-519R (GWA TTA CCG CGG CKG CTG-3’)30. In each run no-template blank controls were used to account for kit and laboratory contamination. The first included buffer ATE (low-EDTA elution buffer) from the QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Crawley, United Kingdom) from the University of Tasmania laboratory where DNA extraction occurred in order to account for laboratory contamination. In addition, a blank distilled water control was also used to account for contamination in the sequencing laboratory (Ramaciotti Centre for Genomics, Sydney, NSW, Australia). Paired-end forward and reverse sequences were denoised, dereplicated, chimera filtered and merged using DADA231 as implemented on Galaxy Australia32 to create a table of amplicon sequence variants (ASV) with sequence counts. Classification of ASVs was performed against the Greengenes2 database33 using Seed234. ASVs from the no template controls were removed from the sequence datasets.

Diversity and multivariate analyses

For subsequent analyses, the data from each trial was analyzed separately. For these analyses chloroplast and mitochondrial-derived 16S rRNA gene sequences were excluded from the dataset. The filtered ASV tables were imported into Primer 735 to calculate taxonomic diversity based on the Shannon index (H’). The data was subsequently processed in two ways. Firstly, ASV read data imported into Primer 7 was logX + 1 transformed, and then a Bray Curtis coefficient resemblance matrix was created. In addition, ASV read count data were converted to centered log ratios (CLRs) using CodaPack36 and imported into Primer 7. The resemblance matrix was then formed by Euclidean distance conversion creating Aitchison distances37. Both resemblance matrices were then analyzed preliminarily by non-metric multidimensional scaling (nMDS). Canonical analysis of principal coordinate analysis (CAP)38 was then used to ascertain how distinct treatment-based groupings (e.g., sampling points, fecal scores) were by utilizing the embedded cross-validation feature. The number of axes used to fit the data for CAP was constrained to the minimum number (m = 4 to 8)39. Permuted multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA)40 was used to determine the influence of treatment and fecal score. For this analysis, all raw data was used, assumed partial sum of squares, applied 9999 permutations, and Monte Carlo adjustments of significance values (assumption α = 0.05). Comparisons between diversity indices utilized the Kruskal-Wallis test where data was not normally distributed.

Differential abundance analysis

VSEARCH41 in SEED2 was used to cluster ASVs into OTUs of 99% similarity. OTU data was then constrained to only include OTUs that occurred in 10% of total samples and that constituted at least 0.1% of total reads within a given trial. Secondly, OTUs were evaluated individually as well as OTUs amalgamated at the genus level. Differential abundance analysis was performed by transforming the OTU count data in multiple ways. This included relative abundances42, Hellinger-transformed relative abundances42, and lognormal transformation42. In addition, CLR transformation was used to account for compositionality43. Significance testing was performed using Welch’s t-test. Significance values (α = 0.05) were adjusted for multiplicity44 by using the online FDR calculator (Carbocation Corp., https://tools.carbocation.com/FDR). Effect size was determined by calculating Cohen’s d. To avoid false positive results a relative abundance for given comparisons of OTUs or genera in treatments (temperature phases) needed to show significance (p < 0.05) for all four transformations following false discovery rate adjustment. Further, the effect size also needed to be large (d = ≥ 0.6).

Quantitative PCR

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was used to estimate 16S rRNA gene copy numbers in the same DNA extracts used for microbiome profiling. The primers used were bacterial universal primers 1369 F (5’-CGG TGA ATA CGT TCY CGG-3’) and 1492R (5’-GGW TAC CTT GTT ACG ACT T-3’)45. The SensiFAST SYBR, NO-ROX Kit (Bioline Australia) was used for all qPCR reactions. A 1369 F/1492R amplicon was generated from Aliivibrio finisterrensis type strain LMG 23869 of 151 bp size using the MyTaq Direct PCR kit (Bioline Australia) and utilizing a PTC-200 Thermal Cycler (MJ Research). The amplicon size was checked using agarose gel electrophoresis and purified using 20 µL MagBio HighPrep PCR magnetic beads (MagBio Genomics, United States) with DNA eluted into pre-warmed (50 °C) of 20 µL 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0 supplemented with 50 mM NaCl. The Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA assay kit and was then used to adjust the amplicon concentrations to 50.5 ± 1.0 and 5.0 ± 0.6 ng/µL with 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0. The amplicon solutions were then diluted to 1.0 ng/µL and then further diluted in ten-fold steps with 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0 using a pipetting robot (CAS-1200, Corbett Research, NSW, Australia). Bacterial population estimation

For Vibrionaceae population calculations, it was assumed an average of 12 copies of 16 S rRNA genes occur per cell (rrnDB database)47. The proportion of reads Vibrionaceae represent in tested samples was used as a reference frame48. In this respect, in initial samples Vibrionaceae tended to be lower in abundance compared to the warm and recovery phase samples. The estimates of 16S rRNA copy number provide a more accurate way to calculate changes in population versus proportions of sequenced reads. Results

Fecal consistency

For salmon held at 15°C for 24 to 28 d (the initial phase, Fig. 1) in the three trials most samples had fecal consistency scores of 1 to 3 (75%, 90%, and 96% for the 3 trials, respectively) denoting typical digesta (Table S1, Fig. 2). Most digesta samples (68%, 83%, and 87%) from fish sampled at the end of the 18.5–19°C temperature (“warm”) phase (Fig. 1) contained fecal casts, represented by either score 3.5 (digesta + casts) or 4 (casts only). The number of samples of each of these categories were equal in number in trials 2 and 3, however only one sample consisted of entirely cast material from the trial 1 warm water phase (Fig. 2). In samples collected after the 2 to 4-week 15°C “recovery” phase (Fig. 1) the proportion of cast-containing samples was observed to vary between trials. These proportions were 30%, 78%, and 36% for the three trials, respectively (Fig. 2).

Microbiome diversity and structure between treatments and trials

7224739, 5269511, and 3114820 sequence reads were obtained in the respective trials. From this data the number of ASVs obtained were 2902, 1691, and 2154, respectively. Samples collected at the end of the initial phase within the three trials showed the highest bacterial diversity based on Shannon index (H’) values (averages: 3.83, 3,32, 2.89). Samples collected at the end of the warm temperature phase had lower diversity (2.57, 1.72, 1.48, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3). The return to 15°C resulted in an increase in H’ values (3.24, 1.87, 1.94, p < 0.01) (Fig. 3). There was a significant difference in diversity between fecal consistency scores with normal digesta consistently higher in diversity (H’ = 2.66 to 2.78, p < 0.01) than that observed for either scores 3.5 (H’ = 1.40 to 2.85) or 4 (H’ = 1.41 to 3.33) (Fig. 3). PERMANOVA analysis indicated in all trials the temperature treatment significantly altered the bacterial community (pseudo-F values of 1.85 to 6.46, p ≤ 0.047). CAP cross-validation (Fig. 4) indicated the temperature treatment groups are non-random with 60.0 to 95.5% of samples correctly classified. Fecal consistency scores also had a significant influence on community structure overall in all three trials (pseudo-F = 1.88 to 3.74, p ≤ 0.033) with normal digesta being most differentiated from the cast containing samples (Fig. 4). The changes in community structure between temperature phases and between fecal score types were independent of each other (pseudo-F = ≤ 1.27, p ≥ 0.182).

Predominance of vibrionaceae occurs in fish held at elevated temperatures

After the initial phase in the three trials most specimens analyzed included a substantial presence of family Vibrionaceae taxa (18.4 to 52.2% of initial phase total reads, Fig. 5). In subsequent phases the Vibrionaceae increased in predominance especially in trials 2 and 3 (Fig. 5) with proportions at 77.3 to 96.9% after the warm phase and 42.6 to 96.7% after the recovery phase. Vibrio ASVs were predominant in initial phase samples from trials 1 and 2 (24% and 39% of reads, Fig. 6) while Aliivibrio ASVs were at low abundance (total < 0.05%, Fig. 6). In trial 3 the initial proportions of Aliivibrio and Vibrio were more equal (8.5 and 17.5%, respectively, Fig. 6). In trials 1 and 2 Vibrio increased significantly in predominance making up 42.6% and 94.4% of total reads, respectively (Fig. 5). In trial 2 Aliivibrio was abundant in only 4 out of 36 specimens but otherwise was not detected. By comparison in trial 3 Aliivibrio was by far the most predominant taxon (96.9% of reads, Fig. 6) with Vibrio barely detected (0.004% of reads). In trials 1 and 2 Vibrio amassed 77 and 81% of total reads after the recovery phase. Also, in trial 2 the Aliivibrio read proportion increased to 16% of total reads with 16 out of 36 specimens having 3.8 to 99.1% of reads represented by Aliivibrio. In the remaining samples Aliivibrio was absent with Vibrio predominating instead. In trial 3 Aliivibrio at the end of the recovery phase made up the vast majority of reads (90.1%, Fig. 6).

For species level classification a best fit approach was used due to the limitations of the resolution of V1-V3 region of 16S rRNA genes, in particular from Vibrionaceae common in Atlantic salmon20. ASVs most similar to Vibrio scophthlami made up most Vibrionaceae reads in trial 1 (Fig. 6), while reads related to Aliivibrio finisterrensis made up most reads in trial 3 (Fig. 6). In trial 2 most Aliivibrio reads were related to Aliivibrio sifiae and related species (A. salmonicida, A. logei) while Vibrio reads mostly belonged to Vibrio scophthalmi (Fig. 6).

Non-vibrionaceae read distributions

Non-Vibrionaceae bacteria were most evident in the initial phase samples (Figs. 5 and 6). The composition differed between trials. Across all trials non-Vibrionaceae belonged to Actinomycetota, Bacillota (Bacilli, Clostridia, Erysipelotrichia, Mycoplasmatales), Bacteroidota, Fusobacteriota, and Pseudomonadota (Figure S1). In trial 1 the most abundant genera present included Myroides (18.9%), Bacteroides (10.8%), Bacteroides_E (7.0%), Shewanella (5.6%), Acinetobacter (5.4%), Streptococcus (4.4%), and Wohlfarhtiimonas (4.3%). In trial 2 Acinetobacter (8.1%), Streptococcus (5.8%), Peribacillus (5.4%), Bacteroides (3.5%), and Rhodococcus (3.5%) were the most abundant genera besides Vibrio in the initial phase samples. Within trial 3 the main non-Vibrionaceae taxa were lactic acid bacteria (Carnobacterium − 12.1%, Weissella – 9.1%, Vagococcus 8.5%, Lactobacillus – 6.7%, Limosilactobacillus – 6.4%, Ligilactobacillus – 6.0%), Pseudomonas_E (3.5%) and Escherichia (3.4%). Virtually all non-Vibrionaceae became less abundant at the end of the warm water phase (Figs. 5 and 6). In trial 1, reads affiliated with genus Mycoplasma_L (family Mycoplasmoidaceae) increased (Figs. 5 and 6) after the recovery phase. This taxon was not detected in either trials 2 or 3.

Differential abundance changes between treatments

Changes in relative abundance of predominant non-Vibrionaceae genera and Vibrionaceae species is shown in Fig. 7. Abundance differences was estimated from the normalized (CLR) values for each sample with significance evaluated multiple ways as indicated in the methods section. Fold differences were inferred from averages of the CLR value differences. For taxa from Trial 1 significant increases after the warm phase occurred for Vibrio (17-fold), Vibrio scophthalmi (21-fold), and Myroides (3-fold). For Mycoplasma_L increases were observed after both warm and recovery phases (1.3- and 2.8-fold) (Fig. 7). By comparison Acinetobacter, Shewanella, and Wohlfartiimonas significantly decreased (1.8–2.7.8.7-fold) after the warm phase but their abundances rebounded (2.5–3.0-fold) after the recovery phase. Vibrio and Vibrio scophthalmi declined in the recovery phase by approximately 5-and 12-fold, respectively. By comparison in trial 2 (Fig. 7) increases in abundance was observed for Vibrio (5.0-fold), V. ichthyoenteri (4.2 fold), and V. scophthalmi (6.9-fold). In trial 2 Aliivibrio increased in the recovery phase averaging 3.0-fold versus only a 1.2-fold increase after the warm phase. This continued increase involved the A. sifiae group (3.4-fold), which was detected in more fish, suggesting growth occurred in the warm phase to recovery phase interval in certain fish specimens, otherwise the DNA contained Vibrio related reads. In trial 2 Rhodococcus, Streptococcus and Myroides abundance became lower after the warm phase by 2.8 to 3.5-fold but no rebound occurred in this case. In the third trial Aliivibrio and the A. finisterrensis group increased 38- to 46-fold by the end of the warm phase. Lactic acid bacteria that made up most non-Vibrionaceae reads in this trial decreased at the end of the warm phase and then rebounded after the recovery phase by 1.7–2.0-fold in each instance.

16S rRNA gene copy and bacterial number estimation

The standard deviation in estimated total 16S rRNA gene copy number between the sets of 8 samples for each treatment and trials ranged between 0.4 and 1.1 log units. The initial sample average log 16S rRNA gene copy number per g ranged from 8.5 to 9.0 (Table 1). The copy number abundances increased 1.0, 1.0, and 1.9 log copies/g (to 9.5 to 10.4 log copies/g) in the warm phases of the respective trials for samples containing only digesta and for samples containing digesta and casts (Table 1). Cast-only samples had only slightly lower abundances (Table 1) as measured for samples from trials 2 and 3. The recovery phase samples had similar 16S rRNA gene copy concentrations to the warm phase ranging from 9.2 to 9.7 log copies/g. Overall, there was a significant increase in the abundance of 16 S rRNA gene copies between the initial and warm phases (p < 0.008) for all trials. The differences between warm and recovery phase were only marginally significant (p < 0.03) for trial 3 samples. There also was no difference between samples containing digesta and casts and cast-only samples when compared for the warm or recovery phases in trials 2 and 3 (Table 1).

Vibrionaceae population were estimated to average 5.7 to 6.5 cells/g in the initial trial samples (Table 1). This population density increased to 8.3 to 9.1 log copies/g after the warm phase. In the recovery phase cell numbers were lower at 7.2 to 8.5 log copies/g. Overall, there was a significant (p < 0.0006) increase in Vibrionaceae populations in the warm phase samples ranging on average from 1.9 to 3.4 log units (Table 1). Reducing the temperature in the recovery phase led to a significant reduction in Vibrionaceae abundance of 0.3 to 1.1 log units (Table 1). Overall, both of the qPCR data and the CLR-based relative abundance data show the same trends, but CLR data underestimates the scale of this change.

Discussion

The experiments conducted in this study documented the impacts of heat stress on Atlantic salmon post-smolts, specifically examining microbiome composition in relation to fecal cast production. Heat stress experiments involving salmonids and other aquacultured fish species have been performed to learn about the effects on microbiomes and overall health. The research designs vary with certain studies exposing fish (typically hatchery smolt) to short term temperature effects ranging from 12 to 48 h49–51. While others, such as a study on sturgeons52 and chinook salmon53 performed lengthier experiments (34 and 49 d, respectively) using larger smolt. In general heat stress was observed to alter gut microbiome compositions and depending on the age of the fish and intensity of the treatment resulted in deleterious health impacts. Focusing only on microbiome-related changes heat stress studies performed in freshwater systems indicated increases in different bacterial genera, but certain commonalities can be observed. Sturgeon, rainbow trout, tsinling lenok trout, and chinook salmon all demonstrated increased gut microbiome presence of either or both Cetobacterium and Aeromonas-related species. Each of these genera have divergent reputations, Cetobacterium being beneficial (e.g., viral disease suppression)54 while Aeromonas includes pathogens55. For salmonids reared in seawater the natural seasonal effects of water temperature results in Vibrionaceae being promoted under summer conditions7,20,56–58. Vibrionaceae also colonize wild salmon when they are at sea as observed in adults returning to rivers to spawn19. Predominance of Vibrionaceae can also be observed in other seawater farmed fish deliberately heat stressed, such as chum salmon59. Surveys of fish in estuaries also suggests higher temperatures drive increased presence of Vibrionaceae26. The latter study linked higher temperatures to possible dysbiosis based on the presence of Photobacterium damselae, which has a capacity for pathogenicity even in humans.

In the experiments performed here Atlantic salmon were exposed to a temperature that was 3–6 °C above their optimal temperature for growth5 for an extended period of time. This is equivalent to an unusually long and warm summer period, mimicking heat waves previously experienced in the region4. In the Tasmanian region climate-based modelling has found that warmer summers would be detrimental to salmon farming productivity but could be offset by increased growth rates during corresponding warmer winter periods60. Since salmon take about 12 to 18 months to reach harvest weight the prospect of warmer sea surface temperatures is nevertheless problematic for the future of the industry.

In terms of fish associated impacts that could be construed as physiological, it was observed that the tank warm phase resulted in a substantial increase in cast containing samples in the period when voluntary feeding had stopped. Fecal cast production in finfish has been attributed to disease, stress, or simply the lack of feeding61–63. The lack of voluntary feeding in salmon is recognized as a coping behavior. Extended fasting can lead to mortalities and size reduction and is considered a serious welfare issue64. It has been shown that Atlantic salmon in sea cages experiencing elevated temperatures and reduced dissolved oxygen increase stress cortisol levels and develop increased intestinal hind gut epithelial layer permeability65. Furthermore, bacterial genes for cytolethal distending genotoxin and EAST1 heat-stable enterotoxin were detected in digesta samples from Atlantic salmon exposed to extended summer conditions in Tasmania57. These proteins, if produced at significant levels, could lead to increased permeability in the gut along with suppressed local immune responses. It is unknown what bacterial effectors relate to cast production, but a temperature relationship seems evident based on the results here and supports a previous study that observed cast production was limited to the summer7. In that prior study an increase in average fecal scores was observed in samples from Atlantic salmon collected during the summer (average score of 3.5 ± 0.1) in which sea surface temperatures reached 17–18 °C. By comparison, average scores in samples collected in winter (10–11 °C) averaged only 2.0 ± 0.1. These corroborating results indicate casts can be another feature indicative of heat stressed Atlantic salmon. The use of repeated trials demonstrated that cast production varied. More cast only samples were obtained in trials 2 and 3 compared to trial 1. The reasons for this remain unclear but it is hypothesized that this could reflect the predominant bacterial species proliferating in samples during the different trials.

Unlike other similar studies the experiments performed here were repeated multiple times and qPCR was used to estimate overall bacterial population change. This was believed to be important since it is first unclear how reproducible microbiome studies are when dealing with different cohorts of fish. Secondly, the size of the microbial community in the gut of fish is often only vaguely defined. From these experiments a major goal was to determine the relationship of temperature exposure to categorized fecal consistency scores and cast production, another feature that other studies have not adopted for fish gut microbiome analysis until quite recently7. Unfortunately, It was not possible to include an isothermal control set of fish for comparison. This lack was due to a combination of logistical and ethical constraints. For an isothermal control group of Atlantic salmon, it is quite possible Vibrionaceae would also become predominant members of the gut microbiome though at a slower rate and possibly with a lower maximum population. Also, the occurrence of cast type excretion at 15 °C cannot be excluded though previous data7 suggests that high fecal scores are much less evident for fish under optimal thermal conditions and is indeed rarely noted in the literature. Future studies would need to integrate such research design considerations and analyses to further unravel the mechanisms of the phenomena observed in this study.

From the results it was clearly observed that most stripped fecal samples from the warm phase became highly enriched in members of family Vibrionaceae corresponding to increased populations due to cell growth. The population expansion resulted in lower alpha diversity after the warm phase, especially in trials 2 and 3. The overall responses had the same basic pattern between the three trials though each trial had variations in the community structure and the apparent degree of growth. When the tank water temperatures was reduced to 15°C there was a small but significant increase in diversity while certain Vibrionaceae having reduced abundance (Table 1). This was most noticeable for Vibrio scophthalmi, which was especially predominant in trials 1 and 2. This response logically could be simply due to growth biokinetics66. The warm phase temperature resulted in more rapid growth rates while reducing the temperature can reduce the growth rate of Vibrionaceae subsequently affecting biomass accumulation in tandem with fish feeding and excretion.

It has been hypothesized previously Vibrionaceae, in particular Aliivibrio could be the cause of the production of fecal casts in fish sampled during summer7. The expected higher growth rates and biomass of Aliivibrio and Vibrio spp. during warm summers potentially increases the risk of dysbiosis or disease in farmed salmon. The experiments indicated only specific Vibrionaceae respond strongly to warm water conditions in a given trial alongside a high incidence in fecal cast production. Cast production may not necessarily be a species-specific effect but could be a generalized response to bacterial overgrowth assuming these phenomena are causally linked.

The primary proliferating bacterial ASVs from trials 1 and 2 were classified as Vibrio scophthalmi. This species is considered an opportunistic pathogen of certain farmed fish species67,68. The pathogenesis relationship of this species could be population dependent since normally this species is considered a harmless commensal69. Overgrowth of this species affects the fish in the experiments performed here temporarily, since they resumed feeding in the recovery phase. Vibrio scophthalmi has been detected at considerable abundance in seawater farmed Atlantic salmon7,14,17 and thus seems to be a well-adapted colonizing species. Genome-based analysis is needed to ascertain whether the associated V. scophthalmi OTUs constitute one or more species and also enable determination of whether they have any potential for virulence beyond an apparent alacrity for gut colonization.

In trial 2 Aliivibrio ASVs also grew, eventually becoming predominant after the recovery phase in about half of the fish sampled. The sequences belonged to the Aliivibrio sifiae group of species. This group includes at least nine closely related species70, including the causative agent of cold water vibriosis - Aliivibrio salmonicida. This group of Aliivibrio seem common to Atlantic salmon in higher latitude waters and proliferation at 15 °C could stem from a preference for lower growth temperatures. From trial 2 the occurrence of cast containing samples remained at the same frequency in the recovery phase as the warm phase. This is unlike the reduction observed for trials 1 and 3 (Fig. 2). Whether the Aliivibrio strains present in Trial 2 exert an influence resulting in a higher proportion of cast only samples is unclear. Taxonomically related strains became more abundant in gut samples in fish suffering from a skin infection caused by Tenacibaculum dicentrarchi20 that had deficient performance related to feeding and growth.

In trial 3 the main species proliferating was most similar to Aliivibrio finisterrensis71. The biology of this species is currently poorly known though it has been observed to be one of the dominant Vibrionaceae in Atlantic salmon and Chinook salmon farmed in seawater in both Tasmania7 and New Zealand58. Typically, in seawater farmed salmon it is predominant in fish alongside Vibrio scophthalmi and Photobacterium species. The results of trial 3 suggests this species group are adroit colonizers of the Atlantic salmon gut as evidenced by having numerous genes enabling this capability20.

Specific experiments are needed to understand what the thresholds for populations in the gut of Atlantic salmon leading to observable negative health impacts and also how this relates to aspects of fish biology. Besides the occurrence of casts and reduced feeding none of aforementioned OTUs seem to be invasive, acknowledging the high populations they can reach. Aliivibrio salmonicida for example was a major disease problem for Norwegian salmon aquaculture until vaccines were developed72. This species’ pathogenesis involves rapid invasiveness and tissue damage73. Besides this species and the faunal mutualist Aliivibrio fischeri74 other Aliivibrio species have not been investigated in any detail. The high populations of Vibrionaceae in cast-containing samples suggests various species can form dense biofilms on and in the Atlantic salmon gut mucosal layer20. This has been studied extensively in relation to Vibrio cholerae, which can form biofilms in the human gastrointestinal tract75. Predominant species occurring within the three trials could be due to exertion of competitive exclusion76 against other possible bacterial colonists. The exclusion is promoted owing to the successful species being already quite abundant in the initial samples from the inputted smolt with the initial Vibrionaceae populations being in the range of 6 log units/g feces. The species detected could be evolved to form biofilm naturally in the gut of Atlantic salmon and other fish species due to possessing inherent antagonism capabilities, biofilm formation capability, and other fitness advantages20. Bacterial strains showing evolved biofilm formation mechanisms have been suggested to trade this capability by being more host compatible77. This latter general concept could explain why the high Vibrionaceae populations occur in the Atlantic salmon gut without generating obvious disease states. This could have been assessed to some degree by examining changes in the gut epithelial layer and by detection of molecular markers that could indicate loss of integrity of tight junctions between enterocytes and other gut cells. A limitation of this study is the lack of such analyses. Experiments that incorporate cell biological techniques are needed in order investigate the nature of the cast production in Atlantic salmon.

Microbiome changes induced by environmental stressors and determinants (temperature, salinity, pH, oxygen, photoperiod length) have been suggested to potentially impact gut mucosal immunity78. Stressors could induce increased immune system responses65 when temperature induced overgrowth of gut bacteria occurs at the same time. Further research connecting immune system functions to heat stress and microbiome-associated functionality is necessary to clarify the relationships and significance to Atlantic salmon health, especially when under sustained warm summer conditions. This would also be aided by developing an understanding of the mechanisms leading to cast excretion.

Conclusions

The results of the repeated heat stress trials demonstrate that cast excretion is accompanied by increased predominance of Vibrionaceae. This increase results from population growth in the range of 2 to 3 log units and could be observed quantitatively occurring in both digesta and in cast samples. The predominant species that proliferate under warm conditions differed between the three trials performed but included a narrow diversity of bacteria including Vibrio scophthalmi, and the Aliivibrio sifiae and Aliivibrio finisterrensis species groups. The role these species play in inducing cast excretion requires elucidation but due to their high populations in cast material are ideal candidates for further study. Such efforts would be well supported by having a deeper understanding of the impact of environmental stressors, in particular increased temperature, on gut mucosal physiology and immunology in Atlantic salmon. These studies would need to include functional interrelations with the gut microbiome.
