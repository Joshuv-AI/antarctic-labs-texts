Diagnostic role of galanin in distinguishing endometrial atypical hyperplasia and carcinoma from benign endometrial conditions
Authors: Can Ata, Tevfik Berk Bildaci, Ufuk Atlihan, Huseyin Aytug Avsar, Selcuk Erkilinc
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEndometrial atypical hyperplasia is recognized as a precursor to endometrial cancer, which ranks as the fourth most common cancer in women and is the leading type of gynecological malignancy in high-income countries. The development and progression of endometrial cancer are strongly linked to increased body fat and insulin resistance. Peptides involved in glucose metabolism, such as leptin, chemerin, and galanin, may contribute to carcinogenesis through shared pathways. This study aims to investigate the role of galanin levels in diagnosing endometrial neoplastic disorders. This prospective case-control study included 85 patients, divided into three groups: endometrial cancer, atypical hyperplasia, and a control group. Serum galanin levels were measured using ELISA across all groups. The mean galanin levels were 472.76 ± 44.86 pg/ml in Control Group, 516.81 ± 51.15 pg/ml in EAH, and 543.15 ± 73.22 pg/ml in Cancer Group. Galanin levels were found to be significantly different among groups. (p < .001) . Galanin levels have the potential to differentiate between normal endometrium and patients with endometrial hyperplasia.Keywords: Endometrial cancer, Galanin, Endometrial hyperplasia, Glucose metabolism, AdipokineSubject terms: Biomarkers, Oncology

Introduction

Endometrial cancer (EC) is the fourth most prevalent cancer among women and stands as the most common type of gynecological cancer in high-income countries. In the European Union alone, over 85,000 new cases are diagnosed annually. Most of these cases occur in women over the age of 501. Endometrial atypical hyperplasia (EAH) and EC encompass a range of endometrial disorders with serious consequences for women’s health. EAH, recognized as a precursor to EC, is marked by the abnormal proliferation of endometrial cells, which can advance to malignancy if not properly managed. Recent studies have increasingly focused on the metabolic mechanisms underlying these conditions, particularly the role of glucose metabolism in their pathogenesis. Altered glucose metabolism, notably characterized by increased glycolysis and glucose uptake, is a hallmark of many cancers, including EC2. This metabolic reprogramming, often referred to as the Warburg effect, provides cancer cells with the necessary energy and biosynthetic materials to support rapid growth and survival3. An increased body mass index (BMI) disrupts the equilibrium between adipocytokines and hormones and is strongly linked to a higher risk of EC4. Although many studies have examined adipokine levels in EC, their role as independent risk factors for EC and their presence in endometrial atypical hyperplasia (EAH) remain uncertain. Additionally, the specific functions of these adipokines in the tumorigenesis and progression of EC are still not fully understood5.

Diabetes Mellitus (DM) is an escalating global health concern and is a major contributor to mortality. It not only serves as a leading cause of death but also significantly exacerbates mortality rates among cancer patients6,7. Galanin, a neuropeptide first discovered in 1983 by Tatemoto et al., is known for its wide array of physiological functions. Recently, it has garnered significant attention for its involvement in glucose metabolism and its potential role in cancer cell proliferation8. Galanin is known for its effects on glucose metabolism through both central and peripheral actions. Galanin inhibits insulin secretion from pancreatic β-cells by binding to receptors on the β-cell surface, primarily through GALR1 and GALR3. The inhibition involves suppression of cAMP production, leading to decreased activation of protein kinase A (PKA) and reduced exocytosis of insulin-containing granules9,10. This mechanism can contribute to hyperglycemia when galanin levels are elevated. Emerging research indicates that galanin significantly increases insulin resistance, processes that are frequently disrupted in cancer cells11. Conversely, some studies suggest that galanin may have a synergistic effect with leptin in reducing insulin resistance12. Enhanced glucose uptake and glycolysis can promote the rapid growth and survival of malignant cells, contributing to the progression of gastric and colon cancer13,14.

Galanin’s interaction with metabolic pathways, especially its modulation of key enzymes and signaling mechanisms, highlights its potential role in promoting oncogenic proliferation. Galanin exerts its effects through three G-protein-coupled receptors: GALR1, GALR2, and GALR3. GALR1 and GALR3 generally mediate inhibitory effects via Gi/o proteins and reduced cAMP production, while GALR2 activates the phospholipase C pathway through Gq proteins, influencing cell proliferation, apoptosis, and inflammatory signaling15.

Objectives

This study aims to examine the expression of galanin in patients with EC and EAH, conditions closely linked to diabetogenic processes such as disrupted glucose metabolism and increased insulin resistance, compared to those with normal endometrial pathologies. By exploring galanin’s role in these metabolic pathways, this research seeks to identify potential therapeutic targets and biomarkers for the diagnosis and treatment of endometrial cancer.

Materials and methods

This prospective case-control study was conducted at Izmir Democracy University Buca Seyfi Demirsoy Research and Training Hospital between May 2024 and October 2024. 85 Patients presenting with abnormal uterine bleeding and undergoing endometrial biopsies at our clinic prior to scheduled surgeries, including hysterectomy for benign and premalignant conditions, as well as FIGO (The International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics) staging surgery for EC, were recruited for the study. These patients were categorized into three distinct groups: Control Group, representing normal endometrial pathologies like endometritis and endometrial polyps; EAH, consisting of endometrial hyperplasia with atypia; and Cancer Group, comprising cases of endometroid type endometrial carcinoma. Groups were formed based on finalized histopathological evaluations of endometrial biopsy specimens conducted by expert gynecologic pathologists prior to staging procedures. Patients with a history of systemic steroid use, alcohol consumption, or hormonal treatments such as estrogen or progestin therapy within the last three months were excluded to eliminate potential confounding effects on circulating galanin levels. The group allocation was based on finalized preoperative endometrial biopsy results confirmed by pathology. In patients who underwent surgery, these findings were consistent with postoperative pathology reports, ensuring classification accuracy.

Blood samples were collected between 7 AM and 8 AM on the day of the surgery as patients were fasting. These samples were promptly refrigerated at + 4 °C and allowed to clot for a minimum of 4 h but no longer than 24 h. Following this clotting period, serum samples underwent centrifugation at 2000G for 10 min at temperatures between 2 °C and 8 °C. The resulting supernatant was then portioned into 1 ml aliquots within cryotubes and immediately froze at -40 °C until further analysis. Prior to analysis, the samples were thawed to room temperature. Notably, the researcher involved in laboratory processes remained blinded to the patients’ endometrial biopsy results throughout the study.

The analysis was conducted utilizing enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits specific to galanin (E-EL-H1301, Elabscience Bionovation Inc.). The inter and intra-assay coefficients of variation (CV) were determined to be < 10% and < 10% for galanin, respectively, ensuring consistency and reliability in the measurements.

Eighty-five patients provided informed consent to participate in the study voluntarily. Ethical approval for this research was granted by the Buca Seyfi Demirsoy Training and Research Hospital Ethical Committee (Reference No: 2024/290). The study was conducted in full compliance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, ensuring the protection of participants and adherence to internationally recognized ethical standards.

Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) v23.0 (IBM Co., Chicago, IL). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used for distribution analysis. Depending on the distribution of the data, the one-way ANOVA test, independent sample t-test, ROC curve analyses, Pearson correlation test and Chi-Square tests were applied as appropriate. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. A post hoc power analysis using observed galanin values across the three groups showed a large effect size (f = 3.31). With a total sample of 85 participants and α = 0.05, the calculated power was > 99.9%, indicating that the study was sufficiently powered to detect differences in galanin levels.

The mean galanin levels were 472.76 ± 44.86 pg/ml in Control Group, 516.81 ± 51.15 pg/ml in EAH, and 543.15 ± 73.22 pg/ml in Cancer Group. Since the galanin levels were confirmed to be normally distributed among groups, a one-way ANOVA revealed significant differences across the groups (p < .001). Post hoc LSD analyses further identified statistically significant differences in galanin levels between Control Group and EAH (p = .006), between Control Group and Cancer Group (p < .001), but a not significant trend between EAH and Cancer Group (p = .095).

We performed linear regression analysis to identify potential predictors of serum galanin levels. The model constant was 512.78 (p < .001), reflecting the average galanin level independent of other factors. Variables including diabetes mellitus (p = .252), hypertension (p = .261), hypothyroidism (p = .771), smoking status (p = .841), and BMI (p = .825) were not significantly associated with galanin levels. Similarly, Pearson correlation showed no significant association between BMI and galanin levels (r = .026, p = .814).

In multivariate logistic regression assessing the risk factors for EAH and EC, age, BMI, parity, and hypertension were not significant. However, diabetes showed a borderline association, increasing the odds for both EAH (OR ~ 4.6, p = .106) and EC (OR ~ 4.6, p = .082).

ROC curve analyses revealed an AUC of 0.819 (CI 0.711–0.927) for patients with EC and an AUC of 0.767 (CI 0.639–0.894) for patients with EAH when compared to patient with normal endometrial pathologies. Detailed analyses and both graphs for groups B and C are presented in Fig. 1.

Discussion

In our study, we found significant differences in galanin levels between patients with EAH and EC compared to those with benign endometrial pathologies. While patients with EAH were not included in their study, our findings are consistent with those of Avcıoğlu et al., who reported higher galanin levels in EC patients compared to a control group16. Although our results suggest that galanin levels are not significantly associated with the presence of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, hypothyroidism, smoking status, or changes in BMI within this sample, existing literature indicates that galanin tends to increase in response to patients’ degree of insulin resistance and glucose control in those with diabetes. Given the close relationship between diabetes and EC, we hypothesize that the rise in galanin levels may be linked to the underlying processes leading to cancer development.

All patient groups included in our study were comparable in terms of demographic characteristics, such as age, gravida, parity, and the presence of coexisting systemic disorders. Recent studies have shown mixed results regarding galanin levels in patients with hypertension and obesity17,18. However, most of the literature consistently indicates that galanin levels tend to increase with obesity19,20. Our findings indicated that, although the difference was not statistically significant, when patients had a BMI below the overweight threshold, the association between galanin levels and the presence of endometrial pathologies, such as hyperplasia and/or cancer, became non-significant. Although diabetes and obesity were more prevalent in the hyperplasia and cancer groups, neither variable was a significant predictor of galanin levels in multivariate regression analysis. This finding suggests that elevated galanin levels may be independently associated with underlying endometrial pathology rather than being solely influenced by metabolic comorbidities. Nonetheless, further studies with larger and more diverse populations are needed to validate these associations and explore potential interactions more thoroughly.

In a recent and, to date, the only study, sensitivity and specificity values for galanin were reported as 82.96% and 68.78%, respectively, at a cut-off value of 371.6 pg/ml for distinguishing between benign endometrium and EC16. Our findings show comparable results, with 73.1% sensitivity and 79.3% specificity for distinguishing between benign endometrial histology and EAH at a cut-off value of 493.55 pg/ml, and 70.0% sensitivity and 79.3% specificity for distinguishing between benign endometrial histology and EC at a cut-off value of 496.05 pg/ml. The observed differences may be attributed to the use of different ELISA kits in these studies. Although diabetes and obesity were more prevalent in the hyperplasia and cancer groups, neither variable was a significant predictor of galanin levels in multivariate regression analysis. This finding suggests that elevated galanin levels may be independently associated with underlying endometrial pathology rather than being solely influenced by metabolic comorbidities. Nonetheless, further studies with larger and more diverse populations are needed to validate these associations and explore potential interactions more thoroughly.

The expression of the GAL gene is induced by protein kinase A (PKA) and protein kinase C (PKC), while its production and release are stimulated by conditions such as axotomy, chronic stress, ischemic brain injury, orofacial pain, viral infections, and chronic constriction nerve injury21. Several studies indicate that galanin can exert antiproliferative or antitumorigenic effects through activation of specific receptors, particularly GALR1 and GALR2, which have been associated with tumor-suppressive pathways in various cancer22. In certain tumors, such as colon cancer, squamous cell carcinoma, and pituitary adenoma, galanin exerts divergent effects—promoting proliferation via GALR1 while inhibiting it through GALR223. In endometrial cancer, GAL1R DNA methylation has been identified as one of the most frequent epigenetic alterations, demonstrating high sensitivity and specificity for malignancy, suggesting a potential diagnostic or prognostic role for the galaninergic system24. However, despite this association, the precise functional implications of galanin signaling in endometrial tumorigenesis remain unclear.

The elevated serum galanin levels observed in our cohort may therefore reflect a compensatory mechanism aimed at restraining tumor growth, consistent with prior reports highlighting its receptor-specific, context-dependent roles in cancer biology. While galanin’s biological role appears dualistic, our findings align with mechanistic evidence suggesting its engagement in homeostatic, antineoplastic processes.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to compare galanin levels in patients with EAH to other endometrial neoplastic pathologies. We believe that the differences observed between the groups could be leveraged to develop a new diagnostic test for patients with endometrial neoplastic conditions, where the only currently reliable method is measuring endometrial thickness.

While the prospective design and blinded laboratory analyses are notable strengths of our study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size, due to financial constraints, may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, the lack of standardized digital histopathological images prevented the inclusion of representative visuals, which reduces interpretability. Third, the absence of detailed histological grading, FIGO staging, and patient follow-up data restricted our ability to assess the prognostic value of galanin. Additionally, we did not evaluate metabolic parameters such as fasting glucose, insulin, HOMA-IR, or HbA1c, which are relevant given galanin’s close association with glucose metabolism and insulin resistance. Future studies should address these limitations by including larger patient populations, comprehensive clinical-pathological data, and assessing galanin levels in alternative biological fluids such as saliva or urine.

Our study can guide researchers in further exploring the role of adipokines and glucose metabolism-related peptides in cancer progression, and in evaluating potential combination tests for early detection and accurate diagnosis of EC.
